## What the specification asks for

| Statement or skill | What to be ready for |
|---|---|
| Substances made of small molecules are usually gases or liquids with relatively low melting points and boiling points | Predicting state and temperatures |
| They have only weak forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces) | Naming the forces |
| The intermolecular forces are overcome, not the covalent bonds, when the substance melts or boils | Avoiding the "bonds break" error |
| Intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules, so larger molecules have higher melting and boiling points | Spotting a trend in data |

## Two kinds of bond in one substance

Take water, H₂O. Inside each molecule, the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are joined by **covalent bonds**. These are
**strong**. Between one water molecule and its neighbours there are **intermolecular forces**. These are
**weak** compared with the covalent bonds.

| Type of interaction | Where it acts | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Covalent bond | Between atoms inside a molecule | Strong |
| Intermolecular force | Between separate molecules | Weak |

When a small-molecule substance melts or boils, the molecules move apart. Only the weak forces between them are
overcome. The atoms inside each molecule stay joined. After water boils, the steam still contains H₂O
molecules, not separate hydrogen and oxygen atoms.

## Why melting and boiling points are low

Little energy is needed to overcome weak forces, so melting points and boiling points are **relatively low**.
Because the boiling points are low, many small-molecule substances are **gases or liquids** at room temperature,
such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, methane, water and ethanol. Some are low-melting solids, such as iodine and
sulfur. They are solid at room temperature but melt easily compared with an ionic compound.

| Substance | Formula | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C | State at 20 °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | O₂ | -219 | -183 | Gas |
| Methane | CH₄ | -182 | -162 | Gas |
| Water | H₂O | 0 | 100 | Liquid |
| Bromine | Br₂ | -7 | 59 | Liquid |
| Iodine | I₂ | 114 | 184 | Solid |

Compare these with sodium chloride, which melts at 801 °C. The difference is the difference between breaking weak
forces between molecules and breaking many strong bonds in a lattice.

## Size of molecule and the strength of the forces

The intermolecular forces **increase with the size of the molecules**. A bigger molecule has more electrons and
a larger surface through which it can attract its neighbours. More energy is needed to separate larger
molecules, so **larger molecules have higher melting points and boiling points**.

| Halogen | Relative formula mass | Boiling point / °C | State at 20 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | 71 | -34 | Gas |
| Bromine, Br₂ | 160 | 59 | Liquid |
| Iodine, I₂ | 254 | 184 | Solid |

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | -162 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | -89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | -42 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | -1 |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 |

In both tables the boiling point rises as the molecule gets bigger. In the second table it rises with every extra
carbon atom in the molecule. The same idea explains
why crude oil can be separated by fractional distillation: the fractions with bigger molecules boil at higher
temperatures.

## No electrical conductivity

To conduct, a substance needs charged particles that can move. Molecules have **no overall electric charge**:
they have equal numbers of positive and negative charges. There are also no free electrons or ions. So
substances made of small molecules **do not conduct electricity**, whether solid, liquid or gas.

Note the contrast: an ionic compound conducts when molten because the ions carry charge, and a metal conducts
because of delocalised electrons. Small molecules have neither.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: explaining a boiling point

*Explain why methane has a low boiling point.*

```
1. Methane is made of small molecules.
2. There are only weak intermolecular forces between the molecules.
3. These forces are overcome when it boils, not the covalent bonds.
4. Little energy is needed, so the boiling point is low.
```

### Worked example 2: using the size trend

*Explain why iodine is a solid at 20 °C but chlorine is a gas.*

```
Iodine molecules (I2) are larger than chlorine molecules (Cl2).
The intermolecular forces between larger molecules are stronger.
More energy is needed to separate iodine molecules, so its melting and boiling points are higher.
At 20 degrees C iodine is below its melting point (114), so solid;
chlorine is above its boiling point (-34), so gas.
```

### Worked example 3: which bonds are broken?

*State whether covalent bonds or intermolecular forces are overcome when liquid bromine boils.*

Intermolecular forces. The bromine molecules separate from each other, but each Br₂ molecule stays intact.
If the covalent bonds broke, bromine would become separate atoms, which does not happen on boiling.

### Worked example 4: explaining non-conduction

*Explain why liquid water at room temperature (pure) does not conduct electricity well.*

The substance is made of molecules. Molecules have no overall electric charge and there are no ions or free
electrons to carry charge, so charge cannot flow. (Dissolved ionic substances make water conduct, but pure water
has almost none.)

### Worked example 5: classifying from data

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts when liquid? |
|---|---|---|
| M | -138 | No |
| N | 1410 | No |
| O | 650 | Yes |

M has a very low melting point and does not conduct, so it is made of small molecules. N melts at a very high
temperature but does not conduct, so it is giant covalent. O conducts as a liquid with a high melting point, so
it is a metal.

### Worked example 6: ranking by boiling point

*Put these in order of increasing boiling point and explain: C₄H₁₀, CH₄, C₂H₆.*

```
Sizes:   CH4 (1 carbon) < C2H6 (2 carbons) < C4H10 (4 carbons)
Order of boiling point:  CH4 < C2H6 < C4H10
Reason:  bigger molecule -> stronger intermolecular forces -> more energy to separate
```

The values are -162 °C, -89 °C and -1 °C. Your answer should name the forces and link the size of the molecule to
the energy needed.

## Where you meet small molecules again

- **Crude oil.** The hydrocarbons in crude oil are small molecules. Fractional distillation separates them
  because larger molecules have higher boiling points, so they condense lower in the fractionating column.
- **Gases in the atmosphere.** Nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour are all small molecules, which
  is why they are gases at ordinary temperatures.
- **Water.** Its low boiling point compared with ionic compounds helps to explain why it is a liquid on Earth's
  surface, and why dissolved salts can be recovered by evaporating the water.
- **Everyday substances.** Ethanol and many fuels are small-molecule liquids that evaporate easily.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "the covalent bonds break when it melts". They do not.
- Writing "the covalent bonds are weak". The covalent bonds are strong; the **forces between molecules** are
  weak.
- Forgetting that a bigger molecule means stronger intermolecular forces and a higher boiling point.
- Writing "because it is a covalent substance, it conducts" or "the electrons are shared so they carry charge".
  Shared electrons are fixed between atoms and cannot carry charge through the substance.
- Using "molecules" for ionic or giant covalent substances.

## Exam technique

- **Explain a low melting point:** small molecules, weak intermolecular forces, little energy, covalent bonds
  not broken.
- **Compare two substances:** say which has the larger molecules and link size to the strength of the forces.
- **Explain non-conduction:** no overall charge, no ions, no free electrons.
- **Use data:** quote the temperatures when naming a state at room temperature.
