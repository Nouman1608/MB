Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.5 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.

## Patterns

- **Dispersed**: scattered farms; large farms, pastoral or plantation farming, water widely
  available.
- **Nucleated**: clustered round a crossroads, well, market or place of worship; defence, shared
  water, cooperation.
- **Linear**: a line along a road, river, coast, railway or valley floor.

## Site factors

- Wet point (water supply) and dry point (above flooding).
- Defence: hilltop, island, meander loop (Durham, River Wear).
- Bridging point or ford; gap through hills.
- Shelter, sunny aspect, fertile land, building materials, fuel.

**Site** = the land built on. **Situation** = position relative to routes, resources and other
places. **Situation decides growth.**

## Functions

- Market town, port, administrative centre, industrial or mining town, resort, commuter settlement.
- Large cities are **multi-functional**.
- Functions **change**: fishing village to resort; mining town declines; farming village becomes
  commuter village.
- Planned administrative capital: **Islamabad**, built in the 1960s.

## Hierarchy

Isolated dwelling → hamlet → village → town → city → conurbation.

Up the hierarchy: **fewer** settlements, **larger**, **further apart**, **more and higher-order
services**, **larger sphere of influence**.

| | Low-order service | High-order service |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Bread, newspaper, primary school | Jewellery, cars, specialist hospital |
| How often used | Frequently | Rarely |
| Cost | Low | High |
| Threshold | Low | High |
| Range | Short | Long |
| Found in | Villages and above | Large towns and cities |

## Changing service provision

- **Rural decline** of shops, banks and post offices: car ownership, online shopping and banking,
  ageing or falling population.
- **Responses**: mobile services, multi-purpose shops, community-run shops.
- **Rapidly growing cities**: services cannot keep pace with demand.

## Studying service provision

- Questionnaire: where do shoppers live, how often do they visit?
- **Desire lines** from the settlement to customers' homes show the **sphere of influence**.
- Evaluate: sample size, time and day, location of the survey.

## Why patterns differ: explaining, not just naming

- **Dispersed** patterns suit farming that needs a lot of land per family, where water is
  available almost everywhere (many springs or wells) and where there is no need for defence.
- **Nucleated** patterns form where a resource is scarce and shared (one reliable well or spring),
  where people needed to group together for safety, or where land was farmed in common.
- **Linear** patterns follow something that runs in a line: a road or railway giving access, a
  river or canal giving water, a strip of flat dry land between a slope and a flood plain.
- In irrigated plains, villages often line the banks of canals because water and access are
  found together.

## Case study: a service hierarchy in a district of Punjab, Pakistan

The public health service shows the hierarchy clearly, because each level is planned for a larger
threshold population:

| Level of settlement | Typical health service | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Village / union council centre | Basic Health Unit: first aid, vaccination, mother-and-child care | Low |
| Small town | Rural Health Centre with a few beds | Low to middle |
| Tehsil (sub-district) town | Tehsil Headquarters Hospital | Middle |
| District headquarters | District Headquarters Hospital with specialist departments | Higher |
| Major city, e.g. Lahore | Teaching hospitals and specialist centres | Highest |

- The same pattern applies to education (primary schools in villages, colleges in district towns,
  universities in cities) and to shops (general stores in villages, bazaars in towns, malls and
  car showrooms in cities).
- Problems: villages far from a road have a long journey to the next level; rapid growth of
  cities can overload higher-order services.
- Global contrast: in many rural areas of the UK, village shops, banks and post offices have
  closed for the reasons in the section above.

## Worked example: explaining a settlement's growth

*Suggest why Settlement B has grown larger than nearby settlements.* Model answer: "B is a
bridging point where the main road crosses the river, so routes from several directions meet
there. This accessible situation made it a good market centre, attracting shops and services with
large ranges. As services grew, more people moved in for work, so it grew larger than villages on
minor roads."

The answer uses **situation** (routes, accessibility) rather than only **site**, and links growth
to **function**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Site | The land on which a settlement is built |
| Situation | Position of a settlement relative to surrounding features and places |
| Function | Main economic or social purpose of a settlement |
| Settlement hierarchy | Ranking of settlements by size and services |
| Threshold population | Minimum number of people needed to support a service |
| Range | Maximum distance people will travel to use a service |
| Sphere of influence | Area served by a settlement or service |
| Low-order service | Frequently used, cheap service with a low threshold |
| High-order service | Rarely used, costly service with a high threshold |
| Wet point site | Site with a reliable water supply in a dry area |
| Dry point site | Raised site safe from flooding in a wet area |
| Bridging point | Place where a river can be crossed by a bridge or ford |
| Hamlet | A very small settlement of a few houses with few or no services |
| Conurbation | Large urban area formed when towns and cities merge as they grow |
| Desire line | Line on a map joining a customer's home to the place where they use a service |
| Multi-functional | A settlement with many different functions |

## Exam traps

- Site and situation mixed up.
- "Nucleated" with no centre named.
- Threshold defined as "number of users" instead of the minimum needed.
- Hierarchy explained without threshold and range.
- Describing a pattern with no reason (dispersed "because it is spread out").
- Saying a high-order service is "better": it is used less often and needs more customers.
- A survey evaluation with no improvement suggested, such as a larger sample or several days.

## Self-test

1. Name the pattern of houses built along both sides of a valley road.
2. Give two defensive site factors.
3. Why does a city have a larger sphere of influence than a village?
4. Give two reasons why village shops have closed.
5. What does a long desire line on a sphere-of-influence map show?
6. Why do villages on an irrigated plain often form a linear pattern?
7. Explain why a district headquarters hospital is found in a larger town than a Basic Health
   Unit.
8. Distinguish between a wet point and a dry point site.
9. Give one way a settlement's function can change, and a reason.
10. Give two weaknesses of a shopping survey carried out on one weekday morning.

**Answers:** 1. Linear. 2. Any two: hilltop, island, meander loop, marsh or river protection.
3. It provides high-order services with long ranges, so people travel further to use them.
4. Any two: car ownership lets people shop in towns; online shopping; ageing or declining
population falls below the threshold. 5. A customer who travelled a long way, suggesting a
high-order service or a large sphere of influence. 6. Houses line the canal banks or the roads
beside them, where both water and access are available. 7. It is a higher-order service with a
high threshold and long range, so it needs the larger population of the town and its surrounding
area; a Basic Health Unit needs only a small threshold. 8. Wet point: a site with water, such as a
spring, in a dry area; dry point: a raised site above flood level in a wet area. 9. For example,
a fishing village becomes a resort as tourism grows, or a farming village becomes a commuter
village as car ownership rises. 10. Any two: it misses weekend and evening shoppers; the sample
may be small; shoppers at that time (for example retired people) may not be typical.
