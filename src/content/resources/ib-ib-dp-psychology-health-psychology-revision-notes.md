> **Course note:** these notes follow IB DP Psychology for **first assessment 2019**, whose last
> examination session is November 2026. The replacement course is first assessed in May 2027.

Health psychology is one of the four IB DP Psychology options, examined on Paper 2 through 22-mark essays. It asks what determines health, how health problems such as stress, addiction or obesity are explained and how common they are, and how effective health promotion can be. Most schools teach one health problem in depth; these notes use **stress** as the main example.

## Determinants of health

| Model or factor | Key idea | Research |
|---|---|---|
| Biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) | Health results from biological, psychological and social factors interacting, unlike the biomedical model | Widely adopted framework; hard to test as a whole |
| Health belief model (Rosenstock, 1966) | Behaviour depends on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, and cues to action | Predicts some behaviours; neglects social and emotional factors |
| Theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen, 1991) | Attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control shape intentions | Intention-behaviour gap |
| Dispositional factors | Optimism, hardiness (Kobasa, 1979), Type A behaviour (Friedman and Rosenman) | Western Collaborative Group Study linked Type A to coronary heart disease; later work points to hostility |
| Risk and protective factors | Risk: poverty, chronic stress, smoking. Protection: social support, self-efficacy | Cohen et al. (1997): people with more diverse social networks were less susceptible to a cold virus |

## Stress: explanations

| Explanation | Key idea | Research |
|---|---|---|
| Biological | HPA axis and cortisol; sympathetic arousal; Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion) | Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1984): medical students' natural killer cell activity fell during exams; Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1995): wounds healed more slowly in carers of relatives with dementia |
| Cognitive | Lazarus's transactional model: primary appraisal (is this a threat?) and secondary appraisal (can I cope?) | Explains individual differences in response to the same stressor |
| Sociocultural | Social status and work conditions | Marmot et al. (Whitehall II): lower-grade civil servants had higher rates of ill health, linked to low job control |

**Prevalence:** national surveys (for example the American Psychological Association's *Stress in America* surveys) report how commonly people experience stress, with differences by age, income and gender. Measurement relies on self-report, so cultural and response biases matter.

## If your school teaches addiction or obesity instead

| Health problem | Biological explanation | Cognitive explanation | Sociocultural explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addiction (e.g. smoking) | Nicotine acts on the brain's dopamine reward pathway; genetic differences in vulnerability | Expectancies about the effects of the drug; self-efficacy about quitting | Peer and family smoking; social norms; advertising and availability |
| Obesity | Genetic influences on appetite and metabolism | Learned associations between food and mood; restraint and disinhibition | Food environment, portion sizes, income and cultural norms about eating |

The same three questions -- what explains it, how common is it and how can it be prevented -- apply to whichever problem you choose.

## Health promotion

| Programme type | Example | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Individual skills | Mindfulness-based stress reduction (Kabat-Zinn) | Shapiro et al. (1998): reduced anxiety and depression in medical and premedical students |
| Cognitive-behavioural | Stress inoculation training (Meichenbaum) | Effective in short-term studies; dependent on motivation |
| Mass media and community | Anti-smoking "truth" campaign in the USA | Farrelly et al. (2005) associated it with part of the fall in youth smoking |

Assessing effectiveness: controlled trials, follow-up periods, attrition, self-report outcomes, cost and whether effects last.

## Worked example: applying the HBM

A student ignores advice to reduce stress before exams.

- **Perceived susceptibility:** "I never get ill."
- **Perceived severity:** "Stress is only temporary."
- **Barriers:** "I have no time for relaxation."
- **Cue to action:** a friend falls ill during revision.

The HBM predicts a change in behaviour only if perceived threat and benefits outweigh barriers -- a useful structure for explaining why health promotion often fails.

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Writing about stress as if it were the only possible health problem.** Name the health problem you are using; questions refer to "a health problem".
- **Describing Selye without linking to illness.** Show the pathway from stress to ill health.
- **Confusing risk factors with causes.** Most evidence is correlational.
- **Evaluating health promotion without outcomes.** Discuss what changed, for whom and for how long.
- **Forgetting culture.** Health beliefs and promotion work differently across cultures.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is this option examined?**
On Paper 2: three 22-mark ERQs per option; SL students answer one question, HL students one from each of two options.

**Can I use addiction or obesity instead of stress?**
Yes. The guide asks for a health problem; choose one and learn its explanations, prevalence and promotion programmes.

**What counts as a dispositional factor?**
A relatively stable personal characteristic, such as optimism or hostility, that influences health.

## Self-test

1. What are the three parts of the biopsychosocial model? *Biological, psychological, social.*
2. Name the three stages of Selye's GAS. *Alarm, resistance, exhaustion.*
3. What did Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1995) find? *Wounds healed more slowly in stressed carers.*
4. What is secondary appraisal? *Judging whether one has the resources to cope.*
5. Give one limitation of the health belief model. *It neglects emotional and social influences on behaviour.*
