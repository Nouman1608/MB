Data used here: 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; rest energies: electron 0.511 MeV, proton 938.3 MeV, neutron 939.6 MeV.

Photon energy E = hf and the electron volt are taught in sections 3.5.9 and 3.5.10; questions here may combine them with particle energies.

## Particles and antiparticles

Every particle has an antiparticle with **the same mass and the same rest energy** but **the opposite charge**. For particles with zero charge the antiparticle still exists and is a different particle: a neutron and an antineutron are distinct, and if they meet they annihilate.

| Particle | Antiparticle | Charges (particle, antiparticle) | Rest energy of each / MeV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electron e⁻ | Positron e⁺ | −e, +e | 0.511 |
| Proton p | Antiproton p̄ | +e, −e | 938.3 |
| Neutron n | Antineutron n̄ | 0, 0 | 939.6 |
| Neutrino ν | Antineutrino ν̄ | 0, 0 | negligible at this level |

**Rest energy** is the energy a particle has because of its mass alone, when it is not moving. Because E = mc² calculations are not required, rest energies are supplied in MeV and used directly. You can still compare masses through rest energies: the ratio of rest energies equals the ratio of masses. For example, 938.3 / 0.511 = 1836, so a proton is about 1836 times as massive as an electron.

### Units

1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J. To use E = hf, the energy must be in joules. A common route:

MeV → × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ → J → ÷ h → frequency → c/f → wavelength.

## Annihilation

When a particle meets its antiparticle, both cease to exist and their total energy (rest energy plus kinetic energy) is carried away as electromagnetic radiation, as photons.

Three conservation laws decide what can happen:

- **Charge.** A particle and its antiparticle have zero total charge, and photons are uncharged, so charge is conserved.
- **Energy.** Total photon energy = sum of the two rest energies + sum of the two kinetic energies.
- **Momentum.** If the pair is at rest (or meets head-on with equal speeds), total momentum is zero. One photon always carries momentum, so a single photon cannot be produced: at least two photons are emitted, travelling in opposite directions. For the symmetrical case they have equal energy.

### Worked example 1: annihilation with kinetic energy

An electron and a positron, each with kinetic energy 0.250 MeV, meet head-on and annihilate into two photons of equal energy. Find the wavelength of each photon.

Energy of each particle = 0.511 + 0.250 = 0.761 MeV, so each photon carries 0.761 MeV.

E = 0.761 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 1.22 × 10⁻¹³ J.

f = E/h = 1.22 × 10⁻¹³ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 1.84 × 10²⁰ Hz.

λ = c/f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 1.84 × 10²⁰ = **1.63 × 10⁻¹² m**, in the gamma region.

### Worked example 2: a proton and an antiproton

A proton and an antiproton annihilate at rest. Find the energy of each photon in joules and compare the photon wavelength with that from an electron-positron pair at rest.

Each photon = 938.3 MeV = 938.3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = **1.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ J**.

λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / 1.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 1.32 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.

For the electron-positron pair at rest, E = 0.511 MeV and λ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m. The wavelength is inversely proportional to the photon energy, so the proton-antiproton photons have a wavelength about 1836 times shorter.

## Pair production

A photon of high enough energy can produce a particle and its antiparticle. It must happen close to a nucleus (or another particle), which absorbs a small amount of momentum so that momentum and energy can both be conserved; the energy the nucleus takes is negligible.

- **Charge:** the photon has none, and the pair has charges +q and −q, totalling zero.
- **Energy:** photon energy = 2 × rest energy of one particle + total kinetic energy of the pair.
- **Threshold:** the minimum photon energy is twice the rest energy of one particle, when the pair is created with no kinetic energy.

### Worked example 3: the longest wavelength

Find the longest wavelength of photon that can produce an electron-positron pair.

Minimum energy = 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 MeV = 1.022 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J.

Longest wavelength = hc/E = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁵ / 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ = **1.22 × 10⁻¹² m**.

Any photon with a longer wavelength has too little energy. A threshold in energy becomes a maximum in wavelength, which is a frequent twist.

### Worked example 4: sharing kinetic energy

A 5.00 MeV photon produces an electron-positron pair. Find the kinetic energy of each particle if they share it equally.

Energy left after creating the rest energies = 5.00 − 1.022 = 3.978 MeV.

Each particle: **1.99 MeV**.

The same photon could not produce a proton-antiproton pair, which needs at least 2 × 938.3 = 1876.6 MeV.

## Comparing the two processes

| | Annihilation | Pair production |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Particle + antiparticle | One photon (near a nucleus) |
| End | Two (or more) photons | Particle + antiparticle |
| Energy rule | Photons' energy = rest energies + kinetic energies | Photon energy = rest energies + kinetic energies |
| Minimum energy | Photons always carry at least the two rest energies | Photon must carry at least twice one rest energy |
| Why the condition | Momentum needs at least two photons | Momentum needs a nucleus nearby |

The two processes are the reverse of each other, and in both the same rest energies appear in the energy balance.

## Common mistakes

- Using one rest energy as the pair-production threshold instead of two.
- Forgetting the kinetic energy in annihilation, or adding it to only one particle.
- Using MeV in E = hf without converting to joules.
- Saying a neutral particle is its own antiparticle: the neutron and antineutron are different.
- Stating that an antiparticle has negative mass or negative rest energy.
- Explaining the two photons with energy conservation instead of momentum conservation.

## Exam technique

- **State**: name the antiparticle exactly (positron, not "anti-electron", is safest).
- **Compare**: say what is the same (mass, rest energy) and what is opposite (charge) in one sentence.
- **Explain** why two photons: momentum before is zero; one photon has momentum; so two photons in opposite directions.
- **Calculate**: write the energy balance in MeV first, then convert once at the end.
- **Show that**: give the converted value to one more significant figure than the target.
