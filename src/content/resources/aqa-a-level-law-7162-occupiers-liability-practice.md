These questions test the section 3.3 row on occupiers' liability: liability to visitors under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 and to trespassers under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the test for deciding who is an occupier, with an authority. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how a lawful visitor can become a trespasser. **[2]**

**3.** State the common duty of care under s2(2) Occupiers' Liability Act 1957. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the effect of s2(3)(b) Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, with an example. **[3]**

**5.** Identify the type of damage for which a trespasser cannot claim under the 1984 Act, and the section that excludes it. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the decision in *Phipps v Rochester Corporation* (1955). **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain when an occupier will not be liable for injury caused by the faulty work of an independent contractor, referring to **two** cases. **[5]**

**8.** Explain the three conditions in s1(3) Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 which must be met for a duty to be owed to a trespasser. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the courts treat obvious risks under both Occupiers' Liability Acts. **[4]**

**10.** Valley Leisure runs a theme park. A cleaner employed by Valley Leisure mops a staircase and leaves it wet without a sign. Mia, a customer, slips and breaks her wrist; her watch is smashed. Later that day, Ash, 14, ignores a "Staff only" sign, climbs onto a maintenance platform and falls through a rotten board that staff had reported a week earlier. Advise Mia and Ash. **[9]**

**11.** Theo hires Sparks Ltd, a firm of electricians recommended by a trade body, to rewire his shop. Sparks Ltd leaves a junction box badly fitted, and a customer, Ola, receives an electric shock. Theo could not have known the work was faulty. Advise Ola whether Theo is liable. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 strikes a fair balance between the interests of occupiers and trespassers. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An occupier is a person with a sufficient degree of control over the premises [1]: *Wheat v E Lacon & Co* (1966), where both the brewery and the manager were occupiers [1].

**2.** A visitor's permission is limited by area, time and purpose [1]. If the visitor goes beyond those limits, for example into an area or activity they are not permitted, they become a trespasser for that part of the visit (*The Calgarth*, 1927; *Tomlinson*, 2003) [1].

**3.** To take such care as in all the circumstances is reasonable to see that the visitor will be reasonably safe [1] in using the premises for the purposes for which they are invited or permitted to be there [1].

**4.** An occupier may expect a person in the exercise of their calling to appreciate and guard against special risks ordinarily incident to it [1]. In *Roles v Nathan* (1963) chimney sweeps who died from carbon monoxide after ignoring warnings could not recover [1]. The protection covers only risks of the specialist's trade, not unrelated dangers [1].

**5.** Damage to property [1], excluded by s1(8) of the 1984 Act [1].

**6.** A five-year-old fell into a trench on open land being developed [1]. The court held that the occupier could expect parents to take responsibility for supervising very young children [1], so the occupier had not breached its duty under the common duty of care [1].

**7.** Under s2(4)(b) the occupier is not liable if it acted reasonably in entrusting the work to a contractor [1], took reasonable steps to check the contractor was competent [1], and where the nature of the work allowed, checked it had been properly done [1]. In *Haseldine v Daw* (1941) the occupier was not liable for a negligently repaired lift because it could not be expected to check technical work [1]. In *Woodward v Mayor of Hastings* (1945) the occupier was liable for an icy step left by a cleaner because checking cleaning required no expertise [1].

**8.** First, the occupier must be aware of the danger or have reasonable grounds to believe it exists [1]. Second, it must know or have reasonable grounds to believe the trespasser is in, or may come into, the vicinity of the danger [1]. Third, the risk must be one against which, in all the circumstances, the occupier may reasonably be expected to offer some protection [1]. All three must be met; for example, in *Donoghue v Folkestone Properties* (2003) the second failed because no one was expected to swim at midnight in winter [1].

**9.** Under both Acts, an occupier need not protect adults against obvious risks that they choose to run [1]. Under the 1957 Act, in *Darby v National Trust* (2001) there was no duty to warn of the obvious risk of drowning in a pond [1]. Under the 1984 Act, in *Tomlinson v Congleton BC* (2003) there was no duty to protect a man who dived into shallow water [1]. The courts reason that people must take responsibility for their own choices and that it is not reasonable to expect occupiers to remove ordinary natural risks [1].

**10.** **Mia:** Valley Leisure controls the park, so it is the occupier [1]. Mia is a paying customer and so a visitor under the 1957 Act [1]. A wet staircase without a sign falls below the common duty of care in s2(2), as a sign or barrier is a cheap precaution against a serious risk [1]. The cleaner is an employee, not a contractor, so s2(4)(b) does not apply [1]. She can recover for her wrist and, under the 1957 Act, her watch [1]. **Ash:** by ignoring the "Staff only" sign he exceeded his permission and is a trespasser, so the 1984 Act applies [1]. Staff knew of the rotten board, so s1(3)(a) is met [1]; whether Valley Leisure had reasonable grounds to believe a customer might go there depends on how easily the platform could be reached, and a theme park full of teenagers makes it arguable [1]. A rotten board is a hidden danger due to the state of the premises, not an obvious risk, so protection could reasonably be expected and a sign alone may not discharge the duty; Ash may recover for injury, possibly reduced for contributory negligence [1].

**11.** Theo is the occupier and Ola a visitor under the 1957 Act [1]. Under s2(4)(b) Theo is not liable if it was reasonable to entrust rewiring to a contractor [1] and he took reasonable steps to check competence, which a trade body recommendation suggests [1]. Electrical work is technical, so, as in *Haseldine v Daw*, he could not be expected to check it; Theo is unlikely to be liable and Ola should sue Sparks Ltd [1].

**12.** The Act gives trespassers some protection, reversing the harsh old position and building on *Herrington* (1972) [1]. Protecting a child trespasser from a hidden danger the occupier knows about is fair, since children may not appreciate risk [1]. However, the duty arises only if the s1(3) conditions are met [1], which protects occupiers who could not reasonably know of the danger or the trespasser (*Rhind*; *Donoghue v Folkestone*) [1]. The exclusion of property damage and of obvious risks (*Tomlinson*) prevents occupiers from bearing the cost of trespassers' own choices [1]. Conclusion: the Act strikes a broadly fair balance, though the standard is uncertain in cases where the occupier ought to suspect trespass [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the 1957 Act for a trespasser.
- Not quoting the s1(3) conditions accurately.
- Forgetting that a trespasser cannot recover for property damage.
- Ignoring whether the cleaner is an employee or a contractor.
