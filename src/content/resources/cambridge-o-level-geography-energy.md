This guide covers **subtopic 3.5 Energy** in Theme 3 Economic
Development of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series
(Version 3). The [Economic Development study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/)
lists the energy types; this page explains how their use varies with
development, how to evaluate nuclear and renewable sources, and how to
build a case study answer.

## What 3.5 asks you to do

| Part | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Importance of sources | Describe the importance of non-renewable fossil fuels, renewable energy, nuclear power and fuelwood, globally and in countries at different levels of economic development |
| Evaluation | Evaluate the benefits and disadvantages of nuclear power and renewable energy sources |
| Case study | Energy supply in a country or area |

## The energy sources

**Non-renewable** sources will run out because they form far more slowly
than we use them.

- **Fossil fuels**: coal, oil and natural gas, formed from the remains of
  plants and animals over millions of years. They still supply most of
  the world's energy, especially for electricity, transport and
  industry.
- **Nuclear power**: heat from splitting uranium atoms boils water to
  drive turbines. Uranium is mined, so nuclear is usually classed as
  non-renewable.

**Renewable** sources are naturally replenished.

- **Hydroelectric power (HEP)**: water stored behind a dam flows through
  turbines.
- **Geothermal**: heat from hot rocks underground, usually near plate
  boundaries or volcanic areas.
- **Wind** and **solar**: turbines and photovoltaic panels.
- **Wave and tidal**: the movement of seawater.
- **Biofuels**: crops such as sugar cane turned into fuel.
- **Fuelwood**: wood and charcoal burned for cooking and heating. It is
  renewable only if trees are replanted as fast as they are cut.

## How energy use varies with development

| Development level | Typical energy pattern | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| Low-income countries | Fuelwood and charcoal are the main household fuels, especially in rural areas; low use per person; many people without mains electricity | Wood is free or cheap to collect; power grids do not reach many villages; little industry |
| Middle-income, industrialising countries | Rapid growth in demand; heavy use of coal and oil for factories and power stations; growing car ownership | Industrialisation and urbanisation; coal is often available locally and cheap |
| High-income countries | High use per person; fossil fuels still important but a rising share of renewables and, in some countries, nuclear | Wealth to invest in new technology; concern about climate change and energy security |

Two ideas earn marks here. First, energy use per person rises with
development, because industry, transport and household appliances all
need power. Second, the *mix* changes: as incomes rise, people move
from fuelwood to electricity and gas, and richer countries can afford
the high building costs of nuclear and renewable schemes.

## Evaluating nuclear power

| Benefits | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Very large, steady output that does not depend on weather | Very high construction and decommissioning costs; long building times |
| Very low carbon dioxide emissions during operation | Radioactive waste stays dangerous for thousands of years and needs secure storage |
| Small amounts of fuel produce large amounts of energy | Rare but severe accidents, such as Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011), spread radioactive contamination and forced people from their homes |
| Reduces dependence on imported oil and gas | Needs high technical skill and strict regulation; public opposition |

France is a well-known example of a country that generates most of its
electricity from nuclear power, which shows the scale it can reach in a
wealthy country.

## Evaluating renewable sources

| Benefits | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Do not run out | Wind and solar output varies with weather and time of day, so storage or back-up is needed |
| Little or no greenhouse gas emission during operation | Some need large areas of land or sea |
| Low running costs once built; can supply remote areas off the grid (for example solar panels in villages) | HEP dams flood valleys, displace people and trap sediment; wind farms can be noisy and visually intrusive |
| Can be produced within the country, improving energy security | Location depends on physical geography: geothermal and tidal power suit only some places |

Evaluate by source, not "renewables" as one block: a solar panel on a
village school and a large HEP dam have very different impacts.

## Case study: energy supply in Iceland

Iceland is an island in the North Atlantic that lies on the Mid-Atlantic
Ridge, a plate boundary with active volcanoes and hot rocks close to the
surface. It also has a wet climate, glaciers and steep river valleys.

- **Sources**: almost all of Iceland's electricity comes from renewable
  sources. Hydroelectric power supplies the larger share and geothermal
  power most of the rest.
- **Geothermal heating**: most homes are heated with geothermal hot
  water piped from boreholes. Large geothermal power stations, such as
  Hellisheiði near the capital Reykjavík, also generate electricity.
- **HEP and industry**: cheap, plentiful electricity has attracted
  energy-intensive industry. The Kárahnjúkar hydroelectric scheme in
  the east of the country was built to supply an aluminium smelter at
  Reyðarfjörður.
- **Disadvantages**: the Kárahnjúkar dams flooded an area of highland
  wilderness and were strongly opposed by environmental groups;
  geothermal plants release some gases such as hydrogen sulphide.
- **Remaining fossil fuel use**: Iceland still imports oil for cars,
  aircraft and its fishing fleet.

Iceland is unusual because its physical geography gives it plentiful
geothermal and HEP resources. When you evaluate, say that the same mix
could not be copied by a country without volcanic heat or large rivers.

## Worked example: an energy mix calculation

**Illustrative data**: a fictional country, Taravia, produces 80 units
of electricity a year: coal 36, natural gas 20, HEP 12, nuclear 8, wind
and solar 4.

```
Share from fossil fuels:  (36 + 20) / 80 x 100 = 70%
Share from renewables:    (12 + 4) / 80 x 100  = 20%
Pie chart angle for coal: 36 / 80 x 360        = 162 degrees
```

A question might then ask you to suggest why Taravia relies on coal
(local coal reserves, cheap to build coal power stations, existing
mines and jobs) and to evaluate a plan to replace coal with wind power
(cleaner, but output varies and needs back-up such as gas or HEP).

## Common mistakes

- Calling fuelwood "renewable" with no condition: it is renewable only
  if replanting keeps pace with cutting.
- Describing advantages only when asked to evaluate.
- Treating "renewables" as one source with identical impacts.
- Forgetting that nuclear output is reliable: its main drawbacks are
  cost, waste and accident risk, not weather.
- Case studies that list sources but give no named places or schemes.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a graph of energy use: overall trend, figures with units,
  any exception.
- **Explain** a pattern: link it to development level, physical
  geography or cost.
- **Evaluate**: for each source give a benefit, a drawback and a
  judgement that depends on the country's circumstances.
