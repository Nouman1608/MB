This study guide teaches Criterion A: Knowing and understanding in IB MYP Sciences. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Sciences guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. MYP Sciences has no fixed list of content to memorise for this criterion. Instead, Criterion A asks what you do with the science you know: explain it, apply it to problems and use it to judge information. This page shows you how, with worked examples.

## What the guide says about Criterion A

The objective is "Knowing and understanding". Students develop scientific knowledge (facts, ideas, concepts, processes, laws, principles, models and theories) and apply it to solve problems and express scientifically supported judgments. The guide says tests and exams must be assessed using this objective.

At the end of Year 5 the three strands are:

1. **Explain** scientific knowledge.
2. **Apply** scientific knowledge and understanding to solve problems set in familiar and unfamiliar situations.
3. **Analyse and evaluate** information to make scientifically supported judgments.

Criterion A has a maximum of 8. The levels are grouped in bands: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8. The guide adds that to reach the highest level you must make scientifically supported judgments about the validity or quality of the information you are given. Examples it names are scientific claims in media articles, or the results and conclusions from experiments carried out by others.

## How the Year 5 bands change

The same three strands appear in every band. What changes is the command word and the kind of situation.

| Band | Strand i (knowledge) | Strand ii (problems) | Strand iii (information) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | state | suggest solutions to problems in familiar situations | interpret information to make judgments |
| 3-4 | outline | solve problems in familiar situations | interpret information to make scientifically supported judgments |
| 5-6 | describe | solve familiar problems and suggest solutions in unfamiliar situations | analyse information to make scientifically supported judgments |
| 7-8 | explain | solve problems in familiar and unfamiliar situations | analyse and evaluate information to make scientifically supported judgments |

Notice three things. First, "state" is the smallest step: a name, a value, a short fact. "Explain" is the biggest: you give reasons and causes. Second, an unfamiliar situation is one where the context or application has been changed so that you have not seen it before. You still use the same science. Third, "scientifically supported" means a judgment that rests on a scientific idea, not on opinion.

Earlier years use the same pattern with gentler wording. In Year 3, band 7-8 asks you to describe scientific knowledge and analyse information. Ask your teacher which year's descriptors your school uses for each task.

## Command terms you must be able to act on

The guide has a list of MYP command terms. These are the ones that matter most for Criterion A.

| Command term | What the guide says it means | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| State | Give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation | One fact, no reasons |
| Outline | Give a brief account or summary | A few linked points |
| Describe | Give a detailed account or picture of a situation, event, pattern or process | What happens, step by step |
| Explain | Give a detailed account including reasons and causes | What happens and why |
| Apply | Use an idea, equation, principle, theory or law in relation to a given problem | Pick the right idea, then use it on the case |
| Calculate | Obtain a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working | Working, answer and unit |
| Suggest | Propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer | A reasoned idea for a new case |
| Analyse | Break down to bring out essential elements or structure | Parts, links and a conclusion |
| Interpret | Use knowledge to recognise trends and draw conclusions from given information | Say what the data or text shows |
| Evaluate | Make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations | Both sides, then a verdict |
| Justify | Give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer | The reason that proves it |

## Strand i: explaining scientific knowledge

An explanation links an observation to a scientific idea through a cause. A useful pattern is **claim, idea, link**.

- **Claim**: what happens.
- **Idea**: the scientific concept or law that applies.
- **Link**: how the idea causes the claim.

**Worked example.** Explain why a bicycle pump gets warm when you use it.

- Claim: the barrel of the pump warms up.
- Idea: when a gas is compressed, work is done on it, and the work increases the kinetic energy of its particles.
- Link: the piston pushes the air particles closer and makes them move faster. Faster particles collide with the walls of the pump with more energy and pass that energy to the metal, so the temperature of the barrel rises. Some warmth also comes from friction between the piston and the barrel.

A band 3-4 answer might only say "the air is squashed so it gets hot", which outlines the knowledge. The answer above explains it, because it gives the cause at particle level.

## Strand ii: applying knowledge to solve problems

Problems come in two forms: calculation problems and reasoning problems. Both need the right idea from the right topic.

**Calculation method.** Write the relationship, substitute with units, calculate, then state the answer with a unit. The guide's examination conventions say final answers are normally given to three significant figures unless the question says otherwise, and numerical values usually carry units.

**Worked example 1 (density).** A rectangular block of metal measures 2.0 cm by 3.0 cm by 4.0 cm and has a mass of 54.0 g. Calculate its density.

1. Volume = 2.0 × 3.0 × 4.0 = 24.0 cm³.
2. Density = mass ÷ volume = 54.0 g ÷ 24.0 cm³.
3. Density = 2.25 g/cm³.

The answer has three significant figures. A common slip is to divide volume by mass, so check that the unit reads g/cm³ and that the number is sensible for a metal.

**Worked example 2 (motion).** A car moving at 12 m/s accelerates at 1.5 m/s² for 8.0 s. Find its final speed and the distance it travels.

1. Final speed: v = u + at = 12 + (1.5 × 8.0) = 12 + 12 = 24 m/s.
2. Distance: s = ut + ½at² = (12 × 8.0) + (0.5 × 1.5 × 8.0²) = 96 + 48 = 144 m.

Write each equation on its own line with the numbers substituted. This earns credit for working even if you slip on the arithmetic.

## Applying knowledge in unfamiliar situations

An unfamiliar situation changes the context, but the science stays the same. Your task is to spot which known idea fits.

**Worked example 3.** A sealed bag of crisps is packed at sea level. On a mountain road its sides bulge. Suggest an explanation.

- Spot the idea: gas pressure depends on the number of particle collisions with a surface.
- Apply it: inside the bag the amount of gas is fixed, so the pressure inside stays about the same. Outside, the air is thinner at altitude, so the pressure outside is lower.
- Conclude: the pressure inside is now greater than the pressure outside, so the bag expands until the pressures are about equal.

You never studied crisps in a lesson, but you did study gas pressure. That link is what the criterion rewards. If you freeze, ask three questions. What type of science is this (particles, forces, energy, cells)? What variable has changed? What idea connects the two?

## Strand iii: making scientifically supported judgments

This strand is what separates the top band. The sources may be a media claim, a data table, a graph or an experiment someone else carried out. You must analyse the information (break it into parts) and then evaluate it (weigh up strengths and limitations) and give a judgment.

**Method.**

1. Identify the claim being made.
2. Identify the scientific idea that should support it.
3. Test the claim against the idea and against any data.
4. Weigh up what supports and what weakens the claim.
5. State a judgment and say what extra evidence would settle it.

**Worked example 4.** A magazine says: "Keep plants in your bedroom, because they give out oxygen all night and help you breathe better."

- Claim: plants release oxygen at night.
- Idea: plants photosynthesise in light, releasing oxygen. They respire all the time, using oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide.
- Test: in the dark there is no photosynthesis, so there is no net release of oxygen at night. The plants respire, which takes oxygen from the room.
- Weigh up: the effect of a few pot plants on the oxygen in a room is tiny in either direction, so neither harm nor benefit is large. Plants may still have other benefits, such as appearance or humidity.
- Judgment: the claim is not supported by the science. The statement about "all night" is the faulty part.

**Worked example 5 (data).** A table gives the activity of an enzyme (in arbitrary units) at four pH values: pH 3 is 2, pH 5 is 8, pH 7 is 14 and pH 9 is 6.

- Interpret: activity is highest at pH 7 among those tested and lower on either side.
- Explain: pH changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate fits less well. At pH 9 the enzyme may start to denature, which is permanent.
- Judge: the optimum is near pH 7, but it could be anywhere between pH 5 and pH 9. More readings between those values are needed to locate it.

## Using scientific language

Criterion A rewards accurate terms. Use "particles" rather than "bits", "thermal energy transferred" rather than "heat goes", and "denatured" rather than "broken". Do not use a word you cannot define. Include correct units, and write chemical formulas with proper subscripts. The guide's examination conventions say chemical formulas missing subscripts or superscripts will not be rewarded.

## How Criterion A is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for sciences, the Knowing and understanding task carries 25 marks, assessed against Criterion A. The whole examination is out of 100, with 25 marks for each of the four criteria. The guide notes that marks in this task may also be awarded against other criteria, for example Criterion C when a question needs interpretation of data. The topics for the examination are set out by subject (biology, chemistry, physics and integrated sciences). Sources can include data tables, images, videos, animations, simulations and graphs, and tools include an on-screen calculator, a measuring tool and a graph plotter.

## Common errors

- Giving a state-level answer (a fact) when the command word is explain.
- Naming a law without applying it to the case in the question.
- Leaving out units, or using the wrong ones.
- Skipping the working in a calculation question.
- Freezing on an unfamiliar context instead of spotting the familiar idea inside it.
- Accepting a media claim because it sounds scientific.
- Giving a judgment with no scientific reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion A in IB MYP Sciences?**
Criterion A is Knowing and understanding. It assesses whether you can explain scientific knowledge, apply it to solve problems in familiar and unfamiliar situations, and analyse and evaluate information to make scientifically supported judgments.

**What is an unfamiliar situation in MYP Sciences?**
The guide's glossary describes it as a problem or situation in which the context or the application has been changed so that it is unfamiliar to the student. The science needed is still science you know.

**How many marks is Criterion A worth in the on-screen examination?**
The Knowing and understanding task is worth 25 marks, out of 100 for the whole examination. The guide says the distribution may vary by no more than three marks.

**What does "scientifically supported judgment" mean?**
It is a conclusion backed by a scientific idea or by data, not by opinion. At the top band you judge the validity or quality of information, such as a media claim or another person's results.
