These notes condense the [Health and wellbeing study guide](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-health-and-wellbeing/). Test yourself afterwards with the [Health and wellbeing practice questions](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-health-and-wellbeing-practice/).

They follow the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025): the topic list in section 3, the assessment objectives in section 2 and the component details in section 4. The topic list is for all components, so Health and wellbeing could be the context for Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report or Component 3 Team Project. **Knowledge of this topic is not assessed.** These notes show how to use it to practise the skills that are: research, analysis and evaluation, reflection, communication and collaboration. See also the [Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and the [0457 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## What each component wants from this topic

| Component | What you do with Health and wellbeing |
|---|---|
| Component 1 Written Exam (1 hour 25 minutes, 70 marks) | Analyse unseen sources on a health issue; evaluate research and evidence; judge arguments; assess actions. AO1 only. |
| Component 2 Individual Report (1500–2000 words, 60 marks) | Choose a health issue that divides opinion; devise a global research question; local and/or national and global perspectives; causes, consequences, one proposed course of action |
| Component 3 Team Project (70 marks) | Pick a local health issue; research different perspectives; plan, carry out and measure an action; write a Reflective Paper |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (syllabus wording where printed) |
|---|---|
| Issue | "an important subject or problem for discussion" |
| Perspective | "a viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning" |

| Evidence | "information about a global issue that helps to develop understanding or prove that something is true or false" |
| Argument | "a series of statements containing reasons and evidence which support a claim about a global issue" |
| Claim | A statement put forward as true, which may or may not be supported |

| Prediction | A statement about what will happen in future |

## Real examples at a glance

Use these only as practice material or as research starting points. They earn no marks on their own.

| Example | What it is | Why it is useful | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| WHO Constitution (adopted 1946, in force 1948) | Defines health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity" | Shows a global perspective that includes mental and social health | Critics say "complete" well-being is an unrealistic standard |
| UN Sustainable Development Goal 3 (2015) | "Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages" | Shows global recognition of the issue | A goal is a value statement, not evidence of progress |
| Mexico sugary drinks tax (2014) | One peso per litre on sugar-sweetened drinks | A national course of action using price | Critics argue it falls hardest on poorer households |
| UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy (April 2018) | Charge on producers by sugar content | Many manufacturers cut sugar before it began | Targets drinks only, not the whole diet |
| WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (adopted 2003, in force 2005) | International treaty on tobacco | Global cooperation on a health issue | Each country still decides how strongly to apply it |
| Smallpox eradication (declared by WHO in 1980) | Global vaccination campaign | Strongest example of global health action | Few diseases are as easy to target |
| Antimicrobial resistance | Bacteria becoming resistant to antibiotics; WHO calls it one of the top global public health threats | Clear causes and consequences across levels | Needs action by doctors, farmers and governments at once |

## Perspectives to look for

- **Patients and families**: cost, distance, waiting, dignity.
- **Health workers**: workload, safety, resources.
- **Governments**: health versus budget, jobs and public support.
- **Businesses**: vested interest in sales; may fund research.
- **International bodies**: global patterns, cooperation, fairness between countries.
- **Cultural and religious groups**: values about the body, diet and treatment.

Always add **why**: the reason or value behind each view.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic versus issue.** "Health and wellbeing" is a topic; "Should sugary drinks be taxed?" is an issue.
- **Fact versus opinion.** "The clinic closed in March" is a fact; "the clinic was useless" is an opinion.
- **Link versus cause.** Two trends rising together does not prove one caused the other.
- **Self-reported versus measured.** "I exercise daily" is weaker than recorded attendance.
- **Personal story versus pattern.** One case is testimony, not proof of a national trend.
- **Cause versus consequence.** Cost of fresh food may cause poor diet; hospital pressure is a consequence.
- **Course of action versus outcome.** The action is what is done; the outcome is the effect it has.

## Evidence checklist for any health source

1. Who wrote or funded it? Any vested interest?
2. Fact, opinion, prediction or generalisation?
3. Sample: how many, chosen how, representative of whom?
4. Self-reported or measured?
5. Comparison group or "before" figure?
6. Short-term or long-term?
7. Does the conclusion go further than the data?

## Mini plan: an "assess actions" answer

Typical task: using several sources, evaluate two or more actions to improve young people's health, and decide which is most effective.

1. **Opening judgement**: name the action you think is most effective and give the main reason.
2. **Action 1**: what it is; which cause it targets; evidence from the sources for it; one limitation (cost, fairness, side effect).
3. **Action 2**: same structure; compare directly with Action 1.
4. **Action 3 or combination**: could two actions work better together?
5. **Weigh up**: which criterion matters most here (effectiveness, fairness, cost, speed) and why.
6. **Conclusion**: a clear, reasoned judgement that follows from the points above.

For the Individual Report, keep the same core: issue, perspectives, causes, consequences, courses of action, then one proposed course of action with how it would be put into practice and its likely impact.

## Quick self-test

1. What does the syllabus say about topic knowledge in 0457?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Which assessment objective is the only one assessed in the Written Exam?
4. What is a vested interest? Give a health example.
5. Is "Junk food adverts should be banned" a fact or an opinion?
6. Explain why "My uncle stopped smoking and got healthier, so smoking bans work" is a generalisation.
7. Name one global, one national and one local health issue.
8. What is the word count for the Individual Report?
9. Give two criteria for judging a course of action.
10. Why is a survey without a comparison group weak evidence that an action worked?
11. What must a Team Project action include, according to the syllabus?

### Answers

1. Knowledge of content is not assessed; topics are contexts for practising skills.
2. "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning."
3. AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation.
4. A personal or financial stake in the outcome, such as a drinks company commenting on a sugar tax.
5. Opinion: it is a judgement about what should happen.
6. It draws a conclusion about a whole policy from one person's case.
7. For example: antimicrobial resistance (global); a sugary drinks tax (national); distance to a rural clinic (local).
8. 1500–2000 words, not counting the bibliography or reference list.
9. Any two of: effectiveness, cost, fairness, side effects, sustainability, who acts.
10. You cannot tell whether the change would have happened anyway.
11. The team plans and carries out the action and measures its success.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding outside facts about health instead of using the sources provided in the Written Exam.
- Saying "this source is biased" without naming who gains or quoting the loaded wording.
- Treating a percentage as strong evidence without asking about sample size and how people were chosen.
- Missing that a health claim is self-reported.
- Describing what a group thinks without explaining the value or reason behind it.
- Explaining a consequence as "bad for health" without saying who is affected and how.
- Evaluating only one action when the question asks you to assess actions.
- Giving a conclusion that sits on the fence instead of making a reasoned judgement.
- In the Individual Report, choosing a question with only one side, or forgetting the global perspective.
- In the Team Project, choosing an action whose success cannot be measured.
