## 4.1.1.1 Atoms, elements and compounds

**Key definitions**

- **Atom:** the smallest part of an element that can exist.
- **Element:** made of only one type of atom; about 100 exist, shown in the periodic table, each with a symbol.
- **Compound:** two or more elements **chemically combined in fixed proportions**. Separated only by chemical
  reactions.
- **Chemical reaction:** always makes one or more new substances; often has a detectable energy change.

**Symbols to know:** the first 20 elements, Groups 1 and 7, and other elements named in the specification.

**Naming rules**

| Formula part | Name |
|---|---|
| Cl, O, S, Br, I on their own | chloride, oxide, sulfide, bromide, iodide |
| SO₄ | sulfate |
| CO₃ | carbonate |
| NO₃ | nitrate |
| OH | hydroxide |

**Method: balancing an equation**

```
1. Word equation first.
2. Correct formulae (never change them).
3. Big numbers in front only.
4. Count every element on both sides.
```

**Worked reminder.** Magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen:
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Check: Mg 1 = 1, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2.

**Higher tier only: ionic and half equations**

- Ionic equation: leave out spectator ions (unchanged on both sides).
- Half equation: one species with electrons, e⁻.
- Both must balance in atoms **and** charge.

Worked reminder: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (electron lost, written on the right). Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻ (electrons gained,
written on the left).

## 4.1.1.2 Mixtures

A **mixture** is two or more elements or compounds **not chemically combined**. Each keeps its own chemical
properties. Separation is **physical**: no reaction, no new substance.

| Mixture | Technique | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Insoluble solid + liquid | Filtration | Solid particles are trapped by filter paper |
| Solution, want the solid | Crystallisation | Solvent evaporates, solute crystallises |
| Solution, want the solvent | Simple distillation | Solvent boils off and is condensed |
| Liquids that mix, different boiling points | Fractional distillation | Each liquid condenses at its own temperature |
| Several dissolved dyes | Chromatography | Substances move different distances |

**Method: choosing a technique**

```
1. Is anything insoluble?            -> filter it out first
2. Want a dissolved solid?           -> crystallisation
3. Want the liquid from a solution?  -> simple distillation
4. Two or more liquids that mix?     -> fractional distillation
5. Several coloured substances?      -> chromatography
```

**Must-know distinction: mixture vs compound**

| Mixture | Compound |
|---|---|
| Not chemically combined | Chemically combined |
| Any proportions | Fixed proportions |
| Properties of each part kept | New properties |
| Separated by physical processes | Separated only by chemical reactions |

## 4.1.1.3 The model of the atom

```
indivisible spheres
  -> electron discovered -> plum pudding (positive ball, electrons embedded)
  -> alpha scattering    -> nuclear model (mass and charge concentrated in a nucleus)
  -> Bohr                -> electrons orbit at specific distances
  -> later experiments   -> positive charge = whole number of protons
  -> Chadwick            -> neutrons in the nucleus (about 20 years after the nucleus was accepted)
```

- **Why the model changed:** the plum pudding model could not explain the scattering results, so new evidence
  forced a new model.
- **Plum pudding vs nuclear:** spread-out positive charge and mass vs a tiny, dense, charged nucleus with electrons
  outside it and mostly empty space.
- Bohr's calculations agreed with experimental observations. You do not need the details of Bohr's or
  Chadwick's experiments.

## 4.1.1.4 and 4.1.1.5 Particles, size and mass

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | -1 | Very small | Shells |

- Atoms: electrons = protons, so **no overall charge**.
- **Atomic number** = protons (defines the element). **Mass number** = protons + neutrons.
- **Isotopes:** same element, same protons, different neutrons.
- Atomic radius about **0.1 nm = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**. Nuclear radius less than 1/10 000 of that, about **1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m**.
- Almost all the mass is in the nucleus.

**Method: protons, neutrons, electrons**

```
p = atomic number (bottom number)
n = mass number - atomic number
e = p - ion charge      (2+ ion: p - 2; 1- ion: p + 1)
```

Worked reminder: ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺ has 12 protons, 24 - 12 = 12 neutrons and 12 - 2 = 10 electrons.

**Unit reminder:** 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m, so 0.1 nm = 0.1 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

**Worked scale reminder.** Scale an atom up to a radius of 50 m. The nucleus radius is
50 ÷ 10 000 = 0.005 m = 5 mm. Almost all the mass sits in that tiny centre.

**Must-know distinctions**

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Atomic number vs mass number | Protons only vs protons + neutrons |
| Isotope vs ion | Different neutrons (still neutral) vs different electrons (charged) |
| Nucleus vs shells | Protons and neutrons, almost all the mass vs electrons, very little mass |
| Simple vs fractional distillation | Solvent from a solution vs liquids that mix with different boiling points |

## 4.1.1.6 Relative atomic mass

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Relative atomic mass, Ar | (Σ isotope mass × percentage abundance) ÷ 100 |
| Neutrons in an isotope | mass number - atomic number |

Ar is an **average** that takes account of the abundance of each isotope, so it is usually not a whole number and
always lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses; with two isotopes it is nearer the more abundant one.

**Worked reminder with three isotopes.** Silicon is 92% silicon-28, 5% silicon-29 and 3% silicon-30.

```
Ar = (28 x 92 + 29 x 5 + 30 x 3) / 100
   = (2576 + 145 + 90) / 100
   = 28.11  ->  28.1 (1 d.p.)
```

Check the percentages add up to 100 before you start; if one is missing, it is 100 minus the others.

## 4.1.1.7 Electronic structure

- Electrons fill the **lowest available energy levels** (innermost shells) first.
- Up to element 20: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second, 8 in the third, then the fourth.
- Give it as numbers (sodium 2,8,1) **or** as a diagram (circles round a nucleus with crosses for electrons).
- "Shells" and "energy levels" are both accepted.

| Element | Atomic number | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium | 3 | 2,1 |
| Oxygen | 8 | 2,6 |
| Sodium | 11 | 2,8,1 |
| Sulfur | 16 | 2,8,6 |
| Potassium | 19 | 2,8,8,1 |

## Quick self-test

1. What is the smallest part of an element that can exist?
2. Name Na₂SO₄.
3. How many atoms are shown in the formula Al₂(SO₄)₃?
4. Balance: __Na + __Cl₂ → __NaCl
5. Two liquids that mix boil at 80 °C and 110 °C. Which technique separates them?
6. Why can a mixture be separated by physical processes but a compound cannot?
7. What did the plum pudding model say an atom was like?
8. Whose experimental work gave evidence for the neutron?
9. Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in ¹⁹₉F⁻.
10. Boron is 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11. Calculate its relative atomic mass.
11. Write the electronic structures of beryllium (4) and phosphorus (15).
12. Higher tier only: write the half equation for a potassium atom forming a potassium ion.

### Answers

1. An atom.
2. Sodium sulfate.
3. 2 + 3 + (3 × 4) = **17** atoms.
4. **2**Na + Cl₂ → **2**NaCl.
5. Fractional distillation.
6. In a mixture the substances are not chemically combined; in a compound they are, so only a chemical reaction
   can separate them.
7. A ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
8. James Chadwick.
9. 9 protons, 10 neutrons, 10 electrons.
10. (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) ÷ 100 = (200 + 880) ÷ 100 = **10.8**.
11. Beryllium 2,2; phosphorus 2,8,5.
12. K → K⁺ + e⁻

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "Mg(NO₃)₂ has 6 atoms": the 2 multiplies everything inside the bracket.
- Balancing by changing subscripts, for example writing NaCl₂.
- Naming CaCO₃ "calcium carbide" or "calcium oxide": CO₃ is carbonate.
- Higher tier: putting electrons on the wrong side of a half equation, or leaving the charges unbalanced.
- Choosing evaporation when the question asks for the pure **liquid** from a solution. That needs distillation.
- Calling a separation process a chemical reaction. It is physical; no new substance forms.
- Describing the plum pudding model as "electrons orbiting a positive centre". It had no nucleus.
- Saying Bohr discovered the nucleus. The nucleus came from the scattering experiment; Bohr placed electrons at
  specific distances.
- Subtracting the charge the wrong way for negative ions: a 2- ion has two **more** electrons than protons.
- Giving Ar as a whole number when the data give a decimal, or forgetting to divide by 100.
