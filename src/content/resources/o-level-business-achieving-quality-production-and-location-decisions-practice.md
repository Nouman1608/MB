## Questions

**1.** Define the term "quality". **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by "quality assurance". **[2]**

**3.** Evenlode Furniture makes 2 400 chairs a day. An inspector checks a sample of 150 chairs and finds 6 with faulty joints. Each faulty chair costs 18 to repair.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of faulty chairs in the sample. **[1]**
**(b)** Estimate how many faulty chairs are made in a day. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the daily cost of repairing them. **[1]**

**4.** Lamorna Clinic is a private health clinic. Explain **one** reason why quality is important to a service business such as this. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** difference between quality control and quality assurance. **[3]**

**6.** Hartsfell Foods wants to introduce quality assurance in its sauce factory. Outline **two** ways in which it could do this. **[4]**

**7.** Dunmarrow Textiles makes 5 000 shirts a week. At present about 4% are faulty. Each faulty shirt costs 4.00 to rework. The owner is considering a quality assurance system that she expects to cut the faulty rate to 1.5%.
**(a)** Calculate the weekly cost of reworking faulty shirts at present. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the weekly saving if the faulty rate falls to 1.5%. **[3]**
**(c)** Justify whether Dunmarrow Textiles should introduce the system. **[4]**

**8.** Oakrill Cafe plans to open a coffee kiosk. Explain **one** location factor the owner should consider. **[3]**

**9.** Zeldon Electronics is choosing between Country A and Country B for a factory making 50 000 speakers a year. Costs per speaker: in Country A labour is 3.40, parts 8.10 and shipping 1.10, with no import tariff. In Country B labour is 1.90, parts 8.10 and shipping 2.30, and the importing market charges a tariff of 6% of labour plus parts.
**(a)** Calculate the cost of one speaker made in Country B, including the tariff. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate how much more or less it would cost to make 50 000 speakers a year in Country B rather than Country A. **[3]**
**(c)** Identify **two** other factors Zeldon Electronics should consider when choosing a country. **[2]**

**10.** Gorsebank Toys wants to build a factory near a residential area. Explain how legal controls might affect its location decision. **[4]**

**11.** Gorsebank Toys later considers relocating its warehouse.
**(a)** Outline **two** reasons why a business might relocate. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** risk of relocating. **[3]**

**12.** Vennor Printing employs 9 staff, with total wages of 270 000 a year at either site. It is choosing between two premises for a new print shop.

| | Site P (town centre) | Site Q (industrial estate) |
|---|---|---|
| Rent per month | 4 800 | 2 900 |
| Transport and delivery costs per year | 16 000 | 31 000 |
| Extra walk-in orders (contribution per year) | 12 000 | none |

Site P has limited parking. Site Q has good road links and room to expand.
**(a)** Calculate the total annual cost (rent, wages and transport) of each site. **[4]**
**(b)** Recommend and justify which site Vennor Printing should choose. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Quality is how well a product or service [1] meets the needs and expectations of the customer [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for linking quality to what the customer needs; "good" or "expensive" alone does not define it.

**2.** Quality assurance is organising the whole business so that faults are prevented [1] at every stage of production or service, rather than being found at the end [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Prevent" and "every stage" are the two ideas to include. A definition that only says "checking quality" describes control rather than assurance.

**3. (a)** 6 ÷ 150 × 100 = **4%** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** 4% of 2 400 = **96 chairs** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** 96 × 18 = **1 728** a day [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each step. Part (b) rests on the assumption that the sample represents the whole day, which is worth stating if the question allows.

**4.** Reason, for example: patients who receive poor care or find mistakes in their treatment will not return [1]. This reduces the clinic's sales revenue [1] and may harm its reputation, so fewer new patients are attracted [1]. (Also credit: legal claims, or staff morale.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the reason to a clinic. A general statement that quality "keeps customers" gains less than one explaining what happens to this clinic's patients.

**5.** Quality control finds faults, by checking items during or after production [1], whereas quality assurance aims to prevent faults, by setting standards and methods across all stages [1]. Therefore control deals with faults already made, and assurance reduces faults being made at all [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A difference needs both sides. Writing only what control does, with nothing on assurance, leaves the contrast incomplete.

**6.** Any two methods, each with how it helps (1 + 1 each). For example: introduce written quality standards for every stage [1], so every worker knows what is acceptable and faults are avoided [1]. Check and approve suppliers [1], so ingredients arrive at the required standard before production starts [1]. (Also credit: training workers, planned machine maintenance, workers checking their own work, using customer feedback.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" means setting out the main points, but a method plus its purpose usually works better than a bare list.

**7. (a)** 4% of 5 000 = 200 faulty shirts [1]; 200 × 4.00 = **800** per week [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 1.5% of 5 000 = 75 faulty shirts [1]; 75 × 4.00 = 300 [1]; saving = 800 − 300 = **500** per week [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Introduce it: the saving of 500 a week is about 26 000 over a year, if the rate falls as expected [1]. Fewer faulty shirts also mean fewer returns and a better reputation [1]. However, the system has set-up and training costs not given, and the 1.5% is only an expectation [1]. Judgement: it should go ahead if the set-up cost is recovered within a reasonable time, because it prevents repeated waste [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A justified decision uses the figures you have calculated and also names what is unknown. A one-sided "yes, it saves money" gains less than an answer that weighs cost against saving.

**8.** Factor, for example: closeness to customers or footfall [1]. A kiosk relies on passing trade, so a site near offices or a station gives more potential customers [1]. This raises sales but rent may be higher there, so the owner should compare extra sales with the extra rent [1]. (Also credit: competitors, visibility, access, rent.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The factor must be linked to a kiosk. Naming "rent" alone, without saying why it matters here, stays at the first mark.

**9. (a)** Tariff = 6% × (1.90 + 8.10) = 6% × 10.00 = 0.60 [1]; sum of labour, parts and shipping = 1.90 + 8.10 + 2.30 = 12.30 [1]; total = 12.30 + 0.60 = **12.90** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Country A: 3.40 + 8.10 + 1.10 = 12.60 [1]; difference = 12.90 − 12.60 = 0.30 more in Country B [1]; × 50 000 = **15 000 more per year** in Country B [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Any two from: political stability, skills of the workforce, exchange rates, size of the market, infrastructure, government incentives, culture and language, legal controls [1] [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Where a tariff is charged on only part of the cost, work out that base first. Giving the unit difference but not the annual total leaves the question unanswered.

**10.** Governments can limit how land is used [1], so building a factory in a residential area may be refused or need permission [1]. Environmental rules on noise or waste may add costs or delay [1]. Therefore Gorsebank Toys may have to choose a different site, such as an industrial area, even if the cost of land elsewhere is higher [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain the effect on the decision, not just that laws exist. Describing the control generally is enough; guessing at the name of a specific law adds no credit.

**11. (a)** Any two, for example: premises have become too small [1]; rent or running costs have risen (or the lease is ending, or customers and suppliers have moved) [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Risk, for example: production or deliveries are disrupted during the move [1], causing late orders and lost sales [1], which may harm customer relationships if the delay is long [1]. (Also credit: staff leaving, customers unable to find the new site, moving costs.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Move beyond naming the risk. "Staff may leave" gains one mark; adding why that harms the business gains the rest.

**12. (a)** Site P: rent = 4 800 × 12 = 57 600 [1]; total = 57 600 + 270 000 + 16 000 = **343 600** [1]
Site Q: rent = 2 900 × 12 = 34 800 [1]; total = 34 800 + 270 000 + 31 000 = **335 800** [1] **[4]**
**(b)** Recommendation, for example Site P [1]. Site Q costs 7 800 a year less (343 600 − 335 800) [1], but Site P brings 12 000 a year of extra contribution from walk-in orders, so its effective cost is 331 600, which is 4 200 less than Site Q [1]. Walk-in trade also raises the visibility of the business [1]. Against this, Site P has limited parking, which could put some customers off, and Site Q has good road links and room to expand [1]. Judgement: Site P is better in the short term, but if the business expects to grow quickly, Q's room to expand could outweigh the 4 200 difference [1]. (Credit a reasoned recommendation of Site Q if it uses the data and weighs Site P.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For "recommend and justify", a clear choice supported by the figures and a comparison with the rejected site gains more than a list of site features.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining quality as a high price or luxury instead of meeting customer needs.
- Describing quality control when asked about quality assurance, or the other way round.
- Writing "it improves quality" as a benefit without saying what the business gains.
- Giving a location factor without linking it to the type of business in the question.
- Choosing the cheapest option without weighing customers, access or growth.
- Missing the base of a percentage, such as applying a tariff to the wrong cost.
- Stopping at the unit difference when the question asks for an annual figure.
- Naming a law you are unsure of, instead of describing how legal controls affect a site.
- Recommending a site with no reason drawn from the case.
