This guide covers **3.1.4 Competitive and concentrated markets** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, examined on Paper 1. At AS this sub-topic is deliberately non-diagrammatic: the specification states that students are not required to draw or use the traditional "theory of the firm" diagrams. That changes how you should answer, and this guide shows how to analyse market power with only a demand curve and a long-run average cost curve. All firms and figures are invented.

The shared A-level pages on [market structures and the objectives of firms](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-market-structures-and-the-objectives-of-firms/) and on [monopoly, monopoly power and price discrimination](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-monopoly-monopoly-power-and-price-discrimination/) go well beyond the AS content, so use them selectively. Afterwards, condense with the [3.1.4 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-competitive-and-concentrated-markets-revision-notes/) and try the [3.1.4 practice questions](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-competitive-and-concentrated-markets-practice/).

Detailed knowledge of different objectives is not required, and the formal model of perfect competition and its diagrams is not required either. What is required is the ability to explain how structure and objectives affect behaviour and performance in real markets.

## 3.1.4.1 Telling market structures apart

| Feature | Perfect competition | Many firms, differentiated products | A few large firms | Pure monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | Very many | Many | Few dominate | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitute |
| Entry | Free | Fairly easy | Barriers | Very high barriers |
| Price-setting power | None (price taker) | Some | Considerable | Greatest |

The three distinguishing factors the specification names are the number of firms, the degree of product differentiation and the ease of entry. Use them explicitly when asked to classify a market from data: "the four largest firms hold most of the market, products are branded, and new entrants need large sums to build networks, so this market is concentrated."

## 3.1.4.2 Objectives shape behaviour

Profit is an important objective of most firms, but not the only one.

| Objective | Likely behaviour |
|---|---|
| Profit | Set prices and output to make the gap between revenue and cost as large as possible; control costs |
| Survival | In a recession or after entry, accept low or zero profit; cut prices to keep cash coming in |
| Growth | Reinvest profit, open new sites, take over rivals; may accept lower short-run profit |
| Market share | Low prices or heavy advertising to win customers from rivals, even at a loss on some lines |

The exam point is the last column: if a question says a firm wants to raise its market share, explain why it might cut prices even though that lowers its profit in the short run.

## 3.1.4.3 Competitive markets

The main characteristics of a perfectly competitive market:

- very many buyers and sellers, each too small to affect the price;
- identical (homogeneous) products;
- perfect information about prices and products;
- no barriers to entry or exit.

Because no firm can affect the price, the price is set by the interaction of market demand and market supply, and each firm is a **price taker**.

**Why profits are likely to be lower than in a market dominated by a few large firms.** If firms in a competitive market earn high profits, new firms enter, market supply shifts right and the price falls until high profits disappear. Customers who face a higher price from one seller simply buy the identical product from another. Where a few large firms dominate and barriers to entry protect them, high profits are not competed away, and each firm has some power to keep its price above its costs.

## 3.1.4.4 Monopoly and monopoly power

A **pure monopoly** is a single seller with the whole market. Few real examples exist. **Monopoly power** is the ability of a firm to set its price above the competitive level without losing all its customers, and many firms have some. The specification lists four influences:

- **barriers to entry**, such as economies of scale, patents, control of supplies or high set-up costs;
- **the number of competitors**: fewer rivals means more power;
- **advertising**, which builds brand loyalty and makes demand less price elastic;
- **the degree of product differentiation**: the more a product seems different, the fewer close substitutes it has.

### Worked example: concentration ratios

Market shares in a country's mobile phone network market:

| Firm | A | B | C | D | E | All others |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share (%) | 31 | 24 | 17 | 9 | 7 | 12 |

```
Three-firm concentration ratio (CR3) = 31 + 24 + 17 = 72%
Five-firm concentration ratio (CR5) = 72 + 9 + 7 = 88%
```

Interpretation: the three largest firms supply nearly three-quarters of the market, so it is highly concentrated. A concentration ratio does not show how the shares are split among the top firms, nor whether new firms could easily enter, so it is evidence of potential market power, not proof of it. If the data give sales instead of shares, divide each top firm's sales by total market sales first.

### Using a demand curve, not the theory-of-firm diagram

The specification expects you to show with a **demand curve** that if a monopolist raises price above the competitive level, output falls.

```
Price
  |\
6 | \   <- monopoly price: 6, quantity 700
4 |  \  <- competitive price: 4, quantity 1,100
  |   \
  +----------------- Quantity
```

Moving up the market demand curve from a price of 4 to 6, the quantity bought falls from 1,100 to 700. Consumers who valued the good at between 4 and 6 no longer buy it, even though it could be produced for 4. Fewer resources go into the good than consumers would want at the competitive price, which is a **misallocation of resources**. Higher profits for the monopolist come at consumers' expense, and without rivals the firm may face little pressure to keep costs down, so it may also be inefficient.

### Using a long-run average cost curve to show benefits

The specification also expects you to use an **LRAC curve** to show benefits from economies of scale. Suppose one large firm produces 100,000 units at an average cost of 3, while each of 20 small competitive firms produces 5,000 units at an average cost of 5. Even with a profit margin of 1 per unit, the monopolist could sell at 4, below the small firms' cost. The large firm sits further down its LRAC curve. Add the second potential benefit: high profits can fund research, so monopoly may bring more invention and innovation.

## 3.1.4.5 The competitive market process

Competition is not only about price. Firms competing for customers also strive to:

- improve products (new features, better quality, wider choice);
- reduce costs, so they can cut prices or earn more;
- improve the service they provide (delivery, after-sales care, opening hours).

Many large firms compete vigorously with each other in these ways, so a high concentration ratio does not always mean poor outcomes for consumers. The specification's balancing point is that monopoly power **may** lead to consumers being exploited. Your evaluation should decide which effect is stronger in the market in the question.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: assess whether a high concentration ratio in a supermarket market is bad for consumers.*

> A CR4 of 70% suggests the largest supermarkets have monopoly power, and the basic model predicts higher prices and lower output than in a competitive market. However, the large chains compete hard on price, product range and online delivery, and their purchasing economies of scale lower their average costs. Whether consumers lose depends mainly on barriers to entry: if discount chains can still open new stores, the threat of entry limits how far the large firms can raise prices.

Why it works: it uses the ratio as evidence, applies the basic model, adds two benefits from the specification and ends with a condition that decides the judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the A-level theory-of-firm diagrams in an AS answer; they are not required and use valuable time.
- Treating monopoly power as only applying to a pure monopoly.
- Adding the wrong number of firms in a concentration ratio, or using sales without converting to shares.
- Stating that monopoly is always bad: the specification names economies of scale and innovation as potential benefits.
- Listing perfect competition's characteristics without explaining why they keep profits low.
- Ignoring the firm's objective given in the data when explaining its pricing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to draw monopoly diagrams for AQA AS Economics?**
No. The AS specification says a formal diagrammatic analysis of monopoly is not required. You should be able to use a demand curve to show output falling when price rises above the competitive level, and an LRAC curve to show economies of scale.

**What does a five-firm concentration ratio of 88% mean?**
The five largest firms together have 88% of the market's sales, so the market is highly concentrated.
