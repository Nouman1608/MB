These notes condense **4.2.2.7 Properties of metals and alloys** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification
(for teaching from September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. For full explanations, see the
[properties of metals and alloys study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-metals-and-alloys/);
then practise with the
[properties of metals and alloys practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-metals-and-alloys-practice/).

## Core facts

- Metals have **giant structures** of atoms with **strong metallic bonding**.
- So **most metals have high melting points and boiling points**.
- In **pure metals**, the atoms are arranged in **layers**, which allows metals to be **bent and shaped**.
- **Pure metals are too soft** for many uses, so they are mixed with other metals to make **alloys**, which are
  **harder**.
- You must be able to **explain why alloys are harder than pure metals** in terms of **distortion of the layers**
  of atoms in the structure of a pure metal.

## Pure metal versus alloy

| | Pure metal | Alloy |
|---|---|---|
| Atoms | All the same size | Atoms of different sizes |
| Layers | Regular | Distorted |
| Layers sliding | Easy | Difficult |
| Hardness | Soft | Harder |
| Can be bent and shaped? | Easily | Less easily |

```
Pure metal:       Alloy:
 o o o o           o o o o
 o o o o           o o O o     (O is a different-sized atom)
 o o o o           o o o o
```

## Examples of alloys

| Alloy | Made from | Compared with the pure metal |
|---|---|---|
| Brass | Copper and zinc | Harder than copper |
| Bronze | Copper and tin | Harder than copper |
| Steel | Iron and a little carbon | Harder than iron |

Alloys are **mixtures**, not compounds. They are usually mixtures of metals, but steel contains carbon, which is
a non-metal.

## Writing the explanation

**High melting point:** giant metallic structure; strong metallic bonding between positive ions and delocalised
electrons; many bonds; large amount of energy.

**Bending a pure metal:** same-size atoms in regular layers; layers slide over each other; the bonding still holds,
so the metal changes shape without breaking.

**Alloy hardness:** different-sized atoms distort the layers; layers cannot slide easily; harder.

## Calculations with alloys

Percentage by mass gives the mass of each component.

| Quantity | Working |
|---|---|
| 250 g of brass, 70% copper | 70 ÷ 100 × 250 = 175 g copper; zinc = 250 - 175 = 75 g |
| 400 g of bronze, 90% copper | 90 ÷ 100 × 400 = 360 g copper; tin = 400 - 360 = 40 g |
| 2000 kg of steel, 0.5% carbon | 0.5 ÷ 100 × 2000 = 10 kg carbon; iron = 1990 kg |

Method: percentage ÷ 100 × total mass. Check that the parts add up to the total.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alloy | A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements |
| Metallic bonding | Attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Layers | The rows of atoms in a pure metal that can slide over one another |
| Malleable | Able to be bent and shaped without breaking |
| Distortion | Disturbance of the regular pattern of layers |
| Hardness | Resistance to being scratched or dented |

## Exceptions to remember

- "Most" metals have high melting points. Group 1 metals melt at fairly low temperatures, and mercury is liquid
  at room temperature.
- Steel contains carbon. An alloy does not have to contain only metals.
- Some metals, like sodium, are soft even though they have metallic bonding. The exam question will tell you
  what it needs.

## Six-mark answer plan

1. Giant structure with strong metallic bonding gives a high melting point.
2. Pure metal: same-size atoms in layers.
3. Layers slide, so it can be bent and shaped.
4. Alloy: atoms of different sizes.
5. Layers are distorted.
6. Sliding is harder, so the alloy is harder.

## Choosing between a pure metal and an alloy

| Need | Better choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Wire that can be drawn out easily | Pure metal | Layers slide easily |
| Strong beams for a bridge | Alloy (steel) | Harder, resists bending |
| A coin that does not wear quickly | Alloy | Harder than the pure metal |

## Exam traps

- **"Alloys have stronger bonds."** The key idea is distorted layers that cannot slide.
- **"The atoms in a pure metal are different sizes."** They are the same size.
- **"The bonds break when a metal is bent."** The layers slide; the metallic bonding remains.
- **"Metals have ionic bonding."** The bonding is metallic.
- **"All metals have high melting points."** Say "most metals".
- **Forgetting the working in a percentage calculation.** Show percentage ÷ 100 × mass.

## Quick self-test

1. What type of bonding is found in metals?
2. Why do most metals have high melting points?
3. Why can a pure metal be bent and shaped?
4. What is an alloy?
5. Why are pure metals often not used in construction?
6. Explain why an alloy is harder than a pure metal.
7. Name an alloy of copper.
8. Name an element, other than a metal, that is found in steel.
9. Calculate the mass of copper in 500 g of bronze that is 90% copper.
10. Name a metal with a low melting point.

### Answers

1. Metallic bonding.
2. There is a giant structure with many strong metallic bonds, so a large amount of energy is needed.
3. The atoms are in layers that can slide over each other.
4. A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements.
5. They are too soft.
6. Atoms of different sizes distort the layers, so the layers cannot slide over each other so easily.
7. Brass or bronze.
8. Carbon.
9. 90 ÷ 100 × 500 = 450 g.
10. Sodium (a Group 1 metal), or mercury.
