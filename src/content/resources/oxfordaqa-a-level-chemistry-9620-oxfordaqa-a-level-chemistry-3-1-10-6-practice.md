These questions cover **3.1.10.6 Buffer action** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification: explaining acidic and basic buffers, applications, and calculating the pH of acidic buffers. This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3. Give pH to 2 decimal places. Ka values: methanoic acid 1.80 × 10⁻⁴, ethanoic acid 1.74 × 10⁻⁵, benzoic acid 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

## Section A

**1.** State what a basic buffer contains and give one example of a pair of substances that form one. **[2]**

**2.** 0.100 mol of NaOH is added to 0.100 mol of ethanoic acid. Explain why the resulting solution is not a buffer. **[1]**

**3.** A solution contains equal amounts of propanoic acid (pKa 4.87) and sodium propanoate. State its pH. **[1]**

**4.** 2.50 g of sodium methanoate, HCOONa (Mr = 68.0), is dissolved in 250 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ methanoic acid. Calculate the pH of the buffer. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how a buffer of ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate keeps its pH almost constant when a small amount of sodium hydroxide is added. **[3]**

**6.** Give two different applications of buffer solutions. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** 30.0 cm³ of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is added to 60.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ benzoic acid.

**(a)** Calculate the amounts, in moles, of benzoic acid and benzoate ions in the mixture. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the pH of the mixture. **[1]**

**(c)** 1.00 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is added to the mixture. Calculate the new pH. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the pH if the same HCl were added to 90.0 cm³ of pure water instead, and comment on the comparison with (c). **[1]**

**8.** A technician needs a buffer of pH 4.50 and has 200 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of sodium ethanoate, CH₃COONa (Mr = 82.0), that must be dissolved in it. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why less sodium ethanoate than ethanoic acid (in moles) is needed for this pH. **[1]**

**9.** Methylamine, CH₃NH₂, is a weak base. A buffer is made from methylamine and methylammonium chloride, CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻.

**(a)** Explain why this is a basic buffer. **[1]**

**(b)** Write equations to show how the buffer removes small amounts of added acid and of added alkali. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the pH changes only slightly in each case. **[1]**

**10.** Blood contains carbonic acid, H₂CO₃, and hydrogencarbonate ions, HCO₃⁻: H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.

**(a)** State which type of buffer this is, and why. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how this system limits the change in blood pH when a small amount of acid enters the blood. **[2]**

**11.** Buffer X contains 0.100 mol ethanoic acid and 0.100 mol sodium ethanoate in 1.00 dm³. Buffer Y contains 0.0100 mol of each in 1.00 dm³. 0.00800 mol of solid NaOH is added to each.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of each buffer before and after the addition. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain the difference in behaviour. **[1]**

**12.** A student wants to make a buffer of pH 5.00 by adding 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH to 100 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid. Calculate the volume of NaOH needed. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A weak base and a salt of that weak base [1]; for example ammonia and ammonium chloride (or methylamine and methylammonium chloride) [1]

**2.** All the ethanoic acid has reacted, so there is no weak acid left to remove added OH⁻ (only ethanoate ions remain) [1]

**3.** pH = pKa = **4.87** [1]

**4.** n(HCOO⁻) = 2.50 / 68.0 = 0.0368 mol; n(HCOOH) = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol [1]; [H⁺] = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.0250 / 0.0368 = 1.22 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **3.91** [1]

**5.** OH⁻ reacts with H⁺: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O (or CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O) [1]; the equilibrium CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ shifts to the right, replacing most of the H⁺ [1]; there are large amounts of CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻, so their ratio and the pH change very little [1]

**6.** Controlling the pH of blood [1]; calibrating pH meters [1]. Also accept keeping the pH of foods, drinks or cosmetics stable, or processes using enzymes that need a narrow pH range.

**7. (a)** n(benzoic acid) at start = 0.200 × 0.0600 = 0.0120 mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.250 × 0.0300 = 0.00750 mol [1]; n(HA) left = **0.00450 mol**; n(A⁻) = **0.00750 mol** [1]

**(b)** [H⁺] = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.00450 / 0.00750 = 3.78 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; pH = **4.42** [1]

**(c)** n(HA) = 0.00550 mol; n(A⁻) = 0.00650 mol [1]; [H⁺] = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.00550 / 0.00650 = 5.33 × 10⁻⁵, pH = **4.27** [1]

**(d)** [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻³ / 0.0910 = 0.0110 mol dm⁻³, pH = 1.96: a fall of about 5 units in water compared with 0.15 units in the buffer [1]

**8. (a)** [H⁺] = 10^(−4.50) = 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; ratio n(A⁻) / n(HA) = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ / 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.550 [1]; n(HA) = 0.500 × 0.200 = 0.100 mol, so n(A⁻) = 0.0550 mol [1]; mass = 0.0550 × 82.0 = **4.51 g** [1]

**(b)** pH 4.50 is below the pKa (4.76), so [H⁺] must be larger than Ka, which needs [HA] / [A⁻] greater than 1 [1]

**9. (a)** It contains a weak base (methylamine) and a salt of that weak base (methylammonium chloride) [1]

**(b)** Acid: **CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺** [1]; alkali: **CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → CH₃NH₂ + H₂O** [1]

**(c)** Both CH₃NH₂ and CH₃NH₃⁺ are present in large amounts compared with the acid or alkali added, so their ratio barely changes [1]

**10. (a)** Acidic: a weak acid (H₂CO₃) with its conjugate base / salt anion (HCO₃⁻) [1]

**(b)** The added H⁺ reacts with HCO₃⁻: H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃, so the equilibrium shifts to the left [1]; HCO₃⁻ and H₂CO₃ are both present in large enough amounts that their ratio, and the pH, change only slightly [1]

**11. (a)** Both start at pH = pKa = **4.76** [1]; X: n(HA) = 0.0920, n(A⁻) = 0.1080, [H⁺] = 1.48 × 10⁻⁵, pH = **4.83** [1]; Y: n(HA) = 0.0020, n(A⁻) = 0.0180 [1]; [H⁺] = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶, pH = **5.71** [1]

**(b)** In Y the NaOH uses up most of the ethanoic acid, so the ratio changes greatly; buffers only resist additions that are small compared with the amounts of HA and A⁻ present [1]

**12.** Required ratio n(A⁻) / n(HA) = Ka / [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ / 1.00 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.74 [1]; with x mol NaOH, x / (0.0100 − x) = 1.74, so x = 6.35 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; volume = 6.35 × 10⁻³ / 0.100 = 0.0635 dm³ = **63.5 cm³** [1]

*Examiner insight:* Total acid is 0.0100 mol; each mole of NaOH turns one mole of HA into A⁻, so both amounts change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting that added NaOH both removes HA and creates A⁻.
- Using the weak-acid square-root method for a mixture that contains the salt.
- Explaining buffer action without naming the species present in large amounts.
- Writing a basic buffer explanation with the acidic buffer equations.
