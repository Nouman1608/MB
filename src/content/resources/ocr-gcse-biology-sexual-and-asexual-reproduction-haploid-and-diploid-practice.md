## Questions

**1.** Human skin cells have 46 chromosomes.
**(a)** How many chromosomes does one human egg cell have? **[1]**
**(b)** State the number of chromosomes in a human zygote. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the term for a cell that contains chromosomes in pairs. **[1]**

**2.** The table shows the number of chromosomes in body cells of four mammals.

| Mammal | Chromosomes in a body cell | Chromosomes in a sperm cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cat | 38 | |
| Mouse | 40 | |
| Chimpanzee | 48 | |
| Horse | 64 | |

**(a)** Complete the last column. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the number in a body cell of a mammal is always an even number. **[2]**

**3.** Give two differences between the offspring produced by asexual reproduction and the offspring produced by sexual reproduction. **[2]**

**4.** A bacterium divides by binary fission every 15 minutes. Assume that no cells die.
**(a)** Calculate the number of bacteria after 2 hours from a single cell. **[2]**
**(b)** A mouse produces a litter of several young. Suggest why a mouse cannot increase its numbers as fast as the bacterium. **[2]**

**5.** A farmer grows strawberries by planting runners from existing plants. A new fungal disease reaches the farm.
**(a)** Explain why all of the plants may be killed. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest two advantages to the farmer of using runners instead of seeds. **[2]**

**6.** Describe what happens to the number of chromosomes in meiosis, and explain why this is necessary. **[3]**

**7.** Complete the table to compare mitosis and meiosis. **[5]**

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Number of cells made from one cell | | |
| Chromosome number of cells made | | |
| Genetically identical or different | | |
| One use in a human | | |
| Where in a flowering plant | all growing tissues | |

**8.** The table gives information about three species. The data are supplied for this question. Assume each adult breeds at the stated rate for the whole 12 years.

| Species | Young per birth | Births per year |
|---|---|---|
| P | 7 | 4 |
| Q | 1 | 0.33 |
| R | 2 | 1 |

**(a)** Calculate how many young one adult of species P produces in 12 years. **[2]**
**(b)** One adult of species Q has one birth every 3 years. Work out how many young it produces in 12 years, and the ratio of the young produced by P to the young produced by Q. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest one disadvantage to species Q of its low number of young. **[1]**

**9.** A fruit fly has 8 chromosomes in a body cell.
**(a)** State the number in a gamete. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many different combinations of chromosomes are possible in the gametes of one fly, if each pair separates independently. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the probability that a gamete contains the four chromosomes that came from the fly's father. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain what your answer to part (b) shows about the offspring of two flies. **[1]**

**10.** Explain how meiosis and fertilisation together keep the chromosome number of a species the same in every generation, and how meiosis produces genetic variation. **[5]**

**11.** A gardener can grow a variety of daffodil from bulbs that split to form new bulbs, or from seeds produced by flowers.
Evaluate which method the gardener should use (i) to grow a large number of flowers that are all the same colour as the parent, and (ii) to start a wild flower meadow in a region where the climate is getting warmer. **[6]**

**12.** Maize has 20 chromosomes in a leaf cell.
**(a)** State how many chromosomes are in (i) a pollen nucleus and (ii) the zygote formed after fertilisation. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of different combinations of chromosomes possible in the pollen nuclei of one plant, assuming only independent separation of pairs. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the probability that a pollen nucleus has all of its chromosomes from the plant's maternal parent. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a cell with 10 chromosomes can only be a gamete and not a leaf cell. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 23 [1] **[1]**
**(b)** 46 [1] **[1]**
**(c)** Diploid [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare number is enough for a one-mark recall question, but writing "23 pairs" for a gamete shows a pair/set confusion, so say "23 chromosomes".

**2. (a)** Halve each number: cat 19 [1], mouse 20, chimpanzee 24, horse 32 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Chromosomes are in pairs [1], one of each pair from the mother and one from the father, so the total is twice the number of pairs [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For "explain" questions, give the reason (pairs, one from each parent) rather than only restating that the number is even.

**3.** Asexual offspring are genetically identical to the parent, and sexual offspring are genetically different from both parents [1]. Asexual offspring come from one parent, sexual offspring from two parents, or from the fusion of two gametes [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate differences are needed, so do not give two versions of the same point such as "identical" and "the same".

**4. (a)** 2 hours = 120 min, and 120 ÷ 15 = 8 divisions [1]. 2⁸ = **256 bacteria** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** A mouse must find a mate and reproduce sexually, and has only a limited number of young in each litter [1]. A mouse also needs a gestation period before each birth, whereas a bacterium doubles in number on every division [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A calculation worked out in steps lets a slip in counting divisions be seen separately from the doubling.

**5. (a)** Plants grown from runners are genetically identical to the parent [1]. They all share the same weaknesses, so a pathogen that can infect one can infect all of them [1]. There is no variation, so there are no resistant plants that survive and reproduce [1] **[3]**
**(b)** One mark each for two of: one parent is enough; the new plants have the same useful features, such as fruit size, as the parent; the plants grow without waiting for seeds [1] [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A link from "identical" to "same weakness" to "the disease affects all" is what scores. Saying "no variation" on its own does not explain the risk.

**6.** The number is halved [1], from the diploid number to the haploid number in the gametes [1]. This is necessary so that when two gametes fuse in fertilisation the diploid number is restored, and does not double each generation [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Halved" plus a reason scores. A statement of only "makes gametes" does not answer a question about chromosome number.

**7.** Mitosis two cells, meiosis four cells [1]. Mitosis same as the parent cell (diploid), meiosis half (haploid) [1]. Mitosis identical, meiosis different [1]. Mitosis: growth or repair, meiosis: making gametes (sperm and egg) [1]. Meiosis in the anthers and ovules [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In a table, a short precise word such as "diploid" or "haploid" is better than a vague one such as "same".

**8. (a)** Births in 12 years = 4 × 12 = 48 [1]. Young = 48 × 7 = **336** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Births = 12 ÷ 3 = 4, and 4 × 1 = **4 young** [1]. Ratio P : Q = 336 : 4 [1] = **84 : 1** [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Any one: the population increases only slowly, so it takes a long time to recover if it is reduced by disease or hunting, or if numbers fall [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the number of births before multiplying by young per birth, and give a ratio in its simplest form.

**9. (a)** 4 [1] **[1]**
**(b)** There are 4 pairs and 2 choices per pair, so 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 [1] = 2⁴ = **16** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The chance for each pair is 1/2, so the probability is (1/2)⁴ [1] = **1/16 = 0.0625** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** Each parent can produce many different gametes, so offspring from the same parents differ genetically [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A power of 2 with the number of pairs, not the number of chromosomes, is the common route to a correct result.

**10.** Meiosis halves the chromosome number, producing haploid gametes [1]. Fertilisation fuses two haploid gametes [1] so the zygote is diploid, restoring the original number [1]. In meiosis the pairs of chromosomes separate independently, so every gamete gets its own combination of chromosomes from the two parents [1]. Any sperm can fuse with any egg, adding to the variation [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Questions with two demands, such as number and variation, need both parts answered. Give each part its own sentence.

**11.** (i) Bulbs [1]. They are clones, so all the flowers will be the same colour as the parent [1]. Bulbs also avoid the need for pollination [1]. (ii) Seeds [1]. Seeds are produced by sexual reproduction, so they vary [1]. Some seedlings are more likely to survive in a warmer climate and can then reproduce, so the meadow persists [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation should reach a clear choice for each situation, supported by a reason that is linked to the situation.

**12. (a)** (i) 10 [1] (ii) 20 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** There are 10 pairs, so 2¹⁰ [1] = **1024** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** (1/2)¹⁰ [1] = 0.000977 (**9.77 × 10⁻⁴**) [1] **[2]**
**(d)** A leaf cell is diploid and has 20 chromosomes [1]. A cell with 10 is haploid, which is the number in a gamete [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A very small probability is best given in standard form or as a fraction, and 3 significant figures means three digits starting from the first non-zero digit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "46 pairs" or "23 pairs" for gametes instead of 23 chromosomes.
- Calling asexual offspring "the same" without saying genetic information, and calling sexual offspring "different" without a reason.
- Saying meiosis makes two cells or that it keeps the number the same.
- Explaining the risk to a crop of clones without linking to a shared weakness.
- Using the number of chromosomes, rather than the number of pairs, as the power of 2.
- Treating sexual reproduction as simply "better", or asexual reproduction as "worse".
- Not stating that fertilisation restores the diploid number.
- Giving a probability as a decimal that is rounded too early.
- Leaving out the situation in an evaluate question.
