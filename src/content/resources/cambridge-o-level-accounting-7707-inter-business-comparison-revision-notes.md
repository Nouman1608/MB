These notes condense section **6.3 Inter-business comparison** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The syllabus asks for "the factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses" and "the problems of inter-business comparison". The worked comparison of two shops is in the [inter-business comparison study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-inter-business-comparison/); test yourself with the [inter-business comparison practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-inter-business-comparison-practice/). Figures are invented and in dollars.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inter-business comparison | Comparing the ratios of two or more businesses for the same period |
| Benchmark | A figure used as a standard, such as a similar business's ratio or a trade average |
| Accounting policy | A method a business chooses, such as its depreciation method or inventory valuation |
| Like-for-like | Adjusted so that both businesses are measured on the same basis |
| Pricing policy | How a business sets prices, such as low prices for high volume |

## Why compare businesses

A trend over years shows improvement; a comparison with a similar business shows whether the result is good. Users include owners judging their own performance, lenders choosing between applicants and investors choosing where to put money.

## Factors that make two businesses' ratios differ

| Factor | Ratios most affected |
|---|---|
| Type of trade | Margins, inventory turnover |
| Size and bulk buying | Gross profit margin |
| Pricing policy | Margins (down) against revenue and inventory turnover (up) |
| Location | Expenses (rent, wages), selling prices, so margins |
| Owning or renting premises | ROCE (capital employed), profit margin (rent against depreciation) |
| Method of finance | Profit margin (interest); ROCE adds interest back |
| Accounting policies | Profit, asset values, so most ratios |
| Age of non-current assets | ROCE (low book values) |
| Credit policy | Trade receivables and payables days, liquidity |
| Year-end date and season | Inventory, receivables, liquidity ratios |
| Owner's pay | A sole trader's drawings are not an expense; a company's directors' salaries are |

## Problems of inter-business comparison

1. Different accounting policies, so the same trading gives different figures.
2. No two businesses are identical in size, trade, location or product mix.
3. Different year-end dates catch different points in a seasonal year.
4. Outsiders may lack figures such as credit sales or opening inventory.
5. Historic cost records assets bought at different times at different prices.
6. One year may include one-off events; a trend needs several years.
7. Non-financial factors, such as staff skill and reputation, are left out.

## Making a comparison like-for-like

If one business owns premises and the other rents, adjust the owner's figures: add back depreciation on the premises, deduct an equivalent rent, and remove the premises from capital employed. Then recalculate the ratio. The adjusted ratio compares trading, not ownership choices.

## Exam traps

- One ratio never proves that one business is "better".
- Link each factor to a named ratio and a direction of change.
- A low gross profit margin can be deliberate pricing, not poor performance.
- ROCE adds back interest, so the method of finance changes ROCE through capital employed, not through interest.
- "Different accounting policies" needs an example, such as straight line against reducing balance depreciation.
- Use comparative language for "compare": "higher than", "both", "whereas".

## Self-test

1. Why compare a business with another business, not only with its own previous year?
2. Shop A has a gross profit margin of 22% and shop B, in the same trade, 35%. Give two possible reasons.
3. How does owning premises instead of renting affect ROCE?
4. Business X depreciates its equipment at 25% reducing balance and Business Y at 10% straight line. Why does this make comparing their profits difficult?
5. Why might different year-end dates distort a comparison of current ratios?
6. State two problems of inter-business comparison other than accounting policies.
7. A sole trader takes $30,000 of drawings; a similar company pays its director a $30,000 salary. How does this affect a comparison of profit margins?
8. Explain why a bakery's rate of inventory turnover is higher than a furniture shop's.
9. Firm X made a profit of $18,000 on revenue of $150,000; Firm Y made $27,000 on $300,000. Which has the higher profit margin?
10. Why might a business with old, almost fully depreciated machinery have a high ROCE?
11. Why is limited information a problem for an outsider comparing two businesses?
12. Give one way to make a comparison between two businesses more reliable.

### Answers

1. The previous year shows only whether the business improved; a similar business shows whether the result is good for that type of business.
2. Any two: B charges higher prices; A follows a low-price, high-volume policy; B buys more cheaply in bulk; A's closing inventory is valued lower; different product mix.
3. Owning adds the premises to capital employed, which lowers ROCE; it also replaces rent with depreciation, which changes profit.
4. Different depreciation charges give different profits for identical trading, so the difference in profit may come from policy, not performance.
5. Inventory, receivables and cash vary through the year, so each statement of financial position may catch a different point in the season.
6. Any two: businesses differ in size, trade or location; different year ends; limited information; historic cost; one year only; non-financial factors ignored.
7. The company's salary is an expense, lowering its profit margin; the sole trader's drawings are not, so the sole trader's profit margin looks higher for the same trading.
8. A bakery sells perishable goods within a day or two, so its inventory is replaced many times a year; furniture is held for weeks or months.
9. X: 18,000 / 150,000 = **12.00%**; Y: 27,000 / 300,000 = **9.00%**. X is higher.
10. Years of depreciation have reduced the machinery's book value, which keeps capital employed low, so the same profit gives a higher percentage return.
11. Published or available statements may not show credit sales, credit purchases or opening inventory, so some ratios cannot be calculated accurately.
12. Any one: compare businesses of similar size and trade; compare several years; adjust for different policies or ownership of premises; use a trade average.
