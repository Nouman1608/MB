This guide covers **3.1.1.3 Electron configuration** in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1 and available to the synoptic Unit 5. It is the most heavily examined part of section 3.1.1, because ionisation energy data let examiners test whether you can argue from evidence rather than recite configurations.

## What the specification asks for

- Electron configurations of atoms and ions up to Z = 36 in terms of shells and sub-shells (orbitals) s, p and d.
- Ionisation energies. You must be able to:
  - define first ionisation energy
  - write equations for first and successive ionisation energies
  - explain how first and successive ionisation energies in Period 3 (Na–Ar) and in Group 2 (Be–Ba) give evidence for electron configuration in sub-shells and in shells.

## Shells, sub-shells and orbitals

| Shell (n) | Sub-shells | Orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1s | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 | 8 |
| 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 | 18 |
| 4 | 4s, 4p (and 4d, 4f, not needed to Z = 36) | 1 + 3 | 8 within Z = 36 |

An **orbital** is a region that can hold up to two electrons, with opposite spins. An s sub-shell has one orbital, a p sub-shell three and a d sub-shell five.

Three rules build every configuration:

1. Sub-shells fill in order of increasing energy: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p.
2. Each orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.
3. Within a sub-shell, electrons occupy orbitals singly before pairing, because paired electrons in one orbital repel.

### Writing configurations

Count electrons, then fill. Germanium (Z = 32): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p². You may shorten the core using the previous noble gas: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p². Write 3d before 4s when giving the full configuration, as is conventional, but remember 4s fills first.

Two atoms break the simple filling pattern and must be learned:

- Chromium (Z = 24): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹
- Copper (Z = 29): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹

### Configurations of ions

- **Negative ions**: add electrons to the next available orbitals. Br⁻ (36 electrons) is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶, the same as krypton.
- **Positive ions of s- and p-block elements**: remove electrons from the highest-energy sub-shell. Al³⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.
- **Positive ions of d-block elements**: once 3d is occupied, the **4s electrons are removed before the 3d electrons**.

### Worked example 1: Fe³⁺

**Question.** Give the electron configuration of Fe³⁺ (Fe has Z = 26).

**Method.**

1. Fe atom: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
2. Remove the two 4s electrons first: Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶.
3. Remove one 3d electron: Fe³⁺ is **[Ar] 3d⁵**, or 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵.

The common wrong answer, [Ar] 3d³ 4s², comes from removing 3d electrons first.

### Blocks of the Periodic Table

The sub-shell that receives the last electron in the filling order names the block: Groups 1 and 2 (with helium) are the s block, Groups 13 to 18 (except helium) the p block, and scandium to zinc in Period 4 the d block. This lets you write any configuration up to Z = 36 by reading across the Periodic Table.

## Ionisation energies

**First ionisation energy** is the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

Equations always use gaseous species and remove one electron at a time:

- First, for calcium: Ca(g) → Ca⁺(g) + e⁻
- Second: Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻
- Third: Ca²⁺(g) → Ca³⁺(g) + e⁻

The value depends on three factors:

- **nuclear charge**: more protons attract the outer electron more strongly
- **distance** of the outer electron from the nucleus: the further away, the weaker the attraction
- **shielding** by inner electrons, which reduces the attraction felt by the outer electron.

### Evidence for shells: Group 2 and successive ionisation energies

First ionisation energy falls down Group 2 (approximate values in kJ mol⁻¹: Be 900, Mg 738, Ca 590, Sr 550, Ba 503). Nuclear charge increases, but each element has an extra filled shell, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded. The decrease shows that electrons occupy shells at increasing distance from the nucleus.

Successive ionisation energies of one element always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. A very large jump shows that the next electron comes from a shell closer to the nucleus. Counting the electrons removed before the first big jump gives the number of outer-shell electrons, and so the group.

### Evidence for sub-shells: Period 3

Across Period 3 the general trend is an increase (Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 787, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521, all approximate, in kJ mol⁻¹), because nuclear charge increases while the outer electrons are in the same shell with similar shielding. Two dips are the evidence for sub-shells:

- **Mg to Al**: aluminium's outer electron is in 3p, which is higher in energy than 3s and slightly shielded by the 3s electrons, so it is easier to remove.
- **P to S**: sulfur has a pair of electrons in one 3p orbital; repulsion between the paired electrons makes one of them easier to remove.

### Worked example 2: second ionisation energies across Period 3

**Question.** Approximate second ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹) are Na 4562, Mg 1451, Al 1817, Si 1577. Explain the pattern.

**Model answer, annotated.**

- "Na⁺ has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, so its second electron comes from the 2p sub-shell, in the second shell, much closer to the nucleus and less shielded ✔. This is why sodium's value is by far the highest."
- "Mg⁺ is [Ne] 3s¹ and loses a 3s electron, so the value is much lower ✔."
- "Al⁺ is [Ne] 3s², and loses a 3s electron with a greater nuclear charge than magnesium, so its value is higher than magnesium's ✔."
- "Si⁺ is [Ne] 3s² 3p¹ and loses a 3p electron, which is higher in energy than 3s, so its value is lower than aluminium's ✔."

**Why it scores.** Each value is explained by first writing the configuration of the **1+ ion**, since that is the species losing the electron. Students who use the atom's configuration get the pattern wrong.

## Common mistakes

- Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming transition metal ions.
- Writing chromium as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² or copper as [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s².
- Leaving out state symbols, or showing a gaseous ion forming from a solid, in ionisation energy equations.
- Writing a second ionisation energy as M(g) → M²⁺(g) + 2e⁻. Each ionisation energy removes one electron.
- Defining first ionisation energy without "one mole", "gaseous" or "1+ ions".
- Explaining the Al dip by "a full 3s sub-shell is stable". Say instead that the 3p electron is higher in energy and is shielded by 3s.
- Explaining the S dip by "a half-filled 3p sub-shell is stable". The reason is repulsion between the paired 3p electrons.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Define**: learn the first ionisation energy definition word for word.
- **Write an equation**: state symbols on every species; one electron per step.
- **Deduce**: from successive data, count electrons before the first large jump and name the group.
- **Explain**: use nuclear charge, distance and shielding, and name the sub-shell or shell the electron leaves.
- **Suggest**: apply the same reasoning to an element or ion you have not studied, such as predicting where the next large jump would occur.
