## Using the solubility rules as a tool (2.34)

Questions usually ask you to use the rules: will a precipitate form, which reactants suit, which method? Work through the ions in a fixed order.

1. Split the compound into its cation and anion.
2. If either ion is sodium, potassium, ammonium or nitrate, the compound is soluble. Stop.
3. Otherwise check the anion's rule and its exceptions: chlorides (silver and lead(II) insoluble), sulfates (barium, calcium and lead(II) insoluble), carbonates (insoluble), hydroxides (insoluble apart from sodium, potassium and the slightly soluble calcium).

**Worked example: will a precipitate form?** Four pairs of solutions are mixed. For each, swap partners and test both new combinations.

| Solutions mixed | New combinations | Precipitate? |
|---|---|---|
| magnesium chloride + sodium carbonate | magnesium carbonate, sodium chloride | Yes: magnesium carbonate |
| barium nitrate + potassium chloride | barium chloride, potassium nitrate | No: both soluble |
| calcium chloride + sodium sulfate | calcium sulfate, sodium chloride | Yes: calcium sulfate |
| zinc sulfate + potassium hydroxide | zinc hydroxide, potassium sulfate | Yes: zinc hydroxide |

Barium chloride is soluble; only barium **sulfate** is an exception.

## Proton transfer in equations (2.35–2.36)

An H⁺ ion is a proton, so an acid is a **proton donor** and a base is a **proton acceptor**. To identify them in an equation, follow the H⁺: the species that loses it is the acid, and the species that gains it is the base.

For an ionic equation, remove the spectator ions that appear unchanged on both sides:

```
MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
MgO(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂O(l)       chloride ions are spectators
```

The oxide ion accepts two protons, so magnesium oxide is the base. Ammonia accepts a proton without forming water:

```
NH₃(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → NH₄NO₃(aq)
NH₃(aq) + H⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq)
```

**A metal is not a base.** When zinc reacts with an acid, the zinc atoms give electrons to hydrogen ions, which become hydrogen gas. No proton is accepted, so this is not neutralisation:

```
Zn(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)
```

## Writing salt formulae and equations (2.37)

The salt takes its cation from the base, metal or carbonate and its anion from the acid. Balance charges first, then the equation.

| Ion from acid | Charge | Example salt | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| chloride, Cl⁻ | 1− | calcium chloride | CaCl₂ |
| nitrate, NO₃⁻ | 1− | magnesium nitrate | Mg(NO₃)₂ |
| sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | 2− | ammonium sulfate | (NH₄)₂SO₄ |
| sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | 2− | iron(III) sulfate | Fe₂(SO₄)₃ |

The number of acid molecules needed equals the number of anions in the salt formula. Making magnesium nitrate needs two nitric acid molecules, because Mg(NO₃)₂ contains two nitrate ions:

```
MgCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Ca(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
```

Remember the exclusion: the specification does not ask for nitric acid reacting with metals, so never use that reaction to make a nitrate. Use the metal oxide, hydroxide or carbonate instead. Metals below hydrogen in the reactivity series, such as copper, do not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, so copper salts are made from copper(II) oxide or copper(II) carbonate.

## Why every step is there

Method questions reward each step that has a purpose. Know the purpose and you will not leave the step out.

| Step | Reason | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| Warm the acid (excess-solid method) | Speeds up the reaction | Solid dissolves slowly; you may stop before the acid is used up |
| Add the solid until some remains | Shows all the acid has reacted | Leftover acid stays in the salt solution and contaminates the crystals |
| Filter off the excess solid | Removes unreacted insoluble reactant | Crystals are mixed with solid reactant |
| Heat to the point of crystallisation, not to dryness | Concentrates the solution so crystals form on cooling | Hydrated salts lose their water of crystallisation; impurities dry onto the solid |
| Repeat the titration without indicator (2.40C) | Gives the exact volumes with nothing added | Indicator contaminates the salt |
| Wash the precipitate with distilled water (2.41C) | Removes the soluble by-product | The salt contains, for example, sodium nitrate |
| Dry between filter papers or in a warm oven | Removes surface water | The mass and purity are wrong |

## Worked example 1: a soluble salt from an insoluble carbonate

*Describe how to prepare pure, dry crystals of magnesium sulfate from magnesium carbonate and dilute sulfuric acid.* **[6]**

Annotated model answer:

- Pour dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and warm it gently. *(reaction speed)*
- Add magnesium carbonate a spatula at a time, stirring, until fizzing stops and some solid is left at the bottom. *(excess shows all acid has reacted)*
- Filter the mixture and keep the filtrate, which is magnesium sulfate solution. *(removes excess carbonate)*
- Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form on a glass rod dipped in and removed. *(saturated solution)*
- Leave the solution to cool so crystals form, then filter them off. *(crystallisation)*
- Dry the crystals between sheets of filter paper. *(dry)*

```
MgCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
```

**Linked calculation.** A student uses 4.20 g of magnesium carbonate, all of which reacts. (Ar: Mg = 24, C = 12, O = 16, S = 32; one mole of gas occupies 24 dm³ at rtp.)

```
Mr MgCO₃ = 24 + 12 + 48 = 84
moles MgCO₃ = 4.20 ÷ 84 = 0.0500 mol
mole ratio MgCO₃ : MgSO₄ : CO₂ = 1 : 1 : 1
mass of MgSO₄ = 0.0500 × 120 = 6.00 g
volume of CO₂ = 0.0500 × 24 = 1.20 dm³
```

## Worked example 2: an insoluble salt (Paper 2 only)

*Suggest two solutions that could be mixed to make silver chloride, and calculate the percentage yield.* 1.70 g of silver nitrate is dissolved and excess sodium chloride solution is added. The washed, dried precipitate has a mass of 1.29 g. (Ar: Ag = 108, N = 14, O = 16, Cl = 35.5)

Choice of reactants: a soluble silver compound (silver nitrate) and a soluble chloride (sodium chloride solution or dilute hydrochloric acid).

```
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

Mr AgNO₃ = 108 + 14 + 48 = 170
moles AgNO₃ = 1.70 ÷ 170 = 0.0100 mol
theoretical mass AgCl = 0.0100 × 143.5 = 1.435 g
percentage yield = 1.29 ÷ 1.435 × 100 = 89.9%
```

Some precipitate is lost on the filter paper, so the yield is below 100%. Sodium chloride is added in excess so all the silver ions precipitate.

## Acid plus alkali: why titration (2.40C, Paper 2 only)

Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are made by titration because every compound of these ions is soluble. There is no insoluble base to add in excess and filter off, so you must measure the exact volumes that react.

Ammonium sulfate is a useful example because it shows ammonia acting as an alkali:

```
2NH₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq)
```

Titrate ammonia solution with dilute sulfuric acid using methyl orange, repeat with the same volumes and no indicator, then partly evaporate, crystallise, filter and dry.

## The two named practicals (2.42 and 2.43C)

For the **copper(II) sulfate** practical (2.42), examiners often ask for observations. Black copper(II) oxide disappears as it reacts; the colourless acid turns blue; once the acid is used up, black solid stays undissolved. The crystals are blue because they are hydrated.

For the **lead(II) sulfate** practical (2.43C, Paper 2 only), the key observation is a white precipitate as soon as the solutions mix. Lead compounds are toxic, so wear gloves and eye protection and dispose of lead waste safely. A warm oven is fine for drying because lead(II) sulfate has no water of crystallisation.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a metal a base, or describing the metal and acid reaction as neutralisation.
- Writing MgNO₃ or NH₄SO₄: charges must balance.
- Choosing an insoluble reactant for a precipitation, such as copper(II) oxide to make copper(II) carbonate.
- Keeping the residue in the excess-solid method, or the filtrate in a precipitation.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State, give, name:** one fact, such as a product or reagent.
- **Describe:** steps in order, with reagents and apparatus named.
- **Explain:** a reason linked to the chemistry, such as "excess solid ensures all the acid reacts".
- **Suggest:** apply the rules to an unfamiliar salt; any sensible soluble reactant is credited.
- **Calculate:** show the moles step, not just the final number.
