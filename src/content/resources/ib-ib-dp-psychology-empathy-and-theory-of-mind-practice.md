> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on theory of mind and the development of
> empathy in the human development context (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2027). They are not
> past-paper questions. Question 13 uses the Paper 1 Section C tariff of 15 marks; the rest use practice
> tariffs. The study in Question 8 is invented, with invented figures.

## Section A

**1.** Define "theory of mind". **[2]**

**2.** In the Sally-Anne task, state the answer that shows understanding of false belief, and explain why. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish affective empathy from cognitive empathy. **[2]**

**4.** State what a violation-of-expectation method measures. **[1]**

**5.** Name Hoffman's first two stages of empathy development. **[2]**

**6.** Outline one reason why a three-year-old might fail a verbal false-belief task while partly understanding false belief. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the findings of Callaghan et al. (2005) and Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001) together support the claim that theory of mind follows a universal developmental pattern. **[4]**

**8.** Invented study: a researcher tests 30 three-year-olds and 30 five-year-olds on an unexpected-contents task. Nine of the three-year-olds and 24 of the five-year-olds pass.
(a) Calculate the percentage passing in each age group.
(b) Identify the research design used to study age differences, and give one limitation of it.
(c) Suggest one ethical consideration the researcher should address. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the purpose of the control condition in Warneken and Tomasello (2006). **[3]**

**10.** A two-year-old sees a crying adult and offers her his own teddy bear. Using Hoffman's stages, explain this behaviour. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) cannot show that a lack of theory of mind causes the social difficulties of autism. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how the development of theory of mind contributes to the development of cognitive empathy. **[3]**

**13.** Evaluate research on the development of theory of mind, with reference to the concept of measurement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** The understanding that other people have mental states, such as beliefs, desires and intentions, that can differ from one's own and from reality **[1]**, and that these mental states guide their behaviour **[1]**.

**2.** That Sally will look in her own basket **[1]**, because she did not see the marble being moved and so still believes it is there; the child predicts from Sally's belief rather than from reality **[1]**.

**3.** Affective empathy is feeling an emotion that matches or responds to another person's emotion **[1]**; cognitive empathy is understanding what another person feels and why **[1]**.

**4.** How long an infant looks at an event, used as a measure of surprise when the event breaks what the infant expects **[1]**.

**5.** Global empathy **[1]**; egocentric empathy **[1]**.

**6.** For example, the question places demands on language and memory **[1]**, and young children may answer with the most salient fact, where the object really is, even if they partly grasp the character's belief **[1]**.

**7.** Wellman, Cross and Watson's meta-analysis found a consistent shift from failing to passing between about three and five across many studies and countries **[2]**. Callaghan et al. used the same task in five very different cultures and found that children began passing at a similar age, about five **[1]**. Together, a consistent age trend and similar timing across cultures suggest a universal pattern rather than one produced by a particular upbringing **[1]**.

**8.** (a) Three-year-olds: 9 / 30 = 30% **[1]**; five-year-olds: 24 / 30 = 80% **[1]**.
(b) Cross-sectional design (or quasi-experiment with age as the variable) **[1]**. Limitation: different children are compared, so cohort or individual differences, such as language ability, may explain the difference rather than development **[1]**.
(c) For example, informed consent from parents and the child's own assent **[1]**, with the task kept short and the child reassured that there is no wrong answer, so the child is not distressed **[1]**.

**9.** In the control condition the adult dropped the pen deliberately rather than accidentally **[1]**. Infants helped in the accidental condition but not in the deliberate one **[1]**, which shows that helping depended on understanding the adult's goal rather than simply picking up any dropped object **[1]**.

**10.** The child shows egocentric empathy, typical of the second year **[1]**. He recognises that the adult is distressed and wants to help **[1]**, but offers what would comfort himself, because he cannot yet fully separate her needs from his own **[1]**.

**11.** The study is a quasi-experiment comparing existing groups, so diagnosis could not be manipulated **[1]**. It shows an association between autism and failing the task **[1]**, and not all autistic children failed, so other factors must contribute to social difficulties **[1]**.

**12.** Cognitive empathy requires understanding what another person feels and why **[1]**. Theory of mind allows a child to recognise that another person's feelings come from that person's own beliefs and desires, which may differ from the child's **[1]**. A child who passes false-belief tasks can therefore respond to someone who is upset about something the child knows is untrue, for example comforting a friend who wrongly believes a pet is lost **[1]**.

**13.** Marked with the Paper 1 Section C markbands (15 marks). The top band, 13 to 15 marks, needs the demands of the question addressed; relevant knowledge and understanding fully explained; well-developed critical analysis; links to the concept of measurement included throughout and fully explained; a reasoned, clearly stated conclusion consistent with the arguments; and accurate, precise psychological terminology. The 10 to 12 band has the same features with analysis that lacks development, and the 7 to 9 band has analysis that lacks development and links that are only partly relevant. Content that can be credited:
- **Knowledge:** theory of mind and false belief; unexpected-location and unexpected-contents tasks; implicit versus explicit understanding.
- **Research used as evidence:** two or three of Wimmer and Perner (1983), Perner, Leekam and Wimmer (1987), Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001), Callaghan et al. (2005) and Onishi and Baillargeon (2005). Details of the studies are not assessed on Paper 1, so credit what the evidence shows rather than a retelling.
- **Critical analysis:** verbal task demands; cross-sectional designs; meta-analysis strength; cross-cultural support; competing interpretations of looking-time data.
- **Measurement:** whether a false-belief answer, or looking time, measures understanding of belief or something else such as language, memory or surprise at an unusual event.
- **Conclusion:** a judgement on the age at which theory of mind develops that depends on how it is measured.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2:** giving the right answer without the reason.
- **Question 6:** saying "the child is too young", which explains nothing.
- **Question 8(b):** calling the study a true experiment, although age cannot be randomly allocated.
- **Question 11:** writing about the dolls rather than the logic of causation.
- **Question 12:** defining both terms without explaining the link.
- **Question 13:** describing tasks without evaluating what they can and cannot show.
