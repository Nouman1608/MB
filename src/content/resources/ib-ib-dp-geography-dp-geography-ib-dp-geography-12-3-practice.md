## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between resistance and negotiation as local responses to global interactions. **[2]**

**2.** Define civil society. **[1]**

**3.** Outline one reason why a local government might charge visitors to enter a historic city. **[2]**

**4.** State two groups within a community that might disagree about a new foreign-owned factory. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one global force that has prompted local responses in historic cities. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A town council scores three responses to rising tourist numbers from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong).

| Criterion | Visitor fee | Cap on tourist flats | Promotion of other districts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reduces crowding | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| Protects housing for residents | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| Cost to the council | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| Support from businesses | 2 | 1 | 4 |

**(a)** Calculate the total score for each response. **[2]**

**(b)** Recalculate the totals if "Protects housing for residents" is counted twice. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what the change in (b) shows about using scoring to evaluate responses. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why local resistance to a transnational corporation may fail to achieve its aims. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how global media and NGOs can strengthen a local response. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest why a community might move from accepting to resisting a global interaction over time. **[2]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine why local responses to global interactions vary between places. **[10]**

**11.** "Local communities have little power to change the effects of global interactions." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Resistance opposes a global interaction, trying to stop a project or practice through protest, boycotts or legal action [1]; negotiation accepts the interaction but tries to shape its terms, such as local jobs, standards or benefit-sharing [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Groups outside government and business, such as NGOs, unions, faith groups and community organisations [1]. **[1]**

**3.** To reduce crowding at peak times by discouraging short day visits [1], and to raise money for maintaining the city and its services, as Venice aimed to do with its day-tripper fee introduced in 2024 [1]. **[2]**

**4.** People employed by the factory [1]; neighbours affected by its pollution or traffic [1]. (Also credit local businesses supplying it, or farmers losing land or water.) **[2]**

**5.** For example, the growth of international tourism through cheap flights and online booking platforms [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Visitor fee: 3 + 1 + 5 + 2 = **11** [1]. Cap on flats: 2 + 5 + 3 + 1 = **11**; promotion: 3 + 2 + 2 + 4 = **11** [1].

**(b)** Visitor fee: 11 + 1 = **12**; cap on flats: 11 + 5 = **16** [1]; promotion: 11 + 2 = **13** [1].

**(c)** With equal weights the three responses tie, but giving more weight to housing makes the cap on tourist flats clearly the best [1], so the result depends on which criterion the council values most, and the weighting should be justified rather than treated as neutral [1]. **[6]**

**7.** The corporation and the state may share an interest in investment and tax revenue, so the state may side with the company [1]. Local campaigners often have fewer resources, legal rights or media access [1], and the community may be divided, with some residents depending on the jobs the company provides, which weakens collective action [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Global media coverage can bring a local issue to international audiences, raising reputational pressure on a company or government [1]. NGOs can provide funding, legal expertise and research [1], and can organise boycotts or campaigns in consumer countries, as international attention did for the Ogoni campaign in Nigeria [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Early benefits such as jobs or income may be followed by costs that appear later, such as pollution, rising rents or loss of land [1], so as more people experience the costs, opinion shifts and organised opposition grows [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines local responses and links them to global interactions [1].
- Economic dependence: places that need investment are more likely to accept it [1].
- Power and organisation of local groups affect whether resistance is possible [1].
- Legal rights and the independence of courts shape outcomes [1].
- State interests: governments tied to resource revenue may suppress opposition, as in Ogoniland [1].
- Culture and identity: strong attachment to land or heritage motivates resistance [1].
- Connection to global networks: media and NGOs can amplify local voices [1].
- Wealthy local governments can filter flows, such as tourism, through fees and licensing [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on which factors matter most [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines local communities and the global interactions concerned [1].
- For: transnational corporations and states often hold far more resources than local groups [1].
- For: resistance can fail, or succeed at a heavy cost, as the execution of Ogoni activists in 1995 showed [1].
- For: filtering measures may move problems elsewhere rather than solve them [1].
- Against: some campaigns have stopped projects or forced companies to withdraw [1].
- Against: local governments can regulate flows such as tourism through fees and licensing [1].
- Against: cooperatives and local enterprises can capture more value from global markets [1].
- Power varies with wealth, legal rights and connection to global networks [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence on both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that local power is real but conditional [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers to questions on local power identify the conditions under which communities succeed, not just examples of success or failure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding scores correctly but not commenting on what the weighting choice means.
- Describing a response without naming the global force behind it.
- Assuming that stopping a project means a response was fully successful.
- Writing about a community as if everyone in it agreed.
