Topic 6 of Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715, 2028) asks how businesses can trade without harming the
planet or treating people unfairly, and how consumers are protected when things go wrong. It has three
short sections - 6.1 Sustainability, 6.2 Ethics and 6.3 Consumer protection - but questions on it are
usually applied to a scenario and often ask you to evaluate a business decision, so learn each action with
a benefit and a drawback.

For full explanations see the
[sustainability and ethics study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-commerce-sustainability-and-ethics-study-guide/),
then practise with the [sustainability and ethics practice questions](/resources/igcse-commerce-sustainability-and-ethics-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage (0715, 2028)

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 6.1 Sustainability | Purpose of sustainability in the supply chain; nine actions to improve sustainability |
| 6.2 Ethics | Meaning of ethics; role of fair trade; misleading advertising and harmful disposal of products |
| 6.3 Consumer protection | Reasons for consumer protection; actions by buyers and sellers to resolve complaints |

## What is the purpose of sustainability in the supply chain? (6.1.1)

To make commercial processes **eco-friendly and environmentally responsible for future generations**. In
other words, today's buying, making, moving and selling should not use up resources or damage the
environment in ways that leave future generations worse off.

## Which actions improve sustainability? (6.1.2)

| Action | Example | Benefit to the business | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sustainable materials | Recycled cotton, certified timber | Appeals to eco-conscious customers | Often more expensive |
| Sustainable logistics | Electric vans, fuller loads, route planning | Lower fuel costs over time | Investment in new vehicles |
| Reducing packaging | Refill stations, minimal wrapping | Lower material and waste costs | Products may be less protected |
| Selling eco-friendly products | Biodegradable cleaning products | New market segment | Higher prices may lose some buyers |
| Recycling and reducing waste | Reusing offcuts, recycling cardboard | Lower disposal costs | Sorting systems cost time |
| Reducing returns and exchanges | Accurate size guides, clear product photos | Fewer delivery journeys and less waste | Needs better product information |
| Digital resale marketplaces | Platform for customers to resell used items | Customer loyalty; extra income | Running the platform costs money |
| Free repair services | Repairing clothes or electronics | Longer product life; good reputation | Fewer repeat purchases in the short term |
| Water stewardship | Reducing and treating water used in production | Lower water bills; protects local supply | Treatment equipment is costly |

## What does ethics mean in commerce? (6.2)

- **Ethics** = moral principles about what is right and wrong, guiding how a business behaves beyond what
  the law requires.
- **Fair trade**: producers in low-income countries are paid a fair, stable price and work in decent
  conditions; buyers may pay a premium for community projects. Role in commerce: protects small producers
  from low world prices, gives consumers an ethical choice and can strengthen a retailer's reputation.
- **Ethical issues**:
  - **Misleading advertising** - false or exaggerated claims about a product, such as hidden charges or
    untrue health benefits. It damages trust and can lead to legal action.
  - **Harmful disposal of products** - dumping waste, unsafe disposal of electronics or chemicals, or
    sending waste to places that cannot treat it.

## Why do consumers need protection? (6.3)

- Sellers usually know more about products than buyers.
- Goods may be unsafe, faulty or not as described.
- Consumers can be misled by advertising or pricing.
- Individual consumers have less power and money than large businesses if a dispute arises.

**How are complaints resolved?**

| Buyer's actions | Seller's actions |
|---|---|
| Keep the receipt and evidence of the fault | Listen to and record the complaint |
| Complain to the seller first, calmly and in writing if needed | Offer a repair, replacement or refund |
| Escalate to the manager or head office | Apologise and offer goodwill, e.g. a voucher |
| Contact a consumer protection organisation or regulator | Investigate and fix the cause |
| Take legal action as a last resort | Train staff to handle complaints well |

## Worked example: applying the content

*A clothing retailer finds that 30% of online orders are returned, mostly for poor fit.*

- Sustainability problem: extra delivery journeys and waste from returned items.
- Actions: reduce returns with better size guides and photos; offer a digital resale marketplace for items
  that do not fit; use reusable packaging for returns.
- Evaluation: these actions cut costs and emissions, but take time and money to set up; the retailer must
  decide whether savings outweigh the costs.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Defining sustainability as "being green" without mentioning future generations.
- Treating ethics as the same as the law - ethics goes beyond legal requirements.
- Saying fair trade means "cheap prices" - it means fair prices to producers.
- Describing consumer protection without saying what the buyer or seller actually does.
- Giving only benefits of sustainable actions when the question asks you to evaluate.
- Mixing up sections: misleading advertising is an ethical issue (6.2), while the steps to resolve a
  complaint belong to consumer protection (6.3).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is sustainability only about the environment?** In 0715 the purpose is stated in environmental terms:
eco-friendly, environmentally responsible processes for future generations. Ethical treatment of people
is covered under 6.2.

**How does fair trade help commerce?** It gives producers a fair, stable income, offers consumers an ethical
choice and can improve the reputation and sales of businesses that stock fair trade goods.

**Why is harmful disposal of products an ethical issue?** Dumping waste or sending old electronics to places
that cannot treat them safely may be cheaper, and may even be legal in some countries, but it damages the
environment and the health of people who live nearby. An ethical business considers that harm, not just
the cost or the law.

**What should a customer do first with a faulty product?** Return to the seller with the receipt and
evidence, and ask for a repair, replacement or refund before escalating.
