Condensed notes for section 3.2, *Diversity and social change*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 2. Full explanations are in the [3.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-diversity-and-social-change/); test yourself with the [3.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-diversity-and-social-change-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Causes and consequences of changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce and separation.
- Household forms: nuclear, extended, lone-parent, reconstituted, single-person (others, such as same-sex families and families of choice, where appropriate locally).
- Dimensions of diversity: organisational, cultural, class.
- The extent of diversity and the dominance of the nuclear family.
- New Right and postmodernist perspectives on diversity.
- The state and social policy as influences on the family.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Household | One person or a group living at the same address and sharing facilities |
| Cohabitation | A couple living together in a relationship without being married |
| Separation | A couple ending their shared life without legal divorce |
| Serial monogamy | A sequence of partners over a lifetime, one at a time |
| Reconstituted family | A family where at least one partner has a child from a previous relationship |
| Vertically extended family | Three or more generations of one family |
| Beanpole family | A long, thin family with many generations but few members in each (useful link to 4.2) |
| Neo-conventional family | Chester (1985): a dual-earner nuclear family |
| Family of choice | Weeks: friends and partners acting as family |
| Individualisation | Beck and Beck-Gernsheim: people construct their own biographies, free of traditional ties |
| Pure relationship | Giddens (1992): a relationship that lasts only while it satisfies both partners |
| Social policy | Government actions and laws that affect people's welfare and lives |

## Why marriage, cohabitation and divorce patterns change

1. **Attitudes and stigma**: less condemnation of cohabitation, divorce and births outside marriage.
2. **Secularisation**: marriage seen as less sacred in some societies.
3. **Women's independence**: education and paid work reduce reliance on a husband's income.
4. **Law**: easier, cheaper divorce; in England and Wales the Divorce Reform Act 1969 (in force from 1971) introduced "irretrievable breakdown".
5. **Higher expectations**: Fletcher; people expect love and companionship, so more marriages are judged to fail.
6. **Individualisation**: Beck and Beck-Gernsheim (1995); relationships last while they meet both partners' needs.

**Consequences**: more lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households; serial monogamy; children living across households. New Right: harm to children; feminists: escape from unequal relationships; postmodernists: more choice.

## Dimensions of diversity (Rapoport and Rapoport, 1982)

| Dimension | Example |
|---|---|
| Organisational | Joint or segregated roles; dual-earner couples; children moving between two homes |
| Cultural | Ethnic and religious differences in kin ties, marriage customs and household size |
| Class | Resources for childcare and education; reliance on nearby kin |
| Life-course (extra) | Families change as members age |
| Cohort (extra) | Generations experience family life differently |

## The extent of diversity

**Nuclear family still dominant**: Chester's neo-conventional family; snapshots exaggerate diversity; most people experience a nuclear family at some point; the nuclear ideal shapes policy.

**Diversity is the norm**: Stacey (1990) on "divorce-extended families" in Silicon Valley; growth of lone-parent, reconstituted and single-person households; families of choice.

**Judgement**: depends on the society and on whether you measure households at one moment or experience over a lifetime.

## New Right versus postmodernist

| | New Right | Postmodernist |
|---|---|---|
| Diversity is | A problem | Choice |
| Ideal | Married two-parent family | None |
| Names | Murray (underclass); Dennis and Erdos (1992) | Stacey (1990) |
| Criticism | Poverty, not family form, explains disadvantage; blames individuals | Overstates choice; structures still constrain |

Late modern addition: Giddens' pure relationship (1992).

## State and social policy

- **Examples**: China's one-child policy (from around 1980, replaced by a two-child policy announced in 2015); Romania's 1966 decree restricting abortion and contraception; child benefits, parental leave, tax and family law.
- **Functionalist**: policy supports families (Fletcher).
- **New Right**: welfare undermines two-parent families.
- **Feminist**: policy assumes male breadwinner and female carer.
- **Marxist**: policy maintains a family that serves capitalism.
- **Donzelot (1977)**: professionals police families, especially poorer ones.

## Exam traps

- **One country as the world.** Patterns vary greatly; say which society.
- **Household versus family.** A single-person household is not a family.
- **"The nuclear family is dead."** Weigh Chester and the life-course argument.
- **Describing New Right views without evaluation.**
- **Forgetting consequences.** The syllabus asks for causes *and* consequences.
- **Skipping social policy.** It is a named bullet.
- **Same-sex families and families of choice** are optional content and not assessed, though you may use them as support where relevant.

## Self-test

1. Define *household*.
2. What is a reconstituted family?
3. Give two causes of rising divorce.
4. What did the Divorce Reform Act 1969 introduce in England and Wales?
5. Explain individualisation.
6. Name the Rapoports' three dimensions of diversity listed in the syllabus.
7. What is Chester's neo-conventional family?
8. What did Stacey find in Silicon Valley?
9. Why do the New Right oppose family diversity?
10. Give one criticism of the postmodernist view.
11. Give one example of social policy affecting family size.
12. What did Donzelot argue in *The Policing of Families*?

### Answers

1. One person or a group living at the same address and sharing facilities.
2. A family in which at least one partner has a child from a previous relationship.
3. Any two: changing attitudes; secularisation; women's financial independence; easier divorce law; higher expectations of marriage; individualisation.
4. Divorce on the ground of irretrievable breakdown of the marriage.
5. People increasingly make their own life choices, free from traditional roles, so relationships depend on meeting individual needs.
6. Organisational, cultural, class.
7. A nuclear family in which both partners are in paid work.
8. Women creating new family forms after divorce, including "divorce-extended families".
9. They see it as family breakdown that harms children, encourages welfare dependency and weakens social order.
10. It overstates choice; class, income, law, religion and gender still constrain family life.
11. China's one-child policy, or Romania's 1966 restrictions on abortion and contraception.
12. Professionals such as social workers and health visitors monitor and control families, especially poorer ones.
