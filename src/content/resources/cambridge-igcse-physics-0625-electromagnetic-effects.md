## Two effects that mirror each other

Most of 4.5 rests on two linked ideas:

- **Current + magnetic field → force (motion).** This is the **motor effect**. Energy goes in as electrical and comes out as kinetic.
- **Motion + magnetic field → induced e.m.f. (current).** This is **electromagnetic induction**. Energy goes in as kinetic and comes out as electrical.

**Extended only:** each has its own hand rule, and mixing them up is the commonest single error in this subtopic. **Left hand for motors** (force on a current), **right hand for generators** (induced current). In both, the first finger is the **F**ield and the second finger is the **C**urrent; the thumb is the motion (thrust) in the left-hand rule and the motion causing induction in the right-hand rule.

## Electromagnetic induction

An e.m.f. is induced in a conductor when it **moves across** a magnetic field, cutting field lines, or when the **magnetic field linking it changes**. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, a current flows.

**Factors that increase the induced e.m.f.:** moving the conductor (or magnet) **faster**; using a **stronger** magnet; using a coil with **more turns**. No e.m.f. is induced if there is no relative movement, or if a wire moves **parallel** to the field lines rather than across them.

**Extended only: direction.** The induced e.m.f. always **opposes the change causing it**. Push the N pole of a magnet into a coil: the induced current makes the near end of the coil an **N pole**, which repels the approaching magnet. Pull the magnet out: the near end becomes an **S pole**, attracting the retreating magnet. In both cases you must do work against this opposing force, and that work becomes the electrical energy. This is why the meter deflects in opposite directions for "in" and "out".

## The a.c. generator (Extended only)

A coil rotates between magnetic poles (or a magnet rotates inside a fixed coil). Each side of a rotating coil moves up through the field and then down, so the induced e.m.f. reverses every half turn: an **alternating** e.m.f. **Slip rings**, which turn with the coil, and **brushes** pressing on them, connect the rotating coil to the external circuit without the wires twisting.

**Reading the e.m.f.–time graph:**

- **Peak (or trough):** the plane of the coil is **parallel** to the field; its sides cut the field lines fastest.
- **Zero:** the plane of the coil is **perpendicular** to the field; its sides move along the lines, cutting none.
- Spinning **faster** gives higher peaks **and** more cycles per second (a shorter time per cycle).

## Magnetic effect of a current

A current in a **straight wire** produces a field of **concentric circles** around the wire. A current in a **solenoid** produces a field like a bar magnet's outside it, with strong, nearly parallel lines inside.

**Direction:** use the right-hand grip rule. For a wire, grip it with the thumb along the conventional current; the curled fingers show the field direction. For a solenoid, curl the fingers in the direction of the current in the turns; the thumb points to the **N pole** end.

**Extended only.** The field around a wire is **strongest close to the wire** and weakens with distance (the circles are drawn further apart). Inside a solenoid the field is **strong and uniform**. A **larger current** gives a stronger field (lines closer together); **reversing the current reverses the field direction**, so every plotting compass turns round.

**Relays and loudspeakers.** In a relay, a small current through a coil magnetises it; it attracts an iron armature that closes contacts in a second circuit, so a small, safe current switches a large one (for example a car starter motor). In a loudspeaker, a varying current in a coil placed in a permanent magnet's field makes the coil and attached cone move back and forth, producing sound waves.

## Force on a current-carrying conductor

A wire carrying a current at right angles to a magnetic field experiences a force at right angles to **both** the current and the field. Reversing the **current** reverses the force; reversing the **field** reverses the force; reversing **both** leaves it unchanged. There is no force if the wire is parallel to the field.

**Extended only: beams of charged particles.** A beam of moving charged particles is a current. For **positive** particles, the current is in their direction of travel; for **electrons**, the current is **opposite** to their motion. Apply Fleming's left-hand rule with that current direction to find the direction of the force, which makes the beam curve.

## The d.c. motor

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a **turning effect**, increased by **more turns** on the coil, a **larger current** and a **stronger field**.

**Extended only.** Current flows in opposite directions in the two sides of the coil, so one side is pushed up and the other down: a turning effect. A **split-ring commutator** turns with the coil, and fixed carbon **brushes** press on it. Every half turn, as the plane of the coil passes the position perpendicular to the field, the commutator reverses the current in the coil, so the forces continue to turn it the same way. Without the commutator the coil would rock back and forth and stop.

## The transformer

A transformer has a **primary coil** and a **secondary coil** wound on the same **soft-iron core**. A **step-up** transformer has more turns on the secondary (Vs > Vp); a **step-down** transformer has fewer (Vs < Vp).

**Vp / Vs = Np / Ns**

**Extended only: how it works.** An alternating current in the primary produces a **changing magnetic field** in the soft-iron core. The core carries this changing field through the secondary coil, inducing an alternating e.m.f. in it. A direct current would give a steady field, so no e.m.f. would be induced. For a transformer that is 100% efficient, power in = power out: **IpVp = IsVs**. So stepping the voltage up steps the current down.

### Worked example 1

A phone charger transformer steps 230 V down to 6.0 V. Its primary has 2300 turns. **Extended only:** the charger supplies 0.50 A. Find the number of secondary turns and the primary current.

```
Ns = Np × Vs / Vp = 2300 × 6.0 / 230 = 60 turns
Extended:  Ip = Is Vs / Vp = (0.50 × 6.0) / 230 = 0.013 A
```

**High-voltage transmission.** A step-up transformer at the power station raises the voltage for the transmission cables; step-down transformers lower it again near the users. Advantages: for the same power, the **current is smaller**, so **less energy is wasted** heating the cables, and **thinner cables** can be used.

### Worked example 2 (Extended)

A power station sends 20 MW through cables of total resistance 5.0 Ω. Compare the power lost at 400 kV and at 20 kV.

```
400 kV:  I = P / V = 20 × 10⁶ / 400 × 10³ = 50 A
         P_loss = I²R = 50² × 5.0 = 12 500 W  (12.5 kW)
20 kV:   I = 20 × 10⁶ / 20 × 10³ = 1000 A
         P_loss = 1000² × 5.0 = 5.0 × 10⁶ W  (5.0 MW, a quarter of the power sent)
```

The voltage is 20 times larger, so the current is 20 times smaller and the loss is 20² = 400 times smaller. Use P = I²R with the **current in the cables**, not the transmission voltage.

## Common mistakes

- Using the right-hand rule for a motor, or the left for a generator.
- Saying a transformer works on d.c.
- Saying the generator's e.m.f. is largest when the coil is vertical (perpendicular to the field).
- Forgetting that electron beams count as current in the opposite direction.
- Calculating cable loss with P = V²/R using the transmission voltage.
- Saying the commutator "reverses the field".

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an experiment: give the apparatus, what you do, what you observe and what changes when you reverse something.
- **State the factors:** list them separately (faster, stronger, more turns).
- **Explain** (Extended): for transformers, chain a.c. → changing field in core → induced e.m.f. in secondary.
- **Sketch** (Extended): an e.m.f.–time graph needs labelled axes and a regular wave crossing zero twice per turn.
- **Calculate:** write the equation, convert kV and MW to V and W, and give units.
