This guide covers **The Land of Pakistan**, the first of eight sections
in Paper 2 The Environment of Pakistan, Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan
Studies (0448), 2026 series. It is a map-and-location-heavy foundation
section: everything examined in the rest of Paper 2 — natural
resources, power, agriculture, industry, trade, transport and
population — assumes candidates can already place Pakistan's physical
geography on a map without prompting.

## Where this fits in 0448

Pakistan Studies 0448 splits into two papers: Paper 1, The History and
Culture of Pakistan, and Paper 2, The Environment of Pakistan. This
guide is the entry point to Paper 2 — Marlbridge already covers Section
2 (Natural Resources – An Issue of Sustainability) separately, and that
guide assumes the location and topography knowledge built here.
Section 1 is worth building first because every later section in Paper
2 refers back to named places, mountain ranges and rivers introduced
in this section without re-explaining them.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE PAKISTAN STUDIES 0448 — PAPER 2, SECTION 1: THE LAND OF PAKISTAN**

- 1(a) Location of Pakistan — identifying the Tropic of Cancer, the
  lines of latitude 30°N and 36°N and longitude 64°E, 70°E and 76°E,
  the Arabian Sea, and the countries that border Pakistan, together
  with Pakistan's position within South and Central Asia
- 1(b) Location of administrative areas and cities — the administrative
  areas of Pakistan and named cities including Islamabad, Murree,
  Rawalpindi, Gujranwala, Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan, Sialkot,
  Peshawar, Chitral, Gilgit, Hyderabad, Karachi, Quetta and Gwadar
- 1(c) The natural topography, including drainage — named landforms
  (the Balochistan Plateau, the Sulaiman Range, Safed Koh, the Potwar
  Plateau, the Salt Range, and the Hindu Kush, Karakoram and Himalaya
  mountain ranges), named rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej,
  Kabul, Hab and Dasht) and named deserts (Thar, Thal and Kharan), plus
  understanding how topography shapes land use, road networks and rail
  networks (knowledge of how the topography itself formed is not
  required)
- 1(d) Climate — the distribution of temperature and rainfall
  (including the monsoon, depressions and convectional rain), seasonal
  and regional variations and their causes, the causes of the monsoon
  specifically, the characteristics of arid, semi-arid, humid and
  highland climates, the influence of latitude and longitude on day
  length and climate, and how climate affects the economy and people's
  lives — including the effects of low temperature, ice and snow in
  the mountains, of rain storms and flooding on agriculture, industry
  and communications, and of drought and water shortage on agriculture
  and industry

## Location (1a–1b): what candidates must be able to do on a map

This section is assessed almost entirely through map-based questions,
so the command word to prepare for is "identify," not "explain." For
1(a), practise plotting the Tropic of Cancer and the three named
longitude lines against Pakistan's outline, and locating the Arabian
Sea and each of Pakistan's neighbouring countries. For 1(b), candidates
need the administrative areas and all 15 named cities memorised well
enough to place them correctly relative to each other, not just
individually — a common mark loss is placing Gilgit or Chitral (in the
north) too far south, or confusing coastal Gwadar with inland Hyderabad.

## Natural topography and drainage (1c)

The specification is explicit that candidates do not need to know how
Pakistan's mountains, plateaux or deserts were formed geologically —
only how to identify and describe them, and how they influence human
activity. Learn the three mountain systems (Hindu Kush, Karakoram,
Himalaya) as a north-to-northeast arc, the Balochistan Plateau and
Sulaiman Range to the west, and the Potwar Plateau and Salt Range in
the north-centre. For rivers, the Indus and its tributaries (Jhelum,
Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej) form the core drainage system that Section 2
(Water, under Natural Resources) builds directly on, so learning their
courses accurately here pays off twice. The two influence-based bullet
points — how steep slopes and flat land shape land use, and how
mountains and deserts shape road and rail networks — are the parts of
this sub-section most likely to appear as short-answer "explain"
questions rather than pure recall.

## Climate (1d)

Climate is the most exam-heavy part of Section 1 because it asks
candidates to both describe and explain, and to connect physical
causes to human consequences. Be able to explain the causes of the
monsoon specifically (the syllabus does not require other rain-type
causes), and to describe how arid, semi-arid, humid and highland zones
differ in temperature and rainfall pattern across the seasons. The
"influence of climate" bullet is where most marks are actually won or
lost: practise writing balanced answers that cover both a benefit and
a problem — for example, monsoon rain supports agriculture but also
causes flooding that disrupts industry and communications, and cold
weather in the mountains constrains people's lives but low temperatures
also feed the glacial meltwater that sustains the Indus system studied
later in the paper.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating this as a pure memorisation section.** Location and
  topography questions are map-based, but climate questions
  consistently reward explanation over listing — know why a monsoon
  happens, not just that it happens.
- **Confusing 1(b)'s administrative-area boundaries with the physical
  landform boundaries in 1(c).** These are assessed separately and
  answers that blend the two lose precision.
- **Forgetting the paired benefit-and-problem structure** expected in
  climate-influence answers — one-sided answers (only benefits, or only
  problems) rarely reach full marks.

## How to approach it

Build a single annotated outline map of Pakistan early in revision and
add to it as you work through each sub-section: administrative areas
and cities first, then landforms and rivers, then a simple climate-zone
overlay. Because Section 1 underpins the rest of Paper 2, revisit this
map whenever a later Paper 2 topic (natural resources, power,
agriculture) refers to a place or feature introduced here, rather than
treating Section 1 as a topic to finish and move on from.
