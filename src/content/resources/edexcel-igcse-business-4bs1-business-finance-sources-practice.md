These questions cover section 3.1 "Business finance – sources" (points 3.1.1 to 3.1.3) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [sources of finance study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-finance-sources/) and the [sources of finance revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-finance-sources-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define crowdfunding. **[2]**

**2.** Give two external sources of finance suitable for a short-term need. **[2]**

**3.** State whether each of these is a short-term or a long-term need for finance.

**(a)** Buying a delivery van expected to last six years. **[1]**
**(b)** Paying for extra stock before a festival weekend. **[1]**

**4.** Nkemi Logistics borrows 45,000 from a bank. It repays 1,350 a month for 40 months. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the total amount repaid. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the interest paid. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the interest as a percentage of the amount borrowed. **[2]**

**5.** Harlan Foods Ltd has 20,000 shares, all owned by the Harlan family. It issues 5,000 new shares to outside investors at 12 each. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the finance raised. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of the company owned by the new investors after the issue. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline one drawback of the share issue for the Harlan family. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one advantage of retained profit as a source of finance. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one disadvantage of a bank loan for a small business. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Kiro Robotics is a new business developing warehouse robots. It has no sales yet. A venture capitalist offers 180,000 in return for 30% of the business.

**(a)** Calculate the value of the whole business implied by the offer. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse one reason why venture capital may be more suitable than a bank loan for Kiro Robotics. **[4]**

**9.** Tumble Toys is a small shop. Most of its sales happen in the eight weeks before a festival season, but it must buy stock months earlier. Analyse how trade payables could help Tumble Toys. **[4]**

**10.** Callow Garden Tools Ltd is owned entirely by one family. It needs 2 million to build a new factory. Its operating profit is 450,000 a year. The family values control of the business. It has two options.

- **Option A:** become a public limited company and float on the stock market, selling 40% of the shares.
- **Option B:** a bank loan of 2 million at 6% interest a year, repaid over 15 years, with the factory as security.

**(a)** Calculate the interest payable in the first year of the loan. **[1]**
**(b)** Justify which option Callow Garden Tools Ltd should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Nadia plans to start a vegan ice cream brand. She needs 15,000 for equipment and first stock and has 3,000 of her own savings. She is considering a crowdfunding campaign that offers backers a box of ice cream in return for each pledge of 25.

**(a)** Calculate how many backers would be needed to raise the remaining 12,000. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether crowdfunding is the best way for Nadia to raise the rest of the finance. **[8]**

**12.** Brookfield Printers needs 90,000 to buy a new press. It owns an unused warehouse valued at 140,000. A buyer offers 85% of this value for a quick sale.

**(a)** State whether selling the warehouse is an internal or external source of finance. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how much money would be left after buying the press if Brookfield accepts the offer. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one drawback of raising finance by selling assets. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Raising finance from a large number of people who each give a modest amount [1], usually through a website, sometimes in return for a reward, a product or a share of the business [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Overdraft [1]; trade payables [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Loans and share capital are usually long-term, so they would not earn marks here.

**3. (a)** Long-term [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Short-term [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 1,350 × 40 = **54,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 54,000 − 45,000 = **9,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 9,000 ÷ 45,000 × 100 [1] = **20%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The percentage is of the amount borrowed, not of the total repaid.

**5. (a)** 5,000 × 12 = **60,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Total shares = 20,000 + 5,000 = 25,000 [1]; 5,000 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The family now shares future dividends with outside investors [1], so a fifth of any profit paid out goes to people outside the family [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer about loss of control is also valid, but note the family still holds 80%, so it keeps control for now.

**6.** Retained profit does not have to be repaid and carries no interest [1], so the business's costs are lower than if it borrowed [1], and no outside lender or investor gains a claim on the business or a say in decisions [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The business must make fixed repayments with interest every month [1], even if sales are low [1], which can cause cash flow problems and, if payments are missed, the lender may take an asset given as security [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 180,000 ÷ 0.30 [1] = **600,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Kiro has no sales yet, so a bank would see a loan as high risk [1] and may refuse or charge high interest [1]. Venture capital has no fixed repayments, so Kiro does not need cash from sales to pay it back while it develops its robots [1], and the investor may bring advice and contacts that help it find its first customers [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Suppliers deliver the stock now and Tumble Toys pays later, for example after 60 days [1], so it does not need cash months before its main selling period [1]. It can pay suppliers out of the cash from festival sales [1], which avoids the interest of an overdraft for its short-term need [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link trade credit to the timing problem in the case. A general description of trade payables with no reference to the seasonal pattern scores less.

**10. (a)** 2,000,000 × 0.06 = **120,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For Option A: there is no interest or repayment, so the 120,000 a year interest is avoided [1], and flotation can raise large sums such as 2 million [1]. Against Option A: selling 40% of shares to the public weakens the family's control, which it values [1], and anyone can buy shares, so the family could face pressure from new shareholders or a takeover in future [1]. For Option B: the family keeps full control, and the interest of 120,000 is well covered by operating profit of 450,000 [1]. Judgement: choose Option B, because control is the family's priority and profits are high enough to cover the interest, although the factory is at risk if repayments cannot be made [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** 12,000 ÷ 25 = **480** backers [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For crowdfunding: Nadia has no trading record, so a bank may not lend [1]. Backers are rewarded with ice cream rather than interest or a share of the business, so she pays no interest and keeps full ownership [1], and the campaign tests whether people want vegan ice cream before she spends her own 3,000 [1]. Backers who receive a box may become loyal customers and spread the word [1]. Against: she needs 480 backers, which is a large number for a new brand with no following [1]. If the campaign is all-or-nothing and misses the target, she may receive nothing after spending time on it [1], and she must still produce and deliver 480 boxes of ice cream, which uses stock and adds costs [1]. Judgement: for example, crowdfunding is suitable because it tests demand and avoids debt, but only if she has a following on social media; otherwise a smaller loan with her savings may be more certain [1]. **[8]**

**12. (a)** Internal [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 140,000 × 0.85 = 119,000 [1]; 119,000 − 90,000 = **29,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The business may have to accept a low price for a quick sale [1], as here, where it receives 21,000 less than the warehouse is valued at [1], so it gets less finance than the asset is worth and loses an asset it might need if it grows [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the figure from the case (140,000 − 119,000 = 21,000) turns a general point into an applied one.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating interest as a percentage of the total repaid.
- Forgetting to add the new shares to the old ones before working out a percentage.
- Recommending retained profit or a loan for a start-up with no trading record without saying why a lender would agree.
- Ignoring the owner's stated priority, such as control.
- Giving a list of sources instead of comparing the two named in the question.
