## The functional groups in this section

A carboxylic acid contains the carboxyl group, –COOH. Name it by counting all the carbons, including the carboxyl carbon, and ending in -oic acid. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COOH is pentanoic acid; (CH₃)₂CHCH₂COOH is 3-methylbutanoic acid.

The derivatives you meet here replace the –OH of the carboxyl group:

- **ester**, –COOR': named alkyl alkanoate. The alkyl part comes from the alcohol and is written first; the alkanoate part comes from the acid.
- **acid anhydride**, (RCO)₂O: two acyl groups joined through one oxygen, e.g. propanoic anhydride, (CH₃CH₂CO)₂O.
- **acyl chloride**, –COCl: named with -oyl chloride, e.g. pentanoyl chloride.
- **amide**: –CONH₂ is a primary amide; –CONHR' is a secondary amide.

## 6.1.3(a): Why carboxylic acids dissolve in water

The carboxyl group can hydrogen bond to water in two ways:

- the δ+ hydrogen of the acid's O–H bonds to a lone pair on a water oxygen;
- a δ+ hydrogen of a water molecule bonds to a lone pair on the acid's C=O oxygen (and on the O–H oxygen).

So the short-chain acids, methanoic to butanoic acid, are miscible with water.

As the hydrocarbon chain gets longer, solubility falls. The non-polar chain cannot hydrogen bond to water and disrupts water's hydrogen-bond network. Hexanoic acid is only slightly soluble.

In an answer, name the bond and say which atoms take part: δ+ H to a lone pair on O.

## 6.1.3(b): Reactions in aqueous conditions

Carboxylic acids are weak acids. In water they partly ionise:

RCOOH(aq) ⇌ RCOO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq)

They still give the usual acid reactions, forming carboxylate salts. Name the salt with the metal first and the acid ending -oate: sodium pentanoate, magnesium propanoate.

| Reacts with | General equation (monoprotic acid) | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Reactive metal, e.g. Mg | 2RCOOH + Mg → (RCOO)₂Mg + H₂ | fizzing; metal dissolves |
| Carbonate, e.g. Na₂CO₃ | 2RCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2RCOONa + H₂O + CO₂ | fizzing; gas turns limewater milky |
| Metal oxide, e.g. CuO | 2RCOOH + CuO → (RCOO)₂Cu + H₂O | black solid dissolves on warming; blue solution |
| Alkali, e.g. KOH | RCOOH + KOH → RCOOK + H₂O | no visible change |

Ionic equations keep the weak acid as RCOOH, because it is mostly un-ionised:

2RCOOH + CO₃²⁻ → 2RCOO⁻ + H₂O + CO₂
RCOOH + OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + H₂O

### The carbonate test (PAG7)

Fizzing with a carbonate, giving CO₂, identifies a carboxylic acid on a test-tube scale (6.3.1(c)(vii)). Phenols are also weakly acidic, but they do not react with carbonates (6.3.1(c)(iii)). So a compound that dissolves in NaOH(aq) but gives no fizzing with Na₂CO₃(aq) is a phenol, not a carboxylic acid.

The specification states that comparing the acidity of different carboxylic acids is not required.

### Worked example 1: a metal and a carboxylic acid

0.851 g of magnesium ribbon reacts completely with excess aqueous propanoic acid. Use Ar(Mg) = 24.3, Ar(C) = 12.0, Ar(H) = 1.0, Ar(O) = 16.0 and a gas molar volume of 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

(a) Write the equation. (b) Calculate the volume of hydrogen, in cm³. (c) Calculate the mass of salt formed.

**(a)** 2CH₃CH₂COOH + Mg → (CH₃CH₂COO)₂Mg + H₂

**(b)** n(Mg) = 0.851 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0350 mol. One Mg gives one H₂, so n(H₂) = 0.0350 mol.
Volume = 0.0350 × 24.0 = 0.840 dm³ = **840 cm³**.

**(c)** Magnesium propanoate is (C₃H₅O₂)₂Mg. Mr = 2(36.0 + 5.0 + 32.0) + 24.3 = 170.3.
Mass = 0.0350 × 170.3 = **5.96 g**.

## 6.1.3(c): Making esters

### (i) Carboxylic acid + alcohol, acid catalyst

Heat the acid and alcohol with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. The reaction is reversible, so the mixture reaches equilibrium and the yield is less than 100%.

CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O
pentanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl pentanoate + water

To name the ester, take the alcohol's chain as the alkyl group (ethanol → ethyl) and the acid's name with -oic acid changed to -oate (pentanoic acid → pentanoate).

### Worked example 2: yield of an ester

A student heats 5.10 g of pentanoic acid with excess ethanol and concentrated H₂SO₄. After purification, 4.55 g of ethyl pentanoate is collected. Calculate the percentage yield. Use Ar(C) = 12.0, Ar(H) = 1.0, Ar(O) = 16.0.

1. Mr(pentanoic acid, C₅H₁₀O₂) = 60.0 + 10.0 + 32.0 = 102.0
2. n(acid) = 5.10 ÷ 102.0 = 0.0500 mol
3. One acid molecule gives one ester molecule: at most 0.0500 mol of ester
4. Mr(ethyl pentanoate, C₇H₁₄O₂) = 84.0 + 14.0 + 32.0 = 130.0
5. Theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 130.0 = 6.50 g
6. Percentage yield = 4.55 ÷ 6.50 × 100 = **70.0%**

### (ii) Acid anhydride + alcohol

An acid anhydride reacts with an alcohol on warming to give an ester and a carboxylic acid (not water). No acid catalyst is needed and the reaction is not reversible, so the yield is better than with the acid.

(CH₃CH₂CO)₂O + CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂COOH
propanoic anhydride + butan-1-ol → butyl propanoate + propanoic acid

## 6.1.3(d): Hydrolysis of esters

Both versions use heat, usually under reflux.

**(i) Hot aqueous acid**, e.g. dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl. This is the reverse of esterification and is reversible, so hydrolysis is incomplete:

CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂OH

**(ii) Hot aqueous alkali**, e.g. NaOH(aq). This goes to completion. The products are the carboxylate salt and the alcohol:

CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + CH₃CH₂OH

The carboxylate ion does not react with the alcohol, so the reaction cannot reverse. Adding a strong acid afterwards gives the free carboxylic acid.

### Worked example 3: identifying an ester from its hydrolysis products

An ester with formula C₇H₁₄O₂ is refluxed with excess KOH(aq). One product is potassium butanoate. The other is an alcohol that acidified dichromate oxidises to propanone. A 1.95 g sample of the ester is used.

(a) Identify the ester. (b) Find the mass of potassium butanoate made. Use Ar(K) = 39.1, Ar(C) = 12.0, Ar(H) = 1.0, Ar(O) = 16.0.

**(a)** Potassium butanoate tells you the acyl part is CH₃CH₂CH₂COO–. Propanone comes from oxidising a secondary alcohol, propan-2-ol, (CH₃)₂CHOH. So the ester is CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH(CH₃)₂, **propan-2-yl butanoate**. Check: C₄H₇O₂ + C₃H₇ = C₇H₁₄O₂.

**(b)** Mr(C₇H₁₄O₂) = 84.0 + 14.0 + 32.0 = 130.0; n = 1.95 ÷ 130.0 = 0.0150 mol.
Mr(CH₃CH₂CH₂COOK) = 48.0 + 7.0 + 32.0 + 39.1 = 126.1.
Mass = 0.0150 × 126.1 = **1.89 g**.

## 6.1.3(e): Making acyl chlorides with SOCl₂

Thionyl chloride, SOCl₂, converts a carboxylic acid into an acyl chloride. Both by-products are gases, which leave the mixture and make the acyl chloride easier to isolate:

RCOOH + SOCl₂ → RCOCl + SO₂ + HCl

For 3-methylbutanoic acid:

(CH₃)₂CHCH₂COOH + SOCl₂ → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂COCl + SO₂ + HCl

The product is 3-methylbutanoyl chloride. Keep everything dry: acyl chlorides react with water.

## 6.1.3(f): Acyl chlorides in synthesis

Acyl chlorides are far more reactive than carboxylic acids. The carbonyl carbon, bonded to both O and Cl, carries a large δ+ charge and is attacked readily by nucleophiles. Reactions happen at room temperature, are not reversible and release HCl.

### Worked example 4: one acyl chloride, five products

Give the organic product when 3-methylbutanoyl chloride, written as RCOCl with R = (CH₃)₂CHCH₂–, reacts with each reagent.

| Reagent | Equation | Organic product |
|---|---|---|
| Water | RCOCl + H₂O → RCOOH + HCl | 3-methylbutanoic acid (carboxylic acid) |
| Propan-1-ol | RCOCl + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → RCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + HCl | propyl 3-methylbutanoate (ester) |
| Phenol | RCOCl + C₆H₅OH → RCOOC₆H₅ + HCl | phenyl 3-methylbutanoate (ester) |
| Ammonia | RCOCl + 2NH₃ → RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl | 3-methylbutanamide (primary amide) |
| Ethylamine | RCOCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → RCONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ | N-ethyl-3-methylbutanamide (secondary amide) |

Notes on the table:

- With water the reaction is vigorous and misty fumes of HCl are given off.
- With ammonia or an amine, two molecules are needed. The first forms the amide; the second, being a base, removes the HCl as an ammonium salt.
- An amide made from ammonia is primary (–CONH₂). One made from a primary amine has one alkyl group on the nitrogen and is secondary (–CONHR').

### Why phenol needs an acyl chloride

The specification notes that phenol is not readily esterified by carboxylic acids. Heating phenol with a carboxylic acid and an acid catalyst gives very little ester. An acyl chloride reacts with phenol at room temperature, so this is the standard route to a phenyl ester.

## Comparing routes to an ester

| Route | Conditions | Reversible? | By-product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid + alcohol | heat, conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst | yes | water |
| Acid anhydride + alcohol | warm | no | carboxylic acid |
| Acyl chloride + alcohol or phenol | room temperature | no | HCl |

## Common errors

- Naming the ester the wrong way round: the alcohol gives the first word. Propyl ethanoate comes from propan-1-ol and ethanoic acid, not ethanol and propanoic acid.
- Writing water as a product of an anhydride esterification. The second product is a carboxylic acid.
- Giving a carboxylic acid, rather than its salt, as the product of alkaline hydrolysis.
- Leaving out HCl when an acyl chloride reacts, or using only one NH₃ with an acyl chloride.
- Writing H⁺ for a carboxylic acid in an ionic equation as if it were fully ionised.
- Saying a phenol fizzes with sodium carbonate. It does not; that is the test that separates it from a carboxylic acid.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you tell a carboxylic acid from a phenol?**
Add sodium carbonate. A carboxylic acid fizzes and gives carbon dioxide, but a phenol does not react. This test is part of PAG7.

**What is the difference between acid and alkaline hydrolysis of an ester?**
Hydrolysis with hot aqueous acid is the reverse of esterification and is reversible. Hydrolysis with hot aqueous alkali goes to completion and gives the carboxylate salt and the alcohol.

**Why does phenol need an acyl chloride to form an ester?**
Phenol is not readily esterified by carboxylic acids, so a phenyl ester is made by reacting phenol with an acyl chloride instead.

**What do you see when an acyl chloride reacts with water?**
The reaction is vigorous and misty fumes of hydrogen chloride are given off, forming the carboxylic acid.
