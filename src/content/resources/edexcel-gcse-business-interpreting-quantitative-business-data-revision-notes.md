## The four kinds of data in 2.4.2

| Kind | What it includes (Appendix 2 names the items in bold) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Graphs and charts | **Information from graphs and charts**: bar charts, line graphs, pie charts and tables are the usual forms | Spot trends, peaks, shares |
| Financial data | **Profit and loss, profitability ratios, average rate of return, cash-flow forecasts** | Judge profitability and cash |
| Marketing data | **Market research data**; results of promotions are a natural further example | Decide product, price, promotion |
| Market data | **Market share, changes in costs, changes in prices** | Compare with competitors and the market |

The specification says the data is used to **support**, **inform** and **justify** business decisions.

## Formulae

| Measure | Formula | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit | sales revenue − cost of sales | Appendix 3 |
| Gross profit margin (%) | gross profit / sales revenue × 100 | Appendix 3 |
| Net profit | gross profit − other operating expenses and interest | Appendix 3 |
| Net profit margin (%) | net profit / sales revenue × 100 | Appendix 3 |
| Average rate of return (%) | average annual profit (total profit / number of years) / cost of investment × 100 | Appendix 3 |
| Net cash flow | cash inflows − cash outflows | Appendix 3 |
| Closing balance | opening balance + net cash flow | Appendix 3 |
| Percentage change | change / original × 100 | Standard form |
| Mean (average) | total of the values / number of values | Standard form |
| Market share (%) | business sales / total market sales × 100 | Standard form |
| Pie chart share | sector angle / 360 × 100 | Standard form |

Formulae are not provided in the examinations, and calculators may be used.

## Method boxes

**Percentage change**
1. Subtract old from new (keep the sign).
2. Divide by the old (original) figure.
3. Multiply by 100 and say increase or decrease.

**Comparing two years of profit information**
1. Work out gross profit and net profit for both years.
2. Work out both margins.
3. Compare the margins first, then the profit totals.
4. Find which cost line moved most and link it to the business.

**ARR**
1. Add the yearly profits to get the total profit.
2. Divide by the number of years.
3. Divide by the cost of the investment and multiply by 100.

**Cash-flow row by row**
1. Net cash flow = inflows − outflows.
2. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
3. Carry the closing balance forward as next month's opening balance.

## Worked reminders (Calder Cycles)

| | Year A | Year B |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | £250,000 | £275,000 |
| Cost of sales | £160,000 | £187,000 |
| Gross profit | £90,000 | £88,000 |
| Other operating expenses and interest | £60,000 | £55,000 |
| Net profit | £30,000 | £33,000 |
| Gross profit margin | 36% | 32% |
| Net profit margin | 12% | 12% |

Sales revenue rose 10%. Cost of sales rose 16.9%, so gross profit fell by £2,000 and the gross profit margin dropped four percentage points. Lower overheads (down £5,000) lifted net profit to £33,000, so the net profit margin stayed at 12%. Reading: the shop controlled overheads but pays more for the bikes it sells. Look at supplier prices and the selling price.

ARR: a repair workshop costs £20,000 and is expected to earn £4,000, £6,000, £8,000 and £6,000 in four years. Total profit = £24,000. Average annual profit = £6,000. ARR = 6,000 / 20,000 × 100 = **30%**.

## Reading graphs and charts

- Check the title, axis labels, units and the starting value of the axis.
- "Identify" means read the value from the graph or table. Add the unit.
- A line graph shows change over time: describe direction, peak and any turning point.
- A pie chart shows shares of a whole. A 72° sector is 72 / 360 = 20%.
- A chart shows what happened, not why. Say what else you would need to know.

## Marketing data

- Convert survey counts into percentages before comparing.
- Compare cost per result across promotions: total cost / number of responses.
- Think about the sample: how many people, and are they the right people?
- Stated intentions may not turn into purchases.

## Market data

- Market share can fall while sales rise, if the market grows faster than the business.
- A 15% share is changed by percentage points, not percentages: 15% to 13% is two percentage points.
- A rise in a supplier's price is a percentage change in costs: £8.00 to £8.60 is 7.5%.
- Business choices after a cost rise: absorb it, raise the price, or find a cheaper supplier. Each has a risk.

## Use and limitations of financial information

| | In understanding business performance | In making business decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Use | Shows profit, margins, cash, change over time, comparison with a target or a competitor | Compares investments (ARR), sets prices, plans borrowing from a forecast |
| Limitation | Backward-looking; says what, not why; ignores quality, reputation and staff | Forecasts are estimates; non-financial factors matter; figures can be out of date |

Must-know distinctions:

- Profit is not cash. A business can be profitable and still run short of cash.
- Gross profit margin looks at cost of sales only. Net profit margin also includes other operating expenses and interest.
- Percentage change is not percentage points.
- A forecast is an estimate; an actual figure is a record of what happened.
- Support, inform and justify: showing what is happening, backing a choice, and giving the reason for it.

Command words (Appendix 1): **Identify** (read from a graph or table), **Calculate** (workings given), **State** (up to a sentence, from the data given), **Explain** (a fact plus two expansion points, no context), **Analyse** (extended, in the context given), **Justify** (use the information to recommend one of two options), **Evaluate** (reach a supported conclusion).

## Quick self-test

1. A stall's takings on five days were £52, £48, £61, £59 and £55. Calculate the mean.
2. A product's price rises from £8.00 to £8.60. Calculate the percentage increase.
3. A firm has sales of £36,000 in a market worth £240,000. Calculate its market share.
4. A pie chart sector is 72°. What percentage of the whole is it?
5. Opening balance £1,200, cash inflows £9,800, cash outflows £10,500. Calculate the net cash flow and the closing balance.
6. A £20,000 investment gives profits of £4,000, £6,000, £8,000 and £6,000 over four years. Calculate the ARR.
7. Gross profit £90,000, sales revenue £250,000. Calculate the gross profit margin.
8. Net profit £33,000, sales revenue £275,000. Calculate the net profit margin.
9. A firm's cost of sales rose from £160,000 to £187,000. Calculate the percentage change, to 1 decimal place.
10. A firm's sales rose 10% while its market rose 25%. What happened to its market share, and why?
11. State one limitation of using a cash-flow forecast to make a decision.

### Answers

1. Total = 275, so the mean is 275 / 5 = **£55**.
2. (8.60 − 8.00) / 8.00 × 100 = **7.5% increase**.
3. 36,000 / 240,000 × 100 = **15%**.
4. 72 / 360 × 100 = **20%**.
5. Net cash flow = 9,800 − 10,500 = **−£700**; closing balance = 1,200 + (−700) = **£500**.
6. Total profit = £24,000; average = £6,000; 6,000 / 20,000 × 100 = **30%**.
7. 90,000 / 250,000 × 100 = **36%**.
8. 33,000 / 275,000 × 100 = **12%**.
9. (187,000 − 160,000) / 160,000 × 100 = 16.875, so **16.9% increase**.
10. Market share **falls**, because the market grew faster than the business, so rivals gained a larger slice.
11. For example: the forecast is an estimate, so late-paying customers or higher costs could make the real balance different.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing a percentage change by the new figure instead of the original.
- Leaving out units, or the words increase and decrease, on a percentage change.
- Using net profit when the question asks for gross profit, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to divide total profit by the number of years in ARR.
- Reading a chart value from the wrong row or column.
- Describing a graph without using any numbers from it.
- Treating a rise in sales revenue as proof that profit rose.
- Writing limitations in general terms with no link to the business in the case.
- Stopping an "evaluate" or "justify" answer before stating which course of action you back.
