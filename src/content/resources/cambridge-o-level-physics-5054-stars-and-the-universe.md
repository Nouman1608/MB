This guide goes deeper into section **6.2 Stars and the Universe** of Cambridge O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series), the second half of Topic 6, Space physics. The topic is not tiered. For a single overview of the whole topic, see the [Space physics study guide](/resources/o-level-physics-space-physics/). This page concentrates on what examiners reward in 6.2: precise wording, the force-balance reasoning behind a star's life cycle, distance calculations with light-years, and a chain of reasoning from redshift to the Big Bang.

Condensed notes are in the [Stars and the Universe revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-physics-5054-stars-and-the-universe-revision-notes/), and exam-style questions are in the [Stars and the Universe practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-physics-5054-stars-and-the-universe-practice/).

The section is almost entirely "know", "state" and "describe" content, so marks depend on exact terms. The one calculation route is the light-year, which joins this section to the speed of electromagnetic waves in section 3.3.

## The Sun as a star (6.2.1)

Three facts, each with a common trap:

- **Medium size.** The Sun is an ordinary star, not an unusually large or small one. "The biggest star" or "a small star" both lose the mark.
- **Mostly hydrogen and helium.** Name both elements.
- **Infrared, visible and ultraviolet.** These three neighbouring regions of the electromagnetic spectrum carry most of the Sun's radiated energy. In order of increasing frequency (section 3.3) they run infrared, visible, ultraviolet. Naming radio waves or gamma rays here is wrong.

**What powers it.** Stars are powered by **nuclear reactions that release energy**. In stable stars, such as the Sun, the reactions are the **fusion of hydrogen into helium**. Section 5.2.4 defines fusion as the formation of a larger nucleus by combining two smaller nuclei, with the release of energy, and recognises fusion as the energy source for stars. Two confusions to avoid: the Sun is not "burning" in the chemical sense, and it is not powered by fission, which splits large nuclei.

## Galaxies and the light-year (6.2.2, part 1)

State these exactly:

- a galaxy is made up of many billions of stars;
- the Sun is a star in the galaxy called the Milky Way;
- the other stars in the Milky Way are much further from the Earth than the Sun is;
- astronomical distances can be measured in light-years, where **one light-year is the distance travelled in a vacuum by light in one year**.

The definition contains three key ideas (distance, light, one year) and examiners look for all three. "The time light takes to travel a year" is wrong, because a light-year is a distance.

**Worked example 1: converting a light-year to metres.**

1. One year in seconds: 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s.
2. Distance = speed × time = 3.0 × 10⁸ × 3.15 × 10⁷ = **9.5 × 10¹⁵ m**.

**Worked example 2: how much further are other stars?** A nearby star is 4.2 light-years away. In metres that is 4.2 × 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 4.0 × 10¹⁶ m. The Sun is 1.5 × 10¹¹ m away (from the 500 s light time in 6.1). One light-year alone is 9.46 × 10¹⁵ ÷ 1.5 × 10¹¹ ≈ 63 000 times the Sun–Earth distance, which shows numerically why the syllabus says the other stars are "much further away".

**Worked example 3: light time as a distance.** Light from a star takes 25 years to reach the Earth. Its distance is 25 light-years, which is 25 × 9.5 × 10¹⁵ = 2.4 × 10¹⁷ m. Any time light spends travelling converts to a distance in the same way, which is why light-years are convenient: the number of years of travel *is* the distance.

## The life cycle of a star (6.2.2, part 2)

Learn the eight syllabus stages, but understand the **forces** behind them, because "explain" questions are built on those.

**Formation and collapse.** A star forms from an **interstellar cloud of gas and dust that contains hydrogen**. The cloud's own **internal gravitational attraction** pulls it inwards. As it collapses, its temperature increases. A collapsing, heating cloud is a **protostar**.

**Becoming stable.** The protostar becomes a **stable star** when the **inward force of gravitational attraction is balanced by an outward force due to the high temperature in the centre of the star**. At this stage hydrogen is fusing into helium in the centre, and the energy released maintains the high temperature that supplies the outward force. A balance of forces means the star neither collapses nor expands, so it stays a steady size for a long time.

**Running out of hydrogen.** All stars eventually run out of hydrogen as fuel for the nuclear reaction. When **most of the hydrogen in the centre has been converted to helium**, the balance changes and the star expands:

- most stars expand to form **red giants**;
- more massive stars expand to form **red supergiants**.

**The two endings.** The branch depends on the star's mass.

| Less massive star | More massive star |
|---|---|
| Red giant | Red supergiant |
| Forms a **planetary nebula** | Explodes as a **supernova** |
| Leaves a **white dwarf** at its centre | Forms a **nebula containing hydrogen and new heavier elements** |
| | Leaves a **neutron star or a black hole** at its centre |

**Recycling.** The nebula from a supernova may form **new stars with orbiting planets**. Because it contains heavier elements made in the earlier star, the later stars and planets contain those elements too.

**Annotated model answer.** *Question: Describe the life cycle of a star much more massive than the Sun, from the formation of a stable star.* (A six-point answer.)

1. "A protostar becomes stable when the inward gravitational force is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature at its centre." (Balance of named forces.)
2. "In the stable star hydrogen fuses into helium." (Energy source.)
3. "Eventually most of the hydrogen in the centre is converted to helium." (Trigger for the next stage.)
4. "The star expands to form a red supergiant." (Correct branch for a massive star; "red giant" would not score.)
5. "The red supergiant explodes as a supernova, forming a nebula containing hydrogen and new heavier elements." (Both components of the nebula.)
6. "A neutron star or a black hole is left at the centre." (Correct remnant; "white dwarf" would contradict the branch.)

## The Universe and redshift (6.2.3)

**Scale.** The Milky Way is one of **many billions of galaxies** that make up the Universe, and its diameter is approximately **100 000 light-years**, about 9.5 × 10²⁰ m. Do not confuse the galaxy with the Universe.

**Redshift** is an **increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation emitted from receding stars and galaxies**. Three words carry the marks: *increase*, *observed wavelength*, *receding*. The light does not "turn red"; the whole pattern of wavelengths is shifted to longer values.

The syllabus adds a pattern: the light from distant galaxies shows redshift, and **the further away the galaxy, the greater the observed redshift and the faster it is moving away from the Earth**.

**Worked example 4: comparing redshift qualitatively.** A particular line in a laboratory spectrum is at 486 nm. In the light from galaxy P the same line is observed at 501 nm; from galaxy R it is at 530 nm.

- Both wavelengths are longer than 486 nm, so both galaxies show redshift and are receding.
- The increase for R (44 nm) is greater than for P (15 nm), so R shows the greater redshift.
- Therefore R is moving away faster, and R is further away.

You are not required to calculate a speed from a redshift in 5054; comparisons like this are enough.

**Redshift as evidence for the Big Bang (qualitative).** Build the answer as a chain:

1. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted, so the galaxies are moving away from us.
2. The further away a galaxy is, the greater its redshift, so the faster it is moving away.
3. This pattern means the Universe is expanding.
4. If the expansion is traced back in time, all the matter in the Universe was once together in a very small region, which is what the Big Bang theory proposes.

Each link earns credit, so do not jump from "redshift" straight to "Big Bang".

## Common mistakes

- Calling the Sun a large star, or saying it is powered by fission or chemical burning.
- Defining a light-year as a time.
- Giving a less massive star a supernova, or a massive star a white dwarf.
- Writing "red giant" for a massive star, which forms a red supergiant.
- Leaving out "new heavier elements" from the supernova nebula.
- Describing redshift as light becoming red, or forgetting the word "receding".
- Saying the Milky Way is the Universe.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** and **Give**: short, exact phrases from the syllabus ("fusion of hydrogen into helium").
- **Describe** (the life cycle): stages in order, each with its correct name, and the mass branch made explicit.
- **Explain** (why a star is stable): name both forces and say they are balanced.
- **Calculate** (light-years): show the seconds in a year, then distance = speed × time, with units.
- **Compare** (two redshifts): say which is greater and what that implies about speed and distance.
- **Describe, qualitatively** (Big Bang): a linked chain, not a single sentence.
