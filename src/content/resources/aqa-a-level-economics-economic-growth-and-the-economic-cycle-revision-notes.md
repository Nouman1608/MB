## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | Increase in real GDP, at constant prices |
| Short-run growth | Increase in actual output, usually from higher AD using spare capacity |
| Long-run growth | Increase in productive capacity; the trend rate of growth of real output over time |
| Trend rate | Average rate at which potential output grows |
| Positive output gap | Real GDP above the economy's productive potential |
| Negative output gap | Real GDP below the economy's productive potential |
| Economic cycle | Fluctuations of actual output around the trend |
| Shock | Unexpected event that shifts AD or aggregate supply |
| Sustainable growth | Growth that does not reduce future generations' ability to meet their needs |

## Formulas and methods

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | Change in real GDP as a percentage of its starting level |
| Real growth from nominal | (1 + nominal growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1 |
| Output gap (%) | (actual − potential) / potential × 100 |
| Real GDP per person | One plus real GDP growth, divided by one plus population growth, minus one |
| Trend rate over n years | (final / start)^(1/n) − 1 |
| Emissions per unit of output | Emissions / real GDP |

**Method in steps for a gap question**

1. Write the formula.
2. Use potential output as the denominator.
3. Keep the sign: positive means above potential, negative below.
4. State what the sign means for the economy.

**Worked reminders**

```
Gap: actual 612, potential 600 -> (612-600)/600 = +2.0%
Gap: actual 585, potential 600 -> (585-600)/600 = -2.5%
Real growth: nominal +6.0%, prices +2.5% -> 1.060/1.025 - 1 = 3.4%
Per head: GDP +2.4%, population +0.6% -> 1.024/1.006 - 1 = 1.8%
Bubble: index 100 -> 140 -> 91. Fall from peak = (91-140)/140 = -35.0%
```

## Short-run versus long-run growth

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | AD rises (or a temporary supply improvement) | Productive capacity rises |
| PPC | Move from inside the curve towards it | Whole curve shifts outward |
| AD/AS | AD shifts right along SRAS | LRAS shifts right |
| Lasts | Until spare capacity runs out | Permanent if capacity is kept |
| Risk | Inflation and a positive gap | Slow to achieve; costs now, gains later |

PPC points to remember: a point on the curve is full use of resources; inside is unemployment or waste; outside is unattainable until capacity grows. Moving along the curve is opportunity cost, not growth.

## Diagrams in words

- **PPC for long-run growth:** capital goods on the vertical axis, consumer goods on the horizontal. Draw the original curve, then a second curve further from the origin. Label both axes and both curves.
- **PPC for short-run growth:** mark a point inside the curve, then a point on or nearer the curve. The curve itself does not move.
- **AD/AS, short-run growth:** AD shifts right along upward-sloping SRAS. Equilibrium moves to a higher real GDP, with prices up too. If it lies right of LRAS, the gap is positive.
- **AD/AS, long-run growth:** LRAS (and usually SRAS) shifts right. Potential output rises and the price level is lower than it would have been.
- **Shock:** state which curve moves, the direction, and what happens to the price level and real GDP.

## Determinants

**Demand side (short run):** consumption (confidence, wealth, interest rates, taxes, credit), investment (expected profit, interest rates, finance), government spending, and net exports (world growth, exchange rate).

**Supply side (long run):** labour quantity and quality, capital stock, technology, productivity, enterprise, natural resources, infrastructure, competition, and a banking system that channels savings to business investment funds.

**Link:** investment raises AD now and capacity later. Education and training raise labour quality but pay off slowly.

## Costs and benefits of growth

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Individuals | Higher incomes, jobs, choice | Inequality, stress, structural change, skills made obsolete |
| Economy | Tax revenue, lower unemployment, investment | Overheating, inflation, bigger import bill |
| Environment | Funds for green technology | Depletion, pollution, climate damage |

**Evaluation prompts**

- What type of growth is it, and who gains?
- Is output growing faster than population? Check growth per head.
- Is growth driven by demand (short-run) or capacity (long-run)?
- Is it sustainable: does it use renewable inputs, and are emissions falling per unit of output and in total?
- Time: costs may fall on later generations; benefits may arrive sooner.

## The cycle and its indicators

| Phase | Real GDP | Output gap | Inflation | Unemployment | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | Growing fast | Positive, may widen | Rising | Low and falling | Strong |
| Slowdown | Growth slowing | Positive but shrinking | Peaking | Low, starting to rise | Slowing |
| Recession | Falling | Negative | Falling | Rising | Falling sharply |
| Recovery | Growth returns | Still negative until output passes potential | Low | Still high, falls later | Rising |

Use at least two indicators and say which lag. Unemployment often lags the turning point. A common convention defines recession as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP.

## Causes of phase changes

| | Global | Domestic |
|---|---|---|
| Demand shock | Fall in trading partners' incomes; world financial turmoil | Confidence shifts; policy tightening; credit squeeze |
| Supply shock | World commodity price rise; supply chain disruption | Natural disaster; strike; new technology; regulation change |

Chain for a negative demand shock: AD falls, output falls, firms cut jobs and investment, incomes fall, AD falls further. Chain for a negative supply shock: costs rise, SRAS shifts left, price level rises and output falls.

Evaluate by size, duration, and how open or flexible the economy is.

## Cyclical instability

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Excessive credit and debt | Spending runs ahead of income; a rate rise or income fall forces repayment |
| Asset price bubble | Prices exceed fundamentals; a wealth effect lifts spending; a fall causes negative equity |
| Destabilising speculation | Buying because prices rise pushes prices up, then selling because they fall pushes them down |
| Animal spirits | Confidence swings not based on calculation |
| Herding | Copying others amplifies swings |

Boom-bust chain: easy credit, rising asset prices, higher collateral values, more lending, more spending, then a trigger, falling prices, negative equity, cuts in lending and spending, recession.

## Must-know distinctions

- Actual growth versus potential growth.
- Real versus nominal GDP.
- Movement along a PPC versus a shift of a PPC.
- Output gap (a level) versus growth rate (a change).
- Growth in total GDP versus growth per head.
- Demand-side shock versus supply-side shock.
- Global shock versus domestic shock.
- Growth versus sustainable growth.

## Quick self-test

1. Define long-run economic growth.
2. Real GDP is 840 and potential output is 800. Calculate the output gap.
3. Real GDP rises from 400 to 412. Calculate growth.
4. Nominal GDP rises 5.0% while prices rise 2.0%. Calculate real growth, to one decimal place.
5. State two demand-side determinants of short-run growth.
6. State two supply-side determinants of the trend rate.
7. Population grows 1.0% and real GDP 3.0%. Calculate growth per head, to one decimal place.
8. What shift on a PPC shows long-run growth?
9. Give an example of a global supply-side shock.
10. Asset prices rise 100 to 160 then fall to 120. Calculate the fall from the peak.
11. Why might a recovery still have a negative output gap?
12. Explain herding in one sentence.

### Answers

1. An increase in the productive capacity of the economy; the trend rate of growth of real output over time.
2. (840 − 800)/800 × 100 = **+5.0%**, a positive gap.
3. (412 − 400)/400 × 100 = **3.0%**.
4. 1.050/1.020 − 1 = **2.9%**.
5. Any two of: consumer confidence, interest rates, investment, government spending, exports.
6. Any two of: labour quality, capital stock, technology, productivity, infrastructure.
7. 1.030/1.010 − 1 = **2.0%** (1.98% unrounded).
8. An outward shift of the whole curve.
9. A rise in world commodity prices raising firms' costs.
10. (120 − 160)/160 × 100 = **−25.0%**.
11. Output is growing again but is still below potential, so the gap is not yet closed.
12. Individuals copy what others do, so trends in buying or selling are amplified.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing growth for any increase in output and ignoring the short-run and long-run distinction.
- Dividing an output gap by actual GDP instead of potential.
- Dropping the sign of a negative gap.
- Using nominal figures when asked about real growth.
- Drawing a PPC shift for a recovery from recession.
- Naming a shock but not saying whether it moves AD, SRAS or LRAS.
- Describing a bubble without a chain of reasoning to spending and output.
- Giving costs and benefits as lists, with no judgement about size, timing or who is affected.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between actual and potential growth?**
Actual growth is a rise in real output; potential growth is a rise in the economy's productive capacity.

**What is the difference between an output gap and a growth rate?**
An output gap is a level, the difference between actual and potential output at a point in time. A growth rate is a change in output over time.

**What is the difference between a global and a domestic shock?**
A global shock starts abroad, such as a fall in trading partners' incomes or a rise in world commodity prices. A domestic shock starts at home, such as a collapse in consumer confidence or a natural disaster.

**What is sustainable growth?**
Growth is sustainable if it can continue without damaging the ability of future generations to meet their needs, for example through pollution or resource depletion.
