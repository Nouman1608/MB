**How to use this set.** On Paper 2 you answer "one passage-based question and one essay question",
without the text in the room. These questions practise the essay. Because set plays change, the
longer questions use an invented play, summarised below with the short quotations a candidate might
have learned.

**The invented play: *The Quarry*** *(original summary for practice)*

- **Act 1.** In a mining town, Daniel, a young foreman, is promoted by the quarry owner, Mr Hale.
  Daniel's father, Tom, a union man, warns him: "They promote you to divide us." Daniel replies,
  "They promote me because I'm good."
- **Act 2, Scene 1.** Daniel discovers that the quarry's safety checks have been faked. Hale tells
  him, "A wise man sees what he is paid to see." Daniel says nothing. The scene ends with him alone,
  turning his new foreman's badge over in his hands.
- **Act 2, Scene 2.** At home, Tom asks whether the rumours about the checks are true. Daniel lies:
  "Everything's in order." The audience has seen the faked papers.
- **Act 3, Scene 1.** A rockfall injures Tom. At the hospital, Daniel speaks alone to his sleeping
  father: "I saw it, and I looked away."
- **Act 3, Scene 2.** Daniel gives the faked papers to the inspectors. He removes his badge and leaves
  it on Hale's desk. Final stage direction: *"The quarry siren sounds, then stops. Silence."*

## Section A

**1.** Underline (or list) the focus, the key word and the instruction in this title: "To what extent
does the playwright present Daniel as a coward?" **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between what "Explore the ways in which…" and "What do you think…?"
ask for in a drama essay. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** reasons why essay evidence should come from across the whole play. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what a "precise reference" is and write one for *The Quarry* that does not quote. **[2]**

**5.** A candidate cannot remember the exact words of a line. Explain what they should and should not
do. **[2]**

**6.** Rewrite this weak topic sentence so that it answers the question "How does the playwright make
the relationship between Daniel and Tom so moving?": "In Act 2, Scene 2, Tom asks Daniel about the
rumours." **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Write a one-sentence answer to: "To what extent does the playwright present Daniel as a
coward?" **[2]**

**8.** Write a five-point plan for the question in Question 7, using contrasting views, with one
quotation or precise reference and one dramatic method for each body point. **[8]**

**9.** Explain how the playwright uses dramatic irony in Act 2, Scene 2, and how this affects the
audience's view of Daniel. **[4]**

**10.** Explore how the playwright uses the foreman's badge as a recurring prop. **[6]**

**11.** Write a conclusion (three to four sentences) to an essay on: "How does the playwright make the
ending of *The Quarry* so powerful?" **[4]**

**12.** A candidate writes an essay on Daniel that retells each act in order and ends: "This shows
Daniel is an interesting character." Give **three** pieces of advice to improve it, explaining why
each would raise the mark. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Focus: Daniel [1]. Key word: "coward" [1]. Instruction: "To what extent", which requires a
judgement weighing evidence for and against [1].

**2.** "Explore the ways in which…" asks for detailed analysis of the playwright's methods in creating
an effect, with personal response implied [1]; "What do you think…?" asks directly for your own view,
which should be stated clearly and supported throughout [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: it shows knowledge of the whole text (AO1); it allows you to show how a
character or theme develops; it gives a more balanced basis for a judgement; questions are set on the
play as a whole. Maximum 2.

**4.** A specific reference to a moment without exact quotation [1], for example: "when Daniel leaves
his badge on Hale's desk in the final scene" [1].

**5.** They should use a precise reference to the moment instead [1]; they should not invent or guess
a quotation, because inaccurate wording weakens AO1 [1].

**6.** A topic sentence that answers the question [1] and names the effect or method [1], for example:
"The relationship becomes most moving when Daniel lies to his father, because the audience has seen
the faked papers and knows the trust Tom places in him is misplaced."

**7.** A clear judgement [1] that acknowledges both sides [1], for example: "The playwright presents
Daniel as a coward for most of the play, when he stays silent and lies to protect his promotion, but
the ending recasts that cowardice as the starting point of real courage."

**8.** One mark for a clear introduction with a judgement [1]; one for a conclusion that resolves the
judgement [1]; up to two marks for each of three body points with evidence and a method, [2] each
[6]. For example:
1. *Introduction:* largely cowardly, but redeemed at the end.
2. *For cowardice:* silence when Hale says "A wise man sees what he is paid to see"; method: his
   silence is a dramatic choice, and the scene ends with him alone turning the badge.
3. *For cowardice:* the lie "Everything's in order"; method: dramatic irony, since the audience has
   seen the papers.
4. *Against:* "I saw it, and I looked away"; method: a speech to his sleeping father works like a
   soliloquy, revealing guilt and honesty to the audience.
5. *Conclusion:* the final act, giving the papers to the inspectors and leaving the badge, shows
   courage at personal cost, so cowardice is a stage he passes through rather than his nature.

**9.** The audience has seen the faked papers, so it knows the checks are not "in order" when Daniel
says they are [1]. Tom's trust in his son becomes painful to watch [1]. The irony makes the audience
judge Daniel more harshly, because he lies to the father who warned him [1], but it may also create
pity, since the audience understands the pressure Hale has put on him [1].

**10.** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- The badge represents the promotion Tom warned against, so it stands for the division between
  father and son [2].
- At the end of Act 2, Scene 1, Daniel "turning his new foreman's badge over in his hands" is a
  silent image of his choice between loyalty to Hale and to the truth [2].
- Leaving the badge on Hale's desk reverses the opening: the reward that divided him from his class
  is given back [2].
- Because it is a visual prop, the audience can follow Daniel's moral journey without his explaining
  it, which suits a character who often stays silent [2].
Maximum 6.

**11.** A judgement on "powerful" [1]; reference to the final action or stage direction [1]; a link
to the play's development [1]; a personal response [1]. For example: "The ending is powerful because
Daniel finally acts rather than speaks: he returns the badge without explanation. The siren that
'sounds, then stops' leaves the audience in silence, recalling the danger that injured Tom and the
silence that allowed it. For me, this wordless ending is more forceful than any speech, because it
shows a man who once 'looked away' now choosing to be seen."

**12.** Three pieces of advice, [2] each (one for the advice, one for why it raises the mark):
- Organise by argument, not by act [1], because a developing argument that answers the question is
  rewarded more than retelling (AO2, AO4) [1].
- Use short quotations and precise references with analysis of dramatic methods [1], because this
  gives AO1 evidence and AO3 analysis that retelling lacks [1].
- Replace the vague ending with a judgement that uses the question's key word [1], because a
  considered personal response is rewarded under AO4 [1].
- Write about the audience and staging, such as the badge and the siren [1], because it shows
  understanding of the text as drama [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering a different question from the one asked.
- Evidence from one act only.
- Strong analysis with no final judgement.
- Paragraphs that open with plot rather than argument.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why use an invented play?**
So that you practise planning and argument on material you can see in full; apply the same steps to
your own set plays.

**How many quotations should I learn per play?**
Enough short, flexible quotations to cover the main characters and themes across the whole play,
together with precise references to key moments.
