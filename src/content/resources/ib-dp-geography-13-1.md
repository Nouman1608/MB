## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how global interactions create geopolitical and economic risks.
- Examine how these risks are transmitted between places and why exposure is uneven.
- Analyse the effects of risks on sovereignty, development and security.
- Evaluate how states and other actors respond to them.

## Weaponised interdependence

Unit 4 showed that interdependence creates power. Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman, writing in 2019, used the term **weaponised interdependence** for the way states that control central nodes in global networks can use them against others. Two mechanisms matter:

- **Watching.** A state that hosts the hubs through which information or money passes can monitor what flows through them.
- **Blocking.** It can cut others off from those hubs, for example by excluding banks from international payment systems or restricting exports of key technologies.

This turns the networks of 11.2 into a source of geopolitical risk: the more a country depends on networks controlled by others, the more exposed it is to their decisions. It also explains why some states try to build alternative payment systems, supply chains and digital infrastructure.

## How sovereign debt becomes dangerous

Governments borrow to invest and to cover deficits. Debt becomes a risk when the cost of servicing it rises faster than the income available to pay it. Three triggers are common:

1. **Falling export earnings**, for example when commodity prices drop or tourism collapses.
2. **Currency depreciation**, because debts owed in foreign currencies become more expensive in local money.
3. **Rising global interest rates**, which increase the cost of new borrowing and of debts with variable rates.

### Worked example: two shocks at once

Figures are invented for Country D, whose foreign debt is owed in dollars.

```
Before the shock
  Export earnings                 20 billion dollars a year
  Debt service due                 5 billion dollars a year
  Debt service ratio               5 / 20 x 100 = 25%

Shock 1: export earnings fall by 30%
  New export earnings              20 x 0.7 = 14 billion dollars
  Debt service ratio               5 / 14 x 100 = 35.7%

Shock 2: the currency falls from 100 to 150 local units per dollar
  Debt service in local money      before: 5 billion x 100 = 500 billion units
                                   after:  5 billion x 150 = 750 billion units
  Increase                         (750 - 500) / 500 x 100 = 50%
```

Neither the debt nor the interest rate has changed, yet Country D must now use over a third of its export earnings to service debt, and its government must raise 50 per cent more local revenue to buy the dollars it needs. The lesson to write: external shocks transmitted through trade and currency markets can turn manageable debt into a crisis, which is why countries with narrow export bases and foreign-currency debt are most exposed.

## Sri Lanka, 2022

Sri Lanka shows how several risks can combine. Tourism, a major source of foreign currency, was hit by the Easter Sunday bombings of 2019 and then by the COVID-19 pandemic. Tax cuts reduced government revenue, and a sudden ban on chemical fertilisers in 2021 damaged harvests. Foreign-currency reserves ran down, and in 2022 the country could not pay for imports of fuel, food and medicine. In April 2022 the government announced that it would suspend payments on its foreign debt, its first default since independence. Mass protests followed, and in July 2022 President Gotabaya Rajapaksa fled the country and resigned. A rescue programme with the IMF was agreed in 2023, with conditions on taxes and spending.

The case links geopolitical risk (terrorism and political instability), economic risk (debt, currency and import dependence) and poor policy choices, and shows how a crisis affects households through shortages and inflation.

## A contested debate: lending for infrastructure

Large loans for infrastructure, some from China, have raised questions about sovereignty. In 2017 Sri Lanka leased the port of Hambantota, built with Chinese loans, to a Chinese state-owned company for 99 years. Some commentators described this as **debt-trap diplomacy**: lending designed to gain strategic assets when borrowers cannot repay. Other researchers, including Deborah Brautigam, have argued that the evidence does not support a deliberate strategy, pointing out that the port project was proposed by Sri Lanka and that most of its debt was owed to other lenders.

For an exam, the value of the case is that it is contested. Present both interpretations, identify the evidence each relies on, and reach a judgement about how far debt has reduced the borrower's sovereignty.

## Analysing exposure

When comparing how exposed two places are to a geopolitical or economic risk, ask:

| Question | Higher exposure if... |
|---|---|
| How concentrated are its exports? | One commodity or service dominates |
| In what currency is its debt? | Mostly foreign currency |
| How large are its reserves? | Reserves cover only a few months of imports |
| How diversified are its suppliers? | Key imports come from one country or route |
| How strong are its institutions? | Weak governance and limited policy options |

## Annotated model answer

*Examine why some countries are more exposed than others to global economic risks.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Countries that depend on a single export are exposed because a fall in its price cuts national income at once, as Venezuela found when oil prices fell from 2014 **[first factor, located]**. Exposure grows where debt is owed in foreign currency, since a falling exchange rate raises the cost of every repayment in local money **[mechanism explained]**. Sri Lanka in 2022 combined both weaknesses with reliance on tourism and low reserves, so a series of shocks led to default and shortages **[factors interacting]**. Exposure is therefore greatest where narrow export bases, foreign-currency debt and weak policy choices coincide, not where any one of them exists alone **[examined conclusion]**."

## Common mistakes

- Describing a crisis without explaining the mechanism that spread it.
- Treating debt as dangerous in itself, rather than in relation to income, currency and interest rates.
- Presenting "debt-trap diplomacy" as established fact rather than a contested interpretation.
- Ignoring domestic policy choices when explaining a crisis.
- Forgetting the effects on households, such as shortages and inflation.

## Exam technique

- **Examine** questions on risk reward tracing transmission: source, mechanism, places affected and responses.
- **Discuss** contested cases by setting out each interpretation and its evidence.
- **Evaluate** responses, such as IMF programmes, against their effects on stability and on vulnerable groups.
- **To what extent** questions on interdependence need a position on whether it increases or reduces risk overall.
- Use dated, located evidence and show calculations clearly when data are provided.
