## What you must be able to do

- Describe **alkanes** as saturated hydrocarbons.
- Describe **petroleum** as a mixture of mainly alkanes.
- Explain how **fractional distillation** separates it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hydrocarbon | Compound of carbon and hydrogen only |
| Saturated | Only single bonds between carbon atoms |
| Alkane | Saturated hydrocarbon, general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ |
| Mixture | Substances not chemically combined; keep their own properties |
| Fraction | Mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points |
| London forces | Weak forces between non-polar molecules from temporary dipoles |

## Alkane data

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | –162 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | –89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | –42 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | –1 |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 |
| Hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 69 |
| Octane | C₈H₁₈ | 126 |
| Decane | C₁₀H₂₂ | 174 |

## How the column works

1. Crude oil is heated; most of it vaporises.
2. Vapour enters the bottom of the column.
3. The column is **hot at the bottom, cooler at the top**.
4. Vapours rise and cool; each condenses where the temperature is below its boiling point.
5. Liquids are drawn off from trays at different heights.
6. The shortest molecules leave at the top as gases; the longest stay at the bottom.

It is a **physical** process. No covalent bonds break.

## Fractions

| Fraction | Carbons | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Refinery gases | 1 to 4 | Heating and cooking |
| Petrol | 5 to 10 | Car fuel |
| Naphtha | 7 to 14 | Chemical feedstock |
| Kerosene | 10 to 16 | Jet fuel |
| Diesel | 14 to 20 | Diesel engines |
| Fuel oil | 20 to 70 | Ships, power stations |
| Bitumen | Over 70 | Roads |

Higher in the column: lower boiling point, shorter chain, more volatile and flammable, less viscous.

## Explaining boiling point trends

**Chain length:**
1. Longer molecule means more electrons and a larger surface area.
2. Stronger London forces between molecules.
3. More energy is needed to separate them.
4. Higher boiling point.

**Branching:** branched isomers are more compact, with less surface contact, so London forces are weaker and boiling points are lower (hexane 69 °C; 2,2-dimethylbutane 50 °C).

## Worked examples

**Order of removal.** Propane, octane and hexadecane: from top to bottom, propane, octane, hexadecane (increasing boiling point).

**Mᵣ.** Decane C₁₀H₂₂: 10 × 12.0 + 22 × 1.0 = 142.0.

**Which has the higher boiling point, C₈H₁₈ or C₁₂H₂₆?** C₁₂H₂₆, because its molecules have more electrons and stronger London forces.

## Laboratory version

- Crude oil substitute in a flask, heated with a water bath or electric heater.
- Fractionating column packed with glass beads gives a large surface for condensation.
- Thermometer at the top shows the vapour temperature.
- Collect fractions over temperature ranges; the temperature rises as heavier fractions distil.

## Properties that change with chain length

| Property | Short chain (top of column) | Long chain (bottom of column) |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling point | Low | High |
| Volatility | High | Low |
| Viscosity | Low (runs easily) | High (thick) |
| Ease of ignition | Easy | Difficult |
| Flame | Clean, blue | Smoky, yellow (more soot) |

These trends all come from the strength of the London forces between molecules. They explain why short-chain fractions are used as fuels for engines, and why long-chain fractions are used for lubricating oils, fuel oil and bitumen.

## Why we need more than distillation

- Crude oil contains more long-chain hydrocarbons than the market demands.
- Fractional distillation only separates what is already there. It does not change the molecules.
- The surplus is converted into shorter molecules by cracking (see the [Modification of alkanes by cracking revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-modification-of-alkanes-by-cracking-revision-notes/)).
- Crude oil is a finite resource, so efficient use matters.

## Describing the apparatus in an exam

1. Heated flask or furnace: vaporises the mixture.
2. Fractionating column: hotter at the bottom, with trays or packing.
3. Condenser: cools vapour that reaches the top.
4. Collection vessels: one for each fraction.
5. Thermometer at the top: shows the vapour temperature in the laboratory version.

## Answer frames

- **Why is crude oil separated into fractions?** The fractions have different uses, and a mixture of all the hydrocarbons would not be suitable as a fuel for any one purpose.
- **Why do the fractions leave at different heights?** Each condenses where the temperature falls below its boiling point.
- **Why is it called fractional distillation?** Distillation separates by boiling point, and fractional refers to collecting fractions rather than a single pure compound.

## Exam traps

- "Alkanes are unsaturated."
- Crude oil called a compound.
- Covalent bonds said to break in distillation.
- "Stronger bonds" instead of "stronger London forces".
- Column hotter at the top.
- Fraction described as a single pure substance.
- Distillation confused with cracking.
- "Intermolecular bonds" instead of forces.

11. Which is more volatile, hexane or decane?

### Answers

1. A compound containing hydrogen and carbon only.
2. Only single bonds between carbon atoms.
3. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
4. It contains many hydrocarbons that are not chemically combined.
5. Near the bottom, where it is hottest.
6. More electrons and a larger surface area give stronger London forces.
7. It is more compact, with less surface contact and weaker London forces.
8. A mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points.
9. Kerosene.
10. No; only London forces between molecules are overcome.
11. Hexane.
12. It contains hydrocarbons of different chain lengths with a range of boiling points.
