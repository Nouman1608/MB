**2.** Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, which section gives the short-term right to reject goods? **[1]**

**3.** Which statement about misrepresentation is correct? **[1]**
A An opinion is always a statement of fact

## Section C - applied questions

**8.** *Elif buys a new washing machine from Brightway Ltd for 500 pounds. After three weeks it floods her
kitchen. Brightway says she must accept a repair and points to a notice in the shop: "No refunds in any
circumstances."* Advise Elif of her rights under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[10]**

**9.** *Marcus sells his café to Lin. He tells her it "takes about 2,000 pounds a week". He honestly believes
this because takings were that high two years ago, but he has the recent accounts showing 1,200 pounds a
week and has not checked them. Lin buys the café and loses money.* Advise Lin. **[10]**

## Section D - extended problem and evaluation

**10.** *Northgate Logistics agrees to transport Harlow Toys' Christmas stock for 20,000 pounds, delivery
by 1 December. Northgate's standard terms, signed by Harlow, exclude "all liability for late delivery".
On 15 November Northgate demands 30,000 pounds, saying it will not deliver otherwise. Harlow, which has
no time to find another carrier, agrees but later refuses to pay the extra 10,000 pounds. The goods
arrive on 10 December, and Harlow loses a lucrative contract with a retailer that Northgate knew
nothing about.*

Advise Harlow, and evaluate whether the law strikes a fair balance between freedom of contract and
protection of the weaker party. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** B. **[1]**

**2.** B (s20, exercised within the 30-day period in s22). **[1]**

**3.** B. **[1]**

**4.** Payment of a smaller sum on the due date is not consideration for a promise to forgive the rest
(Pinnel's Case, 1602; Foakes v Beer, 1884) **[1]**. Exceptions: payment early, in a different place or with
something extra at the creditor's request **[1]**. Promissory estoppel (Central London Property Trust v
High Trees House, 1947) prevents a creditor going back on a clear promise the debtor relied on **[1]**, but
it is a shield not a sword (Combe v Combe, 1951) **[1]** and is unavailable if the promise was extracted by
pressure (D and C Builders v Rees, 1966) **[1]**.

**5.** A condition is a vital term; breach allows termination and damages (Poussard v Spiers, 1876) **[2]**.
A warranty is a minor term; breach allows damages only (Bettini v Gye, 1876) **[1]**. An innominate term is
classified by the consequences of the breach: termination if the innocent party is deprived of
substantially the whole benefit, otherwise damages (Hong Kong Fir, 1962) **[2]**.

**6.** Economic duress is illegitimate pressure, usually a threat to break a contract, which is a
significant cause of the victim entering or varying a contract, leaving no practical alternative **[2]**
(Atlas Express v Kafco, 1989; DSND Subsea, 2000) **[1]**. The contract is voidable and the victim may
rescind **[1]**, but must act promptly, as affirmation bars rescission (The Atlantic Baron, 1979) **[1]**.

**7.** Frustration occurs when, after formation and without either party's fault, an event makes
performance impossible (Taylor v Caldwell, 1863), illegal, or radically different from what was agreed
(Krell v Henry, 1903) **[2]**. It does not apply where performance is merely more expensive (Davis
Contractors v Fareham UDC, 1956), the event was foreseen or provided for, or it was self-induced (Maritime
National Fish, 1935) **[2]**. The contract ends automatically and the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts)
Act 1943 adjusts payments **[1]**.

**8. [10]**
- Elif is a consumer and Brightway a trader, so the CRA 2015 applies.
- s9: goods must be of satisfactory quality; a machine that floods after three weeks is not.
- s20 and s22: the short-term right to reject lasts 30 days from delivery; at three weeks Elif is
  within time and can reject for a full refund.
- She does not have to accept a repair; s23 repair or replacement is an alternative she may choose.
- If she chooses repair and it fails, s24 gives a price reduction or final right to reject.
- The notice: s31 makes a term excluding s9 rights not binding on the consumer.
- She may also claim damages for the flooded kitchen as a natural consequence of the breach (Hadley v
  Baxendale, 1854).
- Conclusion: Elif can reject and claim a refund plus damages.

**9. [10]**
- Statement of fact: past and present takings are fact; Marcus has superior knowledge, so it is not a mere
  opinion (Smith v Land and House Property, 1884).
- False: current takings are 1,200 pounds.
- Inducement: the takings were material to buying a café (Redgrave v Hurd, 1881; contrast Attwood v
  Small, 1838).
- Type: Marcus honestly believes it, so not fraudulent (Derry v Peek, 1889); but he had the accounts and
  could not prove reasonable grounds for belief - negligent under s2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967
  (Howard Marine v Ogden, 1978). Also a failure to correct an out-of-date statement (With v O'Flanagan,
  1936).
- Remedies: rescission if not barred by delay or affirmation; damages on the fraud measure (Royscot
  Trust v Rogerson, 1991).

**10. [30]** Indicative content:

*Extra 10,000 pounds.* Northgate was already bound to deliver for 20,000 pounds; performing an existing
duty is not consideration (Stilk v Myrick, 1809) unless Harlow obtained a practical benefit (Williams v
Roffey, 1991). Even if it did, the threat not to deliver at Christmas is illegitimate pressure leaving no
practical alternative - economic duress (Atlas Express v Kafco, 1989, on very similar facts). Harlow
protested by refusing to pay, so it has not affirmed. The variation is voidable.

*Late delivery and the exclusion clause.* Delivery by 1 December is likely a condition or serious
innominate term; late delivery is breach. Incorporation: signature binds (L'Estrange v Graucob, 1934).
Construction: the clause clearly covers lateness. UCTA 1977 s3 applies because Harlow dealt on Northgate's
standard terms, so the clause is effective only if reasonable; relevant factors include bargaining power
and whether Harlow could insure. A total exclusion for a time-critical service may be unreasonable.

*Damages.* Harlow can recover losses arising naturally from late delivery of Christmas stock. The lost
retail contract was not known to Northgate and is too remote (Victoria Laundry, 1949). Harlow must
mitigate.

*Evaluation.* Classical freedom of contract enforces what parties agree; but UCTA, CRA 2015, economic
duress and the remoteness rule show the law protecting the weaker party. Duress is still narrow and
uncertain (Times Travel, 2021); UCTA's reasonableness test gives flexibility but less certainty. A
reasoned conclusion should follow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not identifying the type of misrepresentation before stating the remedy.
- Missing the time limits that decide which CRA remedy is available.
- Treating a signed clause as automatically valid without UCTA or CRA analysis.
- Evaluation that is generic rather than tied to cases and statutes.

## Frequently asked questions

**How should I plan an AQA contract problem?**
List each legal issue in the order it arises, then for each
give the rule, authority, application and a mini-conclusion. Finish with overall advice.

**Will I be asked about theory?**
Yes. The specification lists theory topics such as freedom of contract,
the postal rule and the effectiveness of remedies, and these feed into evaluation marks.

**Are consumer and business contracts treated differently?**
Yes. Consumers rely on the CRA 2015; business
contracts on UCTA 1977 and the common law, so identify the parties first.
