These questions cover section 3.3 Planning of Unit 3: Practical Skills in Physics I, Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics (YPH11), Issue 3. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻².

## Section A

**1.** State the resolution of Vernier calipers and of a micrometer screw gauge, and the uncertainty in a single reading from each. **[2]**

**2.** A student plans to measure the thickness of a sheet of card, about 0.8 mm, with a metre rule. Calculate the percentage uncertainty, then name a better instrument and calculate the percentage uncertainty it would give. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a systematic error and a random error, and give one way of reducing each. **[4]**

**4.** A student plans to find the stiffness k of a spring by adding masses and measuring the extension. Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable and **one** control variable. **[3]**

**5.** Discuss whether repeat readings are appropriate when (i) timing a ball falling through a tall tube of oil with a stopwatch, and (ii) reading the length of a wire that the student has set to 0.500 m with a metre rule. **[3]**

**6.** Describe how a student should check a digital voltmeter before using it. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Plan an experiment to determine the speed of sound in air using a signal generator, a loudspeaker, a microphone and a 2-beam oscilloscope. Your plan should include the measurements, how you would make them precise, how the data would be used and one safety point. **[8]**

**8.** Plan an experiment to investigate how the current in a filament lamp varies with the potential difference across it, from 0 V to 12 V. Include the circuit, the instruments with suitable ranges, the method, one safety precaution and how the results would be presented. **[8]**

**9.** A student plans to determine the wavelength of light from a laser using a diffraction grating and a screen 1.5 m away.

**(a)** Identify **two** sources of uncertainty and explain how each may be reduced. **[4]**
**(b)** Identify one hazard and a precaution. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on one use of diffraction gratings and its context. **[2]**

**10.** A student plans to find the density of copper from a rod about 8 mm in diameter and 500 mm long, with a mass of about 225 g.

**(a)** Choose an instrument for each of the diameter, the length and the mass, and calculate the percentage uncertainty in a single reading for the diameter and the length. **[5]**
**(b)** State one calibration check the student should make. **[1]**

**11.** A student writes this plan to find the e.m.f. and internal resistance of a cell. "Connect the cell to a variable resistor, an analogue ammeter and an analogue voltmeter. Set the resistor to three values and record I and V with the switch closed throughout. Calculate r from one pair of readings." The voltmeter reads 0.05 V when disconnected. Identify **four** weaknesses and suggest an improvement for each. **[8]**

**12.** A student will hang masses on a long copper wire and measure its extension, to find the Young modulus. Describe how the data collected will be used to find the Young modulus. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Vernier 0.1 mm, uncertainty 0.05 mm [1]; micrometer 0.01 mm, uncertainty 0.005 mm [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Metre rule: 0.5 ÷ 0.8 × 100% = **63%** [1]. A micrometer screw gauge [1]: 0.005 ÷ 0.8 × 100% = **0.63%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A good alternative is to measure a stack of 20 sheets with a micrometer or Vernier and divide by 20.

**3.** A systematic error shifts every reading by the same amount or in the same direction [1], such as a zero error; it is reduced by calibrating or correcting the instrument [1]. A random error causes unpredictable scatter about the true value [1]; it is reduced by repeating readings and taking a mean, or by plotting a graph [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Independent: the load (mass or weight) added [1]. Dependent: the extension [1]. Control: the same spring throughout, or the same temperature [1]. **[3]**

**5.** (i) Yes: the start and stop depend on the student's reaction and judgement, so random scatter is likely [1]; take at least three and use the mean [1]. (ii) No: the length is set by the student, so repeating the same rule reading adds nothing [1]. **[3]**

**6.** With the leads connected together, or not connected to anything, check it reads zero [1]; if not, note the offset and subtract it from every reading [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Connect the signal generator to the loudspeaker and to one channel of the oscilloscope; the microphone goes to the other channel [1]. Set a frequency, for example 2.0 kHz, and read f from the generator, checked against the period on the oscilloscope [1]. Move the microphone along a metre rule fixed on the bench until the two traces are in phase, and note the position [1]. Move it further, counting several more in-phase positions, for example five, and measure the total distance [1]; dividing by the number of wavelengths reduces the percentage uncertainty in λ [1]. Repeat for at least five frequencies [1]. Plot λ against 1/f; the gradient is the speed of sound v, since v = fλ [1]. Safety: keep the amplitude low to avoid exposure to loud sound [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Counting several wavelengths and plotting a graph both earn credit because they reduce uncertainty.

**8.** Circuit: lamp in series with an ammeter, voltmeter in parallel with the lamp [1], supplied through a variable resistor connected as a potential divider so the p.d. can start from 0 V [1]. Voltmeter range 0–20 V and ammeter range suited to the lamp, for example 0–1 A, chosen from the lamp's rating [1]. Increase V in steps of about 1 V from 0 to 12 V, giving at least ten readings [1], and wait for each reading to settle as the filament heats [1]. Repeat with the supply connections reversed to obtain negative values [1]. Safety: the lamp becomes hot, so do not touch it until it has cooled [1]. Plot I against V; the curve shows how resistance V/I changes [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A potential divider is needed because a series variable resistor cannot reduce the p.d. to zero.

**9. (a)** Locating the centre of each spot on the screen [1]: measure between the same order on both sides of the centre and halve it [1]. Measuring the 1.5 m distance with a tape [1]: keep the tape taut and perpendicular to the screen, using a set square at the screen [1].
**(b)** The laser beam can damage the eye [1]: never look along the beam and remove reflective objects from the bench [1].
**(c)** Diffraction gratings are used in spectrometers to separate light into wavelengths [1], which lets scientists identify the elements present in a star or a sample from their spectral lines [1]. **[8]**

**10. (a)** Diameter: Vernier calipers or micrometer [1]; with Vernier, 0.05 ÷ 8 × 100% = **0.63%** [1]. Length: metre rule [1]; 0.5 ÷ 500 × 100% = **0.1%** [1]. Mass: top-pan balance reading to 0.1 g or better [1].
**(b)** Close the Vernier or micrometer jaws and check for a zero error, or check the balance reads zero with nothing on it [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The diameter has the largest percentage uncertainty, so it deserves the most precise instrument and readings at several places.

**11.** Only three values of R [1]: use at least six, spread across the range [1]. The switch is closed throughout, so the cell warms and r changes [1]: open the switch between readings [1]. The voltmeter has a zero error of 0.05 V [1]: subtract 0.05 V from every reading or reset the zero [1]. r from one pair of readings [1]: plot V against I; the intercept gives ε and the gradient −r [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Calculate stress = F/A, where F = mg and A = πd²/4 from the mean diameter measured with a micrometer [1]. Calculate strain = extension ÷ original length [1]. Plot stress against strain [1]. The gradient of the straight-line region, below the limit of proportionality, is the Young modulus [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming an instrument without saying what it measures or its resolution.
- Writing "be careful" instead of naming a hazard and a precaution.
- Saying a series variable resistor can take the p.d. down to zero.
- Measuring a single wavelength or a single spot distance instead of several.
- Calculating a result from one pair of readings instead of a graph.
- Ignoring a stated zero error.
