Section 12.5 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has four learning outcomes. You must be able to describe tests for seven anions (carbonate; the halides chloride, bromide and iodide; nitrate; sulfate; and sulfite); tests with aqueous sodium hydroxide and aqueous ammonia for eight aqueous cations (aluminium, ammonium, calcium, chromium(III), copper(II), iron(II), iron(III) and zinc); tests for six gases (ammonia, carbon dioxide, chlorine, hydrogen, oxygen and sulfur dioxide); and flame tests for six cations (lithium, sodium, potassium, calcium, barium and copper(II)). Notes for use in qualitative analysis are provided in the practical paper and the Alternative to Practical, but the theory papers expect you to know the tests.

The broader [Identification of Ions and Gases guide](/resources/identification-of-ions-and-gases/) lists the results. This guide concentrates on the reasoning behind the tests: why each reagent is chosen, how to tell look-alike results apart, and how to combine results into the name of a compound.

## Anion tests and why they are done this way

| Anion | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | add dilute acid; test the gas with limewater | effervescence; limewater turns milky (carbon dioxide) |
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous silver nitrate | white precipitate |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | as for chloride | cream precipitate |
| Iodide, I⁻ | as for chloride | yellow precipitate |
| Sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous barium nitrate | white precipitate |
| Nitrate, NO₃⁻ | add aqueous sodium hydroxide, then aluminium foil; warm carefully | ammonia given off (damp red litmus turns blue) |
| Sulfite, SO₃²⁻ | add a small volume of acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | purple to colourless |

**Why acidify first?** Carbonate ions would also form precipitates with silver ions and barium ions. Adding dilute nitric acid first removes any carbonate as carbon dioxide, so a precipitate that forms afterwards must come from a halide or a sulfate.

**Why nitric acid and not another acid?** Hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions, which give a white precipitate with silver nitrate whatever the sample contains. Sulfuric acid would add sulfate ions, which give a white precipitate with barium nitrate. Nitric acid adds only nitrate ions, which form no precipitate with either reagent.

**The nitrate test and ammonium ions.** Ammonium ions also give ammonia when warmed with sodium hydroxide. So warm the sample with sodium hydroxide **before** adding aluminium. If ammonia is given off at that stage, ammonium ions are present; ammonia that appears only after the aluminium is added comes from nitrate.

## Cation tests: reading the pattern

Add the reagent a few drops at a time, note any precipitate and its colour, then add excess and note whether the precipitate dissolves.

| Cation | Aqueous sodium hydroxide | Aqueous ammonia |
|---|---|---|
| Al³⁺ | white ppt., soluble in excess, colourless solution | white ppt., insoluble in excess |
| NH₄⁺ | ammonia given off on warming | — |
| Ca²⁺ | white ppt., insoluble in excess | no ppt. or very slight white ppt. |
| Cr³⁺ | green ppt., soluble in excess | green ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Cu²⁺ | light blue ppt., insoluble in excess | light blue ppt., soluble in excess, dark blue solution |
| Fe²⁺ | green ppt., insoluble in excess, turns brown near surface on standing | same as with sodium hydroxide |
| Fe³⁺ | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Zn²⁺ | white ppt., soluble in excess, colourless solution | white ppt., soluble in excess, colourless solution |

### Telling look-alikes apart

- **Aluminium or zinc?** Both give a white precipitate that dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide. Use aqueous ammonia: the zinc precipitate dissolves in excess; the aluminium precipitate does not.
- **Calcium?** The white precipitate with sodium hydroxide does not dissolve in excess, and ammonia gives little or no precipitate. A flame test (orange-red) confirms it.
- **Chromium(III) or iron(II)?** Both give green precipitates. The chromium(III) precipitate dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide; the iron(II) precipitate does not, and it turns brown at the surface on standing.
- **Copper(II)** is the only cation in the list that gives a dark blue solution with excess ammonia.

## Gas tests

| Gas | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ammonia | damp red litmus paper | turns blue |
| Carbon dioxide | bubble through limewater | limewater turns milky |
| Chlorine | damp litmus paper | bleached |
| Hydrogen | lighted splint | "pops" |
| Oxygen | glowing splint | relights |
| Sulfur dioxide | acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | purple to colourless |

Two points examiners check: the litmus must be **damp**, because the gases act on the paper only when dissolved in water; and the splint for hydrogen is **lighted**, while the splint for oxygen is **glowing**.

## Flame tests

A clean flame-test wire is dipped into the solid sample and held in the hot, non-luminous part of a Bunsen flame. The colour identifies the metal ion.

| Ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Li⁺ | red |
| Na⁺ | yellow |
| K⁺ | lilac |
| Ca²⁺ | orange-red |
| Ba²⁺ | light green |
| Cu²⁺ | blue-green |

The wire must be cleaned first, because traces of another compound, especially a sodium compound, give their own colour; sodium's strong yellow can hide a lilac potassium flame.

## Worked example 1: naming a compound from test results

*Solution W gives these results. Name the compound in W.*

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Add aqueous sodium hydroxide, then excess | green precipitate, insoluble in excess; brown near the surface after standing |
| Acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous barium nitrate | white precipitate |

**Reasoning.** A green precipitate insoluble in excess that turns brown at the surface identifies iron(II). A white precipitate with acidified barium nitrate identifies sulfate. **W is iron(II) sulfate.**

## Worked example 2: planning a test

*Two unlabelled white solids are zinc chloride and aluminium chloride. Describe a test to tell them apart. [3]*

**Model answer.** Dissolve each in water and add aqueous ammonia a little at a time until it is in excess [1]. Both give a white precipitate [1]. The precipitate dissolves in excess ammonia for zinc chloride but does not dissolve for aluminium chloride [1].

*Why sodium hydroxide is the wrong choice:* both precipitates dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide, so it cannot distinguish them.

## Common mistakes

- **"White solution"** instead of "white precipitate". A precipitate is a solid; a solution is clear.
- **Bubbles** described as "carbon dioxide produced". Describe the observation (effervescence, fizzing), then the test that identifies the gas.
- **Acidifying with hydrochloric acid** in the chloride test, or sulfuric acid in the sulfate test.
- **Omitting "in excess"**: half the cation information is lost.
- **Dry litmus** for ammonia or chlorine.
- **Glowing splint for hydrogen** or lighted splint for oxygen.
- **Confusing iron(II) (green) with iron(III) (red-brown).**

## Exam technique

- **Describe a test**: name the reagent, how it is added, and the observation for a positive result.
- **Identify** or **deduce**: give the ion with its charge or the full name, such as "iron(III) ion" or Fe³⁺.
- **Observations** are what you see: colour, precipitate, fizzing, colour change. Interpretations are what they mean. Keep them separate when a table asks for both.
