## The subtopic in five lines

1. Benzene, C₆H₆, is a planar regular hexagon with bond angles of 120°.
2. All six carbon–carbon bonds are the same length, between a C–C single bond and a C=C double bond.
3. Each carbon's p orbital overlaps with both neighbours, giving six delocalised electrons above and below the ring.
4. Delocalisation makes benzene more stable than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, shown by its less exothermic enthalpy of hydrogenation.
5. Benzene reacts by substitution, which keeps the delocalised ring, not addition, which would destroy it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Aromatic | Containing a benzene ring of delocalised electrons |
| Planar | All atoms lie in one plane |
| p orbital | Dumbbell-shaped orbital at right angles to the ring; one per carbon holds one electron |
| Delocalised electrons | Electrons shared over more than two atoms; in benzene, six over six carbons |
| Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene | Theoretical C₆H₆ with alternating single and double bonds |
| Enthalpy of hydrogenation | Enthalpy change when a compound reacts with hydrogen, here to form cyclohexane |
| Delocalisation (stabilisation) energy | How much lower in energy benzene is than the triene |
| Electrophilic substitution | Electrophile replaces an H atom on the ring |

## Numbers worth knowing

| Quantity | Typical value |
|---|---|
| C–C single bond length | about 154 pm |
| C=C double bond length | about 134 pm |
| Benzene C–C bond length | about 139 pm |
| ΔH hydrogenation, cyclohexene | about −120 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| ΔH hydrogenation, benzene (to cyclohexane) | about −208 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Predicted ΔH, triene | 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Extra stability of benzene | about 150 kJ mol⁻¹ (depends on the data used) |

Values vary slightly between data sources. Always use the figures printed in the question.

## Method: the stability calculation

1. Find ΔH per C=C from a compound with one C=C (or divide a diene value by two).
2. Multiply by three for the triene.
3. Subtract the measured benzene value (compare sizes, ignore the signs).
4. Write the conclusion: "benzene is X kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, because of delocalisation of p electrons."

From mean bond enthalpies: ΔH per C=C = [E(C=C) + E(H–H)] − [E(C–C) + 2E(C–H)].

**Bond enthalpy version, with typical data** (C=C 612, C–C 348, H–H 436, C–H 412 kJ mol⁻¹): per C=C, 1048 − 1172 = −124 kJ mol⁻¹; for the triene, −372 kJ mol⁻¹; against −208 kJ mol⁻¹ for benzene, the extra stability is 164 kJ mol⁻¹. It differs from the hydrogenation-data value because mean bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds.

## Energy level diagram in words

- Bottom level: cyclohexane.
- Benzene + 3H₂: above cyclohexane by the measured value.
- Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene + 3H₂: higher still, above cyclohexane by the predicted value.
- The gap between benzene and the triene is the extra stability.
- Arrows point down for exothermic hydrogenation.

## Why substitution wins

- Addition would take carbons out of the delocalised system and lose the extra stability.
- Substitution swaps H for another group and keeps the six delocalised electrons.
- Benzene + H₂ → cyclohexa-1,3-diene is endothermic (about +24 kJ mol⁻¹ from typical data), unlike hydrogenation of an isolated C=C.
- Electron density in benzene is spread over six carbons, lower than in a C=C bond, so it needs a strong electrophile.
- Evidence: benzene does not decolourise bromine water at room temperature; cyclohexene does, quickly.

## Exam traps

- "Benzene releases more energy, so it is more stable": wrong. It releases less.
- "Three double bonds" in benzene: wrong. Six equivalent bonds.
- Delocalised electrons "in the plane": wrong. Above and below it.
- Bond length "longer than a single bond": wrong. Between single and double.
- A number without a sentence saying which compound is more stable earns at most part of the marks.
- "Benzene is unreactive" as the reason for substitution: give the delocalisation reason.
- Drawing arrows upwards for an exothermic hydrogenation on the energy diagram.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. What is the H–C–C bond angle in benzene?
2. How many electrons are delocalised in a benzene ring?
3. Where is the delocalised electron density found relative to the ring?
4. How does the C–C bond length in benzene compare with single and double bonds?
5. Why are all six C–C bonds in benzene the same length?
6. Name the theoretical molecule used as a comparison for benzene.
7. Hydrogenation of cyclohexene: ΔH = −119 kJ mol⁻¹. Predict ΔH for hydrogenating cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene.
8. Benzene's value is −207 kJ mol⁻¹. Using your answer to 7, how much more stable is benzene?
9. Why does a smaller release of energy show greater stability?
10. Give one observation showing benzene does not readily undergo addition.
11. Explain why benzene undergoes substitution rather than addition.
12. Why does benzene need a stronger electrophile than cyclohexene does?

### Answers

1. 120°.
2. Six.
3. Above and below the plane of the ring.
4. It is between them: longer than C=C, shorter than C–C.
5. The delocalised electrons are shared equally by all six bonds.
6. Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene.
7. 3 × (−119) = −357 kJ mol⁻¹.
8. 357 − 207 = 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
9. Both reactions give the same product, so the reactant that releases less energy must have started at a lower energy.
10. Benzene does not decolourise bromine water at room temperature.
11. Addition would break up the delocalised ring and lose its extra stability; substitution keeps the delocalised system intact.
12. Its π electron density is spread over six carbons, so it is lower at any one bond than in a localised C=C, and attracts electrophiles less strongly.
