## Core recall

- Urban metabolism: a city takes in water, food, energy and materials, transforms them and releases wastes; quantify each flow to find losses.
- Leaks, wastewater and waste heat are outputs that planning can reduce or reuse.
- Urbanisation is a rising urban *share*; urban growth is a rising urban *number* or area.
- Globally the urban share passed 50% around 2007; megacities have 10 million or more people.
- Low density means longer trips, weak public transport and high car use; higher density supports transit but needs good design.
- Sprawl is driven by cheap edge land, car ownership, road building and demand for larger homes.
- Planning tools: zoning (single or mixed use), green belts, transit-oriented development, bus rapid transit, informal settlement upgrading, cool and green roofs.
- Curitiba (Brazil) combined bus rapid transit from the 1970s with high-density building along bus corridors.
- London's green belt has limited outward growth since the mid-twentieth century, but can raise prices and cause leapfrog development.
- Sustainability indicators make comparison possible but averages can hide inequality.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Urban metabolism | The flows of materials and energy into, through and out of a city |
| Urbanisation | Increase in the proportion of a population living in urban areas |
| Urban growth | Increase in the number of urban residents or the area of a city |
| Megacity | A city of 10 million or more people |
| Population density | People per unit area (per km²) |
| Urban sprawl | Low-density outward spread of a city |
| Zoning | Assigning land to particular uses |
| Mixed-use development | Homes, shops and workplaces in the same area |
| Green belt | Protected open land around a city where most building is banned |
| Leapfrog development | Building that jumps past a green belt to settlements beyond |
| Transit-oriented development | Concentrating homes and jobs around public transport stations |
| Bus rapid transit | Buses on dedicated lanes with prepaid boarding and frequent services |
| In-situ upgrading | Improving an informal settlement where it stands |
| Modal share | Percentage of trips made by each mode of transport |

## Formulas

```
Urban share (%)        = urban population ÷ total population × 100
Growth (%)             = (new − old) ÷ old × 100
Density                = population ÷ area (people per km²)
Per-person indicator   = total ÷ population (convert units first)
Absorbed radiation     = incoming × (1 − albedo)
```

## Planning tools: benefit and limitation

| Tool | Main benefit | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Mixed-use zoning | Shorter trips, lively streets | Conflicts between uses; needs careful design |
| Green belt | Protects countryside and recreation space | Higher prices inside; leapfrogging and longer commutes |
| Transit-oriented development | Less car use, compact growth | Expensive transit investment; rising land values can displace poorer residents |
| Bus rapid transit | Fast, cheaper than metro | Takes road space from cars, causing opposition |
| In-situ upgrading | Keeps people near jobs and networks | Slow; depends on land rights and residents' involvement |
| Cool and green roofs | Cut heat absorption and runoff | Cost; green roofs need maintenance and strong structures |

## Exam traps

- Using "urbanisation" for urban growth.
- Saying density is always good; mention crowding and design quality.
- Evaluating a green belt with benefits only.
- Forgetting stakeholders and who pays.
- Indicators quoted without units or converted incorrectly (km² to m²).
- Water or energy budgets where outputs exceed inputs.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish urbanisation from urban growth.
2. A country of 30 million has 12 million urban residents. Calculate the urban share.
3. The urban population rises from 12 million to 15.6 million. Calculate the urban growth.
4. A city of 2.4 million covers 800 km². Calculate its density.
5. A city of 1.2 million has 9 km² of parks. Calculate the green space per person in m².
6. A city takes in 500,000 m³ of water a day and loses 25% to leaks. How much is delivered?
7. The city in question 6 has 1.5 million people. Calculate the water delivered per person per day in litres.
8. Explain why low-density cities tend to have high car use.
9. Give one benefit and one limitation of a green belt.
10. Name a city known for bus rapid transit and state one feature of its approach.
11. Sunlight of 1,000 W m⁻² falls on roofs of albedo 0.2 and 0.7. Calculate the energy each absorbs.
12. Why should average indicators be interpreted with care?

### Answers

1. Urbanisation is a rise in the proportion of people living in urban areas; urban growth is a rise in the number of urban residents or the city's area.
2. 12 ÷ 30 × 100 = **40%**.
3. (15.6 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = **30%**.
4. 2,400,000 ÷ 800 = **3,000 people per km²**.
5. 9,000,000 m² ÷ 1,200,000 = **7.5 m² per person**.
6. 0.75 × 500,000 = **375,000 m³ per day**.
7. 375,000,000 L ÷ 1,500,000 = **250 L per person per day**.
8. Homes, shops and jobs are far apart and too few people live near each stop for frequent public transport, so most trips are by car.
9. Benefit: protects farmland, habitats and recreation space. Limitation: raises land prices or pushes development to towns beyond it.
10. Curitiba, Brazil: dedicated bus lanes with high-density building allowed along the main corridors.
11. Albedo 0.2: 1,000 × 0.8 = **800 W m⁻²**; albedo 0.7: 1,000 × 0.3 = **300 W m⁻²**.
12. An average can hide large differences between districts or groups, and a city may score well on one indicator but badly on another.
