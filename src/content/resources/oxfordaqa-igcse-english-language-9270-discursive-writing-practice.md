## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Decide whether each task is **argumentative** or **discursive**, and give a reason for one
decision. **[3]**

- (a) "Write a speech for your school assembly persuading students to volunteer locally."
- (b) "'Social media has done more harm than good to friendship.' Discuss this view."

**2.** Rewrite this one-sided sentence as a balanced sentence using a concessive clause: "Homework is
a waste of time." **[2]**

**3.** Identify the modal verbs in this sentence and explain their effect: "A shorter school day might
improve concentration, although it could reduce time for sport." **[3]**

**4.** Explain why this sentence weakens a discursive piece: "Studies show that 87% of teenagers
prefer online friends." **[2]**

**5.** Name the three structures for discursive writing, and state which you would choose for a task on
the effects of tourism on a small town, with a reason. **[4]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**6.** Read this paragraph from a student's discursive piece and suggest **three** improvements. **[6]**

> Some people think cities are better than the countryside because there are lots of things to do.
> Other people think the countryside is better because it is quiet. Both of these are good points.
> Cities also have more jobs. The countryside has fresh air.

**7.** Plan an **alternating** discursive piece on the task: "Should every young person learn to
cook?" Include an opening idea, three aspects with both sides of each, and a concluding judgement.
**[6]**

**8.** Write the **opening paragraph** of a discursive piece on the task: "'Exams are the fairest way to
judge students.' Discuss this view." **[5]**

**9.** Write **one** alternating paragraph, using the paragraph engine (topic sentence, view A, turn,
view B, weighing line), on whether cities should ban cars from their centres. **[5]**

**10.** Write **two** different concluding sentences for a discursive piece on whether teenagers should
have part-time jobs: one qualified view and one reframing. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a discursive piece can show control of sentence structures (W2) and cohesion (W3).
Give one example of each. **[4]**

**12.** Write a complete discursive composition on: "'Living abroad is the best education.' Discuss
this view." Use the checklist in the answers to assess your work. **[16]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For writing tasks, the bracketed
credits show what a strong response includes; in the exam, composition is marked as a whole against
the writing objectives.

**1.** (a) argumentative [1]; (b) discursive [1]. Reason, either [1]: (a) asks you to persuade, so it
needs a clear position; (b) asks you to "discuss" a view, so several angles must be weighed.

**2.** A balanced sentence with a concessive clause [1] that keeps both sides [1], for example:
"Although homework can feel repetitive, it gives students the practice that lessons rarely have time
for."

**3.** "Might" and "could" [1]. They make the claims tentative [1], showing the writer is weighing
possibilities rather than stating certainties, which suits a balanced discussion [1].

**4.** The statistic appears to be invented and the "studies" are not named [1], so the examiner may
doubt the writer's credibility; a realistic example would be more convincing [1].

**5.** Block, alternating and thematic [2 for all three, 1 for two]. A choice with a reason [2]: for
example, thematic (effects on residents, businesses and the environment), because the issue has
several areas, each with benefits and costs; or alternating, because each aspect has clear arguments
on both sides.

**6.** Three improvements, [2] each, with [1] for the improvement and [1] for an explanation or
example: replace "some people" and "other people" with an aspect-led topic sentence; develop each view
with a realistic example or consequence; use concessive or contrasting connectives instead of simple
statements; remove "Both of these are good points" and weigh which is stronger; avoid adding new,
undeveloped points ("more jobs", "fresh air") at the end; vary sentence structures.

**7.** Credit up to 6: an engaging opening idea [1]; three distinct aspects [1 each, up to 3], each
with both sides (for example, independence and health against time and cost; family culture against
convenience; life skills against school priorities); a concluding judgement that weighs rather than
summarises [2]. Maximum 6.

**8.** Credit up to 5: an engaging hook (an anecdote, image or question) [1]; a statement of the issue
[1]; acknowledgement that there is more than one view [1]; a measured, formal or semi-formal voice
[1]; a hint of the direction the discussion will take [1].

**9.** One mark for each element of the engine present and effective: topic sentence naming the
aspect [1]; view A with an example or consequence [1]; a concessive or contrasting turn [1]; view B
with an example or consequence [1]; a weighing line that judges [1].

**10.** Qualified view [2]: a judgement with a condition, for example: "On balance, a part-time job
teaches responsibility, provided it does not take the hours young people need for study and sleep."
Reframing [2]: a restatement of the question, for example: "Perhaps the real question is not whether
teenagers should work, but what kind of work genuinely teaches them something."

**11.** W2 [2]: control shown through concessive and complex sentences that hold two views, with a
short sentence for a key judgement; example [1 within the 2]: "Although…, …". W3 [2]: cohesion shown
through aspect-led topic sentences and connectives that show the logic of the discussion; example
[1 within the 2]: "A second consideration is…; however…".

**12.** Credit up to 16, as a guide: a clear sense of the task type and a measured voice [3]; a planned
structure with three or more developed aspects [3]; balanced weighing within paragraphs using
concession and qualification [3]; realistic examples and reasoning without invented statistics [2];
varied, controlled sentences [2]; a concluding judgement that goes beyond summary [2]; accurate
spelling and punctuation [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a persuasive argument for a "discuss" task.
- Paragraphs that state views without examples or weighing.
- Invented statistics and unnamed authorities.
- Conclusions that simply repeat both sides.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I give my own opinion in a discursive piece?**
Yes, usually in the conclusion, as a considered judgement reached through the discussion. The body
should weigh views fairly before you arrive there.
