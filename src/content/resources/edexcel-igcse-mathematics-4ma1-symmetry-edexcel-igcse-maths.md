## What the specification asks

Statement 4.3 A says students should be taught to "identify any lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry of a given two-dimensional figure". The note beside it gives a typical task: "Name a quadrilateral with no lines of symmetry and order of rotational symmetry of 2". The answer is a **parallelogram** (one that is not a rectangle or rhombus), and the reasons are explained below.

Everything on this page is about flat (two-dimensional) shapes. Planes of symmetry of solids are not part of 4.3.

## Line symmetry

A **line of symmetry** (a mirror line) divides a shape into two halves that are mirror images of each other. If you folded the shape along the line, the two halves would fit exactly on top of each other.

To test a possible line, take each vertex on one side, travel at right angles to the line, and go the same distance out the other side. If you always land on another vertex of the shape, the line is a line of symmetry. This test is the same rule you use for reflections in section 5.2, which is why the two sections support each other.

### The diagonal trap

The diagonals of a rectangle are **not** lines of symmetry. Take a rectangle 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. Reflect one corner in a diagonal: the image lands outside the rectangle, because the sides meeting at that corner are different lengths. The diagonals of a square and of a rhombus *are* lines of symmetry, because in those shapes all four sides are equal.

## Rotational symmetry

A shape has **rotational symmetry** if it fits onto itself more than once during a full turn of 360° about its centre. The **order of rotational symmetry** is the number of positions in one full turn in which the shape looks exactly the same, counting the starting position.

- Every shape fits onto itself once (after a full turn), so the lowest possible order is 1.
- Order 1 means the shape has **no** rotational symmetry. Writing "order 0" is wrong.
- If the order is n, the smallest turn that maps the shape onto itself is 360° ÷ n.

To find the order, use tracing paper. Trace the shape, put your pencil point on the centre, and turn the paper slowly through 360°, counting how many times the tracing fits exactly.

## The standard shapes

These results come up constantly. Learn the reason for each, not just the number.

| Shape | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | 3 | 3 | Each line joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side |
| Isosceles triangle (not equilateral) | 1 | 1 | Only the line through the apex and the midpoint of the base |
| Scalene triangle | 0 | 1 | No two sides are equal |
| Square | 4 | 4 | Two diagonals and two lines through midpoints of opposite sides |
| Rectangle (not a square) | 2 | 2 | Only the lines through midpoints of opposite sides |
| Rhombus (not a square) | 2 | 2 | Only the two diagonals |
| Parallelogram (not a rectangle or rhombus) | 0 | 2 | It fits onto itself after a half turn about the point where the diagonals cross |
| Kite | 1 | 1 | Only the diagonal through the two vertices where equal sides meet |
| Isosceles trapezium | 1 | 1 | The line through the midpoints of the parallel sides |
| Regular polygon with n sides | n | n | Lines through vertices and midpoints of sides |
| Circle | Infinitely many | Infinite | Every diameter is a line of symmetry |

### How the two kinds of symmetry are linked

- A shape with **two or more** lines of symmetry always has rotational symmetry, and its order equals the number of lines. A rectangle has 2 lines and order 2; a regular hexagon has 6 lines and order 6.
- The reverse is not true. A parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2 but no lines of symmetry. This is the fact behind the specification's own example.
- A shape with exactly one line of symmetry (a kite, an isosceles triangle) has order 1.

## Worked example 1: a shape on a coordinate grid

A quadrilateral has vertices A(1, 1), B(5, 1), C(6, 4) and D(2, 4).

(a) Write down the number of lines of symmetry. (b) Write down the order of rotational symmetry. (c) Find the centre of rotational symmetry.

First identify the shape. AB goes 4 across and 0 up; DC goes from (2, 4) to (6, 4), also 4 across. So AB and DC are equal and parallel. AD goes 1 across and 3 up, and BC goes from (5, 1) to (6, 4), also 1 across and 3 up. Both pairs of opposite sides are equal and parallel, so ABCD is a parallelogram. AB = 4, but AD = √(1² + 3²) = √10, so it is not a rhombus, and the angles are not right angles.

```
(a) 0 lines of symmetry
(b) order 2
(c) the diagonals cross at their midpoints:
    midpoint of AC = ((1 + 6)/2, (1 + 4)/2) = (3.5, 2.5)
    midpoint of BD = ((5 + 2)/2, (1 + 4)/2) = (3.5, 2.5)
    centre = (3.5, 2.5)
```

Checking both midpoints confirms that a half turn about (3.5, 2.5) swaps A with C and B with D.

## Worked example 2: completing a shape

The line x = 4 is to be a line of symmetry of a shape. The left half is drawn: it runs from (4, 1) to (1, 1), (1, 3), (2, 5) and back to the mirror line at (4, 5). Complete the shape and list its vertices.

A point on the mirror line stays where it is. For the other points, the image of (x, y) in the line x = 4 is (8 − x, y):

```
(1, 1) -> (7, 1)
(1, 3) -> (7, 3)
(2, 5) -> (6, 5)
```

Because (4, 1) and (4, 5) are on the line, they are not corners of the finished shape: the sides pass straight through them. The completed hexagon has vertices (1, 1), (7, 1), (7, 3), (6, 5), (2, 5) and (1, 3).

Does it have rotational symmetry? Under a half turn about (4, 3), the vertex (1, 1) would go to (7, 5), which is not a vertex. So the order is **1**. One line of symmetry and order 1 is consistent with the rule above.

## Worked example 3: linking symmetry and polygons

A regular polygon has rotational symmetry of order 9. Find the size of each interior angle.

```
order 9 -> 9 sides (a regular polygon with n sides has order n)
exterior angle = 360° ÷ 9 = 40°
interior angle = 180° − 40° = 140°
```

The same idea runs in reverse. If a regular polygon has interior angles of 156°, each exterior angle is 24°, so it has 360 ÷ 24 = 15 sides, 15 lines of symmetry and order 15.

## Shading squares on a grid

A common task gives a grid of squares with some already shaded and asks you to shade more so that the pattern has a stated symmetry.

- For a **line** of symmetry, reflect each shaded square in the line and shade its image.
- For **rotational symmetry of order 2**, rotate each shaded square through 180° about the centre of the grid. In a 4 by 4 grid with columns A to D and rows 1 to 4, column A swaps with D, B with C, row 1 with 4 and row 2 with 3. So square A1 maps to D4 and B3 maps to C2.
- For **order 4**, apply quarter turns: each shaded square must be matched by three more.

Shade exactly the number of squares the question asks for, then check the finished pattern again, because an extra square can destroy symmetry you already had.

## Exam technique

- **"Write down"** means little or no working is needed. Give a number, not a description: "2", not "it has two".
- **"Name"** wants the mathematical name of a shape. "Parallelogram" scores; "a slanted rectangle" does not.
- **"Draw"** or **"Shade"** on a diagram: use a ruler for mirror lines and extend them across the whole shape.
- When asked for the **centre** of rotation, give coordinates. For a shape with order 2, the centre is the midpoint of any vertex and its image.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the diagonals of a rectangle or a parallelogram as lines of symmetry.
- Writing "order 0" for a shape with no rotational symmetry. The answer is order 1.
- Counting a kite as having two lines of symmetry. Only one diagonal works.
- Assuming that a shape with rotational symmetry must have a line of symmetry.
- Forgetting that a point on the mirror line maps to itself when completing a shape.
- Giving the angle of turn instead of the order: a square has order 4, and its smallest angle of turn is 90°.
