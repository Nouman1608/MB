## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between an offer and an invitation to treat, using **one** decided case. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** ways in which an offer may come to an end before it is accepted. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the rule that consideration "must be sufficient but need not be adequate". **[3]**

**4.** Identify the section of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 that gives each remedy:

**(a)** the short-term right to reject **[1]**
**(b)** repeat performance of a service. **[1]**

**5.** Explain the effect of section 2(1) of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** On Monday, Kwame emails Nadia offering to sell her his car for £3,000, saying "let me know by Friday". On Tuesday, Nadia replies, "I'll give you £2,500." Kwame refuses. On Wednesday, Nadia emails, "Fine, I'll pay £3,000." Kwame has meanwhile sold the car to someone else.

Advise Nadia whether a contract was formed with Kwame. **[6]**

**7.** Hana books a hall for her wedding reception. A week before the date, the hall is destroyed by fire through no fault of either party. Hana has paid a deposit.

**(a)** Explain whether the contract has been frustrated. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 affects Hana's deposit. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 changed the doctrine of privity of contract. **[5]**

**9.** Tomás hires a plumber, a trader, to fit a new boiler in his home. The plumber's written terms state: "We accept no liability for any defects in our work." Two months later the boiler leaks because it was fitted carelessly.

Advise Tomás on the terms implied into his contract, the remedies available to him and whether the exclusion clause protects the plumber. **[8]**

**10.** Ravi buys a bottle of fruit juice from a café. It contains a piece of glass, which cuts his mouth. His friend Sunita, who drank from the same bottle, is also injured.

Explain why Sunita's best claim is likely to be in negligence rather than contract, and outline what she must prove. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate whether the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives consumers adequate protection when they buy faulty goods. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** An **offer** is a definite promise to be bound on stated terms as soon as it is accepted [1]. An **invitation to treat** is an invitation to others to make offers, which cannot itself be accepted [1]. Example: in *Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots* [1953] goods on a self-service shelf were an invitation to treat, so the customer made the offer at the till; or *Fisher v Bell* [1961] (window display); or *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] (advert) [1].

**2.** Any two [1] each: **revocation** communicated before acceptance (*Byrne v Van Tienhoven*); **rejection**; a **counter-offer** (*Hyde v Wrench*); **lapse of time**, either a stated deadline or a reasonable time; **death** of a party in some circumstances; failure of a condition.

**3.** Consideration must have **some value in the eyes of the law** (sufficient) [1], but the courts do not ask whether it is a **fair or equal exchange** (adequate) [1]. In *Chappell v Nestlé* [1960] chocolate wrappers were part of the consideration, even though they were thrown away [1].

**4. (a)** Section 20 [1]. **(b)** Section 55 [1].

**5.** A person cannot, by a contract term or notice, **exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury** [1] **resulting from negligence**; such a clause is ineffective, whatever its wording [1].

**6.** **Issue**: was the original offer still open on Wednesday? [1]. Kwame's email was an **offer** that would stay open until Friday unless it ended earlier [1]. Nadia's reply offering £2,500 was a **counter-offer**, not a request for information [1], so it **destroyed the original offer** (*Hyde v Wrench* [1840]) [1]. Her Wednesday email is therefore a **new offer**, which Kwame did not accept [1]. **Conclusion**: no contract; Kwame's promise to keep the offer open until Friday is not binding either, because Nadia gave no consideration for it [1].

**7. (a)** A contract is frustrated when, **after formation**, an event that is **no one's fault** makes performance **impossible** or radically different [1] [1]. The destruction of the hall is like the destruction of the music hall in *Taylor v Caldwell* [1863] [1], so the contract is frustrated and both parties are discharged from future obligations [1].
**(b)** Under the 1943 Act, **money paid before the frustrating event is recoverable** [1] and sums due but unpaid stop being payable [1]. The court may allow the hall's owner to **keep or recover a just sum for expenses** incurred in performing the contract before the fire [1].

**8.** At common law **only a party** to a contract could enforce it, even if it was made for a third party's benefit (*Tweddle v Atkinson* [1861]; *Dunlop v Selfridge* [1915]) [1] [1]. The 1999 Act lets a third party **enforce a term** if the contract **expressly provides** that they may [1], or if the term **purports to confer a benefit** on them, unless the parties did not intend it to be enforceable by them [1]. The third party must be **expressly identified by name, class or description**, though they need not exist when the contract is made [1].

**9.** **Implied terms**: as a contract between a trader and a consumer for a service, it carries an implied term that the service will be performed with **reasonable care and skill** (CRA 2015 s 49) [1]; careless fitting breaches it [1]. The boiler itself, if supplied by the plumber, must also be of satisfactory quality (s 9) [1]. **Remedies**: Tomás can first require **repeat performance** (s 55), done within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience [1]; if that is impossible or not done properly, he can claim a **price reduction** (s 56), which can be up to the full price [1]. **Exclusion clause**: under **s 57** the trader cannot exclude liability for the s 49 term [1], and under **s 62** an unfair term is not binding on the consumer [1]. If the leak causes personal injury through negligence, **s 65** also prevents exclusion [1]. **Conclusion**: the clause does not protect the plumber.

**10.** **Why not contract**: Ravi bought the juice, so he has a contract with the café; Sunita did not buy it, so **privity** prevents her suing in contract [1] [1]. The 1999 Act is unlikely to help, because nothing in the contract was intended to benefit her [1]. **Negligence**: her claim would be against the **manufacturer**, who, as in *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932], owes a **duty of care** to the ultimate consumer [1]. She must prove: a **duty** owed (reasonably foreseeable harm, proximity, fair, just and reasonable) [1]; **breach**, falling below the standard of a reasonable manufacturer, which *res ipsa loquitur* may help show because glass would not normally be in sealed juice without negligence [1]; **causation**, that but for the breach she would not have been injured [1]; and that the injury was **not too remote** (*The Wagon Mound*) [1].

**11.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: the implied terms (ss 9, 10, 11), the tiered remedies (ss 20, 23, 24), the bar on excluding them (s 31), unfair terms (s 62).
- **Strengths**: clear statutory rights in one Act; a fast refund route within 30 days; traders cannot contract out; the remedies give the trader a fair chance to repair first.
- **Weaknesses**: consumers may not know their rights; 30 days may be short for some goods; arguments over whether a fault existed at delivery; enforcing rights may require court action, and the small claims track still costs time and money; online sellers abroad may be hard to pursue.
- **Evaluation**: weigh the strength of the rights on paper against practical access to justice, and reach a conclusion, for example that the Act gives strong rights but enforcement depends on consumers' knowledge and resources.

Top-level answers apply each point, use the correct sections and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a counter-offer as a request for information.
- Saying a promise to keep an offer open is binding without consideration.
- Applying UCTA to a consumer contract instead of the CRA.
- Jumping to a price reduction without first using repeat performance or repair.
- Forgetting privity when a non-purchaser is injured.
