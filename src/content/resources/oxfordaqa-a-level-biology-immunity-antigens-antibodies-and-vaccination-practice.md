## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define the term antigen. **[2]**
**(b)** A kidney from a donor who is not an identical twin is transplanted into a patient. Explain why the patient's immune system may attack the new kidney. **[2]**

**2.** Describe how a neutrophil destroys a bacterium after the bacterium has bound to its surface. **[4]**

**3.** An antibody is drawn as a Y shape. Region X is at the tip of each arm. Region Y is where the arms join the stem. Bonds Z link the four polypeptide chains.
**(a)** Name X, Y and Z. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why an antibody against one bacterium does not bind to a different bacterium. **[2]**

**4.** A scanning electron micrograph shows a macrophage with a diameter of 54 mm on the image. The magnification is x3000.
**(a)** Calculate the actual diameter of the macrophage in micrometres. **[2]**
**(b)** A bacterium in the same image is 6 mm long. Calculate its actual length in micrometres. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the role of lysosomes in the macrophage. **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** State the type of immunity for which T cells are primarily responsible and the type for which B cells are primarily responsible. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why one of these types is described as humoral. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the cells that secrete antibodies. **[1]**

**6.** A macrophage engulfs a bacterium. Describe how this leads to the production of antibodies against the bacterium. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how antibodies lead to the destruction of bacterial cells. **[5]**

**8.** A volunteer was injected with a vaccine on day 0 and given a booster on day 28. The table shows antibody concentration in the blood.

| Day | 0 | 7 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 31 | 34 | 41 | 56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody (arbitrary units) | 0 | 0 | 10 | 25 | 18 | 220 | 900 | 600 | 380 |

**(a)** Between which two days was antibody first detected? **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the highest concentration after the second dose was than the highest after the first dose. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the differences between the response to the first dose and the response to the second dose. **[4]**

**9.** A man was vaccinated against influenza two years ago. This winter he caught influenza.
**(a)** Explain why the earlier vaccine may not have protected him. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest why developing a vaccine against HIV is difficult. **[2]**

**10.** A child who has never been vaccinated against tetanus has a deep, dirty wound. A doctor injects tetanus antibodies and also starts a course of vaccination.
**(a)** Explain why the antibody injection is given. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the antibody injection alone would not protect the child for years. **[2]**
**(c)** State the type of immunity the vaccine course provides and explain how it differs from that given by the injection. **[2]**

**11.** A disease has R = 8, where R is the mean number of new infections caused by a single case when nobody is immune. A simple model states that the minimum fraction of the population that must be immune to stop spread is 1 - 1/R.
**(a)** Calculate the minimum percentage that must be immune. **[2]**
**(b)** Only 95% of people given the vaccine become immune. Calculate the vaccination coverage needed. **[2]**
**(c)** A town vaccinates 88% of its people. Calculate the percentage immune and state whether herd immunity is reached. **[2]**

**12.** A trial of a new vaccine gave the results below. Allocation to groups was random and neither participants nor staff knew who received the vaccine.

| Group | Participants | Cases of disease over 3 months |
|---|---|---|
| Vaccine, all ages | 9,000 | 27 |
| Placebo, all ages | 9,000 | 135 |
| Vaccine, over 65 only | 1,500 | 12 |
| Placebo, over 65 only | 1,500 | 30 |

Mild side effects occurred in 12% of the vaccine group and 4% of the placebo group.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of each all-ages group who developed the disease. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the efficacy of the vaccine for all ages. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the efficacy for the over-65 group. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate the trial and its data. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A molecule, usually a protein, [1] that is recognised as foreign and triggers an immune response [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The donor cells carry surface molecules (antigens) that differ from the patient's own [1]; the patient's immune system recognises them as non-self and responds [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Defining an antigen only as "something that causes disease" misses transplants and other harmless foreign cells.

**2.** The neutrophil engulfs the bacterium, forming a phagosome [1]. Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome [1] and release hydrolytic enzymes [1], which digest the bacterium; the products are absorbed or removed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming lysosomes is not enough on its own; a complete answer says that they fuse with the phagosome and what they release.

**3. (a)** X is the variable region [1]; Y is the hinge region [1]; Z are disulfide bridges [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The variable region has a specific tertiary structure, formed by its amino acid sequence, giving a particular binding-site shape [1]; the second bacterium has an antigen with a different shape that is not complementary [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say "complementary" and link shape to amino acid sequence; "the antibody has the wrong shape" alone is a weaker explanation.

**4. (a)** 54 / 3000 = 0.018 mm [1] = **18 micrometres** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 6 / 3000 = 0.002 mm [1] = **2 micrometres** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes [1] and fuse with the phagosome so the engulfed bacterium is digested [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert units explicitly (1 mm = 1000 micrometres); state the unit with every answer so that the size of the value is clear.

**5. (a)** T cells: cell-mediated immunity [1]; B cells: humoral immunity [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Humoral immunity works through antibodies in body fluids (blood and tissue fluid) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Plasma cells [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Plasma cells are the cells that secrete antibody; answers that say "B cells" generally need to be more precise when the question asks which cells secrete.

**6.** The macrophage presents the bacterial antigen on its surface [1]. A T cell carrying a complementary receptor attaches to it [1] and is activated, helping to stimulate B cells [1]. A B cell with a complementary surface antibody binds the antigen [1] and divides by mitosis to form a clone [1]. Cells of the clone become plasma cells that secrete the antibody [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Sequence matters in a "describe how" question; listing the cells without saying what each one does earns little.

**7.** Antibodies bind to antigens on the bacterial surface, forming antigen-antibody complexes [1]. An antibody has two binding sites, so it can attach to two bacteria [1]; many antibodies link many bacteria into clumps (agglutination) [1]. Phagocytes bind to the antibody-coated or clumped bacteria and engulf them [1], and lysosomal enzymes digest them [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Antibodies kill bacteria" does not describe a mechanism; name agglutination and phagocytosis and give the cause of each.

**8. (a)** Between day 7 and day 14 [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 900 / 25 [1] = **36 times** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** After the second dose, antibody appeared much sooner (220 units within 3 days compared with 10 units at day 14) [1] and reached a much higher peak (900 compared with 25) [1]. Memory cells were formed by the first dose [1] and on re-exposure they divide rapidly into many plasma cells that secrete antibody [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table to support each difference, and explain with memory cells rather than saying only that the body "remembers".

**9. (a)** The antigens on the virus had changed (by mutation) [1]; antibodies and memory cells from the earlier vaccine were not complementary to the new antigens [1]; so no rapid secondary response occurred [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** HIV mutates rapidly, so its antigens vary [1]; a vaccine would need to protect against many different forms of the antigen [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause (change in antigen) to the consequence (memory cells no longer match); stating that "the virus has become resistant" is a different idea.

**10. (a)** Antibodies give immediate protection [1]; the child's own response would be too slow to deal with the toxin or bacteria already present [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The antibodies are not replaced and are broken down within weeks [1]; no memory cells are formed [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Active immunity [1]; the child makes its own antibodies and memory cells, giving long-term protection, whereas the injection gives short-term passive immunity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both sides; saying only that the vaccine "lasts longer" earns less than naming memory cells.

**11. (a)** 1 - 1/8 = 0.875 [1], so **87.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 87.5 / 0.95 [1] = **92.1%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 88% x 0.95 = **83.6%** immune [1]; this is below 87.5%, so herd immunity is not reached [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c), a comparison with the threshold from part (a) is needed to answer "state whether"; the calculation alone is incomplete.

**12. (a)** Vaccine: 27 / 9000 x 100 = **0.3%** [1]; placebo: 135 / 9000 x 100 = **1.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (1.5 - 0.3) / 1.5 x 100 [1] = **80%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Over-65 attack rates: 12 / 1500 = 0.8% and 30 / 1500 = 2.0% [1]; efficacy = (2.0 - 0.8) / 2.0 x 100 = **60%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Random allocation, a placebo and double-blinding reduce bias, and the large groups make results more reliable [1]; the vaccine cut cases from 1.5% to 0.3%, so it appears effective [1]; it was less effective in the over-65 group (60% against 80%), and that subgroup is small [1]; follow-up of three months does not show long-term protection, and side effects were three times as common (12% against 4%) though described as mild [1]. Other valid points, such as the absence of a statistical test, can also gain credit. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A good evaluation gives at least one strength and one limitation, each supported by a figure; repeating that the sample was "big" without saying why it matters is rarely enough.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining an antigen without saying that it triggers an immune response.
- Writing that antibodies "destroy" or "kill" bacteria without naming agglutination or phagocytosis.
- Forgetting to say that each antibody has two binding sites when explaining agglutination.
- Missing out the lysosome or the enzymes when describing digestion after phagocytosis.
- Confusing plasma cells with memory cells.
- Saying vaccines contain antibodies, or that passive immunity produces memory cells.
- Mixing up units in magnification questions.
- Giving efficacy as the percentage of vaccinated people who became ill, instead of the reduction compared with placebo.
- Evaluating a trial without figures or a limitation.
