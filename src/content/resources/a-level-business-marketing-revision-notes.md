## How the three sub-topics connect

**3.1** gives concepts (orientation, segmentation) → **3.2** gives
the method for finding out which apply to a market → **3.3** gives
the tools for acting on findings. A strong marketing-mix answer
connects back to segmentation/research findings, rather than
recommending an element in isolation.

## 3.1 Nature of marketing

| Concept | Key distinction |
|---|---|
| Orientation | Product (what business can make) vs customer/market (what customers want) |
| Market type | Consumer vs industrial; local/national/international |
| Marketing scope | Mass (whole market) vs niche (specific segment) |

**Segmentation methods** (3): geographic, demographic, psychographic
— know an advantage and disadvantage of each.

**CRM**: aims, plus costs vs benefits to the business.

## 3.2 Market research

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Collected first-hand (e.g. a survey the business commissions) | Already exists (e.g. government trade statistics) |

**Anchor to concrete examples** — scenario questions test whether you
can *identify* which type a business used, not just define the
terms. **Sampling**: know why it's needed and its limitations.
**Data**: reliability, quantitative vs qualitative, interpreting
tables/charts/graphs.

## 3.3 The marketing mix — 4Ps

| P | Key content |
|---|---|
| **Product** | Goods vs services; differentiation; USP; product life cycle; extension strategies; **Boston Matrix** |
| **Price** | 7 named methods: competitive, penetration, skimming, price discrimination, dynamic, cost-based, psychological |
| **Promotion** | Advertising, sales, direct, digital promotion; packaging; branding |
| **Place** | Distribution channels — digital and physical |

**Be ready to advise on a pricing method and justify it** — know
*which market condition* suits each pricing method, not just its
definition (e.g. penetration pricing for a new entrant building
market share quickly; skimming for a genuinely novel product with
few early competitors).

## Connecting the Boston Matrix and product life cycle

The syllabus names both under product portfolio analysis (3.3.3). A
Boston Matrix **"dog"** is a product with a **low market share in a
low-growth market**. A dog may be, but need not be, in the **decline**
stage of its life cycle: a product can have a low share of a slow-growing
market at any stage. Practise using one framework to explain a decision
made about the other, without treating them as the same measure.

## Worked example: recommending a pricing method

A scenario describes a technology start-up launching a genuinely
novel smart-home device with no direct competitors yet on the market.

```
Method recommended:  Price skimming
Justification:       With no direct competitors, the business can
                      set a high initial price to recover
                      development costs quickly and capture
                      early adopters willing to pay a premium for
                      novelty, before lowering the price once
                      competitors enter the market.
Contrast:             Penetration pricing (a low initial price to
                      build market share fast) would suit a business
                      entering an already-crowded market instead --
                      the opposite scenario from this one.
```

Practising this recommend-justify-contrast structure -- naming the
method, justifying it with a scenario detail, then contrasting it
with the alternative that would suit a different scenario -- is a
reliable way to demonstrate the depth that questions asking you to
advise on and justify a method reward.

## Data response questions on marketing

Case-study-based papers reward answers that quote **specific figures
or details from the source material**, not generic marketing theory.
If the case gives sales figures over three years alongside a
description of a new promotional campaign, a strong answer links the
figures directly to the campaign rather than describing the campaign
in isolation.

**Worked example.** A case study states that a firm's sales grew from
$2m to $3.2m in the year after switching from mass to niche
marketing, targeting a specific demographic segment with tailored
digital promotion.

```
Analysis:      Sales grew by $1.2m (60%) following the switch to
               niche marketing, suggesting the tailored digital
               promotion reached the target segment more
               effectively than the previous mass-marketing
               approach.
Evaluation:    However, the case does not rule out other causes of
               the growth (e.g. a wider market upturn, a competitor
               exiting), so the link between the marketing change and
               the sales figure, while plausible, cannot be
               presented as proven from this data alone.
```

This is the same "state the figure, link it to the theory, then
evaluate whether the link is actually proven" structure that scores
well across every data-response topic in this syllabus, not only
marketing.

## Common confusion: segmentation vs targeting vs positioning

These three terms are often used loosely in student answers but are
tested as distinct steps:

- **Segmentation** — dividing the whole market into groups with
  shared characteristics (geographic, demographic, psychographic).
- **Targeting** — choosing which segment(s) the business will focus
  on serving.
- **Positioning** — deciding how the product should be perceived
  relative to competitors within the targeted segment.

Answering "explain how a business segments its market" with content
that actually describes targeting (which segment was chosen) rather
than segmentation (how the market was divided) is a common lost-marks
pattern worth checking for specifically.

## Exam traps

- Recommending a marketing-mix element without connecting it back to
segmentation or research findings from the same scenario.
- Defining a pricing method without matching it to the market
condition that makes it the sensible choice.
- Treating the Boston Matrix and product life cycle as unconnected
frameworks rather than cross-referencing them.
- Confusing primary and secondary research in a scenario (e.g.
calling government statistics "primary" because the business used
them directly).
- Listing segmentation methods without an advantage/disadvantage for
each.

## Self-test

1. What is the key difference between product orientation and
customer/market orientation?
2. Name the three segmentation methods.
3. Give an example of primary research and an example of secondary
research.
4. Which pricing method suits a new entrant trying to build market
share quickly, and which suits a genuinely novel product with few
early competitors?
5. How does a product in Boston Matrix "dog" territory relate to the
product life cycle?

**Answers:** 1. Product orientation starts from what the business can
make; customer/market orientation starts from what customers actually
want. 2. Geographic, demographic, psychographic. 3. Primary: a survey
the business commissions itself; secondary: government trade
statistics the business already has access to. 4. Penetration pricing
for the new entrant; price skimming for the novel product. 5. A "dog"
has a low market share in a low-growth market. It may coincide with the
**decline** stage of the product life cycle, but need not: the matrix
measures share and market growth, the life cycle measures the product's
own sales over time.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three segmentation methods in Cambridge 9609?**
Geographic, demographic and psychographic segmentation. Know an advantage and a disadvantage of each, because listing them without these is a common exam trap.

**When should a business use price skimming or penetration pricing?**
Price skimming suits a genuinely novel product with few early competitors, so a high initial price can recover development costs. Penetration pricing suits a new entrant trying to build market share quickly in a crowded market.

**What is the difference between segmentation, targeting and positioning?**
Segmentation divides the market into groups with shared characteristics, targeting chooses which segments to serve, and positioning decides how the product should be perceived against competitors within the target segment.

**Are government statistics primary or secondary research?**
Secondary. They already exist, so calling them primary because the business used them directly is a common error; primary research is data the business collects itself, such as a survey it commissions.
