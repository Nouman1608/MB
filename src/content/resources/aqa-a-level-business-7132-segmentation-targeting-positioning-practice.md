These questions cover section 3.3.3 Making marketing decisions: segmentation, targeting, positioning of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [segmentation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-segmentation-targeting-positioning/) and the [segmentation revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-segmentation-targeting-positioning-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A clothing brand designs one range for teenagers and another for people over 60. Which method of segmentation is it using? **[1]**

A: Geographic

B: Demographic

C: Behavioural

D: Income

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes niche marketing? **[1]**

A: Selling a standard product to the whole market

B: Focusing on a small, specialised segment with a tailored mix

C: Cutting prices to gain market share

D: Selling only through the internet

**3.** Define the term "positioning". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit to a business of segmenting its market. **[3]**

**5.** Marlowe Meals delivers recipe boxes. Research on two segments shows:

| Segment | Households | Expected to subscribe | Spend per household per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young families | 40,000 | 12% | £90 |
| Busy couples | 25,000 | 20% | £120 |

**(a)** Calculate the expected annual revenue from each segment. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain which segment Marlowe should target first, using your answers and one other factor. **[3]**

**6.** A market map of four hotels uses price (low to high) and facilities (basic to extensive). Ashgrove is low price and basic. Brindle is medium price and medium facilities. Calder is high price and extensive facilities. Dunmore is high price and basic.

**(a)** Identify one gap in the market. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the gap may not be a good opportunity. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** example of behavioural segmentation. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a small independent bakery of using niche marketing. **[6]**

**9.** A large supermarket chain currently sells the same range in all its stores. Assess whether it should move to a more segmented approach. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how developments in technology have affected the way businesses segment and target their markets. **[6]**

**11.** A budget clothing brand wants to reposition itself as a mid-priced, fashionable brand. Explain **two** risks of this decision. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a business must segment its market in order to be successful. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. The groups are based on age, which is demographic. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A niche is a small, specialised segment served with its own marketing mix. **[1]**

**3.** How customers see a product or brand compared with competing ones [1], which the business shapes through the marketing mix, for example as cheapest, most reliable or most stylish [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Marketing money is focused on the customers most likely to buy [1], so there is less waste on people who have no interest [1], which improves the return on spending or allows the business to grow faster with the same budget [1]. Credit also: products designed closely to customers' needs; discovering gaps; charging different prices. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Young families: 40,000 × 0.12 × £90 = 4,800 × £90 = **£432,000** [1]. Busy couples: 25,000 × 0.20 × £120 = 5,000 × £120 = **£600,000** [1].

**(b)** Busy couples have the higher revenue potential, £600,000 against £432,000 [1], even though the segment is smaller, because more of them subscribe and each spends more [1]. Other factor (one): cost of reaching each segment; competition; whether the firm can deliver the right product, so the better choice depends on cost per customer won [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** Low price with extensive facilities (or medium facilities at low price, or medium price with extensive facilities) [1].

**(b)** It may cost too much to provide extensive facilities at a low price, so the business would not make a profit [1]. Alternatively, there may be too little demand because customers who want extensive facilities expect to pay more [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Segmenting by how customers behave, for example frequent flyers, who are offered a loyalty scheme [1], because airlines can reward high-usage customers and encourage them to keep choosing the airline [1]. (Accept other valid behaviour, such as occasion of purchase or benefits sought.) **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: less competition [1]. A small bakery cannot compete with supermarkets on price or range, but a specialist niche, such as gluten-free or traditional sourdough, has few rivals [1], so it can win a loyal local customer base [1]. Benefit 2: higher prices [1]. Customers who value a specialist product are often willing to pay more for it [1], which helps a small business with high unit costs to earn a profit without needing large volumes [1]. Credit also: lower marketing costs through targeted promotion. **[6]**

**9.** For: segmenting would let the chain meet different needs, for example premium ranges in wealthier areas and value ranges elsewhere [1], which could raise sales per customer [1] and build loyalty [1]. Against: different ranges increase complexity and may reduce economies of scale in buying [1]; stock and distribution costs rise [1]; research is needed to identify the segments [1]. A varied approach may blur the chain's image if prices and quality differ between stores [1]. Judgement: a more segmented approach is likely to be worthwhile for a large chain, because it has the data and scale to manage it [1], provided the core range and brand message stay consistent [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Point 1: data [1]. Loyalty cards, website analytics and social media give detailed data on behaviour and preferences [1], so segments can be defined more precisely, for example by buying habits [1]. Point 2: reach [1]. Digital advertising can be aimed at specific ages, places or interests at a low cost [1], making small niche segments profitable to serve [1]. Credit also: dynamic pricing for different segments; concerns over privacy. **[6]**

**11.** Risk 1: existing customers may be lost [1], because budget buyers will not accept the higher prices and may switch to a cheaper rival [1]. Risk 2: customers may not believe the new image [1], so the business pays for new products, promotion and perhaps new stores, but sales do not rise enough to cover them [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: segmentation focuses limited resources on the customers most likely to buy [1]. It allows products to be designed for the needs of a group [1] and can show gaps rivals have missed [1]. Businesses that try to appeal to everyone may appeal to nobody [1]. Niche businesses depend on a segment to survive [1]. Against: some products have a very wide appeal, such as basic foods, and a mass approach may give lower unit costs [1]. Segmentation is costly, because it needs research and perhaps several products or campaigns [1]. A segment may be too small to be profitable [1]. Some successful firms grew by selling a good, cheap product to everyone [1]. Factors: the type of product, the size of the business, and the diversity of customers' needs [1]. Judgement: most businesses benefit from at least some segmentation, even informally, because customers differ [1], but it is not essential for every product, and the right amount depends on the market and the firm's resources [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Identify the segmentation method from the evidence in the case, then link the choice of target market to the mix.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up segmentation, targeting and positioning.
- Naming the wrong segmentation method for the evidence in the case.
- Treating any small business as a niche business.
- Assuming a gap on a market map is automatically profitable.
- Giving general benefits of segmentation without applying them to the business named.
- Recommending repositioning without noticing the effect on existing customers.
