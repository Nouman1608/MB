## Section A

**1.** State **two** reasons why the availability of coal encouraged industrialization in Britain. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **three** ways in which the Meiji government promoted industrialization after 1868. **[3]**

**3.** Define "proto-industrialization" and give **one** example of it. **[2]**

**4.** Outline **two** provisions of the British Factory Act of 1833. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what Alexander Gerschenkron meant by the advantages and needs of "backward" economies. **[3]**

**6.** Identify **two** new industries associated with the "second industrial revolution" after about 1870, and **one** country that led in them. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain why railways accelerated industrialization in **one** country you have studied. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** limits on the impact of railways in the same country. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Outline the role of Sergei Witte in Russian industrialization in the 1890s. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why state-led industrialization in Russia produced political instability by 1905. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain **two** political effects of industrialization in **one** country. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why Bismarck introduced social insurance in Germany in the 1880s. **[3]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the role of the state in the industrialization of Britain and Japan. **[6]**

**11.** "The standard of living of industrial workers worsened during early industrialization." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view, using **one** country. **[8]**

**12.** "Industrialization brought more benefits than costs to the societies that experienced it." With reference to **two** countries, each chosen from a different region, to what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: coal fuelled steam engines, freeing factories from water-power sites; coal was used to smelt iron (coke smelting) for machines and rails; British coalfields lay near rivers, canals and ports, so it was cheap to move; abundant cheap coal made it profitable to replace labour with coal-powered machines. **[2]**

**2.** Any three, 1 mark each: built railways (Tokyo-Yokohama, 1872) and telegraphs; set up model factories such as the Tomioka silk mill (1872); brought in foreign experts and sent the Iwakura Mission (1871-73) to study Western industry; created a national education system; reformed the land tax to give the state a steady revenue for investment; sold state enterprises cheaply to private firms (future *zaibatsu*) in the 1880s. **[3]**

**3.** 1 mark for the definition: rural, household-based production of goods for distant markets, often organized by merchants, before factories. 1 mark for an example: spinning and weaving in cottages under the "putting-out" system in eighteenth-century England or Flanders. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: children under nine could not be employed in textile mills; hours for children aged nine to thirteen were limited; some schooling was required for working children; factory inspectors were appointed to enforce the law. **[2]**

**5.** 1 mark: late industrializers could borrow existing technology rather than invent it. 1 mark: but they lacked the capital, skills and markets that early industrializers built up gradually. 1 mark: so banks and the state stepped in to supply capital and direction, as in Germany and Russia. **[3]**

**6.** 1 mark each for two industries: steel (Bessemer and later processes), chemicals (dyes, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals), electrical engineering, oil. 1 mark for a leading country: Germany (chemicals and electricals) or the USA (steel, oil, electricity). **[3]**

**7.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks for developed explanation, for Britain for example: railways cut the cost and time of moving coal, raw materials and finished goods (1); they created a national market, so firms could grow (1); building them created huge demand for iron, coal and engineering (1); and they mobilized capital through joint-stock investment, as in the "railway mania" of the 1840s (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed limit: by 1830 Britain was already industrializing, using canals and coastal shipping, so railways deepened rather than started growth; speculation in the 1840s led to failed companies and lost savings; some regions and industries remained dependent on older transport and methods. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** 1 mark each: as finance minister from 1892, Witte attracted foreign loans and investment, especially from France; he put Russia on the gold standard (1897) to win investor confidence; he used high tariffs to protect industry; he promoted railway building, including the Trans-Siberian Railway (begun 1891), which boosted coal, iron and steel. **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed point: growth concentrated workers in cities such as St Petersburg with poor housing and long hours, and strikes grew; heavy taxation of the peasantry to pay for industry deepened rural poverty; there was no parliament to absorb discontent, so economic grievances became political; defeat by Japan (1904-05) triggered the 1905 Revolution in these conditions. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** 2 marks for each developed effect, for Britain for example: pressure for wider representation as industrial towns and the middle class grew, leading to the 1832 Reform Act; the rise of working-class movements such as Chartism (1838-48) and, later, trade-union support for a Labour Party; the state took on new roles in regulating factories and public health. **[4]**
**(b)** 1 mark each: to weaken support for the socialist SPD, which grew with the industrial working class; to bind workers to the state and the empire ("state socialism"); alongside the Anti-Socialist Laws (from 1878), so it was a combination of repression and concession. **[3]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference. Similarities (up to 2): in both countries the state protected property and supported infrastructure; both used naval and imperial power to secure markets. Differences (up to 4): Britain's state was largely enabling (patents, enclosure acts, navy), while Japan's directed growth (model factories, railways, education); Japan's state acted because of foreign pressure and the unequal treaties, whereas Britain faced no such threat; Japan's state sold its enterprises to private groups in the 1880s, while in Britain enterprise had always been private; the timescale differed, gradual in Britain and rapid in Japan. A final judgement on the scale of difference earns the top mark. **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument, for Britain. For: early factory work imposed long hours and strict discipline, including on children; rapid urbanization caused overcrowding, poor sanitation and cholera outbreaks in the 1830s and 1840s; E. P. Thompson stressed the loss of independence for skilled workers such as handloom weavers. Against: real wages rose, especially after about 1820 and clearly after 1850; cheap factory goods such as cotton clothing became widely available; regulation (Factory Acts, Public Health Act 1848) improved conditions over time. **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *Britain (Europe) and Japan (Asia and Oceania), for example.* Benefits: long-run economic growth, rising real wages in Britain after about 1850, cheaper goods, railways and new jobs; in Japan, the economic and military strength that ended the unequal treaties and avoided colonization. Costs: urban overcrowding and disease, child labour, harsh discipline; in Japan, long hours for young women in silk and cotton mills, rural poverty and the link between industry, militarism and empire. Political dimension: reform and welfare in Britain against oligarchic, then militarist, rule in Japan. Environmental costs in both. A strong answer defines "benefits" and "costs", asks "for whom and when", compares the two countries in each paragraph, and reaches a judgement such as: benefits outweighed costs in the long run, but they were unevenly shared and the short-run costs fell on workers who did not share the gains. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Are short-answer questions like these on Paper 2?**
No. Paper 2 is two 15-mark essays. The short and structured items build the knowledge and paragraph skills those essays depend on.

**Can I use Britain and Germany together?**
Only if the question does not ask for different regions; both are in Europe.
