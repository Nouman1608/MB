## 3.1 Coordinates

A point is written (x, y). The x-value says how far across you go from the origin O, positive to the right. The y-value says how far up, positive upwards. The axes split the plane into four quadrants. Both values are positive in the top right; x is negative and y positive in the top left; both are negative in the bottom left; x is positive and y negative in the bottom right.

Points on the x-axis have y = 0. Points on the y-axis have x = 0. A horizontal line has the same y-value at every point, and a vertical line has the same x-value at every point.

**Worked example 1.** Three corners of a rectangle are A(−2, 1), B(4, 1) and C(4, 5). Find D.

A and B have the same y-value, so AB is horizontal. B and C have the same x-value, so BC is vertical. In a rectangle, D lies directly above A and directly left of C.

D has the x-value of A (−2) and the y-value of C (5), so **D = (−2, 5)**.

Sketch the points: it catches most slips.

## 3.2 Drawing linear graphs

An equation in which x and y appear only to the power 1 gives a straight line. Two points define it, but plot three so that a slip shows up as an off-line point.

**Method A: a table of values** (for equations with y as the subject).

**Worked example 2.** Draw y = 4 − 2x for −1 ≤ x ≤ 3.

| x | −1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 6 | 4 | 2 | 0 | −2 |

Plot (−1, 6), (0, 4), (1, 2), (2, 0) and (3, −2), then join them with a ruler. The graph slopes down from left to right, which matches the negative coefficient of x.

**Method B: intercepts** (for equations such as ax + by = c).

**Worked example 3.** Draw 3x + 4y = 12.

Put x = 0: 4y = 12, so y = 3. The line crosses the y-axis at (0, 3).
Put y = 0: 3x = 12, so x = 4. The line crosses the x-axis at (4, 0).

Join (0, 3) and (4, 0) and extend the line. For a check point, try x = −4: 3(−4) + 4y = 12, so y = 6. The point (−4, 6) should lie on your line.

If the line passes through the origin both intercepts are the same point, so use a table instead.

## 3.3 Gradient of linear graphs

The gradient measures steepness: how far the line goes up for each unit it goes across.

**gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)**

On a drawn graph, choose two points on the line where it crosses grid intersections, draw a right-angled triangle under or over the line, and divide the vertical side by the horizontal side. Read the sign from the picture: a line rising to the right has a positive gradient; one falling to the right has a negative gradient.

Special cases: a horizontal line has gradient 0. A vertical line has no gradient (the change in x is 0, so you cannot divide).

**Worked example 4.** Find the gradient of the line through P(−1, 7) and Q(5, −2).

Gradient = (−2 − 7) ÷ (5 − (−1)) = −9 ÷ 6 = **−3/2**.

Use the same point first on top and bottom. The answer is the same whichever point you call first.

## 3.4 Length and midpoint

**Length.** The segment joining two points is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose sides are the differences in x and in y. Pythagoras' theorem gives

length = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)

**Midpoint.** The midpoint is the mean of the x-values and the mean of the y-values:

midpoint = ((x₁ + x₂) ÷ 2, (y₁ + y₂) ÷ 2)

**Worked example 5.** A(−4, −1) and B(8, 4). Find the length of AB and its midpoint.

Differences: 8 − (−4) = 12 across and 4 − (−1) = 5 up.
Length = √(12² + 5²) = √(144 + 25) = √169 = **13 units**.
Midpoint = ((−4 + 8) ÷ 2, (−1 + 4) ÷ 2) = **(2, 1.5)**.

**Worked example 6.** Find the length between (−2, 3) and (3, 5), giving the answer as a surd and then to 3 significant figures.

Differences: 5 across, 2 up. Length = √(25 + 4) = **√29**. With a calculator, √29 = 5.385..., which is **5.39** to 3 significant figures.

**Worked example 7 (finding an end point).** M(3, −1) is the midpoint of AB, and A is (7, 2). Find B.

The midpoint is halfway, so B is as far from M as A is, in the opposite direction. From A to M the x-value changes by 3 − 7 = −4 and the y-value by −1 − 2 = −3. Apply the same change again from M:

B = (3 − 4, −1 − 3) = **(−1, −4)**.

Check: the midpoint of (7, 2) and (−1, −4) is (3, −1).

## 3.5 Equations of linear graphs

**Forms you meet.**

| Form | Example | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| y = mx + c | y = 2x − 3 | m is the gradient, c is the y-intercept |
| ax + by = c | 3x − 2y = 8 | Rearrange to find the gradient and intercept |
| x = k | x = 4 | Vertical line through (4, 0) |
| y = k | y = −2 | Horizontal line through (0, −2) |

The syllabus says you may be asked for a line in any of these forms and that you should give equations in a fully simplified form. Do not leave the equation half-rearranged.

**Worked example 8 (reading a gradient and intercept).** Find the gradient and y-intercept of 2x − 5y = 15.

Make y the subject. Subtract 2x from both sides: −5y = 15 − 2x. Divide every term by −5: y = −3 + (2/5)x.

So y = (2/5)x − 3. The gradient is **2/5** and the y-intercept is **−3**, which is the point (0, −3).

**Worked example 9 (equation from two points).** Find the equation of the line through (2, −1) and (6, 5).

1. Gradient = (5 − (−1)) ÷ (6 − 2) = 6 ÷ 4 = 3/2.
2. Use y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) with (2, −1): y + 1 = (3/2)(x − 2).
3. Expand: y + 1 = (3/2)x − 3, so **y = (3/2)x − 4**.
4. Check with (6, 5): (3/2)(6) − 4 = 9 − 4 = 5. This agrees.

To give the answer as 3x − 2y = 8, multiply by 2 to get 2y = 3x − 8 and rearrange. Either form is a correct equation, but follow any form the question asks for.

**Equation from a graph.** Read the y-intercept c where the line meets the y-axis. Find the gradient with a triangle. If a line crosses the y-axis at 4 and passes through (3, −2), the gradient is (−2 − 4) ÷ 3 = −2, so y = −2x + 4.

## 3.6 Parallel lines

Parallel lines have the same gradient.

**Worked example 10.** Find the equation of the line parallel to y = −3x + 5 that passes through (2, −4).

The gradient is −3. Substitute the point into y = −3x + c: −4 = −3(2) + c, so c = 2.

The line is **y = −3x + 2**.

**Worked example 11.** Find the line parallel to 4x − 2y = 9 through (3, 1).

Rearrange: −2y = 9 − 4x, so y = 2x − 9/2. The gradient is 2.
Use y − 1 = 2(x − 3), so y = 2x − 5. The line is **y = 2x − 5**.

To show two lines are parallel, rearrange both into y = mx + c and state that the gradients are equal. Write the conclusion; a pair of matching numbers is not enough on its own.

## 3.7 Perpendicular lines

Two lines are perpendicular when the product of their gradients is −1. If one line has gradient m, a line perpendicular to it has gradient −1/m: turn the fraction upside down and change the sign.

| Given gradient | Gradient of a perpendicular line |
|---|---|
| 2 | −1/2 |
| −3/5 | 5/3 |
| 1/4 | −4 |

A horizontal line (y = k) is perpendicular to a vertical line (x = h), even though the rule with m cannot be applied to them.

**Worked example 12.** Find the gradient of a line perpendicular to 2y = 3x + 1.

Divide by 2: y = (3/2)x + 1/2, so the gradient is 3/2. The perpendicular gradient is **−2/3**.

**Worked example 13.** Find the line perpendicular to 3x + 5y = 10 through (6, −2).

Rearrange: 5y = 10 − 3x, so y = −(3/5)x + 2. The gradient is −3/5, so the perpendicular gradient is 5/3.
Use y + 2 = (5/3)(x − 6): y = (5/3)x − 10 − 2, so **y = (5/3)x − 12**.
As an integer form: multiply by 3 to get 3y = 5x − 36, so **5x − 3y = 36**.

**Worked example 14 (perpendicular bisector).** Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of the segment AB, where A is (−5, 2) and B is (7, −6).

A perpendicular bisector cuts the segment at its midpoint at a right angle, so you need the midpoint and the perpendicular gradient.

1. Midpoint = ((−5 + 7) ÷ 2, (2 − 6) ÷ 2) = (1, −2).
2. Gradient of AB = (−6 − 2) ÷ (7 − (−5)) = −8 ÷ 12 = −2/3.
3. Perpendicular gradient = 3/2.
4. Use y + 2 = (3/2)(x − 1), so y = (3/2)x − 7/2.
5. Multiply by 2 for a neat form: **3x − 2y = 7**.

Check: the midpoint (1, −2) gives 3 + 4 = 7. Correct.

**Worked example 15 (a right angle in a triangle).** A(1, 1), B(5, 3) and C(3, 7). Show that angle ABC is a right angle.

Gradient of AB = (3 − 1) ÷ (5 − 1) = 1/2. Gradient of BC = (7 − 3) ÷ (3 − 5) = 4 ÷ (−2) = −2.
The product is (1/2) × (−2) = −1, so AB and BC are perpendicular and angle ABC is 90°.

## Common errors

- Subtracting in different orders on top and bottom of the gradient fraction, which flips the sign.
- Taking the gradient of ax + by = c to be a, or b, or a/b. Always rearrange to y = mx + c first. For 3x + 4y = 12 the gradient is −3/4, not 3/4.
- Forgetting the square root for length, or forgetting to halve for the midpoint.
- Changing the sign but not inverting the fraction (or the reverse) for a perpendicular gradient.
- Substituting the point into the wrong variable when finding c.
- Calling a vertical line y = k, or a horizontal line x = k.
- Leaving a final line equation unsimplified when the question asks for a fully simplified form.
