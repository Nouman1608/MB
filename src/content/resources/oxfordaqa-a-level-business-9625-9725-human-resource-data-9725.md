This guide covers **3.2.2.2 Human resource data** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 (International AS). The section is short in the specification but heavily used in calculation questions. The [Human resources study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-human-resources/) introduces the formulas with one example; this page shows how to compare two sites, read trends, and turn the numbers into judgements. Afterwards, use the [human resource data revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-human-resource-data-9725-revision-notes/) and the [human resource data practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-human-resource-data-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

Students should be able to **calculate and interpret**:

- labour turnover;
- employee retention rates, including the percentage of employees still at the business one year after recruitment;
- labour productivity;
- employee costs as a percentage of turnover;
- labour costs per unit;
- sales and profit per employee.

They should also understand that measures of human resource performance, such as labour productivity, **vary in different contexts**.

## The formulas (9725 Appendix: Formulae and Key data)

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover (%) | number of employees leaving in a year ÷ average number of total employees × 100 |
| Employee retention rate (%) | number of employees at the end of a period ÷ average number of employees at the start of a period × 100 |
| Labour productivity | output of the organisation in a period of time ÷ average number of employees during the period |
| Employee costs as a percentage of turnover (%) | total employee costs in the period ÷ total revenue in the period × 100 |
| Labour costs per unit | total labour costs in the period ÷ units of output produced in the period |
| Sales per employee | total sales revenue in a period ÷ average number of employees in the period |
| Profit per employee | total profit in a period ÷ average number of employees in the period |

**Average number of employees.** If a question gives start and end figures, the average is (start + end) ÷ 2. Using only the end figure is a common error.

**Retention: read the question.** The appendix formula compares end and start headcounts, so new joiners count towards the result and it can exceed 100% when a business is growing. The specification also names a second measure: the **percentage of employees still at the business one year after recruitment** = recruits still employed after a year ÷ number recruited × 100. Use the measure the question asks for, and say which you have used.

## Worked example: comparing two depots

Norland Logistics (invented) runs two parcel depots. Figures are for one year.

| | North depot | South depot |
|---|---|---|
| Employees at start of year | 120 | 84 |
| Employees at end of year | 130 | 76 |
| Employees who left during the year | 30 | 8 |
| Parcels handled | 1,500,000 | 1,120,000 |
| Revenue | $7,500,000 | $5,120,000 |
| Employee costs | $2,250,000 | $1,792,000 |
| Profit | $625,000 | $480,000 |

```
Average employees     North: (120 + 130) ÷ 2 = 125     South: (84 + 76) ÷ 2 = 80

Labour turnover       North: 30 ÷ 125 × 100 = 24%      South: 8 ÷ 80 × 100 = 10%
Labour productivity   North: 1,500,000 ÷ 125 = 12,000  South: 1,120,000 ÷ 80 = 14,000 parcels
Employee costs % rev. North: 2.25 ÷ 7.5 × 100 = 30%    South: 1.792 ÷ 5.12 × 100 = 35%
Labour cost per unit  North: 2,250,000 ÷ 1,500,000 = $1.50
                      South: 1,792,000 ÷ 1,120,000 = $1.60
Sales per employee    North: 7,500,000 ÷ 125 = $60,000 South: 5,120,000 ÷ 80 = $64,000
Profit per employee   North: 625,000 ÷ 125 = $5,000    South: 480,000 ÷ 80 = $6,000
Retention (appendix)  North: 130 ÷ 120 × 100 = 108.3%  South: 76 ÷ 84 × 100 = 90.5%
```

**Interpreting the comparison.**

- South pays more per employee: 1,792,000 ÷ 80 = $22,400 against North's 2,250,000 ÷ 125 = $18,000. That explains its higher employee costs as a share of revenue.
- South's staff are more productive (14,000 against 12,000 parcels each) and far fewer leave (10% against 24%). Better pay may be reducing turnover, and experienced staff work faster.
- Even so, South's labour cost per parcel ($1.60) is slightly above North's ($1.50): the extra productivity does not fully cover the higher pay.
- South earns more profit per employee, so its higher pay is not hurting overall performance.
- North's retention figure above 100% reflects growth (it hired more than it lost), not that nobody left: 30 people did. This is why the appendix retention measure must be read alongside labour turnover.

A strong answer would conclude that North's high turnover is its main HR problem: replacing 30 staff a year means recruitment and training costs and less experienced teams, which may explain its lower productivity.

## The one-year retention measure

Norland's North depot recruited 40 drivers last year. 26 were still employed a year after joining.

```
Percentage still employed after one year = 26 ÷ 40 × 100 = 65%
```

So 35% of new recruits left within a year. That points to problems early in employment: poor selection, weak induction, or a job that does not match what was advertised. It directs managers to recruitment and induction rather than to pay for long-serving staff.

## Reading trends

Single figures say little; trends and comparisons say more.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labour turnover | 12% | 17% | 23% |
| Labour productivity (units per employee) | 4,100 | 3,950 | 3,700 |

Rising turnover alongside falling productivity suggests that as experienced staff leave, newer staff produce less. The business should look for causes (pay, management, conditions) before productivity falls further.

## Why context matters

The specification stresses that measures vary with context. Some examples:

- **Labour productivity** is higher in capital-intensive operations (an automated bottling plant) than in labour-intensive ones (a craft furniture workshop), so comparing them says nothing about effort.
- **Employee costs as a percentage of turnover** are naturally high in services such as consultancy or care, and low in businesses that resell goods, where most costs are stock.
- **Labour turnover** is usually higher in sectors with many part-time, seasonal or student staff, such as fast food, than in professional firms. Some turnover can be healthy, bringing new ideas.
- **Sales per employee** rises if staff are replaced by outsourcing, even if total costs do not fall.

So compare like with like: the same business over time, similar sites, or competitors in the same sector.

## Using HR data in decisions

- **High labour turnover** → investigate causes; review pay, conditions and management; improve selection and induction.
- **Low productivity** → training, equipment, better methods, motivation.
- **Rising employee costs as % of turnover** → is pay rising faster than revenue? Can productivity rise?
- **Rising labour cost per unit** → threatens margins or competitiveness on price.

HR data rarely gives the answer by itself. It tells managers where to look.

## Common mistakes

- Using the end-of-year headcount instead of the average.
- Forgetting × 100 for percentage measures.
- Saying "labour productivity rose, so staff work harder" when output may have risen because of new machinery.
- Judging a turnover rate as high or low without a comparison.
- Confusing employee costs as a percentage of turnover (a ratio of revenue) with labour cost per unit (a cost per item).

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the average-employees step separately; it often carries a mark.
- **Interpret**: say what the figure means, compare it with something in the case, and suggest a cause.
- **Analyse**: link measures together, such as turnover → experience → productivity → labour cost per unit.
- **Evaluate**: judge how far the data supports a decision, mentioning context and what further data would help.
