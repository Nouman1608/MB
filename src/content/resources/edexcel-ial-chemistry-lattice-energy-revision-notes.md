## Definitions (12.12 and 12.16)

Every definition below applies under standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K.

| Term | Definition | Example equation | Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔatH | Enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms forms from the element in its standard state | ½I₂(s) → I(g) | Always + |
| First electron affinity | Enthalpy change when each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms gains one electron, forming one mole of gaseous 1− ions | Br(g) + e⁻ → Br⁻(g) | Usually − |
| Second electron affinity | Enthalpy change when each 1− ion in one mole of gaseous 1− ions gains one electron | S⁻(g) + e⁻ → S²⁻(g) | + |
| Lattice energy | Enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid forms from its gaseous ions | Ca²⁺(g) + 2I⁻(g) → CaI₂(s) | Always − |
| Enthalpy change of solution, ΔsolH | Enthalpy change when one mole of ionic solid dissolves to give an infinitely dilute solution | CaI₂(s) + aq → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) | + or − |
| Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔhydH, of an ion | Enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions forms one mole of aqueous ions | I⁻(g) + aq → I⁻(aq) | Always − |

Remember: lattice energy in this specification is the exothermic formation of the lattice, not its break-up.

## Key relationships

| Use | Relationship |
|---|---|
| Born-Haber cycle (12.13) | ΔfH = ΔatH(metal) + ionisation energies + ΔatH(non-metal) + electron affinities + lattice energy |
| Solution cycle (12.17) | ΔsolH = −(lattice energy) + Σ ΔhydH(ions) |
| Solubility (12.19) | ΔStotal = ΔSsystem − ΔsolH / T (convert ΔsolH to J mol⁻¹) |
| Covalent character (12.14) | Compare experimental (Born-Haber) lattice energy with theoretical (electrostatic) value |

## Method: any Born-Haber calculation

1. Write the formula and count the ions. For a 1:2 compound such as CaI₂ you need two moles of I atoms and two electron affinities.
2. List every step from elements to gaseous ions: atomise the metal, ionise it (first, then second if 2+), atomise the non-metal, add electrons.
3. Write ΔfH = sum of all steps + lattice energy.
4. Rearrange for the unknown. Keep every sign from the data.
5. Check the sign of the answer makes sense (lattice energy negative, ΔatH positive).

**Worked reminder.** Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH(RbI) = −334; ΔatH(Rb) = +81; first ionisation energy of Rb = +403; ΔatH(I) = +107; first electron affinity of I = −295.

Lattice energy = −334 − (81 + 403 + 107 − 295) = −334 − 296 = **−630 kJ mol⁻¹**

## Method: drawing a Born-Haber energy level diagram

- Put the elements in their standard states on a zero line, for example Rb(s) + ½I₂(s).
- Draw endothermic steps (atomisation, ionisation) as upward arrows.
- Draw the first electron affinity as a downward arrow.
- Draw the lattice energy as a long downward arrow from the gaseous ions to the solid.
- Draw ΔfH as a downward arrow from the elements to the solid (for an exothermic ΔfH).
- Label every level with species and state symbols. Electrons are written on the level where they are free, for example Rb⁺(g) + e⁻ + I(g).

## Method: enthalpy change of solution

1. Reverse the lattice energy (it becomes positive).
2. Add the hydration enthalpies, multiplying by the number of each ion.
3. Sum them.

**Worked reminder.** Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹): lattice energy of CaI₂ = −2074; ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) = −1616; ΔhydH(I⁻) = −297.

ΔsolH = 2074 + (−1616) + 2(−297) = **−136 kJ mol⁻¹**

On an energy level diagram, the gaseous ions sit at the top. The solid lies 2074 below them and the aqueous ions lie 2210 below them, so the solution ends 136 below the solid.

## Covalent character and polarisation (12.14, 12.15)

- **Theoretical** lattice energy assumes perfect spherical ions and pure electrostatic attraction.
- **Experimental** lattice energy comes from a Born-Haber cycle, using measured data.
- Close agreement: the compound is close to purely ionic.
- Experimental noticeably more exothermic: extra attraction from partial covalent bonding.
- Cause: the cation polarises (distorts) the anion's electron cloud, pulling electron density between the nuclei.
- Most polarisation: small, highly charged cation with a large, highly charged anion.

## Charge and radius (12.18)

| Change | Effect on lattice energy | Effect on ΔhydH |
|---|---|---|
| Higher ionic charge | More exothermic | More exothermic |
| Smaller ionic radius | More exothermic | More exothermic |

Charge is usually the bigger factor. Example comparisons: Ca²⁺ is hydrated more exothermically than K⁺; LiF has a more exothermic lattice energy than LiI.

## Solubility (12.19)

- An endothermic ΔsolH does not mean a salt is insoluble. Check ΔStotal.
- ΔSsystem for dissolving has two parts: ions leaving the lattice (more disorder) and water ordered round the ions (less disorder). For small, highly charged ions ΔSsystem can be negative.
- Group 2 sulfates: large anion, so lattice energy changes little down the group while cation hydration becomes much less exothermic. ΔsolH more endothermic, so solubility falls.
- Group 2 hydroxides: small anion, so lattice energy becomes much less exothermic down the group, faster than hydration does. ΔsolH more favourable, so solubility rises.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Atomisation vs bond enthalpy:** ΔatH(Cl) is per mole of Cl atoms, so it is half the Cl–Cl bond enthalpy.
- **Lattice energy vs ΔsolH:** lattice energy starts from gaseous ions; ΔsolH starts from the solid.
- **ΔhydH vs ΔsolH:** hydration starts from gaseous ions of one kind; solution starts from the solid compound.
- **First vs second electron affinity:** first usually exothermic, second always endothermic.
- **Covalent character vs covalent compound:** a large experimental–theoretical gap shows partial covalency, not a molecular substance.

## Quick self-test

1. Write the equation for the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of bromine.
2. Why is the second electron affinity of oxygen endothermic?
3. Write the equation that represents the lattice energy of strontium chloride.
4. Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹) to find the lattice energy of NaBr: ΔhydH(Na⁺) = −406; ΔhydH(Br⁻) = −332; ΔsolH(NaBr) = −1.
5. Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹) to find ΔfH(LiBr): ΔatH(Li) = +159; first ionisation energy of Li = +520; ΔatH(Br) = +112; first electron affinity of Br = −325; lattice energy of LiBr = −805.
6. Which of these has the most exothermic lattice energy: KCl, CaCl₂, CaO? Explain briefly.
7. Arrange Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and K⁺ in order of increasingly exothermic ΔhydH.
8. A salt has experimental lattice energy −850 and theoretical −848 kJ mol⁻¹. What does this suggest?
9. A salt has ΔsolH = +25.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔSsystem = +60.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate ΔStotal at 298 K and say whether it dissolves.
10. For the salt in question 9, above what temperature does dissolving become feasible?

### Answers

1. ½Br₂(l) → Br(g)
2. The electron is added to an O⁻ ion, which repels it; energy must be supplied to overcome the repulsion.
3. Sr²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) → SrCl₂(s)
4. Lattice energy = ΣΔhydH − ΔsolH = (−406 − 332) − (−1) = **−737 kJ mol⁻¹**
5. ΔfH = 159 + 520 + 112 − 325 − 805 = **−339 kJ mol⁻¹**
6. **CaO**: both ions carry a double charge, so attraction is strongest. CaCl₂ has a 2+ cation but 1− anions; KCl has only single charges.
7. K⁺ < Na⁺ < Mg²⁺ (K⁺ is largest; Mg²⁺ is smallest with the highest charge).
8. The values agree closely, so the bonding is almost purely ionic, with very little polarisation.
9. ΔSsurroundings = −25 000 / 298 = −83.9; ΔStotal = 60.0 − 83.9 = **−23.9 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹**, so it does not dissolve to give a standard solution at 298 K.
10. ΔStotal = 0 when T = 25 000 / 60.0 = **417 K** (assuming ΔsolH and ΔSsystem do not change with temperature).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining lattice energy as the energy to break the lattice. In this specification it is the formation of one mole of solid from gaseous ions.
- Missing "one mole of gaseous atoms" in the atomisation definition, or using the full bond enthalpy for a diatomic element.
- Missing the "(g)" on ions in electron affinity and lattice energy equations.
- Leaving out the second ionisation energy for a 2+ cation.
- Not doubling the anion's atomisation and electron affinity in 1:2 compounds.
- Sign slips when rearranging: write the full Hess equation before substituting.
- Mixing kJ and J when calculating ΔSsurroundings for dissolving.
- Explaining covalent character with "the bonding is more covalent" without mentioning polarisation of the anion by the cation.
- Explaining the Group 2 trends with only one of lattice energy or hydration enthalpy. You need to compare how both change.
