## What 4.5 asks for

| Official point | Where it is covered |
|---|---|
| the valuation of inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | sections 1 and 2 |
| how to calculate the value of inventory | sections 2 and 3 |
| the effect of an incorrect valuation of inventory on gross profit, profit for the year, equity and asset valuation | sections 4 and 5 |

## 1. The rule: lower of cost and net realisable value

**Cost** is what the business paid for the units. In a question it is
given as a cost per unit.

**Net realisable value (NRV)** is the amount the business expects to
receive when it sells the goods, less any costs still to be paid before
the sale is complete. Typical deductions are repairs, repackaging,
commission to a sales agent and delivery to the customer.

```
Net realisable value per unit = expected selling price
                                − costs still to be paid
```

The value of each item of inventory is **whichever is lower, cost or NRV**.
There are two reasons.

- **Prudence** (section 7.1): a loss that is expected is recognised as soon
  as it is foreseen, so the cost of unsellable goods is charged to the
  year in which they lose value.
- **Realisation and historic cost** (section 7.1): a profit is recognised
  only when the goods are sold, so inventory is never shown above what it
  cost, even when it could be sold for more.

If NRV is higher than cost, cost is used. If NRV is lower, NRV is used and
the difference is a reduction in the closing inventory figure.

## 2. Calculating the value of inventory, item by item

Compare cost and NRV for **each item (or each group of identical items)**
and multiply the lower figure by the number of units.

**Worked example 1.** Marisol Pendleton owns Beresford Cycles. At
31 December 2028 the inventory count gave these figures (dollars):

| Item | Units | Cost per unit | Selling price | Costs still to pay | NRV per unit | Lower | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Helmets | 30 | 22 | 35 | 4 commission | 31 | 22 (cost) | 660 |
| Child bike K | 12 | 140 | 150 after repair | 25 repair + 10 commission | 115 | 115 (NRV) | 1,380 |
| Pumps | 50 | 9 | 12 | 1 commission | 11 | 9 (cost) | 450 |
| Frames | 8 | 310 | 340 | 45 delivery | 295 | 295 (NRV) | 2,360 |
| **Closing inventory** | | | | | | | **4,850** |

Total cost is 5,270 and total NRV is 5,220. Comparing the two totals would
give 5,220, but that lets the extra value in the helmets and pumps hide the
losses on child bike K and the frames. Item by item, closing inventory is
**4,850**, which is 370 lower. Section 4.5 does not list methods such as
FIFO or average cost for choosing a unit cost, so a question will give you the cost per unit.

## 3. Adjusting a count taken on another date

A business does not always count its inventory on the last day of the
year. If the count is on another date, start from the counted value at
cost and move it to the year-end date using the transactions in between.
Sales are recorded at selling price, so convert them to **cost** first
(using the mark-up or margin the question gives). Mark-up is profit expressed
as a percentage of cost. Margin is profit expressed as a percentage of selling price.

**Count after the year end:** take off purchases made since year end, add
back the cost of goods sold since year end, and adjust for returns.

**Worked example 2.** Imara Sandrine's year end at Ravenscroft Kitchenware
is 31 December 2028. She counted inventory on 5 January 2029 and valued it
at cost at 23,640. Between 1 and 5 January:

- purchases of 3,150 were received into inventory
- goods costing 420 were returned to a supplier
- sales were 5,320 at selling price, and the gross profit margin is 30%
- customers returned goods with a selling price of 350

```
Cost of goods sold      5,320 × 70%                = 3,724
Cost of goods returned    350 × 70%                =   245
Count on 5 January                                   23,640
Less purchases received                              (3,150)
Add goods returned to supplier (left before count)      420
Add cost of goods sold                                3,724
Less goods returned by customers (back in the count)   (245)
Inventory at 31 December 2028                        24,389
```

**Count before the year end:** do the reverse. Add purchases made after
the count, and subtract the cost of goods sold after the count.

**Correcting a draft inventory figure.** Questions often list problems with
the first count. Work through them one at a time.

**Worked example 3.** Soraya Danforth's draft closing inventory at Danforth
Garden Supplies was 41,820 at cost.

1. Goods costing 2,150 were invoiced to a customer on 30 December but were
   still in the warehouse at the count. The sale is recorded, so the goods
   are deducted.
2. Damaged goods costing 1,800 were counted at cost. They can be sold for
   1,300 with selling costs of 150, so NRV is 1,150 and the reduction is 650.
3. Goods costing 960 arrived on 30 December and were not counted. They are
   added.

Corrected closing inventory is 41,820 − 2,150 − 650 + 960 = **39,980**.

## 4. The effect of an incorrect valuation

Closing inventory is subtracted in the calculation of cost of sales and is
shown as a current asset:

```
Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
```

If closing inventory is **understated**, cost of sales is **overstated**,
so gross profit and profit for the year are **understated** by the same
amount. Equity (capital) rises by the profit for the year, so equity is
understated, and the inventory figure in current assets is understated.
An **overstatement** has the opposite effect on every figure.

The error does not stay in one year. Closing inventory becomes the next
year's opening inventory, so in the next year the effects on cost of sales,
on gross profit and on profit for the year are reversed.

## 5. Worked example: an understatement over two years

Yusra Ellsworth sells audio equipment. At the end of Year 1 a batch of
goods costing 3,000 in a storeroom was not counted, so closing inventory
was recorded as 15,000 instead of 18,000. Year 2 closing inventory (20,000)
was correct. Opening capital was 60,000 and drawings were 12,000 in Year 1
and 13,000 in Year 2.

| ($) | Year 1 recorded | Year 1 correct | Year 2 recorded | Year 2 correct |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 150,000 | 150,000 | 165,000 | 165,000 |
| Opening inventory | 14,000 | 14,000 | 15,000 | 18,000 |
| Purchases | 96,000 | 96,000 | 104,000 | 104,000 |
| Closing inventory | (15,000) | (18,000) | (20,000) | (20,000) |
| Cost of sales | 95,000 | 92,000 | 99,000 | 102,000 |
| Gross profit | 55,000 | 58,000 | 66,000 | 63,000 |
| Expenses | 38,000 | 38,000 | 41,000 | 41,000 |
| Profit for the year | 17,000 | 20,000 | 25,000 | 22,000 |
| Equity at year end | 65,000 | 68,000 | 77,000 | 77,000 |

In Year 1, gross profit, profit for the year, equity and the inventory
asset were each understated by 3,000. In Year 2, gross profit and profit
for the year were overstated by 3,000. Equity at the end of Year 2 is
correct, because the two profit errors cancel, and so is the Year 2 inventory
asset. Total profit over the two years is 42,000 either way. The error
moves profit between years and gives a misleading comparison of results (see
[Analysis and Interpretation](/resources/igcse-accounting-analysis-and-interpretation/)).

The same logic applies if inventory was overstated: for example, goods
still valued at cost when their NRV was lower. Gross profit, profit for
the year, equity and the inventory asset are all too high, and the next
year's profit is too low.

## Common errors

- Comparing total cost with total NRV instead of working item by item.
- Deducting selling costs from cost instead of from the selling price.
- Using NRV when it is higher than cost.
- Forgetting that a repair cost is deducted in finding NRV when the selling
  price quoted is the price after repair.
- Using the selling price of goods sold in a count-date adjustment without
  converting to cost.
- Confusing a mark-up, which is based on cost, with a margin, which is based on selling price.
- Stating the effect on the first year only and leaving out the opposite
  effect in the second year.
- Saying equity is affected in the second year: it is corrected, because
  the two years' profit errors cancel.
