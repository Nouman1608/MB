## Section A

**1.** Define *marine aquaculture*. **[1]**

**2.** State the **three** groups of marine organisms farmed in marine aquaculture. **[3]**

**3.** Define *bycatch*. **[1]**

**4.** State what is meant by a *quota*. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** benefits of marine aquaculture. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how overfishing of a small prey fish can affect other species in a marine food chain. **[3]**

**7.** The table shows the annual catch of a fish species in one fishery.

| Year | Catch (thousand tonnes) |
|---|---|
| 2000 | 64 |
| 2005 | 58 |
| 2010 | 41 |
| 2015 | 16 |

(a) Calculate the percentage fall in catch between 2000 and 2015.
(b) Suggest **two** reasons for the fall. **[4]**

**8.** Describe and explain **three** negative impacts of a fish farm in a sheltered coastal bay. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why farming seaweed has fewer negative impacts than farming fish in cages. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how **(a)** increasing the mesh size of nets and **(b)** closed seasons help to conserve fish stocks. **[4]**

**11.** Pole and line fishing is described as a sustainable method. Explain why, and give **one** limitation. **[3]**

**12.** A sea is fished by boats from four countries. Explain why international agreements are needed, and why implementation and monitoring are important. **[4]**

**13.** "Marine aquaculture will solve the problem of overfishing." Discuss this statement. **[6]**

**14.** A government limits the number of days each boat may fish. Explain why the total catch may fall less than expected. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The farming of marine species in captivity [1].

**2.** Fish [1]; crustaceans [1]; seaweeds [1].

**3.** Organisms caught unintentionally while fishing for a different, target species [1].

**4.** A maximum mass or amount of a species [1] that may be caught in a set period, such as a year [1].

**5.** Reduced exploitation of natural fisheries [1]; increased food supply for humans [1].

**6.** Predators of the prey fish, such as seabirds, seals or larger fish, have less food [1]; their breeding success falls and their populations decline [1]; organisms eaten by the prey fish, such as zooplankton, may increase because they are eaten less [1].

**7. (a)** Fall = 64 − 16 = 48 thousand tonnes [1]; 48 / 64 × 100 = 75% [1].
**(b)** Any two, 1 mark each: overfishing removed breeding adults faster than they were replaced, so fewer young were produced [1]; small mesh nets caught juveniles before they bred [1]. Also accept: larger or more boats, sonar, increasing demand, bycatch of juveniles.

**8.** Any three, 2 marks each. Risk of escape [1]: escaped farmed fish compete with wild fish for food and space and may interbreed with them [1]. Risk of disease [1]: fish crowded in cages spread disease and parasites, which pass to wild fish nearby [1]. Nutrient enrichment from waste [1]: uneaten feed and faeces add nutrients, causing algal blooms and low oxygen in the bay [1]. Also accept: source of food for farmed fish, as feed is made from wild-caught fish; energy usage; changes to local food webs; each with an explanation.

**9.** Seaweeds need no feed, so no wild fish are caught to supply them [1]; there is no feed waste or faeces, and seaweeds absorb nutrients from the water, so there is little nutrient enrichment [1]; seaweeds do not suffer the same crowding disease risk or escape problems as caged fish, so wild fish populations are not affected in the same way [1].

**10. (a)** Young, small fish swim through the larger holes [1], so they survive to breed and replace the population [1]. **(b)** Fishing is banned at certain times, usually the breeding season [1], so fish can spawn without disturbance and numbers recover [1].

**11.** Fish are caught one at a time [1], so there is very little bycatch and unwanted fish can be released alive [1]. Limitation: catches are smaller, so income per trip is lower and it cannot supply large markets [1].

**12.** Fish move freely across national boundaries, so one country's limits are useless if others overfish the same stock [1]; an agreement sets shared limits such as quotas for each country [1]. Implementation means each country must actually turn the agreement into rules for its own boats [1]; monitoring, such as inspections and vessel tracking, checks that limits are kept, otherwise boats may cheat [1].

**13.** For: aquaculture reduces exploitation of natural fisheries because farmed fish replace some wild catch [1]; it increases the food supply reliably and can expand as demand grows [1]. Against: many farmed fish are fed on fishmeal made from wild-caught fish, so wild fishing continues [1]; escapes and disease from farms can harm the wild stocks the farm was meant to protect [1]; demand for fish keeps rising, so wild fisheries still need quotas, protected areas and other controls [1]. Judgement: aquaculture can reduce pressure, especially seaweed and crustacean farming that needs little wild feed, but it will not solve overfishing alone [1].

**14.** On the days they are allowed out, boats may fish more intensively, for longer hours or with bigger nets, so the catch per day rises [1]; owners may also add more boats to the fleet, so total effort is not reduced as much as planned [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving "fish farming" as the definition and forgetting crustaceans and seaweeds.
- Naming an impact such as "disease" without explaining how it affects wild populations.
- Saying larger mesh catches more fish.
- Agreeing or disagreeing with a statement without considering the other side.
- Working out percentage change from the final value rather than the starting value.
- Describing a management strategy without saying how fishers might respond to it, which is often the limitation the mark scheme wants.
