**What do these questions practise?** They cover every learning outcome in Section 3 Power of
Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448 Paper 2: extraction methods, non-renewable supplies and their
transport, electricity generation, renewables and hydel schemes, and the reasons for unreliable
supply. Short recall items come first, followed by explain and evaluate items like the final parts
of Paper 2 questions.

## Section A: recall and description

**1.** State the difference between a renewable and a non-renewable source of electricity. **[2]**

**2.** Name **one** coalfield and **one** natural gas field in Pakistan. **[2]**

**3.** Draw a simple labelled diagram to show how coal is obtained by adit mining. **[3]**

**4.** Name **two** methods, other than pipeline, used to transport oil within Pakistan. **[2]**

**5.** Name **one** nuclear power station site and **one** wind power area in Pakistan. **[2]**

## Section B: explanation

**6.** Explain why most of the coal mined in Pakistan is used close to where it is mined. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why Pakistan has to import large amounts of oil. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how natural gas is supplied to areas of Pakistan away from the pipeline network, and
the limitations of this method. **[4]**

**9.** Describe the physical and human conditions that favour the building of a multi-purpose hydel
scheme. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why the supply of electricity is not sufficient or reliable in many parts of
Pakistan. **[5]**

## Section C: evaluation

**11.** "Small-scale solar power is the best way to supply electricity to remote villages in
Pakistan." To what extent do you agree? Give reasons and refer to examples. **[6]**

**12.** Assess whether Pakistan should rely more on Thar coal or on wind power to increase electricity
generation. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A renewable source is naturally replenished and will not run out, for example water, wind or
sunlight [1]. A non-renewable source cannot be replaced once used, for example coal, oil or natural
gas [1].

**2.** Coalfield: Thar, Lakhra or the Salt Range [1]. Gas field: Sui (or another named field in
Sindh or Balochistan) [1].

**3.** One mark each for: a hillside drawn with a coal seam outcropping or lying near-horizontally
inside it [1]; a horizontal tunnel (adit) driven from the valley side into the seam [1]; a label
showing coal being carried out of the tunnel entrance, for example by tubs or a conveyor [1].

**4.** Any two of: rail tankers, road tankers, coastal tankers by sea [2].

**5.** Nuclear: Chashma, or the coastal site near Karachi [1]. Wind: the Gharo–Jhimpir corridor in
Sindh [1].

**6.** Most local coal is low-grade lignite with high ash and moisture [1], so it has a low heat
value per tonne [1]. Transporting large, heavy amounts of low-value coal is costly, so it is
cheaper to build power stations at or near the mine and send electricity by transmission line
instead [1].

**7.** Domestic oil fields, mainly on the Potwar Plateau and in Sindh, are small [1] and produce only
a small share of what the country needs [1]. Demand is high and rising from transport, industry
and thermal power stations [1]. Exploration is costly and new finds have not kept pace with demand,
so the gap is filled with crude oil and refined products imported, mainly from the Gulf [1].

**8.** Gas is bottled as LPG and carried in cylinders by road to homes and businesses [1–2].
Limitations, any two: cylinders cost more than piped gas [1]; supply depends on roads, which may be
poor or blocked in mountain or desert areas [1]; supply can be irregular, especially in winter
when demand peaks [1]; cylinders carry a safety risk if handled badly [1].

**9.** Physical (up to 3): a narrow, deep valley to keep the dam short [1]; strong, impermeable rock
for foundations and to hold water [1]; a large, reliable river flow fed by snowmelt and monsoon
rain [1]; a large area upstream for a reservoir [1]. Human (up to 3): capital, often from
international loans [1]; engineering expertise [1]; demand for irrigation water and electricity
downstream [1]; few people to be resettled when the reservoir floods land [1]. Maximum 5, with both
physical and human factors needed for full marks.

**10.** Any five developed points: demand has grown faster than generating capacity [1]; many
thermal stations rely on expensive imported oil and gas, so they cannot always run [1]; hydel
output falls in winter when river flow is low [1]; reservoirs silt up, reducing storage [1]; high
transmission and distribution losses, including theft [1]; remote areas are far from the grid and
costly to connect [1]; this leads to load-shedding [1].

**11.** Level-marked answer. Points for: Pakistan has high sunshine across most of the year [1];
panels can be installed without a grid connection, so they suit scattered villages in Balochistan
or the Thar [1]; low running costs [1]. Points against: no output at night and less in cloudy
weather [1]; batteries add cost and need replacing [1]; dust lowers efficiency [1]; output may be
too small for machinery or industry [1]. Alternatives: micro-hydel suits mountain valleys with fast
streams, so solar is not always best [1]. A clear judgement linked to type of location earns the
top mark.

**12.** Thar coal, for: very large local reserves reduce fuel imports and help the balance of trade
[1]; provides steady, round-the-clock output [1]; brings jobs to a poor desert area [1]. Against:
low-grade lignite produces high emissions and ash [1]; open cast mining damages land and needs
water in a dry area [1]; local people may be displaced [1]. Wind, for: no fuel cost or emissions
[1]; steady winds in southern Sindh near demand centres [1]. Against: output varies with wind speed
[1]; turbines and new transmission lines need high capital [1]. Credit a judgement such as
combining both, with wind expanded for sustainability and coal used to cut imports in the short
term. Maximum 6.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a 6-mark evaluation answer be?**
Aim for both sides with at least two developed
points each, a named example, and a one-sentence judgement at the end.

**Will diagrams be asked for?**
The syllabus states that candidates should describe extraction
methods with the help of a simple diagram, so practise drawing open cast, adit, shaft and drilling
diagrams.
