Condensed recall for **5.2 Tourism and sport at the local and national scale** (IB DP Geography, Option E: Leisure, tourism and sport). See the [Leisure, tourism and sport study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-study-guide/).

## Types of tourism

Domestic and international; mass and niche; beach, cultural, heritage, adventure, ecotourism, urban.

- **Mass tourism:** large numbers, packaged and standardised, often at coastal resorts.
- **Niche tourism:** smaller markets built around a special interest, such as wildlife, food, pilgrimage or extreme sport.
- **Urban tourism:** visits to cities for heritage, culture, shopping, events and business. Tourist activity tends to cluster in a historic core or waterfront.
- **Rural tourism:** countryside visits for walking, scenery and farm stays; pressure concentrates at **honeypot** sites, attractions that draw large crowds to a small area.

## Hierarchy of facilities

Leisure and sports facilities form a **hierarchy**, like shops and services:

| Level | Example | Threshold and sphere of influence |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Playground, park, community pitch | Small threshold; serves a neighbourhood |
| District | Leisure centre, swimming pool | Needs a town-sized population |
| Regional | Professional football stadium, large arena | Draws spectators from a whole region |
| National | National stadium, major heritage site | Serves the whole country and international visitors |

- **Threshold population:** the minimum number of users needed to support a facility.
- **Sphere of influence (catchment area):** the area from which a facility draws its users; it is larger for higher-order facilities.
- Users usually show **distance decay**: the number of visitors falls with distance from the facility.

## Models (evaluate, do not treat as fact)

| Model | Idea |
|---|---|
| Butler's tourism area life cycle | Exploration, involvement, development, consolidation, stagnation, then decline or rejuvenation |
| Doxey's irritation index | Host attitudes move from euphoria through apathy and annoyance to antagonism as visitor numbers rise |
| Plog's model | Links traveller personality types to destination popularity |

Limits: not every destination follows Butler's curve, and management can alter its path.

### Applying the models

- **Butler:** British seaside resorts such as Blackpool grew rapidly in the age of rail travel, then stagnated and declined when cheap package holidays to sunnier destinations became available from the late twentieth century. Attempts at rejuvenation include new attractions, events and conference business. Stagnation is often marked by reaching **carrying capacity**, ageing infrastructure and a fall in visitor spending.
- **Plog:** **allocentric** (venturer) travellers seek new, unspoilt places; **psychocentric** (dependable) travellers prefer familiar, safe, developed ones; most people are **mid-centric**. As a destination develops, its visitors shift from allocentric towards psychocentric, which links Plog to Butler.
- **Doxey:** useful for explaining resident protests against overcrowding in popular cities, but attitudes vary within a community: residents who earn from tourism may stay positive while others become hostile.

## Carrying capacity

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Physical | The maximum number of people a site can physically hold, such as a beach or stadium |
| Ecological (environmental) | The level of use beyond which habitats and wildlife are damaged |
| Perceptual (social) | The level of crowding beyond which visitors' or residents' enjoyment falls |

Perceptual capacity is often reached first in wilderness areas, where visitors expect solitude.

## Impacts

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Employment, income, multiplier effects, foreign exchange | Seasonal work, low wages, leakage of profits, price inflation |
| Social and cultural | Cultural exchange, preservation of traditions | Commodification, crowding, displacement |
| Environmental | Funding for conservation | Pressure on habitats and water, waste, erosion, carbon emissions |

Always say **for whom** an impact is positive or negative.

**Multiplier effect:** tourist spending in a hotel pays wages and buys supplies from local businesses, whose owners and workers then spend their income locally, so the total effect on the economy is larger than the first spending. Leakage weakens the multiplier.

## Sport at local and national scale

- Local clubs support health and community.
- National stadiums and events promote identity and regeneration.
- Large events can bring investment and infrastructure but may leave costly, underused facilities.
- Evaluate **legacy**: who benefits, and for how long?

### Case study: London 2012 and Stratford

The Olympic Park was built on former industrial and contaminated land in Stratford, east London. Legacy plans converted the main stadium for use by a football club and athletics, reused other venues, and added new housing, parkland and transport links, with a large shopping centre next to the site. Supporters point to regeneration of a deprived area; critics argue that rising house prices and rents meant many long-standing residents gained less than newcomers, an example of gentrification. A further example of reuse is the stadium built for the 2002 Commonwealth Games in Manchester, which became a Premier League football ground.

## Worked check

## Model paragraph

*Evaluate the usefulness of Butler's model for explaining the growth of a resort.* "Butler's model helps explain why a resort such as Blackpool grew, stagnated and then declined, because it links visitor numbers to accessibility, investment and competition. It also highlights management choices at the stagnation stage. However, it is descriptive rather than predictive: it cannot say when a stage will begin, it assumes a single curve for the whole resort, and many destinations, particularly cities, never decline because they constantly diversify. It is therefore useful as a framework but not as a forecast."

## Exam traps

- Listing impacts without saying for whom.
- Using Butler's model as fact.
- Writing a general holiday description rather than geography.
- Ignoring leakage when judging economic benefit.
- Confusing threshold (minimum users) with sphere of influence (catchment area).
- Treating the whole host community as holding one attitude in Doxey's index.
