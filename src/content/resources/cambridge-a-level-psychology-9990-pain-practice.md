**This practice set covers topic 3.3, Pain, in Specialist Option 3 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All patients and data are fictional.

Related: [Pain study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-pain/) · [Pain revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-pain-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** differences between acute and chronic pain. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why specificity theory cannot account for phantom limb pain. **[2]**

**3.** Outline mirror treatment for phantom limb pain. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between attention diversion and cognitive redefinition as ways of managing pain. **[4]**

**5.** Pain can be measured in several ways.

**(a)** Describe the McGill pain questionnaire and the visual analogue scale. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate ways of measuring pain, including a discussion of idiographic versus nomothetic approaches. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Ingrid has had back pain for eight months. Her doctor wants to know how the pain affects her movement and posture during the day.

**(a)** Suggest which measure of pain the doctor should use, and explain why. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way to make this measure reliable. **[2]**

**7.** Brudvik et al. (2016) compared pain ratings by children, parents and physicians.

**(a)** State the order of the three groups' mean ratings, from highest to lowest. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** practical implication of these findings for emergency departments. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the study can be described as having high ecological validity. **[2]**

**8.** A physiotherapist offers TENS to patients with knee pain.

**(a)** Using gate control theory, explain how TENS might reduce pain. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest how the physiotherapist could design a study to test whether TENS works better than a placebo. **[4]**

**9.** A psychologist asks 20 patients to rate their pain on a 10 cm visual analogue scale before and after a course of non-pain imagery training (fictional data).

| | Before training | After training |
|---|---|---|
| Mean rating (cm) | 6.8 | 4.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean reduction in pain rating. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the experimental design. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason, other than the training, why ratings might fall. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how gate control theory accounts for the finding that soldiers sometimes do not feel serious wounds until after a battle. **[3]**

**11.** Give **one** strength and **one** weakness of biochemical treatment of pain. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for any two: acute pain is short-term, chronic pain persists, usually beyond three months (1); acute pain has a clear cause, chronic pain may have no ongoing damage (1); acute pain fades with healing, chronic pain continues after healing (1).

**2.** Up to 2 marks. Specificity theory says pain results from signals sent by pain receptors in damaged tissue (1); in phantom limb pain the limb is absent, so there are no receptors to send signals, yet pain is felt (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. A mirror is placed so that the patient sees the reflection of the intact limb where the missing limb would be (1). The patient moves the intact limb while watching the reflection (1). The visual feedback that the missing limb is moving can reduce phantom pain, as in the case study by MacLachlan et al. (2004) (1).

**4.** Up to 4 marks. Attention diversion means focusing attention on something other than the pain (1), e.g. watching a film during a painful procedure (1). Cognitive redefinition means changing how the pain is thought about, reinterpreting it in a more rational, less threatening way (1), e.g. telling oneself "this sensation means the muscle is healing" (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks, 3 per measure. McGill: the patient chooses words from groups describing the sensory quality of pain, e.g. stabbing (1), the affective quality, e.g. exhausting (1), and the evaluative intensity, with a body diagram for location (1). VAS: a line, typically 10 cm, from "no pain" to "worst possible pain" (1); the patient marks a point on the line (1), and the distance from the "no pain" end gives a score (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Idiographic versus nomothetic*: the McGill questionnaire and clinical interviews capture the individual's own experience of pain (idiographic), while the VAS and numeric scales give scores that can be compared across patients (nomothetic); both are needed for treatment and research.
- *Subjective data*: self-reports depend on honesty and ability to describe pain; Brudvik et al. showed raters differ.
- *Observational measures*: the UAB scale avoids self-report but depends on trained observers and can be affected by patients suppressing or exaggerating behaviour.
- *Practicality*: the VAS is quick; McGill is slow and needs language skills.
- *Use with children*: age-adapted scales or parents' ratings are needed.
- *Conclusion*: no single measure is complete; combining self-report and observation improves validity.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. The UAB pain behaviour scale (1), because it rates observable pain behaviours such as posture and mobility (1), which is exactly what the doctor wants to know, and it does not rely only on Ingrid's report (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Train two observers and have them rate Ingrid independently (1), then check inter-rater reliability by correlating their ratings (1).

**7.**

**(a)** Children, then parents, then physicians (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Physicians underestimated children's pain (1), so departments should use children's self-ratings or parents' ratings when deciding on pain relief (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. It was carried out in a real emergency department with children who were actually in pain (1), so the ratings reflect real clinical situations, not artificial tasks (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. TENS passes a mild electrical current through electrodes on the skin (1). This stimulates large-diameter fibres (1), which close the gate in the spinal cord so fewer pain signals reach the brain (1).

**(b)** Up to 4 marks. Randomly allocate patients to real TENS or placebo TENS, where the device is attached but no current, or a negligible one, is delivered (1). Keep patients unaware of their condition (single-blind), or both patients and the person rating pain unaware (double-blind) (1). Measure pain before and after treatment with the same scale, such as a VAS (1). Compare the mean change in each group (1).

**9.**

**(a)** 6.8 − 4.6 = 2.2 cm (1).

**(b)** Repeated measures (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Pain may have improved naturally over time, or patients may have expected the training to work (a placebo effect or demand characteristics) (1), so the fall cannot be attributed to the imagery training without a control group (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. Gate control theory proposes that signals descending from the brain can close the gate (1). In battle, attention is focused on survival and arousal is high (1), so descending signals close the gate and pain signals from the wound do not reach the brain until the danger is over (1).

**11.** One mark each. Strength: fast and effective for acute and severe pain (1). Weakness: side effects, or risk of dependence with opiates (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 3, describing the mirror but not the movement and feedback.
- In question 5(a), describing the VAS as a numbered scale.
- In question 8(b), leaving out the placebo condition or random allocation.
