## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Arene | Compound containing a benzene ring |
| Delocalised p electrons | The six electrons from the carbons' p orbitals, spread over the whole ring above and below the plane |
| Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene | Hypothetical C₆H₆ with alternating single and double bonds; not real |
| Electrophile | Electron pair acceptor |
| Electrophilic substitution | An electrophile replaces one ring H; the delocalised ring is kept |
| Monosubstitution | One ring hydrogen replaced: the limit set by the specification |
| Side chain | Carbon atoms attached to the ring but not part of it, such as the CH₃ in methylbenzene |
| Free radical | Species with an unpaired electron, such as Cl• |

## 3.3.10.1 Bonding

- Each C forms three σ bonds (two C, one H) at **120°**, so the molecule is **planar**.
- Each C has one p electron. The six p orbitals overlap sideways, so the electrons are **delocalised** over all six carbons.
- All six C–C bonds are equal, with length **between single and double** (about 139 pm, against roughly 154 pm and 134 pm).
- Result: benzene is lower in energy (**more stable**) than the triene.

> **Method: hydrogenation evidence**
> 1. Take ΔH for hydrogenating one C=C (from cyclohexene).
> 2. Multiply by 3 to predict ΔH for the triene.
> 3. Compare with benzene's measured ΔH, which is less exothermic.
> 4. Same product (cyclohexane) but less energy released, so benzene starts further down.
> 5. Size of the gap = stabilisation from delocalisation.

Worked reminder: the study guide's data give 3 × (−118) = −354 kJ mol⁻¹ predicted, −206 kJ mol⁻¹ measured, so benzene is 148 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable. Always use the values a question gives you.

**Substitution, not addition.** An addition product has no complete delocalised ring, so the stabilisation is gone. Substitution leaves the ring whole, so it is preferred.

## 3.3.10.2 Electrophilic substitution

> **Method: drawing any electrophilic substitution**
> 1. Equation to form the electrophile.
> 2. Arrow from the ring's circle to the attacking atom (N of NO₂⁺, S of SO₃, C of RCO⁺ or R⁺).
> 3. Intermediate: attacked C carries H and the new group; horseshoe over five carbons, **+** inside, opening towards the attacked C.
> 4. Arrow from the C–H bond into the ring; H⁺ released.
> 5. Show the catalyst re-formed where there is one.

### Reaction summary

| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Electrophile | Product from benzene |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitration | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | NO₂⁺ | nitrobenzene, C₆H₅NO₂ |
| Sulfonation | fuming H₂SO₄, warm (or conc. H₂SO₄, reflux) | SO₃ | benzenesulfonic acid, C₆H₅SO₃H |
| Acylation | RCOCl + AlCl₃, dry | RCO⁺ | phenyl ketone, C₆H₅COR |
| Alkylation | RCl + AlCl₃, dry | R⁺ | alkylbenzene, C₆H₅R |

### Equations to learn

| Purpose | Equation |
|---|---|
| Form NO₂⁺ | HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ |
| Re-form H₂SO₄ | H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → H₂SO₄ |
| Form SO₃ in conc. acid | 2H₂SO₄ → SO₃ + H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻ |
| Overall sulfonation | C₆H₆ + SO₃ → C₆H₅SO₃H |
| Form RCO⁺ | RCOCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ |
| Form R⁺ | RCl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ |
| Re-form AlCl₃ | H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl |

### Methylbenzene

- The CH₃ group pushes electron density into the ring, so methylbenzene reacts **faster** than benzene (nitration is done at a lower temperature).
- Substitution happens mainly at positions **2 and 4**.
- Nitration: **2-nitromethylbenzene** and **4-nitromethylbenzene**.
- Sulfonation: mainly **4-methylbenzenesulfonic acid**, with some of the 2-isomer.

### Why these reactions matter in synthesis

| Reaction | Importance named in the specification |
|---|---|
| Nitration | manufacture of explosives; formation of amines (reduce the nitro group, 3.3.11) |
| Sulfonation | manufacture of surfactants (alkylbenzenesulfonate detergents) and sulfonamides (including antibacterial drugs) |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | joins a carbon chain to the ring |

## Free-radical attack by chlorine

Conditions: Cl₂ with **UV light**, no catalyst. Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl•.

| Compound | What happens | Product(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Benzene | ring **addition**, one Cl at a time | 1,2,3,4,5,6-hexachlorocyclohexane, C₆H₆Cl₆ |
| Methylbenzene | **side-chain substitution**; ring untouched | (chloromethyl)benzene, then (dichloromethyl)benzene and (trichloromethyl)benzene |

Propagation for methylbenzene:

```
C₆H₅CH₃ + Cl• → C₆H₅CH₂• + HCl
C₆H₅CH₂• + Cl₂ → C₆H₅CH₂Cl + Cl•
```

Termination: any two radicals join, for example 2C₆H₅CH₂• → C₆H₅CH₂CH₂C₆H₅.

### Ring chlorine versus side-chain chlorine

| | Chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl | (Chloromethyl)benzene, C₆H₅CH₂Cl |
|---|---|---|
| Where Cl is | bonded to a ring carbon | bonded to an sp³ side-chain carbon |
| C–Cl bond | Cl lone pair overlaps with the ring: partial double-bond character, shorter and stronger | ordinary polar C–Cl |
| Nucleophiles | repelled by the delocalised electrons | attack the δ+ carbon |
| Reactivity | no nucleophilic substitution under normal conditions | substituted readily, like a halogenoalkane |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Catalyst vs light:** AlCl₃ or conc. acid gives electrophilic substitution on the ring; UV light gives radical reactions.
- **Benzene vs methylbenzene in UV light:** benzene **adds** chlorine to the ring; methylbenzene **substitutes** on the side chain.
- **Acylation vs alkylation:** RCO⁺ gives a ketone; R⁺ gives an alkylbenzene. Both give HCl.
- **NO₂⁺ vs SO₃:** the nitronium ion is charged; sulfur trioxide is neutral, with a strongly δ+ sulfur.
- **Predicted vs measured:** the triene value is calculated; benzene's value is measured.

## Quick self-test

Use Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, S = 32.1, Cl = 35.5.

1. How many electrons are delocalised in a benzene molecule?
2. Put these in order of increasing length: C–C single bond, C=C double bond, carbon–carbon bond in benzene.
3. Benzene's enthalpy of hydrogenation is −206 kJ mol⁻¹ and it is 148 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than the triene. Deduce ΔH for hydrogenating one C=C.
4. Name the electrophile in sulfonation and the atom the ring attacks.
5. Name the two main products when methylbenzene is nitrated.
6. Give the conditions and the product formula when chlorine adds to benzene.
7. Calculate the percentage atom economy of C₆H₆ + SO₃ → C₆H₅SO₃H and of C₆H₆ + H₂SO₄ → C₆H₅SO₃H + H₂O.
8. Write an equation for the electrophile formed from chloroethane and AlCl₃.
9. Calculate the percentage atom economy of C₆H₆ + CH₃CH₂Cl → C₆H₅CH₂CH₃ + HCl.
10. Which of chlorobenzene and (chloromethyl)benzene reacts with warm aqueous hydroxide ions? Give one reason.
11. Calculate Mr of 4-methylbenzenesulfonic acid, CH₃C₆H₄SO₃H.
12. Using −206 kJ mol⁻¹, calculate the heat released when 3.12 g of benzene is fully hydrogenated.

### Answers

1. **Six** (one p electron from each carbon).
2. C=C double < benzene bond < C–C single.
3. (−206 − 148) / 3 = **−118 kJ mol⁻¹**.
4. Sulfur trioxide, SO₃; the ring attacks the **sulfur** atom.
5. **2-nitromethylbenzene** and **4-nitromethylbenzene**.
6. UV light, no catalyst; **C₆H₆Cl₆** (1,2,3,4,5,6-hexachlorocyclohexane).
7. With SO₃ there is one product: **100%**. With H₂SO₄: Mr C₆H₅SO₃H = 158.1; 158.1 / (78.0 + 98.1) × 100 = **89.8%**.
8. CH₃CH₂Cl + AlCl₃ → **CH₃CH₂⁺** + AlCl₄⁻.
9. Mr C₈H₁₀ = 106.0; 106.0 / (78.0 + 64.5) × 100 = **74.4%**.
10. **(Chloromethyl)benzene**: its Cl is on a side-chain carbon with an ordinary C–Cl bond; in chlorobenzene the C–Cl bond is strengthened by overlap with the ring.
11. C₇H₈SO₃: 84.0 + 8.0 + 32.1 + 48.0 = **172.1**.
12. n = 3.12 / 78.0 = 0.0400 mol; 0.0400 × 206 = **8.24 kJ**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the bonds are "in between" but never naming bond **length**.
- Giving the stability figure without saying benzene is the more stable one, or without naming delocalisation.
- Starting the first arrow on a ring carbon or C–H bond instead of the delocalised ring.
- Horseshoe drawn as a full circle, missing the **+**, or opening away from the attacked carbon.
- Pointing the arrow at an oxygen of NO₂⁺ or SO₃.
- Naming 3-nitromethylbenzene as a main product of methylbenzene nitration.
- Writing chlorobenzene as the product of benzene with chlorine in UV light.
- Showing the ring being chlorinated when methylbenzene reacts in UV light.
- Leaving out HCl in Friedel–Crafts equations, or calling it the catalyst.
- Explaining the low reactivity of chlorobenzene by bond polarity alone, with no mention of the lone pair overlapping with the ring.
