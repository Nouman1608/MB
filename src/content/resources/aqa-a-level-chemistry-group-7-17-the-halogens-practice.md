These questions cover section **3.2.3 Group 7(17), the halogens** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry
specification (7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards:
3.2.3.1 Trends in properties, 3.2.3.2 Uses of chlorine and chlorate(I), and Required practical 4. All of
it is AS content, so nothing here is A-level only. The topic is examined on AS Paper 1 and A-level Paper 1,
and A-level Paper 3 can test any content.

Learn the ideas first in the [halogens study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-group-7-17-the-halogens/) and the
[halogens revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-group-7-17-the-halogens-revision-notes/). The course hub is
[AQA A-Level Chemistry](/boards/aqa/a-level/chemistry/), and the
[printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/chemistry/) lists every specification point. The half-equation
for bromide reducing sulfuric acid to SO₂ is practised in the
[redox practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-oxidation-reduction-and-redox-equations-practice/),
so it is not repeated here. Questions 7 and 9 need a calculator. Use Ar: Ag = 107.9, Cl = 35.5, Na = 23.0,
O = 16.0, H = 1.0.

## Questions

**1.** Which ion is the strongest reducing agent?

- A F⁻
- B Cl⁻
- C Br⁻
- D I⁻

**[1]**

**2.** State and explain the trend in electronegativity from fluorine to iodine. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why bromine has a higher boiling point than chlorine. **[3]**

**4.** A student adds chlorine water to potassium bromide solution in one tube and to potassium iodide
solution in another.

**(a)** State what the student sees in each tube. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an ionic equation for the reaction in the potassium iodide tube. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why no reaction is seen when iodine solution is added to potassium chloride solution. **[2]**

**5.** Concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium iodide. One product is a yellow solid.

**(a)** Identify the yellow solid. **[1]**
**(b)** Write a half-equation for the formation of the yellow solid from H₂SO₄. **[2]**
**(c)** Write an equation for the reaction of hydrogen iodide with sulfuric acid to form iodine, the yellow
solid and water. **[1]**
**(d)** State the role of the iodide ions in this reaction. **[1]**

**6.** Three solutions, P, Q and R, each contain one sodium halide. Each is acidified with dilute nitric
acid and then silver nitrate solution is added.

- P: yellow precipitate that does not dissolve in concentrated ammonia.
- Q: white precipitate that dissolves in dilute ammonia.
- R: no precipitate.

**(a)** Identify the halide ion in each solution. **[3]**
**(b)** Write an ionic equation, with state symbols, for the reaction in Q. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the solutions are acidified with dilute nitric acid. **[1]**

**7.** A 25.0 cm³ sample of sodium chloride solution is treated with excess acidified silver nitrate. The
dried precipitate has a mass of 0.430 g. Calculate the concentration of the sodium chloride solution in
mol dm⁻³. **[3]**

**8.** Chlorine reacts with water.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction that forms chloride ions and chlorate(I) ions. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the oxidation state of chlorine in each chlorine-containing species in your equation, and name
this type of reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** In sunlight, chlorine water releases a gas. Write an equation for this reaction. **[2]**

**9.** Chlorine reacts with cold, dilute sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** State one use of the solution formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of sodium hydroxide that reacts with 3.55 g of chlorine. **[2]**

**10.** Compare the reactions of concentrated sulfuric acid with solid sodium chloride and with solid sodium
bromide. Include observations and equations, and explain the difference in terms of the halide ions. **[6]**

**11.** Chlorine is added to the drinking water supply. Evaluate this practice. **[6]**

**12.** Describe tests to show that a white solid is ammonium bromide. Give the result of each test. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. Reducing ability of halide ions increases down the group. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* B is a common wrong answer from confusing this trend with the oxidising ability of the halogens, which is greatest for fluorine.

**2.** Electronegativity **decreases** from F to I [1]. Atomic radius increases and there is more shielding
from extra inner shells [1]. So the bonding pair of electrons is further from the nucleus and is attracted
less strongly [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Nuclear charge decreases" is wrong and loses the explanation mark; nuclear charge increases but is outweighed.

**3.** Both are simple molecular, made of diatomic molecules [1]. Br₂ has more electrons (70) than Cl₂ (34),
so the van der Waals forces between Br₂ molecules are stronger [1]. More energy is needed to overcome these
forces [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Any mention of breaking Br–Br or Cl–Cl bonds contradicts the answer and can lose the
last mark.

**4. (a)** KBr tube: solution turns **yellow/orange** [1]; KI tube: solution turns **brown** [1].
**(b)** **Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂** [1]
**(c)** Iodine is a weaker oxidising agent than chlorine [1], so it cannot oxidise Cl⁻ ions (it cannot gain
electrons from them) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) "a colour change" alone scores nothing; the colour must be named for each tube.

**5. (a)** **Sulfur** [1]
**(b)** Correct species balanced with H⁺ and H₂O: H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ → S + 4H₂O [1]; with electrons:
**H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O** [1]
**(c)** **6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O** [1]
**(d)** **Reducing agent** (electron donor) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A half-equation with the wrong number of electrons can still earn the first mark in (b)
if atoms are balanced, but not the second.

**6. (a)** P: **iodide** [1]; Q: **chloride** [1]; R: **fluoride** [1].
**(b)** **Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)** [1]
**(c)** To react with (remove) carbonate or hydroxide ions, which would otherwise form other silver
precipitates [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State symbols are part of the mark in (b) when the question asks for them; AgCl without
(s) does not score.

**7.** Mr(AgCl) = 107.9 + 35.5 = 143.4; n(AgCl) = 0.430 / 143.4 = 0.002999 mol [1].
n(NaCl) = n(AgCl) = 0.002999 mol (1 : 1) [1].
Concentration = 0.002999 / 0.0250 = **0.120 mol dm⁻³** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting to convert 25.0 cm³ to dm³ gives an answer 1000 times too small; an error
carried forward from a wrong Mr can still earn the later marks.

**8. (a)** **Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO** (or Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ 2H⁺ + Cl⁻ + ClO⁻) [1]
**(b)** Cl₂ 0; Cl⁻ (or HCl) −1; ClO⁻ (or HClO) +1 [1]; **disproportionation** [1].
**(c)** Correct species: Cl₂ and H₂O forming H⁺/HCl and O₂ [1]; balanced: **2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4HCl + O₂** [1].
**[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) all three oxidation states are needed for the mark; "redox" alone does not earn
the name mark.

**9. (a)** **Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O** [1]
**(b)** **Bleach** (or disinfectant) [1]
**(c)** n(Cl₂) = 3.55 / 71.0 = 0.0500 mol, so n(NaOH) = 0.100 mol [1]; mass = 0.100 × 40.0 = **4.00 g** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Using a 1 : 1 ratio instead of 1 : 2 gives 2.00 g and loses the first mark, but the
second can be credited as an error carried forward.

**10.** Award one mark for each point.

- Both form steamy white fumes of hydrogen halide in an acid–base reaction [1].
- NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl, and no further reaction occurs [1].
- With NaBr, HBr forms and then orange-brown fumes of bromine are seen [1].
- 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; sulfur is reduced from +6 to +4 [1].
- Br⁻ is a stronger reducing agent than Cl⁻ [1].
- Br⁻ is larger, so its outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded, and are lost more
  easily [1].

**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A six-mark comparison needs both halides covered; describing only the bromide reaction
caps the answer.

**11.** Award one mark for each point, up to 6.

- Chlorine kills bacteria in water [1].
- This prevents water-borne disease, a large health benefit [1].
- Chlorine is toxic [1].
- It can react with organic matter to form chlorinated organic compounds that may be harmful [1].
- The amounts added are small and controlled [1].
- Judgement: the health benefits outweigh the toxic effects, so chlorination is justified [1].

**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs points on both sides and a final judgement; a list of benefits alone
cannot reach full marks.

**12.** NH₄⁺: warm with sodium hydroxide solution and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube [1];
litmus turns **blue** (ammonia given off) [1].
Br⁻: dissolve, add dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate solution [1]; **cream precipitate**, which does
not dissolve in dilute ammonia but dissolves in concentrated ammonia [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each ion is worth a method mark and a result mark, so a correct colour with no reagent named
earns only half the credit for that ion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the oxidising trend of the halogens with the reducing trend of the halides.
- Saying covalent bonds break when halogens boil.
- Describing colour changes without naming the colour.
- Unbalanced charge in sulfuric acid half-equations.
- Missing the acid–base step with NaCl, or calling it redox.
- Acidifying with HCl before the silver nitrate test.
- Mixing up dilute and concentrated ammonia for AgBr.
- Not naming disproportionation for chlorine with water or NaOH.
- Using the wrong mole ratio for chlorine and sodium hydroxide.
- Ending an "evaluate" answer without a judgement.
