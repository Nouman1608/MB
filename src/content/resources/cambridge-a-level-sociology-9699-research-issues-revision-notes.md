Condensed notes for section 2.3, *Research issues*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 1. The [2.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-research-issues/) explains each point fully with a worked scenario; test yourself with the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-research-issues-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Theoretical, practical and ethical considerations influencing the choice of topic, choice of method(s) and conduct of research.
- How findings may be biased by the actions and values of the sociologist and by choices in funding, designing and conducting research.
- Validity, reliability, objectivity, representativeness and ethics as concepts for assessing research methods.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Theoretical consideration | How a researcher's perspective (for example positivist or interpretivist) shapes topic and method |
| Practical consideration | Time, money, access, funding, skills and personal characteristics affecting research |
| Ethical consideration | Moral principles protecting participants and researchers |
| Informed consent | Agreement to take part, given with full knowledge of the research and the right to withdraw |
| Anonymity | Participants cannot be identified in the findings |
| Confidentiality | Information given is kept private and used only for the research |
| Gatekeeper | A person who controls access to a group or institution |
| Bias | Systematic distortion of findings away from the truth |
| Interviewer effect | Answers shaped by the interviewer's characteristics or behaviour |
| Going native | Over-identifying with the group studied and losing detachment |
| Validity | Data gives a true picture of what it claims to measure |
| Reliability | The same method would produce the same results if repeated |
| Objectivity | Findings are free from the researcher's values |
| Representativeness | The sample is typical of the wider population |

## Theoretical, practical, ethical: the grid

| | Topic | Method | Conduct |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theoretical | Perspective decides what matters | Positivist: reliable, quantitative; interpretivist: valid, qualitative | Detachment or involvement |
| Practical | Funding, access, public concern | Time, cost, sample size, skills | Researcher's age, sex, ethnicity; safety |
| Ethical | Vulnerable groups, illegal activity | Deception in covert methods | Consent, anonymity, avoiding harm |

**Remember:** factors interact. No sampling frame (practical) may force a snowball sample, weakening representativeness (theoretical aim).

## Ethical principles

- Informed consent, including from parents or guardians for children.
- Confidentiality and anonymity, in fieldwork and in publication.
- Avoid physical, psychological, legal and social harm.
- Avoid deception unless the research could not otherwise happen and the benefit is judged to justify it.
- Protect vulnerable groups.
- Stay within the law.

Professional bodies such as the British Sociological Association publish ethical guidance. Covert studies, such as Patrick's study of a Glasgow gang (1973), show ethical and safety concerns overlapping.

## Where bias enters

**From the sociologist's values and actions**
- Choice of topic reflects what the researcher cares about (Weber accepted this).
- Interviewer effect and social desirability.
- Going native in participant observation.
- Selective recording, quoting and coding in qualitative work.
- Becker (1967): research always takes sides.

**From funding, design and conduct**
- Sponsors shape questions and may not publish unwelcome results.
- Leading questions, narrow categories, poor sampling frames.
- Inconsistent procedures between interviewers; low or uneven response.
- Reporting only some results; treating correlation as causation.

## The five concepts and their trade-offs

| Concept | Improved by | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | Rapport, natural settings, triangulation | Often lowers reliability and representativeness |
| Reliability | Standardised questions, pilot studies, coding frames | Fixed questions may lower validity |
| Objectivity | Standardisation, peer review, declaring values | Some argue full objectivity is impossible |
| Representativeness | Good frame, random or stratified sample, high response | Large samples usually mean less depth |
| Ethics | Consent, anonymity, overt methods | Overt methods may cause the Hawthorne effect |

## Method profiles

- **Questionnaires**: high reliability and representativeness; lower validity.
- **Unstructured interviews**: high validity; lower reliability and representativeness.
- **Covert participant observation**: high validity; serious ethical problems; low reliability.
- **Official statistics**: reliable and representative; validity questioned for soft statistics.

## Exam traps

- **Validity versus reliability.** Valid means true; reliable means repeatable. A method can be reliable but invalid, such as a consistent but misleading question.
- **Ethics is more than consent.** Include harm, anonymity, vulnerable groups and legality.
- **Quantitative does not mean objective.** Question wording and categories carry values.
- **Vague bias.** Say whether it comes from values, funding, design or conduct.
- **Unapplied concepts.** Link every concept to the scenario or method in the question.
- **Forgetting "conduct".** The syllabus asks about topic, method *and* conduct.

## Self-test

1. Give one theoretical reason a researcher might choose a questionnaire.
2. Give two practical factors that influence choice of method.
3. What is a gatekeeper? Give an example.
4. Distinguish anonymity from confidentiality.
5. Why do children need extra ethical protection in research?
6. Explain how funding can bias research.
7. What is the interviewer effect?
8. How can a researcher's values affect qualitative findings?
9. Define validity and reliability.
10. Explain one trade-off between validity and reliability.
11. Why might an online survey be unrepresentative?
12. What did Becker argue in "Whose side are we on?" (1967)?

### Answers

1. A positivist wants reliable, quantitative data that can test a hypothesis and be generalised.
2. Any two: time, money, access, funding requirements, the researcher's skills or personal characteristics, the nature of the topic.
3. Someone who controls access to a group or setting, such as a head teacher, prison governor or gang leader.
4. Anonymity means participants cannot be identified; confidentiality means information they give is kept private.
5. They may not fully understand the research or feel able to refuse, so consent from parents or guardians and extra care to avoid harm are needed.
6. Sponsors may influence which questions are asked, shape the design, or decline to publish unwelcome results.
7. The influence of the interviewer's characteristics or behaviour on the answers given.
8. They shape what is recorded, which quotations are used and how material is interpreted.
9. Validity: a true picture of what is being measured. Reliability: consistent results if the research is repeated.
10. Standardised questions make research repeatable (reliable) but may miss what matters to respondents (less valid).
11. Only people online and willing to respond take part, so it is self-selecting and may miss groups such as older or poorer people.
12. That research always involves sympathy with one side, so sociologists should be open about it.
