## 16.1 Nuclear division

**Chromosomes** are found in the nucleus. They contain **DNA**, which carries genetic information as **genes**.

- A **haploid** nucleus contains a **single set** of chromosomes. Gametes are haploid.
- A **diploid** nucleus contains **two sets** of chromosomes. In a diploid cell there is a **pair of each type** of chromosome. A human diploid cell has **23 pairs** (46 chromosomes).

**Mitosis** is nuclear division that gives rise to **genetically identical** cells in which the **chromosome number is maintained**. You do not need the stages. Mitosis is used for:

- **growth** (more cells)
- **repair** of damaged tissues
- **replacement** of dying cells
- **asexual reproduction**

**Stem cells** are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.

**Meiosis** is involved in the production of **gametes**. It is a **reduction division**: the chromosome number is **halved from diploid to haploid**, and the cells produced are **genetically different**. Again, no stages are needed.

**Cancers** form as a result of **uncontrolled cell division**.

### Worked example 1: chromosome numbers

*A species has a diploid number of 36. State the number of chromosomes in (a) a gamete, (b) a zygote, (c) a skin cell made by mitosis, and (d) how many pairs a body cell has.*

1. A gamete is made by meiosis, which halves the number: 36 ÷ 2 = **18**.
2. A zygote forms when two haploid gametes fuse: 18 + 18 = **36**.
3. Mitosis keeps the number the same: **36**.
4. A diploid cell holds a pair of each type: 36 ÷ 2 = **18 pairs**.

## 16.2 Asexual and sexual reproduction

**Asexual reproduction** is a process resulting in the production of **genetically identical offspring from one parent**. Examples: bacteria dividing by binary fission, yeast budding, potato tubers, strawberry runners, and bulbs such as onions.

**Sexual reproduction** involves the **fusion of haploid nuclei (fertilisation)** to form a **diploid zygote**, and produces **genetically different offspring**.

| | Asexual | Sexual |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | One parent is enough; no mate or pollinator needed; fast, many offspring; useful features kept | Variation, so a population is more likely to survive an environmental change; seeds spread offspring widely |
| Disadvantages | No variation, so an environmental change or new disease can affect all offspring; offspring crowd and compete with the parent | Needs two parents (or pollen transfer); slower; uses more energy, e.g. making flowers |

## 16.3 Sexual reproduction in plants

### Flower parts and their functions (16.3.1–16.3.3)

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Sepals | Protect the flower in bud |
| Petals | In insect-pollinated flowers, large and brightly coloured to attract insects |
| Stamen: anther | Makes pollen grains (containing male gametes) |
| Stamen: filament | Holds the anther up in position |
| Carpel: stigma | Receives pollen grains |
| Carpel: style | Holds up the stigma; the pollen tube grows through it |
| Carpel: ovary | Contains the ovules; becomes the fruit |
| Carpel: ovule | Contains the female gamete; becomes the seed |

When you **draw** a flower, use clear single lines and no shading. In a wind-pollinated flower, identify the **anthers** and **stigmas**.

### Insect- and wind-pollinated flowers compared (16.3.4)

| Feature | Insect-pollinated | Wind-pollinated |
|---|---|---|
| Petals | Large, brightly coloured, often scented, with nectar | Small or absent, often green; no scent or nectar |
| Anthers | Inside the flower, so insects brush against them | Hang outside the flower on long filaments |
| Stigmas | Inside the flower, often sticky | Large and feathery, hanging outside to catch pollen |
| Pollen | Larger, sticky or spiky so it clings to insects; smaller amounts | Small, light and smooth so it is carried by air; very large amounts |

### Pollination (16.3.5–16.3.6)

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma.

- **Self-pollination**: pollen goes to a stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant.
- **Cross-pollination**: pollen goes to a stigma of a flower on a **different plant of the same species**.

Effects on a population:

- **Self-pollination** gives **less variation**, so the population has less **capacity to respond to changes in the environment**. But it does **not rely on pollinators**, so seeds can still form if insects are scarce.
- **Cross-pollination** gives **more variation**, so some individuals are more likely to survive a change. But the plant **relies on pollinators** (or wind), so a fall in insect numbers can reduce seed production.

### Fertilisation, seeds and fruits (16.3.7–16.3.8)

After pollination, a **pollen tube** grows out of the pollen grain, down through the style to the ovary. It enters the ovule through a small hole (the micropyle). The male nucleus passes down the tube and **fuses with the female nucleus** in the ovule: this is fertilisation. Afterwards, the **ovules develop into seeds** and the **ovary develops into a fruit**.

### Seeds, dispersal and germination (16.3.9–16.3.13)

A seed has a **testa** (tough seed coat that protects the embryo) and an **embryo** made of a **radicle** (first root), a **plumule** (first shoot) and **cotyledons** (food stores). To investigate, soak a bean seed, peel off the testa and open the cotyledons to find the radicle and plumule.

**Dispersal** of seeds and fruits by wind and by animals lets plants **colonise new areas** and **reduces competition** with the parent and with each other for light, water and mineral ions.

- **Wind-dispersed** fruits are light, with wings or hair-like parachutes that increase surface area, so they stay in the air longer and travel further.
- **Animal-dispersed** fruits either have **hooks** that catch on fur, or are **fleshy, coloured and sweet** so animals eat them. The seeds have tough coats, survive the gut and are egested away from the parent.

**Germination** needs a **suitable temperature**, **water** and **oxygen**. During germination the seed **absorbs water** and swells, and the testa splits. Water **activates enzymes**: amylase breaks down stored starch into maltose, and proteases break down proteins into amino acids. These soluble products move to the growing radicle and plumule, where they are used in **respiration** and for **growth**. The radicle emerges first.

### Worked example 2: magnification of a pollen grain

*A drawing of a pollen grain is 45 mm wide. The real grain is 0.03 mm wide. Calculate the magnification.*

magnification = image size ÷ actual size = 45 ÷ 0.03 = **×1500**. Use the same unit for both lengths.

### Worked example 3: a germination investigation

Four tubes each held 40 seeds on cotton wool for five days.

| Tube | Conditions | Germinated | Percentage |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Damp cotton wool, air, 25 °C | 36 | 36 ÷ 40 × 100 = **90%** |
| B | Dry cotton wool, air, 25 °C | 0 | **0%** |
| C | Seeds under boiled, cooled water with an oil layer, 25 °C | 2 | **5%** |
| D | Damp cotton wool, air, 4 °C (refrigerator) | 6 | **15%** |

**Conclusion:** A is the control. B shows **water** is needed, C that **oxygen** is needed (boiling removes dissolved oxygen and oil stops more dissolving), and D that a **suitable temperature** is needed: enzymes work slowly at 4 °C.

## 16.4 Sexual reproduction in humans

### Reproductive systems (16.4.1–16.4.2)

| Male organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Testes | Make sperm and testosterone |
| Scrotum | Holds the testes outside the body, where it is cooler |
| Sperm ducts | Carry sperm from the testes to the urethra |
| Prostate gland | Adds fluid to the sperm to make semen |
| Urethra | Carries semen (and, at other times, urine) out through the penis |
| Penis | Places semen in the vagina |

| Female organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Ovaries | Make egg cells; make oestrogen and progesterone |
| Oviducts | Carry the egg to the uterus; fertilisation happens here |
| Uterus | Where the embryo implants and the fetus develops |
| Cervix | Ring of muscle at the base of the uterus; widens at birth |
| Vagina | Receives the penis and semen; the birth canal |

### Gametes and fertilisation (16.4.3–16.4.6)

**Sperm cell:**
- **flagellum** lashes so the sperm swims to the egg
- many **mitochondria** release energy by respiration for swimming
- the **acrosome** contains **enzymes** that digest a way through the jelly coat of the egg

**Egg cell:**
- **energy stores** in the cytoplasm supply the early embryo
- the **jelly coat changes at fertilisation** so that no more sperm can enter

**Fertilisation** is the fusion of the nuclei from a male gamete (sperm) and a female gamete (egg cell).

| | Sperm | Egg |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Much smaller | Much larger |
| Structure | Flagellum, mitochondria, acrosome | Energy stores, jelly coat, no flagellum |
| Numbers | Millions released at a time | Usually one released each cycle |
| Motility | Swims | Cannot swim; moved along the oviduct |

### Puberty and the menstrual cycle (16.4.7–16.4.9)

**Testosterone** (from the testes) causes male secondary sexual characteristics: facial and body hair, a deeper voice, muscle growth, and the start of sperm production. **Oestrogen** (from the ovaries) causes female ones: breast development, wider hips, and the start of the menstrual cycle.

In the **menstrual cycle** the uterus lining breaks down (menstruation), then thickens again, while an egg develops in the ovary and is released (ovulation) roughly halfway through. Four hormones control it:

1. **FSH** from the pituitary gland causes an egg to develop in the ovary and stimulates the ovary to make oestrogen.
2. **Oestrogen** makes the uterus lining thicken again. Rising oestrogen inhibits FSH and stimulates LH release.
3. **LH** from the pituitary gland causes **ovulation**.
4. **Progesterone**, made by the empty follicle in the ovary, keeps the lining thick and inhibits FSH and LH. If there is no pregnancy, progesterone falls and the lining breaks down; FSH can rise again.

### Pregnancy (16.4.10–16.4.13)

The zygote divides by mitosis to form a **ball of cells** (the embryo) that **implants in the lining of the uterus**. The **amniotic sac** encloses the fetus and contains **amniotic fluid**, which protects the fetus from knocks and supports it.

The **placenta** is where substances are exchanged between the mother's blood and the fetus's blood, which do not mix. Oxygen and dissolved nutrients such as glucose and amino acids pass to the fetus; carbon dioxide and urea pass to the mother. The **umbilical cord** carries the fetus's blood to and from the placenta. **Some viruses can pass across the placenta** and affect the fetus, for example rubella and HIV (see [Disease and immunity](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-disease-and-immunity/)).

## Common errors

- Saying mitosis halves the chromosome number. It is meiosis that halves it.
- Calling pollination "fertilisation". Pollination moves pollen; fertilisation fuses nuclei.
- Writing that the ovule becomes the fruit. The ovary does.
- Listing light as a germination condition. Only water, oxygen and a suitable temperature are listed.
