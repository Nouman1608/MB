## The specification in brief

- **Purposes:** identify and understand customer needs; identify gaps in the market; reduce risk; inform business decisions.
- **Primary methods:** survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation.
- **Secondary methods:** internet, market reports, government reports.
- **Use of data:** qualitative and quantitative data; the role of social media; the importance of reliability.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market research | Gathering and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market |
| Primary research | New information collected first-hand by or for the business, for its own purpose |
| Secondary research | Information that already exists, collected earlier by someone else |
| Survey | Gathering answers from a sample of people |
| Questionnaire | The written set of questions used to collect answers |
| Focus group | A small group discussing a product in depth, led by a facilitator |
| Observation | Watching what customers actually do |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data: counts, percentages, ratings |
| Qualitative data | Descriptive data: opinions, feelings, reasons |
| Sample | The people chosen to take part in the research |
| Reliability | How far the data can be trusted to reflect the whole market |
| Gap in the market | A customer need that no business meets well |

## Condensed recall

### Primary against secondary

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Usually higher | Often low or free |
| Time | Slower | Quicker |
| Specific to the business? | Yes | Often not |
| Up to date? | Yes | May be out of date |
| Best for | Opinions about the business's own idea | Background facts about the market and population |

### Closed and open questions
- Closed (tick box, yes/no, rating) → quantitative data, easy to analyse.
- Open (own words) → qualitative data, explains reasons, harder to compare.
- Avoid leading, vague and double questions.

### Social media
- Strengths: fast, cheap, wide reach, polls and comments, can test ideas before launch.
- Weaknesses: followers are not representative; non-customers can respond; reacting is not buying.

### Reliability checklist
1. Is the sample big enough?
2. Does it match the target market (age, location, income)?
3. Were the questions or the place of asking biased?
4. Is the data up to date?
5. Is the source trustworthy?

## Calculations you may meet

- **Percentage:** number giving an answer ÷ total asked × 100.
- **Mean (from a table):** multiply each value by how many chose it, add, divide by the total number of people.
- **Scaling up:** percentage who would buy × number of people in the market. Treat the answer as an estimate, not a forecast you can rely on.

Mini example: 90 people rate a menu out of 5. Twenty give 3, fifty give 4 and twenty give 5. Mean = (60 + 200 + 100) ÷ 90 = 360 ÷ 90 = 4.0.

## Exam traps

- Calling a market report primary because the business bought it.
- Writing "research removes risk".
- Saying qualitative data is less useful because it has no numbers.
- Assuming a big sample is a reliable one. A big but unrepresentative sample is still unreliable.
- Treating social media votes as sales.
- Averaging the categories in a table instead of weighting by the number of people.

## Self-test

1. State the four purposes of market research in the specification.
2. Name the four primary methods in the specification.
3. Name the three secondary methods in the specification.
4. Give one advantage of secondary research for a new business.
5. Give one disadvantage of a focus group.
6. Is "How would you improve our menu?" an open or closed question?
7. What type of data does a 1 to 5 rating produce?
8. Of 250 people asked, 85 say they would buy. Calculate the percentage.
9. Give two reasons why a social media poll may be unreliable.
10. Why does a sample need to match the target market?
11. Explain one way market research can inform a business decision.
12. Explain why unreliable research could increase risk.

### Answers

1. To identify and understand customer needs; to identify gaps in the market; to reduce risk; to inform business decisions.
2. Survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation.
3. Internet, market reports, government reports.
4. It is usually cheaper or quicker to obtain, because the data already exists.
5. Any one: the group is small so may not represent the market; one strong voice can sway others; it can be costly to run.
6. Open.
7. Quantitative data.
8. 85 ÷ 250 × 100 = 34%.
9. Any two: followers already like the business; non-customers can vote; voting is easy and does not mean buying; people can vote more than once on some sites.
10. Otherwise the answers reflect people who will not buy, so the results give a false picture of real customers.
11. For example, finding the price most customers will pay helps the owner set a price that sells.
12. The owner may act confidently on wrong figures, such as ordering too much stock or choosing the wrong location, and lose money.
