These notes condense section 5.1, *Theories about the role of education*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. The syllabus lists five strands: functionalist views on value consensus and social solidarity; role allocation; Marxist views on maintaining the capitalist economic system; education as ideological control and cultural reproduction; and New Right and social democratic views on education and the economy.

For full explanations and worked answers, use the [theories about the role of education study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-theories-role-of-education/).

## The core contrast in one line each

- **Functionalism:** education serves society by building shared values and sorting talent fairly.
- **Marxism:** education serves capitalism by producing compliant workers and making inequality look fair.
- **Neo-Marxism:** the same aims, but pupils resist, so reproduction is contested and uneven.
- **Social democrats:** education is a public investment; equal opportunity and economic growth go together.
- **New Right:** education should serve the economy, but markets and parental choice do this better than the state.

## Functionalism

| Thinker | Claim | Use in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| Durkheim, *Moral Education* (1925) | Schools teach discipline, attachment to the group and self-control; punishment should explain why rules matter | Value consensus and solidarity |
| Parsons, "The School Class as a Social System" (1959) | The school class moves children from family values to achievement and equal opportunity | Bridge between family and society |
| Davis and Moore (1945) | Exams sift talent into the most important roles | Role allocation |

**Critics:** Tumin (1953) — job "importance" cannot be measured and privilege blocks talent; Hargreaves (1982) — schools teach individual competition and deny many working-class pupils dignity; Wrong (1961) — functionalism over-socialises people; Marxists — "consensus" is ruling-class ideology.

## Marxism

Three functions for capitalism:

1. **Reproducing labour power** — different skills and attitudes for different classes.
2. **Legitimating inequality** — the myth of meritocracy makes failure look personal.
3. **Transmitting ideology** — individualism, competition, acceptance of hierarchy.

| Thinker | Claim |
|---|---|
| Althusser | Education is the dominant ideological state apparatus |
| Bowles and Gintis | Schooling lies in "the long shadow of work"; family background predicts income in ways IQ does not explain |
| Bourdieu and Passeron (1970; English 1977) | **Symbolic violence**: dominant-class culture is imposed as if it were natural taste or intelligence |
| Willis (1977) | Lads resist school yet still end up in manual work |
| Giroux (1983) | Schools are sites of struggle; resistance can reproduce or challenge inequality |

## New Right and social democratic views

| | Social democratic | New Right |
|---|---|---|
| Core aim | Equal opportunity and growth together | Standards, efficiency and choice |
| Key idea | Human capital: Schultz (1961) | Vouchers: Friedman (1955); competition: Chubb and Moe (1990) |
| Policy | Comprehensive schools, free state education, wider higher education | Marketisation, league tables, vocationalism |
| Main criticism | Expansion has not closed class gaps; Wolf doubts spending automatically boosts growth | Brown (1990): **parentocracy** — wealth and wishes of parents replace ability and effort; Finn (1987): youth training gave cheap labour, not real skills |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Value consensus | Broad agreement on shared norms and values |
| Social solidarity | Sense of belonging that binds individuals to society |
| Role allocation | Sorting people into jobs that suit their abilities |
| Meritocratic principle | Rewards should follow ability and effort |
| Reproduction of labour power | Producing the kinds of workers capitalism needs |
| Legitimation | Making inequality appear fair and deserved |
| Ideological state apparatus | Institution that controls through ideas (Althusser) |
| Correspondence principle | School organisation mirrors the workplace |
| Cultural reproduction | Passing on dominant-class culture so class advantage is reproduced |
| Symbolic violence | Imposing one class's culture as if it were naturally superior |
| Human capital | Skills and knowledge treated as an economic investment |
| Marketisation | Introducing competition and consumer choice into education |
| Parentocracy | Education shaped by parental wealth and wishes |
| Vocationalism | Education aimed at work-related skills |

## Linking the theories

- Functionalists and Marxists **agree** that school and economy are closely linked; they **disagree** about who benefits.
- Social democrats and the New Right both tie education to growth; they disagree about **state versus market**.
- Interactionist and neo-Marxist evidence of resistance undermines **all** structural theories that treat pupils as passive.

## Exam traps

- Saying Durkheim used "particularistic" and "universalistic": these are Parsons's terms.
- Calling Bourdieu's cultural capital a form of money. It is culture that schools convert into qualifications.
- In Question 3, writing arguments that support the quotation. Every point must go against it.
- Describing the New Right as anti-education. It wants schools run differently, not less schooling.
- Listing six theorists without a judgement. Evaluate means reaching a reasoned conclusion.
- Treating Willis as straightforward support for Bowles and Gintis: resistance complicates reproduction.

## Quick recall: one-sentence evaluations

- **Functionalism:** explains why societies invest in mass schooling but ignores conflict and inequality.
- **Marxism:** explains persistent class gaps but overstates how passive pupils are and is hard to test.
- **Social democrats:** strong on fairness, weaker on why expansion has not closed gaps.
- **New Right:** plausible on standards, but choice tends to favour advantaged parents.

## Self-test

1. Name the work in which Parsons analysed the school class.
2. What did Durkheim think school punishment should achieve?
3. State Tumin's two main criticisms of Davis and Moore.
4. What three functions do Marxists say education performs for capitalism?
5. What does "the long shadow of work" refer to?
6. Define symbolic violence.
7. How does Giroux's view differ from classical Marxism?
8. What is human capital theory?
9. Which economist proposed school vouchers in 1955?
10. Define parentocracy and name who coined it.
11. Give one criticism of vocationalism from a Marxist viewpoint.
12. Give one point on which functionalists and Marxists agree.

### Answers

1. "The School Class as a Social System" (1959).
2. Help pupils understand why a rule matters to the group, not simply make them afraid.
3. Job importance cannot be measured objectively; unequal rewards let privileged families pass on advantage, blocking talent.
4. Reproducing labour power, legitimating inequality and transmitting ideology.
5. Bowles and Gintis's view that schooling is organised on the model of the capitalist workplace.
6. Imposing the dominant class's culture as if it were natural taste or intelligence, so others feel education is not for them.
7. Giroux sees schools as sites of struggle where pupils can resist and contest dominant ideas, not simply be shaped by them.
8. The idea that spending on education is an investment that raises productivity (Schultz, 1961).
9. Milton Friedman.
10. Education shaped by the wealth and wishes of parents rather than pupils' ability and effort; Phillip Brown (1990).
11. Finn (1987): youth training supplied cheap labour and lowered unemployment figures without real skills.
12. Both see a close link between education and the economy and treat pupils as shaped by social structure.
