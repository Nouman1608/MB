These notes condense **Exchange rates** from The global context in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The full explanations and worked examples are in the [exchange rates study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-exchange-rates-ocr-aslevel-economics/), and the [exchange rates practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-exchange-rates-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/) give exam-style work with mark schemes. All rates and figures are invented.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification wording | In one line |
|---|---|
| Explain and calculate exchange rates | The price of one currency in another; convert prices and find percentage changes |
| Explain, with a diagram, determination in a floating system | Demand for and supply of the currency set the rate |
| Explain, with a diagram, determination in a fixed system | The authorities hold a target using reserves and interest rates |
| Evaluate causes of exchange rate changes | Interest rates, inflation, trade, investment, speculation, confidence |
| Evaluate consequences of exchange rate changes | Current account, inflation, growth, jobs; depends on elasticity and spare capacity |
| Evaluate advantages and disadvantages of systems | Floating gives flexibility; fixed gives certainty |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another |
| Floating system | The rate is set by market demand and supply, with no target |
| Fixed (pegged) system | The authorities set a rate and act to keep it |
| Appreciation | A rise in a floating currency's value |
| Depreciation | A fall in a floating currency's value |
| Revaluation | An official rise in a fixed rate |
| Devaluation | An official fall in a fixed rate |
| Foreign currency reserves | Stocks of foreign currency held by the central bank, used to buy the home currency |
| Speculation | Buying or selling currency to profit from expected changes in its value |
| Hot money | Short-term financial flows that move quickly in search of higher returns |
| Price competitiveness | How cheap a country's goods are relative to rivals' once converted into the buyer's currency |

## Calculation methods

- **Convert to foreign currency:** home price × rate (foreign per unit of home).
- **Convert to home currency:** foreign price ÷ rate.
- **Invert a rate:** 1 ÷ rate. If €1 = ¥160, then ¥1 = €0.00625.
- **Percentage change:** (new − old) ÷ old × 100, for the currency named.

**Quick example.** The euro rises from €1 = $1.05 to €1 = $1.12.

- Euro appreciation: 0.07 ÷ 1.05 = **+6.7%**.
- Dollar value of €1 rises, so $1 buys fewer euros: 1 ÷ 1.05 = €0.952 before and 1 ÷ 1.12 = €0.893 after, a dollar depreciation of **6.25%**.
- A €200 machine sold to the US rises from $210 to **$224**.
- A $50 American textbook bought in the eurozone falls from €47.62 to **€44.64**.

## Floating system: what shifts the curves

| Shift | Example cause | Effect on the rate |
|---|---|---|
| Demand for the currency rises | Higher relative interest rates; stronger export demand; inward investment; tourism into the country | Appreciates |
| Demand for the currency falls | Lower relative interest rates; recession abroad; loss of confidence | Depreciates |
| Supply of the currency rises | Residents buy more imports or foreign assets; speculators sell | Depreciates |
| Supply of the currency falls | Residents cut imports or bring savings home | Appreciates |

Diagram checklist: vertical axis "price of the currency in [other currency]", horizontal axis "quantity of the currency", D slopes down, S slopes up, original and new equilibrium rates marked.

## Fixed system: defending the peg

| Pressure at the peg | Central bank action | Reserves |
|---|---|---|
| Excess supply of home currency (pressure to fall) | Buys home currency; raises interest rates | Fall |
| Excess demand for home currency (pressure to rise) | Sells home currency; lowers interest rates | Rise |

**Reserves arithmetic.** Excess supply of 50 million units a day at a peg of 2.0 foreign units per home unit costs 100 million foreign units a day. Reserves of 3 billion last 3,000 ÷ 100 = **30 days**.

## Causes: evaluation prompts

- Short run: financial flows, interest rates and expectations dominate.
- Long run: relative inflation and competitiveness dominate.
- Only relative changes matter: compare with other countries.
- Expected changes are already in the price.

## Consequences of a depreciation

| Area | Effect | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Current account | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer | Price elasticity of demand for exports and imports; time |
| Inflation | Dearer imported goods and inputs | Share of imports in spending and costs; whether firms pass costs on |
| Growth and jobs | Higher net exports raise AD | Spare capacity; state of the world economy |
| Firms and households | Exporters gain; importers and foreign travellers lose | Each firm's mix of exports and imported inputs |

An appreciation reverses each effect: cheaper imports and lower inflation, but weaker export competitiveness.

## Systems compared

| | Floating | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| For | Automatic adjustment; interest rates free for domestic goals; small reserves needed | Certainty for trade and investment; inflation discipline |
| Against | Volatility; imported inflation after falls; speculative swings | Needs reserves; interest rates tied to the peg; wrong rate harms competitiveness; speculative attack |

## Exam traps

- "Devalued" for a floating currency, or "depreciated" for a peg.
- Giving the percentage change of the wrong currency.
- Unlabelled axes, or axes labelled only "price" and "quantity".
- Claiming a depreciation always improves the current account.
- Forgetting reserves or interest rates when explaining a peg.
- Treating a fall in the exchange rate as a fall in domestic prices.

## Self-test

1. Define an exchange rate.
2. Distinguish depreciation from devaluation.
3. £1 = $1.25. What is the dollar price of a £60 UK good?
4. £1 = $1.25. What is the pound price of a $50 US good?
5. The rate falls from £1 = $1.25 to £1 = $1.10. Calculate the percentage depreciation of the pound.
6. State two sources of demand for a country's currency.
7. Explain how a rise in a country's interest rates affects its floating exchange rate.
8. At a peg there is excess supply of the home currency. State two actions the central bank could take.
9. Excess supply is 40 million units a day at a peg of 1.5. How much foreign currency does the central bank spend each day?
10. Why might a depreciation fail to improve the current account in the short run?
11. Give one advantage of a fixed exchange rate system.
12. Give one disadvantage of a floating exchange rate system.

### Answers

1. The price of one currency expressed in terms of another.
2. Depreciation is a market-driven fall in a floating currency; devaluation is an official lowering of a fixed rate.
3. 60 × 1.25 = **$75**.
4. 50 ÷ 1.25 = **£40**.
5. (1.10 − 1.25) ÷ 1.25 × 100 = **−12%**, a 12% depreciation.
6. Any two of: foreign buyers of its exports; foreign tourists; inward direct investment; foreign purchases of its financial assets; speculators expecting a rise.
7. Higher rates attract savings from abroad, raising demand for the currency, so it appreciates.
8. Buy the home currency using reserves; raise interest rates (or impose controls on currency flows).
9. 40 × 1.5 = **60 million** units of foreign currency.
10. Demand for exports and imports may be price inelastic in the short run, so volumes barely change while each import costs more in home currency.
11. Any one of: certainty for traders and investors; discipline on inflation.
12. Any one of: volatility and uncertainty; imported inflation after a fall; large swings from speculation.
