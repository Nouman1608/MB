## Section A: short questions

**1.** Define *soliloquy* and explain one effect a soliloquy can have on an audience. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between blank verse and prose in a Shakespeare play, and give one reason
a playwright might move a character from one to the other. **[3]**

**3.** Read this original line: *"The night is kind; it does not ask my name."* Identify the metre,
and explain the effect of the pause after "kind". **[3]**

**4.** Explain what dramatic irony is and how it can build tension in a scene. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: *"Aldric is a bad brother because he wants the land."* Rewrite the sentence so
that it focuses on the writer's methods, and explain what you changed. **[3]**

**6.** A student ends an essay with a paragraph that begins: *"In Shakespeare's time there was a king
and people believed in God."* Explain two weaknesses in this use of context and say how to fix them.
**[4]**

**7.** In the original line *"Kneel, then, and kiss the hand that I extend,"* identify the action the
words imply on stage, and explain why this matters when writing about a play. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

Read the original scene, then answer Questions 8 and 9.

> *Original scene.* Enter ALDRIC, a nobleman, and HOB, his servant, with a letter.
>
> ALDRIC: Thou bring'st it late. What says my brother? Speak.
> HOB: Nothing, my lord, for he cannot write and ride at once, and he was riding. His man gave it me
> at the gate, with mud on it.
> ALDRIC *(reads)*: "Come to me at the abbey. Come alone."
> Alone. He trusts me, then. Or tests my trust.
> HOB: Shall I saddle the grey, my lord?
> ALDRIC: The grey? No. Saddle nothing. Stay. The grey.
> HOB: The grey it is. *(Aside)* He has changed his mind twice in one breath. A man that changes his
> horse so fast will change his master faster.
> ALDRIC: Go. *(Exit HOB.)* A brother calls, and I must answer him;
> Yet every word he writes is soft as wax,
> And wax, once warmed, will take what seal I please.

**8.** How does the writer present Aldric's uncertainty in this scene? Refer to language, verse and
prose, and stage action. **[8]**

**9.** How does the writer use Hob in this scene? Consider his speech, his status and his aside.
**[6]**

**10.** Using your own studied play, write a five-minute plan for this task: *Starting with an extract
of your choice, explore how Shakespeare presents a character's ambition or desire for power.* Your
plan should show a line of argument, the extract details you would use, and two moments from
elsewhere in the play. **[6]**

**11.** Using your own studied play: *Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how
Shakespeare presents loyalty or betrayal. Write about how Shakespeare presents loyalty or betrayal in
this extract, and how Shakespeare presents loyalty or betrayal in the play as a whole.* **[34]**

**12.** Using your own studied play: *Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how
Shakespeare presents fear or guilt. Write about how Shakespeare presents fear or guilt in this
extract, and how Shakespeare presents fear or guilt in the play as a whole.* **[34]**

## Answers

**1.** A soliloquy is a speech in which a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud [1].
One effect: the audience learns private motives that other characters do not know, which can create
sympathy, complicity or dramatic irony in later scenes [1].

**2.** Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter, a regular ten-syllable line [1]; prose is ordinary
speech without metre [1]. One reason for a switch: to mark a change in status, mood or state of mind,
such as a character dropping into prose when relaxed, comic, disguised or losing control [1].

**3.** Iambic pentameter: ten syllables in five unstressed-stressed pairs [1]. The pause after
"kind" is a caesura [1]; it splits the line so the second half reads as a darker afterthought,
suggesting the speaker is relieved that the darkness hides him and so revealing his guilt [1].

**4.** Dramatic irony is when the audience knows something that a character on stage does not [1].
It builds tension because the audience anticipates the consequences of a character's ignorance and
hears hidden meaning in ordinary lines [1].

**5.** Example rewrite: *"The writer presents Aldric's ambition as stronger than his loyalty, using
the clothing metaphor to show how easily he dismisses family ties."* [1] Changes: the writer is now
the subject of the sentence, so the focus is on choices rather than a real person [1]; a specific
method (metaphor) is named and linked to an idea, replacing the simple moral judgement "bad" [1].

**6.** Weakness one: the context is vague and partly inaccurate as phrased, giving no specific
belief or attitude [1]. Weakness two: it sits in a separate final paragraph unconnected to any line or
moment in the play [1]. Fix one: name a precise, relevant belief, such as the idea that a monarch's
authority came from God [1]. Fix two: attach it to a specific moment, explaining how it shapes the
audience's response to that line [1].

**7.** The line implies that the other character kneels and kisses the speaker's hand, a gesture of
submission [1]. This matters because Shakespeare's scripts contain few stage directions, so actions
written into dialogue show how power is staged, and commenting on them shows awareness of performance
[1].

**8.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 8:

- The question-and-answer pattern of "He trusts me, then. Or tests my trust." shows Aldric arguing
  with himself [1].
- The short sentences and full stops break the pentameter into fragments, enacting hesitation [1].
- The repetition of "trust" with a shift from trusting to testing shows suspicion overtaking
  confidence [1].
- "No. Saddle nothing. Stay. The grey." reverses his decision within a single line, which shows
  indecision through structure [1].
- On stage, the reversals could be played with pauses or movement towards and away from the door,
  so the audience sees his uncertainty [1].
- Once Hob exits, Aldric's speech settles into regular blank verse, suggesting that alone he regains
  control and resolve [1].
- The wax metaphor shows a decision forming: his brother's soft words can be reshaped, hinting at
  manipulation or betrayal [1].
- The scene ends with a confident, controlled image after uncertain fragments, so uncertainty is
  presented as a stage that leads to a darker certainty [1].

**9.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 6:

- Hob speaks in prose, marking his lower status in contrast to Aldric's verse [1].
- His joke that Aldric's brother "cannot write and ride at once" brings comedy and a down-to-earth tone
  [1].
- The mud on the letter is a concrete, practical detail that contrasts with Aldric's anxious thoughts
  [1].
- His aside shares a private judgement with the audience, creating a bond with them [1].
- The aside predicts that Aldric "will change his master faster", foreshadowing betrayal [1].
- Hob therefore acts as a commentator whose plain speech exposes the noble character's instability
  [1].

**10.** Award marks as follows:

- A clear one-sentence argument that answers the question across the play [1].
- Three or four specific details chosen from the extract, each linked to ambition or power [2].
- Two other moments from the play, ideally one earlier and one later, each with a short quotation or
  precise reference [2].
- One relevant context point tied to a specific moment rather than added generally [1].

**11.** AQA marks this holistically against level descriptors. The weighting below is a guide to what
the response needs, not a tick-list.

- **AO1 [12]:** a sustained argument about loyalty or betrayal that answers both bullets; apt,
  precise references from the extract and from at least two other points in the play; a personal
  response that weighs alternatives, such as whether a betrayal is presented as choice or pressure.
- **AO2 [12]:** close analysis of language in the extract (imagery, key words, shifts in tone);
  dramatic methods such as soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony or shared lines; structure, such as where
  the extract sits and how loyalty is tested, broken or restored by the end; terminology used to
  explain effects.
- **AO3 [6]:** relevant context woven into analysis, for example attitudes to duty, kinship, rank or
  allegiance that shape how an original audience would judge the characters.
- **AO4 [4]:** a range of vocabulary and sentence structures; accurate spelling and punctuation.

**12.** As for Question 11, marked holistically:

- **AO1 [12]:** a clear argument about how fear or guilt is presented and how it changes; references
  from the extract and from earlier and later moments; a developed personal judgement, such as
  whether guilt is shown as a punishment or a sign of conscience.
- **AO2 [12]:** analysis of how language conveys fear or guilt, for instance imagery, questions,
  broken metre or repetition; dramatic methods such as soliloquy or what the audience sees on stage;
  structure, such as how fear builds towards a climax or how guilt returns later.
- **AO3 [6]:** context integrated with specific moments, for example beliefs about sin, conscience,
  the supernatural or order, as relevant to the play.
- **AO4 [4]:** controlled, accurate writing with varied vocabulary and sentence structures.

## Using these questions well

Do Questions 1 to 9 first: they rehearse the vocabulary and close-reading moves that the full essays
depend on. Then plan Question 10 against the clock and write either Question 11 or Question 12 in full
under timed conditions, without your text. Afterwards, check each quotation you used against the play
and note any you misremembered; those are the ones to drill before the exam.
