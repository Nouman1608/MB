## Part A: Fiscal policy (3.2.3.1)

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Changes in government spending and taxation used to influence the economy and meet the government's objectives |
| The Budget | The government's yearly plan for taxation and spending, presented in the UK by the Chancellor of the Exchequer |
| Balanced budget | Government revenue equals government spending |
| Budget surplus | Revenue is greater than spending |
| Budget deficit | Spending is greater than revenue, so the government must borrow |
| National debt | The total the government owes from all past borrowing |
| Expansionary fiscal policy | Higher spending and/or lower taxes, to raise total demand |
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Lower spending and/or higher taxes, to reduce total demand |

### How fiscal policy affects income and spending

- **Government spending** is itself part of total spending. Spending on roads, schools or hospitals creates income for firms and their workers, who then spend part of it.
- **Lower income tax** leaves households with more disposable income, so consumer spending rises.
- **Lower taxes on business profits** leave firms more to invest.
- **Higher VAT** raises prices, so households can buy less with the same income.
- Some effects happen without new decisions: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises, which supports spending.

### Using fiscal policy to meet objectives

| Aim | Fiscal action | Possible conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Growth and lower unemployment | Raise spending, cut taxes | Inflation, more imports, a bigger deficit |
| Lower inflation | Cut spending, raise taxes | Slower growth, job losses |
| Better current account | Cut demand, so fewer imports | Slower growth |
| Less inequality | Progressive taxes, benefits, public services | Weaker incentives, cost |
| Long-run growth | Spending on education, training and infrastructure | Takes years; must be funded now |

**UK example.** In 2020 the government spent very heavily on support during the pandemic, including the furlough scheme that paid part of the wages of workers who could not work. Borrowing rose to its highest level since the Second World War.

### Consequences of a surplus and a deficit

| Budget deficit | Budget surplus |
|---|---|
| Government borrows, so the national debt rises | Can repay debt, so future interest payments fall |
| More of future budgets goes on debt interest instead of services | May mean taxes are high or services have been cut |
| Lenders may demand higher interest if debt grows too large | Can take spending power out of the economy and slow growth |
| May be useful in a recession to support demand and jobs | Gives room to borrow in a future recession |
| Future taxes may need to rise | |

### Calculations you must be able to do

**1. Budget position.** Spending £1,100bn, revenue £1,040bn. Deficit = 1,100 − 1,040 = **£60bn**.
As a share of GDP of £2,400bn: 60 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **2.5%**.

**2. Debt.** The national debt was £2,000bn. After the £60bn deficit it is **£2,060bn**. If the average interest rate on the debt is 3%, yearly debt interest is 0.03 × 2,060 = **£61.8bn**.

**3. Effect of a tax change on a household.** A worker earns £30,000, of which £17,430 is taxed at 20%: tax = £3,486. If the rate falls to 19%: tax = £3,311.70. Disposable income rises by **£174.30** a year.

### Limits of fiscal policy

- **Time lags:** budgets are set once or twice a year, and spending projects take time to start.
- **Borrowing and debt:** expansionary policy can push up debt and interest costs.
- **Incentives:** high tax rates may discourage work and enterprise.
- **Imports:** some extra spending leaks abroad.
- **Opportunity cost:** money spent on one area cannot be spent on another.

## Part B: Monetary policy (3.2.3.2)

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monetary policy | Changes in interest rates (and the supply of money) by the central bank to influence spending in the economy |
| Bank of England | The UK's central bank |
| Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) | The nine-member Bank of England committee that votes on Bank Rate, about eight times a year |
| Bank Rate | The interest rate the Bank of England pays on banks' reserves; it influences the rates banks charge and pay |
| Inflation target | The government sets a CPI inflation target of 2% for the Bank of England |

The Bank of England has set interest rates independently of the government since 1997. If CPI inflation is more than 1 percentage point above or below the 2% target, the Governor must write an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why and what the Bank will do.

### How monetary policy controls inflation

A rise in Bank Rate leads to:

1. Banks raising interest rates on loans, mortgages and savings.
2. Borrowing becoming dearer and saving more rewarding.
3. Households with variable-rate mortgages having less to spend.
4. Firms investing less, as projects funded by loans cost more.
5. The pound tending to rise, as UK savings attract foreign money, so imports become cheaper and exports dearer.
6. Lower total demand, so less pressure on prices, and inflation falls back towards 2%.

**UK example.** Bank Rate was cut to 0.1% in March 2020. As inflation rose, the MPC raised it in a series of steps to 5.25% by August 2023.

### Using monetary policy for other objectives

- **Growth and jobs:** cutting interest rates makes borrowing cheaper, so spending and investment rise, output grows and firms hire.
- **Conflict:** extra demand may push up inflation and draw in imports, worsening the current account.
- **Balance of payments:** higher interest rates reduce spending on imports, but a stronger pound makes exports dearer.
- Since 2009 the Bank has also used quantitative easing (creating money to buy bonds) when rates were already very low. You only need to know that interest rates are the main tool.

### Calculations you must be able to do

**1. Effect on a borrower.** An interest-only mortgage of £150,000 at 4% costs 0.04 × 150,000 = £6,000 a year. At 5% it costs £7,500. The household has **£1,500** a year less to spend.

**2. Effect on a saver.** £10,000 saved at 2% earns £200 a year. At 4% it earns **£400**.

**3. Real interest rate.** If a savings account pays 5% and inflation is 3%, the real interest rate is about 5 − 3 = **2%**.

### Limits of monetary policy

- It can take a year or two for a change in interest rates to have its full effect.
- If confidence is very low, people may not borrow even when rates are low.
- Banks may not pass the full change on to customers.
- One interest rate applies to the whole UK, even if regions are in different positions.
- It works best on demand-pull inflation. It cannot reduce world energy or food prices.
- Low rates hurt savers; high rates hurt borrowers.

## Fiscal and monetary policy compared

| | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The government (Chancellor and HM Treasury) | The Bank of England's MPC |
| Tools | Government spending and taxes | Bank Rate (and quantitative easing) |
| Main use | Growth, jobs, redistribution, public services | Keeping inflation at the 2% target |
| Can target | Particular groups, regions or industries | The whole economy at once |
| Speed | Changes made in the Budget; projects slow to start | Can be changed at any MPC meeting; effect takes time |

## Exam traps

- The **budget deficit** is about government finances. The **current account deficit** is about trade with the rest of the world.
- The deficit is one year's gap; the debt is the total built up. A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
- Monetary policy in the UK is set by the **Bank of England**, not the government.
- Interest rates are a monetary tool, not a fiscal one.
- Always give the chain of effects, not just "rates rise, so inflation falls".

## Quick self-test

1. Define fiscal policy.
2. What is a balanced budget?
3. Spending is £950bn and revenue £980bn. What is the budget position?
4. Give two consequences of a budget deficit.
5. Describe one expansionary fiscal policy and one possible drawback.
6. Define monetary policy and say who carries it out in the UK.
7. What is the UK inflation target?
8. Explain, step by step, how a cut in interest rates could reduce unemployment.
9. A £200,000 interest-only mortgage moves from 3% to 4.5%. By how much do yearly payments rise?
10. Give two limits of monetary policy.

### Answers

1. Changes in government spending and taxation to influence the economy and meet objectives.
2. One where government revenue equals government spending.
3. Revenue is greater, so there is a budget surplus of £30bn.
4. Any two: the national debt rises, more is spent on debt interest, future taxes may rise, lenders may charge more; it can support demand in a recession.
5. For example, cutting income tax raises disposable income and spending; drawbacks include a bigger deficit, inflation or more imports.
6. Changes in interest rates to influence spending; in the UK the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee sets Bank Rate.
7. 2% CPI inflation.
8. Borrowing becomes cheaper, so households spend and firms invest more; demand for goods rises, so firms need more workers.
9. 3% = £6,000, 4.5% = £9,000, so payments rise by £3,000 a year.
10. Any two: time lags, low confidence, banks not passing changes on, one rate for the whole country, less effective on cost-push inflation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling interest rates "fiscal policy".
- Confusing the budget deficit with the national debt, or with the current account.
- Saying the government sets UK interest rates.
- Giving a policy with no conflict or drawback in an evaluation question.
