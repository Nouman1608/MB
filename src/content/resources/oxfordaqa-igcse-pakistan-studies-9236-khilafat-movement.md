Key development 3.1.1.2 of the 9236 specification, Controversy, crises and reaction to repression
(1905-1927), names "the Khilafat movement and its importance for Muslim-Hindu relations", followed by
"non-cooperation and the growth of communalism 1920-27". This guide covers that content in depth. It
sits inside the compulsory Pakistan movement topic, examined in Paper 1 Section A; for the wider
story from 1857 to 1947 use the
[History and Culture of Pakistan study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-history-and-culture/).

## Why Indian Muslims cared about the caliphate

The Ottoman Sultan in Istanbul held the title of **Khalifa** (caliph), and many Muslims in the
subcontinent saw him as the symbolic head of the Muslim world and guardian of the holy places of
Mecca, Medina and Jerusalem. In the First World War the Ottoman Empire fought on Germany's side,
against Britain, yet many Indian Muslims served in the British Indian army. They hoped Britain would
treat the Ottoman Empire fairly once the war was over.

The peace settlement disappointed them. Allied forces occupied Istanbul, Arab lands including the
holy places passed out of Ottoman control, and the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) divided much of what was
left of the empire. To many Muslims in India this looked like a deliberate attack on Islam by the
power that ruled them.

## Leaders and aims

The movement was led by the brothers **Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar** and **Maulana Shaukat Ali**,
with figures such as **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** and **Hakim Ajmal Khan**. A Khilafat Committee was
set up in 1919 to organise the campaign. Its aims were:

- to keep the Ottoman Sultan as Khalifa;
- to keep the holy places of Islam under Muslim control;
- to prevent the break-up of the Ottoman Empire.

In 1920 Muhammad Ali led a delegation to London to put the Muslim case, but the British government
would not change its policy.

## The alliance with Gandhi and Non-Cooperation

Gandhi saw the Khilafat issue as a chance to unite Hindus and Muslims against British rule. With
Khilafat leaders, he launched the **Non-Cooperation movement** in 1920, adopted by Congress that
year. Supporters were asked to:

- return British titles and honours;
- boycott government schools, colleges and law courts;
- boycott elections to the legislative councils;
- boycott British goods and use home-spun cloth instead.

For a time the two communities campaigned together on a scale not seen before. Not every Muslim
leader agreed with the method: **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** opposed mass non-cooperation as likely to lead
to disorder, and he left Congress in 1920.

## The Hijrat movement (1920)

Some religious leaders declared that India under British rule was *dar-ul-harb*, a land where
Muslims could not freely live by their faith, and urged them to migrate to Afghanistan, a Muslim
country. Thousands of Muslims, many from Sindh and the North-West Frontier, sold their land and
property and set out. Afghanistan could not absorb them and closed the border. Many migrants died on
the journey or returned home with nothing, and the episode weakened the movement and caused great
hardship.

## Violence and the end of Non-Cooperation

Two events damaged the alliance.

- **The Moplah uprising (1921).** In Malabar on the south-west coast, Muslim Moplah (Mappila)
  peasants rose against British authority and against Hindu landlords. Attacks on Hindus during the
  uprising were reported across India and increased distrust between the communities.
- **Chauri Chaura (February 1922).** A crowd in the United Provinces set fire to a police station,
  killing the policemen inside. Gandhi called off Non-Cooperation, saying the country was not ready
  for non-violent struggle. Khilafat leaders had not been consulted, and many Muslims felt Congress
  had abandoned them at the moment of greatest effort.

The Ali brothers had already been arrested and imprisoned in 1921, and Gandhi himself was arrested
in 1922.

## Collapse of the movement

The decisive blow came from Turkey. Mustafa Kemal's nationalist government abolished the Ottoman
sultanate in 1922 and the caliphate in **March 1924**. With no Khalifa left to defend, the movement
lost its purpose.

## Importance for Muslim-Hindu relations

The specification asks about importance, so you need to weigh short-term and long-term effects.

**Short term: unity.** Khilafat and Non-Cooperation produced the most united campaign the two
communities had fought together. Hindu leaders supported a Muslim cause, Muslim leaders accepted
Gandhi's leadership, and joint meetings, boycotts and arrests created shared experience.

**Long term: division.** The unity proved fragile.

- The calling-off of Non-Cooperation after Chauri Chaura left many Muslims distrustful of Congress
  leadership.
- The Moplah violence and its reporting hardened attitudes.
- In the mid-1920s Hindu revival movements such as *shuddhi* (reconversion) and *sangathan*
  (organisation) grew, and Muslim organisations answered with *tabligh* and *tanzim*; communal riots
  increased in these years.

**For the Pakistan movement.** The movement brought large numbers of ordinary Muslims and many
ulema into politics for the first time, and gave them experience of organising, campaigning and
going to prison. When it collapsed, many concluded that Muslims needed their own political
organisation rather than reliance on Congress, which strengthened the Muslim League's later appeal.
Some of the ulema involved continued to work with Congress, so the legacy was not one-directional.

## Was the movement a failure?

Judged by its own aims, yes: the Khalifa was not saved, the Ottoman Empire was not preserved and the
holy places did not return to Ottoman control. Judged by its effects, the picture is mixed.

- **Political experience.** A generation of Muslim activists learned to organise mass campaigns,
  raise funds, run newspapers and accept imprisonment. These skills were used again in the 1930s and
  1940s.
- **A test of co-operation.** The movement showed that Hindu-Muslim unity was possible, but also how
  quickly it could break when one partner acted alone. Many Muslims drew the lesson that safeguards
  had to be written down rather than trusted to goodwill, which shaped later demands such as
  Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929).
- **Religion and politics.** The campaign tied a religious cause to political action. That link
  energised Muslims but also made communal tension more likely.

A strong extended answer uses this distinction between aims and effects to reach a judgement.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why the Khilafat movement failed. **[4]**

```
Point 1   Gandhi called off Non-Cooperation after Chauri Chaura (1922)
Explain   Khilafat leaders lost the mass Hindu support their campaign
          depended on, and many Muslims felt betrayed by Congress

Point 2   Mustafa Kemal abolished the caliphate in 1924
Explain   the movement's central aim, keeping the Khalifa, could no
          longer be achieved, so it lost its purpose
```

Each point is identified, then explained with dated detail. The Hijrat or the arrest of leaders
would also be creditable.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the Khilafat and Hijrat movements.** The Hijrat was one episode within the wider
  campaign.
- **Saying Britain abolished the caliphate.** It was abolished by the new Turkish government.
- **Describing unity without the breakdown.** Questions on importance need both.
- **Dating Chauri Chaura to 1921.** It happened in February 1922.
- **Forgetting Jinnah's position.** He opposed the non-cooperation method and left Congress.

## Exam technique

Section A of Paper 1 mixes multiple choice, short answers and extended responses. For 6-mark analysis
questions, develop two effects with dated evidence and link each to Muslim-Hindu relations. For
9-mark judgements, weigh the factor in the question against at least one other and reach a supported
conclusion.
