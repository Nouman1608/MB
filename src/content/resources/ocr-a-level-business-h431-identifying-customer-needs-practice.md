## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between primary and secondary market research. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by trial marketing. **[2]**

**3.** A shop has 4,500 customers on its mailing list and wants a systematic sample of 150. Calculate which customers should be chosen. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** weakness of convenience sampling. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**5.** Orchard Cafe records the number of pastries sold at 9.00 am on five days: 12, 15, 18, 15, 10.

**(a)** Calculate the mean. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the standard deviation, to two decimal places. **[4]**

**(c)** Interpret your answer to part (b) for the manager. **[2]**

**6.** The market for oat drinks in a region was worth £80.0 million last year and £84.8 million this year. Hazel Oat's sales were £9.6 million last year and £10.0 million this year.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of market growth. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate Hazel Oat's market share in each year, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the sales Hazel Oat needed this year to hold its share from last year. **[2]**

**(d)** Analyse the impact on Hazel Oat of the change in its market share. **[6]**

**7.** Dunmore Games is planning a board game for families. It has £6,000 for research. A friend suggests buying a published report on the toy market for £1,800. Another suggests a survey of 400 parents at school gates. Evaluate the usefulness of primary and secondary research to Dunmore Games. **[9]**

**8.** A theme park has 30,000 visitors on a busy day, from three ticket types: children 12,000, adults 15,000 and seniors 3,000. It wants to survey 600 visitors. Evaluate the sampling method the park should use. **[9]**

**9.** A mobile phone repair business finds that its market is growing by 12% a year but its own market share has fallen for two years. Recommend and justify how the business should respond. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary research is new information collected by the business for its own purpose [1], such as a survey or focus group [1]. Secondary research uses information that already exists [1], such as government statistics or competitors' reports. **[3]**

**2.** Offering a product to a limited group or area before the full launch [1] in order to measure customer reaction and correct problems [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 4,500 ÷ 150 = 30 [1], so choose every 30th customer on the list [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It asks whoever is easiest to reach [1], so the sample may not represent the target market, for example only people who shop on weekdays [1], which means results could mislead decisions on product or price [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** (12 + 15 + 18 + 15 + 10) ÷ 5 = 70 ÷ 5 = **14** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Deviations: −2, 1, 4, 1, −4 [1]. Squares: 4, 1, 16, 1, 16, total 38 [1]. Variance = 38 ÷ 5 = 7.6 [1]. Standard deviation = √7.6 = **2.76** [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** On average, sales are about 2.76 pastries from the mean of 14 [1], which is fairly consistent, so the manager can bake close to 14 and plan with confidence, checking the unusual days such as 18 and 10 [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** (84.8 − 80.0) ÷ 80.0 × 100 [1] = **6.0%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Last year: 9.6 ÷ 80.0 × 100 = **12.0%** [1]. This year: 10.0 ÷ 84.8 × 100 = **11.8%** [2: 1 for method, 1 for answer]. **[3]**

**(c)** 12.0% × 84.8 [1] = **£10.176 million** (about £10.18 million) [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Hazel Oat's sales grew by 4.2% (10.0 ÷ 9.6) [1], less than the market's 6.0% [1], so its share fell from 12.0% to 11.8% [1]. This means competitors gained customers, so growth in sales hides a weaker position [1]. If the trend continues the business may lose economies of scale, retailer shelf space or brand strength [1]. Hazel Oat should investigate the cause, such as price, range or rival promotion, using research before responding [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Secondary: the report is quick and gives the size and trends of the whole market, which helps check whether the idea is worthwhile [1]; but it is general, may be out of date and rivals can buy it too [1]; it cannot say whether families like this particular game [1].
Primary: the survey of 400 parents can ask about the actual game, the price they would pay and how likely they are to buy [1]; Dunmore controls the questions and the information is private [1]; but a school-gate sample may be biased towards local families, and the cost and time may use much of the £6,000 [1]; the answers show stated intentions, which may not match purchases [1].
Judgement: because £1,800 of the £6,000 is affordable, Dunmore could buy the report for market size and spend the rest on a carefully sampled survey and a trial run, since the report alone cannot show whether customers want this game [1]. Primary research is the more useful of the two, but secondary research gives a low-cost starting point [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Stratified: the groups differ greatly in size, so a stratified sample takes children 600 × 12,000 ÷ 30,000 = 240, adults 600 × 15,000 ÷ 30,000 = 300 and seniors 600 × 3,000 ÷ 30,000 = 60 [2] (one mark if only some are correct). This represents each group in proportion [1] and ensures that each ticket group's views are included, which matters for pricing [1].
Weakness: it needs knowledge of the groups and may take more staff time than a simple method [1].
Alternatives: random sampling could by chance include too few seniors, who are only 10% of visitors [1]; quota sampling is quicker but staff choose who to ask, which can bias results [1]; convenience sampling at the entrance is cheap but would favour early arrivals [1].
Judgement: stratified is the best choice for a large, varied population, unless time or cost is critical [1]. **[9]**

**9.** The market is growing, so there is demand to capture [1], but falling share means rivals are growing faster [1]. A response needs first to find the cause using market research, such as customer surveys on price, speed and reputation [1].
Possible responses: if price is the issue, offer targeted discounts or bundles [1]; if speed or convenience, add collection or mobile repair [1]; increase promotion online where customers search for repairs [1]. Invest in staff training and capacity to keep pace with a market growing at 12% [1].
Justification: in a growing market, it is cheaper to win new customers than in a mature one, so investment now is more likely to pay back [1]. Risk: spending without research could waste money, and price cuts reduce margin [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Squaring nothing, or taking the root before dividing, in standard deviation.
- Using last year's market value for this year's share.
- Describing sampling methods without saying whether they suit the population.
- Judging research as good or bad without linking to the case.
