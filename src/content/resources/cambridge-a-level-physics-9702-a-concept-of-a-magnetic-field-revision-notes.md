Subtopic 20.1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) asks you to understand that a magnetic field is an example of a field of force produced either by moving charges or by permanent magnets, and to represent a magnetic field by field lines. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and annotated answers, read the [Concept of a magnetic field study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-concept-of-a-magnetic-field/); to apply the ideas, try the [Concept of a magnetic field practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-concept-of-a-magnetic-field-practice/).

## The definition to learn

A **magnetic field** is a region in which a **moving charge**, a **current-carrying conductor** or a **magnetic material** (including another magnet) experiences a force.

- It is a **field of force**: the force acts without contact.
- It is produced by **moving charges** (currents, beams of charged particles) or by **permanent magnets**.
- A **stationary** charge neither produces a magnetic field nor feels a magnetic force.

## Three fields compared

| | Gravitational | Electric | Magnetic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Mass | Charge | Moving charge or permanent magnet |
| Acts on | Mass | Charge, moving or not | Moving charge, current, magnetic material |
| Strength | g (N kg⁻¹) | E (N C⁻¹) | B, flux density (T) |
| Lines | End on masses | Start on +, end on − | Closed loops |
| Force | Always attractive | Attractive or repulsive | Attractive or repulsive between poles and between currents |

## What field lines tell you

- **Direction**: the tangent to the line, arrow pointing the way a compass north pole points (direction of force on a north pole).
- **Strength**: closer lines = stronger field (larger B).

## Drawing rules

- Arrows on every line; outside a magnet, N → S.
- Lines never cross.
- Lines form closed loops (inside a magnet they run S → N).
- Lines are crowded near poles, spread out far away.
- Uniform field: parallel, equally spaced, same direction.
- Neutral point: resultant field zero; no line passes through it.
- Into the page: crosses (×). Out of the page: dots (•). Same convention for currents.

## Patterns to know

| Arrangement | Key features |
|---|---|
| Single bar magnet | N to S outside, densest at poles, symmetrical |
| N facing S | Straight lines across the gap; nearly uniform in the middle; attraction |
| N facing N (or S facing S) | Lines curve away; neutral point midway (for identical magnets); repulsion |
| U-shaped magnet | Uniform between flat pole faces; fringing at the edges |
| Current-carrying coil | Like a bar magnet: one face N, the other S |
| Wire plus Earth's field | Circles near the wire; Earth's field far away; neutral point on one side |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Field of force | Region where an object experiences a non-contact force |
| Magnetic field | Region where a moving charge, current or magnetic material experiences a force |
| Field line | Line whose tangent gives the field direction; spacing shows strength |
| Uniform field | Same strength and direction everywhere; parallel, equally spaced lines |
| Neutral point | Point where fields cancel, so the resultant field is zero |
| Magnetic flux density B | Measure of field strength, in tesla (T); defined in 20.2 |
| Permanent magnet | Material that produces its own magnetic field without an external current |

## Scale check

- Earth's field at the surface: of the order of 10⁻⁵ T.
- Strong laboratory magnets: roughly 0.1 T to 1 T.

## Exam traps

- Leaving out "moving" from the definition, or saying a stationary charge is pushed by a magnetic field.
- Lines that cross or have no arrows.
- Arrows from S to N outside the magnet.
- Lines that start or end in empty space.
- Claiming a compass at a neutral point "spins" or "points nowhere": it lines up with any remaining background field, such as the Earth's.
- Drawing a uniform field with uneven spacing or curved lines.
- Thinking charged particles move along field lines.

## Self-test

1. Name the two sources of a magnetic field listed in the syllabus.
2. Complete the definition: a magnetic field is a region in which …
3. Does a stationary electron in a uniform magnetic field experience a magnetic force?
4. What two pieces of information does a field-line diagram give?
5. Why can field lines never cross?
6. In which direction do field lines run inside a bar magnet?
7. How is a uniform field represented?
8. What does a grid of evenly spaced dots on a diagram represent?
9. Two identical bar magnets have their south poles facing. Where is the neutral point?
10. State one way in which magnetic field lines differ from electric field lines.
11. Why does a current-carrying coil attract a bar magnet?
12. A compass is placed at a neutral point between two magnets on a laboratory bench. Which way does it point?

**Answers:** 1. Moving charges (including currents) and permanent magnets. 2. … a moving charge, a current-carrying conductor or a magnetic material experiences a force. 3. No. 4. The direction of the field (tangent and arrow) and its relative strength (spacing). 5. The field has only one direction at each point; crossing lines would give two. 6. From S to N, completing the closed loop. 7. Parallel, equally spaced straight lines with arrows in the same direction. 8. A uniform field directed out of the page. 9. Midway between the two south poles. 10. Magnetic lines form closed loops; electric lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge. 11. The current produces a magnetic field, so the coil behaves like a magnet with N and S faces; the face nearer an opposite pole attracts it. 12. Along the Earth's magnetic field (roughly north), since the magnets' fields cancel there.
