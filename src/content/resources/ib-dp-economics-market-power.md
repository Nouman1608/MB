## Market power and the four structures

**Market power** is a firm's ability to influence the price it charges. A firm with none is a **price taker**. A firm with some is a **price maker**.

| Structure | Firms | Entry | Product | Market power |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many | Free | Homogeneous | None |
| Monopoly | One, or one dominant | High barriers | No close substitutes | Highest |
| Oligopoly | A few large | High barriers | Often differentiated | High, but interdependent |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Free | Differentiated | Some, limited by substitutes |

Imperfect competition covers the last three. The more substitutes consumers have, the more elastic each firm's demand curve and the less market power it has.

## Profit maximisation (HL calculations)

Definitions:

- Total revenue TR = price × quantity. Average revenue AR = TR/Q, which is the price, so AR is the demand curve.
- Marginal revenue (MR) is what one more unit adds to revenue.
- Average cost AC = TC/Q. Marginal cost MC is the extra cost of one more unit.
- Profit = TR - TC.
- **Abnormal profit**: AR > AC. **Normal profit**: AR = AC. **Loss**: AR < AC.

A rational producer maximises profit at the output where **MC = MR**. Up to that point each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Beyond it, each unit adds more to cost.

**Worked example (data).** A made-up firm, Quillon Ltd, faces the demand P = 60 - 5Q (units are illustrative).

| Q | P = AR | TR | MR | TC | MC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 55 | 55 | 55 | 50 | 20 | 5 |
| 2 | 50 | 100 | 45 | 62 | 12 | 38 |
| 3 | 45 | 135 | 35 | 72 | 10 | 63 |
| 4 | 40 | 160 | 25 | 84 | 12 | 76 |
| 5 | 35 | 175 | 15 | 102 | 18 | 73 |
| 6 | 30 | 180 | 5 | 126 | 24 | 54 |

Working: MR is the change in TR, for example MR at Q = 4 is 160 - 135 = 25. MC is the change in TC, for example MC at Q = 4 is 84 - 72 = 12. At Q = 4, MR (25) exceeds MC (12); at Q = 5, MR (15) is below MC (18). The firm produces 4 units at price 40. AC at Q = 4 is 84/4 = 21, so AR (40) > AC (21): abnormal profit of (40 - 21) × 4 = 76, which matches TR - TC = 160 - 84.

On a diagram, abnormal profit is the rectangle between AR and AC, from the axis to the profit-maximising quantity.

## Perfect competition: no market power

A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker, so its demand curve is horizontal at the market price: **P = D = AR = MR**. Each extra unit sells at the same price, so MR equals the price.

**Short run.** The firm sets MC = MR, which here means MC = P. Three outcomes follow:

- P > AC: abnormal profit.
- P = AC: normal profit.
- P < AC: loss.

**Long run.** Entry is free. Abnormal profit attracts new firms, market supply shifts right, and price falls. Losses push firms out, supply shifts left, and price rises. Entry or exit stops when firms earn normal profit.

**Worked example.** Take a made-up firm with TC = 100 + 4Q + Q², so MC = 4 + 2Q and AC = 100/Q + 4 + Q. AC is lowest at Q = 10, where AC = 24 and MC = 24.

| Price | MC = P gives Q | TR | TC | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 18 | 720 | 496 | Abnormal profit 224 |
| 24 | 10 | 240 | 240 | Normal profit |
| 16 | 6 | 96 | 160 | Loss 64 |

At price 40, entry follows and price falls. At price 16, exit follows and price rises. The long-run price is 24, at the lowest point of AC.

**Allocative efficiency** means resources go where they are most valued: the price (what buyers will pay for the final unit) equals the marginal cost of making it, **P = MC**. In terms of the demand and supply diagram, MB = MC and social (community) surplus is at its maximum. Perfect competition meets this condition in the long-run example above (P = MC = 24).

## Monopoly

A monopolist is the only seller or the dominant one, faces the whole market demand, and has AR above MR (the demand curve slopes down, so selling more means lowering the price on every unit). It still maximises profit at MC = MR, but it then reads the price from the AR curve above that point. Its market power appears as **AR > MC**.

**Worked example.** A made-up monopolist faces P = 96 - 2Q, so MR = 96 - 4Q. Its costs are TC = 180 + 12Q + Q², so MC = 12 + 2Q.

- MC = MR: 12 + 2Q = 96 - 4Q, so 6Q = 84 and Q = 14.
- Price: P = 96 - 28 = 68. MC at Q = 14 is 40.
- TR = 68 × 14 = 952. TC = 180 + 168 + 196 = 544. Profit = 408. AC = 544/14 = 38.9, below AR.

Change the fixed cost and the same demand gives other outcomes. With fixed cost 588, TC = 952 and AR = AC at Q = 14, so the monopolist earns **normal profit**. With fixed cost 700, TC = 1,064 and the monopolist makes a **loss** of 112, because AR is below AC. On the diagram the AC curve then touches, or lies above, the AR curve at the profit-maximising output.

### Compared with perfect competition

Suppose the same industry were perfectly competitive, with the same MC curve acting as supply. Then P = MC: 96 - 2Q = 12 + 2Q, so Q = 21 and P = 54.

| | Perfect competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 21 | 14 |
| Price | 54 | 68 |
| P compared with MC | P = MC (54 = 54) | P > MC (68 > 40) |

The monopolist restricts output and charges more. Output 14 is allocatively inefficient, because units between 14 and 21 are valued by consumers above their marginal cost but are not produced. The **welfare loss** is the triangle between the demand and MC curves from Q = 14 to Q = 21: ½ × (21 - 14) × (68 - 40) = ½ × 7 × 28 = 98. This is **market failure** from market power.

### Natural monopoly

A natural monopoly arises where economies of scale are so large that average cost keeps falling across the whole market demand, so one firm can supply at lower cost than two. The AC curve is still falling where it meets demand.

**Worked example.** A made-up water network has demand P = 40 - Q, constant MC = 4 and fixed cost 180, so AC = 180/Q + 4.

- Unregulated: MR = 40 - 2Q = 4, so Q = 18 and P = 22. AC = 14, giving profit (22 - 14) × 18 = 144.
- P = MC (allocatively efficient): Q = 36, P = 4, but AC = 9, so a loss of (9 - 4) × 36 = 180. No private firm would stay.
- P = AC: 40 - Q = 180/Q + 4 gives Q² - 36Q + 180 = 0, so Q = 30 (or 6). P = 10 = AC, normal profit, and more output than the unregulated case.

A regulator may therefore cap the price at AC rather than MC.

## Oligopoly

An oligopoly has a few large firms and high barriers to entry. Firms are **interdependent**: each firm's best choice depends on how rivals react.

**Concentration ratio.** The CR for n firms is the sum of the market shares of the n largest. With made-up shares of 30, 25, 15, 10, 8, 7 and 5 per cent, CR4 = 30 + 25 + 15 + 10 = 80 per cent. A high ratio signals a market dominated by few firms, though it says nothing about how they behave.

**Collusive and non-collusive.** Firms that collude agree prices or output, often secretly. Together they can behave like a monopoly: the group sets industry output where industry MC = MR and shares the profit. Non-collusive firms compete, and the risk is a **price war**, where rivals cut price in turn and profits fall. Whichever way they behave, oligopolists with market power price above marginal cost (P > MC), so oligopoly is allocatively inefficient, a market failure like monopoly; a collusive group that acts as a monopoly shows the same output restriction and welfare loss.

**Payoff matrix.** Two made-up firms, Aurel and Borin, each choose High or Low price. Payoffs are profit (Aurel, Borin):

| | Borin High | Borin Low |
|---|---|---|
| **Aurel High** | 40, 40 | 15, 55 |
| **Aurel Low** | 55, 15 | 25, 25 |

If Borin charges High, Aurel gains by choosing Low (55 against 40). If Borin charges Low, Aurel again does better with Low (25 against 15). The same holds for Borin, so both choose Low and earn 25 each, though both would prefer 40 each. This is the **incentive to collude** (joint profit 80 against 50) and the **incentive to cheat** (55 for the firm that undercuts).

**Price and non-price competition.** Price competition means cutting price. Non-price competition uses advertising, branding, quality, service and loyalty schemes, and avoids starting a price war.

## Monopolistic competition

Many firms sell differentiated products and entry is free. Because substitutes are close, each firm's demand is more elastic than a monopolist's.

**Worked example.** A made-up café has AR = 50 - 2Q, MR = 50 - 4Q, TC = 100 + 10Q so MC = 10 and AC = 100/Q + 10.

- Short run: MR = MC gives Q = 10, P = 30, AC = 20. Abnormal profit = (30 - 20) × 10 = 100.
- Long run: profit attracts entrants. Their rival products take customers, so the café's demand shifts left and becomes more elastic. Entry stops when the demand curve is tangent to AC. In the example the long-run demand is AR = 30 - Q, which is lower and flatter than 50 - 2Q (more elastic at any given price): MR = 30 - 2Q = 10 gives Q = 10, P = 20, AC = 100/10 + 10 = 20. AR = AC and the curves touch, so the café earns normal profit.
- Losses do the opposite: exit shifts the remaining firms' demand right.

In the long run P (20) exceeds MC (10), so there is allocative inefficiency, but less than monopoly, and consumers gain **product variety**.

## Large firms: advantages and risks

**Advantages.** *Economies of scale* lower average cost, and natural monopolies are the extreme case. *Abnormal profit* can pay for research and development, producing innovation.

**Risks.** Less output and higher price (shown above), reduced consumer choice, and possible abuse of power such as price fixing.

## Government intervention

- **Legislation and regulation**: laws against collusion and abuse of a dominant position, and price caps for natural monopolies.
- **Government ownership**: taking the firm into public ownership, so output and price can serve social goals.
- **Fines**: penalising firms that break the rules.

To evaluate, weigh the gains in price and output against the costs: regulators may lack cost information, public firms may be inefficient, and fines may be passed to consumers.

## Common errors

- Reading the monopolist's price off the MR curve. Find Q at MC = MR, then go up to AR.
- Calling a positive profit "normal". Normal profit means AR = AC.
- Drawing the welfare loss to the MR curve instead of between demand and MC.
- Treating a high concentration ratio as proof of collusion.
