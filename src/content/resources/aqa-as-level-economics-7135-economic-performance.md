## 3.2.3.1 Growth, the cycle and output gaps

**Short-run growth** is an increase in real national income, which can come from higher AD using spare capacity: a movement from inside a production possibility curve towards it, or a rightward AD shift towards LRAS. **Long-run growth** is an increase in productive capacity, shown as an outward shift of the PPC and a rightward shift of LRAS. The **trend rate of growth** is the long-run average rate at which real output grows over time.

**The economic cycle** is the fluctuation of real GDP around its trend. The specification asks you to use a range of indicators, such as real GDP, inflation, unemployment and investment, to identify the phase.

| Phase | Real GDP | Unemployment | Inflation | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | Growing above trend | Low and falling | Rising | Strong |
| Downturn | Growth slowing or negative | Starting to rise | Starting to ease | Falling |
| Recession | Falling | Rising | Low | Weak |
| Recovery | Growing again | High but starting to fall | Low | Picking up |

**Output gaps.** A **positive output gap** occurs when real GDP is above the economy's productive potential; a **negative output gap** when it is below.

```
Output gap (%) = (actual real GDP - potential real GDP) / potential x 100
Actual 1,940, potential 2,000:  -60 / 2,000 = -3%  (negative gap)
Actual 2,050, potential 2,000:  +50 / 2,000 = +2.5% (positive gap)
```

**Shocks.** Demand-side shocks (a fall in export demand during a world recession) and supply-side shocks (a jump in world energy prices) both move domestic economic activity away from trend.

## 3.2.3.2 Employment and unemployment

**The two UK measures.**

| Measure | Who is counted |
|---|---|
| Claimant count | People claiming unemployment-related benefits |
| Labour Force Survey (LFS) | People without work who are available to start work and have been actively seeking work, identified through a large regular survey of households |

The two can differ: some unemployed people do not claim benefits, and the benefit rules decide who is counted in the claimant count.

**Unemployment rate** = number unemployed / labour force x 100. With 1.6 million unemployed and a labour force of 34 million, the rate is 4.7%.

**Types and the matching policies.** The specification stresses that unemployment has a variety of causes, so the right policy depends on the cause.

| Type | Cause | Demand or supply side | Likely remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seasonal | Jobs that exist only at certain times of year | Regular seasonal pattern in demand for labour | Diversifying local industries |
| Frictional | Time spent moving between jobs | Supply side | Better job information |
| Structural | Decline of an industry; skills or location mismatch | Supply side | Retraining, help with moving |
| Cyclical | Too little AD in a downturn | Demand side | Raising AD |

**The rest of the world** affects UK employment through exports (a recession abroad cuts jobs in exporting firms), through competition from imports, and through firms moving production abroad.

## 3.2.3.3 Inflation and deflation

- **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general price level. **Deflation**: a falling price level. **Disinflation**: a falling rate of inflation, with prices still rising.
- **Demand-pull**: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity. **Cost-push**: rising costs, such as wages or imported materials, shift SRAS left.
- **World commodity prices**: oil, gas, metals and food are inputs for many firms. If energy is 10% of a firm's costs and its price rises 30%, its total costs rise by about 3%, which feeds into prices.
- **Other economies**: inflation abroad raises import prices; strong growth abroad raises demand for exports and for world commodities.

The specification also notes that **deflationary policies** are policies to reduce AD; they do not necessarily cause deflation. Lowering inflation from 6% to 3% is disinflation, not deflation.

## 3.2.3.4 The current account

International trade matters to an economy such as the UK because it allows specialisation, access to goods it cannot produce efficiently, and larger markets for its firms. The current account has four parts: **trade in goods, trade in services, primary income** (for example investment income and pay of workers from abroad) and **secondary income** (transfers such as foreign aid and money sent home by migrants).

```
Trade in goods -60, trade in services +45, primary income -10, secondary income -15
Current account balance = -40 bn, a deficit
With GDP of 2,000 bn: -40 / 2,000 = -2.0% of GDP
```

Influences named in the specification: **productivity, inflation, the exchange rate and economic activity in other countries**. Higher productivity and lower inflation than rivals make exports more competitive; a higher exchange rate makes exports dearer and imports cheaper; faster growth abroad raises demand for exports. You need only know that the current account is one part of the balance of payments, not the other parts.

## 3.2.3.5 Conflicts and output gaps

The output gap links the objectives:

- **Negative gap**: spare capacity, so unemployment is above its normal level and inflationary pressure is weak.
- **Positive gap**: resources are stretched, so unemployment is low, but wages and prices are pushed up, and strong demand draws in imports.

Expanding AD to close a negative gap cuts unemployment but, as the gap closes, raises inflation and imports: the short-run conflicts. Policies to **reconcile** them include timing demand-side policy so the gap closes gradually, and using supply-side policies to raise potential output so that growth does not create a positive gap. In the long run, rising productivity lets growth, low unemployment and price stability coexist.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: using the data, explain why inflation rose while unemployment fell.*

> Real GDP grew by 4% while potential output grew by about 2%, so a negative output gap closed and turned positive. With firms producing above their normal capacity, demand for workers rose, which reduced unemployment from 5.2% to 4.1%. Labour shortages pushed up wages and firms faced rising costs, so the inflation rate rose from 2% to 3.5%, an example of demand-pull pressure adding to cost-push effects.

Why it works: it quotes the data, uses the output gap to connect the two objectives and names the type of inflation.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a fall in the inflation rate "deflation".
- Treating any rise in GDP as long-run growth.
- Giving the same policy for every type of unemployment.
- Mixing the current account with the whole balance of payments.
- Stating that a negative output gap means output is falling; it means output is below potential.

## Exam technique

- When identifying the phase of the cycle, use at least two indicators from the data.
- For unemployment questions, decide the type from the data before suggesting a cause or policy.
- In evaluation, use the size and sign of the output gap and whether a shock is demand-side or supply-side.
