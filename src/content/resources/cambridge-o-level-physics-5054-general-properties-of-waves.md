This guide covers subtopic **3.1 General properties of waves** from Topic 3, Waves, in Cambridge
O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series). The [waves topic guide](/resources/o-level-physics-waves/)
gives an overview of all of Topic 3. This page goes further into reading wave measurements from
diagrams and data, the full range of v = fλ calculations, and exactly what changes and what stays
the same in reflection, refraction and diffraction.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL PHYSICS 5054, subtopic 3.1**

1. Know that waves transfer energy without transferring matter
2. Describe wave motion as illustrated by vibrations in ropes and springs and by experiments using
   water waves
3. Describe the features of a wave in terms of wavefront, wavelength, frequency, crest (peak),
   trough, amplitude and wave speed
4. Define frequency as the number of wavelengths that pass a point per unit time; wavelength as
   the distance between two consecutive, identical points such as two consecutive crests; and
   amplitude as the maximum distance from the mean position
5. Recall and use wave speed = frequency × wavelength, v = fλ
6. Know that for a transverse wave the direction of vibration is at right angles to the direction
   of energy transfer, with examples: electromagnetic radiation, waves on the surface of water and
   seismic S-waves (secondary)
7. Know that for a longitudinal wave the direction of vibration is parallel to the direction of
   energy transfer, with examples: sound waves and seismic P-waves (primary)
8. Describe how waves can undergo reflection at a plane surface, refraction due to a change of
   speed, and diffraction through a gap
9. Describe how wavelength and gap size affect diffraction through a gap
10. Describe the use of a ripple tank to show reflection at a plane surface, refraction due to a
    change in speed caused by a change in depth, diffraction due to a gap and diffraction due to
    an edge
11. Describe how wavelength affects diffraction at an edge

## Energy, not matter

A cork floating on a pond bobs up and down as ripples pass, but it does not travel along with
them. The water particles vibrate about their mean positions while the **energy** moves outwards.
The same is true of a rope: each part moves up and down, but the rope does not travel across the
room.

## Showing wave motion

- **Rope:** fix one end and move the other up and down. A transverse wave travels along it.
- **Slinky spring, side to side:** a transverse wave.
- **Slinky spring, pushed and pulled along its length:** coils bunch up and spread out as a
  longitudinal wave travels along it.
- **Water waves:** made in a ripple tank by a vibrating bar (straight wavefronts) or a dipper
  (circular wavefronts).

## Wave features

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Wavefront** | A line joining points on a wave that are in step, such as a line along a crest |
| **Crest (peak)** | Highest point of a transverse wave |
| **Trough** | Lowest point of a transverse wave |
| **Wavelength (λ)** | Distance between two consecutive identical points, such as two consecutive crests (m) |
| **Amplitude** | Maximum distance from the mean position (m) |
| **Frequency (f)** | Number of wavelengths passing a point per unit time (Hz) |
| **Wave speed (v)** | Distance travelled by a wavefront per unit time (m/s) |

**Amplitude is measured from the middle**, not from crest to trough. A wave whose crests and
troughs are 6.0 cm apart vertically has an amplitude of 3.0 cm.

## Getting measurements from a diagram or data

**Wavelength:** count complete waves, not crests. Between the first crest and the sixth crest
there are **five** wavelengths.

**Frequency:** count the waves passing a point and divide by the time.

**Worked example 1.** In a large wave tank, 15 waves pass a mark in 5.0 s. The distance from the
first crest to the sixth crest is 1.25 m.

```
f = 15 / 5.0 = 3.0 Hz
λ = 1.25 / 5 = 0.25 m
v = f λ = 3.0 × 0.25 = 0.75 m/s
```

## Using v = fλ

```
v = f λ        f = v / λ        λ = v / f
```

Keep units consistent: hertz, metres and m/s. Convert kHz to Hz (× 1000) and cm to m (÷ 100).

**Worked example 2 (sound).** A note of frequency 680 Hz travels through air at 340 m/s.

```
λ = v / f = 340 / 680 = 0.50 m
```

**Worked example 3 (seismic wave, illustrative values).** A P-wave travels through rock at
6000 m/s with a frequency of 4.0 Hz.

```
λ = 6000 / 4.0 = 1500 m
```

**Worked example 4 (unit conversion).** Ripples have wavelength 1.5 cm and frequency 12 Hz.

```
λ = 0.015 m;  v = 12 × 0.015 = 0.18 m/s
```

**What sets what?** The **source** sets the frequency. The **medium** sets the speed. The
wavelength then follows from λ = v ÷ f. So if a vibrator speeds up in a ripple tank of constant
depth, the frequency rises, the speed stays the same and the wavelength gets shorter.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Vibration compared with energy transfer | At right angles | Parallel |
| Syllabus examples | Electromagnetic radiation, water surface waves, seismic S-waves | Sound, seismic P-waves |
| Demonstration | Rope or slinky moved side to side | Slinky pushed and pulled along its length |

## Reflection, refraction and diffraction

| Effect | What happens | Speed | Wavelength | Frequency | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reflection at a plane surface | Wave bounces off the barrier | Same | Same | Same | Changes; angle of incidence = angle of reflection |
| Refraction | Wave changes speed in a new medium or depth | Changes | Changes | Same | Changes if the wave meets the boundary at an angle |
| Diffraction | Wave spreads out through a gap or round an edge | Same | Same | Same | Spreads |

**Refraction from deep to shallow water.** Waves travel **more slowly** in shallow water. The
frequency is unchanged (set by the source), so the wavelength **decreases**: the wavefronts get
closer together. If the wavefronts meet the boundary at an angle, the part that enters the
shallow region first slows first, so the wavefronts turn and the direction of travel bends
**towards the normal**.

**Diffraction through a gap.**

- Most noticeable when the **gap width is similar to the wavelength**: the wavefronts emerge as
  nearly circular arcs, spreading widely.
- When the gap is **much wider** than the wavelength, the waves pass through mostly straight, with
  slight spreading only at the edges.
- For a fixed gap, **longer wavelength** gives more diffraction.

**Diffraction at an edge.** Waves passing the end of a barrier bend into the region behind it.
**Longer wavelengths bend more** into this "shadow" region. For example, long-wavelength water
waves curl further round the end of a harbour wall than short ripples.

## Using a ripple tank

**Set-up:** a shallow transparent tray of water; a vibrating bar for straight waves or a dipper
for circular waves; a lamp above, so the crests act as lenses and form bright lines on a screen or
paper below, making the wavefronts visible. A stroboscope can make the pattern appear stationary.

| To show | Arrangement | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Reflection | Straight metal barrier placed in the path at an angle | Reflected wavefronts leave at an equal angle |
| Refraction | Glass plate on the floor of the tank to make a shallow region | Wavefronts closer together over the plate; direction changes if the boundary is at an angle |
| Diffraction by a gap | Two barriers with a gap between them | Waves spread beyond the gap; more spreading when the gap is narrowed towards one wavelength |
| Diffraction by an edge | A single barrier | Waves bend into the region behind the end of the barrier |

## Common mistakes

- Measuring amplitude from crest to trough.
- Counting crests instead of wavelengths when reading λ from a diagram.
- Saying the frequency changes during refraction.
- Saying diffraction changes the wavelength.
- Mixing cm with m, or kHz with Hz, in v = fλ.
- Giving sound as transverse, or electromagnetic waves as longitudinal.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Define**: use the syllabus wording exactly, for example "the number of wavelengths that pass a
  point per unit time".
- **Describe** (a ripple tank demonstration): the arrangement and what is seen.
- **Calculate**: rearrange v = fλ, convert units, give the answer with a unit.
- **State**: a short answer, such as "frequency is unchanged".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does refraction happen?** Because the wave speed changes when the wave enters a different
medium or depth.

**Does diffraction happen with any gap?** Yes, but it is only obvious when the gap is not much
larger than the wavelength.

**Why is the ripple tank lit from above?** The curved water surface focuses light, so the crests
appear as bright lines on the screen below, making wavefronts easy to see and measure.
