**How to use this set.** The syllabus allows that "One of the assignments may be an empathic response
to a prose or drama text." These questions practise the rules, the planning and the writing. The
longer questions use two invented texts, summarised below, so that you can see all the material a
character would know.

**Invented text 1: *The Salt Road* (a novel).** Leila, seventeen, idolises her father, a sea captain
she believes was lost in a storm. She often repeats his phrase "Steady as she goes." Her mother speaks
of the storm in a flat voice that Leila resents. Halfway through, Leila finds eleven letters in his
trunk, tied with net-mending cord, showing he survived and chose not to return. A week later she
leaves home to find him.

**Invented text 2: *The Glasshouse* (a play).** Mr Ward, a strict, formal gardener in his sixties,
has tended an estate's glasshouse for forty years. In Act 2 the new owner tells him the glasshouse will
be demolished. He says only, "Very good, sir." In Act 3 he is found at dawn watering plants that are
due to be destroyed. He speaks in short, precise sentences and calls every plant by its Latin name.

## Section A

**1.** State **two** rules from the syllabus that limit when an empathic response may be submitted.
**[2]**

**2.** A student plans an empathic response in the voice of the speaker of a poem. Explain whether
this is allowed. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what a hindsight error is, using *The Salt Road* to give an example. **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite this weak task so that it gives a moment and allows for all four assessment objectives:
"Write as Mr Ward." **[2]**

**5.** Identify **two** features of Mr Ward's voice that an empathic piece should reproduce. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the difference between AO3 at Level 8 and at Level 4 for an empathic task, using the
syllabus's wording. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** You are Leila on the night you find the letters. Make a voice sheet: list **four** words or
phrases, **two** sentence shapes and **two** things Leila would never say, and justify **two** of your
choices. **[6]**

**8.** Write an opening of about 100 words for: "You are Mr Ward, at dawn on the day the glasshouse is
to be demolished. Write your thoughts." **[6]**

**9.** Read this extract from a candidate's response for *The Salt Road*:

> I found the letters and they said Dad was alive. He was in a harbour. Mum knew. Then I went to bed.
> I felt really angry and sad. Next week I am going to find him and it will be hard.

**(a)** Identify **three** weaknesses. **[3]**
**(b)** Rewrite the extract to correct them, in about 80 words. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how an empathic piece can show critical understanding (AO2) without stepping out of
role, using Mr Ward's line "Very good, sir." **[4]**

**11.** Plan the shape of an empathic piece for Leila using the four stages reaction, memory,
struggle and ending, with one detail from the text for each stage. **[4]**

**12.** A student's portfolio contains an empathic piece on *The Glasshouse* and a critical essay on
two poems. Explain **two** reasons why this combination meets the rules, and give **one** piece of
advice on balancing the two assignments. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, [1] each: only one of the two assignments may be empathic; it must be on a prose or
drama text; the text must have been studied as a whole and be equivalent in scope and demand to the
set texts; the title must allow scope for all four assessment objectives. Maximum 2.

**2.** It is not allowed [1], because the empathic option applies only to a prose or drama text; the
student could write a critical assignment on two or more poems instead [1].

**3.** Letting the character know about events that happen later in the text [1]; for example, Leila
on the night she finds the letters thinking about the journey she only decides to make a week later
[1].

**4.** A specific moment [1] with a clear invitation to thought or feeling [1], for example: "You are
Mr Ward, on the evening the new owner tells you the glasshouse will be demolished. Write your
thoughts."

**5.** Any two, [1] each: short, precise sentences; formal politeness ("sir"); the Latin names of
plants; restraint, with feeling held back rather than stated. Maximum 2.

**6.** At Level 8 the candidate "sustains an entirely convincing voice for the character" [1]; at
Level 4 the candidate only "begins to assume a voice for the character" [1].

**7.** Four suitable words or phrases [2] ([1] for each pair); two sentence shapes [1]; two things she
would never say [1]; two justifications, [1] each [2]. Total 6.
For example: words "Steady as she goes", "tide", "storm", "Father"; sentence shapes: quick confident
statements, and broken one-word sentences when hurt; never says: that her mother was right all along;
anything calm about the storm. Justification: "Steady as she goes" is her father's phrase, so using
it shows her attachment; one-word sentences match the way she falters in the novel when hurt.

**8.** Award up to [6]: a voice consistent with Mr Ward's short, precise sentences [1]; formal
register [1]; integrated detail, such as forty years of work or Latin plant names [1]; a clear moment
with no hindsight [1]; feeling implied rather than stated [1]; a sense of where the piece is going
[1]. For example: "Five o'clock. The light comes in from the east, as it always has. Forty years I
have opened these doors at five. *Camellia japonica* first; she does not like to wait. They tell me
the machines come at nine. Very good. I shall water her all the same. A plant does not know about
owners. It knows about water, and light, and whether someone comes. I come. That is all I have ever
done. It is not a thing one stops because one is told."

**9. (a)** Any three, [1] each: it retells the plot; it states emotions ("really angry and sad")
instead of showing them; it uses a flat, generic voice unlike Leila's; it contains a hindsight error
about next week; it lacks textual detail such as the cord, the eleven letters or the father's phrase.
Maximum 3.
**(b)** Award up to [5]: a recognisable Leila voice [1]; integrated detail [1]; feelings shown
through thought and rhythm [1]; no hindsight [1]; a sense of struggle or movement [1]. For example:
"Eleven letters, tied with his net cord. Eleven. Steady as she goes, he used to say, and I thought
steadiness was something he had. A harbour. Alive, in a harbour. Mother's flat voice, every March, the
storm, the storm. She knew. She knew, and she let me light candles for a man who was buying stamps.
I will not cry. Not for him."

**10.** Understanding is shown through what the character notices, remembers and misreads [1]. Mr Ward
might replay "Very good, sir." in his thoughts, revealing the gap between his polite words and his
private devastation [1]. This shows the candidate understands the play's use of restraint and
subtext [1] without the piece ever explaining it, which keeps it in role [1].

**11.** One mark for each stage with a suitable text detail [4], for example: reaction: the
discovery of eleven letters tied with net cord; memory: her father repeating "Steady as
she goes"; struggle: re-reading her mother's flat voice as knowledge, not coldness; ending: refusing
to cry, without any reference to the journey she makes a week later.

**12.** Two reasons, [1] each: only one assignment is empathic; the empathic piece is on a drama text;
the assignments are on different texts; the poetry assignment covers more than one poem. Advice [1]:
for example, keep both within 600–1200 words, or make sure the critical essay's title allows for all
four assessment objectives. Maximum 3.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating feelings instead of revealing them through voice.
- Plot summary in the first person.
- Generic voices that could belong to any character.
- Hindsight about later events.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an empathic response easier than a critical essay?**
It is different: it is marked against the same eight levels, and a convincing voice with integrated
detail is demanding to sustain.

**Can I choose the moment myself?**
Discuss it with your teacher; the moment should give the character something important to think
about and allow scope for all four assessment objectives.
