This guide covers sub-topic **8.2 Water uptake** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. | 8.2.2 | State | The large surface area of root hairs increases the uptake of water and mineral ions |
| 8.2.3 | Outline | The pathway of water through root, stem and leaf: root hair cells, root cortex cells, xylem, mesophyll cells |

## Root hair cells (8.2.1)

### Recognising one

Root hair cells are found in the outer layer of a young root, a little way behind the root tip. In a diagram or micrograph look for:

- a cell in the **outer layer** of the root;
- a **long, thin extension** (the root hair) growing out sideways from the cell into the soil;
- a **large vacuole** and a nucleus, often near the base of the hair;
- **no chloroplasts**: root hairs are underground, so there is no light for photosynthesis.

The hair is part of the cell, not a separate structure. It is a narrow outgrowth of the same cell wall, membrane and cytoplasm.

### Their functions

Root hair cells **take up water and mineral ions** from the soil. Water enters by **osmosis**, moving from the soil water into the cell through the partially permeable cell membrane. Mineral ions such as nitrate and magnesium ions are taken in as well. (If you are taking Extended, you will also explain water movement using water potential and ion uptake by active transport in topic 3.)

## Large surface area (8.2.2)

A young root carries a great many root hairs, and each hair pushes out between soil particles. Together they give the root a **large surface area** in contact with soil water, so more water and mineral ions can be taken up at the same time.

### Worked example: how much difference do root hairs make?

The figures are for practice only. A short length of root without hairs has a surface area of 0.8 mm². The same length with root hairs has a surface area of 9.6 mm².

- How many times larger? 9.6 ÷ 0.8 = **12 times**.
- Percentage increase? (9.6 − 0.8) ÷ 0.8 × 100 = 8.8 ÷ 0.8 × 100 = **1100%**.
- Conclusion: the root hairs greatly increase the area through which water and mineral ions can enter, so uptake is faster.

### Worked example: transplanting seedlings

**A gardener moves seedlings from a tray to a flower bed. Seedlings pulled out of the soil wilt, but seedlings lifted with a ball of soil around their roots do not. Suggest why.** **[3]**

- Pulling the seedlings out tears off many root hairs [1].
- This reduces the surface area for water uptake [1].
- So the seedling takes up less water than it loses from its leaves, and it wilts; keeping soil around the roots protects the root hairs [1].

## The pathway of water (8.2.3)

Learn the four stages in order, exactly as the syllabus gives them:

```
root hair cells → root cortex cells → xylem → mesophyll cells
```

| Stage | What happens to the water |
|---|---|
| Root hair cells | water enters from the soil by osmosis |
| Root cortex cells | water passes from cell to cell across the cortex towards the centre of the root |
| Xylem | water enters the xylem in the centre of the root and is carried up the stem |
| Mesophyll cells | water leaves the xylem in the leaf veins and enters the mesophyll cells |

From the mesophyll cells, water is used in photosynthesis, and much of it evaporates and is lost as water vapour in transpiration (sub-topic 8.3). Transpiration is what keeps water moving along this pathway.

A useful self-check: the pathway goes **outside to centre** in the root (root hair → cortex → central xylem), then **up** the stem in xylem, then **out** into the leaf cells.

## Investigating the pathway with a stain (8.2.4)

The statement asks you to investigate the pathway through the **above-ground parts** of a plant, so you use a cut shoot, not the roots.

### Plan

1. **Choose the plant:** a leafy stalk of celery, or a shoot with white flowers, so the coloured dye shows clearly.
2. **Choose the stain:** a water-soluble dye such as food colouring or a suitable biological stain, made up in water in a beaker.
3. **Cut the stem under water** or immediately before placing it in the dye, so that the xylem fills with dye solution rather than air.
4. **Leave it in conditions that encourage transpiration**, for example in a warm, bright place, for a few hours.
5. **Cut thin sections** across the stalk at different heights, and across a leaf, using a sharp scalpel on a white tile, cutting away from your fingers.
6. **Observe** with a hand lens or a microscope and record where the colour appears.

### Results and conclusion

- In the stem section, the dye appears as **coloured spots in a ring** near the edge: these are the **xylem** in the vascular bundles.
- In the leaf, the **veins** become coloured, and white petals take on the colour.
- Conclusion: water travels up the stem and into the leaves and flowers in the **xylem**.

### Making it more informative

- Cutting sections at several heights and noting the highest coloured section shows how far the water has travelled in the time allowed.
- A shoot left in cool, still conditions can be compared with one left in warm, moving air, linking the pathway to the rate of transpiration. Keep the type of shoot, its length and the dye concentration the same.
- Safety: scalpels are sharp, so cut on a tile away from the body; many dyes stain skin and clothing, so wear gloves and a lab coat.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the root hair a separate cell or a "hair on the root". It is an extension of one root hair cell.
- Drawing chloroplasts in a root hair cell.
- Saying root hairs absorb "food" or "nutrients from the soil". They absorb **water and mineral ions**.
- Saying root hairs increase "the surface area of the plant" without linking it to uptake of water and mineral ions.
- Missing out the root cortex or the mesophyll cells in the pathway, or putting the xylem first.
- Describing the stain result as "the stem turned red" instead of naming the xylem.
- Putting the dye into the soil of a potted plant when the statement asks about the above-ground parts.

## Exam technique for 8.2

- **Identify**: point to the cell with the long extension in the outer layer of a young root.
- **State** functions: "uptake of water and mineral ions".
- **State** the surface-area point: "large surface area increases the uptake of water and mineral ions".
- **Outline** the pathway: all four stages, in order, using the syllabus names.
- **Describe** the investigation: plant, stain, time, sectioning, observation, conclusion.
