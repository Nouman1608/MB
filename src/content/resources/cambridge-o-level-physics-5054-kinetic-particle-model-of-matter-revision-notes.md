## States of matter at a glance

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes container's shape | Fills container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Not fixed |
| Compressible? | No | No | Yes |
| Arrangement | Close, regular | Close, irregular | Far apart, random |
| Forces between particles | Strong | Weaker | Negligible (except in collisions) |
| Motion | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around each other | Fast, random, straight lines between collisions |

**Changes of state:** melting (s → l), freezing or solidification (l → s), boiling or
evaporation (l → g), condensation (g → l).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Particle** | Atom, molecule, ion or electron |
| **Kinetic energy (of particles)** | Energy of motion; average value linked to temperature |
| **Absolute zero** | −273 °C (0 K): lowest possible temperature, particles have least kinetic energy |
| **Pressure** | Force per unit area |
| **Gas pressure** | Force per unit area from particles colliding with the walls |
| **Inverse proportion** | One quantity doubles as the other halves; product stays constant |
| **Fixed mass of gas** | No gas enters or leaves, so the number of particles is constant |

## Temperature and particle motion

- Higher temperature → higher **average kinetic energy** → particles move faster.
- At absolute zero, **−273 °C**, particles have the **least** kinetic energy possible.

## Gas pressure in four steps

1. Particles collide with the walls.
2. Each collision exerts a force on the wall.
3. Many collisions per second give a steady total force.
4. Pressure = force per unit area.

## The three relationships

| Condition | Change | Particle explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Constant volume | T up → p up | Faster particles hit walls more often and harder |
| Constant pressure | T up → V up | Faster particles push walls out until the collision rate per unit area restores the pressure |
| Constant temperature | V down → p up | Same particles, same speed, smaller area: more collisions per unit area per second |

## Equation

```
p1 V1 = p2 V2     (fixed mass of gas, constant temperature)
```

- Same units on both sides (kPa with kPa, cm³ with cm³).
- Example: 120 cm³ at 100 kPa compressed to 40 cm³ → p2 = 100 × 120 ÷ 40 = **300 kPa**.
- Example: 5.0 m³ at 150 kPa is compressed until the pressure is 250 kPa → V2 = 150 × 5.0 ÷ 250
  = **3.0 m³**. Check: higher pressure, so the volume must be smaller.

## Graphs (constant temperature)

- **p against V:** smooth downward curve, steep at small V, flattening at large V, never touching
  the axes.
- **p against 1/V:** straight line through the origin.
- Check data by multiplying: equal pV values confirm the relationship.

## p1V1 = p2V2 in exam layout

*A balloon holds 3.0 litres of air at 100 kPa. It is taken under water to where the pressure is
150 kPa, at constant temperature. Find the new volume.*

```
p1 V1 = p2 V2
100 × 3.0 = 150 × V2
V2 = 300 / 150 = 2.0 litres
```

Check: pressure up, so volume down. Litres are fine because both volumes use the same unit.

## Linking to other subtopics

- **1.6 Momentum:** each collision changes a particle's momentum, which is why it exerts a force.
- **1.8 Pressure:** pressure = force ÷ area, the same definition used for gases.
- **2.2:** the kelvin scale starts at absolute zero; evaporation uses the idea that particles have
  a range of speeds.

## Exam traps

- Particles do not expand when heated; the spaces between them grow.
- Compressing a gas at constant temperature does **not** speed up its particles.
- "Per unit area" is essential in a pressure answer.
- Solid particles vibrate; they are not stationary.
- p1V1 = p2V2 only applies at constant temperature for a fixed mass of gas.
- A p–V sketch is a curve, not a straight line.
- Gas to solid and solid to gas are not required in 5054.

## Self-test

1. Give two properties that distinguish a gas from a liquid.
2. Name the change of state from gas to liquid.
3. Describe the motion of particles in a solid.
4. Why are liquids almost incompressible?
5. State the value of absolute zero in °C.
6. What happens to particles at absolute zero?
7. Explain, in terms of particles, how a gas exerts pressure.
8. Why does the pressure of a gas in a sealed rigid container rise when it is heated?
9. A gas at 200 kPa occupies 30 cm³. Find its volume at 120 kPa, at constant temperature.
10. Sketch in words the graph of p against V at constant temperature.
11. Why does compressing a gas at constant temperature increase its pressure?
12. What graph gives a straight line through the origin for a gas at constant temperature?

### Answers

1. Any two: a gas fills its container; has no fixed volume; is easily compressed; has very low
   density.
2. Condensation.
3. They vibrate about fixed positions in a regular arrangement.
4. The particles are already close together, so there is little space to reduce.
5. **−273 °C**.
6. They have the least possible kinetic energy.
7. Particles collide with the walls, exerting forces; the total force per unit area is the
   pressure.
8. Particles move faster, hitting the walls more often and harder, so force per unit area rises.
9. V2 = 200 × 30 ÷ 120 = **50 cm³**.
10. A smooth curve falling steeply at small volumes and levelling off at large volumes.
11. The same number of particles, at the same speed, hit a smaller wall area, so there are more
    collisions per unit area per second.
12. p against 1/V.
