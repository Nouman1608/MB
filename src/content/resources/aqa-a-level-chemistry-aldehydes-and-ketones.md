## The carbonyl group

Aldehydes and ketones both contain the **carbonyl group**, C=O.

- In an **aldehyde** the C=O is at the end of the chain, written –CHO. Names end in **-al**: ethanal, CH₃CHO.
- In a **ketone** the C=O is joined to two carbon atoms. Names end in **-one**: propanone, CH₃COCH₃.

Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C=O bond is polar: **Cδ+=Oδ−**. The carbon is electron
deficient. A **nucleophile** (an electron-pair donor) is attracted to it and forms a new bond. At the same time
one pair of electrons in the C=O double bond moves onto the oxygen. Overall, the nucleophile adds across the
C=O. This is **nucleophilic addition**, and every reaction in this section except oxidation works this way.

The carbon of the C=O has three bonding regions, so it is **trigonal planar**, with bond angles of about 120°.
This flat shape matters later, when you explain why some products are racemic.

## Oxidation of aldehydes

Aldehydes are **readily oxidised to carboxylic acids**. The carbon of the –CHO group carries a hydrogen atom,
and oxidation turns C–H into C–OH:

```
RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
CH₃CH₂CHO + [O] → CH₃CH₂COOH        (propanal → propanoic acid)
```

Ketones are not easily oxidised. Their C=O carbon has no hydrogen atom, and oxidising them would mean breaking a
C–C bond. Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) oxidises aldehydes too (orange to green), but it also oxidises
primary and secondary alcohols, so it is not a specific test for aldehydes. Mild oxidising agents are used
instead.

## Tests: Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution

Both reagents are mild oxidising agents. They oxidise an aldehyde but not a ketone. Warm the test tube in a hot
water bath, not over a flame, because the organic compounds are flammable.

| Test | What the reagent contains | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tollens' reagent | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, made from silver nitrate and aqueous ammonia | **Silver mirror** on the tube (silver(I) reduced to Ag) | No change |
| Fehling's solution | A blue copper(II) complex in alkali | Blue solution gives a **red precipitate** of Cu₂O (copper(I) oxide) | Stays blue |

In each positive test the aldehyde is the **reducing agent**. It is oxidised to a carboxylic acid. The reagents
are alkaline, so the acid ends up as its carboxylate ion.

Each silver(I) ion gains one electron, Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. Oxidising one aldehyde molecule to the acid releases two
electrons, so one mole of aldehyde reduces **two moles** of silver(I) ions.

**Worked example 1.** 1.16 g of propanal is warmed with excess Tollens' reagent. Calculate the maximum mass of
silver formed. (Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0, Ag = 107.9)

```
Mr(propanal, C₃H₆O) = 3(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 16.0 = 58.0
n(propanal)         = 1.16 ÷ 58.0 = 0.0200 mol
n(Ag)               = 2 × 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol
mass of Ag          = 0.0400 × 107.9 = 4.316 g
```

Answer: **4.32 g** of silver (3 significant figures).

## Reduction with NaBH₄

Sodium tetrahydridoborate(III), **NaBH₄**, in **aqueous solution** reduces carbonyl compounds:

- **aldehyde → primary alcohol**
- **ketone → secondary alcohol**

Write overall equations with **[H]** for the reductant. Two [H] add across the C=O:

```
CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH         (butanal → butan-1-ol)
CH₃COCH₂CH₃  + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃        (butanone → butan-2-ol)
```

### Mechanism: nucleophilic addition with H⁻

NaBH₄ acts as a source of the **hydride ion, H⁻**, which is the nucleophile. Show it as H⁻ with a lone pair.

**Worked example 2.** Outline the mechanism for the reduction of propanone by NaBH₄ in aqueous solution.

- Step 1: draw propanone with the C=O polarised, Cδ+ and Oδ−.
- Step 2: draw a curly arrow from the **lone pair on H⁻** to the carbonyl **carbon**.
- Step 3: in the same step, draw a curly arrow from the **C=O double bond** to the **oxygen**. The oxygen now
  carries a negative charge and three lone pairs.
- Step 4: the intermediate is (CH₃)₂CH–O⁻. Draw a curly arrow from a **lone pair on O⁻** to an H atom of a
  water molecule (or to H⁺), and an arrow from that O–H bond in water onto its oxygen.
- Answer: the product is **propan-2-ol**, (CH₃)₂CHOH.

```
        Oδ−                    O⁻                     OH
        ‖                      |                      |
CH₃ – Cδ+ – CH₃  + :H⁻  →  CH₃–C–CH₃   then +H⁺ →  CH₃–C–CH₃
                               |                      |
                               H                      H
```

**Worked example 3.** 3.60 g of butanone is reduced with NaBH₄ and 2.96 g of butan-2-ol is collected.
Calculate the percentage yield. (Mr: butanone 72.0, butan-2-ol 74.0)

```
n(butanone)       = 3.60 ÷ 72.0 = 0.0500 mol
theoretical n(alcohol) = 0.0500 mol  (1 : 1)
actual n(alcohol) = 2.96 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0400 mol
percentage yield  = 0.0400 ÷ 0.0500 × 100 = 80.0 %
```

Answer: **80.0 %**. Compare moles, not masses.

## Forming hydroxynitriles: KCN followed by dilute acid

Carbonyl compounds react with **potassium cyanide, KCN, followed by dilute acid**. The product is a
**hydroxynitrile**: an OH group and a C≡N group on the same carbon. The carbon chain grows by one carbon.

Write the **overall equation using HCN**:

```
CH₃CHO + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN              (ethanal → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile)
CH₃COCH₃ + HCN → (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN          (propanone → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile)
```

**Naming.** The nitrile carbon is carbon 1 and is included in the chain length. The OH is then on carbon 2.
From ethanal (2 carbons) the product has 3 carbons, so it is a propanenitrile.

### Mechanism: nucleophilic addition with CN⁻

KCN provides **cyanide ions, CN⁻**. The nucleophile is the **lone pair on the carbon** of CN⁻, which is why
the new bond is C–C and the product is a nitrile.

**Worked example 4.** Outline the mechanism for the reaction of ethanal with KCN followed by dilute acid.

- Step 1: curly arrow from the **lone pair on the carbon of :CN⁻** to the δ+ carbonyl carbon.
- Step 2: curly arrow from the **C=O double bond** to the oxygen, giving the intermediate
  CH₃CH(CN)–O⁻.
- Step 3: curly arrow from a **lone pair on O⁻** to **H⁺** from the dilute acid.
- Answer: **2-hydroxypropanenitrile**, CH₃CH(OH)CN.

```
       Oδ−                       O⁻                    OH
       ‖                         |                     |
CH₃ – Cδ+ – H  +  :CN⁻  →   CH₃–C–H   then +H⁺ →  CH₃–C–H
                                 |                     |
                                 CN                    CN
```

**Worked example 5.** Calculate the maximum mass of hydroxynitrile from 2.90 g of propanone.
(Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0)

```
n(propanone) = 2.90 ÷ 58.0 = 0.0500 mol
product (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN = C₄H₇NO, Mr = 48.0 + 7.0 + 14.0 + 16.0 = 85.0
maximum mass = 0.0500 × 85.0 = 4.25 g
```

Answer: **4.25 g**.

### The hazards of using KCN

- KCN is **highly toxic** if swallowed, inhaled as dust or absorbed.
- With acid, KCN releases **hydrogen cyanide, HCN**, which is highly toxic and very volatile, so it escapes
  easily as a gas.

So you wear gloves and eye protection, work in a fume cupboard, and add the acid in a controlled way. KCN is
used rather than HCN itself because HCN is toxic and volatile, so it is hard to handle safely, and because HCN is a weak
acid, so it supplies few CN⁻ ions. KCN is ionic and supplies plenty of CN⁻ nucleophile.

You met chirality in optical isomerism (3.3.7). A carbon with four different groups is a chiral centre, and its
two non-superimposable mirror images are **enantiomers**. A 50 : 50 mixture is a **racemic mixture (racemate)**
and is optically inactive.

**The explanation for KCN followed by dilute acid, in steps:**

1. The C=O carbon and the three atoms bonded to it lie in one plane (trigonal planar).
2. CN⁻ can attack the carbon **from either side** of this plane.
3. Attack from each side is **equally likely**.
4. If the new carbon has four different groups, the two directions of attack give the two **enantiomers**.
5. They form in **equal amounts**, so the product is a **racemic mixture**. The rotations of plane-polarised
   light cancel, so the mixture is **optically inactive**.

**When does a chiral centre form?** Look at the two groups already on the C=O carbon. The product carbon carries
those two groups, plus OH and CN.

| Carbonyl compound | Groups on C=O carbon | Product chiral? |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanal (aldehyde) | CH₃ and H | Yes: CH₃, H, OH, CN are all different, so racemic |
| Butanone (unsymmetrical ketone) | CH₃ and C₂H₅ | Yes: racemic |
| Propanone (symmetrical ketone) | CH₃ and CH₃ | No: two identical groups |
| Methanal | H and H | No |

This is why the specification says **aldehydes and unsymmetrical ketones** form mixtures of enantiomers.
Methanal is the one aldehyde that does not, because its carbonyl carbon carries two H atoms.

## Common errors

- Drawing the CN⁻ arrow from the nitrogen. It starts at the lone pair on the carbon.
- Starting the H⁻ arrow at a B–H bond of BH₄⁻. The specification asks for H⁻ as the nucleophile.
- Forgetting the second arrow, from C=O to O. Without it the carbon would have five bonds.
- Writing reduction equations with one [H] instead of two.
- Naming CH₃CH(OH)CN as "2-hydroxyethanenitrile". Count the nitrile carbon: it is a propanenitrile.
- Saying a racemic product forms "because the product is chiral". The reason is equal attack from both sides of
  a planar C=O group.
- Saying propanone gives a racemic mixture. Its product has two identical CH₃ groups, so no chiral centre.
- Giving "toxic" for KCN without saying that acid releases HCN gas.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a nucleophile attack the carbon of a carbonyl group?**
Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C=O bond is polar, Cδ+=Oδ−, and the carbon is electron deficient. A nucleophile donates an electron pair to this carbon while a pair of electrons from the double bond moves onto the oxygen.

**What is the difference between an aldehyde and a ketone?**
In an aldehyde the C=O group is at the end of the chain (–CHO) and the name ends in -al; in a ketone the C=O is joined to two carbon atoms and the name ends in -one. Aldehydes are readily oxidised to carboxylic acids, while ketones are not easily oxidised.

**Why is acidified potassium dichromate not used to test for aldehydes?**
Acidified dichromate oxidises aldehydes, but it also oxidises primary and secondary alcohols, so a colour change does not prove an aldehyde is present. Mild oxidising agents, Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution, are used instead.

**How do you name the product of a carbonyl compound with KCN and dilute acid?**
The product is a hydroxynitrile, and the nitrile carbon counts as carbon 1 in the chain. For example, the product from ethanal, CH₃CH(OH)CN, has three carbons, so it is named as a propanenitrile.

**Is there a required practical for aldehydes and ketones in AQA A-level Chemistry 7405?**
No. Section 3.3.8 is A-level only and lists no required practical of its own. It is examined on A-level Paper 2, and Paper 3 can test any content and practical skill.
