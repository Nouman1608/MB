## Why the role matters in economics

Every economy is in practice a **mixed economy**: markets decide much of what is produced, but government also acts. The economic case for government action comes from the problems markets leave unsolved:

- public goods would not be supplied;
- merit goods would be under-consumed and demerit goods over-consumed;
- external costs and benefits would be ignored;
- monopolies could abuse their power;
- incomes could be very unequal;
- unemployment, inflation and slow growth could persist.

## Six economic roles of government

| Role | What it means | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Provider | Supplies goods and services directly or pays for them | Defence, police, roads, state schools, hospitals |
| Regulator | Makes and enforces rules for firms, workers and consumers | Safety standards, pollution limits, competition rules, minimum wage |
| Redistributor | Moves income from richer to poorer households | Progressive taxes, pensions, unemployment benefits |
| Manager of the economy | Uses policy to pursue the macroeconomic aims | Fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy |
| Employer and producer | Employs workers and may own firms | Teachers, nurses, civil servants; state-owned rail or power companies |
| International partner | Acts with other countries | Trade agreements, aid, shared action on cross-border problems |

A good answer names the role and then links it to the problem it solves. For example: "The government provides street lighting (provider) because it is a public good that firms cannot charge for."

## Local government

Local government (for example a city, town or district council) works close to the people it serves.

Typical roles:

- **Provider:** refuse collection, local roads, street lighting, parks, libraries, sometimes schools and public transport.
- **Regulator:** planning permission for buildings, licensing of shops and restaurants, local environmental health checks.
- **Employer:** local services employ many people, from teachers to street cleaners.

**Why local?** Local officials know local needs and conditions, and residents can see the results and hold them to account.

**Worked example 1 (invented).** The council of Town R spends 48 million a year.

| Service | Spending (million) | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Schools | 19.2 | 40% |
| Social care | 12.0 | 25% |
| Roads and transport | 7.2 | 15% |
| Waste collection | 4.8 | 10% |
| Parks and libraries | 4.8 | 10% |
| **Total** | **48.0** | **100%** |

Local taxes raise 30 million; the rest, 18 million (37.5% of spending), is a grant from national government. Local government often depends on the national level for funds, which limits what it can do on its own.

## National government

National government acts for the whole country and has powers no local body has.

- **Manager of the economy:** sets taxes and national spending (fiscal policy), oversees the central bank's monetary policy and uses supply-side policy to pursue growth, low unemployment, stable prices, balance of payments stability and fairer income distribution.
- **Provider:** defence, the courts, national roads, funding for health and education.
- **Regulator:** national laws on employment, consumer protection, competition and the environment.
- **Redistributor:** income tax systems and welfare benefits.
- **Employer and producer:** often the largest employer in the country; may own key industries.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** In Country N, government spending is 210 billion and GDP is 600 billion.

```
Government spending as a share of GDP = 210 / 600 x 100 = 35%
```

If the government employs 4.5 million of a workforce of 30 million, it employs 4.5 / 30 x 100 = 15% of all workers. Figures like these show how large the government's role is in a mixed economy.

## International role

Some problems and opportunities cross borders, so no single government can deal with them alone.

- **Trade:** negotiating trade agreements, setting tariffs and quotas, and joining trade organisations.
- **Cross-border external costs:** pollution of shared rivers, seas and air; overfishing in shared waters.
- **Aid:** giving or receiving financial and technical help for development.
- **Cooperation:** working with other governments on disease control, financial stability and security.

**Worked example 3 (invented).** Three countries share a fishing ground. Fish stocks can sustain a catch of 90,000 tonnes a year. Each country's fleet wants 45,000 tonnes.

```
Total wanted = 3 x 45,000 = 135,000 tonnes
Over-fishing = 135,000 - 90,000 = 45,000 tonnes, or 50% above the sustainable catch
```

If one country cuts its catch alone, the others may simply catch more, so the fish stock still falls. An international agreement giving each country a quota of 30,000 tonnes protects the stock for all. This is the kind of reasoning that explains why the international level is needed.

## Deciding which level should act

Use three questions:

1. **How far does the problem spread?** A single town, the whole country, or across borders?
2. **Who has the powers and funds?** Only national government sets national taxes and interest rate policy.
3. **Who has the best knowledge?** Local bodies understand local needs.

| Problem (invented) | Best level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Potholes on town streets | Local | Local knowledge; the problem is confined to one area |
| Rising national unemployment | National | Needs national fiscal and monetary policy |
| Smoke from factories drifting into a neighbouring country | International | Damage crosses borders |
| A shortage of school places in one district | Local, with national funding | Local planning, but national grants may be needed |
| High inflation | National | Interest rates and national taxes are national tools |

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Explain why a national government, rather than local government, provides defence. [4]

> Defence is a public good: it protects everyone in the country, and people who do not pay cannot be excluded [identifies the economic reason for government provision]. A threat to the country affects every region, so it is a national problem [scale of the problem]. Defence needs very large spending on equipment and forces, which only national taxation can fund [powers and funds]. If each local council organised its own defence, there would be duplication and gaps between areas [why the local level would fail].

## Common mistakes

- Listing services without explaining the economic reason for government involvement.
- Ignoring the international level, or describing it only as "foreign policy".
- Assuming local government can act independently; it often depends on national funding and laws.
- Confusing the government's role as a provider with its role as a producer: a government can pay for a service that private firms supply.
- Using real statistics you are unsure of. Invented or general examples earn the same credit if the reasoning is sound.
