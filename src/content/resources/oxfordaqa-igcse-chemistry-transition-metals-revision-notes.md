## The two statements in one line each

- **3.7.2 a:** transition metals sit in the centre of the periodic table, between Groups 2 and 3; many have ions with different charges, form coloured compounds and are useful as catalysts.
- **3.7.2 b:** compared with Group 1 they have higher melting points (except mercury) and higher densities, are stronger and harder, and are much less reactive, so they do not react as vigorously with water or oxygen.

## Key words

- **Transition metal:** an element in the middle block of the periodic table, between Groups 2 and 3.
- **Ion:** a charged particle. Metals form positive ions by losing electrons.
- **Roman numeral in a name:** the charge on the metal ion. Copper(I) = Cu⁺; vanadium(V) = V⁵⁺.
- **Catalyst:** alters the rate of a reaction; the same amount is left at the end.
- **Density:** mass per unit volume, in g/cm³.
- **Strong:** hard to break. **Hard:** hard to scratch or dent.

## Group 1 against transition metals

| Property | Group 1 metals | Transition metals |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | low | high (mercury is the exception) |
| Density | low; the first three float on water | high |
| Hardness | soft enough to slice with a knife | hard |
| Strength | weak | strong |
| With water | react with cold water, release hydrogen, form an alkali | no reaction (copper) or very slow (iron); hot iron reacts with steam |
| With oxygen | tarnish within seconds; stored under oil | slow, or only on heating |
| Ions | always 1+ | many form ions with different charges |
| Compounds | white; colourless solutions | many coloured |

### The mercury exception

Mercury is liquid at room temperature. It melts at −39 °C, lower than any Group 1 metal. Its density, 13.5 g/cm³, is still far higher than any Group 1 metal's. **The exception is for melting point only.**

### Method: a "compare" answer

1. Take one property at a time.
2. Say which metal is higher, harder or more reactive, using a comparative word.
3. If data are given, quote both values or work out a difference or a ratio.
4. Move on. Never make the same point twice in different words.

**Small reminder.** Silver (10.5 g/cm³) against rubidium (1.53 g/cm³): 10.5 ÷ 1.53 = 6.9, so silver is about seven times as dense.

## Ions with different charges

| Element | Ions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| iron | Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ | iron(III) nitrate Fe(NO₃)₃ |
| copper | Cu⁺, Cu²⁺ | copper(I) sulfide Cu₂S |
| cobalt | Co²⁺, Co³⁺ | cobalt(III) fluoride CoF₃ |
| manganese | Mn²⁺ and others | manganese(II) sulfate MnSO₄ |
| titanium | Ti⁴⁺ and others | titanium(IV) oxide TiO₂ |
| vanadium | V⁵⁺ and others | vanadium(V) oxide V₂O₅ |

**Method: charge from a formula**

1. Add up the negative charge (O²⁻, S²⁻, SO₄²⁻, F⁻, Br⁻, NO₃⁻).
2. Divide by the number of metal ions.
3. The answer is the charge on each metal ion, and the Roman numeral in the name.

Worked reminder: V₂O₅ has 5 × 2− = 10− in total. Two vanadium ions share 10+, so each is V⁵⁺: vanadium(V) oxide.

**Method: formula from a name**

1. Read the metal ion's charge from the numeral.
2. Choose enough negative ions to balance it.
3. Use brackets for a group ion that appears more than once: Fe(NO₃)₃.

## Coloured compounds

| Compound or ion | Colour |
|---|---|
| copper(II) sulfate, hydrated or in solution | blue |
| anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | white |
| iron(III) oxide | red-brown |
| hydrated cobalt(II) chloride | pink |
| potassium manganate(VII) | purple |
| zinc compounds | white |

Zinc and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate are the reason the specification says **many**, not all.

### Sodium hydroxide test (3.4.3 c)

| Ion | Precipitate | Ionic equation |
|---|---|---|
| Cu²⁺ | blue | Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂ |
| Fe²⁺ | green | Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂ |
| Fe³⁺ | brown | Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃ |

The number of hydroxide ions equals the charge on the metal ion. Iron(II) and iron(III) are the same element but give different colours.

## Catalysts

| Process | Catalyst | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Haber process (3.8.3 c) | iron | nitrogen + hydrogen ⇌ ammonia, about 450 °C, about 200 atmospheres |
| Contact process, stage 2 (3.8.3 d) | vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅ | 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, about 450 °C, atmospheric pressure |

Why industry uses catalysts: faster reactions give more product in a given time and reduce costs (3.8.1 h). The catalyst is not used up, so it can be used again and again.

How to spot a catalyst from an experiment: with it, the reaction goes faster, and at the end you recover the same mass of it (or see the same colour of solution).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Less reactive** (transition) vs **more reactive** (Group 1). Not "unreactive": iron rusts.
- **Denser** vs **heavier**. Density compares equal volumes.
- **Strong** vs **hard**. Name both when asked about strength and hardness.
- **Iron(II)** Fe²⁺, green precipitate, vs **iron(III)** Fe³⁺, brown precipitate.
- **Catalyst** (not used up) vs **reactant** (used up).
- **Aluminium** (Group 3, white precipitate that dissolves in excess) vs a **transition metal** ion (coloured precipitate).

## Property to use

Exam questions may name a use and ask for the property behind it. Name the property, then say why a Group 1 metal would fail.

| Use | Property | Why not a Group 1 metal |
|---|---|---|
| titanium in aircraft frames | strong; high melting point | soft and weak; melts easily |
| tungsten lamp filaments | very high melting point | would melt at a low temperature |
| copper hot-water pipes | does not react with water | reacts with cold water, releasing hydrogen |
| iron catalyst in ammonia production | catalyst; solid at about 450 °C | would be liquid, and is far too reactive |
| coloured pottery glazes | coloured compounds | compounds are white |

**Small reminder (catalyst data).** A transition metal powder doubles the volume of gas collected in a fixed time, and the same mass is recovered at the end. It is a catalyst: faster **and** not used up. If the mass fell, it was being used up as a reactant.

## Quick self-test

1. Between which two groups are the transition metals found?
2. State the three typical properties that many transition metals have.
3. Give the charge on the metal ion in TiO₂.
4. Name the compound CuBr.
5. Write the formula of manganese(II) sulfate.
6. Which transition metal has a lower melting point than any Group 1 metal?
7. Tungsten melts at 3422 °C and rubidium at 39 °C. Calculate the difference.
8. Why does an iron nail stay shiny for days in dry air when a freshly cut piece of sodium does not?
9. A solution gives a green precipitate with sodium hydroxide solution. Name the ion.
10. Name the catalyst in stage 2 of the contact process.
11. Give one reason why industrial processes use catalysts.
12. True or false: every compound of a transition metal is coloured.

### Answers

1. Group 2 and Group 3.
2. Ions with different charges; coloured compounds; useful as catalysts.
3. Two O²⁻ give 4−, so the ion is **Ti⁴⁺**.
4. Br⁻ is 1−, so copper is Cu⁺: **copper(I) bromide**.
5. Mn²⁺ and SO₄²⁻: **MnSO₄**.
6. **Mercury** (melting point −39 °C).
7. 3422 − 39 = **3383 °C**.
8. Iron is much less reactive than sodium, so it reacts with oxygen much more slowly.
9. **Iron(II)**, Fe²⁺.
10. **Vanadium(V) oxide**, V₂O₅.
11. It speeds up the reaction, so more product is made in a given time and costs are reduced.
12. **False.** Many are coloured, but zinc compounds and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate are white.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that transition metals are more reactive than Group 1, or that they are unreactive.
- Using "all" instead of "many" for the three typical properties.
- Forgetting mercury when asked about melting points, or applying the exception to density.
- Treating the Roman numeral as a number of atoms.
- Writing the wrong number of hydroxide ions in an ionic equation, for example Fe³⁺ + 2OH⁻.
- Swapping the iron(II) and iron(III) precipitate colours.
- Calling aluminium a transition metal because it forms a precipitate.
- Giving a fact about one metal only when the question says "compare".
- Saying a catalyst is used up, or that it changes the amount of product rather than the rate.
