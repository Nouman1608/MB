This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 6.1, Structure of nucleic acids and replication of DNA, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content. The five learning outcomes cover the structure of nucleotides including ATP, purines and pyrimidines, the DNA double helix (complementary base pairing, antiparallel strands, hydrogen bonding and phosphodiester bonds), semi-conservative replication in S phase with DNA polymerase, DNA ligase and the leading and lagging strands, and the structure of mRNA. Structural formulae are not expected.

## 6.1.1 Three nucleotides compared

Every nucleotide has a pentose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. They differ in the sugar, the base and the number of phosphates.

| Feature | DNA nucleotide | RNA nucleotide | ATP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose | Ribose |
| Base | A, T, C or G | A, U, C or G | Adenine only |
| Phosphate groups | One (in the chain) | One (in the chain) | Three |
| Main role | Stores genetic information | Carries a copy of genetic information (mRNA) | Energy currency of the cell |

ATP is a phosphorylated nucleotide: an RNA-type nucleotide containing adenine, with two extra phosphate groups.

## 6.1.2 Purines and pyrimidines

| Group | Ring structure | Bases |
|---|---|---|
| Purines | Double ring (larger) | Adenine, guanine |
| Pyrimidines | Single ring (smaller) | Cytosine, thymine, uracil |

Every base pair in DNA joins one purine to one pyrimidine. Two purines would be too wide and two pyrimidines too narrow, so purine–pyrimidine pairing keeps the double helix the same width along its whole length.

## 6.1.3 The double helix

- Nucleotides in each strand are joined by **phosphodiester bonds** between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next, formed by condensation. This gives a strong sugar–phosphate backbone.
- The two strands are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between complementary bases: **A–T with two hydrogen bonds**, **C–G with three**.
- The strands are **antiparallel**: one runs 5′ to 3′ and the other 3′ to 5′.

### Worked example 1: writing the complementary strand

Strand 1: 5′ – G C T T A G C C A T – 3′

1. Pair each base: G–C, C–G, T–A, A–T.
2. Complementary strand, read in the same left-to-right order: 3′ – C G A A T C G G T A – 5′.
3. Sequences are conventionally written 5′ to 3′, so reversed: **5′ – A T G G C T A A G C – 3′**.

The 5′ and 3′ labels must be on opposite ends of the two strands. Forgetting to reverse, or to label, is a common loss of marks.

### Worked example 2: base proportions

A sample of double-stranded DNA contains 28% guanine.

```
C pairs with G, so C = 28%
G + C = 56%
A + T = 100 - 56 = 44%, so A = T = 22%
```

If a sample has A = 30% but T = 18%, the DNA cannot be double-stranded, because in a double helix every A is paired with a T.

### Worked example 3: hydrogen bonds and strand separation

Two 30-base-pair sections of DNA differ in their C–G content.

```
Section X: 40% C-G = 12 C-G pairs, 18 A-T pairs
  12 x 3 + 18 x 2 = 36 + 36 = 72 hydrogen bonds
Section Y: 60% C-G = 18 C-G pairs, 12 A-T pairs
  18 x 3 + 12 x 2 = 54 + 24 = 78 hydrogen bonds
```

Section Y has more hydrogen bonds, so more energy is needed to separate its strands. Hydrogen bonds are individually weak, which allows strands to separate for replication, but there are so many that the molecule is stable.

## 6.1.4 Semi-conservative replication

Replication happens in the **S phase** of interphase.

1. Hydrogen bonds between bases are broken and the two strands separate. Both act as templates.
2. Free DNA nucleotides pair with exposed bases by complementary base pairing.
3. **DNA polymerase** joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand by catalysing the formation of phosphodiester bonds.
4. **DNA ligase** joins separate sections of new DNA into a continuous strand, again forming phosphodiester bonds.
5. Each new DNA molecule contains **one original strand and one new strand**: semi-conservative.

### Why there is a lagging strand

DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the **3′ end** of a growing strand, so new DNA is always made in the **5′ to 3′ direction**. The two template strands are antiparallel, so:

- on one template, building 5′ to 3′ means moving **towards** the opening replication fork: the **leading strand** is made continuously;
- on the other template, building 5′ to 3′ means moving **away** from the fork: the **lagging strand** is made in short fragments, each started as more template is exposed, and **DNA ligase** joins the fragments.

The lagging strand is a direct consequence of two facts: the strands are antiparallel and DNA polymerase works only 5′ to 3′. An answer needs both.

### Evidence: the density experiment

In 1958 Meselson and Stahl grew bacteria for many generations in a medium containing a heavy isotope of nitrogen, ¹⁵N, so all their DNA was "heavy". They then moved the bacteria to a medium with the normal, lighter ¹⁴N and extracted DNA after each generation. Centrifugation separates DNA by density.

| Generations in ¹⁴N | Observed DNA bands | Semi-conservative prediction |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | All heavy | All heavy |
| 1 | All intermediate (hybrid) | Each molecule one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand |
| 2 | Half intermediate, half light | Half hybrid, half light |

**Worked example 4: predicting later generations.** One original molecule has two heavy strands. After *n* generations there are 2ⁿ molecules, and exactly two of them still contain a heavy strand.

```
Generation 3: 2^3 = 8 molecules; 2 hybrid, 6 light -> 25% hybrid, 75% light
Generation 4: 16 molecules; 2 hybrid, 14 light -> 12.5% hybrid
```

If replication were conservative, generation 1 would show one heavy band and one light band, with no intermediate band. The single intermediate band after one generation rules this out.

## 6.1.5 Structure of mRNA

- A single polynucleotide strand, not a double helix.
- Sugar is ribose; uracil replaces thymine.
- Its sequence is complementary to the template strand of one gene.
- Much shorter than a DNA molecule, and it is read in triplets called codons.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Explain why one new strand is made continuously and the other in fragments during DNA replication. **[4]**

> The two strands of DNA are antiparallel, running in opposite directions. *(First fact.)*
>
> DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the 3′ end of a growing strand, so it builds new DNA only in the 5′ to 3′ direction. *(Second fact.)*
>
> On one template this direction follows the replication fork, so the leading strand is made continuously. *(Leading strand.)*
>
> On the other template, polymerase must work away from the fork, so the lagging strand is made in short sections that DNA ligase joins together. *(Lagging strand and the role of ligase.)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying A–T has three hydrogen bonds; C–G has three.
- Writing a complementary strand without 5′ and 3′ labels, or not reversing it.
- Saying DNA polymerase breaks hydrogen bonds or forms them; it forms phosphodiester bonds.
- Saying DNA ligase joins bases; it joins sugar–phosphate backbones of fragments.
- Describing replication as producing "one old molecule and one new molecule".
- Giving ATP deoxyribose or one phosphate.
