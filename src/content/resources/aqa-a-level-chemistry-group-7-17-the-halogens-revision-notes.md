## Trends down Group 7 (3.2.3.1)

| Property | Trend down the group | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | decreases | bigger atoms, more shielding, bonding pair further from nucleus |
| Boiling point | increases | more electrons per molecule, stronger van der Waals forces |
| Oxidising ability of X₂ | decreases | incoming electron further from nucleus, more shielding |
| Reducing ability of X⁻ | increases | outer electrons further from nucleus, more shielding, lost more easily |
| Solubility of AgX in NH₃ | decreases | (describe only) |

**Definition:** electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent
bond.

**Boiling point answer in steps:**

1. Simple molecular, diatomic molecules.
2. More electrons per molecule down the group (Cl₂ 34, Br₂ 70, I₂ 106).
3. Stronger van der Waals forces between molecules.
4. More energy needed to overcome them.

Never say the X–X bond breaks on boiling.

## Displacement reactions

A halogen displaces any halogen **below** it from a solution of its halide ions.

| Mixture | Result | Ionic equation |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ + Br⁻ | yellow/orange solution | Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ |
| Cl₂ + I⁻ | brown solution | Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂ |
| Br₂ + I⁻ | brown solution | Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ |
| Br₂ + Cl⁻, I₂ + Cl⁻, I₂ + Br⁻ | no reaction | -- |

Halogen = oxidising agent (gains electrons). Halide ion = reducing agent (loses electrons).

## Sodium halides with concentrated sulfuric acid

Step 1 for every halide (acid–base, not redox):

```
NaX + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HX       (steamy white fumes of HX)
```

| Halide | Further reaction | Extra observations | S product |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻, Cl⁻ | none | -- | none (+6) |
| Br⁻ | 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | orange-brown fumes | SO₂ (+4) |
| I⁻ | 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | black solid / purple vapour | SO₂ (+4) |
| I⁻ | 6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O | yellow solid | S (0) |
| I⁻ | 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O | bad-egg smell | H₂S (−2) |

**Sulfuric acid half-equations (reductions):**

```
H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O
H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O
H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O
```

The lower the oxidation state of sulfur in the product, the stronger the halide's reducing power.

## Silver nitrate test

**Method in steps:**

1. Add dilute nitric acid to the halide solution.
2. Add silver nitrate solution. Note the precipitate colour.
3. Add dilute ammonia; if the precipitate stays, try concentrated ammonia.

| Ion | AgX colour | Dilute NH₃ | Conc. NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻ | no precipitate | -- | -- |
| Cl⁻ | white | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br⁻ | cream | no | dissolves |
| I⁻ | yellow | no | no |

**Three "why" answers the specification names:**

- **Silver nitrate:** forms precipitates of different colours with Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻.
- **Acidified (with HNO₃):** removes carbonate and hydroxide ions, which would also give precipitates.
  Not HCl (adds Cl⁻).
- **Ammonia:** the colours are similar, so solubility in ammonia separates them.

```
Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
AgCl(s) + 2NH₃(aq) → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
```

## Uses of chlorine and chlorate(I) (3.2.3.2)

| Reaction | Equation | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ + water | Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO (or 2H⁺ + Cl⁻ + ClO⁻) | disproportionation, reversible |
| Cl₂ + water in sunlight | 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂ | chloride ions and oxygen |
| Cl₂ + cold, dilute NaOH | Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O | disproportionation; NaClO solution is bleach |

**Oxidation states of chlorine:** Cl₂ 0, Cl⁻ −1, ClO⁻ +1.

**Water treatment:** chlorine kills bacteria. It is toxic and can form chlorinated organic compounds, but
the doses are controlled and the health benefits outweigh the toxic effects. Society weighs advantages
against disadvantages before adding chemicals (for example sodium fluoride) to water supplies.

## Small worked reminders

**Reminder 1: tracking sulfur with sodium bromide.** In 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O, bromine goes from
−1 to 0 (oxidised) and sulfur goes from +6 to +4 (reduced). Two bromide ions each lose one electron; one
sulfur atom gains two. Electrons lost equal electrons gained, so the equation is consistent.

**Reminder 2: building an iodide equation from half-equations.** To get iodide reducing H₂SO₄ to SO₂:

```
H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O
2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
Add:  H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2I⁻ → SO₂ + I₂ + 2H₂O
```

Electrons cancel because both half-equations already involve two. For S (6e⁻) multiply the iodide
half-equation by 3; for H₂S (8e⁻) multiply by 4.

**Reminder 3: a silver halide calculation.** Excess acidified AgNO₃ gives 1 mol AgX for every 1 mol X⁻.
So mass of precipitate ÷ Mr(AgX) = moles of halide ion in the sample. Divide by the volume in dm³ for a
concentration. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.

**Reminder 4: why chlorine water is a disproportionation.** One Cl atom in each Cl₂ goes up to +1 (in ClO⁻
or HClO) and the other goes down to −1 (in Cl⁻ or HCl). The same element is oxidised and reduced at once.

**Reminder 5: order of tests in Required practical 4.** If a sample may contain carbonate as well as a
halide, test for carbonate first with dilute acid, then use nitric acid before the silver nitrate. Use
acidified barium chloride for sulfate so that carbonate does not also give a white precipitate.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Oxidising ability (halogens)** decreases down; **reducing ability (halides)** increases down.
- **Acid–base** step (HX made) vs **redox** step (H₂SO₄ reduced).
- **Dilute** vs **concentrated** ammonia: only AgCl dissolves in dilute.
- **Chlorine water** (equilibrium) vs **sunlight** (oxygen released).

## Quick self-test

1. Define electronegativity.
2. State and explain the trend in electronegativity down Group 7.
3. Which halogen is a liquid at room temperature?
4. Predict the result of adding chlorine water to sodium iodide solution and give the ionic equation.
5. Why does iodine not react with potassium bromide solution?
6. Give the observation when concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium chloride.
7. What is the oxidation state of sulfur in H₂S, and which halide produces it with concentrated H₂SO₄?
8. A solution gives a cream precipitate with acidified silver nitrate. Which ion is present, and what happens with dilute ammonia?
9. Why is hydrochloric acid not used to acidify silver nitrate?
10. Give the oxidation states of chlorine in Cl₂, Cl⁻ and ClO⁻, and name this type of reaction.
11. Write the equation for chlorine with cold, dilute sodium hydroxide.
12. State one use of the solution formed in question 11.

### Answers

1. The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
2. Decreases; atoms get bigger with more shielding, so the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and
   less strongly attracted.
3. Bromine.
4. Solution turns brown; Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
5. Iodine is a weaker oxidising agent than bromine, so it cannot oxidise Br⁻.
6. Steamy white fumes (HCl).
7. −2; iodide (sodium iodide).
8. Bromide; the precipitate does not dissolve in dilute ammonia (it dissolves in concentrated).
9. It adds chloride ions, which give a white AgCl precipitate.
10. 0, −1, +1; disproportionation.
11. Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O.
12. Bleach (also disinfecting or cleaning).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining boiling points by covalent bonds breaking.
- Writing "Group 7 gets less reactive because it has more shells" without linking to attraction for an
  electron.
- Mixing up the two trends: halogens get weaker as oxidising agents, halides stronger as reducing agents.
- Leaving out the acid–base step for NaCl, or calling it redox.
- Giving H₂S as a product with bromide.
- Unbalanced half-equations for sulfuric acid; check charge as well as atoms.
- Writing "AgBr is insoluble in ammonia" without saying dilute.
- Forgetting to name disproportionation for chlorine with water or NaOH.
- Saying chlorine is added to water to "clean" it rather than to kill bacteria.
