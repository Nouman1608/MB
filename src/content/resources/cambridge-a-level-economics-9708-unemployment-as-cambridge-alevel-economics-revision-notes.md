## Meaning (4.5.1)

- **Unemployed:** of working age, without a job, available for work and actively seeking it.
- **Labour force** = employed + unemployed.
- **Economically inactive:** not working and not seeking work (students, carers, retired, long-term sick).

```
Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force x 100
```

**Quick check:** labour force 12.5 million, unemployed 0.75 million -> rate = 6.0%.

A rate can rise while employment rises, if the labour force grows faster than jobs.

**Worked recall (invented).** Labour force 20 million, unemployed 1.0 million: rate 5.0%. Then 0.3 million unemployed people give up looking. Unemployed 0.7 million, labour force 19.7 million: rate 0.7 / 19.7 x 100 = 3.6%. The rate fell by 1.4 percentage points, yet no one found a job. Always ask whether the labour force changed.

## Measures (4.5.2)

| Labour force survey | Claimant count |
|---|---|
| Household sample using the ILO definition | Count of people claiming unemployment-related benefit |
| Internationally comparable | Quick and cheap |
| Includes people not entitled to benefit | Changes when benefit rules change |
| Sampling error; costly | Misses jobless people who do not claim |

## Difficulties in measurement

- **Discouraged workers** leave the labour force: unemployment understated.
- **Underemployment:** part-timers wanting full-time work count as employed.
- **Informal sector:** unregistered work is hard to record.
- **Hidden unemployment:** people on schemes or sickness benefits who would work.
- **Benefit fraud or undeclared work** can overstate the claimant count.
- **National averages** hide regional and age differences.

## Types (4.5.3)

| Type | Cause | Clue in data |
|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Time to move between jobs; imperfect information | Short spells; vacancies and job-seekers coexist |
| Structural | Decline of an industry; skill or location mismatch | Long spells; concentrated in one region or industry |
| Cyclical | Fall in aggregate demand | Rising everywhere as real GDP falls; few vacancies |
| Seasonal | Labour demand varies with the season | Regular yearly pattern |
| Technological | Machines or software replace workers | Losses in occupations being automated |

Technological unemployment becomes structural when displaced workers lack the skills for new jobs.

**Diagnosis drill.** Match the evidence to the type before you write.

- "Graduates take two to three months to find their first job" -> frictional.
- "Unemployment in the mining region has been high for six years" -> structural.
- "Joblessness rose in every sector as real GDP fell for two quarters" -> cyclical.
- "Fruit pickers are out of work each winter" -> seasonal.
- "Banks closed branches after customers moved to app-based banking" -> technological.

## Consequences (4.5.4)

- **Individuals:** lost income, loss of skills, poorer health, stigma for long gaps.
- **Firms:** weaker demand; easier recruitment and less wage pressure.
- **Government:** higher benefit spending, lower tax revenue, wider budget deficit.
- **Economy:** output below potential (inside the PPC), wasted resources, inequality, social problems.

**Evaluation:** cost depends on **type**, **duration**, **who** is affected and the **benefit system**. Short frictional spells may even improve job matching.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour force | People of working age who are employed or unemployed |
| Unemployment rate | Unemployed as a percentage of the labour force |
| Claimant count | Number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits |
| Labour force survey | Household survey measuring unemployment using the ILO definition |
| Discouraged worker | Someone who wants work but has stopped looking |
| Underemployment | Working fewer hours, or in a lower-skilled job, than the person wants |
| Frictional unemployment | Unemployment while moving between jobs |
| Structural unemployment | Unemployment from a long-term mismatch after industrial change |
| Cyclical unemployment | Unemployment caused by a fall in aggregate demand |

## Exam traps

- Dividing by total population instead of the labour force.
- Counting students or retired people as unemployed.
- Taking a fall in the claimant count as evidence of more jobs.
- Naming a type without its cause.
- Calling technological unemployment "seasonal" because both can be sudden.
- Listing consequences without saying who bears them or how long they last.

## Self-test

1. Define unemployment.
2. Who makes up the labour force?
3. Labour force 25 million; unemployed 1.5 million. Calculate the unemployment rate.
4. Why might the claimant count be lower than the labour force survey figure?
5. How do discouraged workers affect measured unemployment?
6. Unemployment among ski instructors rises every summer. Which type?
7. A region's shipyards close and workers remain jobless for two years. Which type?
8. Unemployment rises across all regions as real GDP falls. Which type?
9. Give one cost of unemployment to the government.
10. Give one way unemployment might benefit firms.
11. Why does the duration of unemployment matter?
12. How can the unemployment rate rise while the number employed also rises?

**Answers**

1. Being of working age, without a job, available for work and actively seeking work.
2. The employed and the unemployed.
3. 1.5 / 25 x 100 = 6.0%.
4. Many jobless people are not eligible for or do not claim benefit.
5. They stop looking, are counted as inactive, so measured unemployment understates the true level.
6. Seasonal.
7. Structural.
8. Cyclical.
9. Higher spending on benefits or lower tax revenue (a wider budget deficit).
10. A larger pool of applicants and less pressure to raise wages.
11. Long spells erode skills and confidence, making it harder to return to work and wasting output for longer.
12. If the labour force grows faster than the number of jobs, unemployment rises as a share of the labour force.
