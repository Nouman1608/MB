Data for this set: Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Ca = 40.1, Fe = 55.8. Specific heat capacity of water and dilute solutions = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Density of dilute solutions = 1.00 g cm⁻³.

## Questions

**1.** This question is about standard enthalpy changes.

**(a)** State the standard conditions used for standard enthalpy changes. **[2]**
**(b)** State the standard enthalpy change of formation of oxygen gas, O₂(g). **[1]**

**2.** Write equations, with state symbols, for the reactions whose enthalpy changes are:

**(a)** the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of iodine **[1]**
**(b)** the standard enthalpy change of combustion of but-1-ene, C₄H₈(g) **[1]**
**(c)** the standard enthalpy change of formation of sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃(s). **[1]**

**3.** Potassium nitrate dissolves in water: KNO₃(s) + aq → K⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq), ΔH = +35 kJ mol⁻¹. Describe the enthalpy level diagram for this change. **[3]**

**4.** 40.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution is placed in a polystyrene cup. Its temperature is recorded each minute. At 3.0 minutes, 2.0 g of iron powder is added and the mixture is stirred.

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

| Time / min | 0.0 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 4.0 | 5.0 | 6.0 | 7.0 | 8.0 | 9.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 20.5 | 20.5 | 20.5 | 34.6 | 37.0 | 36.6 | 36.2 | 35.8 | 35.4 |

**(a)** The readings from 5.0 to 9.0 minutes lie on a straight line. Use them to find the corrected temperature at 3.0 minutes, and hence the corrected temperature change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy transferred to the solution, in J. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the enthalpy change of the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**
**(d)** Show that the iron is in excess. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why the corrected temperature change is used rather than the highest reading. **[1]**

**5.** A spirit burner containing pentan-1-ol (Mr = 88.0) heats 250 g of water in a copper can. The burner loses 0.660 g and the water temperature rises by 20.0 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of pentan-1-ol. **[3]**
**(b)** Use the value −3330 kJ mol⁻¹ as the accepted standard enthalpy change of combustion. Give **two** reasons why your answer is less exothermic. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** change to the apparatus that would improve the result. **[1]**

**6.** Calculate ΔrH for Fe₂O₃(s) + 3C(s) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO(g). Use these values of ΔfH (kJ mol⁻¹): Fe₂O₃(s) −824, CO(g) −111. **[3]**

**7.** Calculate the standard enthalpy change of formation of benzene, C₆H₆(l), using these standard enthalpy changes of combustion (kJ mol⁻¹): H₂(g) −286, C₆H₆(l) −3268, C(s) −394. **[4]**

**8.** A student uses Core Practical 2 to find ΔH for CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). In each run, a weighed solid is tipped into a polystyrene cup holding 50.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid, concentration 2.00 mol dm⁻³ (excess).

Run A: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l). 2.50 g of CaCO₃ gives a temperature rise of 1.8 °C.
Run B: CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l). 1.40 g of CaO gives a temperature rise of 22.4 °C.

**(a)** Draw a Hess cycle linking the decomposition to runs A and B, and write an expression for ΔH of the decomposition. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔH for run A, per mole of CaCO₃. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate ΔH for run B, per mole of CaO. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate ΔH for the decomposition of calcium carbonate. **[1]**
**(e)** Each temperature reading is ±0.1 °C. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the temperature change for run A, and suggest how to reduce it. **[2]**

**9.** Use these mean bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): O–H 463, C=O 804, C≡C 839, C–H 412, O=O 496.

C₂H₂(g) + 2½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + H₂O(g)

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change for this reaction. **[3]**
**(b)** The standard enthalpy change of combustion of ethyne is −1301 kJ mol⁻¹. Give **two** reasons for the difference. **[2]**

**10.** For N₂(g) + 3F₂(g) → 2NF₃(g), ΔH = −264 kJ mol⁻¹. Use N≡N 945 kJ mol⁻¹ and F–F 158 kJ mol⁻¹ to calculate the mean N–F bond enthalpy in NF₃. **[3]**

**11.** This question is about bond enthalpies and reactivity.

**(a)** Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹): C–F 484, C–Cl 338, C–Br 276. Explain why fluoroalkanes are hydrolysed far more slowly than bromoalkanes. **[2]**
**(b)** The combustion of methane is very exothermic, yet methane and air can be mixed at room temperature without reacting. Explain this using C–H 413 kJ mol⁻¹ and O=O 498 kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A pressure of 100 kPa [1]; a stated temperature, usually 298 K [1].
**(b)** 0 kJ mol⁻¹, because it is an element in its standard state [1].
*Examiner insight:* Old values such as 101 kPa or 1 atm do not match this specification; quote 100 kPa.

**2. (a)** ½I₂(s) → I(g) [1]
**(b)** C₄H₈(g) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l) [1]
**(c)** Na(s) + ½H₂(g) + C(s) + 1½O₂(g) → NaHCO₃(s) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Iodine is a solid in its standard state; writing I₂(g) or making two moles of atoms loses the mark.

**3.** Products, K⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq), drawn **above** the reactants, KNO₃(s) + aq [1]. Arrow pointing **upwards** from reactants to products [1]. Arrow labelled ΔH = +35 kJ mol⁻¹, with both levels labelled with formulae and state symbols [1].
*Examiner insight:* A diagram with a hump is a reaction profile, not an enthalpy level diagram; keep to two levels and one arrow.

**4. (a)** The line falls 0.4 °C per minute, so at 3.0 min it reads 36.6 + 3 × 0.4 = 37.8 °C [1]. ΔT = 37.8 − 20.5 = **17.3 °C** [1].
**(b)** Energy = 40.0 × 4.18 × 17.3 = **2893 J** [1].
**(c)** n(CuSO₄) = 0.500 × 40.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0200 mol [1]. ΔH = −2.893 ÷ 0.0200 = **−145 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** n(Fe) = 2.0 ÷ 55.8 = 0.036 mol, more than the 0.0200 mol of CuSO₄ needed [1].
**(e)** Heat is lost while the reaction proceeds, so the highest reading is too low; extrapolating back to the time of mixing allows for this loss [1].
*Examiner insight:* Divide by moles of copper(II) sulfate, the limiting reagent; using moles of iron gives a value that is too small.

**5. (a)** Energy = 250 × 4.18 × 20.0 = 20 900 J [1]. n = 0.660 ÷ 88.0 = 0.00750 mol [1]. ΔcH = −20.9 ÷ 0.00750 = **−2790 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** Any two: heat lost to the air and the can [1]; incomplete combustion [1]; evaporation of fuel from the wick; non-standard conditions.
**(c)** Any one: a draught shield, a lid on the can, or a smaller gap between flame and can [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Heat loss" alone is often too vague for credit; say where the heat goes.

**6.** Products: 3(−111) = −333, with Fe(s) zero [1]. Reactants: −824, with C(s) zero [1]. ΔrH = −333 − (−824) = **+491 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Iron and carbon are elements in their standard states, so they add nothing to either total; giving them a value loses credit.

**7.** Cycle with combustion products at the bottom, or ΔfH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΔcH(benzene) [1]. 6(−394) + 3(−286) = −2364 − 858 = −3222 [1]. ΔfH = −3222 − (−3268) [1] = **+46 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A positive answer is correct here, so write the + sign; leaving it out can cost the final mark.

**8. (a)** Cycle: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) across the top; both sides react with 2HCl(aq) to give CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) at the bottom [1]. ΔH = ΔH(A) − ΔH(B) [1].
**(b)** Energy = 50.0 × 4.18 × 1.8 = 376.2 J; n(CaCO₃) = 2.50 ÷ 100.1 = 0.02498 mol [1]. ΔH(A) = −0.3762 ÷ 0.02498 = **−15.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Energy = 50.0 × 4.18 × 22.4 = 4681.6 J; n(CaO) = 1.40 ÷ 56.1 = 0.02496 mol [1]. ΔH(B) = −4.6816 ÷ 0.02496 = **−187.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** ΔH = −15.06 − (−187.6) = **+173 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(e)** 0.2 ÷ 1.8 × 100 = **11%** [1]. Use more CaCO₃ (with acid still in excess) or less acid to get a larger temperature rise, or use a more precise thermometer [1].
*Examiner insight:* For ΔT, add the uncertainties of both readings; using ±0.1 °C alone halves the percentage uncertainty.

**9. (a)** Broken: 839 + 2(412) + 2.5(496) = 2903 kJ [1]. Made: 4(804) + 2(463) = 4142 kJ [1]. ΔH = 2903 − 4142 = **−1239 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** The values are means taken from many different compounds, so they are not exact for ethyne, CO₂ or water [1]. Water is H₂O(g) here but H₂O(l) under standard conditions, so the enthalpy change of condensing is missing [1].
*Examiner insight:* Count the bonds in each CO₂ molecule (two C=O each); one C=O per CO₂ is a common slip.

**10.** Broken − made = ΔH: 945 + 3(158) − 6x = −264 [1]. 6x = 1419 + 264 = 1683 [1]. x = **281 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* There are six N–F bonds in two NF₃ molecules; dividing by three gives double the right answer.

**11. (a)** The C–halogen bond must break during hydrolysis [1]. C–F (484) is much stronger than C–Br (276), so it is far harder to break and the reaction is much slower [1].
**(b)** C–H and O=O bonds must break before new bonds form; their bond enthalpies are high [1]. So the energy needed to start the reaction is large and very few molecules have it at room temperature, so the rate is negligible [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link the bond enthalpy to rate; saying only "C–F is strong" without stating the effect on the reaction does not answer the question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting the fuel or solid mass into m × c × ΔT in place of the water or solution mass.
- Giving the energy in J as the final answer instead of ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹.
- Missing signs, especially + on endothermic or positive answers.
- Dividing by moles of the excess reagent.
- Reading ΔT from the highest temperature instead of extrapolating.
- Reversing the subtraction in combustion-data cycles.
- Forgetting coefficients when counting bonds or multiplying ΔfH values.
- Giving vague evaluation points such as "human error".
