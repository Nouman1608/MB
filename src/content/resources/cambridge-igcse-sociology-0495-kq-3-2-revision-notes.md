Key question 3.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *What attempts have been made to reduce
social inequalities?* In short: governments pass **laws** (progressive tax, benefits, minimum wages,
equal opportunities laws and other measures), build **welfare states** (education, benefits, pensions,
housing, healthcare, possibly universal basic income), and **NGOs** work globally on poverty, health,
business and education. Sociologists disagree about success: the New Right say welfare does too much,
Marxists that it does too little, feminists that equality laws leave patriarchy intact.

Full explanations and examples are in the
[study guide for key question 3.2](/resources/cambridge-igcse-sociology-0495-kq-3-2/).

## Syllabus coverage

0495 syllabus 2025–2027, **3.2**:

- **3.2.1** Legislation: progressive taxation; redistribution of wealth through benefits; minimum
  wage; equal opportunities laws; other government measures.
- **3.2.2** Welfare states: free and/or universal education; unemployment benefits; pensions;
  low-cost housing; free healthcare; universal basic income.
- **3.2.3** NGOs: reducing poverty; providing medical assistance; promoting business; improving
  education.
- **3.2.4** Views of success: New Right/underclass; culture of poverty and dependency culture; Marxist
  criticisms; criticisms of NGOs; feminist criticisms of equality legislation.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Progressive tax** | A tax taking a higher percentage of income as income rises |
| **Regressive tax** | A tax taking a higher percentage of poorer people's income, e.g. sales tax |
| **Redistribution** | Moving resources from richer to poorer groups through tax and benefits |
| **Minimum wage** | The lowest hourly pay employers may legally offer |
| **Equal opportunities laws** | Laws making discrimination by sex, race, disability, age and so on unlawful |
| **Conditional cash transfer** | Payments to poor families on condition they meet goals such as school attendance |
| **Welfare state** | A system in which the state protects citizens' basic well-being |
| **Universal benefit** | Available to everyone regardless of income |
| **Means-tested benefit** | Available only to people below an income or savings level |
| **Universal basic income** | A regular, unconditional payment to every citizen |
| **NGO** | A non-profit organisation independent of government |
| **Microfinance** | Small loans and financial services for people without access to banks |
| **Underclass** | New Right term for a group said to depend on welfare and live outside mainstream values |
| **Dependency culture** | The idea that people come to rely on benefits and lose self-reliance |

## Examples to quote

- **Minimum wage:** UK National Minimum Wage, 1999.
- **Equality laws:** UK Equal Pay Act 1970; Equality Act 2010; India's constitutional reservations for
  Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- **Cash transfers:** Mexico's *Progresa*, 1997.
- **Near-universal payment:** Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend, since 1982.
- **NGOs:** Oxfam (1942); Save the Children (1919).

## How each measure works

- **Progressive tax** narrows gaps after tax; funds services.
- **Benefits** raise the incomes of the poorest.
- **Minimum wage** reduces in-work poverty; helps groups over-represented in low pay (women, young
  workers).
- **Equal opportunities laws** give legal routes to challenge discrimination.
- **Welfare services** improve life chances regardless of income: schooling, healthcare, housing,
  pensions.
- **UBI** removes the poverty trap because the payment is not withdrawn when people earn.
- **NGOs** reach people governments fail to reach; microfinance builds income.

## The debate on success

| Perspective | Verdict | Key names |
|---|---|---|
| New Right | Welfare does **too much** — dependency, underclass | Murray; Marsland |
| Culture of poverty | Poverty persists because of values passed down | Lewis |
| Marxist | Welfare does **too little** — softens capitalism, prevents revolt | — |
| NGO critics | Dependency, donor-led, may let governments off the hook | Moyo, *Dead Aid* (2009), on foreign aid |
| Feminist | Equality laws have not closed the pay gap or reached unpaid care; welfare assumed a male breadwinner | — |

**Balance:** absolute deprivation has fallen in many societies; relative and wealth inequality often
remain wide.

## Exam traps

- Progressive tax is about the **percentage** paid, not the amount.
- New Right and Marxists both criticise welfare but from **opposite** directions.
- NGOs are not part of government.
- Universal ≠ means-tested.
- Always add a judgement on **success** — 3.2.4 is about evaluation.

## Worked example: a 6-mark "explain" answer

*Explain **three** ways a minimum wage may reduce social inequality.* (practice)

1. **Raising the lowest pay** — employers cannot pay below the legal rate, so the poorest workers'
   incomes rise, narrowing the gap with higher earners.
2. **Reducing in-work poverty** — families with working adults are less likely to need to rely on
   benefits or debt.
3. **Narrowing the gender pay gap** — women are over-represented in low-paid jobs, so a minimum wage
   raises their pay relative to men's.

## Self-test

1. What makes a tax progressive?
2. Give one example of a regressive tax.
3. When was the UK National Minimum Wage introduced?
4. What is a conditional cash transfer?
5. Distinguish universal and means-tested benefits.
6. How can a universal basic income remove the poverty trap?
7. Name the four kinds of NGO work in the syllabus.
8. What is microfinance?
9. What is the New Right's main criticism of the welfare state?
10. What is the Marxist criticism of the welfare state?
11. Give one feminist criticism of equality legislation.
12. Give one criticism of NGOs.

## Answers

1. The percentage of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
2. A sales tax on everyday goods.
3. 1999.
4. A payment to poor families on condition they meet goals such as children's school attendance.
5. Universal benefits go to everyone; means-tested benefits only to those below an income level.
6. It is paid to everyone and not withdrawn when people earn more, so working always raises income.
7. Reducing poverty, providing medical assistance, promoting business, improving education.
8. Small loans and financial services for people without access to banks, often to start a business.
9. Generous welfare creates dependency and an underclass.
10. It only softens capitalism, keeping workers fit to be exploited and preventing revolt, without
    changing who owns wealth.
11. The gender pay gap persists; laws cannot reach unpaid domestic and care work.
12. Any one: creates dependency; accountable to donors not local people; lets governments avoid
    responsibility; too small to change structural causes.
