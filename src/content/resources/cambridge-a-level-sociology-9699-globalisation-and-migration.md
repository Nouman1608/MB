Section 8.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Globalisation and migration*,
sits in Paper 4 under heading 8, *Contemporary issues*. The syllabus lists three things to know:
the causes of global migration (including immigration, emigration, net migration, push and pull
factors, global labour patterns and tourism); its positive and negative consequences (including
cultural diversity, economic benefits, concerns over scarce resources and negative perceptions);
and debates about who benefits. The introduction to heading 8 links migration to the key concept
*Structure and human agency*, so the strongest answers ask how far migrants choose and how far
they are moved by forces beyond their control.

## Measuring and classifying migration

Before explaining migration, be precise about what is being counted.

- **Immigration** is movement into a country; **emigration** is movement out of it.
- **Net migration** is immigration minus emigration over a period. A positive figure means the
  population gains through migration; a negative figure means it loses. Net migration can hide
  very large flows in both directions, so a country with small net migration may still be
  changing fast.
- **International and internal migration.** The syllabus focuses on global movement, but
  rural-to-urban migration inside a country is often the first step in a longer journey.
- **Voluntary and forced migration.** Refugees flee persecution or conflict and are protected by
  the 1951 UN Refugee Convention; asylum seekers have applied for that protection and are waiting
  for a decision. Economic migrants move mainly for work.
- **Temporary, circular and permanent migration.** Seasonal farm workers and contract workers move
  and return; others settle and bring family members.
- **Irregular migration** means entering or staying without legal permission.

**Worked example (invented figures).** In one year a country records 420,000 immigrants and
310,000 emigrants. Net migration = 420,000 − 310,000 = +110,000. If emigration rose to 450,000
the next year with immigration unchanged, net migration would be −30,000, even though 420,000
people still arrived. The lesson for essays: "net migration fell" does not mean fewer people came.

Classification is a sociological question too. Whether a person is labelled a "refugee", an
"economic migrant" or an "illegal immigrant" affects their rights and how the public sees them,
which links causes to the consequences discussed later.

## Causes: from individual choice to global structure

### Push and pull factors

Everett Lee's push–pull model (1966) explains migration as a decision that weighs factors at the
place of origin against factors at the destination. Lee also included **intervening obstacles**,
such as distance, cost and immigration law, and **personal factors**, such as age, skills and
family ties. That is why two neighbours facing the same poverty may make different decisions.

*Evaluation:* the model treats migrants as individuals making rational calculations. It cannot
easily explain why migration flows follow particular routes (often between former colonies and
colonial powers) or why the poorest people, who have the strongest push factors, are often least
able to move.

### Structural explanations

- **Dual labour market theory.** Michael Piore's *Birds of Passage* (1979) argued that wealthy
  economies have a secondary labour market of low-paid, insecure jobs that native workers avoid.
  Employers' demand for workers to fill it is a permanent pull, so migration is driven by the
  structure of receiving economies, not only by poverty in sending ones.
- **World-systems and dependency views.** Saskia Sassen argued that investment by core economies
  in peripheral regions disrupts traditional farming and work, uprooting people and creating links
  that later become migration routes. Her work on the "global city" shows financial centres
  needing both highly paid professionals and low-paid cleaners, carers and caterers.
- **Networks and chain migration.** Once some people have settled, they send information, money
  and invitations, lowering the costs for relatives and neighbours who follow.

### Global labour patterns

Globalisation has created labour flows organised by sector:

- **Care and domestic work**, where women from poorer countries care for children and older people
  in richer ones. Arlie Hochschild's idea of the **global care chain** and Barbara Ehrenreich and
  Hochschild's *Global Woman* (2002) show this as a gendered pattern.
- **Construction and services in the Gulf states**, where many workers are recruited on temporary
  contracts tied to a sponsoring employer (the *kafala* system), limiting their freedom to change
  jobs.
- **Highly skilled migration**, where doctors, nurses, engineers and technology workers are
  recruited internationally. The loss is called **brain drain**; when skills and money later
  flow back, some writers speak of **brain circulation**.

Stephen Castles and Mark Miller's *The Age of Migration* (first edition 1993) describes these
trends as globalisation, acceleration, differentiation and feminisation of migration.

### Tourism

Tourism is temporary movement, but the syllabus lists it among the causes of global migration
because it creates longer-term flows. Resort economies draw in seasonal workers from elsewhere;
visitors who first came as tourists may later retire abroad or buy second homes (sometimes called
lifestyle migration); and tourism spreads images of other places that become pull factors. John
Urry's *The Tourist Gaze* (1990) shows that tourism is socially organised: places are packaged
and represented for visitors, which also shapes how migrants imagine destinations.

## Consequences of migration

### Positive consequences

- **Cultural diversity.** Migration brings languages, religions, food, music and ideas. Stuart
  Hall's writing on diaspora ("Cultural Identity and Diaspora", 1990) treats migrant identities as
  hybrid and constantly in production, not as fixed cultures carried from one place to another.
  This links back to hybrid identity in 7.2.
- **Economic benefits.** Migrants fill labour shortages, pay taxes, start businesses and support
  ageing populations in low-birth-rate societies. In sending countries, **remittances** pay for
  schooling, health care and housing.
- **Transnational ties.** Migrants maintain links across borders, carrying investment, political
  ideas and skills back home.

### Negative consequences and concerns

- **Concerns over scarce resources.** People in receiving areas may worry about competition for
  housing, school places, health services and jobs. Sociologists distinguish the *perception* of
  pressure from the *evidence*: pressure may be real in particular places, especially where public
  spending has not grown with population, even if national effects are small.
- **Negative perceptions.** Media framing of migrants as a "flood" or a threat can create moral
  panics, prejudice and racism. Marxists argue that such perceptions turn workers against each
  other rather than against employers.
- **Costs to sending countries.** Brain drain of health workers, family separation, and children
  growing up with one or both parents abroad.
- **Costs to migrants.** Exploitation, debt to recruiters, insecure legal status and loss of
  occupational status, for example qualified teachers working as cleaners.

## Debates about who benefits

| Perspective | Who benefits? | Key idea or source |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionalist / neo-liberal** | Everyone, on balance | Labour moves to where it is needed; remittances and skills spread development |
| **Marxist** | Employers and capital in receiving countries | Stephen Castles and Godula Kosack's *Immigrant Workers and Class Structure in Western Europe* (1973): migrant workers formed the lowest stratum of the working class, doing the worst-paid jobs |
| **Feminist** | Richer households, often richer women, at the expense of migrant women and their families | Global care chains; emotional costs to children left behind |
| **Transnational / postmodern** | Migrants can be active agents | Migrants build lives across borders and use networks and new media to keep families together |

**Using the debate.** A strong answer avoids treating "migrants" as one group. A refugee doctor,
a seasonal farm worker and a retired tourist turned resident gain and lose very different things.
Benefits also differ by gender, legal status and skill level, and between the short term and the
long term.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that migration is mainly a matter of individual choice.*

> Lee's push–pull model (1966) supports the view, because it explains migration as a decision in
> which individuals weigh conditions at home against opportunities abroad, shaped by personal
> factors such as skills and family. **[AO1: accurate knowledge]** However, Piore (1979) shows
> that the "pull" is created by the structure of rich economies, which depend on a secondary
> labour market that native workers avoid. **[AO3: counter-argument]** Workers recruited under
> the kafala system in the Gulf states, for example, may choose to go, but their employer then
> controls whether they can change jobs. **[AO2: application]** Migration is therefore better
> seen as agency exercised within structural limits, which supports the view only partly.
> **[AO3: judgement]**

Notice the pattern: claim, theory, counter-theory, concrete example, then a judgement that answers
the question.

## Common mistakes

- Using "immigration" and "migration" as if they meant the same thing.
- Calculating net migration the wrong way round (it is immigration minus emigration).
- Writing only about economic migrants and forgetting refugees, family reunion and tourism.
- Describing consequences without saying *for whom*: receiving society, sending society or the
  migrants themselves.
- Treating public concern about scarce resources as either proven fact or simple prejudice; the
  sociological point is to examine the evidence and the framing.
- Quoting migration statistics you cannot source. Use named studies and clearly labelled
  examples instead.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions need a reason or process, developed with a concept and an example. One
  sentence per point is rarely enough.
- **Evaluate** questions need contrasting views, a judgement and evidence for that judgement.
  For 8.2, the natural contrast is between individual-choice (push–pull) and structural
  explanations, or between optimistic and critical views of who benefits.
- Make the **glocal** link the syllabus asks for: show how global forces play out in a particular
  town, labour market or family.
- Use concepts from other headings: hybrid identity (7.2), global inequality (8.1) and moral
  panics (10.1) all strengthen migration answers.
