## Rules the syllabus sets for ratio work

- The formulae in the appendix to section 3 are the only ones accepted in answers, and ratios are not given in the question paper. Learn each formula exactly.
- The syllabus says an incorrect formula is not credited, but the figure it produces is credited as your own figure when used in later calculations. A wrong formula costs that calculation only if you carry on sensibly.
- Use year-end balances unless the question specifies average figures, and give the number of decimal places the question asks for.
- Where you are asked for a recommendation or decision, support it with a balance of reasons and justify it.
- Calculators are essential for every paper.

## The data used in this guide

Ardenmoor Textiles plc (an invented company) weaves curtain fabric. Amounts are in $000 unless shown per share. Interest is 12% on loan notes: the loan notes stood at 1,000 in 2025, a further 1,000 was issued on the first day of 2026, and 2026 interest also includes 40 of overdraft interest.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 8,400 | 9,600 |
| Credit sales | 7,560 | 8,640 |
| Cost of sales | 5,040 | 6,048 |
| Credit purchases | 4,200 | 5,400 |
| Year-end inventories | 840 | 1,260 |
| Trade receivables | 840 | 1,296 |
| Trade payables | 560 | 600 |
| Profit from operations | 1,260 | 1,344 |
| Interest payable | 120 | 280 |
| Profit for the year (after tax) | 855 | 798 |
| Issued ordinary shares (thousands) | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Non-current liabilities (loan notes) | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| Issued ordinary share capital | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Reserves | 2,400 | 2,928 |
| Interim dividend paid | 90 | 90 |
| Final dividend proposed | 180 | 120 |
| Market price per share (dollars) | 3.99 | 3.20 |

## Working capital ratios

**Working capital cycle (days)** = trade receivables turnover (days) + inventory turnover (days) − trade payables turnover (days). It measures how long cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Each part comes from the appendix:

- Trade receivables turnover = trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365
- Inventory turnover = average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 (year-end inventory here, as no average is specified)
- Trade payables turnover = trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365

| 2026 | Working | Days |
|---|---|---|
| Receivables | 1,296 ÷ 8,640 × 365 | 54.8 |
| Inventory | 1,260 ÷ 6,048 × 365 | 76.0 |
| Payables | 600 ÷ 5,400 × 365 | 40.6 |
| Working capital cycle | 54.8 + 76.0 − 40.6 | **90.2** |

For 2025 the same method gives 40.6 + 60.8 − 48.7 = **52.7** days.

**Net working assets to revenue (%)** = net working assets ÷ revenue × 100, where net working assets = inventories + trade receivables − trade payables. For 2026: (1,260 + 1,296 − 600) = 1,956; 1,956 ÷ 9,600 × 100 = **20.4%**. For 2025: 1,120 ÷ 8,400 × 100 = **13.3%**. This shows how much working investment each dollar of sales needs.

## Financing ratios

**Interest cover (times)** = profit from operations ÷ interest payable. 2026: 1,344 ÷ 280 = **4.8 times**. 2025: 1,260 ÷ 120 = **10.5 times**.

**Gearing (%)** = non-current liabilities ÷ (issued ordinary share capital + all reserves + non-current liabilities) × 100. 2026: 2,000 ÷ (3,000 + 2,928 + 2,000) × 100 = 2,000 ÷ 7,928 × 100 = **25.2%**. 2025: 1,000 ÷ 6,400 × 100 = **15.6%**.

## Investor ratios

These are the stock exchange ratios in the appendix, labelled A Level only.

- **Earnings per share** = profit for the year ÷ number of issued ordinary shares. 2026: 798 ÷ 3,000 = $0.266, which is **26.6 cents**. 2025: 855 ÷ 3,000 = **28.5 cents**.
- **Price/earnings** = market price per share ÷ earnings per share. 2026: 3.20 ÷ 0.266 = **12.0**. 2025: 3.99 ÷ 0.285 = **14.0**. It is the number of years of current earnings the price represents.
- **Dividend per share** = annual ordinary dividend ÷ number of issued ordinary shares, where annual ordinary dividend = interim dividend paid + final dividend proposed. 2026: (90 + 120) ÷ 3,000 = **7.0 cents**. 2025: (90 + 180) ÷ 3,000 = **9.0 cents**.
- **Dividend yield** = dividend per share ÷ market price per share. 2026: 0.07 ÷ 3.20 = **0.0219**, or 2.19% when written as a percentage. 2025: 0.09 ÷ 3.99 = 0.0226 (2.26%). The appendix prints no multiplier or unit for this ratio, so give the form the question asks for.
- **Dividend cover** = profit for the year available to pay ordinary dividend ÷ annual ordinary dividend. 2026: 798 ÷ 210 = **3.8 times**. 2025: 855 ÷ 270 = **3.2 times**. Ardenmoor has only ordinary shares, so all of the profit is available. If a question gives another prior claim on profit, deduct it before dividing.

## Turning figures into analysis

A calculation alone earns little here, because Paper 3 assesses analysis and evaluation as well as knowledge. For each ratio, write a four-part point: state the change with both figures, give a likely cause, explain the effect on a named stakeholder, then judge how serious it is.

| Ratio | A rise usually suggests | Look out for |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital cycle | Cash is tied up longer; more finance needed | A fall can come from stretching payables |
| Net working assets to revenue | More working investment per dollar of sales | Growth, or slack control of inventory and receivables |
| Interest cover | A bigger safety margin for lenders | A low figure means profit could be wiped out by a small fall |
| Gearing | More fixed-cost finance, so more risk | Borrowing can lift returns when the business earns more than it pays |
| Earnings per share | More profit for each share | Only comparable if share numbers are unchanged |
| Price/earnings | The market expects growth or low risk | A fall can signal lost confidence |
| Dividend per share | A larger cash reward | May be paid out of profits that are falling |
| Dividend yield | A higher return on the current price | A high yield can come from a fallen price |
| Dividend cover | A safer dividend and more retained profit | Very high cover may mean cash is held back |

**Ardenmoor conclusions.** Revenue rose 14.3% but profit for the year fell 6.7%. The working capital cycle lengthened from 52.7 to 90.2 days because customers took longer to pay (40.6 to 54.8 days), inventory was held longer (60.8 to 76.0 days) and suppliers were paid sooner (48.7 to 40.6 days). Net working assets rose by 836, financed partly by loan notes and overdraft, so interest payable more than doubled and interest cover halved to 4.8. Gearing rose to 25.2%, which is still moderate, yet the borrowing costs 12% while ROCE (profit from operations ÷ capital employed × 100, from section 1.6) fell from 19.7% to 17.0%, so the margin of benefit from borrowing is narrowing.

## How the ratios connect

1. **Working capital to profit.** A longer cycle needs more finance. Borrowing adds interest, which lowers interest cover and profit for the year, so earnings per share falls.
2. **Gearing and interest cover.** They move together: new loan notes raise gearing and, unless operating profit grows by enough, lower cover. A highly geared business has a larger fall in earnings when profit from operations drops.
3. **Earnings, price and dividends.** With only ordinary shares, dividend cover equals earnings per share ÷ dividend per share. Ardenmoor's cover rose from 3.2 to 3.8 only because the dividend was cut harder (22.2%) than earnings fell (6.7%). Price/earnings fell from 14.0 to 12.0 because the price fell by 19.8%, more than the earnings.
4. **Yield and price.** Yield = dividend per share ÷ price, so a lower dividend and a lower price pull in opposite directions; Ardenmoor's yield barely moved.
5. **Efficiency and return.** Slower inventory and receivables turnover tie up capital, which depresses ROCE even when sales grow.

## Recommendations and improvement measures

A model point on Ardenmoor: "Interest cover fell from 10.5 to 4.8 times because interest payable rose from 120 to 280 while profit from operations rose only 6.7%. A lender has a thinner safety margin, although 4.8 times is still adequate, so the lender should watch the next set of accounts."

State who you are advising, give the recommendation, then a reason for and a reason against, then your decision.

- **Shareholder:** hold rather than buy. For: earnings per share is still positive, cover of 3.8 means the dividend is safe. Against: earnings are falling and the dividend has been cut, so the price may fall further.
- **Lender:** do not lend more without conditions. For: interest cover of 4.8 still covers payments. Against: it has halved and the cycle is the cause, so extra lending may only fund slow collection.
- **Manager:** target the cycle. Cutting receivables to 45 days releases about 231 (1,296 less 1,065). Cutting inventory to 70 days releases about 100 (1,260 less 1,160). Using 300 of the cash to repay loan notes would save interest of 36, lift interest cover to 5.5 and cut gearing to 22.3%, but pressing customers may cost sales if they value credit.

Other measures: negotiate longer supplier credit without losing discounts, reduce slow-moving inventory, fund growth with share capital rather than debt, and hold dividends until earnings recover.

## Common errors

- Using total revenue instead of credit sales or credit purchases in the days ratios.
- Adding the payables days to the cycle instead of subtracting them.
- Using profit for the year in interest cover instead of profit from operations.
- Leaving non-current liabilities out of the denominator of gearing.
- Using only the final dividend in dividend per share, rather than interim plus final.
- Describing a ratio without saying whether the change is good or bad for a stated user.
