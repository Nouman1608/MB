## Section A

**1.** A coil is connected to a sensitive centre-zero ammeter. A student pushes the N pole of a magnet into the coil and the needle flicks to the right. State what the needle does when:

**(a)** the magnet is held still inside the coil **[1]**
**(b)** the magnet is pulled out of the coil **[1]**
**(c)** the S pole is pushed into the coil at a greater speed. **[2]**

**2.** A wire is held between the poles of a U-shaped magnet and connected to a meter. Describe how the wire must be moved to induce the largest voltage. **[2]**

**3.** State three factors that affect the size of the voltage induced in a coil of a generator. **[3]**

**4.** A bicycle dynamo has a magnet that rotates inside a fixed coil when the wheel turns. Explain why a voltage is induced in the coil. **[2]**

**5.** A student rotates the coil of a small generator at a steady speed and displays the output on an oscilloscope. She then rotates it at a greater speed. Describe two ways in which the trace changes. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a transformer has an iron core linking the two coils. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A transformer has 400 turns on its primary coil and 8000 turns on its secondary coil. The primary coil is connected to a 12 V a.c. supply.

**(a)** State whether this is a step-up or a step-down transformer. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the output voltage. **[2]**

**(c)** The primary current is 4.0 A. Assuming the transformer is 100% efficient, calculate the current in the secondary coil. **[3]**

**8.** A student wants to make a transformer that gives a 6.0 V output from a 230 V supply. The primary coil has 2300 turns.

**(a)** Calculate the number of turns needed on the secondary coil. **[2]**

**(b)** The student tests the transformer and finds that the input power is greater than the output power. Suggest one reason why. **[1]**

**(c)** The student connects the primary coil to a 230 V d.c. supply instead. State and explain the output voltage. **[2]**

**9.** A power station generates 50 MW of electrical power at 20 kV.

**(a)** Calculate the current if the power were transmitted at 20 kV. **[2]**

**(b)** A step-up transformer raises the voltage to 200 kV. Calculate the new current. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why transmitting at 200 kV wastes less energy than transmitting at 20 kV. **[2]**

**10.** A substation transformer reduces the voltage from 132 kV to 33 kV.

**(a)** Show that the turns ratio Np : Ns is 4 : 1. **[2]**

**(b)** The secondary current is 100 A. Assuming 100% efficiency, calculate the primary current. **[2]**

**11.** Describe the structure of a transformer and explain how an alternating voltage applied to the primary coil produces an alternating voltage across the secondary coil. **[5]**

**12.** An engineer says: "A step-up transformer gives out more energy than it takes in, because the output voltage is bigger." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Reads zero (no deflection) [1].
**(b)** Flicks to the left (opposite direction) [1].
**(c)** Flicks to the left [1], with a larger deflection [1].

**2.** Move the wire at right angles to the field lines (up or down between the poles, cutting across the field) [1], as fast as possible [1].

**3.** Speed of rotation [1]; strength of the magnetic field [1]; number of turns on the coil [1]. (Also accept area of the coil or presence of an iron core.)

**4.** As the magnet rotates, the magnetic field through the coil keeps changing [1]; a changing field through a coil induces a voltage across it [1].

**5.** The peaks are taller (larger peak voltage) [1]; the peaks are closer together (higher frequency, shorter time period) [1].

**6.** The iron core is easily magnetised, so the changing magnetic field produced by the primary is concentrated in it [1] and is carried through the secondary coil, so a voltage is induced there [1].

**7. (a)** Step-up (more turns on the secondary) [1].
**(b)** Vs = Vp × Ns / Np [1] = 12 × 8000 / 400 = **240 V** [1].
**(c)** Vp × Ip = Vs × Is [1]; 12 × 4.0 = 240 × Is [1]; Is = 48 / 240 = **0.20 A** [1].

**8. (a)** Ns = Np × Vs / Vp [1] = 2300 × 6.0 / 230 = **60 turns** [1].
**(b)** Energy is wasted, for example by heating in the coils or core, so the transformer is not 100% efficient [1].
**(c)** Zero output voltage (after the moment of switching on) [1], because d.c. gives a steady magnetic field in the core, and a field that is not changing induces no voltage in the secondary [1].

**9. (a)** I = P / V = 50 000 000 / 20 000 [1] = **2500 A** [1].
**(b)** I = 50 000 000 / 200 000 [1] = **250 A** [1].
**(c)** The current is ten times smaller [1], so there is much less heating in the cables and less energy is transferred to the surroundings [1].

**10. (a)** Np / Ns = Vp / Vs [1] = 132 / 33 = 4, so Np : Ns = 4 : 1 [1].
**(b)** Ip = Vs × Is / Vp = 33 000 × 100 / 132 000 [1] = **25 A** [1].

**11.** Primary coil connected to the a.c. supply [1]; secondary coil connected to the output (load) [1]; both wound on a soft iron core [1]. The alternating current in the primary produces a changing magnetic field in the core [1]; this changing field passes through the secondary coil and induces an alternating voltage across it [1].

**12.** The statement is wrong [1]. Energy is conserved: at best (100% efficiency) input power equals output power, VpIp = VsIs [1]. The output voltage is larger, but the output current is smaller by the same factor, so the power (and energy) out is no more than the power in [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that a stationary magnet in a coil induces a voltage.
- Putting the turns ratio upside down in Questions 7, 8 and 10. Check: more turns, more volts.
- Leaving kV and MW unconverted: 50 MW ÷ 20 kV in mixed units gives the wrong power of ten.
- Saying high-voltage transmission "reduces the resistance".
- Describing a transformer without saying the field in the core is changing.
