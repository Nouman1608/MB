The IB Middle Years Programme Individuals and Societies subject group
incorporates disciplines traditionally studied under humanities and
social sciences. It encourages learners to respect and understand the
world around them, and equips them with the skills to inquire into
historical, geographical, political, social, economic and cultural
factors that affect individuals, societies and environments.

## What schools can offer within this subject group

For MYP Individuals and Societies, schools develop courses drawing on
integrated humanities, history, economics, geography, philosophy,
sociology/anthropology, business management, psychology, and world
religions. A school may teach these as separate (discrete) courses,
as modules studied one after another, or as one integrated course such
as humanities or social studies. The IB's approach includes a strong
focus on inquiry and investigation: students collect, describe and
analyse data, test hypotheses, and learn to interpret increasingly
complex information, including original source material.

## Aims

The course encourages and enables students to:

- appreciate human and environmental commonalities and diversity
- understand the interactions and interdependence of individuals,
  societies and the environment
- understand how both environmental and human systems operate and
  evolve
- identify and develop concern for the well-being of human
  communities and the natural environment
- act as responsible citizens of local and global communities
- develop inquiry skills that lead towards conceptual understandings
  of the relationships between individuals, societies and the
  environments in which they live.

## Key concepts

The IB names four key concepts that broadly frame MYP Individuals and
Societies. They are the big ideas that link what you study across
different topics and disciplines.

- **Change** — how and why things become different over time: the
  causes, processes and consequences.
- **Global interactions** — how people, places and systems around the
  world connect and affect one another, through trade, migration,
  conflict or cooperation.
- **Time, place and space** — where and when something happens, and how
  its location and period shape it.
- **Systems** — sets of connected parts, such as an economy, a
  government or an ecosystem, where a change in one part affects the
  others.

## Related concepts

Related concepts are narrower ideas that belong to particular
disciplines and take your understanding deeper. The IB's subject brief
gives these examples:

- **Causality** — why events happen: the links between causes and
  effects.
- **Globalization** — the growing interconnection of economies,
  cultures and people around the world.
- **Culture** — the shared beliefs, values, customs and ways of life of
  a group.
- **Sustainability** — meeting people's needs today without damaging
  the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

The full MYP subject guide lists more related concepts for each
discipline (history, geography, economics and integrated humanities).
Your teacher will tell you which ones your units use.

## The six global contexts

Global contexts give each unit a real-world setting. You explore the key
and related concepts through them.

- **Identities and relationships** — who we are, what we believe and
  value, and how we relate to families, communities and cultures.
- **Orientation in space and time** — personal and historical
  journeys, turning points in history, and how places and people are
  connected.
- **Personal and cultural expression** — how people express ideas,
  feelings, beliefs and culture.
- **Scientific and technical innovation** — how people use science and
  technology to change their environments and societies.
- **Globalization and sustainability** — how local and global systems
  are linked, and the effects of human decisions on people and the
  planet.
- **Fairness and development** — rights and responsibilities, access
  to opportunities, and how communities share resources and settle
  conflicts.

## The four assessment criteria

Your teachers assess your work throughout the course against four
equally weighted criteria. There is no Standard/Higher Level split:
every student is assessed against the same criteria, and each one is
marked on a 0–8 scale (the four bands run from 1 to 8, and 0 is given
when work does not reach level 1). The four criteria together give a
total out of 32, which the programme converts to a final grade.

- **Criterion A: Knowing and understanding** — show accurate factual
  knowledge and understanding of concepts, using subject terms
  correctly.
- **Criterion B: Investigating** — plan and carry out research: choose
  a clear focus, use suitable methods and sources, and reflect on how
  the investigation went.
- **Criterion C: Communicating** — organise and present your ideas
  clearly, in a format and style that fits the audience and purpose,
  and document your sources.
- **Criterion D: Thinking critically** — analyse and evaluate
  information, arguments, sources and perspectives, and reach
  conclusions you can support with evidence.

Most assessment is classroom-based, so you have many chances across the
year to show what you can do against each criterion.

## MYP eAssessment

Students who want IB-validated course results or the MYP certificate
take a **2-hour on-screen examination** at the end of MYP year 5. The
IB's subject brief lists on-screen examinations in history, geography
and integrated humanities; the IB's current Individuals and Societies
web page describes eAssessment for integrated humanities courses (which
can include economics, geography and history). Ask your school which
examination you are entered for.

According to the subject brief (2021 edition), the examination has three
tasks, and every task uses source material:

| Task | Criteria | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| Investigating — evaluate an investigation and plan your own | A and B | 26 |
| Communicating — present information and ideas for a given audience, purpose and format | A and C | 18 |
| Thinking critically — discuss issues, arguments and perspectives, ending in extended writing | A, C and D | 36 |

Topics the brief says the examinations explore include demographics and
human movements; settlement and urban morphology; superpowers, empires,
and supra-national alliances and organizations; significant
individuals; warfare and peacekeeping; rights and social protest; trade,
aid and exchange; economic agents (consumers, producers, governments,
banks); measurements and trends; and industrialization and
technological developments.

Because the tasks are built on unfamiliar sources, practising with new
sources under timed conditions is a better use of revision time than
memorising facts in isolation.

## Looking ahead to the Diploma Programme

Working from sources, building an evidence-based argument and weighing
different perspectives all carry forward to DP History, Geography,
Global Politics, Economics and other individuals-and-societies
subjects. The main change is that most DP assessment is by timed
external examination, so practising timed, exam-style answers before
DP begins helps.
