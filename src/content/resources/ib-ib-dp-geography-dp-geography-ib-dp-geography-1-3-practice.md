## Section A

**1.** Define economic water scarcity. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a point source and a non-point source of water pollution, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**3.** Country S has 18,000 million cubic metres of renewable water a year and withdraws 7,920 million cubic metres. Calculate its withdrawal-to-availability ratio. **[1]**

**4.** Outline the role of secondary treatment in a wastewater plant. **[2]**

**5.** Outline why households without a piped connection may pay more per litre than connected households. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented data for two countries.

| | Country K | Country L |
|---|---|---|
| Renewable water (million cubic metres per year) | 5,400 | 40,000 |
| Population (millions) | 3 | 50 |
| Water withdrawn (million cubic metres per year) | 1,080 | 8,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the renewable water per person in each country and classify each using the Falkenmark thresholds (stress below 1,700; scarcity below 1,000; absolute scarcity below 500 cubic metres per person per year). **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the withdrawal ratio for each country. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the two indicators give different impressions of water pressure in Country L. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which buffer strips along rivers improve water quality. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why a fall in nitrate fertiliser use may take many years to improve groundwater quality. **[2]**

**9.** Outline how the Indus Waters Treaty divides the rivers of the Indus system. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the consequences of water scarcity for different social groups. **[10]**

**11.** "International agreements are the most effective way to prevent conflict over shared rivers." To what extent do you agree with this statement? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Water is physically available but people cannot access enough of it because of a lack of money, infrastructure or effective institutions [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A point source enters at a single identifiable place, such as a factory outfall or sewage pipe [1]; a non-point source comes from a wide area, such as fertiliser washing off many fields [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 7,920 / 18,000 = **0.44** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Bacteria are used to break down dissolved and fine organic matter [1], which lowers the biochemical oxygen demand of the water before it is released [1]. **[2]**

**5.** They must buy from vendors or tankers, whose costs of transport and profit are added to each litre [1], whereas piped supply spreads the cost of infrastructure over many users and is often subsidised [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Country K: 5,400 / 3 = 1,800 cubic metres per person, above 1,700, so **not under stress** on this indicator [1]. Country L: 40,000 / 50 = 800 cubic metres per person [1], which is **scarcity** (below 1,000 but above 500) [1].

**(b)** Country K: 1,080 / 5,400 = **0.20** [1]. Country L: 8,000 / 40,000 = **0.20** [1].

**(c)** The Falkenmark indicator compares supply with population, so Country L's large population makes it appear scarce [1]; the withdrawal ratio compares use with supply, and Country L withdraws only a fifth of its water, so current use is not under great pressure, though low per-person supply limits future growth [1]. **[7]**

**7.** Vegetation slows overland flow so that sediment settles out before reaching the river [1], reducing turbidity and the attached pollutants [1]. Plant roots and soil microbes take up nitrates and phosphates from runoff [1], so fewer nutrients reach the water and the risk of eutrophication falls [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Nitrate moves slowly downward through soil and rock to the aquifer [1], so nitrate applied years ago is still travelling and stored, and it takes many years to be flushed out even after use falls [1]. **[2]**

**9.** The eastern rivers, the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej, were allocated to India [1]. The western rivers, the Indus, Jhelum and Chenab, were allocated mainly to Pakistan [1], with India allowed limited uses on them such as run-of-river hydroelectricity [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Women and girls:** time spent collecting water from distant sources reduces schooling and paid work [1] [1].
- **Children:** greatest health risk from unsafe water, through diarrhoeal disease and cholera [1].
- **Poor urban households:** reliance on vendors means higher prices per litre and less water used [1].
- **Farmers:** loss of irrigation reduces yields and income, and can drive migration [1].
- **Wealthier groups:** can buy storage, filters, deeper wells or bottled water, so are better able to cope [1].
- **Downstream communities:** depend on upstream decisions, as in shared rivers [1].
- **Examination:** the same scarcity has unequal effects because of income, gender, location and political voice [1]; physical scarcity hurts everyone in drought, but economic scarcity is concentrated on the poor [1].
- **Conclusion:** a supported judgement, for example that consequences fall most heavily on poor women and children, so solutions must address access and not only supply [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Agreement:** treaties set rules and allocations, reducing uncertainty [1]; the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 lasted through several conflicts between India and Pakistan [1]; dispute procedures give a non-violent route for disagreement [1].
- **Limits:** fixed allocations may not fit changing flows or demand [1]; agreements depend on political will and can be suspended, as India announced in 2025 for the Indus treaty [1]; some basins lack an agreement all riparian states accept, as with the Blue Nile dam negotiations [1].
- **Alternatives and complements:** joint data sharing and river basin organisations build trust [1]; benefit-sharing (for example, selling hydroelectric power downstream) can make cooperation attractive [1]; demand management reduces the pressure that drives conflict [1].
- **Conclusion:** agreements are necessary but not sufficient; they are most effective when combined with shared institutions and trust, so the statement is only partly true [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* A "to what extent" answer needs a position from the start and a conclusion that matches it. Use at least two located shared rivers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a single indicator without commenting on what it misses.
- Writing that buffer strips "clean" water without the mechanism.
- Describing scarcity's effects on "people" generally rather than on named groups.
- Treating a treaty as a guarantee of peace.
