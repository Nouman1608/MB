This guide goes deeper into section **3.2.5 Breathing** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and this section
can be assessed on either paper. Statements a to c are shared with Combined Science; statement **d, mechanical
ventilators, is marked B** (Biology only).

The existing [Breathing and respiration study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-breathing-and-respiration/)
covers 3.2.5 and 3.2.6 together, including how to read a pressure trace. This page concentrates on breathing and
adds new material: the bell-jar model and its limits, why many small alveoli beat one large sac, and what the
air you breathe out tells you about gas exchange. Afterwards, use the
[Breathing revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-breathing-oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-revision-notes/)
and the [Breathing practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-breathing-oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-practice/).

## What 3.2.5 asks you to know

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | The breathing system takes air in and out so oxygen can diffuse into the blood and carbon dioxide out of it; the lungs are in the thorax, protected by the ribcage and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm; recognise ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm, lungs, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli on a diagram |
| b | Inhaling and exhaling in terms of the intercostal muscles, diaphragm, thorax volume and pressure |
| c | Alveoli provide a very large surface area, richly supplied with blood capillaries |
| d (B) | Spontaneous breathing may stop through disease or injury; negative and positive pressure ventilators |

## Breathing is not respiration

Students lose marks every year by mixing these up. **Breathing** (ventilation) is the movement of air into and
out of the lungs. **Respiration** is the chemical reaction in cells that transfers energy from glucose. Breathing
supplies the oxygen for respiration and removes the carbon dioxide it produces, but it is a physical process done
by muscles, not a reaction.

## Recognising the parts: a top-to-bottom description

Diagrams in the exam may be unfamiliar drawings, so learn the parts by their position and shape:

| Part | How to recognise it |
|---|---|
| Trachea | Single wide tube running down the middle of the neck into the chest |
| Bronchi | The two branches of the trachea, one to each lung |
| Bronchioles | Many narrower tubes branching from each bronchus inside the lung |
| Alveoli | Tiny air sacs in clusters at the ends of the bronchioles |
| Ribs | Curved bones forming a cage around the lungs |
| Intercostal muscles | Muscles between neighbouring ribs |
| Diaphragm | Dome-shaped sheet of muscle below the lungs, separating thorax from abdomen |
| Lungs | Two spongy organs filling most of the thorax |

## Volume and pressure: the core idea

Gases move from **higher pressure to lower pressure**. If the volume of a sealed space increases, the pressure
inside falls; if the volume decreases, the pressure rises. The thorax behaves like this.

```
INHALE: intercostals contract → ribcage up (and out)
        diaphragm muscles contract → diaphragm flattens
        → thorax volume increases → pressure falls below atmospheric → air enters lungs

EXHALE: intercostals relax → ribcage moves down
        diaphragm muscles relax → diaphragm returns to dome
        → thorax volume decreases → pressure rises → air leaves lungs
```

A full-mark answer always runs in this order: **muscles → volume → pressure → air movement**. Missing out the
pressure step is the most common reason for losing a mark.

## The bell-jar model

Teachers often demonstrate breathing with a model: a glass bell jar (the thorax) with a Y-shaped tube (trachea and
bronchi) leading to two balloons (lungs), and a rubber sheet stretched across the open bottom (the diaphragm).

**Worked example 1: using and evaluating the model**

*What happens when the rubber sheet is pulled down?*

> The volume inside the jar increases, so the pressure inside falls below atmospheric pressure. Air flows in
> through the tube and the balloons inflate. This models inhalation as the diaphragm flattens.

*Give two ways the model is not like the real thorax.*

> 1. The jar is rigid, so it cannot model the ribcage moving up and out by the intercostal muscles.
> 2. The rubber sheet is pulled down by hand from outside, whereas the real diaphragm flattens when its own
>    muscles contract. (Also accepted: balloons are not made of alveoli, so there is no gas exchange; the
>    real diaphragm is dome-shaped at rest, not flat.)

Evaluation questions on models want **what the model shows correctly** and **what it leaves out**.

## Alveoli: surface area and blood supply

The specification gives two features: alveoli provide a **very large surface area**, and they are **richly
supplied with blood capillaries**, so gases can readily diffuse into and out of the blood.

**Worked example 2: why many small sacs?** Compare one sphere of radius 1 cm with 1000 tiny spheres of radius
0.1 cm. They hold the same total volume of air.

```
volume check: 1000 × (0.1)³ = 1000 × 0.001 = 1 = 1³  ✓ same volume
surface area of a sphere = 4πr²
one large sphere:     4 × 3.14 × 1² = 12.6 cm²
1000 small spheres:   1000 × 4 × 3.14 × 0.1² = 1000 × 0.126 = 126 cm²
```

Dividing the same volume into many small sacs gives about **ten times** the surface area here. The lungs contain
a vast number of tiny alveoli, so the area for diffusion is very large.

The rich capillary network matters because blood **carries oxygen away** from the alveoli and **brings carbon
dioxide to them** all the time. That keeps the concentration of oxygen in the alveolar air higher than in the
blood, and carbon dioxide lower, so diffusion continues.

## What exhaled air tells you

Approximate typical values for a person at rest:

| Gas | Inhaled air (%) | Exhaled air (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | 21 | 16 |
| Carbon dioxide | 0.04 | 4 |

**Worked example 3.** A person at rest breathes 12 times a minute, taking in 0.5 dm³ of air per breath. Estimate
the volume of oxygen absorbed into the blood per minute.

```
air breathed per minute = 12 × 0.5 = 6.0 dm³
oxygen removed from each dm³ of air = 21% − 16% = 5% = 0.05
oxygen absorbed = 6.0 × 0.05 = 0.30 dm³ per minute
```

Exhaled air still contains a lot of oxygen, because not all the air in the lungs reaches the alveoli and
diffusion does not remove all of it.

## Mechanical ventilators (B)

A healthy person constantly breathes automatically. **Spontaneous breathing may stop due to disease or injury**,
and then the patient can be helped to breathe by a **mechanical ventilator**. There are two main types:

| | Negative pressure ventilator | Positive pressure ventilator |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Causes air to be **drawn** into the lungs | **Forces** air into the lungs |
| Where the pressure is changed | Around the outside of the chest, which is sealed in a chamber | In the air delivered to the airway |
| Pressure change | Chamber pressure lowered, so the chest expands and lung pressure falls below atmospheric | Air supplied above atmospheric pressure |
| Which natural process it is most like | Normal inhalation, where lowered pressure in the thorax draws air in | Not like normal breathing: air is pushed, not drawn |

In both types, air still moves from higher to lower pressure. When a negative pressure ventilator returns the
chamber to atmospheric pressure, the chest falls back and air leaves the lungs.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the diaphragm moves **up** when you breathe in. It flattens and moves down.
- Saying the lungs "suck" air in. Lungs have no muscle; the change in thorax volume does the work.
- Leaving out the pressure step in a breathing explanation.
- Confusing breathing with respiration.
- Saying exhaled air contains no oxygen.
- Mixing up the ventilators: **negative** draws air in, **positive** forces it in.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** inhaling or exhaling: muscles, then volume, then pressure, then air movement.
- **Explain** how alveoli are adapted: large surface area and rich blood supply, each linked to faster diffusion.
- **Evaluate** a model: one thing it shows well, at least one limitation.
- **Compare** ventilators: paired points on what changes the pressure and how air moves.
