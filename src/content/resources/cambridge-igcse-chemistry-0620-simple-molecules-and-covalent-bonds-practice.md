## Section A

**1.** Which substance contains covalent bonds?

A: potassium chloride

B: magnesium

C: hydrogen chloride

D: sodium oxide

**[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by a covalent bond. **[2]**

**3.** How many lone pairs of electrons are there on the nitrogen atom in an ammonia molecule, NH₃?

A: 0

B: 1

C: 2

D: 3

**[1]**

**4.** State two physical properties that are typical of simple molecular substances. **[2]**

**5.** *(Supplement)* State the type of covalent bond between the two atoms in a nitrogen molecule, N₂, and the number of electrons it contains. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Hydrogen chloride, HCl, is a simple molecular compound.

**(a)** Describe the dot-and-cross diagram for HCl, showing outer electrons only. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the bonding gives each atom a noble gas configuration. **[2]**

**7.** Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, is a molecule not named in the syllabus. Sulfur is in the same group as oxygen.

**(a)** Predict the number of covalent bonds sulfur forms in H₂S and the number of lone pairs on the sulfur atom. **[2]**

**(b)** State the total number of outer electrons shown in a dot-and-cross diagram of H₂S. **[1]**

**8.** *(Supplement)* Carbon dioxide, CO₂, has a carbon atom between two oxygen atoms.

**(a)** Describe the bonding between the carbon atom and each oxygen atom. **[2]**

**(b)** Show by counting that each atom in CO₂ has eight outer electrons. **[2]**

**9.** *(Supplement)* A student draws ethene, C₂H₄, with a single bond between the two carbon atoms and a lone pair on each carbon atom.

**(a)** Explain why this diagram is incorrect. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the correct bonding between the two carbon atoms. **[1]**

**10.** *(Supplement)* Methanol, CH₃OH, is a liquid at room temperature.

**(a)** State the number of single covalent bonds in one methanol molecule. **[1]**

**(b)** State the number of lone pairs on the oxygen atom. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why methanol has a low boiling point. **[2]**

**11.** The boiling points of three Group VII elements are: chlorine −34 °C; bromine 59 °C; iodine 184 °C.

**(a)** State which of these elements is a liquid at 25 °C. **[1]**

**(b)** *(Supplement)* Suggest why the boiling point increases from chlorine to iodine. **[2]**

**12.** *(Supplement)* A student writes: "When water boils, the O–H bonds break and hydrogen and oxygen gases are given off." Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[3]**

**13.** Explain why sulfur, a simple molecular solid, does not conduct electricity. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Hydrogen chloride is formed from two non-metals; the other three contain a metal.

**2.** A **pair of electrons** [1] **shared between two atoms** (giving noble gas configurations) [1].

**3.** **B** [1]. Nitrogen uses three of its five outer electrons in N–H bonds, leaving one pair.

**4.** **Low melting point and/or low boiling point** [1]; **poor electrical conductivity** [1].

**5.** A **triple bond** [1] containing **six electrons** (three shared pairs) [1].

**6.**

**(a)** **One shared pair** (one dot and one cross) between H and Cl [1]; **three lone pairs** on Cl [1]; hydrogen's electron shown as one symbol and chlorine's seven as the other symbol [1].

**(b)** Hydrogen counts the shared pair as **two electrons**, like helium [1]; chlorine has six lone-pair electrons plus the shared two, **eight**, like argon [1].

**7.**

**(a)** **Two** covalent bonds (S–H) [1]; **two** lone pairs on sulfur [1].

**(b)** **Eight** (six from S and one from each H) [1].

**8.**

**(a)** A **double covalent bond** to each oxygen [1], i.e. **two shared pairs** between C and each O [1].

**(b)** Carbon: two double bonds = **4 shared pairs = 8 electrons** [1]. Each oxygen: **2 shared pairs (4) + 2 lone pairs (4) = 8** [1].

**9.**

**(a)** The diagram uses **five** electrons from each carbon atom (one in C–C, one in each of two C–H bonds and two in the lone pair), but carbon has only **four** outer electrons [1]. Carbon forms four bonds and has **no lone pairs**, so its fourth electron must be shared with the other carbon atom [1].

**(b)** A **double bond**: two shared pairs between the carbon atoms [1].

**10.**

**(a)** **Five** (three C–H, one C–O, one O–H) [1].

**(b)** **Two** [1].

**(c)** Methanol has **weak intermolecular forces** between molecules [1], so **little energy** is needed to overcome them [1].

**11.**

**(a)** **Bromine** [1] (25 °C lies between its melting point and its boiling point of 59 °C; chlorine is above its boiling point and iodine is below its melting point).

**(b)** The molecules get **larger** [1], so the **intermolecular forces get stronger** and more energy is needed to overcome them [1].

**12.** Boiling is a **physical change**: the covalent O–H bonds **do not break** [1]. Only the **weak intermolecular forces** between water molecules are overcome [1]. The steam formed is still **H₂O molecules**, not hydrogen and oxygen [1].

**13.** Sulfur molecules are **neutral** [1]; there are **no ions or free electrons** to move and carry charge [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the covalent bonds are weak instead of the intermolecular forces.
- Missing lone pairs on oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine atoms.
- Drawing only one shared pair for double or triple bonds.
- Not using two different symbols, so the examiner cannot see which atom each electron came from.
- Answering "and similar molecules" questions from memory instead of counting electrons.
- Describing simple molecular conductivity with "no free electrons" alone, without mentioning ions.
