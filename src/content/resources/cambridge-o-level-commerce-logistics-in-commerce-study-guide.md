Goods are rarely made where they are wanted, and they are rarely wanted at the moment they are made. **Logistics** is the part of commerce that closes those gaps by organising the movement and storage of goods and the information that goes with them. This guide for Cambridge O Level Commerce explains the transport choices open to a trader, the factors that decide between them, the role of containers and warehouses, and the simple stock calculations that keep a business supplied without wasting money.

## Where this fits in the specification

In the 7100 syllabus, Topic 4 comes after Topic 3 on global trade and supply chains. Where Topic 3 asks why goods cross borders, this topic asks how they physically get there. It leads into [Aids to trade that support commerce](/resources/cambridge-o-level-commerce-aids-to-trade-that-support-commerce-study-guide/), since banking, insurance and communication support the movement of goods. The O Level assessment has a multiple-choice paper and a written paper of structured questions, and logistics questions often ask you to pick a method for a stated business and give reasons.

## Transport modes

Think about each mode under four headings: what it carries, how fast it is, what it costs and what limits it.

- **Road** uses lorries, vans and motorbikes. It is flexible and reaches the customer's door, so it suits short and medium journeys and small loads. It is affected by congestion, fuel prices and weather, and a lorry carries much less than a train or ship.
- **Rail** suits heavy, bulky goods such as coal, grain and vehicles over long distances on land. It is cost-effective for large loads and avoids road traffic, but it follows fixed lines and timetables, and goods usually need a lorry for the first and last stage.
- **Sea** moves enormous quantities at a very low cost per tonne, so it dominates international trade in bulk goods. It is slow, depends on ports and can be delayed by storms.
- **Air** is the fastest way to cross long distances and has a good safety record for fragile or valuable cargo. It is the most expensive, airports are limited in number and the loads are small.
- **Pipeline** moves oil, gas and water without interruption at low running cost, but only for liquids and gases and only along the built route.

**Containerisation** places goods in standard-sized steel boxes. The container is sealed once and then lifted by crane between ship, train and lorry. This cuts handling time, theft and damage, and speeds up the loading of ships. The business pays for the container space rather than for each individual item.

## Selecting transport

A trader should ask the following questions:

1. How urgent is the delivery, and what happens if it is late?
2. Is the cargo perishable, fragile, valuable, heavy or dangerous?
3. How far must it go, and are the roads, rails, ports or airports available at both ends?
4. How much will each option cost in total, including loading, handling and insurance?
5. How reliable and secure is the carrier?

No mode is best everywhere. Fruit that spoils within days may go by air even though it costs more, because slow transport would make it worthless. A cargo of cement can go slowly by sea because the saving outweighs the time.

## Warehouses

A **warehouse** stores goods between production and sale. It makes goods available at the right time, so that a shop can restock quickly and a farmer's harvest can be sold through the year. Warehousing is also used to:

- collect goods from several producers and combine them into larger loads;
- divide large deliveries into smaller orders for retailers (breaking bulk);
- keep goods safe from theft, damp, pests and fire;
- provide special conditions such as refrigeration or controlled temperature;
- hold imports in bond, which means customs duty is not due until the goods leave the warehouse for sale;
- pack and label goods ready for delivery.

Siting a warehouse involves a balance of land cost, road and rail links, distance from customers and suppliers, labour and space for growth.

## Controlling stock

Stock is an asset but also a cost. It occupies space, may become damaged or old, and uses up cash. Too little stock leaves customers disappointed. Common terms are:

- **Maximum level**: the highest stock the business wishes to hold, limited by space and money.
- **Buffer (minimum) level**: a safety stock for delays or unexpectedly high demand.
- **Reorder level**: the stock level at which an order is placed, equal to usage during the lead time plus the buffer.
- **Lead time**: the time from placing an order to receiving the goods.
- **Just-in-time**: delivery of materials exactly when they are needed, which lowers storage costs but depends on reliable suppliers.

Efficient delivery completes the process. Customers want goods that match the order, arrive on time and are not damaged, so businesses use protective packaging, clear labels and tracking systems.

## Worked example 1: sea or air?

An exporter has 250 cartons to send overseas. Each carton measures 0.6 m by 0.5 m by 0.4 m and weighs 8 kg. A container offers 30 cubic metres of usable space and costs $3,000 to ship. Air freight is charged at $4.50 per kg.

Step 1: volume of one carton = 0.6 x 0.5 x 0.4 = 0.12 cubic metres.
Step 2: cartons per container = 30 / 0.12 = 250, so all the cartons fill one container.
Step 3: sea cost per carton = 3,000 / 250 = $12.
Step 4: total weight = 250 x 8 = 2,000 kg.
Step 5: air cost = 2,000 x 4.50 = $9,000, which is $36 per carton (9,000 / 250).
Step 6: extra cost of air = 9,000 - 3,000 = $6,000.

Sea freight is $6,000 cheaper. Air would be justified only if the goods were urgent or perishable, for example if the buyer's own deadline would otherwise be missed.

## Worked example 2: reorder level and a delay

A shop sells 25 packs of batteries a day. Its supplier takes 8 days to deliver and the shop wants a buffer of 60 packs.

Step 1: usage in the lead time = 25 x 8 = 200 packs.
Step 2: reorder level = 200 + 60 = 260 packs.

If a delivery is 3 days late, extra sales are 25 x 3 = 75 packs. This is more than the buffer of 60, so the shop would run out for about 15 packs' worth of demand (75 - 60 = 15), about half a day of sales (15 / 25 = 0.6 of a day). The shop might raise its buffer if the supplier is often late.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that road is "fastest" in general. It is fast for short trips and door delivery, but air is fastest over long distances.
- Describing containers as a type of warehouse. They are transport and storage units, not buildings.
- Forgetting the buffer in a reorder level calculation.
- Recommending a method without referring to the goods or the customer's needs given in the question.
- Giving only advantages of a method when asked to compare or evaluate.

## Exam technique

- Read the command word. **State** wants a brief point, **describe** wants features, **explain** wants a reason, and **recommend** or **justify** wants a decision supported by reasons.
- In a case study, reuse a detail such as the goods' weight, perishability or the destination in your reasoning.
- In calculations, give the working, the unit and the currency. Check that the answer is sensible, for example that a sea cost per carton is smaller than an air cost.
- For multiple-choice questions, work out the answer before you look at the options.

## Check your understanding

1. Give two advantages of sea transport for international trade. Answer: very low cost per tonne and very large capacity for bulky cargo.
2. Why might a trader use road transport for the last part of a rail journey? Answer: rail runs only between stations, so lorries are needed to deliver to the customer's door.
3. What is breaking bulk? Answer: dividing large consignments at a warehouse into smaller lots for retailers.
4. A firm uses 30 units a day, with a 5-day lead time and a 40-unit buffer. Find its reorder level. Answer: 30 x 5 = 150, plus 40 = 190 units.
5. State one disadvantage of air freight. Answer: high cost, limited load size, or the need for airports.
6. How does containerisation reduce damage and theft? Answer: goods are sealed in one box and are not handled individually when moved between ship, train and lorry.
7. Why do businesses hold buffer stock? Answer: to avoid running out if deliveries are late or demand rises unexpectedly.
