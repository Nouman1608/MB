## 4.5.2.1 Structure and function

The nervous system **enables humans to react to their surroundings and to coordinate their behaviour**.
Information from **receptors** passes along cells called **neurones** as **electrical impulses** to the
**central nervous system (CNS)**, which is the **brain and spinal cord**. The CNS coordinates the response of
**effectors**, which may be **muscles contracting** or **glands secreting hormones**.

```
stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response
```

### How the structure suits the function

| Structure | Feature | How it suits the function |
|---|---|---|
| Receptors | Specialised for one type of stimulus (light, sound, temperature, chemicals, pressure) | Each detects one kind of change |
| Neurones | Long, thin cells | Carry impulses quickly over long distances, such as from toe to spinal cord |
| Neurones | Branched endings | Connect with many other neurones |
| Synapse | A gap between two neurones; a chemical diffuses across it | Passes the signal from one neurone to the next, in one direction |
| CNS | Brain and spinal cord with very many connections | Processes information and selects a response |
| Effectors | Muscles and glands | Bring about the response |

### The reflex arc

**Reflex actions are automatic and rapid; they do not involve the conscious part of the brain.** They are
important because they protect the body from harm faster than a thought-out response could.

1. A **receptor** detects a stimulus, for example a sharp object under the foot.
2. Impulses travel along a **sensory neurone** to the CNS.
3. At a **synapse**, a chemical diffuses across the gap and starts an impulse in a **relay neurone** in the
   spinal cord.
4. Across another synapse, the impulse passes to a **motor neurone**.
5. The motor neurone carries the impulse to the **effector**, here a muscle in the leg, which contracts and
   lifts the foot.

The conscious brain is informed, but only after the response has started. That is why you feel the pain a moment
after you have already moved.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Explain why a reflex is faster than a response that involves thinking. [2]*

> In a reflex the impulse passes through a relay neurone in the spinal cord, so there are few synapses on the
> pathway [1]. The conscious part of the brain is not involved, so no time is spent processing and deciding [1].

## Required practical 7: reaction time

The specification requires you to **plan and carry out an investigation into the effect of a factor on human
reaction time**. Common factors are caffeine, practice, distraction (such as noise) and the hand used.

**Ruler-drop method.** A partner holds a ruler vertically with zero level with your thumb and finger. They drop
it without warning, and you catch it as quickly as possible. The distance the ruler falls before you catch it is
the measure: a shorter distance means a faster reaction. A conversion table can turn distance into time.

| Variable type | Example for "the effect of caffeine on reaction time" |
|---|---|
| Independent | Whether caffeine is taken (or amount) |
| Dependent | Distance the ruler falls, or reaction time |
| Control | Same person dropping; same hand; same starting position; same time of day; same ruler |

Take several repeats, ignore anomalies and calculate a mean. People improve with practice, so give some practice
attempts before recording, or alternate conditions.

**Worked example 1** (invented data). Ruler-drop distances (cm) for one student:

| Attempt | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance (cm) | 18 | 16 | 17 | 29 | 15 |

```
Attempt 4 (29 cm) is anomalous, so leave it out.
mean = (18 + 16 + 17 + 15) ÷ 4 = 66 ÷ 4 = 16.5 cm
```

You should be able to translate reaction time data between tables and graphs. Reaction times for a group are
usually shown as a bar chart, because the independent variable is categoric.

## 4.5.2.2 The brain (Biology only)

The brain **controls complex behaviour**. It is made of **billions of interconnected neurones** and has different
regions with different functions.

| Region | Function |
|---|---|
| Cerebral cortex | Consciousness, intelligence, memory and language |
| Cerebellum | Coordination of muscular activity (such as balance) |
| Medulla | Unconscious activities such as heartbeat and breathing |

**Higher tier only.** Neuroscientists have mapped regions of the brain to particular functions by **studying
patients with brain damage**, **electrically stimulating different parts of the brain** and using **MRI scanning**.
The **complexity and delicacy** of the brain make investigating and treating brain disorders very difficult:
damage may be permanent, regions interact, and treatment can harm healthy tissue. You may be asked to evaluate
the benefits and risks of a procedure from given information.

## 4.5.2.3 The eye (Biology only)

The eye is a sense organ containing **receptors sensitive to light intensity and colour**.

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Cornea | Transparent front layer that refracts light into the eye |
| Iris | Controls the size of the pupil, and so how much light enters |
| Lens | Refracts light to focus it on the retina |
| Ciliary muscles | Change the shape of the lens |
| Suspensory ligaments | Connect the ciliary muscles to the lens |
| Retina | Contains the light receptors |
| Optic nerve | Carries impulses from the retina to the brain |
| Sclera | Tough white outer layer that protects the eye |

**Adaptation to dim light.** In dim light the pupil becomes larger, letting more light reach the retina; in bright
light it becomes smaller, protecting the retina. The iris muscles bring this about.

**Accommodation** is changing the shape of the lens to focus:

| | Near object | Distant object |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliary muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Suspensory ligaments | Loosen | Pulled tight |
| Lens | Thicker | Pulled thin |
| Refraction | Strong | Slight |

**Defects.** In **myopia** (short sightedness) and **hyperopia** (long sightedness), light rays do not focus on the
retina. In ray diagrams, myopia focuses distant objects **in front of** the retina and is corrected by a
**concave** lens; hyperopia focuses near objects **behind** the retina and is corrected by a **convex** lens.
Spectacles refract the rays so they focus on the retina. Newer options include **hard and soft contact lenses**,
**laser surgery to change the shape of the cornea**, and a **replacement lens** in the eye.

## 4.5.2.4 Control of body temperature (Biology only)

The **thermoregulatory centre** in the brain monitors and controls body temperature. It contains **receptors
sensitive to the temperature of the blood**. The **skin** also contains temperature receptors, which send
impulses to the thermoregulatory centre.

| Too hot | Too cold |
|---|---|
| Blood vessels dilate (vasodilation) | Blood vessels constrict (vasoconstriction) |
| Sweat produced by sweat glands | Sweating stops |
| | Skeletal muscles contract (shiver) |

**Higher tier only: explaining the mechanisms.** Vasodilation sends more blood near the skin surface, so more
energy is transferred from the skin to the environment. Sweat evaporating from the skin transfers energy away.
Vasoconstriction reduces blood flow near the surface, so less energy is lost. Shivering needs respiration, which
transfers energy that warms the body.

## Common mistakes

- Saying impulses "jump" across a synapse; a chemical diffuses across it.
- Saying a reflex goes "to the brain". In the arc above, the relay neurone is in the spinal cord.
- Swapping the cerebellum (coordination) and cerebral cortex (consciousness, memory, language).
- Writing "the ciliary muscles relax for near objects". They contract.
- Saying blood vessels "move" closer to the skin. They dilate; they do not move.
- Correcting myopia with a convex lens.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Describe** a reflex arc: name every neurone and synapse in order.
- **Explain** a structure: link the feature to the function it allows.
- **Plan** (practical 7): independent, dependent and control variables, repeats and a mean.
- **Evaluate** a brain procedure (Higher tier only): weigh benefits against risks from the information given.
