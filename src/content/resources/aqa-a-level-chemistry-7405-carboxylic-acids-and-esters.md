## Structures and names

A **carboxylic acid** contains the carboxyl group, –COOH. An **ester** contains –COO– joined to carbon on both sides, written RCOOR′.

```
        O                       O
        ‖                       ‖
CH₃CH₂–C–OH             CH₃CH₂–C–O–CH₃
 propanoic acid           methyl propanoate
```

### Naming esters

An ester name has two words. The first comes from the **alcohol** part (the group on the oxygen); the second comes from the **acid** part.

| Acid part | Alcohol part | Ester name | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethanoic acid | Methanol | Methyl ethanoate | CH₃COOCH₃ |
| Ethanoic acid | Propan-1-ol | Propyl ethanoate | CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| Methanoic acid | Ethanol | Ethyl methanoate | HCOOCH₂CH₃ |
| Butanoic acid | Ethanol | Ethyl butanoate | CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ |

To avoid reversing the parts, remember that the **carbonyl carbon** belongs to the acid and the alkyl group on the single-bonded oxygen belongs to the alcohol. Propyl ethanoate and ethyl propanoate are different compounds with the same formula, C₅H₁₀O₂.

## Carboxylic acids as weak acids

In water a carboxylic acid partially dissociates:

```
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
```

The equilibrium lies well to the left, so the acid is **weak**. Even so, it is acidic enough to react with carbonates and hydrogencarbonates, which only react with acids:

```
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
```

Effervescence is observed. This is the standard test to separate a carboxylic acid from an alcohol or an ester, which do not react.

## Esterification

A carboxylic acid and an alcohol react, in the presence of an **acid catalyst**, to give an ester and water.

```
CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O
```

- The catalyst is a few drops of **concentrated sulfuric acid**.
- The mixture is heated under **reflux**, so vapours condense and return.
- The reaction is **reversible**, so a mixture of all four substances forms. Using an excess of one reactant, or removing the ester as it forms, increases the yield of ester.

### Purifying the ester (the practical)

1. Distil the mixture and collect the fraction in the boiling range of the ester. The ester has a lower boiling point than the acid and the alcohol because its molecules cannot hydrogen bond to each other.
2. Shake the distillate with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a **separating funnel**, releasing the pressure often. This removes acid, shown by fizzing.
3. Run off the lower aqueous layer. The ester forms the upper layer (it is less dense than water).
4. Dry the ester with anhydrous calcium chloride or anhydrous sodium sulfate.
5. Redistil and collect the fraction at the boiling point of the ester.

## Uses of esters

| Use | Reason |
|---|---|
| Solvents | They dissolve many organic compounds and evaporate easily |
| Plasticisers | Small molecules between polymer chains make them flexible |
| Perfumes | Many esters have pleasant smells |
| Food flavourings | Fruity smells and tastes, for example in drinks and sweets |

## Hydrolysis of esters

Hydrolysis is the breakdown of an ester by water, usually with an acid or an alkali.

**Acid hydrolysis** (dilute acid, reflux) is the reverse of esterification and is reversible. It gives a carboxylic acid and an alcohol:

```
CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH
```

**Alkaline hydrolysis** (aqueous sodium hydroxide, reflux) goes to completion. It gives the **salt of the carboxylic acid** and the alcohol:

```
CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + CH₃CH₂OH
```

Alkaline hydrolysis is irreversible because the carboxylate ion is not attacked by the alcohol. To obtain the free carboxylic acid afterwards, acidify the mixture with a strong acid.

## Fats, oils, soap and glycerol

Vegetable oils and animal fats are **esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol)** with three long-chain carboxylic acids. Each of the three –OH groups of glycerol has formed an ester link.

```
CH₂–O–CO–R
|
CH –O–CO–R′
|
CH₂–O–CO–R″
```

Heating with aqueous sodium hydroxide hydrolyses all three ester links:

```
fat or oil + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3 RCOONa
```

The salts of long-chain carboxylic acids are **soap**. The glycerol is a by-product. The skills column suggests that students could make soap.

## Biodiesel

Biodiesel is a mixture of **methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids**. It is made by reacting a vegetable oil with methanol in the presence of a catalyst:

```
oil (triester of glycerol) + 3CH₃OH → glycerol + 3 RCOOCH₃
```

The ester links of the oil are exchanged: glycerol is released, and each long-chain acid group ends up joined to a methyl group. Biodiesel can be burned in diesel engines. The skills column suggests that students could make biodiesel.

## Worked example 1: naming and equation

**Question.** Write an equation for the formation of propyl butanoate and name the reactants.

The acid part is butanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH, and the alcohol part is propan-1-ol.

```
CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + H₂O
```

Conditions: a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, heat under reflux.

## Worked example 2: percentage yield of an ester

6.00 g of ethanoic acid (Mᵣ = 60.0) is heated under reflux with excess ethanol and a catalyst. 5.28 g of ethyl ethanoate (Mᵣ = 88.0) is obtained.

```
n(CH₃COOH)           = 6.00 ÷ 60.0 = 0.100 mol
theoretical n(ester) = 0.100 mol
theoretical mass     = 0.100 × 88.0 = 8.80 g
percentage yield     = 5.28 ÷ 8.80 × 100 = 60.0 %
```

The yield is well below 100 % because the equilibrium means not all the acid is converted, and because some ester is lost in purification.

## Worked example 3: making soap

89.0 g of glyceryl tristearate, (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ (Mᵣ = 890.0) is heated with excess sodium hydroxide. Calculate the mass of sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COONa (Mᵣ = 306.0), and of glycerol (Mᵣ = 92.0) formed.

```
(C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → C₃H₅(OH)₃ + 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa
n(fat)           = 89.0 ÷ 890.0 = 0.100 mol
n(soap)          = 3 × 0.100 = 0.300 mol
mass of soap     = 0.300 × 306.0 = 91.8 g
n(glycerol)      = 0.100 mol, mass = 0.100 × 92.0 = 9.20 g
```

Check using conservation of mass: 89.0 g of fat plus 0.300 mol × 40.0 = 12.0 g of NaOH gives 101.0 g, and 91.8 g + 9.20 g = 101.0 g.

## Worked example 4: identifying hydrolysis products

**Question.** Ethyl propanoate is heated under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid. Name the products.

The ester has the acid part propanoate and the alcohol part ethyl. Acid hydrolysis gives **propanoic acid** and **ethanol**. In alkaline hydrolysis with sodium hydroxide, the products would be sodium propanoate and ethanol.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the ester name the wrong way round (alcohol part second).
- Forgetting water as a product of esterification, or drawing a one-way arrow for a reversible reaction.
- Giving the product of alkaline hydrolysis as the free acid instead of its salt.
- Writing the catalyst as dilute sulfuric acid rather than concentrated.
- Confusing a carboxylic acid with an ester when interpreting the carbonate test.
- Stating that soap is made from glycerol. Soap is the sodium or potassium salt of the long-chain acids.
- Counting only one mole of NaOH per mole of fat when hydrolysing a triglyceride.
- Describing biodiesel as a hydrocarbon. It is a mixture of esters.

## Exam technique

- **Name an ester**: write the alkyl group of the alcohol first, then the acid name ending -oate.
- **Esterification**: reagents, concentrated sulfuric acid, reflux, reversible arrow, water as a product.
- **Hydrolysis**: state acid or alkaline, and give the correct products (acid and alcohol, or salt and alcohol).
- **Fats and oils**: draw the triester, and write three moles of NaOH per mole of fat.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the ester the upper layer in a separating funnel?**
It is less dense than water and does not mix with it.

**Why is sodium hydrogencarbonate used rather than sodium hydroxide to wash the ester?**
Sodium hydroxide would hydrolyse the ester. Hydrogencarbonate is a mild base that removes the acid without attacking the ester.

**Why is alkaline hydrolysis preferred for making soap?**
It goes to completion, and it produces the sodium salt directly.
