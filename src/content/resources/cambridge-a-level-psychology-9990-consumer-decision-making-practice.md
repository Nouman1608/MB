**This practice set covers topic 2.3, Consumer decision-making, in Specialist Option 2 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short-answer questions and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style questions that apply the topic and research methods to scenarios. All scenarios, shops and people are fictional.

Related: [Consumer decision-making study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-consumer-decision-making/) · [Consumer decision-making revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-consumer-decision-making-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline prospect theory as a model of consumer decision-making. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a compensatory and a non-compensatory decision-making strategy. Use an example of buying a bicycle. **[4]**

**3.** Outline the take-the-best heuristic and the recognition heuristic. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by "system 1" thinking, using a consumer example. **[2]**

**5.** Hall et al. (2010) studied choice blindness.

**(a)** State the hypothesis of a study like this, written as a testable prediction. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the results may be reliable. **[2]**

**6.** Rival adverts can affect how well consumers remember an advert.

**(a)** Describe research into consumer memory for advertising, including retroactive and proactive interference. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate research into mistakes in consumer decision-making, including a discussion of determinism versus free will. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**7.** Greenfield Market puts up the sign "Pasta sauce: 3 jars for £4. Maximum 9 per customer."

**(a)** Explain how the sign could increase the number of jars bought, using the anchoring heuristic. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** way the shop could test whether the sign works, naming the independent and dependent variables. **[3]**

**8.** An online shoe shop finds that customers leave the site without buying. A psychologist advises that the site should suit the decision strategy its customers use.

**(a)** Suggest **one** website feature that would help customers using a non-compensatory strategy and **one** that would help customers using a compensatory strategy. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **one** weakness of generalising research on website design to all shoppers. **[2]**

**9.** Aisha books the first holiday cottage she finds that has two bedrooms, a garden and a price under her budget.

**(a)** Identify the model of decision-making that Aisha is using. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why utility theory would predict a different way of choosing. **[2]**

**10.** A researcher replicates Hall et al. (2010) with chocolate at a market stall. She wants to find out how participants decided, so she interviews them after each trial.

**(a)** Suggest **one** open question she could ask. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of collecting qualitative data in this study. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain how she should deal with the ethical issue of deception. **[2]**

**11.** A phone shop labels a handset "Was £600, now £450". Using **two** concepts from consumer decision-making, explain why a customer may judge £450 to be good value. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 3 marks. Outcomes are judged as gains or losses relative to a reference point, not as final amounts (1). Losses are felt more strongly than equal gains, which is loss aversion (1). Credit any one further point, such as sensitivity decreasing as amounts grow, or the same offer being chosen differently when framed as a gain or a loss, e.g. "save £20" versus "avoid losing £20" (1).

**2.** Up to 4 marks. A compensatory strategy scores every attribute and combines the scores, so a weakness can be offset by a strength (1); e.g. a heavy bicycle may still be chosen because it is cheap and has good gears (1). A non-compensatory strategy rejects any option that fails on a key attribute, whatever its other strengths (1); e.g. any bicycle without a light frame is ruled out, even if it is cheaper (1).

**3.** Two marks for each heuristic. Take-the-best: the consumer compares options on the single most important cue (1) and, if that cue separates them, chooses on it without considering any other information, e.g. choosing the café nearest the station (1). Recognition: if one option is recognised and the other is not (1), the recognised option is judged better and chosen, e.g. choosing a well-known brand of headphones (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. System 1 is fast, automatic and intuitive and needs little effort (1). Example: grabbing a product with a large "SALE" sign without checking whether it is cheaper than a rival product (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. One mark for the variables and one for direction, e.g. "Fewer than half of the trials in which the chosen jam is secretly switched will be detected by participants" (2). A vague statement such as "people will not notice" gets 1.

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. The procedure was standardised: every participant used the same type of double-ended container and the same sequence of tasting, choosing and explaining (1), so the study could be repeated in the same way and give consistent results (1). Credit using two raters who agree on what counts as a detection, if explained.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Burke and Srull (1988) studied memory for advertising (1). Participants saw adverts for brands, with or without adverts for competing brands from the same product category (1). Memory for brand information was worse when competing adverts were also seen (1). Retroactive interference: later adverts disrupt memory of an earlier advert (1). Proactive interference: earlier adverts disrupt learning of a later advert (1). Interference occurs because similar information competes in memory, so the effect is strongest for direct competitors (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for detailed, balanced evaluation with the named debate and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Determinism versus free will*: choice blindness and interference suggest choices and memories are shaped by factors consumers are unaware of, which is deterministic; yet some participants in Hall et al. did detect switches, so people are not wholly controlled by the situation.
- *Validity*: Hall et al. is high in ecological validity; laboratory studies of advert memory use artificial exposure that may not reflect how people watch adverts at home.
- *Reliability*: standardised procedures allow replication.
- *Application*: advertisers can avoid placing adverts next to competitors; retailers can use system 1 cues.
- *Ethics*: deception in Hall et al.; manipulation of consumers raises ethical questions.
- *Individual and situational explanations*: errors depend on time pressure and the setting but also on individual decision styles.
- *Conclusion*: strong evidence that consumer decisions are error-prone, though the size of the effects varies.

**7.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. "Maximum 9" acts as an anchor (1). Shoppers start from that number and adjust down when deciding how many to buy (1), but adjust too little, so they buy more than they would without the sign; Wansink et al. (1998) found purchase limits and multiple unit pricing increase quantity bought (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Run the sign in some weeks and a plain price label in matched weeks (1). IV: type of sign, anchor or plain (1). DV: number of jars sold per customer, from till data (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Two marks per feature. Non-compensatory: filters that remove all shoes failing a requirement, such as size or a price limit (1), so the shopper can cut off unsuitable options quickly (1). Compensatory: a comparison table showing ratings for several shoes on every feature side by side (1), so the shopper can weigh strengths against weaknesses (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Shoppers differ in which strategy they prefer, for example by experience or decision style (1), so one design may suit some customers and not others; findings are nomothetic and may not fit individuals (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Satisficing (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Utility theory assumes a rational consumer who compares all the options (1) and chooses the one with the greatest value, not the first acceptable one (1).

**10.**

**(a)** One mark for a genuinely open question, e.g. "Why did you prefer that chocolate?" (1).

**(b)** Strength, up to 2: answers show how participants justify their choices in their own words (1), which reveals whether they construct reasons for an option they did not choose (1). Weakness, up to 2: answers are harder to analyse and compare objectively (1), and the researcher's interpretation may be biased, reducing reliability (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. After the trial, participants should be fully debriefed, told about the switch and the aim (1), and offered the right to withdraw their data (1).

**11.** Two marks per concept, applied. Anchoring: £600 is the starting value (1), so £450 is judged relative to it and seems cheap (1). Prospect theory or framing: the reference point is £600, so paying £450 is coded as a gain of £150 (1), which feels attractive even if the phone was never really worth £600 (1). Credit system 1 thinking if applied.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 2, giving examples that are not about the bicycle, or examples that do not show the trade-off.
- In question 7, saying "the shop makes it seem cheap" without naming the anchor.
- In question 10, giving a closed question such as "Did you notice the switch?"
