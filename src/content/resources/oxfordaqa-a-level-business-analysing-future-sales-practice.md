## Questions

**1.** Define the term extrapolation, as used in sales forecasting. **[2]**

**2.** Velloway Gym sold the following numbers of new memberships in five consecutive months: 84, 90, 99, 93, 108. Calculate the three period moving averages. **[3]**

**3.** Nimbrel Pet Supplies has calculated three period moving averages of its monthly unit sales. They are placed against months 2 to 6: 2,140; 2,180; 2,205; 2,250; 2,300. Its actual sales data runs to month 7. Calculate the sales forecast for month 8. **[4]**

**4.** State **two** advantages of qualitative sales forecasting. **[2]**

**5.** Ostravel Tiles plans to forecast sales of a new range of outdoor tiles by asking its sales representatives for estimates. Explain **one** disadvantage of this approach for Ostravel. **[3]**

**6.** Glasswell Bikes forecast sales of 1,250 bikes for the spring quarter. It actually sold 1,175.

**(a)** Calculate the forecast error as a percentage of the forecast. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** possible reason for the error. **[2]**

**7.** Larchfold Ceramics received the following orders in months 1 to 6: 1,530; 1,590; 1,620; 1,665; 1,650; 1,731. It can produce 1,760 orders a month. Using three period moving averages, forecast orders for month 8 and state whether Larchfold has enough capacity. **[5]**

**8.** Quennell Water supplies bottled water to offices, a market with stable demand. Explain **two** reasons why extrapolation may be of value to Quennell. **[4]**

**9.** Marrowdene Festivals runs outdoor music events. Explain **two** difficulties it may face in forecasting ticket sales. **[4]**

**10.** Tilsbury Kitchens has forecast a 20% rise in orders for fitted kitchens next year. Analyse how this forecast could affect decisions in its human resources and finance functions. **[6]**

**11.** Fothergale Organics delivers boxes of organic vegetables. Its weekly deliveries for weeks 1 to 7 were: 312, 327, 333, 330, 348, 357, 351. Its packing shed can handle 380 boxes a week. The owner, Morwenna Fothergale, is considering leasing a second packing site for three years. A national supermarket chain has announced it will launch its own vegetable box scheme in the region next year. A survey of Fothergale's customers found that most would recommend the service to friends.

**(a)** Calculate the three period moving averages placed against week 2 and week 6. Use them to forecast deliveries for week 9. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Morwenna should rely on this forecast when deciding whether to lease the second packing site. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Projecting a past trend in sales into the future [1], on the assumption that the past pattern will continue [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The assumption that the pattern continues is the part that gains the second mark; "using past data" alone describes most forecasting.

**2.** (84 + 90 + 99) ÷ 3 = **91** [1]; (90 + 99 + 93) ÷ 3 = **94** [1]; (99 + 93 + 108) ÷ 3 = **100** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each three-month total; if one addition slips, visible working makes it clear the other averages are right.

**3.** Total change = 2,300 − 2,140 = 160 [1]. Average change = 160 ÷ 4 = 40 per month [1]. Month 8 is 2 months after month 6 [1]. Forecast = 2,300 + (2 × 40) = **2,380 units** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Five averages have four gaps; dividing by 5 is a common slip, and it changes the final figure.

**4.** Any two: it can be used where there is no past data, such as for a new product [1]; it can take account of expected changes that past figures cannot show, or it draws on the knowledge of staff close to customers [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct points are needed; "uses opinions" and "uses judgement" are the same idea.

**5.** Representatives may be paid a bonus for beating targets [1], so they may give low estimates to make targets easier to reach [1]. Ostravel could then under-produce the new range and lose sales in its first summer [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark comes from the consequence for Ostravel; a general bias point with no effect on the business stops at two.

**6. (a)** (1,175 − 1,250) ÷ 1,250 × 100 [1] = **−6%** [1].
**(b)** A wet spring may have reduced demand for leisure cycling [1], so actual sales fell below a forecast built on past trends that did not expect it [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign or say "6% below forecast"; a bare "6%" leaves the direction unclear.

**7.** Moving averages: 4,740 ÷ 3 = 1,580; 4,875 ÷ 3 = 1,625; 4,935 ÷ 3 = 1,645; 5,046 ÷ 3 = 1,682 [1]. Average change = (1,682 − 1,580) ÷ 3 = 34 [1]. Month 8 is 3 months after month 5 [1]. Forecast = 1,682 + (3 × 34) = **1,784 orders** [1]. This is 24 above capacity of 1,760, so Larchfold **does not have enough capacity** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The last average sits against month 5, not month 6; counting two months ahead gives 1,750 and the wrong capacity conclusion.

**8.** Demand is stable, so the past trend is likely to continue [1], which makes an extrapolated forecast reasonably reliable for planning deliveries and stock [1]. It is quick and cheap because Quennell already holds its sales records [1], so managers can update the forecast often without paying for research [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the value to Quennell's stable market is what lifts this above a generic list.

**9.** Ticket sales depend heavily on the weather forecast close to each event [1], which cannot be known when the forecast is made months ahead [1]. Each festival may feature different headline acts, so past ticket sales are a weak guide to future events [1], making extrapolation unreliable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate difficulties, each explained in this context, are needed for full credit.

**10.** HR: Tilsbury may need more fitters and designers [1], so it must recruit and train early because skilled fitters take time to find [1]. If the forecast proves too high, it may have to make staff redundant, so it may use temporary contracts or overtime first [1]. Finance: the forecast feeds the budget and cash flow forecast [1]. More orders mean buying materials and paying wages before customers pay, so Tilsbury may need an overdraft or loan [1]. However, a 20% rise is large; finance may plan for a lower figure to protect cash if orders fall short [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis credit comes from chains of cause and effect in each function; listing "recruit staff, get a loan" without the links earns little.

**11. (a)** Week 2: (312 + 327 + 333) ÷ 3 = 972 ÷ 3 = 324 [1]. Week 6: (348 + 357 + 351) ÷ 3 = 1,056 ÷ 3 = 352 [1]. Average change = (352 − 324) ÷ 4 = 7 boxes a week [1]. Week 9 is 3 weeks after week 6: 352 + (3 × 7) = **373 boxes** [1].
**(b)** The forecast suggests a steady upward trend of about 7 boxes a week [1]. At that rate deliveries reach 380 by week 10, so the shed will soon be at full capacity [1], and without more space Morwenna would have to turn customers away [1]. The survey supports continued growth because satisfied customers recommend the service [1]. However, the forecast rests on only seven weeks of data [1], which may reflect a seasonal peak rather than long-term growth [1]. Extrapolating seven weeks of data across a three-year lease is a large step, and reliability falls the further ahead it goes [1]. The supermarket's scheme is exactly the kind of external change extrapolation cannot see [1]; with its buying power and wide reach it could take customers and reverse the trend [1]. A three-year lease is hard to reverse and adds fixed costs [1], so an over-forecast could leave Fothergale paying for unused space [1]. Judgement: Morwenna should not rely on this forecast alone; she should gather a longer run of data or consider short-term options, such as extra shifts, until the effect of the supermarket's scheme is clear [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluations use the calculated forecast and the case details (lease length, new rival, short data run) to reach a supported decision, rather than discussing forecasting in general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a moving average against the third period instead of the middle period.
- Dividing the total change by the number of moving averages instead of the gaps between them.
- Counting periods ahead from the last actual figure instead of the last moving average.
- Dropping the sign on a forecast error, or not saying whether sales were above or below forecast.
- Leaving units off a forecast, or giving a fractional number of products.
- Explaining difficulties of forecasting in general with no link to the business in the case.
- Treating a forecast as a certainty when evaluating a large, hard-to-reverse decision.
- Ending an evaluation without a clear, justified judgement.
