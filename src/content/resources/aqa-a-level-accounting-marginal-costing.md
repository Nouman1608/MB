## 1. Costs by behaviour and the key terms

Costs are grouped in two separate ways: can the cost be traced to a unit, and how does it behave as
output changes?

**Traced to the product or not**

- **Direct costs** can be traced directly to each unit made: direct materials, direct labour and any
  direct expenses (for example a royalty paid per unit).
- **Indirect costs** (overheads) cannot be traced to a single unit: factory rent, a supervisor's salary,
  insurance.

**Behaviour as output changes (within the relevant range)**

- **Variable costs** rise in total in direct proportion to output. The cost per unit stays the same.
- **Fixed costs** stay the same in total whatever the output, within a period and a range of activity.
  Fixed cost per unit falls as output rises.
- **Semi-variable costs** have a fixed element and a variable element, such as electricity with a
  standing charge plus a charge per unit used.
- **Stepped costs** are fixed over a range of output, then jump to a new level when a capacity limit is
  passed, such as a second supervisor once output exceeds what one can oversee.

**Marginal cost** is the extra cost of making one more unit. In practice it is the variable cost per
unit: direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses + variable overheads.

**Contribution** is selling price minus marginal cost. Contribution per unit = selling price per unit
− variable cost per unit. Total contribution = sales revenue − total variable costs, or contribution
per unit × units sold. Contribution first covers fixed costs; after that, every extra pound is profit.

**Break-even** is the level of output or sales at which total contribution exactly equals fixed costs,
so there is neither profit nor loss.

### Worked example 1: classifying costs

Fernhill Joinery makes garden benches.

| Cost | Direct or indirect? | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Timber, £38 per bench | Direct | Variable |
| Joiners paid £22 per bench completed | Direct | Variable |
| Workshop rent, £18 000 a year | Indirect | Fixed |
| Electricity: £2 400 a year standing charge + £1.50 per bench | Indirect | Semi-variable |
| Supervisor, £28 000 a year; one supervisor per 2 500 benches | Indirect | Stepped |

At 2 000 benches, electricity = 2 400 + (2 000 × 1.50) = **£5 400**. At 2 500 benches one supervisor is
enough (£28 000); at 2 501 a second is needed and the cost steps up to **£56 000**.

Each bench sells for £110. Variable costs are timber £38, labour £22 and variable overheads £6
(including the electricity variable element). Total fixed costs are £61 600 a year.

- Marginal cost per bench = 38 + 22 + 6 = **£66**
- Contribution per bench = 110 − 66 = **£44**

## 2. Break-even analysis

### Break-even by calculation

```
Break-even point (units) = total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Break-even revenue      = break-even units × selling price
                        or total fixed costs ÷ C/S ratio
C/S ratio               = contribution per unit ÷ selling price per unit
Units for target profit = (total fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit
```

### Worked example 2: Fernhill Joinery

1. Break-even = 61 600 ÷ 44 = **1 400 benches**.
2. Break-even revenue = 1 400 × 110 = **£154 000**. Check: C/S ratio = 44 ÷ 110 = 0.4, and
   61 600 ÷ 0.4 = £154 000.
3. Budgeted sales are 2 000 benches. Profit = (2 000 × 44) − 61 600 = **£26 400**.
4. The gap between budgeted sales and break-even is often called the margin of safety:
   2 000 − 1 400 = **600 benches**, or 600 ÷ 2 000 = **30%** of budgeted sales. Sales could fall by 30%
   before a loss is made.
5. To earn a target profit of £44 000: (61 600 + 44 000) ÷ 44 = **2 400 benches**. This is still within
   one supervisor's range, so fixed costs do not step up.

Always check a target output against stepped costs. Had the target needed more than 2 500 benches, the
extra supervisor's salary would have to be added to fixed costs before dividing.

### Break-even charts

A break-even chart plots output (units) on the horizontal axis and costs and revenue (£) on the vertical
axis. It has three lines:

- **fixed costs**: horizontal, at £61 600
- **total costs**: starts at £61 600 on the vertical axis and rises by £66 per unit
- **sales revenue**: starts at the origin and rises by £110 per unit.

The figures for Fernhill's chart:

| Benches | Fixed costs £ | Total costs £ | Revenue £ | Profit/(loss) £ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 61 600 | 61 600 | 0 | (61 600) |
| 1 000 | 61 600 | 127 600 | 110 000 | (17 600) |
| 1 400 | 61 600 | 154 000 | 154 000 | 0 |
| 2 000 | 61 600 | 193 600 | 220 000 | 26 400 |
| 2 500 | 61 600 | 226 600 | 275 000 | 48 400 |

**Interpreting the chart.** Break-even is where the revenue line crosses the total cost line (1 400
benches, £154 000). The gap between the lines to the left of that point is the loss; to the right it is
the profit. The vertical distance between the two lines at any output is the profit or loss there. A steeper revenue line (higher price) or a flatter total cost
line (lower variable cost) moves break-even to the left.

### Uses and limitations of break-even analysis

**Uses**

- shows the minimum sales needed to avoid a loss
- shows the effect of "what if" changes in price, variable cost or fixed cost
- gives the output needed for a target profit
- a chart shows the position clearly to non-accountants.

**Limitations**

- assumes all costs can be split cleanly into fixed and variable; semi-variable costs make this hard
- assumes a single selling price and constant variable cost per unit at every output, ignoring bulk
  discounts and price cuts needed to sell more
- assumes fixed costs are constant, which ignores stepped costs outside the relevant range
- assumes everything produced is sold, with no change in inventory
- is harder to apply with several products, because the result depends on the sales mix
- relies on estimates, so it is only as reliable as the forecasts.

## 3. Marginal costing in decision making

The principle is the same in every situation: **compare the change in contribution with any change in
fixed costs.** Fixed costs that continue whatever the decision are not relevant. Then weigh
non-financial factors before recommending.

### Make or buy

Fernhill makes its own bench brackets: direct materials £4.20, direct labour £3.10 and variable
overheads £0.90, plus £2.50 of fixed overheads absorbed per bracket (full cost £10.70). A supplier
offers brackets at £9.40. Fernhill uses 12 000 a year.

- Marginal cost of making = 4.20 + 3.10 + 0.90 = **£8.20**
- The £2.50 of fixed overheads will still be paid if Fernhill buys, so it is not relevant.
- Buying costs 9.40 − 8.20 = £1.20 more per bracket: 12 000 × 1.20 = **£14 400** less profit.

Decision on financial grounds: **keep making**, unless freed capacity could earn over £14 400 of
contribution or buying avoided some fixed costs. Non-financial factors: the
supplier's quality and reliability, dependence on one supplier, and lost in-house skills and jobs.

### Acceptance of additional work

Fernhill has spare capacity. A garden centre offers a one-off order for 500 benches at £80 each, below
the normal £110.

- Extra contribution = 500 × (80 − 66) = **£7 000**. Fixed costs are already covered by normal sales.
- If a special jig costing £2 500 were needed, the gain would be 7 000 − 2 500 = **£4 500**.

Accept on financial grounds if there is spare capacity. Non-financial factors: regular customers may
demand the same lower price; the order may bring repeat business.

### Price setting

In the short run, any price above marginal cost adds contribution. In the long run, prices must cover
fixed costs and give a profit. A price can be built up from marginal cost plus the contribution needed.

Fernhill expects to sell 1 800 benches next year and wants a profit of £28 400.

- Contribution needed = 61 600 + 28 400 = £90 000
- Contribution per bench = 90 000 ÷ 1 800 = £50
- Price = 66 + 50 = **£116**

Competitors' prices and likely demand must be checked before using it.

### Optimum use of scarce resources

When one input (the **limiting factor**) is in short supply, rank products by **contribution per unit
of the limiting factor**, not by contribution per unit.

Kestrel Kitchens has 4 000 skilled labour hours. Fixed costs are £30 000.

| Product | Price £ | Variable cost £ | Contribution £ | Hours per unit | Contribution per hour £ | Maximum demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 60 | 36 | 24 | 2 | 12 | 800 |
| B | 90 | 54 | 36 | 4 | 9 | 600 |
| C | 45 | 30 | 15 | 1 | 15 | 1 000 |

Rank: C, then A, then B.

| Product | Units | Hours used | Contribution £ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 1 000 | 1 000 | 15 000 |
| A | 800 | 1 600 | 19 200 |
| B | 350 (1 400 hours ÷ 4) | 1 400 | 12 600 |
| Total | | 4 000 | 46 800 |

Maximum profit = 46 800 − 30 000 = **£16 800**. Product B has the highest contribution per unit but uses
the scarce hours least well. Non-financial factors: B's customers may go elsewhere and take other
business with them.

### Closing a potentially loss-making line or department

A department may show a loss only because general fixed costs are apportioned to it. Ask what would
actually change if it closed.

Hollin Stores, statement for the year:

| | Garden £ | Home £ | Gifts £ | Total £ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 180 000 | 120 000 | 60 000 | 360 000 |
| Variable costs | 99 000 | 66 000 | 42 000 | 207 000 |
| Contribution | 81 000 | 54 000 | 18 000 | 153 000 |
| Fixed costs apportioned | 58 500 | 39 000 | 19 500 | 117 000 |
| Profit/(loss) | 22 500 | 15 000 | (1 500) | 36 000 |

Only £6 000 of the fixed costs charged to Gifts would be saved if it closed. Closing loses £18 000 of
contribution and saves £6 000, so profit falls by **£12 000**, from £36 000 to £24 000.

Keep Gifts open unless its space could earn more than £12 000 of extra contribution. Non-financial
factors: redundancies, staff morale, and gift customers who also buy in other departments.

### Target profit

Use (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit, as in worked example 2 (2 400 benches for
£44 000).

## Common errors

- Including absorbed fixed overheads in the marginal cost in make-or-buy or special-order questions.
- Ranking by contribution per unit when a limiting factor is given.
- Using apportioned fixed costs as if closing a department would save them all.
- Rounding a break-even or target figure down: if the formula gives 1 812.5 units, you need 1 813 to
  reach the target.
- Recommending with no non-financial factors when asked to evaluate.
- Ignoring a stepped cost when output moves past a capacity limit.
