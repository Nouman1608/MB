This guide covers section 7.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus requires a case study: "candidates must study some of the threats to (exploitation) and problems of sustainable management of areas within either the rainforest ecosystem or the savanna ecosystem and evaluate attempted solutions." It completes Topic 7, Tropical environments, in Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options, where the management element is synoptic: it combines physical and human geography.

The [Tropical environments study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-tropical-environments/) works through the Brazilian Amazon. This guide gives you the framework to build and evaluate any case study, and two further examples: payments for forest protection in Costa Rica (rainforest) and community wildlife management in Zimbabwe and Kenya (savanna). Your teacher will confirm which case study your class uses; you need only one ecosystem, studied in depth. Condense it with the [7.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-management-of-tropical-environments-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [7.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-management-of-tropical-environments-practice/).

## What the case study needs

| Element | What to include |
|---|---|
| Area | A named, located area within one ecosystem |
| Threats (exploitation) | Who uses the area, how, and why: farming, ranching, logging, mining, roads, fuelwood, poaching, tourism |
| Problems of sustainable management | Why it is hard to use the area without degrading it: physical fragility, poverty, land rights, weak enforcement, conflicting interests |
| Attempted solutions | Named schemes, with dates and the groups involved |
| Evaluation | Evidence of success and failure, and a judgement |

**Sustainable management** means using an area to meet present needs without reducing its ability to meet future needs. It has environmental, economic and social dimensions: a scheme that protects forest but impoverishes local people is not sustainable, and neither is one that raises incomes by clearing forest.

## Why tropical ecosystems are hard to manage sustainably

**Physical fragility (link to 7.3).**

- Rainforest nutrients are held in the biomass, so clearance removes the main store and soils lose fertility within a few years.
- Heavy rain on bare soil causes leaching, soil erosion and sometimes laterite crusts.
- Rainforest recovers slowly; secondary forest takes decades to regain its structure, and much longer for its species richness.
- Savanna is adapted to fire and grazing, but changes in their frequency shift the balance: too much grazing and burning degrades soil and grass, while fire suppression allows bush encroachment.
- Savanna rainfall is variable, so stocking levels that are safe in a wet year cause overgrazing in a drought.

**Human problems.**

- **Poverty and population growth** make short-term clearance or hunting rational for households.
- **Insecure land rights** give people little reason to invest in long-term care of land.
- **Global demand** for beef, soy, palm oil, timber and minerals rewards conversion.
- **Weak enforcement** in remote areas, and corruption, limit the effect of laws.
- **Conflicting interests** between governments, companies, farmers, indigenous peoples and conservationists.
- **Wildlife conflict** in savannas, where elephants and predators damage crops and kill livestock.

## Example 1 (rainforest): payments for environmental services in Costa Rica

**Context.** Costa Rica lost much of its forest to cattle pasture in the decades after 1950. By the 1980s forest covered only around a fifth of the country.

**The solution.** A forestry law of 1996 banned the conversion of forest to other land uses and set up a national programme of **payments for environmental services**, which began in 1997. Through a national fund (FONAFIFO), landowners are paid per hectare per year to protect forest, plant trees or adopt agroforestry. Payments recognise four services: carbon storage, watershed protection, biodiversity and scenic beauty for tourism. A share of the national fuel tax pays for the programme, with support from water users and international partners.

**Evaluation.**

- *Success:* forest cover has recovered to over half the land area, and Costa Rica is often cited as a country that reversed deforestation. Ecotourism, based on national parks and private reserves, became a major source of income, giving forests economic value.
- *Limits:* much of the recovery happened because cattle ranching became less profitable and rural people moved to towns, so not all of it was caused by the payments. Some payments went to land that was unlikely to be cleared anyway. Wealthier landowners found it easier to join than poor smallholders.
- *Judgement:* the scheme succeeded because it was combined with a ban on conversion, stable government and a tourism economy. It shows that rainforest can be worth more standing than cleared, but it may be hard to copy in countries with weaker institutions or stronger pressure from commodity farming.

## Example 2 (savanna): community wildlife management

### CAMPFIRE, Zimbabwe

**Context.** In Zimbabwe's communal lands, people shared the savanna with elephants, buffalo and predators, which damaged crops and killed livestock. Wildlife belonged to the state, so local people gained nothing from it and had every reason to kill it.

**The solution.** The Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources (CAMPFIRE) began in 1989. Rural district councils were given authority over wildlife and could sell hunting and tourism rights, mainly trophy hunting safaris. Revenue was meant to be shared with the communities living with the wildlife, which used it for cash payments, schools, clinics, wells and fences.

**Evaluation.**

- *Success:* in its early years, communities gained income and wildlife was seen as an asset; in some districts wildlife numbers and habitat were maintained.
- *Limits:* councils often kept a large share of revenue, so benefits per household were small. The scheme depended heavily on trophy hunting, so it was vulnerable to outside decisions: when the United States suspended imports of elephant trophies from Zimbabwe in 2014, income fell. Economic and political crisis in the 2000s reduced tourism and weakened the programme.
- *Judgement:* CAMPFIRE showed that giving rural people a stake in wildlife can align conservation with livelihoods, but its success depended on fair revenue sharing and stable external markets.

### Maasai Mara conservancies, Kenya

Since the mid-2000s, Maasai landowners next to the Maasai Mara National Reserve have pooled their individually owned plots into **conservancies** and leased them to safari operators. Operators pay landowners regular fees, limit the number of tourist beds and agree managed grazing with the owners. This gives families a steady income from keeping land open for wildlife instead of fencing, farming or selling it. Critics point out that income depends on international tourism, which collapsed during the COVID-19 pandemic, and that families without land in a conservancy do not benefit.

## Comparing types of solution

| Solution | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Strict protected areas | Clear legal protection | Can exclude local people; hard to enforce in remote areas |
| Payments for ecosystem services | Gives standing forest a cash value | Needs long-term funding; may pay for land not under threat |
| Community management | Local stake; uses local knowledge | Benefits can be captured by elites; depends on markets |
| Certification (timber, palm oil) | Uses consumer pressure | Covers only part of the market; monitoring is costly |
| Agroforestry and sustainable farming | Keeps tree cover with production | Lower short-term returns; needs training |
| Satellite monitoring and enforcement | Detects clearance quickly | Works only if action follows |

## Annotated model answer

*Question: With reference to a case study of either rainforest or savanna, evaluate the success of attempts to manage it sustainably. [20] (one paragraph shown)*

> In Costa Rica, the 1996 forestry law banned conversion of forest and set up payments to landowners from 1997 (**named solution and date**). Forest cover rose from around a fifth of the country in the 1980s to over half (**evidence of success**), and ecotourism gave forest a lasting economic value (**economic sustainability**). However, part of this recovery reflects the decline of cattle ranching rather than the payments (**alternative explanation**), and poorer smallholders found the scheme harder to join (**social limitation**). The scheme was therefore successful environmentally, but its success depended on wider economic change and strong institutions, so it is only partly transferable (**judgement with qualification**).

## Common mistakes

- Writing about both rainforest and savanna thinly. The case study is of one ecosystem.
- Describing threats without linking them to the physical fragility studied in 7.3.
- Listing solutions without evidence of their results.
- Treating sustainability as purely environmental.
- No dates, figures or named schemes.
- A conclusion that simply says "it was partly successful" without saying why, and for whom.

## Exam technique

- In 20-mark essays, plan a structure: threats, problems, solutions, evaluation, judgement.
- Use the AO4 weighting: Paper 3 gives evaluation a large share of marks, so every solution needs a "how well and for whom" sentence.
- Link physical and human factors explicitly; the management element is synoptic.
- If a question names a type of solution, such as community-based schemes, compare it with at least one other type.
