## Equations

```
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
c = ΔE / (m Δθ)       ΔE = m c Δθ
energy from a heater: ΔE = P × t
```

c in J/(kg °C); water ≈ 4200 J/(kg °C).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Thermal expansion** | Increase in size when heated, because particles move further apart |
| **Internal energy** | Total energy of the particles; increases when temperature rises |
| **Temperature** | Linked to the average kinetic energy of the particles |
| **Specific heat capacity** | Energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase |
| **Latent heat** | Energy required to change the state of a substance |
| **Boiling** | Rapid change to gas throughout the liquid at the boiling point |
| **Evaporation** | Escape of more energetic particles from the surface, at any temperature |
| **Condensation** | Gas to liquid, releasing energy at constant temperature |

## Expansion

- Order: **gases ≫ liquids > solids**.
- Particles gain kinetic energy and move further apart; they do **not** get bigger.
- Uses and problems: liquid-in-glass thermometer; expansion gaps in bridges and rails; sagging
  overhead cables; loosening a tight metal lid with hot water.
- Thermometer: liquid expands more than glass; narrow bore turns a small volume change into a
  long movement; thin bulb gives quick response.

## Kelvin conversions

| °C | K |
|---|---|
| −273 | 0 |
| 0 | 273 |
| 37 | 310 |
| 100 | 373 |

A change of 1 °C = a change of 1 K.

## Specific heat capacity: calculation patterns

- **Energy:** 1.2 kg of water from 18 °C to 43 °C → 1.2 × 4200 × 25 = **126 000 J**.
- **Temperature rise:** 6000 J into 0.50 kg of a metal with c = 500 → Δθ = 6000 ÷ 250 = **24 °C**.
- **Find c:** 12 000 J raises 1.0 kg by 12 °C → c = **1000 J/(kg °C)**.
- **Time:** 126 000 J from a 1.4 kW heater → t = 126 000 ÷ 1400 = **90 s**.

## Experiments in one line each

- **Liquid:** known mass in insulated container + immersion heater of known power + timer +
  thermometer + stirring.
- **Solid:** metal block with heater and thermometer holes, insulated; E = Pt; c = E ÷ (mΔθ).
- **Result too high?** Energy lost to surroundings and container → temperature rise too small.

## Changes of state

- Melting, boiling, solidification and condensation all happen at **constant temperature**.
- Water at standard atmospheric pressure: melts at **0 °C**, boils at **100 °C**.
- Heating curve: slope → flat (melting) → slope → flat (boiling) → slope.
- Latent heat: energy works against forces between particles, separating them, so kinetic energy
  and temperature do not rise.

## Boiling versus evaporation

| | Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Any | Boiling point only |
| Where | Surface | Throughout the liquid |
| Rate | Slow | Fast |

**Evaporation cools** because the faster particles escape, lowering the average kinetic energy of
those left.

**Faster evaporation:** higher temperature, larger surface area, more air movement.

## Experiment analysis in exam layout

*A 30 W heater warms a 1.0 kg steel block for 300 s; the temperature rises by 19 °C.*

```
ΔE = P t = 30 × 300 = 9000 J
c = ΔE / (m Δθ) = 9000 / (1.0 × 19) = 474 J/(kg °C)
```

If the accepted value is lower, explain that energy escaped to the surroundings, so Δθ was too
small. Improvement: insulate the block and put oil in the thermometer hole.

## Exam traps

- Use the temperature **change**, not the final temperature, in ΔE = mcΔθ.
- Convert kJ to J and minutes to seconds.
- Temperature is constant during melting and boiling.
- Kelvin numbers are always 273 larger than Celsius numbers.
- "Particles expand" loses the mark.
- In "why does evaporation cool?" mention the **more energetic** particles leaving.

## Self-test

1. Put solids, liquids and gases in order of expansion for the same temperature rise.
2. Why does the liquid in a thermometer rise when it is warmed?
3. Convert 18 °C to kelvin and 20 K to °C.
4. Define specific heat capacity.
5. How much energy raises 2.0 kg of water by 30 °C?
6. A 0.20 kg block absorbs 1800 J and warms by 10 °C. Find c.
7. Why does an experimental value of c usually come out too high?
8. State the melting and boiling points of water at standard atmospheric pressure.
9. What is happening on a flat section of a heating curve?
10. Give two differences between boiling and evaporation.
11. Explain why evaporation cools a liquid.
12. Name three factors that increase the rate of evaporation.

### Answers

1. Gases most, then liquids, then solids least.
2. The liquid expands more than the glass, and the narrow tube shows the small volume increase as
   a rise in level.
3. **291 K**; **−253 °C**.
4. The energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase.
5. 2.0 × 4200 × 30 = **252 000 J**.
6. 1800 ÷ (0.20 × 10) = **900 J/(kg °C)**.
7. Energy is lost to the surroundings and container, so the temperature rise is smaller than it
   should be.
8. **0 °C** and **100 °C**.
9. A change of state: energy is transferred but the temperature stays constant.
10. Any two: evaporation occurs at any temperature, boiling only at the boiling point;
    evaporation is from the surface, boiling throughout; evaporation is slower.
11. The more energetic particles escape from the surface, so the average kinetic energy of the
    remaining particles, and so the temperature, falls.
12. Higher temperature, larger surface area, more air movement over the surface.
