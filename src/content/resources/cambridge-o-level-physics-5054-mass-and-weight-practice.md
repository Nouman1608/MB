## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the mass of an object. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by inertia and give **one** everyday example. **[2]**

**3.** Define gravitational field strength and state its unit. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by a gravitational field. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate the weight of a 2.5 kg bag of rice on Earth. Take g = 9.8 N/kg. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A space agency's rover has a mass of 180 kg. On Mars its weight is 666 N.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational field strength on Mars. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rover's weight on Earth, where g = 9.8 N/kg. **[2]**
**(c)** State the rover's mass on Mars. **[1]**

**7.** Describe how to measure the weight of a small stone using a force meter. **[3]**

**8.** Describe how to determine the mass of 100 cm³ of water using an electronic balance and a beaker. **[3]**

**9.** A beam balance is used to compare an unknown mass with standard masses.

**(a)** Explain why the beam is level when the masses on the two pans are equal. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the beam balance would give the same result on the Moon. **[1]**

**10.** Show that the unit N/kg is equivalent to m/s², and explain why the gravitational field strength near the Earth equals the acceleration of free fall. **[3]**

## Section C

**11.** A student hangs different masses from a force meter and records the weight.

| Mass / kg | 0.100 | 0.200 | 0.300 | 0.400 | 0.500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weight / N | 1.0 | 2.0 | 2.9 | 3.9 | 4.9 |

**(a)** Describe the graph of weight against mass you would expect from these results. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the last result to estimate the gravitational field strength. **[1]**
**(c)** State why the line should pass through the origin. **[1]**

**12.** An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. The gravitational field strength on the Moon is 1.6 N/kg.

**(a)** Calculate the astronaut's weight on the Moon. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a heavy box on the Moon is easier to lift than on Earth, but just as hard to start moving sideways. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Mass is a measure of the **quantity of matter** in an object [1] **at rest relative to the observer** [1].

**2.** Inertia is the **resistance of a mass to a change in its state of rest or motion** [1]. Example: passengers lurch forwards when a bus brakes suddenly; or a full trolley is harder to start and stop than an empty one [1].

**3.** Gravitational field strength is the **force per unit mass** [1]; unit **N/kg** [1].

**4.** A **region** [1] in which a **mass experiences a force due to gravitational attraction** [1].

**5.** W = mg = 2.5 × 9.8 [1] = **24.5 N** [1].

**6. (a)** g = W ÷ m = 666 ÷ 180 [1] = **3.7 N/kg** [1].
**(b)** W = 180 × 9.8 [1] = **1760 N** (to 3 s.f.) [1].
**(c)** **180 kg**: mass does not change from place to place [1].

**7.** Check the force meter reads **zero** with nothing attached [1]. Hold it **vertically**, hang the stone from the hook and wait for it to stop moving [1]. Read the scale **at eye level** in newtons [1].

**8.** Place the empty beaker on the balance and **press tare/zero** [1]. Measure 100 cm³ of water with a measuring cylinder and pour it into the beaker [1]. Read the **steady display**, which is the mass of the water alone [1].

**9. (a)** The beam is level when the **weights on the two pans are equal** [1]; both pans are in the **same gravitational field**, so equal weights mean equal masses [1].
**(b)** Both weights would be **reduced by the same factor**, so they would still balance when the masses are equal [1].

**10.** 1 N = 1 kg m/s², so 1 N/kg = 1 kg m/s² ÷ 1 kg = **1 m/s²** [1]. For a freely falling object, weight is the only force, so W = ma [1]; since W = mg, the acceleration a equals g [1].

**11. (a)** A **straight line** [1] passing **through the origin**, showing weight is proportional to mass [1].
**(b)** g = 4.9 ÷ 0.500 = **9.8 N/kg** [1].
**(c)** An object with zero mass has zero weight [1].

**12. (a)** W = 80 × 1.6 [1] = **128 N** [1].
**(b)** Lifting works against **weight**, which is smaller on the Moon because g is smaller [1]. Starting it moving sideways depends on its **inertia**, which depends on **mass**, and mass is unchanged [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing weight in kilograms.
- Forgetting "at rest relative to the observer" when defining mass.
- Dividing the wrong way: g = W ÷ m, not m ÷ W.
- Describing a force meter without checking zero or reading at eye level.
- Saying an astronaut's mass changes on the Moon.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these mass and weight questions from past papers?**
No. They are original practice questions for subtopic 1.3. They do not copy the exam's structure, question count or mark tariffs.

**Why is the Mars rover's weight on Earth given to 3 significant figures?**
The data (180 kg and 9.8 N/kg) support 2 or 3 significant figures, so 1760 N is an appropriate answer; 1764 N implies more precision than the data have.

**Is mass ever measured with a force meter?**
A force meter measures weight. You can then calculate mass with m = W ÷ g, but the instrument itself reads newtons.
