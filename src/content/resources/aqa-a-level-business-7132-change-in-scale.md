This guide goes deeper into section **3.9.1 Assessing a change in scale** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the reasons why businesses grow or retrench**
- **the difference between organic and external growth**
- **how to manage issues caused by growth and retrenchment**, which should include economies of scale (technical, purchasing and managerial), economies of scope, diseconomies of scale, synergy and overtrading
- **the impact of growth or retrenchment on the functional areas** of the business
- **assessing methods and types of growth**: mergers, takeovers, ventures and franchising; and vertical (backward and forward), horizontal and conglomerate integration.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why businesses grow

- **Economies of scale:** lower unit costs as output rises.
- **Market power:** a larger market share gives more control over prices and stronger bargaining with suppliers.
- **Spreading risk:** a wider range of products or markets reduces dependence on one.
- **Survival:** a small business may be vulnerable to larger rivals or takeover.
- **Owners' and managers' motives:** profit growth, status, bigger rewards.
- **Access to new markets or technology.**
- **Shareholder pressure** for higher returns.

## Why businesses retrench

**Retrenchment** is a deliberate reduction in the size of the business, through closing sites, selling divisions or cutting staff.

- Falling demand or a recession
- Loss-making activities
- Raising cash to repay debt
- Refocusing on core competences after over-expansion
- Responding to changes in the market, such as the decline of a product line
- Poor results from earlier acquisitions

Retrenchment may be a sensible strategy, not a failure, if it restores profitability. It has costs: redundancy payments, loss of skills and morale, and damage to reputation.

## Organic and external growth

| | Organic (internal) growth | External growth |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Growth from the firm's own resources: more sales, new products, new sites | Growth by joining with another firm: merger, takeover, joint venture |
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Risk | Lower; usually funded from profit or loans in stages | Higher; large sums; integration problems |
| Control | Retained; culture preserved | Possibly shared; clashes of cultures |
| Finance | Retained profit, loans, new shares | Often large loans or share exchanges |
| Examples | Opening new shops; launching products | Buying a competitor |

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** are falls in the average (unit) cost of production as output increases.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Technical | Larger firms can use more efficient machinery, and specialise | A large bakery uses an automatic line unaffordable to a small one |
| Purchasing | Bulk buying gets discounts | A chain negotiates 6% off on large orders |
| Managerial | Specialist managers can be employed and their cost is spread | A big firm employs a full-time finance director |
| Financial | Larger firms borrow more cheaply | Lower interest rates from banks |
| Marketing | Advertising costs spread over more units | National TV campaign |

**Economies of scope** arise when producing several related products together costs less than producing them separately, for example sharing a factory, a brand or a distribution network.

**Diseconomies of scale** are rises in average cost when a firm becomes too large:

- communication problems and slow decisions through long chains of command
- lower motivation as staff feel remote
- co-ordination problems between sites
- duplication and bureaucracy.

### Worked example: unit costs

A factory has fixed costs of £400,000 a year and variable costs of £6 per unit.

| Output | Total cost | Average cost per unit |
|---|---|---|
| 50,000 | £700,000 | £14.00 |
| 100,000 | £1,000,000 | £10.00 |
| 200,000 | £1,600,000 | £8.00 |
| 300,000 | £2,200,000 | £7.33 |
| 400,000 (with extra supervisors and a second site) | £3,200,000 | £8.00 |

Between 50,000 and 300,000 units, fixed costs are spread over more units, so average cost falls: economies of scale. At 400,000 units, extra layers of management and a second site raise total cost faster than output: average cost rises to £8.00. This shows diseconomies of scale. The most efficient output is about 300,000 units.

## Synergy

**Synergy** is when two businesses combined are worth more than the total of their separate values ("2 + 2 = 5"). Sources: shared costs, combined skills, complementary products, access to each other's customers.

*Example:* two firms each spend £300,000 a year on administration. After merging, a single administration costs £450,000: a saving of £150,000, or 25%. **Negative synergy** occurs when combining causes problems such as culture clashes, with the result worth less than expected.

## Overtrading

**Overtrading** occurs when a business expands sales faster than its finances can support. It runs short of cash even though it may be profitable, because money is tied up in inventory and receivables.

### Worked example

Tarn Foods grows revenue from £2.4m to £3.6m (up 50%). Cost of sales is 65% of revenue. It gives customers 60 days' credit, holds inventory for 45 days of cost of sales and pays suppliers in 30 days.

- Extra receivables: (£3.6m − £2.4m) × 60 ÷ 365 = **£197,260**
- Extra inventory: (£2.34m − £1.56m) × 45 ÷ 365 = **£96,164**
- Extra payables: (£2.34m − £1.56m) × 30 ÷ 365 = **£64,110**
- Net extra working capital needed: 197,260 + 96,164 − 64,110 = **£229,315**

So Tarn must find about £229,000 of extra cash just to fund growth, before buying equipment or paying staff. If it cannot, it may struggle to pay bills and fail even with rising sales.

**Solutions:** slow the pace of growth; arrange an overdraft or loan in advance; chase customers for earlier payment; reduce inventory; negotiate longer supplier credit; raise new equity.

## Impact on the functional areas

| Function | Growth | Retrenchment |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | Funding needs; cash-flow risk; economies of scale | Cash from selling assets; redundancy costs |
| Marketing | Wider reach and brand; managing more products | Dropping products or markets; protecting reputation |
| Operations | More capacity, new sites, complex supply chains; risk of lower quality | Closing capacity; spare capacity cut |
| Human resources | Recruitment, training and communication; culture risk | Redundancy, morale, loss of knowledge |

## Methods of growth

| Method | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Merger | Two firms agree to combine into one, usually of similar size |
| Takeover (acquisition) | One firm buys control of another, which may be friendly or hostile |
| Joint venture | Two firms set up a new business jointly for a specific purpose, sharing costs, risks and rewards |
| Franchising | The owner (franchisor) lets others (franchisees) use its brand and system in return for fees and a share of revenue |

*Franchising example.* A franchisee runs a shop earning £450,000 a year and pays 6% of revenue to the franchisor: **£27,000**. The franchisor grows fast without investing in shops, but gives up control of the day-to-day operation and may suffer if a franchisee harms the brand.

## Types of growth (integration)

| Type | Direction | Example | Advantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Joins a firm at the same stage in the same industry | Two supermarkets merge | Larger share; scale economies; less competition |
| Backward vertical | Joins a firm earlier in the supply chain | Bakery buys a flour mill | Secure supplies; control cost and quality |
| Forward vertical | Joins a firm later in the supply chain | Brewer buys pubs | Control the route to customers; capture retail margin |
| Conglomerate | Joins a firm in an unrelated industry | Food firm buys a hotel | Spreads risk; new markets |

Risks: regulators may block horizontal mergers that reduce competition; vertical integration needs new skills; conglomerates may lack focus.

## Assessing a change in scale

Questions often ask whether growth or retrenchment is the right decision. Consider:

1. **Objectives:** does the change serve them?
2. **Finance:** can it be funded without overtrading?
3. **Risk and speed:** is organic growth safer but slower than a takeover?
4. **Economies and diseconomies:** at what scale does the business reach its lowest unit cost?
5. **Culture and people:** will the integration succeed?
6. **Market and competitors:** how will rivals and regulators react?
7. **Time frame:** the benefits of growth take time to appear.

## Common mistakes

- Treating growth as always good. It can reduce profit per unit and strain cash.
- Confusing overtrading with overspending; it is caused by growth running ahead of working capital.
- Mixing up mergers (joined by agreement) and takeovers (one buys the other).
- Mislabelling integration types, for example calling a purchase of a supplier "forward".
- Ignoring the cost and disruption of retrenchment.
