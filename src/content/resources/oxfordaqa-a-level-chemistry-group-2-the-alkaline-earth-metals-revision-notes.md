## 1. Core facts

- Elements in this topic: Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba. Outer configuration ns², so s block.
- They react by losing two electrons: M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻. Oxidation state +2 in compounds.
- Structure of each element: giant metallic lattice.

## 2. Physical trends (Mg → Ba)

| Property | Direction | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | increases | an extra occupied shell at each step |
| First ionisation energy | decreases | outer electron further out and better shielded, so weaker attraction |
| Melting point | falls from Ca to Ba; Mg is lower than Ca | 2+ ions get bigger, so ion–electron attraction weakens |

**Steps: writing a first ionisation energy explanation**

```
1. Name the shell: outer electron is in a higher shell (e.g. 5s vs 4s).
2. Shielding: more inner shells between it and the nucleus.
3. Compare: weaker attraction, so less energy needed.
4. Deal with nuclear charge: it rises, but steps 1 and 2 outweigh it.
```

Equation, gaseous on both sides: Ba(g) → Ba⁺(g) + e⁻

**Steps: writing a melting point explanation**

```
1. Structure: giant metallic lattice.
2. Bonding: attraction between M²⁺ ions and delocalised electrons.
3. Size: the ion higher in the group is smaller, charge is the same.
4. Result: stronger attraction, more energy to overcome, higher melting point.
```

Magnesium is the exception; its crystal arrangement differs, so the size argument does not predict it.

## 3. Reactions with water

| Metal | Conditions | Equation | Key observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mg | cold water | Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂ | barely any bubbles |
| Mg | steam | Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂ | bright white flame, white powder left |
| Ca | cold water | Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ | fizzing, cloudy white liquid |
| Sr | cold water | Sr + 2H₂O → Sr(OH)₂ + H₂ | faster than Ca |
| Ba | cold water | Ba + 2H₂O → Ba(OH)₂ + H₂ | fastest |

- Trend: more vigorous down the group, because the outer electrons are removed more easily.
- Redox: metal 0 → +2 (oxidised); H +1 → 0 (reduced).
- One mole of metal gives one mole of H₂ and one mole of M(OH)₂, which supplies two moles of OH⁻.

## 4. Titanium extraction

```
TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂
Ti: +4 → 0 (reduced)      Mg: 0 → +2 (oxidised, so Mg is the reducing agent)
```

**Worked reminder.** Mass of TiCl₄ that reacts with 1.215 kg of Mg (Mr TiCl₄ 189.9; Aᵣ Mg = 24.3):

```
n(Mg)       = 1215 ÷ 24.3    = 50.0 mol
n(TiCl₄)    = 50.0 ÷ 2       = 25.0 mol
mass TiCl₄  = 25.0 × 189.9   = 4748 g = 4.75 kg
```

## 5. Solubility trends

| Compound | Trend down group | Spec wording to use |
|---|---|---|
| M(OH)₂ | more soluble | Mg(OH)₂ is **sparingly soluble** |
| MSO₄ | less soluble | BaSO₄ is **insoluble** |

Hook: "hydroxides **rise**, sulfates **sink**". A saturated Ba(OH)₂ solution is far more alkaline than a
saturated Mg(OH)₂ solution because it holds more OH⁻.

## 6. Uses linked to solubility

| Compound | Use | Why it suits the job |
|---|---|---|
| Mg(OH)₂ | antacid (milk of magnesia) | a base that is sparingly soluble, so it neutralises acid without a strongly alkaline solution |
| Ca(OH)₂ | raising soil pH in agriculture | a cheap base that neutralises H⁺ in acidic soil |
| CaO or CaCO₃ | removing SO₂ from flue gases | bases that react with the acidic gas |
| BaSO₄ | barium meal for X-ray imaging | absorbs X-rays; insoluble, so toxic Ba²⁺ is not absorbed |

Equations to learn:

```
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺  → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O
CaO + SO₂      → CaSO₃
CaCO₃ + SO₂    → CaSO₃ + CO₂
```

CaO and CaCO₃ both react 1 : 1 with SO₂; only the carbonate route releases CO₂.

## 7. The sulfate test

```
1. Acidify the sample with dilute HCl (or HNO₃).
2. Add BaCl₂(aq).
3. White precipitate means sulfate:  Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

| Question | Answer to give |
|---|---|
| Why BaCl₂? | BaSO₄ is insoluble, so Ba²⁺ precipitates any sulfate present |
| Why acidify? | to remove CO₃²⁻ (as CO₂), which would otherwise give white BaCO₃ |
| Why not H₂SO₄? | it adds SO₄²⁻, so every sample would test positive |

**Worked reminder.** Excess acidified BaCl₂ is added to 15.0 cm³ of 0.0620 mol dm⁻³ Na₂SO₄
(Mr BaSO₄ = 233.4):

```
n(SO₄²⁻)   = 0.0150 × 0.0620  = 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ mol
mass BaSO₄ = 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ × 233.4 = 0.217 g
```

## 8. Required practical 3: Group 2 cations

| Test on a fresh portion | Mg²⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Sr²⁺ | Ba²⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| add NaOH(aq) | white ppt | slight ppt | very little | none |
| add dilute H₂SO₄ | none | slight ppt | white ppt | heavy white ppt |

Read the rows from the trends: a hydroxide precipitate shrinks down the group; a sulfate precipitate grows.
Compare solutions of similar concentration.

## 9. Must-know distinctions

- **Atomic radius vs ionisation energy:** opposite directions down the group (radius up, IE down).
- **Mg with cold water vs steam:** hydroxide vs oxide.
- **Sparingly soluble vs insoluble:** Mg(OH)₂ vs BaSO₄, in the specification's own words.
- **Toxic vs safe barium:** soluble barium compounds release Ba²⁺; insoluble BaSO₄ does not.
- **Reduced vs reducing agent:** titanium is reduced; magnesium is the reducing agent.

## 10. Linking ideas across the topic

- **Radius → IE → reactivity.** Bigger atoms hold outer electrons less tightly, so ionisation energy
  falls and the metals react with water more vigorously. One chain of reasoning explains three trends.
- **Solubility → uses.** The least soluble hydroxide (Mg) is the gentle antacid; the insoluble sulfate (Ba)
  is the safe X-ray meal; the insolubility of BaSO₄ is also what makes the sulfate test work.
- **Solubility → identification.** The same two trends give the NaOH(aq) and dilute H₂SO₄ tests in
  Required practical 3.
- **Redox everywhere.** The water reactions and titanium extraction are both redox, with the Group 2
  metal oxidised each time.

## Quick self-test

1. State the trend in first ionisation energy from Mg to Ba.
2. Name the type of bonding in solid strontium and the particles involved.
3. Which element in Mg–Ba does not fit the melting point trend?
4. Give the products of magnesium with steam.
5. Write an equation for calcium reacting with water.
6. Calculate the amount of hydrogen, in mol, from 2.746 g of barium with excess water. (Aᵣ Ba = 137.3)
7. Which Group 2 hydroxide does the specification call sparingly soluble?
8. Calculate the mass of Ca(OH)₂ that neutralises 0.300 mol of H⁺ in acidic soil. (Mr 74.1)
9. Calculate the mass of SO₂, in kg, removed by 1.683 kg of CaO. (Mr SO₂ = 64.1, CaO = 56.1)
10. Give the oxidation state change of titanium in its extraction.
11. Why is barium chloride solution acidified in the sulfate test?
12. A solution gives a white precipitate with dilute H₂SO₄ but none with NaOH(aq). Which Group 2 ion is
    most likely present?

### Answers

1. It decreases.
2. Metallic bonding: attraction between Sr²⁺ ions and delocalised electrons.
3. Magnesium (its melting point is below calcium's).
4. Magnesium oxide and hydrogen.
5. Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂
6. n(Ba) = 2.746 ÷ 137.3 = 0.0200 mol, so **0.0200 mol** of H₂.
7. Magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂.
8. n(Ca(OH)₂) = 0.300 ÷ 2 = 0.150 mol; mass = 0.150 × 74.1 = **11.1 g**.
9. n(CaO) = 1683 ÷ 56.1 = 30.0 mol = n(SO₂); mass = 30.0 × 64.1 = 1923 g = **1.92 kg**.
10. +4 to 0.
11. To remove carbonate ions, which would also give a white precipitate with barium ions.
12. Ba²⁺.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that atoms "get bigger, so IE falls" without naming shielding or the weaker attraction.
- Leaving out state symbols, or writing (s), in a first ionisation energy equation.
- Explaining melting point with "intermolecular forces" or "stronger atoms" instead of metallic bonding.
- Giving the product of magnesium and steam as Mg(OH)₂.
- Swapping the hydroxide and sulfate trends, or calling Mg(OH)₂ "insoluble".
- Saying BaSO₄ is safe because "barium is not poisonous"; the point is that it does not dissolve.
- Using the wrong TiCl₄ : Mg ratio (it is 1 : 2) in extraction calculations.
- Acidifying the sulfate test with sulfuric acid, or giving no reason for the acid.
- Forgetting that each M(OH)₂ gives two OH⁻ ions in titration or concentration work.
