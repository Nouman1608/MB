## Purpose: what research tells a business

Market research is collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market. It reduces the risk of decisions by replacing guesswork with evidence.

- **Identifying and understanding customers:** who they are and what they need (links to 3.5.1).
- **Identifying market opportunities:** needs that no business meets well, or a growing segment.
- **Demand:** how many people want the product and at what price.
- **Competition:** who the rivals are, what they charge, and what customers think of them.
- **Target market:** the characteristics of the customers most likely to buy (links to 3.5.2).

## Methods

### Primary research

Primary research (field research) is new information collected first-hand by, or for, the business to answer its own questions.

| Method | How it works | Benefit | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | A set of written questions, on paper or online | Cheap to send out; easy to compare answers | People may not reply, or may not answer honestly |
| Survey | Collecting answers from many people, often using a questionnaire, in the street, by phone or online | Large numbers give more reliable results | Time and money to carry out and analyse |
| Interview | A one-to-one conversation with set or open questions | Detailed answers; interviewer can explain and follow up | Slow and costly per person; the interviewer may influence answers |
| Focus group | A small group discusses a product with a leader | Reveals feelings, reasons and reactions to new ideas | A few people may not represent the market; one strong voice can sway the group |

### Secondary research

Secondary research (desk research) uses information that already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose.

| Method | Examples | Benefit | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internet research | Competitors' websites and prices, government statistics, online reviews | Quick and often free | May be out of date, inaccurate or biased |
| Printed press | Newspapers, magazines, trade journals | Cheap; reports trends and rivals' activities | Not designed for the business's exact question |

**Primary or secondary first?** Many businesses start with secondary research because it is cheap and quick, then use primary research to answer the specific questions secondary data cannot.

## Types of data

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Numerical data that can be counted and analysed statistically | Opinions, feelings and reasons |
| Example | "62% of respondents would pay £3 or more" | "I'd pay more if it came in recyclable packaging" |
| Typical methods | Surveys and questionnaires with closed questions | Focus groups, interviews, open questions |
| Strength | Easy to compare and present in tables and charts | Explains why customers think as they do |
| Weakness | Does not explain reasons | Hard to summarise; may come from few people |

Most businesses need both: quantitative data to show how many, qualitative data to show why.

## Choosing the most appropriate method

When the question says "evaluate the most appropriate method", use these factors from the case:

- **Money available.** A start-up with little cash leans on internet research and a short questionnaire.
- **Time.** Secondary research is fastest; a large survey takes weeks.
- **What the business needs to know.** "How many?" points to a survey. "Why?" or "What do you think of this design?" points to a focus group or interviews.
- **How much is at stake.** A costly launch justifies paying for more reliable research.

Example: Dunmere Dog Treats wants to know whether owners would buy a new grain-free treat and why. A short online questionnaire to its 2,000 email customers gives cheap quantitative data on how many would buy; a focus group of eight owners tasting samples explains why. Together they cost far less than a national survey.

## Using the findings

You must interpret data in tables and charts and use it to support a decision. Read the data, calculate what is asked, then say what it means for the business.

### Worked example: interpreting survey data

Corrin Cakes asked 250 people which new flavour they would buy.

| Flavour | Number choosing it |
|---|---|
| Salted caramel | 95 |
| Lemon and lime | 60 |
| Chocolate orange | 70 |
| None of these | 25 |

- Check the total: 95 + 60 + 70 + 25 = 250.
- Salted caramel share: 95 ÷ 250 × 100 = **38%**.
- People choosing a flavour: 250 − 25 = 225, which is 225 ÷ 250 × 100 = **90%**.

Interpretation: salted caramel is the most popular choice, so it is the safest launch. But 38% is not a majority: 62% chose something else or nothing. The research does not say why, so a focus group would help before committing to one flavour. It is also worth checking who was asked: if all 250 were existing customers, the result may not reflect new customers.

## Market size and market share

**Market size** is the total sales of all businesses in a market over a period. It can be measured by **value** (sales revenue in £) or **volume** (number of units sold).

**Market share** is one business's sales as a percentage of the total market:

Market share (%) = (the business's sales ÷ total market sales) × 100

Use the same measure on top and bottom: value with value, volume with volume.

### Worked example: market share over two years

The market for electric bike hire in a city:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Total market sales | £600,000 | £800,000 |
| Velo Hire's sales | £150,000 | £176,000 |

- Year 1 share: £150,000 ÷ £600,000 × 100 = **25%**
- Year 2 share: £176,000 ÷ £800,000 × 100 = **22%**
- Velo Hire's sales grew: (£176,000 − £150,000) ÷ £150,000 × 100 = about **17.3%**
- The market grew: (£800,000 − £600,000) ÷ £600,000 × 100 = about **33.3%**

Velo Hire's sales grew, yet its share fell, because the market grew faster than it did. Rivals are winning more of the new customers. That is a warning even though revenue is up.

### Finding the total market from a share

If a business sells £45,000 and has a 15% share, the market is £45,000 ÷ 0.15 = **£300,000**. Check: £45,000 ÷ £300,000 × 100 = 15%.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing primary (new, first-hand) with secondary (already exists) research.
- Saying a focus group is quantitative. It produces opinions and reasons.
- Treating a questionnaire as automatically reliable; who answers, and how many, matters.
- Confusing market size with market share.
- Mixing value and volume in a market share calculation.
- Saying a business is doing badly because its share fell, without noticing its sales rose.
- Recommending an expensive method for a business the case says has little money.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify:** one method, or a figure from a table.
- **Calculate:** show the method, then give the answer with % or £.
- **Explain:** a benefit or drawback of a method, linked to the business.
- **Analyse:** what the data shows and what the business should do as a result.
- **Evaluate:** compare two methods against cost, time and what the business needs to know, then choose.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between primary and secondary market research?**
Primary research collects new information first-hand for the business's own purpose, such as a survey. Secondary research uses information that already exists, such as internet research or newspapers.

**What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative research?**
Quantitative research produces numbers that can be counted and compared. Qualitative research produces opinions and reasons that explain why customers think as they do.

**How do you calculate market share?**
Divide the business's sales by total market sales and multiply by 100, using the same measure, value or volume, for both.
