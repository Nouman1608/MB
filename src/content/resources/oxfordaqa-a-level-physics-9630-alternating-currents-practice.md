These questions cover section 3.10.5, Alternating currents, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level content. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [alternating currents study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-alternating-currents/) or the [alternating currents revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-alternating-currents-revision-notes/) first.

All waveforms are sinusoidal. Supply values are given in each question.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the root mean square value of an alternating voltage. **[2]**

**2.** A sinusoidal voltage has a peak-to-peak value of 20 V. What is its rms value?

A: 14.1 V

B: 7.1 V

C: 10 V

D: 28 V

**[1]**

**3.** A mains supply is 230 V rms. Calculate its peak and peak-to-peak values. **[2]**

**4.** A sinusoidal current of peak value 2.5 A flows in a 12 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the mean power. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the peak power. **[1]**

**5.** An oscilloscope's time-base is switched off and an alternating signal is connected. Describe the trace and state what its length measures. **[2]**

**6.** An oscilloscope is used as a dc voltmeter with the Y-gain on 2.0 V per division. When a cell is connected, the trace moves 2.6 divisions downwards from its zero line. State the p.d. of the cell and what the downward shift shows. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** A sinusoidal current I = Io sin ωt flows in a resistor R.

**(a)** Write an expression for the instantaneous power. **[1]**

**(b)** Use the fact that the mean of sin² ωt over a cycle is ½ to show that Irms = Io/√2. **[2]**

**8.** An oscilloscope trace has a peak-to-peak height of 5.2 divisions on 5.0 V per division. Two complete cycles occupy 8.0 divisions on 2.0 ms per division.

**(a)** Calculate the peak voltage. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the rms voltage. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the frequency. **[2]**

**9.** A 3.0 V rms, 250 Hz signal is to be displayed on an oscilloscope screen 8 divisions high and 10 divisions wide. The Y-gain can be set to 0.5, 1, 2 or 5 V per division, and the time-base to 0.5, 1, 2 or 5 ms per division.

**(a)** Choose the best Y-gain and explain your choice. **[2]**

**(b)** Choose a suitable time-base and explain your choice. **[2]**

**10.** A heater is rated 2.4 kW on a 240 V rms supply.

**(a)** Calculate the rms current. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the peak current. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the resistance of the heater. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the peak power. **[1]**

**11.** An ac supply has a peak-to-peak voltage of 34 V. Calculate the dc voltage that would heat the same resistor at the same mean rate. **[2]**

**12.** An oscilloscope shows a transmitted pulse and a reflected pulse 4.5 divisions apart, with the time-base on 20 μs per division. Each position can be read to ±0.1 division.

**(a)** Calculate the time interval. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the time interval. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest how the time-base could be changed to reduce this percentage uncertainty. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The steady (dc) voltage [1] that would give the same mean power in a resistor as the alternating voltage [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Vo = 10 V, so Vrms = 10/√2 = 7.1 V. **[1]**

**3.** Vo = √2 × 230 = **325 V** [1]; peak-to-peak = 2 × 325 = **650 V** [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Irms = 2.5/√2 = 1.77 A [1]; P = Irms²R = 1.77² × 12 = **37.5 W** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Io²R = 2.5² × 12 = **75 W** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A vertical straight line, because the spot moves only up and down [1]; its length gives the peak-to-peak voltage (divisions × Y-gain) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** 2.6 × 2.0 = **5.2 V** [1]; the input terminal is negative relative to the ground terminal (the cell is connected the opposite way round to a positive reading) [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** P = I²R = Io²R sin² ωt [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Mean power = ½Io²R [1]; the rms current gives the same power: Irms²R = ½Io²R, so Irms = Io/√2 [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Peak-to-peak = 5.2 × 5.0 = 26 V, so Vo = **13 V** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Vrms = 13/√2 = **9.2 V** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** One cycle = 4.0 divisions, so T = 4.0 × 2.0 ms = 8.0 ms [1]; f = 1/0.0080 = **125 Hz** [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** Peak-to-peak = 2√2 × 3.0 = 8.5 V [1]. At 1 V per division the trace is 8.5 divisions and goes off the screen, so **2 V per division**, giving 4.2 divisions, is the largest trace that fits [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** T = 1/250 = 4.0 ms [1]. At **1 ms per division**, one cycle is 4 divisions and 2.5 cycles fit across the screen, so two whole cycles can be measured (0.5 ms per division shows only 1.25 cycles; 2 ms per division is acceptable but less precise per cycle) [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Irms = 2400/240 = **10 A** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Io = √2 × 10 = **14 A** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** R = 240/10 = **24 Ω** [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** IoVo = 2 × 2400 = **4.8 kW** [1]. **[1]**

**11.** Vo = 17 V [1]; Vrms = 17/√2 = **12 V**, which is the equivalent dc voltage [1]. **[2]**

**12. (a)** 4.5 × 20 μs = **90 μs** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Two readings each ±0.1 division give ±0.2 division [1]; 0.2/4.5 × 100 = **4.4%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Use a smaller time per division (for example 10 μs per division), so the same interval spans more divisions [1]. **[1]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining rms with the calculation steps only, without mentioning equal mean power.
- Halving the peak-to-peak value and then forgetting to divide by √2.
- Using peak values for mean power.
- Reading the period from a single cycle when several are available.
- Choosing a Y-gain that sends the trace off the screen.
