This guide covers **Paper 1 Literary Non-fiction and Composition**,
one component of OxfordAQA International GCSE English Language (9270),
which overlaps with AQA's UK GCSE English Language (8700). Students
take Paper 1 plus either Paper 2 (examined) or a non-exam assessment
project as an alternative route.

## Where this fits in 9270

Paper 1 pairs close reading and analysis of literary non-fiction with
students' own composition writing, establishing both the analytical
and creative writing skills that the specification draws on
throughout. An optional Speaking and Listening endorsement is reported
separately and does not count toward the qualification grade.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LANGUAGE (9270) — PAPER 1
LITERARY NON-FICTION AND COMPOSITION**

- Section A: Literary Non-fiction (40 marks) — a single unseen
literary non-fiction text for close study, divided into sections with
questions, focusing on how writers use language and structure to
achieve effects; at least one question addresses the whole text
- Section B: Composition (40 marks) — four possible task types
(descriptive, imaginative, argumentative or discursive), offered as a
choice of three tasks, of which students write one

Paper 1 is **2 hours** long and carries **60%** of the qualification's
overall marks, with Paper 2 (or the NEA alternative) carrying the
remaining 40%.

## How to approach it

Section A rewards precise, evidence-based analysis -- practise
identifying specific language and structural choices in unseen
non-fiction texts and explaining their effect, rather than describing
content in general terms. Section B benefits from planning before
writing: a strong composition typically has a clear structure and a
consistent, deliberate voice, so practise planning openings and
endings in particular, since these are where marks are often won or
lost. Because Section A tests reading and Section B tests writing,
build revision time for both independently rather than assuming skill
in one transfers automatically to the other. Reading widely in the
literary non-fiction genre -- journalism, memoir, travel writing --
before the exam helps with Section A, since familiarity with the
genre's typical techniques makes unseen texts less unfamiliar on the
day, while regularly timed composition practice for Section B builds
the pacing and control needed to complete a well-structured piece
within the paper's time limit.

## Literary non-fiction

**Literary non-fiction** is factual writing that uses the techniques of imaginative writing — travel writing, memoir, biography, essays, speeches, opinion journalism. It is true in content but crafted in style, which is precisely what makes it worth analysing.

Reading such texts, work through:

- **Purpose** — to inform, describe, persuade, entertain, or a combination.
- **Voice and perspective** — first-person immediacy, or a detached observer.
- **Structure** — how it opens, where it shifts, how it closes; chronological or thematic.
- **Language** — imagery, sensory detail, semantic fields, connotation.
- **Tone** — admiring, critical, wry, nostalgic, and whether it changes.

## Analysing effectively

Every analytical point follows the same shape: **identify, quote briefly, explain the effect**. The explanation must say what the reader is made to think or feel, and why that serves the writer's purpose.

```
Weak     "The writer uses adjectives to describe the city."
Strong   "The adjectives 'cramped', 'clamorous' and 'unrelenting'
          accumulate to make the city feel oppressive, positioning
          the reader to share the narrator's exhaustion."
```

Analyse **structure** as well as language — where a writer places a short sentence, or delays information, is a deliberate choice with an effect.

Section A is set on a **single text for close study**, divided into sections with questions -- at least one question addresses the whole text. There is no second text and no comparison task on this paper; comparing two texts is a Paper 2 skill instead.

## Composition

Section B offers **three tasks from which students choose one**: descriptive, imaginative, and
argumentative and/or discursive. Only one is written; there is no separate "viewpoint" category and
no requirement to attempt more than one.

**Descriptive** writing succeeds on precision and the senses. Choose one moment and render it fully rather than covering an hour thinly. Vary sentence length: a short sentence after several long ones lands with force.

**Imaginative** writing needs shape — a clear situation, a development, a resolution. Decide the ending before you begin, and resist introducing too many characters.

**Argumentative/discursive** writing needs a clear line of argument, two or three developed points, and a genuine counter-argument addressed rather than dismissed. Rhetorical devices — direct address, rhetorical questions, tricolon, anecdote — should serve the argument, not decorate it.

Register must stay consistent with the chosen task and form throughout, even though (unlike Paper 2's directed writing) Paper 1's composition tasks do not specify a named audience; drifting halfway through costs marks even where each sentence is correct.

## Accuracy and range

Technical accuracy carries substantial marks: sentence demarcation, avoiding comma splices, consistent tense, subject–verb agreement, apostrophes, and paragraphing that signals shifts in time, place or topic.

Range means varied sentence structures and precise vocabulary. Ambition is rewarded only when controlled.

## Worked example

Opening a descriptive piece on a market at dawn.

```
Weak      "The market was busy and noisy and there were lots of people."
          -- abstract, general, one flat sentence

Strong    "Crates thudded onto wet stone. Somewhere behind the fish
          stalls a radio crackled into life, and the smell of diesel
          mixed with salt. The city was waking, one shutter at a time."

Why:  concrete sensory detail (sound, smell, touch)
      varied sentence lengths, short opener for impact
      a closing image that suggests rather than states
```

## Common mistakes

Feature-spotting without explaining effect. Summarising content instead of analysing craft. Treating Section A as if it set two texts to compare, when it is a single text throughout. Describing a whole day rather than one moment. Beginning imaginative writing with no ending planned. Losing register partway through. Using rhetorical questions as filler.

## Quick revision checklist

- Identify purpose, voice, structure, language and tone in literary non-fiction.
- Identify, quote and explain — always ending on the effect on the reader.
- Analyse structural choices, not only language.
- Remember Section A is a single text with no comparison task.
- Choose one moment for description and shape imaginative writing around a planned ending.
- Sustain register and check sentence demarcation, tense and paragraphing.
