## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by damping, and what causes it. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between light, critical and heavy damping. **[3]**

**3.** State what is meant by resonance and the condition under which it occurs. **[2]**

**4.** In a demonstration of Barton's pendulums, a heavy driver pendulum of length 0.60 m hangs from a taut string together with light pendulums of lengths 0.30 m, 0.45 m, 0.60 m and 0.90 m. The driver is set swinging. State which light pendulum oscillates with the largest amplitude, and explain why. **[2]**

**5.** The amplitude of a lightly damped oscillator falls from 50 mm to 40 mm during one oscillation. Calculate the fraction of its energy lost during that oscillation. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the period of a lightly damped oscillator remains almost constant while its amplitude decreases. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A mass on a spring is displaced and released from rest at t = 0. Describe the displacement–time graphs you would sketch, on the same axes, for light, critical and heavy damping. State the key features that each curve must show. **[5]**

**8.** A lightly damped system is driven by a periodic force of variable frequency. The graph of amplitude against driving frequency has a sharp peak at 6.0 Hz.

**(a)** State the natural frequency of the system. **[1]**

**(b)** The damping is increased. Describe the changes to the graph. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain, in terms of energy, why the amplitude at resonance does not increase without limit. **[2]**

**9.** The drum assembly of a washing machine is mounted on springs and has a natural frequency of 3.5 Hz. During the spin cycle the drum speed increases steadily from rest to 1000 revolutions per minute.

**(a)** Calculate the drum speed, in revolutions per minute, at which the machine vibrates most violently. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two changes to the design that would reduce the vibration at this speed. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the vibration becomes smaller as the drum speeds up beyond this value. **[1]**

**10.** The pointer of an analogue meter is designed to be critically damped. Explain why critical damping is preferred to light damping and to heavy damping for this purpose. **[3]**

**11.** A lightly damped oscillator has a period of 0.50 s and an initial energy of 0.40 J. Its amplitude halves every 6 oscillations.

**(a)** Calculate the energy remaining after 6 oscillations. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy remaining after 12 oscillations. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the mean rate of energy loss during the first 3.0 s. **[2]**

**12.** A child on a swing is pushed by a parent. Explain why the amplitude of the swing increases greatly if the parent pushes once per swing at the right moment, but stays small if the pushes are at a different frequency. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Damping is the **loss of energy, and so decrease in amplitude**, of an oscillating system [1], caused by a **resistive force** opposing the motion (such as air resistance or friction) [1].

**2.** **Light:** the system oscillates with gradually decreasing amplitude [1]. **Critical:** it returns to equilibrium in the **shortest possible time without oscillating** [1]. **Heavy:** it returns to equilibrium **without oscillating but more slowly** than with critical damping [1].

**3.** Resonance is the **maximum amplitude** of a forced oscillation [1], occurring when the **driving frequency equals the natural frequency** of the system [1].

**4.** The **0.60 m** pendulum [1]. It has the same length, so the same natural frequency as the driving frequency, so it resonates [1].

**5.** Energy ∝ amplitude², so energy remaining = (40/50)² = 0.64 [1]; fraction lost = **0.36** (36%) [1].

**6.** For simple harmonic motion the **period is independent of amplitude** [1]; light damping reduces the amplitude but barely changes the restoring force per unit displacement, so the period is almost unchanged [1].

**7.** All three start at the **same displacement with zero gradient** [1]. **Light:** oscillates about the time axis with a **constant period** [1] and **gradually decreasing** peaks [1]. **Critical:** falls to zero in the **shortest time without crossing** the axis [1]. **Heavy:** falls **more slowly** than critical, also without crossing the axis [1].

**8. (a)** **6.0 Hz** [1].

**(b)** The peak becomes **lower** [1]; the peak becomes **broader** (less sharp) [1]; the peak moves to a slightly **lower frequency** (and the amplitude is smaller at all frequencies) [1].

**(c)** The energy lost per cycle to damping **increases as the amplitude increases** [1]; the amplitude stops growing when the energy lost per cycle **equals the energy supplied per cycle** by the driver [1].

**9. (a)** Resonance at 3.5 revolutions per second [1]; 3.5 × 60 = **210 rev min⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Any two: **add damping** (shock absorbers) [1]; **change the natural frequency** by changing the stiffness of the springs or the mass of the assembly [1]; balance the load better to reduce the driving force.

**(c)** The driving frequency is now **far above the natural frequency**, so the amplitude of the forced oscillation is small [1].

**10.** With critical damping the pointer reaches its final reading in the **shortest time without overshooting** [1]. With light damping it would **oscillate** about the reading before settling, making it hard to read [1]. With heavy damping it would **move too slowly** to the reading [1].

**11. (a)** Amplitude × ½, so energy × ¼ [1]: 0.40 × 0.25 = **0.10 J** [1].

**(b)** Amplitude × ¼, energy × 1/16: **0.025 J** [1].

**(c)** 3.0 s is 6 oscillations, so 0.30 J is lost [1]; rate = 0.30 ÷ 3.0 = **0.10 W** [1].

**12.** The swing has a **natural frequency** [1]. Pushing once per swing makes the **driving frequency equal the natural frequency**, so each push is in the direction of motion and transfers energy to the swing: this is resonance and the amplitude becomes large [1]. At other frequencies some pushes act **against the motion**, so little net energy is transferred and the amplitude stays small [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining critical damping without "shortest time".
- Sketching a critically damped curve that crosses the axis, or a heavy curve that settles fastest.
- Changing the period of a lightly damped oscillation on a sketch.
- Saying that at resonance the frequency, rather than the amplitude, is maximum.
- Forgetting that energy goes as amplitude squared.
- Explaining the limited resonant amplitude without referring to energy lost to damping.
