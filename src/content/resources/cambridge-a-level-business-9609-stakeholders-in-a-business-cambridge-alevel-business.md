This guide goes deeper on **1.5 Stakeholders in a business**, the last subtopic of AS Level topic 1 (Business and its environment) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028.

The syllabus content is:

- **1.5.1 Business stakeholders**: individuals or groups with an interest in the activities of a business; internal and external stakeholders; the roles, rights and responsibilities of stakeholders.
- **1.5.2 The relative importance and influence of stakeholders on business activities**: the impact of business decisions on stakeholders and their reactions; the impact of stakeholder aims on business decisions; how and why a business needs to be accountable to its stakeholders; how conflict might arise from stakeholders having different aims and objectives; and how changing business objectives might affect stakeholders.

## Stakeholder or shareholder?

A **stakeholder** is any individual or group with an interest in what a business does. A **shareholder** owns part of a company. Every shareholder is a stakeholder, but most stakeholders are not shareholders. Confusing the two words in an exam answer is costly because it suggests the concept has not been understood.

Two broad views shape how businesses treat stakeholders:

- The **shareholder view**: managers should run the business mainly to benefit its owners. Other groups matter only so far as they affect returns.
- The **stakeholder view**: managers should take account of all groups affected by the business, even when this reduces short-term profit.

The two often converge over time. Well-treated employees are more productive and loyal customers return, so looking after stakeholders can raise long-term returns. They diverge most sharply in the short run, for example when a cost cut would raise this year's profit at the expense of jobs.

## Where stakeholder influence comes from

The syllabus asks about *relative* importance and influence. Influence comes from what a group controls that the business needs.

| Stakeholder | What it controls | How it can use that |
|---|---|---|
| Shareholders | Capital and votes | Vote at meetings, sell shares, back a takeover |
| Employees | Labour and skills | Work harder or leave; unions can bargain or take industrial action |
| Customers | Revenue | Switch to rivals, complain publicly |
| Suppliers | Inputs and credit | Raise prices, shorten credit terms, refuse to supply |
| Lenders | Loans and overdrafts | Refuse finance, raise interest, demand repayment |
| Government | Laws, licences, taxes | Fine, refuse permission, change regulations |
| Local community | Local opinion and planning objections | Campaign, object to planning applications |
| Pressure groups | Publicity | Media campaigns, boycotts |

From this table, the factors that make a stakeholder *more* influential are:

1. **How much the business depends on it**: one customer that buys 40% of output has far more power than thousands of small ones.
2. **How easily it can be replaced**: a supplier of a rare component has more power than a supplier of a common one.
3. **How organised it is**: a unionised workforce or a coordinated campaign has more power than scattered individuals.
4. **Legal rights**: shareholders' voting rights and government's legal powers give influence regardless of numbers.
5. **Publicity**: social media can give a small group influence far beyond its size.

## Mapping stakeholders by power and interest

A **power–interest grid** places each group on two scales: how much power it has over the decision, and how much it is affected by or interested in it.

- **High power, high interest**: manage closely; consult early.
- **High power, low interest**: keep satisfied; avoid giving them a reason to become involved.
- **Low power, high interest**: keep informed; their power may grow if they organise.
- **Low power, low interest**: monitor.

The key exam point is that positions change **with the decision**. Local residents may have little interest in a firm's pricing but high interest in a plan to run lorries past a school at night.

## Impact of decisions and stakeholder reactions: worked example

**Ferrowby Distribution** plans to automate its warehouse. The machines cost $1.2 million and will save $300 000 a year in wages by replacing 25 of its 80 warehouse jobs. The saving would cover the cost in 1 200 000 ÷ 300 000 = 4 years.

| Group | Impact | Likely reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Shareholders | Lower costs, higher future profit | Support, especially if four years to recover the cost is acceptable |
| Employees losing jobs | Redundancy | Opposition; union may negotiate redundancy terms or resist |
| Remaining employees | Retraining; fear of further cuts | Lower morale unless the change is explained |
| Customers | Faster, more accurate orders | Support, may order more |
| Local community | Fewer local jobs, less spending in shops | Opposition, possible media coverage |
| Lenders | Larger loan if borrowed, but stronger profits | Cautious support |

Strong analysis does not stop at the table. It explains **which reaction matters most here**. If the union could halt deliveries during peak season, employees' influence is high even though they are fewer than customers.

## Stakeholder aims shaping decisions before they are made

The syllabus also asks how stakeholder aims affect decisions, not just how decisions affect stakeholders. Managers anticipate reactions:

- A business may delay a price rise because it expects its largest customer to switch.
- A bank's lending conditions can limit how much a firm expands.
- Government targets on emissions can push a business to choose cleaner equipment.
- Shareholders' demand for dividends can reduce the profit kept for investment.

## Accountability: how and why

A business is **accountable** when it must explain and justify its actions to those affected.

**Why it matters**: stakeholders provide what the business needs, so lost trust has costs. Lenders charge more, customers leave, and regulators look more closely.

**How it is achieved**:

- Annual reports and published accounts for shareholders and lenders.
- Annual general meetings where shareholders can question directors and vote.
- Consultation with employees and their representatives before major changes.
- Reports to regulators and compliance with legal standards.
- Customer complaint procedures and public statements.
- For public-sector organisations, reporting to government and to the public on how tax money is used.

## Resolving stakeholder conflict

Conflict arises because aims differ; every decision creates winners and losers. The syllabus expects you to explain how the conflict arises, but the best answers also judge how it might be managed:

- **Prioritise**: decide which group's aim comes first, often the most powerful group, and accept opposition from others.
- **Compromise**: give each group part of what it wants, such as a smaller pay rise plus a smaller dividend increase.
- **Compensate**: redundancy pay, retraining or community funding can reduce opposition.
- **Communicate and consult**: explaining reasons early can reduce resistance even when the decision does not change.

## Changing objectives and their effect on stakeholders

When objectives change, so does the balance of winners and losers. Suppose a privately owned bus company is bought by a plc that switches its objective from steady service to maximising profit:

- Shareholders gain from higher expected returns.
- Passengers on less profitable rural routes may lose their service.
- Drivers may face tighter schedules but more job security if the company is financially stronger.
- The local council may face pressure to subsidise routes the company drops.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: To what extent should a business put shareholders' interests ahead of other stakeholders?*

> Shareholders own the business and bear the risk of losing their capital, so they have a legal claim to profit and can replace directors who ignore them. *(Knowledge and analysis: why shareholders have power.)* However, profit depends on other stakeholders: if employees are poorly paid, productivity may fall, and if customers feel exploited, they switch to rivals, so ignoring them can reduce shareholder returns. *(Counter-argument with a chain of reasoning.)* The extent depends on the time horizon and the power of the other groups: in the short term a cost cut may favour shareholders, but where employees or customers can easily leave, balancing their interests is more likely to protect long-term returns. *(Judgement stating what it depends on.)*

## Common mistakes

- Using "stakeholder" and "shareholder" interchangeably.
- Treating all stakeholders as equally influential.
- Explaining the impact on a group without its likely reaction, or the reaction without the reason.
- Saying "the business should satisfy everyone" without explaining how.
- Ignoring that power changes with the decision.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify**: name a stakeholder group or a right; no explanation needed.
- **Explain**: give the impact on a named group and why it reacts as it does.
- **Analyse**: trace consequences, such as decision → impact on group → reaction → effect back on the business.
- **Evaluate**, **Assess** or **To what extent**: weigh at least two groups' interests and decide which should carry most weight in this case.
- **Advise**: recommend how the business should handle the conflict, with reasons drawn from the case.
