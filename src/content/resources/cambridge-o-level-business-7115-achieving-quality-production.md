This guide covers subtopic **4.3 Achieving quality production** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series). The combined [Achieving Quality Production and Location Decisions study guide](/resources/o-level-business-achieving-quality-production-and-location-decisions/) explains quality control and quality assurance with sample-inspection calculations. This page goes further: quality in service businesses, the full cost of poor quality, where checks happen in a production process, how each approach is introduced step by step, and how to recommend an approach for a particular business.

## What the syllabus asks for

Section 4.3.1, why quality is important and how quality production might be achieved:

- what quality means and why it is important for all businesses
- the concept of quality control and how businesses implement quality control
- the concept of quality assurance and how this can be implemented.

## What quality means

A good or service is of **quality** when it meets customers' expectations and is fit for its purpose, **consistently**. Consistency matters: a restaurant that serves an excellent meal one day and a cold one the next does not have quality production.

What customers expect depends on the product and price:

| Product | What quality means to customers |
|---|---|
| A low-cost umbrella | Opens easily and keeps the rain off for a reasonable time |
| A mobile phone | Works reliably, battery lasts, no faults within normal use |
| A bus service | Runs on time, clean, safe, friendly drivers |
| A dental clinic | Correct treatment, hygiene, short waiting times, clear advice |
| An online shop | Correct item delivered on the promised day, easy returns |

For services, quality includes the **experience**: waiting time, staff manners, accuracy and reliability.

## Why quality matters for all businesses

**Benefits of good quality:**

- customers buy again and recommend the business, increasing sales
- a reputation for quality can support a higher price and strengthen the brand
- fewer complaints and returns save staff time and money
- business customers keep ordering from a reliable supplier
- the business is less likely to face legal action over unsafe products.

**The cost of poor quality** is often larger than it first appears.

**Worked example 1 (invented figures).** Brookvale Furniture makes 2,000 chairs a month at a production cost of $40 each and sells them for $75. Last month 5% were faulty.

```
Faulty chairs = 2,000 × 5% = 100
Found in the factory and scrapped: 60 chairs × $40 = $2,400 of wasted cost
Reached customers and were refunded: 40 chairs × $75 = $3,000 of lost revenue
Staff time handling the 40 complaints: 40 × $12 = $480
Visible cost of poor quality = 2,400 + 3,000 + 480 = $5,880 a month
```

There are further costs that cannot be measured easily: customers who never buy again, bad online reviews, and shops that stop stocking Brookvale's chairs. Quality problems found **after** the customer receives the product usually cost more than those found in the factory.

## Quality control

**Quality control** means checking products (or services) to find faults and remove faulty items before they reach the customer. Its focus is on **detecting** problems, usually by **inspectors**.

### Where checks happen in production

| Stage | Example check |
|---|---|
| Raw materials arriving | Inspect a delivery of wood for cracks or damp before it is used |
| During production | Measure a sample of chair legs after cutting to see they are the right length |
| Finished products | Test a sample of finished chairs for strength and finish before dispatch |

### How a business implements quality control

1. Set the standard each item must meet, such as a size, weight or appearance.
2. Decide how many items to inspect: every item for costly or safety-critical products, a sample for large quantities.
3. Train inspectors and give them measuring and testing equipment.
4. Remove faulty items to be scrapped or reworked, and record the number.
5. Report patterns of faults to managers.

In **service businesses**, quality control can mean checking work after it is done, for example a supervisor checking a cleaned hotel room, or reviewing recorded customer calls.

**Strength**: stops many faulty items reaching customers. **Weakness**: the fault has already been made, so materials and labour are wasted; inspectors cost money; sampling can miss faults; workers may think quality is someone else's job.

## Quality assurance

**Quality assurance** means making sure quality standards are met **at every stage** of production so that faults are **prevented** rather than found. Every employee is responsible for the quality of their own work.

### How a business implements quality assurance

1. **Agree standards for every stage** and explain them to all workers, from buying materials to delivery.
2. **Work with suppliers** so materials arrive to an agreed standard and need less checking.
3. **Train employees** so they can check their own work and know what to do if something is wrong.
4. **Give workers responsibility** to stop a process when they see a problem.
5. **Maintain machinery** on a schedule so it does not drift out of tolerance.
6. **Collect customer feedback** and use it to change the process.
7. **Review faults** to find the cause and change the process so the fault does not happen again.

**Worked example 2 (invented, continuing Brookvale).** Brookvale introduces quality assurance: suppliers must supply dry, graded wood, workers check each joint as they make it, and machines are serviced weekly. The extra cost is $2,000 a month for training time, servicing and higher-grade wood. The faulty rate falls to 1%.

```
Faulty chairs = 2,000 × 1% = 20; say 12 found in the factory and 8 reach customers
Visible cost of poor quality = (12 × 40) + (8 × 75) + (8 × 12) = 480 + 600 + 96 = $1,176
Saving compared with before = 5,880 − 1,176 = $4,704
Net saving after the extra cost = 4,704 − 2,000 = $2,704 a month
```

The visible net saving is $2,704 a month, and fewer unhappy customers should protect future sales too. The benefits took time to appear: training and supplier agreements must be in place before faults fall.

## Control or assurance? Making a recommendation

Most businesses use both: quality assurance to prevent faults, with some final checks as a safety net. When a question asks which to use, judge the business:

- **Size and type of production**: a large producer of identical items gains more from preventing faults across thousands of units; a craft workshop may simply check each item.
- **Cost of faults reaching customers**: safety-critical products such as car parts need strong assurance and final testing.
- **Workforce skills**: assurance needs trained, trusted workers.
- **Finance and time available**: assurance costs more at the start.

**Annotated model answer.** *Case: a food factory producing 50,000 ready meals a week has rising customer complaints about meals that are under-filled or past their date.*

> "The factory should introduce quality assurance. At 50,000 meals a week, inspecting at the end cannot check every meal, so faults will still reach customers." *(Application: uses the scale from the case.)*
>
> "Training workers to check portion weights at the filling stage and to record dates as meals are packed would prevent the faults happening, which reduces wasted food and complaints." *(Analysis: links method to cause and effect.)*
>
> "This will cost money for training and take time to work, so the factory should keep final sample checks until complaints fall. Because food safety and reputation are at stake, the long-term saving justifies the cost." *(Evaluation: weighs drawback, reaches judgement.)*

## Common mistakes

- Defining quality as "the best" or "expensive"; it means meeting customer expectations consistently.
- Saying quality control prevents faults; it finds them.
- Describing quality assurance without saying how it is implemented.
- Ignoring service businesses when the question says "all businesses".
- Recommending an approach without linking it to the size, product or problem in the case.

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Define**: for quality assurance, mention standards at every stage and prevention.
- **Calculate**: show the number of faulty items and each cost separately, as in the worked examples.
- **Explain**: give the method, then why it reduces faults or costs in this business.
- **Recommend / Justify**: choose control, assurance or both, link to two facts in the case, and weigh the cost.
