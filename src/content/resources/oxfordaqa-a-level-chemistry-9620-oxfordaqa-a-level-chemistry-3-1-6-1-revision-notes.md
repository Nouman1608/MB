These notes condense **3.1.6.1 Collision theory** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. For full explanations, read the [collision theory study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-1/); for exam-style questions, use the [collision theory practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-1-practice/).

## Core ideas

- Reactions happen only when particles **collide** with **sufficient energy**.
- That energy is the **activation energy, Ea**.
- **Ea**: the **minimum energy** that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur.
- Most collisions are **unsuccessful** because most have energy **less than Ea**.
- Rate depends on the **number of successful collisions per second** = collision frequency × fraction with E ≥ Ea.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Collision theory | The model that reactions occur through collisions between particles with enough energy |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy particles must have on collision for reaction to occur (kJ mol⁻¹) |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy ≥ Ea that leads to reaction |
| Collision frequency | Number of collisions per unit time |
| Enthalpy profile | Graph of enthalpy against progress of reaction, showing Ea and ΔH |
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time |

## Why most collisions fail

- Particles collide millions of times per second; if every collision worked, reactions would be almost instant.
- Particles have a spread of energies; only a small fraction of collisions reach Ea.
- Below Ea, the particles bounce apart unchanged.
- Colliding particles must also meet in a suitable orientation (a further reason some energetic collisions fail).

## Enthalpy profiles

| Feature | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Products relative to reactants | Lower | Higher |
| ΔH | Negative | Positive |
| Ea | Reactants to peak | Reactants to peak (at least as large as ΔH) |
| Ea of reverse reaction | Ea plus the size of ΔH (larger) | Ea − ΔH (smaller) |

General rule: **Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH(forward)**.

Axes: enthalpy (vertical), progress of reaction (horizontal). Arrows: Ea from reactant level up to the peak; ΔH from reactant level to product level.

## ΔH and rate are separate

- ΔH: how much energy is transferred overall.
- Ea: how easily the reaction starts.
- A large negative ΔH does **not** mean a fast reaction (hydrogen peroxide decomposes slowly without a catalyst; paper does not ignite in air until heated).

## Answer frame: "Why does this mixture not react?"

1. The particles are colliding.
2. Reaction needs collisions with at least Ea.
3. At this temperature very few collisions reach Ea.
4. So there are almost no successful collisions; the rate is negligible.

## Worked reminders

- **Reading a profile.** Reactants at 0, peak at +160, products at −70 (all kJ mol⁻¹): Ea = 160 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH = −70 kJ mol⁻¹, Ea(reverse) = 230 kJ mol⁻¹.
- **Comparing two reactions.** At the same temperature and collision frequency, a reaction with Ea = 45 kJ mol⁻¹ is faster than one with Ea = 85 kJ mol⁻¹, because a larger fraction of its collisions reach Ea.
- **Starting a reaction.** A flame or spark gives some particles energy above Ea; for an exothermic reaction, the heat released then lets further collisions exceed Ea, so the reaction continues on its own.

## Links to other subtopics

- The spread of energies, and the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea, is shown on the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (3.1.6.2).
- Temperature (3.1.6.3) changes that fraction; concentration and pressure (3.1.6.4) change only the collision frequency.
- A catalyst (3.1.6.5) provides a route with a lower Ea.

## Exam traps

- "Minimum" missing from the definition; or "energy needed to react" without mention of collisions.
- Ea drawn from the products, or from the bottom of the axis.
- "Exothermic reactions do not need activation energy."
- "The particles do not collide" as the reason for no reaction.
- Assuming a more exothermic reaction is faster.
- An endothermic profile with the hump lower than the products.

## Self-test

1. Define activation energy.
2. State the two conditions in collision theory for particles to react, according to this specification.
3. Why are most collisions between reactant particles unsuccessful?
4. What two quantities combine to give the number of successful collisions per second?
5. On an enthalpy profile, where is Ea measured from and to?
6. Is Ea positive or negative for an exothermic reaction?
7. A reaction has Ea = 120 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −85 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Ea for the reverse reaction.
8. A reaction has Ea = 95 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = +60 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Ea for the reverse reaction.
9. Can an endothermic reaction have Ea smaller than ΔH? Explain.
10. Why can a mixture of petrol vapour and air be stored without reacting?
11. A reaction is very exothermic. Does this tell you it is fast? Explain.

### Answers

1. The minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur.
2. They must collide, and the collision must have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.
3. Most collisions have less energy than the activation energy.
4. Collision frequency and the fraction of collisions with energy ≥ Ea.
5. From the reactants' enthalpy level up to the peak of the profile.
6. Positive (activation energy is always positive).
7. 120 + 85 = **205 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. 95 − 60 = **35 kJ mol⁻¹**.
9. No: the peak must be at least as high as the products, so Ea ≥ ΔH.
10. Almost no collisions at room temperature have energy ≥ Ea, so there are almost no successful collisions; a spark is needed.
11. No: ΔH says nothing about the activation energy, which controls how many collisions succeed.
