These notes condense section 14.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), for examination in 2025 and 2026: global patterns of resources, production and markets; FDI and the NIDL; the growth and spatial structure of TNCs, with one TNC case study; the emergence of NICs; and changes in the location of economic activity, such as outsourcing of manufacturing and offshoring of services. The full explanation is in the [14.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-globalisation-of-economic-activity/), and exam-style questions are in the [14.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-globalisation-of-economic-activity-practice/). The Nike case study is summarised in the [Topic 14 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-economic-transition-revision-notes/).

## Three maps of the global economy

- **Resources**: oil and gas (Middle East, Russia, the Americas); metal ores (Australia, Latin America, Africa); lower-cost labour (South and South-East Asia, Africa).
- **Production**: manufacturing concentrated in East and South-East Asia since the 1970s; design and research in high-income countries.
- **Markets**: greatest purchasing power in North America, Europe and East Asia; fastest growth in middle-income Asia and Latin America.

Globalisation links the three through flows of goods, capital, information and people.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| FDI | Investment giving a firm lasting control of a productive asset abroad |
| Portfolio investment | Buying shares or bonds without control; not FDI |
| Greenfield FDI | Building new facilities abroad |
| Brownfield FDI | Acquiring or merging with an existing foreign firm |
| Horizontal integration | Same activity in several countries |
| Vertical integration | Control of different stages of a supply chain |
| NIDL | Division of production between high-income countries (control, research) and lower-wage countries (assembly) |
| Glocalisation | Adapting a global product or service to local markets |
| NIC | A country that has moved quickly from a farming-based economy to one led by manufacturing exports |
| Export processing zone | Area with tax and customs incentives for export production |
| Outsourcing | Contracting work to another firm |
| Offshoring | Moving work to another country |
| Reshoring / nearshoring | Bringing work back home / moving it closer to the home market |

## FDI motives

- **Resource-seeking**: minerals, energy, crops.
- **Market-seeking**: sell inside a large market; avoid tariffs and currency risk.
- **Efficiency-seeking**: lower wages, land costs or tax.

Reverse flows now matter: Tata Motors (India) bought Jaguar Land Rover in 2008; Lenovo (China) bought IBM's PC business in 2005.

## TNC spatial structures

| Structure | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Hierarchical | Headquarters and research at home; regional headquarters in world cities; production in lower-cost places | Classic NIDL firms |
| Regional production | Plants inside each main market region | Toyota: Georgetown, Kentucky (1988); Burnaston, Derbyshire (1992) |
| Networked or contract | Brand controls design and logistics; contractors make the goods | Nike |

**Factors changing structure:** transport and ICT costs, tariffs and trade agreements, wage gaps, incentives, exchange rates, hazards and political risk.

## TNC case study template

1. Origin and headquarters. 2. Research, design and management. 3. Production (owned or contracted, where). 4. Markets and distribution. 5. Operation (supply chain, standards). 6. Change over time. 7. Evaluation (home and host impacts; fit with the NIDL).

## NICs

| Generation | Countries | Typical route |
|---|---|---|
| First (1960s onwards) | Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan | Strong state direction; domestic firms; export focus |
| Second (1970s-1980s onwards) | Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines | More reliance on inward FDI; export processing zones |
| Later | China, Vietnam | Very large labour pools; special economic zones |

**Malaysia:** Penang free trade zone in the early 1970s; Intel's first assembly plant outside the US opened there in 1972; electronics became a leading export.

## Relocation matrix

| | Same firm | Another firm |
|---|---|---|
| Same country | In-house | Domestic outsourcing |
| Another country | Captive offshoring | Offshore outsourcing |

**Causes:** wage gaps, ICT, containerisation, skilled graduates, incentives, focus on core work. **Host impacts:** jobs, skills, exports; low wages, dependence, urban concentration. **Source impacts:** cheaper goods, profits; job losses and regional decline.

## Exam traps

- Calling contracting "FDI".
- Listing locations without explaining why each function is there.
- Treating all NICs as following South Korea's route.
- Mixing up outsourcing and offshoring.
- Forgetting impacts on source countries.
- Presenting the NIDL as a complete explanation of today's patterns.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish FDI from portfolio investment.
2. What is the difference between greenfield and brownfield FDI?
3. Give the three main motives for FDI.
4. What is glocalisation?
5. Why did Toyota build plants in Kentucky and Derbyshire?
6. Name the four first-generation NICs.
7. How did the route of second-generation NICs often differ from South Korea's?
8. What happened in Penang in 1972?
9. Place "a UK bank moving its call centre to its own subsidiary in India" in the relocation matrix.
10. Give two impacts of offshoring on a source country.
11. Give two reasons for reshoring or nearshoring.
12. Give one way in which the NIDL fails to describe current patterns.

### Answers

1. FDI gives lasting control of a productive asset; portfolio investment buys shares or bonds without control.
2. Greenfield builds new facilities; brownfield acquires or merges with an existing firm.
3. Resource-seeking, market-seeking, efficiency-seeking.
4. Adapting a global product or service to suit local markets.
5. Market-seeking: producing inside the North American and European markets reduced exposure to trade barriers and exchange-rate swings.
6. Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan.
7. They relied more on inward FDI by foreign TNCs than on large domestic conglomerates.
8. Intel opened its first assembly plant outside the United States in the Penang free trade zone.
9. Captive offshoring (same firm, another country).
10. Job losses in affected sectors and regions; cheaper services and higher profits for firms (accept one cost and one benefit or two costs).
11. Rising wages in former low-cost locations; trade tensions; supply-chain disruption; automation (any two).
12. Firms from emerging economies invest in high-income countries, and NICs now do high-value design and research.
