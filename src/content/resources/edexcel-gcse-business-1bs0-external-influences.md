This guide covers content item 1.5.5, External influences, in Topic 1.5 (Understanding external influences on business) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It is the final item of Theme 1 and brings together 1.5.2 (technology), 1.5.3 (legislation) and 1.5.4 (the economy). The broader [guide to legislation, the economy and external influences](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-legislation-the-economy-and-external-influences/) lists possible responses. This page shows how to choose between responses and justify the choice, which is what the longer Paper 1 questions demand. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 1.5.5 contains

The specification lists one heading, the importance of external influences on business, with one point: possible responses by the business to changes in technology, legislation and the economic climate.

An **external influence** is a factor outside the business that it cannot control but that affects its revenue, costs or decisions. The specification names three for this item.

| External influence | Covered in | Examples of change |
|---|---|---|
| Technology | 1.5.2 | New payment systems; rivals selling online; new social media platforms |
| Legislation | 1.5.3 | A higher legal minimum wage; stricter safety or product rules |
| The economic climate | 1.5.4 | Rising unemployment; inflation; interest rate or exchange rate changes; tax changes |

## Why external influences are important

- **They can change revenue quickly.** A fall in consumer income or a rival's new website can cut sales without the business doing anything differently.
- **They can change costs.** A new law or a weaker pound can raise costs overnight.
- **They cannot be ignored.** Legal changes must be followed; economic and technological changes affect competitors, so ignoring them means falling behind.
- **They create opportunities as well as threats.** A weaker pound helps exporters; new technology can open new markets.

Small start-ups are often more exposed, because they have little cash to absorb a shock, but they can also respond quickly because decisions are made by one or two people.

## A toolkit of possible responses

Most responses fall into a few families. Learning them lets you build an answer for any change.

| Family of response | Examples |
|---|---|
| Change price | Raise price to cover higher costs; cut price to keep customers when incomes fall |
| Change product or range | Offer cheaper lines; drop unprofitable products; add features |
| Change promotion or place | Promote value; start selling online; offer delivery |
| Cut or control costs | Find cheaper suppliers; reduce staff hours; reduce waste; renegotiate rent |
| Invest | Buy new technology; train staff; improve safety equipment |
| Change finance | Pay off variable-rate loans; delay borrowing; build a cash reserve |
| Change markets or suppliers | Sell abroad when the pound is weak; buy from UK suppliers when imports are dear |

### Matching responses to each type of change

**Technology change:** adopt the technology (for example, contactless payment or an online shop), train staff to use it, or compete differently (for example, on personal service) if the business cannot afford it.

**Legislation change:** comply first, because there is no legal choice. Then decide how to cover the cost: raise prices, absorb the cost, or find savings. A business may also use compliance in its promotion, for example highlighting fair treatment of staff.

**Economic change:** depends on the factor. Falling incomes: cheaper ranges, value promotion. Rising interest rates: reduce borrowing, delay expansion. Inflation: review prices and suppliers. Exchange rate changes: switch suppliers or target export markets.

## Judging a response: five tests

To reach a supported judgement, test each response against five questions:

1. **Cost:** how much does it cost, and can the business afford it?
2. **Speed:** how quickly will it work?
3. **Risk:** what could go wrong, and how likely is it?
4. **Customers and competitors:** how will customers react, and what will rivals do?
5. **Objectives:** does it fit the owner's aims, such as survival, profit or independence?

A response can be right for one business and wrong for another. A price rise suits a business with loyal customers and few rivals; it could be disastrous for one in a crowded market.

## Worked example: Larkspur Florist responds to two changes at once

Larkspur Florist buys many of its flowers from the Netherlands, paid in euros. Two changes happen together:

- The pound weakens from £1 = €1.15 to £1 = €1.05.
- Consumer incomes fall, and the owner forecasts 10% fewer bouquet sales.

Before the changes, Larkspur sells 4,000 bouquets a year at £30. Imported flowers cost €11.50 per bouquet, and other variable costs are £6 per bouquet.

**Step 1: the effect of the exchange rate on costs**

- Flower cost before = €11.50 ÷ 1.15 = £10.00
- Flower cost after = €11.50 ÷ 1.05 = £10.95 (to the nearest penny)
- Contribution per bouquet falls from £30 − £16.00 = £14.00 to £30 − £16.95 = £13.05

**Step 2: the effect of falling incomes on sales**

- Sales fall to 4,000 × 0.9 = 3,600 bouquets
- Total contribution before = 4,000 × £14.00 = £56,000
- Total contribution after = 3,600 × £13.05 = £46,980, a fall of £9,020

**Step 3: compare two responses**

- **Response A: raise the price to £32.** Contribution becomes £32 − £16.95 = £15.05. If sales fall further, to 3,300, total contribution = 3,300 × £15.05 = £49,665.
- **Response B: switch to UK-grown flowers costing £10.40, paid in pounds.** Contribution = £30 − £16.40 = £13.60. At 3,600 sales, total contribution = 3,600 × £13.60 = £48,960.

On these forecasts Response A gives slightly more (£705 more), but it rests on sales falling only to 3,300 when customers are already short of money. Response B protects the florist from further falls in the pound and keeps prices unchanged at a time when incomes are falling. A good judgement might choose B, or combine B with a small price rise, and say that the decision depends on how customers react to price at this time.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse style):** Analyse how Hilltop Bakery could respond to a rise in the legal minimum wage. [6]

> Hilltop must pay the new minimum, so its wage costs will rise *(legislation, no choice)*. It could raise the price of its bread slightly to cover the cost *(response 1)*, but customers might buy from the supermarket instead, which could reduce sales *(risk)*. Alternatively it could buy a more efficient oven so the same staff produce more loaves per hour *(response 2)*, which spreads the higher wage cost over more output, although the oven costs money now and only saves money later *(cost and timing)*.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the change without suggesting a response.
- Suggesting a response that ignores the law, such as paying below the legal minimum.
- Offering a response with no evaluation of its cost, speed or risk.
- Giving the same response to every change, usually "raise prices".
- Forgetting that external changes can be opportunities.

## Exam technique

- **Outline:** one response and its effect on the named business.
- **Analyse:** response, then consequence, then a risk or limitation.
- **Justify:** two responses are given; use the data to recommend one and say what the recommendation depends on.
- **Evaluate:** weigh responses with the five tests and reach a supported conclusion.
