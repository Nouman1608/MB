These notes condense the topic **Employment** in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, for first assessment in 2016. The topic covers employment and unemployment, the objective of full employment, the Labour Force Survey and claimant count, the causes and consequences of unemployment and the effects of full employment. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [employment and unemployment study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-employment-unemployment-ocr-aslevel-economics/). Practise with the [employment and unemployment practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-employment-unemployment-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Employed | In paid work, including part-time and self-employed |
| Unemployed | Without a job, willing and able to work, actively seeking work |
| Economically inactive | Neither in work nor seeking it (students, retired, carers, many long-term sick) |
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Unemployment rate | Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 |
| Full employment | Everyone who wants a job at the going wage can find one, apart from short spells between jobs |
| Labour Force Survey (LFS) | Household survey using the international (ILO) definition |
| Claimant count | Number claiming unemployment-related benefits |
| Hidden unemployment | People who want work but have stopped looking, so count as inactive |
| Underemployment | Workers who want more hours or a job matching their skills |

## Method: the unemployment rate

1. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
2. Rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
3. Changes in a rate are in **percentage points**.

**Worked reminder.** Country T: employed 15.2 m, unemployed 0.8 m. Labour force = 16.0 m; rate = **5.0%**. If unemployment rises to 1.2 m with the labour force unchanged, the rate is **7.5%**, a rise of **2.5 percentage points** (and a 50% rise in the number unemployed).

**Lost output reminder.** 0.4 m extra unemployed × £40,000 output per worker = **£16 bn** a year.

## LFS versus claimant count

| | LFS | Claimant count |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Household survey | Benefit records |
| Counts | Jobless, sought work in last four weeks, can start within two weeks (or waiting to start a job) | Benefit claimants only |
| Plus | Internationally comparable; includes non-claimants | Fast, cheap, local detail |
| Minus | Sampling error; slower | Misses non-claimants; changes with benefit rules |
| Usually | Higher | Lower |

Neither captures hidden unemployment or underemployment.

## Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Remedy | Responds to more AD? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Search time between jobs | Better vacancy information | Little |
| Structural | Skills or location mismatch, industrial decline, technology | Retraining, relocation help | Little |
| Cyclical | Low aggregate demand | Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy | Yes |
| Seasonal | Predictable changes in demand during the year | Rarely a policy target | No |

## Consequences of unemployment

- **Individuals:** lost income, skill loss, poorer health, lower future earnings.
- **Firms:** weaker demand; easier recruitment, less wage pressure.
- **Government:** less tax, more benefits, wider deficit.
- **Economy:** lost output (inside the PPC), lower growth, inequality, regional decline.

Severity depends on duration, type and concentration.

## Effects of full employment

| Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|
| Output near potential | Wage-push inflation |
| Higher tax revenue, lower benefit spending | Skill shortages |
| Less poverty and inequality | More imports, weaker current account |

Sustainable only if productive capacity grows.

## Evaluation chains

- **Cyclical unemployment:** more AD (lower interest rates, higher government spending) raises output and hiring, but works with time lags and only if firms and households are confident enough to spend.
- **Structural unemployment:** retraining and help with moving can reduce it, but they take years, cost money and work only if employers want the new skills.
- **Duration:** the longer people are unemployed, the more their skills and contacts fade, so cyclical unemployment can turn into structural unemployment.
- **Concentration:** unemployment focused in one region lowers local spending, which spreads job losses to other local firms.
- **Measurement:** a fall in the claimant count may reflect stricter benefit rules rather than more jobs, so check the LFS figure too.
- **Links to other diagrams:** unemployment caused by low AD puts the economy inside its PPC; on an AD/AS diagram, it is output below the full-employment level.

## Exam traps

- Use the labour force, not the population, as the denominator.
- Students and retired people are inactive, not unemployed.
- Full employment is not zero unemployment.
- Rate changes are in percentage points.
- Match the remedy to the type: more AD does little for structural unemployment.
- The claimant count is not simply "wrong"; it measures a different group.

## Self-test

1. Define unemployment.
2. Who is included in the labour force?
3. Employed 27.6 m, unemployed 2.4 m. Calculate the unemployment rate.
4. The rate rises from 4.0% to 5.5%. State the change.
5. Why is the LFS measure usually higher than the claimant count?
6. Give one strength of the claimant count.
7. Distinguish frictional from structural unemployment.
8. Which type of unemployment is reduced most by raising aggregate demand?
9. 0.25 m workers become unemployed; each produced £48,000 a year. Calculate the lost output.
10. Give two costs of unemployment to the government.
11. Why does full employment not mean zero unemployment?
12. Give one risk of full employment.

### Answers

1. Being without a job while willing and able to work and actively seeking work.
2. The employed plus the unemployed.
3. Labour force 30.0 m; rate = 2.4 ÷ 30.0 × 100 = **8.0%**.
4. A rise of **1.5 percentage points**.
5. Many unemployed people are not entitled to or do not claim benefits, but are counted by the survey.
6. Any one of: quick, cheap, available for small areas, no sampling error.
7. Frictional: short spells between jobs while searching. Structural: a lasting mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available.
8. Cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
9. 0.25 m × £48,000 = **£12 bn** a year.
10. Lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending.
11. Some frictional unemployment always exists as people move between jobs or enter the labour market.
12. Any one of: wage-push inflation, skill shortages, a worsening current account.
