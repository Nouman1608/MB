## The four outcomes in 3.8

1. Calculate and interpret twelve financial measures and ratios.
2. Appraise performance: profitability, liquidity, efficiency, capital structure.
3. Explain the difference between cash and profits, and the effect of transactions on profitability and liquidity.
4. State the limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis, both financial and non-financial.

The specification says you must know the ratio formulae.

## Formula sheet

| Ratio | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | GP / Revenue × 100 | % |
| Markup | GP / Cost of sales × 100 | % |
| Profit in relation to revenue | Profit for year (before tax) / Revenue × 100 | % |
| Expenses in relation to revenue | Expenses / Revenue × 100 | % |
| ROCE, sole trader | Profit before interest / (Capital + NCL) × 100 | % |
| ROCE, limited company | Profit from operations / (Equity + NCL) × 100 | % |
| Current ratio | CA / CL | x:1 |
| Liquid capital (acid test) ratio | (CA - Inventory) / CL | x:1 |
| Rate of inventory turnover | Cost of sales / Average inventory | times |
| Inventory turnover (days) | Average inventory / Cost of sales × 365 | days |
| Trade receivable days | Trade receivables / Credit sales × 365 | days |
| Trade payable days | Trade payables / Credit purchases × 365 | days |
| Capital gearing | NCL / (Issued share capital + Reserves + NCL) × 100 | % |

NCL = non-current liabilities; CA and CL = current assets and current liabilities. Average inventory = (opening + closing) / 2. For ROCE, opening or closing capital (or equity) may be used. For a sole trader's gearing, use capital in place of share capital plus reserves.

## Useful links between ratios

- Gross margin % - expenses to revenue % = profit to revenue %.
- Margin from markup: margin = markup / (100 + markup) × 100. Markup 25% gives margin 20%.
- Markup from margin: markup = margin / (100 - margin) × 100. Margin 40% gives markup 66.67%.
- Inventory days = 365 / inventory turnover.

## What a change usually means

| Ratio moves | Possible causes | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin falls | Selling prices cut, discounts, dearer purchases, change in sales mix | Less profit per £ of sales |
| Expenses to revenue rises | Overheads growing faster than sales, one-off costs | Profit squeezed below gross profit |
| ROCE falls | Lower profit, or new capital or loans not yet earning | Weaker return on long-term funds |
| Current ratio rises well above 2:1 | Surplus inventory, slow receivables, idle cash | Resources not working |
| Liquid ratio falls below 1:1 | Cash drained, inventory built up | May struggle to pay debts on time |
| Inventory days rise | Overbuying, slow-moving or obsolete lines | Cash tied up, risk of write-down |
| Receivable days rise | Weak credit control, longer terms to win sales | Cash arrives later; irrecoverable debt risk |
| Payable days rise | Using supplier credit, or struggling to pay | Helps cash but may cost discounts or goodwill |
| Gearing rises | New loan, or equity falling | Higher fixed interest; more risk to owners and lenders |

Treat these as possible causes. In an answer, pick the one the data supports and say why.

## Method in steps: appraisal answer

1. Calculate the ratios asked for and show the formula with figures.
2. Group them: profitability, liquidity, efficiency, capital structure.
3. Compare each with last year, a competitor or the business's own target.
4. Give a likely cause from the information in the question.
5. Link groups: for example, longer receivable days explain a lower liquid ratio.
6. Bring in a limitation or a non-financial factor.
7. Finish with a judgement that answers the question set.

## Cash versus profit

| Item | Profit | Cash |
|---|---|---|
| Credit sale | Up when the sale is made | Up only when the customer pays |
| Depreciation | Down | No effect |
| Buy non-current asset for cash | No effect now (depreciation later) | Down |
| Buy inventory for cash | No effect until sold | Down |
| Loan received | No effect (interest later is an expense) | Up |
| Loan repaid | No effect | Down |
| Capital introduced | No effect | Up |
| Drawings | No effect | Down |
| Accrued expense | Down | No effect until paid |

**Key point:** a profitable business can run out of cash, and a loss-making one can hold plenty of cash for a while.

## Effect of transactions on the current ratio

- When current assets and current liabilities change by the same amount and the ratio is above 1:1, both rising pulls the ratio **towards** 1:1 and both falling pushes it **further above** 1:1.
- Swaps within current assets (receivable pays cash) leave the current ratio unchanged.
- Cash spent on inventory leaves the current ratio unchanged but lowers the liquid ratio.
- Selling inventory at a profit raises current assets and both ratios.

**Worked reminder.** Current assets are £30,000, including inventory of £12,000, and current liabilities are £20,000. The current ratio is 1.50:1 and the liquid ratio is 18,000 / 20,000 = 0.90:1. The business then buys inventory of £4,000 and pays cash. Current assets stay at £30,000, so the current ratio stays at 1.50:1. Inventory rises to £16,000, so the liquid ratio falls to 14,000 / 20,000 = 0.70:1. Profit is unchanged.

## Limitations to quote

**Financial:** historic data; historic cost ignores inflation; different accounting policies; estimates (depreciation, doubtful debts); one year-end date may be untypical or window-dressed; needs a benchmark; missing data such as credit sales.

**Non-financial:** staff skill and morale; customer satisfaction and reputation; market conditions and competitors; product quality; environmental and social impact; quality of management.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Margin vs markup:** same gross profit, different base (revenue vs cost of sales).
- **Profit to revenue vs ROCE:** one is per £ of sales; the other is per £ of long-term funds.
- **Current vs liquid ratio:** the liquid ratio leaves out inventory because it is the least liquid current asset.
- **Turnover in times vs days:** a higher "times" figure and a lower "days" figure both mean faster-moving inventory.
- **Sole trader vs company ROCE:** profit before interest with capital, or profit from operations with equity.

## Quick self-test

1. Markup is 50%. What is the gross profit margin?
2. Gross profit margin is 20%. What is the markup?
3. Cost of sales £96,000; average inventory £12,000. Find turnover in times and days.
4. CA £45,000 including inventory £15,000; CL £25,000. Find the current and liquid ratios.
5. Trade receivables £18,000; credit sales £146,000. Find receivable days.
6. Share capital £150,000, reserves £60,000, NCL £90,000. Find capital gearing.
7. A company has profit from operations £42,000, equity £250,000 and NCL £100,000. Find ROCE.
8. A trade receivable pays £3,000 by cheque. Effect on the current ratio?
9. A machine is bought for cash. Effect on this year's profit at the date of purchase?
10. Name two non-financial factors that ratios ignore.
11. Expenses £36,000; revenue £160,000. Find expenses in relation to revenue.
12. A sole trader's profit for the year is £27,000 after loan interest of £3,000. Capital £120,000; loan £30,000. Find ROCE.

### Answers

1. 50 / 150 × 100 = **33.33%**
2. 20 / 80 × 100 = **25.00%**
3. 96,000 / 12,000 = **8 times**; 12,000 / 96,000 × 365 = **45.6 days**
4. 45,000 / 25,000 = **1.80:1**; 30,000 / 25,000 = **1.20:1**
5. 18,000 / 146,000 × 365 = **45.0 days**
6. 90,000 / 300,000 × 100 = **30.00%**
7. 42,000 / 350,000 × 100 = **12.00%**
8. **No change**: one current asset falls and another rises by the same amount.
9. **None** at purchase; depreciation reduces profit over the asset's life. Liquidity falls.
10. Any two: staff morale, customer satisfaction, reputation, market conditions, product quality, environmental impact.
11. 36,000 / 160,000 × 100 = **22.50%**
12. (27,000 + 3,000) / 150,000 × 100 = **20.00%**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total revenue for receivable days when credit sales are given.
- Using closing inventory instead of average inventory.
- Writing the current ratio as 1.8 or 180% instead of 1.80:1.
- Leaving loan interest in sole-trader ROCE, so the return is understated.
- Using profit for the year instead of profit from operations in company ROCE.
- Describing a change ("receivable days rose") with no cause and no consequence.
- Calling a high current ratio "good" without checking what makes it up.
- Saying depreciation reduces cash.
- Listing limitations that have nothing to do with the business in the question.
- Giving no final judgement when the question says evaluate or recommend.
