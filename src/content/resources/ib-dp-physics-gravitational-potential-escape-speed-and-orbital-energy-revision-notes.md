## Definitions

- **Gravitational potential energy** Ep of a system: the work needed to build the system by bringing its parts in from infinite separation.
- **Gravitational potential** Vg at a point: work done per kilogram when a small test mass is carried in from infinity to that point. Zero at infinity.
- **Equipotential surface**: a surface on which Vg has the same value everywhere.
- **Escape speed**: the speed at a given point that lets a body reach infinity with no kinetic energy left (no drag, no further push).

## Formulas

| Quantity | Result | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ep, two bodies | -G m1 m2 / r | r between centres; negative |
| Vg | -GM / r | scalar; J kg⁻¹ |
| Ep and Vg | Ep = m Vg | |
| Field strength | g = -ΔVg / Δr | gradient; points to lower Vg |
| Work moving a mass | W = m ΔVg | ΔVg = final - initial |
| Escape speed | vesc = √(2GM/r) | independent of m |
| Orbital speed | vorbital = √(GM/r) | circular orbit |
| Orbital energies | Ek = GMm/2r; Ep = -GMm/r; E = -GMm/2r | Ek = -E, Ep = 2E |

## Method boxes

**Work to move a mass between two points**
1. Find Vg at both positions with r from the centre.
2. ΔVg = final - initial.
3. W = m ΔVg. Positive means you supply energy.

**Escape or maximum height**
1. Write total energy per kilogram at the start: ½v² - GM/r.
2. Escape: this is zero or more. Not escaping: set it equal to -GM/rmax and solve for rmax.
3. Subtract the radius if the question asks for height.

**Orbit changes**
1. Work out E = -GMm/2r at each radius.
2. Energy to supply = E(final) - E(initial).
3. Find speeds with √(GM/r); the larger orbit has the smaller speed.

**Launch from rest on a non-rotating planet to a circular orbit**
1. Energy needed = E(orbit) - E(surface) = GMm(1/R - 1/2r).
2. Split it: Ek at orbit = GMm/2r; the rest raises Ep.

## Equipotentials and field lines

- Concentric spheres around a point or spherical mass, with centre-to-centre distance used for r.
- Field lines meet equipotentials at 90 degrees and point from high to low Vg.
- Equally spaced Vg steps: close spacing means a strong field; wide spacing means a weak field.
- Moving a mass along an equipotential costs no work.
- Near a surface the equipotentials are almost parallel and equally spaced, so the field is approximately uniform.
- Potentials of several bodies add as scalars. Along the line joining two masses, g = 0 where the gradient of the total Vg is zero.

## Atmospheric drag on a satellite

Drag is small, so treat the orbit as a slow spiral through near-circular orbits.

| Quantity | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Total energy | decreases | drag does negative work |
| Orbit radius | decreases | E = -GMm/2r becomes more negative |
| Speed | **increases** | v = √(GM/r) |
| Ep | decreases (by twice the Ek gain) | smaller r |
| Ek | increases | v larger |

## Worked reminders

**Ratio reasoning.** Planet B has twice the mass and half the radius of planet A. vesc scales as √(M/R), so vesc(B) = √(2 ÷ 0.5) = 2 × vesc(A).

**Equal-spaced check.** For GM = 1.2 × 10¹³ N m² kg⁻¹, the surface Vg = -3.0 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ is at 4.0 × 10⁶ m. Halving r doubles |Vg|: the equal Vg steps are closer together near the planet.

**Escape from orbit.** At any circular orbit vesc = √2 × vorbital, so an increase in speed of 41% from orbital speed is enough to escape. The energy needed is |E| = GMm/2r, equal to the satellite's Ek.

**Launch energetics with numbers.** For the same planet (GM = 1.2 × 10¹³, R = 4.0 × 10⁶ m), a 100 kg satellite is launched from rest at the surface into a circular orbit of radius 5.0 × 10⁶ m. The surface energy is -GMm/R = -3.0 × 10⁸ J. The orbit energy is -GMm/2r = -1.2 × 10⁸ J. The input is 1.8 × 10⁸ J. Of this, 1.2 × 10⁸ J is the orbital Ek and 6.0 × 10⁷ J is the rise in Ep (from -3.0 × 10⁸ J to -2.4 × 10⁸ J).

## Reading graphs

- **Vg against r**: all values are negative and rise towards zero as r increases. The gradient at a point is the magnitude of g there, so the curve is steepest near the body.
- **g against r**: the area under the curve between two radii equals the change in Vg between them, in J kg⁻¹. Count squares or use trapezia and state the units.
- **Ep against r**: the same shape as Vg, scaled by m. The depth of the well at the starting radius is the energy needed to escape from rest.
- A tangent drawn on a Vg graph gives g at that point. Use a large triangle and show both sides of it.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Ep and Vg**: energy of a system against potential per unit mass.
- **Escape speed and orbital speed**: factor √2 at the same r.
- **Orbit energy and escape energy**: E is negative; escape needs E raised to zero.
- **Field strength and potential**: g is a vector (gradient); Vg is a scalar (value).
- **Speed and energy in a bigger orbit**: the energy rises, the speed falls.

## Command words in this unit

- **Define**: give the exact wording, including "per unit mass", "from infinity" and the zero reference.
- **Derive**: start from a physical statement (centripetal force equal to gravity, or total energy equal to zero) and rearrange step by step.
- **Explain**: link cause to effect in order, for example drag, energy loss, smaller radius, larger speed.
- **Sketch**: label axes, show the sign of Vg and make the spacing of equipotentials match the field strength.
- **Estimate**: use a small interval for a gradient and state the assumption.

## Quick self-test

Use GM = 1.2 × 10¹³ N m² kg⁻¹ for a planet, r measured from its centre, where a calculation is needed.

1. State what gravitational potential at a point means.
2. Calculate Vg at r = 4.0 × 10⁶ m.
3. Find Ep for a 50 kg mass at that distance.
4. Find the work to move the 50 kg mass from r = 4.0 × 10⁶ m to 8.0 × 10⁶ m.
5. Over a short distance Vg rises by 6.0 × 10⁴ J kg⁻¹ across 2.0 × 10⁴ m, moving away from the body. State |g| and its direction.
6. Find the escape speed at r = 4.0 × 10⁶ m.
7. Find the orbital speed at that radius.
8. A 200 kg satellite orbits at 4.0 × 10⁶ m. Find Ek, Ep and E.
9. How much energy is needed to move it to 8.0 × 10⁶ m?
10. State what happens to the orbit height and speed of a satellite in very thin atmosphere.
11. Why can no work be done by gravity along an equipotential?
12. By what factor must the speed of a satellite in circular orbit increase for it to escape?

### Answers

1. The work done per kilogram of a small test mass carried from infinity to the point.
2. -GM/r = -1.2e13 / 4.0e6 = **-3.0 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹**.
3. Ep = 50 × (-3.0e6) = **-1.5 × 10⁸ J**.
4. Vg at 8.0e6 is -1.5e6; ΔVg = +1.5e6; W = 50 × 1.5e6 = **7.5 × 10⁷ J**.
5. |g| = 6.0e4 / 2.0e4 = **3.0 N kg⁻¹**, directed towards the body (towards lower potential).
6. √(2 × 1.2e13 / 4.0e6) = √(6.0e6) = **2.4 × 10³ m s⁻¹** (2.45 × 10³).
7. √(1.2e13 / 4.0e6) = √(3.0e6) = **1.7 × 10³ m s⁻¹** (1.73 × 10³).
8. Ek = GMm/2r = **3.0 × 10⁸ J**; Ep = **-6.0 × 10⁸ J**; E = **-3.0 × 10⁸ J**.
9. E at 8.0e6 is -1.5e8 J; energy to add = **1.5 × 10⁸ J**.
10. The orbit shrinks and the speed increases.
11. ΔVg = 0, so W = mΔVg = 0; the field is perpendicular to the surface.
12. **√2** (about 1.41).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Substituting altitude for r, which must run to the planet centre.
- Dropping the minus sign on Vg or Ep.
- Using mgh for a large change in height.
- Saying drag makes the satellite slower.
- Confusing vesc with vorbital, or quoting vesc as √(GM/r).
- Using the wrong sign for ΔVg, so the work comes out negative when energy must be supplied.
- Leaving out units, or giving a potential in joules rather than J kg⁻¹.
- Forgetting that field lines cross equipotentials at right angles in a sketch.
- Reporting that a bigger orbit has a faster speed because it has more energy.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is gravitational potential examined at IB DP Physics SL?**
No. This is the additional higher level part of D.1, so all of it is HL only.

**Does escape speed depend on the mass of the escaping object?**
No. The escape speed vesc = √(2GM/r) is independent of the escaping object's mass and assumes no drag.

**How are escape speed and orbital speed related?**
At the same radius the escape speed is √2 times the orbital speed, so a satellite in circular orbit escapes if its speed increases by about 41%.

**What does the area under a g against r graph show?**
The area under the curve between two radii equals the change in gravitational potential between them, in J kg⁻¹.
