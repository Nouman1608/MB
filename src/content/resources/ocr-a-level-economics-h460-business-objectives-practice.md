## Section A: Short questions

**1.** A firm produces where marginal revenue is zero. Which objective is it pursuing? **[1]**

A: Profit maximisation

B: Sales revenue maximisation

C: Sales volume maximisation

D: Profit satisficing

**2.** Which **one** of the following best defines normal profit? **[1]**

A: Total revenue minus total explicit costs

B: The minimum return needed to keep resources in their current use

C: Profit above the level needed to keep the firm in the industry

D: Profit when marginal revenue equals marginal cost

**3.** Which **one** of the following is an external economy of scale? **[1]**

A: A firm buys raw materials in bulk at a discount

B: A firm borrows at a lower interest rate because of its size

C: A local college trains workers in skills the whole industry needs

D: A firm installs larger, more efficient machinery

**4.** Distinguish between fixed costs and variable costs, giving an example of each for a café. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why the law of diminishing returns causes marginal cost to rise in the short run. **[3]**

**6.** A plumber's business earns revenue of £400 thousand a year and pays explicit costs of £340 thousand. The owner gave up a salaried job paying £45 thousand a year to run it. Calculate the accounting profit and the supernormal profit, and state what the figures show. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** Use the Tern Tiles table.

**(a)** Calculate average cost at an output of 4 thousand boxes. **[1]**

**(b)** State the profit-maximising output, price and profit, and explain why a higher output would reduce profit. **[3]**

**(c)** Tern Tiles maximises sales revenue subject to a profit of at least £40 thousand. State the output and total revenue. **[2]**

**(d)** State the output that maximises sales volume while avoiding a loss, and explain why output 9 is ruled out. **[2]**

**8.** The owner of Tern Tiles satisfices, accepting any profit of at least £45 thousand.

**(a)** State the outputs that meet this target. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why an owner might satisfice rather than maximise profit. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **two** internal economies of scale that a large supermarket chain might enjoy. **[4]**

**10.** Tern Tiles is bought by a large group. Its new managers receive a bonus based on sales revenue, while shareholders want maximum profit. Using the table, explain how this could create a principal-agent problem. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that large firms will always benefit from economies of scale. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that firms should pursue corporate social responsibility rather than profit maximisation. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Total revenue is at its maximum where MR = 0. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Option A is accounting profit; C is supernormal profit; D is the profit-maximising condition. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. It lowers costs for all firms in the industry, not just one. A, B and D are internal economies. **[1]**

**4.** Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run [1], for example the café's rent or insurance [1]. Variable costs change directly with output [1], for example coffee beans, milk or hourly staff wages [1]. **[4]**

**5.** In the short run at least one factor, such as capital, is fixed [1]. As more of a variable factor such as labour is added, each extra worker eventually adds less output than the previous one, because they share the fixed factor [1]. Each extra unit of output therefore needs more labour, so the cost of producing it, marginal cost, rises [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Accounting profit = 400 − 340 = £60 thousand [1]. Supernormal profit = 60 − 45 = £15 thousand, treating the forgone salary as normal profit [1]. The business earns more than enough to keep the owner in it, so it is worth continuing [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Average cost = 62 ÷ 4 = **£15.50** per box (£15.50 thousand per thousand boxes) [1].

**(b)** Output 5 thousand boxes, price £26, profit £54 thousand [1]. Moving from 4 to 5, MR (18) exceeds MC (14), so profit rises [1]. Moving from 5 to 6, MR (14) is less than MC (18), so profit would fall, to £50 thousand [1].

**(c)** Outputs with profit of at least 40 are 4, 5 and 6; the highest revenue among them is at **6 thousand boxes** [1], with TR of **£144 thousand** [1].

**(d)** Output **8 thousand boxes**, where profit is still positive (£18 thousand) [1]. At output 9 total cost (172) exceeds total revenue (162), a loss of £10 thousand, so it is not sustainable [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** Profits of 50, 54 and 50 meet the target, at outputs **4, 5 and 6 thousand boxes** [1]. Outputs 3 (38) and 7 (38) fall short [1].

**(b)** The owner may have other aims, such as more leisure or less stress, once profit is satisfactory [1]; or may lack the information on MR and MC needed to find the exact profit maximum, so settles for an acceptable result [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Purchasing economies: the chain buys stock in huge quantities [1], so it can negotiate lower prices per unit from suppliers, lowering average cost [1]. Financial economies: a large, established chain is seen as less risky by lenders [1], so it can borrow at lower interest rates or raise finance by issuing shares more cheaply [1]. Other valid economies: technical (large distribution centres and automated tills); managerial (specialist managers); risk-bearing (many product lines and locations). **[4]**

**10.** Shareholders (principals) want output 5, where profit is highest at £54 thousand [1]. Managers (agents) earn more if revenue is higher, so they have an incentive to expand output, for example to 7, where revenue is £154 thousand rather than £130 thousand [1]. Profit at output 7 is only £38 thousand, £16 thousand less than the maximum [1]. Shareholders cannot easily see whether lower profit reflects managerial choice or market conditions, because managers have more information; this asymmetric information is what allows the problem to persist [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Define economies of scale: falling long-run average cost as the scale of output rises [1].
- Purchasing economies: bulk buying lowers unit costs [1].
- Technical economies: larger, specialised capital is more efficient at high output [1].
- Financial and risk-bearing economies: cheaper finance and diversification [1].
- Application: an example such as a car maker or supermarket chain [1].
- But diseconomies arise: communication and co-ordination problems raise average cost beyond some size [1].
- Motivation may fall in very large organisations, lowering productivity [1].
- Minimum efficient scale may be reached at a modest output, so further growth brings no cost advantage [1].
- Benefits depend on the industry: high in industries with large fixed costs, low in personal services [1].
- Judgement: large firms often benefit up to a point, but not always, with a reasoned conclusion naming the deciding factor [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Define CSR: voluntary action for stakeholders beyond legal requirements [1].
- Define profit maximisation: output where MR = MC [1].
- For CSR: it can build brand loyalty, raising demand and long-run revenue [1].
- For CSR: it can help recruit and keep staff, lowering costs [1].
- For CSR: it can reduce risks such as fines, boycotts or supply disruption [1].
- Against: CSR raises costs, reducing short-run profit and funds for investment [1].
- Against: in highly competitive markets, firms with higher costs may not survive [1].
- Against: shareholders, as owners, may expect returns, and managers may use CSR to pursue their own aims [1].
- CSR and profit need not conflict: CSR may be a way of maximising long-run profit [1].
- Judgement: the choice depends on competition, ownership and customers' willingness to pay, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers point out that CSR and long-run profit can be complements, then judge when they conflict.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading MR and MC from the wrong row.
- Choosing the highest-revenue output as profit maximising.
- Ignoring the profit floor in constrained questions.
- Forgetting the owner's opportunity cost when finding supernormal profit.
- Confusing diminishing returns with diseconomies of scale.
- Describing the principal-agent problem without the information gap.
