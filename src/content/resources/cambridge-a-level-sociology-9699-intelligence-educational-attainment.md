Section 6.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) is short but demanding. The syllabus lists three strands: the difficulties in defining intelligence; IQ tests and the extent to which they are influenced by social factors; and intelligence as an influence on educational attainment. It sits in topic 6, Education and inequality, linked to the key concept of Inequality and opportunity, and is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

The [intelligence, social class and educational attainment overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-intelligence-social-class-and-educational-attainment/) covers the core names: Spearman, Gardner, Sternberg, Gould, Binet and Simon, the eleven-plus, the Flynn effect, Kamin and Burt, Jensen and *The Bell Curve*. This page builds the sociological argument around them: what "heritability" does and does not show, how schools themselves construct "ability", and how to turn the debate into high-scoring answers.

## Why this is a sociology topic

Psychologists ask how intelligence works. Sociologists ask different questions: who defines intelligence, how the definition is used to sort people, and whether "intelligence" explains inequality or disguises it. Keep this sociological angle in every answer. A paragraph on brain function earns little; a paragraph on how a definition of intelligence becomes a gatekeeper to opportunity earns much more.

## The difficulties in defining intelligence: four problems

1. **One ability or many?** Theories range from a single general factor to multiple separate intelligences. Each theory implies a different test.
2. **Circularity.** If no definition is agreed, intelligence risks becoming "whatever intelligence tests measure". The test then defines the concept it claims to measure.
3. **Cultural variation.** What counts as intelligent behaviour differs between societies. Some emphasise speed and abstract reasoning; others value practical wisdom, social responsibility or memory for oral tradition. A definition built in one culture may misjudge people from another.
4. **Expanding definitions.** Ideas such as emotional intelligence, popularised by Daniel Goleman's *Emotional Intelligence* (1995), widen the concept further. The wider it becomes, the harder it is to measure and the less a single score can capture.

**Why the difficulty matters sociologically.** Whoever controls the definition controls who is labelled able. If schools adopt a narrow, verbal, abstract definition, pupils whose strengths lie elsewhere are judged less intelligent. The definition is therefore a source of unequal opportunity in itself.

## IQ tests and social factors

The overview page explains cultural bias in items, language, test-wiseness and the Flynn effect. Two further ideas strengthen this part of an answer.

**Stereotype threat.** Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995) found that Black American students performed worse on a difficult verbal test when it was presented as a measure of intellectual ability than when it was presented as a non-diagnostic task. The awareness of a negative stereotype about one's group can lower performance. A test score can therefore reflect the social situation of testing, not only the person tested.

**Heritability depends on environment.** Heritability is a statistic describing how much of the *variation within a particular population* is linked to genetic differences. It is not a fixed property of intelligence. Eric Turkheimer and colleagues (2003) found that in poorer American families most of the variation in children's IQ was linked to environment, while in more affluent families genes accounted for more. Where environments are unequal, environment matters more.

**Lewontin's seed analogy (1970).** Plant genetically varied seeds in two fields, one rich in nutrients and one poor. Within each field, height differences may be largely genetic; but the difference *between* the fields is entirely environmental. High heritability within groups tells us nothing about the cause of differences between groups, such as social classes.

**Evaluation.** Supporters of testing reply that modern tests are standardised carefully, that scores are fairly stable over time and that they predict school results. But prediction does not prove that the test measures innate ability: tests and school exams reward overlapping skills, many of them learned.

## How schools construct "ability"

Even where schools no longer use formal IQ tests, the idea of fixed intelligence shapes practice.

- **"New IQism".** Gillborn and Youdell (2000) used this term for teachers' and managers' belief that pupils have a fixed, measurable potential, often judged from baseline tests. Pupils labelled low-potential were placed in lower sets and lower exam tiers, which then limited what they could achieve.
- **The ideology of giftedness.** Bourdieu and Passeron, in *Les Héritiers* (1964, translated as *The Inheritors*), argued that schools treat the cultural advantages of privileged students as a natural "gift". Success that comes from upbringing is credited to talent, which makes class reproduction look like nature.
- **Learning without Limits.** Susan Hart and colleagues (2004) studied teachers who rejected fixed-ability thinking and organised teaching on the assumption that every pupil's capacity to learn can change. Their work suggests that "ability" labels are a choice schools make, not a discovery about pupils.

These arguments let you connect 6.1 to labelling and ability grouping in [social class and educational attainment](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-class-educational-attainment/).

## Intelligence as an influence on attainment: weighing the case

**The case that intelligence matters.**

- Measured intelligence correlates with school results in many societies, and few sociologists deny that individual ability plays some part.
- Functionalists assume a meritocracy in which ability and effort sort pupils fairly.
- Hereditarian writers argue that differences in attainment largely reflect differences in inherited ability.

**The case that intelligence is a weak explanation of group differences.**

- **Correlation is not causation.** Home advantages may raise both IQ scores and exam results, so the correlation does not show that intelligence causes attainment.
- **Between-group differences.** Lewontin's analogy shows why individual heritability cannot explain class or ethnic gaps.
- **Change over time.** Gender gaps in attainment have reversed in many countries within a generation, far too fast for genetic change. Something social must be at work.
- **Selection effects.** Where tests are used to select, the label itself shapes later attainment through different teaching, expectations and peer groups.
- **Bowles and Gintis** argued that IQ explains little of the link between family background and later economic success.

**A balanced judgement.** Intelligence may help explain why individuals within the same group differ, but it is a weak explanation of why groups differ, and the way it is defined and measured is itself socially shaped.

## Worked example: Question 2 style

*Explain two reasons why IQ test scores may be influenced by social factors.*

> **Reason 1: the testing situation.** Steele and Aronson (1995) showed that when Black American students believed a test measured ability, awareness of a negative stereotype lowered their scores. The same person can score differently depending on how a test is framed, so a score reflects the social situation as well as ability.
>
> **Reason 2: unequal environments.** Turkheimer and colleagues (2003) found that environment explained most IQ variation among children from poorer families. Where some children lack books, stable housing or schooling that practises test-like tasks, their scores reflect those conditions. Scores therefore partly measure social advantage.

Notice that neither paragraph repeats the familiar "cultural bias in questions" point; using less common material shows wider knowledge.

## Worked essay plan

*"Intelligence is the most important influence on educational attainment." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define intelligence and show it is contested; explain IQ.
2. For: correlations between IQ and results; hereditarian arguments; functionalist meritocracy.
3. Against on definition: circularity, cultural variation, multiple intelligences.
4. Against on measurement: stereotype threat; heritability depends on environment; Flynn effect.
5. Against on explanation: Lewontin's analogy; rapid change in gender gaps; new IQism and the ideology of giftedness.
6. Alternative factors: class, ethnicity and gender (the rest of topic 6).
7. Judgement: intelligence may explain some individual differences but not the main patterns between groups.

## Common mistakes

- Writing as a psychologist: describing brain processes instead of the social uses of intelligence.
- Saying "IQ tests are biased, so intelligence does not exist". The stronger claim is that tests measure a socially shaped version of it.
- Treating a heritability estimate as fixed or as applying to differences between groups.
- Forgetting the third strand: the question of how far intelligence *influences attainment*.

## Exam technique

- **Give** and **Describe** questions on this subtopic often reward a precise definition: learn to define IQ, heritability and reification in one sentence each.
- **Explain** answers need a mechanism: show *how* a social factor raises or lowers scores.
- **Evaluate** essays should end by distinguishing individual differences from group differences.
