## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Competition policy | Government action to keep markets competitive and stop the abuse of market power |
| Cartel | Firms agreeing to fix prices, share markets or limit output |
| Abuse of dominance | A dominant firm using its power to harm rivals or consumers, such as predatory pricing |
| Public ownership | The state owns and runs a firm or industry |
| Privatisation | Transfer of a state-owned firm to private owners |
| Regulation | Rules set by government or a regulator |
| Deregulation | Removal or reduction of rules, often to ease entry |
| Regulatory capture | A regulator that ends up serving the regulated industry, not the public |
| Government failure | Intervention that leads to a misallocation of resources |
| X-inefficiency | Costs above the minimum possible because of weak cost pressure |

## Competition policy in one page

**UK principles.** The CMA is the UK body. Policy has four strands: ban anti-competitive agreements, stop abuse of a dominant position, review mergers and investigate markets. Detailed law is not required.

**EU awareness.** Similar rules on cartels and dominance, review of large mergers with an EU dimension and limits on state aid.

**Remedies.** Fines (capped as a share of turnover), breaking up or selling parts of a firm, behaviour orders, personal sanctions.

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Lower prices, wider choice, better quality | Cost of enforcement and compliance |
| Less collusion and deadweight loss | Wrong decisions in either direction |
| Contestable markets and innovation | Delays, uncertainty, deterred efficient mergers |

**Method for judging a merger**

1. Describe the market and the shares of the merging firms.
2. Say how competition changes: price, entry, rivals' response.
3. Weigh cost savings (economies of scale) against higher prices and less choice.
4. Decide whose welfare counts: consumers only, or total surplus.
5. Conclude and state the evidence that would change your view.

**Concentration check.** Market shares of 28, 22, 18, 12, 10, 10 give a three-firm concentration ratio of 28 + 22 + 18 = 68%. If the 18% and 12% firms merge, the combined firm has 30% and the ratio is 30 + 28 + 22 = 80%. A rise of 12 points does not prove harm. You still need entry barriers and efficiency evidence.

**Deterrence check.** Gain £30 million, fine £8 million, chance of detection 10%. Expected fine = 0.1 × 8 = £0.8 million. The fine is far too small to deter, so detection chance and fine size both matter.

## Public ownership and privatisation

| Idea | Chain of reasoning |
|---|---|
| Natural monopoly | High fixed costs, so one firm is cheapest. Competition duplicates cost. State ownership can price near marginal cost and avoid private monopoly profit. |
| Externalities and merit goods | Private owners ignore external benefits, so output is too low. The state can provide it. |
| X-inefficiency | No profit motive or takeover threat, so costs drift up. |
| Soft budget | Losses are covered by taxpayers, so managers face no discipline. |
| Efficiency from privatisation | Shareholders and takeover threat press managers to cut cost, which raises profit. |
| Privatised monopoly | Efficiency gains may go to owners as higher prices if no regulation or competition exists. |

**Sale price test.** A state firm earns £15 million profit a year. At a 5% discount rate its value as a permanent income is 15/0.05 = £300 million. A sale at £240 million loses the state £60 million of value. Remember that this assumes the same profit continues and that the state could not do better itself.

**UK application.** The UK privatised several utilities in the 1980s and 1990s and created independent regulators for the ones with natural monopoly networks. The specification asks you to assess application in the UK and the effects on economic performance, so use context if the question supplies it.

## Regulation and deregulation

**Tools.** Price caps, quality and service standards, limits on profit, rules on entry and ownership.

**Price cap.** Permitted price change = inflation − X. Check: inflation 3%, X = 1.5, so permitted change is +1.5%. A price of £50 becomes 50 × 1.015 = £50.75.

| Regulation: strengths | Regulation: weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Limits monopoly pricing | Regulator lacks cost information |
| Sets safety and quality | Compliance and administrative costs |
| Mimics competition | Tight caps reduce investment |

| Deregulation: strengths | Deregulation: weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Easier entry, more competition | Safety and quality risks |
| Lower business costs | Market power returns |
| Fewer regulator costs | Informed buyers needed |

**Regulatory capture checklist.** Causes: information dependence, revolving door, lobbying, concentrated benefits for firms against scattered consumers. Effects: weak caps, entry barriers, higher prices. Safeguards: independence, transparency, cooling-off periods, appeals, several information sources.

## Government failure

| Source | Short chain |
|---|---|
| Inadequate information | Wrong tax or subsidy rate, so output moves past the optimum |
| Conflicting objectives | A government wanting revenue and low prices sells a monopoly at a high price |
| Administrative costs | Setting up, monitoring and enforcing use resources, which may exceed benefits |
| Distortions | Subsidies or protection keep inefficient firms alive or move prices away from marginal cost |
| Unintended consequences | Black markets, cross-border buying, supplier capture of subsidy |

**Net benefit check.** A grant scheme gives 50,000 grants of £400, which costs £20 million, and administration costs £3 million. Of the households, 40% would have acted anyway (deadweight), so 30,000 act because of the grant. Each such action gives a social benefit of £600, so the benefit is 30,000 × 600 = £18 million. Net = 18 − 23 = −£5 million. The scheme misallocates resources even though the aim was sound.

**Evaluating.** Government failure does not prove that markets should be left alone. Compare realistic magnitudes of market and government failure, flexibility and reversibility of the policy, time lags and the quality of information.

## Quick self-test

1. Define regulatory capture.
2. State two strands of UK competition policy.
3. Inflation is 3% and X is 1.5. By what percentage may price rise, and what is the new price if it was £50?
4. A state firm earns £15 million a year. It is sold for £240 million. At a 5% discount rate, how much value is lost?
5. Shares are 28, 22, 18, 12, 10, 10. Find the three-firm concentration ratio before and after the 18% and 12% firms merge.
6. Give one argument for keeping a natural monopoly in public ownership.
7. A cartel gains £30 million. The fine is £8 million and detection chance is 10%. Find the expected fine and comment.
8. Name two sources of government failure and link each to misallocation.
9. A scheme costs £20 million plus £3 million administration. It leads to 30,000 extra actions worth £600 each. Find the net benefit.
10. Why might privatising a monopoly raise prices?

### Answers

1. Capture: a regulator that, over time, favours the firms it oversees rather than consumers, typically through reliance on their data or staff moving between the two.
2. Any two of: banning anti-competitive agreements, preventing abuse of dominance, reviewing mergers, investigating markets.
3. 3 − 1.5 = 1.5%. New price = 50 × 1.015 = £50.75.
4. 15/0.05 = £300 million, so 300 − 240 = £60 million lost.
5. Before 68%, after 80%.
6. Competition would duplicate fixed costs, and public ownership can price near marginal cost while keeping profit out of private hands.
7. 0.1 × 8 = £0.8 million, much less than £30 million, so it will not deter. Raise the detection chance and the penalty.
8. For example, inadequate information gives a wrong tax rate and output moves away from the optimum. Administrative costs use resources that exceed the benefit.
9. 30,000 × 600 = £18 million benefit. Cost £23 million. Net = −£5 million.
10. The private owner has market power and a profit motive. Without competition or a cap it raises price and cuts output.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining competition policy without naming the tools (cartels, dominance, mergers, market investigations).
- Giving only benefits or only costs of a policy. A balanced judgement needs both.
- Writing "privatisation increases efficiency" with no chain and no mention of competition or regulation.
- Confusing regulation with deregulation, or treating a price cap as a price control that sets an exact price.
- Saying regulatory capture is bribery rather than influence and information dependence.
- Listing sources of government failure with no link to misallocation of resources.
- Concluding that government failure means no intervention. Compare it with market failure.
- Using real figures from memory. Use only the data provided in the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four strands of UK competition policy?**
The CMA bans anti-competitive agreements, stops abuse of a dominant position, reviews mergers and investigates markets. AQA does not require detailed competition law.

**How does an inflation minus X price cap work?**
The permitted price change equals inflation minus X, an efficiency target. With inflation of 3% and X of 1.5, prices may rise by 1.5%.

**What is X-inefficiency?**
X-inefficiency is when costs are above the minimum possible because of weak cost pressure, such as having no profit motive or takeover threat.

**Why might a privatised monopoly not benefit consumers?**
Without regulation or competition, the efficiency gains from privatisation may go to the owners as higher prices rather than to consumers.
