## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Distinguish between an irrecoverable debt and a provision for irrecoverable debts. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two reasons why a business makes a provision for irrecoverable debts. **[4]**

**3.** On 18 May, J. Okoro, who owes 460, is declared bankrupt and will pay nothing. Prepare the journal entry, with a narrative. **[2]**

**4.** Trade receivables are 41,800, which includes a debt of 1,800 that is to be written off. The provision is 3% of trade receivables, and the opening provision is 1,050. Calculate the required provision and the change in the provision. **[3]**

**5.** Using your answer to question 4, prepare the trade receivables extract for the statement of financial position. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** Fairway Sports wrote off these debts during 2027: 14 April L. Hart 520; 9 September G. Ames 275; 30 November N. Cole 185.

Prepare the irrecoverable debts account for 2027, showing the transfer at the year end. **[4]**

**7.** Brookside Pharmacy keeps a provision of 4% of trade receivables. It made its first provision in 2025. Trade receivables after write-offs were:

| Year end | Trade receivables |
|---|---:|
| 31 December 2025 | 25,000 |
| 31 December 2026 | 31,500 |
| 31 December 2027 | 27,000 |

**(a)** Prepare the provision for irrecoverable debts account for the three years. **[6]**
**(b)** State the entry in the income statement for each year. **[3]**

**8.** Calder Cycles has a draft profit of 48,600. It has not yet dealt with:

- a debt of 1,100 owed by M. Ross, which is to be written off
- a provision of 5% on trade receivables, which stand at 23,100 before the write-off; the opening provision is 950.

Calculate the adjusted profit. **[4]**

**9.** Kittering Electrical estimates its provision by age of debt, after write-offs.

| Age of debt | Amount | Provision rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0 to 30 days | 18,000 | 1% |
| 31 to 60 days | 6,500 | 4% |
| Over 60 days | 2,400 | 15% |

The opening provision is 950.

**(a)** Calculate the required provision. **[3]**
**(b)** State the double entry for the change in the provision. **[2]**

**10.** A junior bookkeeper at Lyle Stores made two errors at the year end. The opening provision was 1,350 and the required provision was 1,600.

- The whole 1,600 was charged as an expense in the income statement.
- The provision was deducted from trade payables in the statement of financial position.

**(a)** Calculate the effect of the first error on profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the second error misstates the statement of financial position and how it should be corrected. **[2]**

**11.** Explain:

**(a)** why a decrease in the provision is credited to the income statement **[2]**
**(b)** why making a provision does not change any customer's account. **[2]**

**12.** The owner of Holbeck Fabrics refuses to make a provision for irrecoverable debts: "Our customers have always paid in the end, and a provision just makes profit look worse." Trade receivables are 36,000 and the business's usual rate would be 2.5%.

Evaluate the owner's view. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An irrecoverable debt is a known loss on a named customer, written off by closing the customer's account [1]. A provision is an estimate of possible losses on the remaining trade receivables, with no customer named and no customer account changed [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two, each explained. Prudence [1]: trade receivables and profit should not be overstated when some customers are likely not to pay [1]. Accruals [1]: the expected losses arise from this year's credit sales, so they are matched to this year [1]. Also acceptable: realistic receivables for users judging liquidity; avoids withdrawal of profit that will not be received. **[4]**

**3.** Dr Irrecoverable debts 460, Cr J. Okoro 460 [1]. Narrative: debt written off, customer bankrupt [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Receivables after write-off = 41,800 − 1,800 = 40,000 [1]. Required provision = 40,000 × 3% = **1,200** [1]. Change = 1,200 − 1,050 = **increase of 150** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Trade receivables 40,000, less provision for irrecoverable debts 1,200 [1], giving **38,800** under current assets [1]. **[2]**

**6.**

```
Irrecoverable debts account (2027)
Debit                                Credit
14 Apr L. Hart           520         31 Dec Income statement   980
9 Sep  G. Ames           275
30 Nov N. Cole           185
                         980                                   980
```

Three write-offs on the debit side, dated [2]. Transfer of 980 to the income statement [1]. Totals balanced [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Required provisions: 2025 25,000 × 4% = 1,000; 2026 31,500 × 4% = 1,260; 2027 27,000 × 4% = 1,080.

```
Provision for irrecoverable debts account
Debit                                Credit
2025 31 Dec Balance c/d   1,000      2025 31 Dec Income statement   1,000
2026 31 Dec Balance c/d   1,260      2026 1 Jan  Balance b/d        1,000
                                     2026 31 Dec Income statement     260
                          1,260                                     1,260
2027 31 Dec Income statement 180     2027 1 Jan  Balance b/d        1,260
2027 31 Dec Balance c/d   1,080
                          1,260                                     1,260
                                     2028 1 Jan  Balance b/d        1,080
```

2025 provision 1,000 [1]. 2026 opening balance and increase of 260 [1]. 2026 balance c/d 1,260 [1]. 2027 decrease of 180 on the debit side [1]. 2027 balance c/d 1,080 [1]. Balances brought down correctly [1].
**(b)** 2025: expense, provision for irrecoverable debts 1,000 [1]. 2026: expense, increase in provision 260 [1]. 2027: income, decrease in provision 180 [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Receivables after write-off = 23,100 − 1,100 = 22,000 [1]. Required provision = 22,000 × 5% = 1,100; increase = 1,100 − 950 = 150 [1]. Adjusted profit = 48,600 − 1,100 − 150 [1] = **47,350** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Deduct the write-off before taking 5%; using 23,100 gives a provision of 1,155 and loses the second mark.

**9. (a)** 18,000 × 1% = 180; 6,500 × 4% = 260 [1]; 2,400 × 15% = 360 [1]. Total **800** [1].
**(b)** Decrease of 950 − 800 = 150 [1]: Dr Provision for irrecoverable debts 150, Cr Income statement 150 [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** Only the increase of 1,600 − 1,350 = 250 should be charged [1]. Profit is understated by 1,600 − 250 = **1,350** [1].
**(b)** Trade payables are understated by 1,600 and trade receivables are overstated by 1,600, so both current assets and current liabilities are wrong [1]. The provision should be deducted from trade receivables in current assets, and payables shown at their full amount [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** The earlier, larger provision was charged as an expense in previous years [1]; less is now needed, so the excess is reversed, increasing profit [1].
**(b)** The provision is an estimate and no particular customer is known to be unable to pay [1]; every customer still owes the full amount and may pay it, so their accounts stay unchanged [1]. **[4]**

**12.**
- In the owner's favour, if past experience really shows no bad debts, a large provision would understate profit [1].
- But a provision is an estimate of likely losses and prudence requires that receivables are not overstated [1].
- Customers can fail unexpectedly, and past payment does not guarantee future payment [1].
- The provision at the usual rate is 36,000 × 2.5% = 900, so profit and current assets are overstated by 900 without it [1].
- Accruals: any loss from this year's credit sales belongs in this year's income statement [1].
- Conclusion: the owner should make the provision, perhaps reviewing the rate against actual experience, because the purpose is a realistic statement, not to make profit "look worse" [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the percentage before deducting write-offs.
- Charging the whole provision instead of only the change.
- Treating a decrease as an expense.
- Showing the provision as a liability.
- Not bringing down the new balance on the provision account.
- Evaluations that never reach a conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the irrecoverable debts expense the same as the provision?**
No. The expense is the total of debts actually written off; the provision is a separate estimate, and only its change goes to the income statement.

**What if there is no opening provision?**
Then the whole required provision is the increase and is charged as an expense in that year.
