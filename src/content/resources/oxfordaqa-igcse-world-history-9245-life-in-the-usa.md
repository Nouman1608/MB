**This guide covers the whole of the Paper 2 Section A option Life in the USA, 1920-1968 in OxfordAQA International GCSE History (9245).** The specification organises it in three parts: "American people and the 'Boom' of the 1920s"; "Bust - Americans' experiences of the Depression and New Deal, 1929-1945"; and "Post-war America, 1945-68". Section A is assessed through a 3-mark knowledge question, a 6-mark question explaining one cause or change, a 9-mark question explaining two, and a 12-mark question that ends in a judgement. The [Paper 2 Section A study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-world-history-societies-in-change-study-guide/) explains these question types for all three options. Follow up with the [Life in the USA revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-life-in-the-usa-revision-notes/) and the [Life in the USA practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-life-in-the-usa-practice/).

## What the specification says

The study "focuses on life for the people of the United States during a turbulent period. It was a period of opportunity and inequality - when some Americans lived the 'American Dream' whilst others struggled with poverty, discrimination and prejudice." You study how lives were affected by the political, social, economic and cultural aspects of the period, the role of ideas in influencing change, and the role of key individuals and groups.

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| The Boom of the 1920s | Benefits and inequalities of the boom; social and cultural developments; a divided society |
| Bust, 1929-45 | The Depression; the New Deal and its limits; the effect of the Second World War |
| Post-war America, 1945-68 | Consumerism and McCarthyism; the New Frontier and Great Society; civil rights campaigns |

## American people and the Boom of the 1920s

### Why did the economy boom?

After the First World War the USA was the richest country in the world. Republican presidents (Harding, Coolidge) followed a policy of **laissez-faire**, keeping government out of business, with low taxes and high **tariffs** on imports that protected American industry. **Mass production** transformed manufacturing. Henry Ford's assembly line cut the price of the Model T to under $300 by the mid-1920s, so many families could afford a car. The motor industry created jobs in steel, rubber, glass and road building. New electrical goods such as radios, vacuum cleaners and refrigerators were also mass-produced.

**Advertising** and the **consumer society** encouraged people to buy. Many goods were bought on **hire purchase**, paying in instalments. The **stock market** boomed as share prices rose and many people bought shares "on the margin", borrowing money to buy.

### Inequalities of wealth

The boom did not reach everyone. Farmers suffered from overproduction and falling prices. Older industries such as coal and textiles had low wages and unemployment. Many African Americans in the South and many recent immigrants remained poor. A small group of very rich families held a large share of the nation's wealth, while many ordinary workers' wages rose only slowly. The boom depended on people buying on credit, which was a risk.

### Social and cultural developments

**Entertainment.** Cinema became a mass entertainment; talking films began in 1927 and Hollywood created stars. **Jazz** spread from African American communities in New Orleans, Chicago and Harlem, and radio spread popular music. Sport also drew crowds, and dances such as the Charleston were popular.

**Women.** Women gained the vote in 1920. Some young women, called **flappers**, wore short skirts, cut their hair short, smoked and went out without chaperones. Many took jobs as typists, telephone operators and teachers. But most women's lives did not change: traditional values remained strong, especially in small towns and the South, and women were paid less than men. The women's rights movement campaigned for equal rights, and the **Sheppard-Towner Act** (1921) provided federal money for maternal and child health clinics, although it lapsed in 1929.

### A divided society

- **Prohibition and organised crime.** The 18th Amendment banned the sale of alcohol from 1920. It proved hard to enforce. Illegal bars called speakeasies flourished, and gangsters such as Al Capone in Chicago made fortunes from bootlegging, bribed police, and fought violent turf wars. Crime and corruption increased and respect for the law fell.
- **Racial tensions and the Jim Crow laws.** In the South the Jim Crow laws segregated schools, transport and public places, and African Americans were often prevented from voting. The **Ku Klux Klan** was revived, with millions of members at its peak in the early 1920s, using violence and lynching against African Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.
- **Immigration controls.** Many Americans feared the effect of new immigrants on jobs and on American culture. The Quota Act of 1921 and the **National Origins Act of 1924** restricted immigration to a small percentage of the numbers from each country already in the USA, favouring northern Europeans and excluding Asians.
- **The Red Scare.** After the Russian Revolution, many Americans feared communists and anarchists. In 1919-20 the **Palmer Raids** arrested thousands of suspected radicals, and hundreds were deported. In 1921 the Italian immigrants **Sacco and Vanzetti**, who were anarchists, were convicted of murder on weak evidence; many believed that prejudice caused the verdict, and they were executed in 1927.
- **Fighting prejudice.** The **NAACP**, founded in 1909, used the courts and campaigned against lynching. The **UNIA**, led by Marcus Garvey, promoted black pride and economic independence, and it attracted a large following before Garvey was convicted of fraud in 1923.

## Bust: the Depression and the New Deal, 1929-45

### The Depression

In October 1929 share prices on Wall Street collapsed. Banks failed and thousands of businesses closed, and savings were lost. By 1933 about a quarter of workers (roughly 13 million) had no job. Farmers lost their land through foreclosure, and drought turned the Plains into a dust bowl. Homeless families lived in shanty towns called **Hoovervilles**.

President **Hoover** believed in "rugged individualism" and that government should not interfere. He encouraged voluntary action and set up the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to lend to banks and businesses, but the help was too little. In 1932 the army cleared the veterans of the **Bonus Army** from Washington, which made him very unpopular. He lost the 1932 election.

### Roosevelt and the New Deal

**Franklin D. Roosevelt** won the 1932 election by a landslide. In his first days he declared a bank holiday and passed the Emergency Banking Act (March 1933), which restored confidence in the banks. He spoke directly to the people on the radio in **fireside chats**, which created trust.

The **New Deal** brought many "alphabet agencies".

| Agency or law | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) | Work camps for unemployed young men |
| Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA) | Paid farmers to reduce output and raise prices |
| Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) | Dams, electricity and jobs in a poor region |
| Works Progress Administration (WPA) | Government jobs building roads, schools and public works |
| Social Security Act (1935) | Pensions for the elderly, unemployment insurance and help for disabled people and children |
| Wagner Act (1935) | Protected the right of workers to join unions |

**Successes and limits.** Unemployment fell and banks and farm prices recovered, and the Social Security Act created a lasting safety net. But unemployment was still high in 1938, after a recession in 1937. Different groups benefited differently. **African Americans** were often last to be hired and were excluded from some programmes. **Women** received lower pay and fewer jobs in public works, though Frances Perkins served as Secretary of Labor. **Indigenous peoples** gained from the Indian Reorganization Act (1934), which ended the sale of tribal land, but remained among the poorest Americans. Farmers, homeowners and the elderly received help, while tenant farmers and sharecroppers gained less.

**Opposition.** The **Supreme Court** ruled that the National Recovery Administration (1935) and the AAA (1936) were unconstitutional, and Roosevelt's plan to add judges failed. **Republicans** said that the New Deal gave government too much power and was too costly. **Radical politicians** such as Huey Long, who proposed to share the wealth, said it did not go far enough.

### The Second World War

War production ended the Depression. Unemployment almost disappeared by 1943. The **Lend-Lease** programme supplied Britain and its allies from 1941 and boosted exports. Millions of women took jobs in factories. African Americans moved from the South to northern and western cities to find defence work, and pressure from civil rights leaders led Roosevelt in 1941 to ban discrimination in defence industries, though segregation continued in the armed forces and racial tension led to riots, such as Detroit in 1943. After Pearl Harbor around 120,000 people of Japanese descent, most of them American citizens, were forced into internment camps on the west coast.

## Post-war America, 1945-68

### Consumerism and the American Dream

The economy boomed after the war. Reasons include the savings and demand built up during the war, the GI Bill, which helped veterans to study and buy homes, the baby boom, and heavy defence spending in the Cold War. Families moved to the **suburbs**, bought cars and television sets, and enjoyed the **American Dream** of a home and a good job. **Popular culture** included television and **rock and roll**, which gave young people a new identity. But poverty remained among African Americans, in rural areas and in inner cities.

### McCarthyism

Fear of communism grew with the Cold War. Senator **Joseph McCarthy** claimed in 1950 that communists had infiltrated the government. The House Un-American Activities Committee investigated writers and film makers, and many people lost their jobs or were blacklisted. The Rosenbergs were executed in 1953 for passing atomic secrets to the USSR. McCarthy lost support in 1954 after televised hearings, and the Senate condemned him.

### The New Frontier and the Great Society

**Kennedy's New Frontier** promised tax cuts, the Peace Corps and a space programme, and he sought civil rights laws, although he was assassinated in November 1963 before passing them. **Johnson's Great Society** was a "war on poverty" that produced Medicare and Medicaid, funding for schools, and the civil rights laws. The **Equal Pay Act** (1963) outlawed unequal pay for women doing the same work, and writers such as Betty Friedan helped to start the feminist movement of the 1960s.

### Civil rights campaigns in the 1950s and 1960s

- **Legal challenges.** In **Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka** (1954) the Supreme Court ruled that segregated schools were unconstitutional.
- **Rosa Parks and the Montgomery Bus Boycott.** In December 1955 Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a bus. African Americans boycotted the buses for over a year, and the Supreme Court ruled that segregated buses were illegal. The boycott made **Martin Luther King** a national leader.
- **The Little Rock Nine (1957).** Nine black students tried to attend a white school in Arkansas. The governor tried to stop them, and President Eisenhower sent in federal troops.
- **Freedom Rides and marches.** In 1961 groups travelled on buses through the South to challenge segregation. In 1963, a march on Washington brought over 200,000 people together, where King made his "I have a dream" speech.
- **Organisations.** The NAACP used the courts, while King's SCLC organised peaceful protests inspired by non-violence.
- **Malcolm X and Black Power.** Malcolm X argued that African Americans should defend themselves and take pride in black identity. After his assassination in 1965, the Black Power movement grew, and some young activists rejected non-violence.
- **Race riots.** Riots broke out in Watts, Los Angeles (1965), and in Newark and Detroit (1967), showing frustration at poverty and discrimination in northern cities.
- **The media.** Television showed viewers the violence used against peaceful protesters, which won support for civil rights.
- **Presidents and the federal government.** Eisenhower used troops at Little Rock; Kennedy supported a civil rights bill in 1963; Johnson passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1964** banning discrimination in public places and jobs, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and the Civil Rights Act of 1968 on housing, passed after King was assassinated in April 1968.

## Writing the answers

**Worked example: a 6-mark answer.**

*Explain one way in which the Wall Street Crash affected the lives of American farmers.* **[6]**

> **It made an existing crisis worse and cost many farmers their land.** Farmers had struggled in the 1920s because of overproduction and low prices **[way]**. After the Crash in 1929 banks failed or called in loans, and prices fell further, so many farmers could not pay their mortgages **[knowledge]**. Thousands lost their farms through foreclosure, and in the Plains drought created the Dust Bowl, forcing families to leave **[knowledge]**. They had little income to buy goods, which deepened the Depression **[explanation]**.

**The 12-mark judgement.** For "The New Deal was the most important development in American life in the years 1929-45", cover the New Deal (agencies, Social Security, limits), then another development (the Depression, the Second World War, the changes for women and African Americans), compare, and judge.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that all Americans shared in the boom.
- Saying the Crash alone caused the Depression. It exposed weaknesses already present.
- Confusing Hoover's approach (limited help) with Roosevelt's (federal action).
- Saying the New Deal ended the Depression; the war did more.
- Confusing Brown (1954), Montgomery (1955-56) and Little Rock (1957).
- Treating civil rights as only the work of King. The NAACP, young activists, courts and presidents also mattered.
