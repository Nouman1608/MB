Section 3.2.4 of the AQA A-level Biology (7402) specification, *Cell recognition and the immune system*, explains how
the body recognises "non-self" molecules called antigens and responds with phagocytes, T lymphocytes (the cellular
response) and B lymphocytes (the humoral response). You also need vaccines and herd immunity, active and passive
immunity, HIV and AIDS, monoclonal antibodies, the ELISA test, and the ethics of vaccines and monoclonal antibodies.

## What is an antigen?

An **antigen** is a foreign molecule, usually a protein, that triggers an immune response by lymphocytes. Every type
of cell has specific molecules on its surface, so the immune system can identify **pathogens**, **cells from other
organisms of the same species** (for example transplanted organs), **abnormal body cells** (such as cancer cells)
and **toxins**.

**Antigen variability.** Some pathogens change their antigens frequently, for example as a result of mutation in
the influenza virus. Memory cells from an earlier infection or vaccine no longer have receptors complementary to the
new antigen, so a person can catch the disease again, and a new vaccine has to be developed regularly.

## How does phagocytosis destroy a pathogen?

1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen and recognises its antigens as non-self.
2. It engulfs the pathogen, enclosing it in a vesicle called a **phagosome**.
3. A **lysosome** fuses with the phagosome.
4. **Lysozymes** (hydrolytic enzymes) from the lysosome hydrolyse the pathogen.
5. The phagocyte displays the pathogen's antigens on its own surface, becoming an **antigen-presenting cell**.

## What is the cellular response?

The cellular response is the response of **T lymphocytes** to a foreign antigen.

1. An antigen-presenting cell (for example a phagocyte, or an infected body cell) displays the foreign antigen.
2. A **helper T cell (TH cell)** with a receptor complementary to that antigen binds to it and is activated.
3. The activated TH cell divides by mitosis to form a clone.
4. TH cells then stimulate:
   - **cytotoxic T cells (TC cells)**, which kill infected body cells displaying the antigen;
   - **B cells**, to begin the humoral response;
   - **phagocytes**, to engulf more pathogens.

The specification says the roles of other T cells are not required.

## What is the humoral response?

The humoral response is the response of **B lymphocytes**, and involves antibodies in body fluids.

1. A B cell with antibody receptors complementary to the antigen binds to it, takes it in and presents it.
2. A helper T cell binds to the B cell and activates it. This selection of the one B cell type with the
   complementary receptor is **clonal selection**.
3. The B cell divides by mitosis to make a clone of **plasma cells** and **memory cells**.
4. Plasma cells secrete large numbers of identical, **monoclonal** antibodies specific to the antigen.
5. Memory cells remain in the blood for a long time.

### What is an antibody and how is it built?

An **antibody** is a protein that binds specifically to an antigen. It is made of **four polypeptide chains**: two
long **heavy chains** and two short **light chains**, held together by **disulfide bridges**. Each antibody has:

- two **variable regions** at the tips, each with an **antigen-binding site** whose shape is complementary to one
  antigen (the variable region's amino acid sequence differs between antibodies);
- a **constant region**, the same in antibodies of the same class;
- a **hinge region**, which lets the two arms move to bind antigens a different distance apart.

Binding forms an **antigen-antibody complex**. The specification limits destruction of the antigen to
**agglutination** (each antibody has two binding sites, so it clumps bacteria together) and **phagocytosis** of the
clumped bacterial cells.

### Why is the secondary response faster?

| | Primary response | Secondary response |
|---|---|---|
| When | First exposure to the antigen | Later exposure to the same antigen |
| Cells involved | Few B and T cells with the right receptor; clonal selection takes time | Many memory cells already present |
| Speed and size | Slow; small amount of antibody; symptoms usually develop | Fast; far more antibody; pathogen usually destroyed before symptoms |

Memory cells divide rapidly into plasma cells on re-exposure, which is why a second infection is often symptomless.

## How do vaccines and herd immunity work?

A **vaccine** contains antigens (for example dead or attenuated pathogens, or isolated antigens) that stimulate a
primary response and the production of **memory cells** without causing the disease. If the real pathogen enters
later, a secondary response destroys it quickly.

**Herd immunity** arises when a large proportion of a population is vaccinated: the pathogen cannot spread easily
because most contacts are immune, so unvaccinated people (for example babies too young to be vaccinated, or people
whose immune systems are weakened) are also protected.

### What is the difference between active and passive immunity?

| | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| How it arises | The body makes its own antibodies after exposure to the antigen (natural infection, or a vaccine) | Antibodies are introduced from another source (across the placenta, in breast milk, or by injection) |
| Memory cells | Yes | No |
| Speed | Slow to develop | Immediate |
| Duration | Long term | Short term, as the antibodies are broken down |

## How does HIV cause AIDS?

**Structure of HIV.** HIV has a core of **RNA** and the enzyme **reverse transcriptase**, inside a protein
**capsid**, surrounded by a **lipid envelope** (from the host cell membrane) carrying **attachment proteins**.

**Replication in helper T cells.**

1. The attachment protein binds to a receptor protein on a helper T cell.
2. The capsid is released into the cell, and RNA and enzymes are released.
3. **Reverse transcriptase** makes a DNA copy of the viral RNA.
4. The DNA is inserted into the helper T cell's DNA.
5. The cell transcribes and translates the viral genes, making new HIV particles that bud from the cell.

**AIDS.** HIV kills or prevents the replication of helper T cells. As their number falls, the immune system cannot
stimulate B cells and cytotoxic T cells properly, so the person cannot fight **opportunistic infections**. The
symptoms of AIDS are the symptoms of these infections and cancers, not of HIV itself.

**Why antibiotics do not work on viruses.** Antibiotics work by damaging bacterial structures or processes, such
as the murein cell wall. Viruses have no murein cell wall, and no ribosomes or metabolic enzymes of their own: they
replicate using the host cell's enzymes and ribosomes, so there is nothing for the antibiotic to target.

## What are monoclonal antibodies used for?

Monoclonal antibodies are antibodies from a single clone of plasma cells, so all are identical and bind one
specific antigen.

- **Targeted medication.** A therapeutic drug is attached to an antibody that binds an antigen found only on one
  cell type (for example a cancer cell), so the drug acts on those cells and fewer healthy cells are harmed.
- **Medical diagnosis.** For example, pregnancy tests use monoclonal antibodies that bind a hormone in urine.

Details of producing monoclonal antibodies are **not required**.

### How does the ELISA test work?

ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) uses antibodies to detect an antigen or an antibody in a sample. In one
form, the antigen is fixed to a well; the patient's sample is added and any complementary antibodies bind. After
washing, a second antibody with an **enzyme** attached is added, which binds to the first antibody. The well is
washed again so unbound enzyme-linked antibody is removed; a substrate is then added, and the enzyme turns it a
colour. A colour change shows that the target antibody was present. **Washing** is essential to prevent a false
positive from unbound enzyme.

## What ethical issues are raised?

- **Vaccines:** testing on animals; trials on volunteers who may suffer side effects; whether vaccination should be
  compulsory; balancing small risks to individuals against the benefit of herd immunity; fair access between
  countries.
- **Monoclonal antibodies:** animals (mice) have been used in their production; drug trials carry risks for
  volunteers; treatments are expensive, raising questions about who receives them.

When you **evaluate** vaccine or monoclonal antibody data, comment on sample size, whether a placebo or control
group was used, whether the trial was blind, how long people were followed, and whether differences are large
enough to be meaningful.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a cellular and a humoral response?**
The cellular response is carried out by T lymphocytes acting on cells. The humoral response is carried out by
B lymphocytes and the antibodies they release into body fluids.

**Why can you catch flu more than once?**
The influenza virus changes its antigens. Memory cells from an earlier infection do not recognise the new antigen,
so there is no fast secondary response.

**Is a pregnancy test an example of passive immunity?**
No. It is a diagnostic use of monoclonal antibodies. Passive immunity means receiving antibodies made by another
organism.

**Why does HIV infection not immediately cause symptoms?**
The symptoms of AIDS appear when the number of helper T cells has fallen so far that the immune system can no longer
fight opportunistic infections.

## Common mistakes

- Saying antibodies "kill" bacteria; they bind to antigens, causing agglutination, and phagocytes destroy them.
- Confusing **lysozymes** (enzymes) with **lysosomes** (organelles).
- Describing antibodies and antigens as the "same shape" rather than **complementary**.
- Saying vaccines contain antibodies (that is passive immunity).
- Saying HIV is AIDS, or that antibiotics treat HIV.
- Forgetting the washing steps when explaining ELISA results.
