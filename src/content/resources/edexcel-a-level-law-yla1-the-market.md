This guide covers **section 2.1, The market**, the first of the three sections of Paper 2 (The Law in Action) in Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4. The [Law in Action guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-the-law-in-action/) sets out each rule briefly. This page adds the leading cases, shows how the rules are applied to a scenario, and explains how negligence fits alongside contract.

## What section 2.1 covers

- **2.1.1 Formation**: offer, acceptance, consideration, legal intention, capacity.
- **2.1.2 Terms**: express and implied terms, conditions and warranties, and exclusion clauses under the **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** (ss 2, 3, 11) and the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** (ss 31, 57, 62, 65).
- **2.1.3 Consumer Rights Act 2015**: implied terms and remedies for goods (ss 9, 10, 11; 20, 23, 24) and services (ss 49, 52; 55, 56).
- **2.1.4 Validity and discharge**: misrepresentation, mistake, and discharge by performance, agreement, breach and frustration.
- **2.1.5 Privity** and the **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999**.
- **2.1.6 to 2.1.8 Negligence "as an alternative to contract"**: duty, breach, damage, defences and remedies, including damages with mitigation of loss, and injunctions.

The specification explains that each heading is broad: "offer", for example, includes rejection, counter-offers, when an offer ends and invitations to treat.

## Formation: the cases behind the rules

**Offer or invitation to treat?** Goods on a shop shelf are an invitation to treat, so the customer makes the offer at the till (*Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists* [1953]); so are goods in a window (*Fisher v Bell* [1961]) and most advertisements (*Partridge v Crittenden* [1968]). An advertisement can be a unilateral offer to the world if it shows a clear intention to be bound, as in *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893].

**When an offer ends.** A counter-offer destroys the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench* [1840]), but a mere request for information does not (*Stevenson v McLean* [1880]). An offer can be revoked before acceptance (*Routledge v Grant* [1828]), but revocation must be communicated (*Byrne v Van Tienhoven* [1880]), which can happen through a reliable third party (*Dickinson v Dodds* [1876]).

**Acceptance** must be unconditional and communicated; silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse v Bindley* [1862]). Under the postal rule, a posted acceptance takes effect when posted (*Adams v Lindsell* [1818]); instantaneous communications take effect when received (*Entores v Miles Far East Corporation* [1955]).

**Consideration** must be sufficient but need not be adequate (*Chappell v Nestlé* [1960]); past consideration is not good consideration (*Re McArdle* [1951]); performing an existing contractual duty is not consideration (*Stilk v Myrick* [1809]) unless it gives the other party a practical benefit (*Williams v Roffey Bros* [1990]).

**Intention**: domestic agreements are presumed not binding (*Balfour v Balfour* [1919]), but the presumption is rebutted where a couple had separated (*Merritt v Merritt* [1970]). **Capacity**: a minor is bound to pay a reasonable price for necessaries, judged by their station in life (*Nash v Inman* [1908]).

## Terms and exclusion clauses

A **condition** goes to the root of the contract: a singer missing the opening nights broke a condition (*Poussard v Spiers* [1876]), while missing rehearsals broke only a warranty (*Bettini v Gye* [1876]).

An exclusion clause must first be **incorporated**: by signature (*L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934]), by reasonable notice given before or at the time of the contract (*Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel* [1949]; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* [1971]), or by a consistent course of dealing. It must then survive statute:

| Provision | Effect |
|---|---|
| UCTA 1977 s 2(1) | Cannot exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence |
| UCTA 1977 s 2(2), s 3 | Other negligence and standard-terms clauses must be reasonable |
| UCTA 1977 s 11 | The reasonableness test |
| CRA 2015 s 31 | Cannot exclude the implied terms in goods contracts |
| CRA 2015 s 57 | Cannot exclude the implied terms in services contracts |
| CRA 2015 s 62 | An unfair term is not binding on the consumer |
| CRA 2015 s 65 | Cannot exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence |

UCTA governs business-to-business contracts; the CRA governs contracts between a trader and a consumer.

## The Consumer Rights Act 2015 remedies in order

```
Goods breach s 9, 10 or 11
  -> s 20 short-term right to reject (generally within 30 days)
  -> s 23 repair or replacement
  -> s 24 price reduction or final right to reject
          (after one failed repair or replacement)

Services breach s 49 (reasonable care and skill) or s 52 (reasonable time)
  -> s 55 repeat performance
  -> s 56 price reduction
```

## Validity and discharge

**Misrepresentation** is a false statement of fact (not opinion: *Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927]) that induces the contract. It may be **fraudulent** (*Derry v Peek* [1889]), **negligent** (actionable under s 2(1) of the **Misrepresentation Act 1967**) or **innocent**. Rescission and damages are the remedies, and damages may be reduced where the claimant was partly at fault, which is why the specification also names the **Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945** here. **Mistake** rarely makes a contract void: examples are where the subject matter had ceased to exist (*Couturier v Hastie* [1856]) and some cases of mistaken identity.

**Discharge** happens by:

- **Performance**: traditionally exact (*Cutter v Powell* [1795]), softened by substantial performance (*Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952]).
- **Agreement**: both sides agree to end the contract.
- **Breach**: including anticipatory breach before performance is due (*Hochster v De La Tour* [1853]).
- **Frustration**: an event after formation makes performance impossible (*Taylor v Caldwell* [1863]) or defeats its purpose (*Krell v Henry* [1903]). It is not enough that performance becomes more expensive (*Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC* [1956]). The **Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943** allows money paid to be recovered, subject to the court allowing a party to keep expenses incurred.

## Privity, and why negligence is the alternative

At common law only a party can enforce a contract (*Tweddle v Atkinson* [1861]; *Dunlop v Selfridge* [1915]). The **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999** lets a third party enforce a term if the contract expressly allows it or the term purports to confer a benefit on them, provided they are identified by name, class or description.

Where there is no contract, or privity blocks a claim, the injured person may sue in **negligence** instead. That is the point of *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932]: the consumer of the ginger beer had no contract with the manufacturer, yet was owed a duty of care.

## Negligence in outline

- **Duty**: *Caparo v Dickman* [1990] (foreseeability, proximity, fair, just and reasonable); in novel cases the courts develop the law by analogy with existing cases (*Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018]).
- **Breach**: the reasonable person standard (*Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks* [1856]), weighing likelihood of harm (*Bolton v Stone* [1951]), seriousness (*Paris v Stepney BC* [1951]) and cost of precautions (*Latimer v AEC* [1953]).
- **Damage**: "but for" causation (*Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC* [1969]) and remoteness (*The Wagon Mound (No 1)* [1961]). **Res ipsa loquitur** applies where the thing was under the defendant's control and the accident would not normally happen without negligence (*Scott v London and St Katherine Docks* [1865]).
- **Defences**: *volenti non fit injuria* (complete; *ICI v Shatwell* [1965]); contributory negligence (reduces damages; *Froom v Butcher* [1976], failure to wear a seat belt).
- **Remedies**: damages, with the claimant expected to **mitigate** their loss; **injunctions** to stop continuing harm.

## Worked example: applying CRA remedies

*Leila buys a laptop online from a trader. Three weeks later the screen fails. Advise her.*

1. **Rule**: goods must be of satisfactory quality (s 9).
2. **Apply**: a screen failing after three weeks suggests it was not of satisfactory quality.
3. **Remedy**: she is within 30 days, so she can use the short-term right to reject (s 20) for a refund. If she prefers, she can ask for repair or replacement (s 23); if that fails, price reduction or final rejection (s 24).
4. **Exclusion**: any term in the trader's conditions excluding s 9 is not binding (s 31).

## Common mistakes

- Treating a shop display as an offer.
- Mixing up UCTA (business contracts) with the CRA (consumer contracts).
- Skipping straight to s 24 without the s 20 and s 23 stages.
- Saying frustration applies whenever a contract becomes harder to perform.
- Stating the "but for" test without remoteness.

## Exam technique

Paper 2 questions are built on scenarios. For **explain**, state the rule and its source, then link it to the facts. For **assess** or **evaluate**, weigh how well the law works and reach a conclusion. Use **IRAC**: issue, rule (with case or section), application, conclusion.
