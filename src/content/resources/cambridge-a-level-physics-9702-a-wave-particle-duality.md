The broader [Quantum Physics study guide](/resources/a-physics-quantum-physics/) introduces the whole of Topic 22 in a few paragraphs; this page goes further into the evidence for each model of light, the electron diffraction experiment and how to interpret it, and calculations with the de Broglie wavelength for accelerated particles. For quick recall use the [Wave-particle duality revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-wave-particle-duality-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Wave-particle duality practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-wave-particle-duality-practice/).

## What 22.3 asks you to do

The syllabus lists four outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand that the **photoelectric effect** provides evidence for a particulate nature of electromagnetic radiation, while phenomena such as **interference and diffraction** provide evidence for a wave nature
- describe and interpret **qualitatively** the evidence provided by **electron diffraction** for the wave nature of particles
- understand the **de Broglie wavelength** as the wavelength associated with a moving particle
- recall and use **λ = h/p**

The word "qualitatively" matters: you must explain what the electron diffraction pattern shows and how it changes, but you will not be asked to calculate ring radii from crystal geometry.

## Two models, two kinds of evidence

Physics uses two models for electromagnetic radiation. Neither is "the right one"; each explains a different set of observations, and an exam answer must match the evidence to the model.

| Observation | Model it supports | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Interference fringes from a double slit | Wave | Fringes need superposition of waves with a fixed phase difference |
| Diffraction through a narrow gap or by a grating | Wave | Spreading depends on the ratio of wavelength to gap width |
| Photoelectric emission only above a threshold frequency | Particle | One photon gives all its energy to one electron, so energy per photon (hf) must reach the work function |
| Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons independent of intensity | Particle | More intense light means more photons per second, not more energy per photon |
| Emission with no measurable delay at low intensity | Particle | A single photon arriving delivers its energy at once; a spread-out wave would need time to build up enough energy |

The photoelectric effect cannot be explained by a wave model, because a wave of any frequency would eventually deliver enough energy to free an electron if it were intense enough or left on long enough. Interference cannot be explained by a model of particles following straight paths, because dark fringes are places where waves cancel. So electromagnetic radiation shows **both** behaviours, and which one appears depends on the experiment. This is wave-particle duality.

A useful rule of thumb: radiation shows its **wave** nature when it **travels** and spreads out (interference, diffraction), and its **particle** nature when it **interacts** with matter one quantum at a time (photoelectric emission, absorption by an atom).

## The de Broglie idea

If waves can behave as particles, can particles behave as waves? de Broglie proposed that any moving particle has an associated wavelength:

```
λ = h / p
```

where h is the Planck constant (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and p is the momentum of the particle. For a particle of mass m moving at speed v (well below the speed of light), p = mv, so λ = h/(mv).

Two consequences follow straight from the equation:

- **Faster or heavier particles have shorter wavelengths.** Doubling the momentum halves the wavelength.
- **Everyday objects have absurdly small wavelengths.** A 0.16 kg ball moving at 30 m s⁻¹ has λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (0.16 × 30) ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻³⁴ m. Nothing has a gap that small, so its wave behaviour can never be observed.

The same relation holds for photons. A photon with momentum p has wavelength h/p, so λ = h/p links the two halves of the topic: light, known as a wave, has momentum like a particle; electrons, known as particles, have a wavelength like a wave.

## Electron diffraction: the evidence for matter waves

### The apparatus

An electron diffraction tube is an evacuated glass bulb containing:

- an **electron gun**: a heated filament releases electrons, which are accelerated through a potential difference V of a few kilovolts towards an anode with a hole in it
- a very thin layer of **polycrystalline graphite** on a mesh in the path of the beam
- a **fluorescent screen** at the far end, which glows where electrons strike it

### What is seen

The screen shows a bright central spot surrounded by **concentric rings**. Rings like this are a diffraction pattern: the same kind of pattern appears when X-rays (known to be waves) pass through a powdered crystal.

### Interpreting the pattern

Examiners expect each of the following steps:

1. **Diffraction is a wave property.** Particles travelling in straight lines would produce a single spot or a uniform glow, not rings of high and low intensity. The rings are regions where the electron waves scattered by layers of atoms interfere constructively.
2. **The wavelength is comparable with the spacing of the atoms.** Diffraction is only noticeable when the wavelength is similar to the gap or spacing. Atomic spacings in graphite are of order 10⁻¹⁰ m, and electrons accelerated through a few kilovolts have wavelengths of order 10⁻¹¹ m, close enough for clear diffraction.
3. **Rings, not spots,** appear because the graphite is made of a huge number of tiny crystals at random orientations. Each orientation diffracts the beam in a different direction around the axis, so the pattern is smeared into circles.
4. **Increasing the accelerating p.d. makes the rings smaller.** A larger p.d. gives the electrons more kinetic energy and more momentum, so λ = h/p is shorter. A shorter wavelength is diffracted through smaller angles, so the rings move closer to the centre.
5. **It is the electrons that diffract.** Bringing a magnet near the tube shifts the whole pattern. X-rays or light are not deflected by a magnetic field, so the pattern must be made by charged particles.

Point 4 is the one most often asked, because it links the observation to λ = h/p. Write it as a chain: p.d. up, kinetic energy up, momentum up, wavelength down, diffraction angle down, ring radius down.

## The wavelength of an accelerated electron

An electron (charge e, mass mₑ) accelerated from rest through a p.d. V gains kinetic energy eV. Since kinetic energy E_k = p²/(2m), the momentum is:

```
p = √(2mₑeV)        λ = h / √(2mₑeV)
```

You do not need to memorise the combined form. It is safer to work in steps, as below, so that each step can earn credit.

## Worked example 1: a diffraction tube

Electrons are accelerated from rest through 4.0 kV. Calculate their de Broglie wavelength.

```
E_k = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 4000 = 6.40 × 10⁻¹⁶ J
p   = √(2mₑE_k) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 6.40 × 10⁻¹⁶) = 3.41 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹
λ   = h / p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 3.41 × 10⁻²³ = 1.94 × 10⁻¹¹ m
```

*Annotation.* If the p.d. is raised to 8.0 kV, the kinetic energy doubles, so momentum increases by a factor of √2 and the wavelength falls to 1.94 × 10⁻¹¹ / √2 = 1.37 × 10⁻¹¹ m. The wavelength halves only when the p.d. is multiplied by four. Many candidates assume λ is inversely proportional to V; it is inversely proportional to √V.

## Worked example 2: matching a wavelength

Calculate the speed an electron needs for a de Broglie wavelength of 0.15 nm, similar to an atomic spacing, and the accelerating p.d. that would give it this speed.

```
p = h / λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 0.15 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.42 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹
v = p / mₑ = 4.42 × 10⁻²⁴ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 4.9 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
E_k = ½mₑv² = 1.07 × 10⁻¹⁷ J,  so V = E_k / e = 67 V
```

*Annotation.* The speed is well below 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, so p = mv is valid. A proton at the same speed would have a wavelength about 1800 times smaller, because its mass is about 1800 times greater: momentum, not speed alone, sets the wavelength.

## Common mistakes

- **Pairing the wrong evidence with the wrong model**, for example citing the photoelectric effect as evidence for waves. Interference and diffraction support waves; the photoelectric effect supports particles.
- **Saying electron diffraction proves electrons "are waves".** It shows that moving electrons have a wave nature; they still arrive at the screen as individual particles, one flash at a time.
- **Stating that the rings get larger** at higher p.d. Higher p.d. means shorter wavelength and smaller diffraction angles.
- **Using the p.d. as if it were the energy.** Multiply by e to get joules before finding the momentum.
- **Forgetting the square root** when finding momentum from kinetic energy, or using E_k = pc, which applies to photons, not to slow electrons.
- **Explaining why the graphite must be thin** as "so the electrons can see the atoms". The point is that the spacing of atoms must be comparable with the wavelength, and the layer must be thin enough for electrons to pass through.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (what is meant by the de Broglie wavelength): the wavelength associated with a moving particle, given by λ = h/p.
- **Describe** (the evidence from electron diffraction): name the apparatus, the pattern of concentric rings, and that diffraction is a wave property.
- **Explain** (why the rings shrink at higher p.d.): the full causal chain from p.d. to momentum to wavelength to angle.
- **Calculate**: kinetic energy in joules, then momentum, then wavelength, with units on each line.
- **Suggest** (why a heavy particle shows no diffraction): its momentum is so large that λ is far smaller than any available gap.
