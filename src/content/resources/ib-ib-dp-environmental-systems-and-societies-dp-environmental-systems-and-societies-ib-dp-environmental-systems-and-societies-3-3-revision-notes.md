## Condensed recall

### Values

- **Instrumental value**: direct use (food, timber, medicine, crop genes), indirect use (ecosystem services), economic (tourism).
- **Intrinsic value**: worth in itself, independent of human use.
- **Non-use value**: existence value (knowing it exists) and bequest value (keeping it for future generations).
- Technocentric arguments lean on instrumental value; ecocentric arguments lean on intrinsic value.

### Organisations and agreements

- **Governmental bodies** (national park agencies, UNEP): make and enforce law, large budgets; slow and politically constrained.
- **NGOs** (WWF, Greenpeace): fast, media-savvy, fund-raise from the public, work locally; no legal power.
- **IUCN**: members include states and NGOs; scientific authority; publishes the Red List; advises rather than enforces.
- **CITES (1973)**: regulates international trade in listed species by permit.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity (1992, Rio)**: conservation, sustainable use, fair sharing of benefits from genetic resources.
- **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022)**: "30 by 30", conserving 30% of land and 30% of sea areas by 2030.
- Weak point of all agreements: enforcement, funding and "paper parks".

### Island biogeography (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967)

- Species number = balance between immigration and extinction.
- Larger island → larger populations → lower extinction rate, plus more habitats.
- Nearer island → more colonists → higher immigration rate.
- Reserves act as habitat islands, so: large, close together, connected by corridors.

### Shape

- For equal area, a circle has the shortest boundary and the largest core.
- Core of a circular reserve = π × (radius − edge depth)².
- Reminder: radius 4 km, edge 1 km: total = π × 16 = 50.27 km²; core = π × 9 = 28.27 km²; share = 9 ÷ 16 = **56.25%**.

### Land sharing versus land sparing

| | Land sparing | Land sharing |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Farm less land intensively; protect the rest | Farm more land gently; wildlife lives within farms |
| Helps | Species needing large undisturbed areas | Species adapted to farmland; on-farm services |
| Risks | Pollution from intensive farms; profits may drive more clearance | More land needed for the same food |

### Setting priorities

- **Biodiversity hotspot**: many endemic species and much original habitat already lost.
- **EDGE species** (Zoological Society of London): Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered.
- **Community-based conservation**: local people share management and benefits.
- **Biosphere reserve** (UNESCO): core, buffer zone, transition zone.

### Regeneration and rewilding

- **Regeneration**: actively restore a degraded ecosystem (replanting, re-wetting peat, removing invasives).
- **Rewilding**: restore self-sustaining processes; reintroduce missing species; reduce management.
- Example: grey wolves reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995; links to elk behaviour and riverside vegetation recovery are still debated.
- Evaluate with: aim, evidence, time scale, costs and conflicts, choice of baseline, judgement.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic value | Value of a species or ecosystem in itself |
| Instrumental value | Value because of its use to humans |
| Existence value | Value from knowing something exists, without using it |
| NGO | Non-governmental organisation, independent of government |
| CITES | Convention regulating international trade in listed species |
| Island biogeography | Theory that species number reflects immigration and extinction rates |
| Habitat island | A patch of habitat surrounded by unsuitable land |
| Land sparing | Intensive farming on less land so more can be protected |
| Land sharing | Wildlife-friendly farming across a larger area |
| Biodiversity hotspot | Region of high endemism that has lost much original habitat |
| EDGE species | Evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered species |
| Trophic cascade | Effects of a predator passing down through lower trophic levels |
| Paper park | A protected area that exists in law but is not effectively managed |

## Exam traps

- CITES is a permit system, not a blanket ban on trade.
- Island biogeography: give the mechanism (immigration and extinction), not "bigger means more".
- Rewilding is not the same as planting trees; it centres on processes and reduced management.
- Land sharing or sparing: say which species or region you mean, then judge.
- "NGOs are better than governments": compare roles, do not rank them without evidence.
- In circle calculations, subtract the edge depth from the radius once, not twice.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between intrinsic and instrumental value.
2. Give one example each of existence value and bequest value.
3. State two strengths of NGOs in conservation.
4. What does CITES regulate, and how?
5. What is the "30 by 30" target, and in which framework does it appear?
6. According to island biogeography, why does a nearer island hold more species?
7. Calculate the core share of a circular reserve of radius 5 km with edge effects reaching 1 km.
8. State one advantage and one risk of land sparing.
9. What does EDGE stand for?
10. Name the three zones of a UNESCO biosphere reserve.
11. Give two difficulties in judging whether a rewilding project has succeeded.
12. What is a paper park?

### Answers

1. Intrinsic value is the worth of a species in itself; instrumental value is its worth because of what it provides for people.
2. Existence: valuing that blue whales exist though you will never see one. Bequest: wanting a forest kept for your grandchildren. (Any suitable examples.)
3. Any two: act quickly; use media to raise awareness; raise funds from the public; work directly with local communities; independent of election cycles.
4. International trade in listed species and their products, through a system of permits.
5. Conserving 30% of land and 30% of sea areas by 2030; the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022).
6. Colonising species arrive more often from the mainland, so the immigration rate is higher.
7. Core radius 4 km; share = 4² ÷ 5² = 16 ÷ 25 = **64%**.
8. Advantage: large undisturbed areas protected for sensitive species. Risk: pollution from intensive farms, or higher profits driving more clearance.
9. Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered.
10. Core, buffer zone, transition zone.
11. Any two: results take decades; the chosen baseline is debatable; changes may have other causes (such as climate); monitoring is costly.
12. A protected area that exists on paper but is not effectively managed or enforced.
