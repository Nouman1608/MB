These notes condense **Information asymmetries** for the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, for first assessment in 2016, Component 01, Microeconomics. The current specification names "information provision" as an AS intervention, while asymmetric information and moral hazard are named in the A Level (H460) content, so confirm the scope with your teacher. Full explanations, including the scooter and phone insurance examples, are in the [information asymmetries study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-information-asymmetries/). Practise with the [information asymmetries practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-information-asymmetries-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Perfect information | Everyone knows all prices, qualities and consequences: a model assumption |
| Information failure | People lack the information needed to make choices in their own interest, so demand or supply is wrong |
| Asymmetric information | One party to a transaction knows more than the other |
| Moral hazard | Behaviour changes after an agreement because one party is protected from the consequences and cannot be observed |
| Perceived benefit | What a buyer believes they will gain |
| True (informed) benefit | What the buyer would value if fully informed |
| Information provision | Government supplies or requires information to close the gap |
| Excess | Part of each insurance claim paid by the policyholder |

## Who knows more?

- **Seller knows more:** used cars, home repairs, financial products, health treatments.
- **Buyer knows more:** insurance (own risk), loans (own ability to repay).
- **Worker knows more:** own effort and ability.

## Two problems, two timings

| | Before the deal | After the deal |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Hidden quality or risk (asymmetric information) | Hidden behaviour (moral hazard) |
| Example | Faulty bike sold as good | Insured owner stops locking the bike |
| Effect | Buyers pay less or leave; good products withdrawn | Losses rise; premiums rise for everyone |
| Fix | Warranties, inspections, disclosure rules | Excess, no-claims discount, monitoring |

## Worked reminders

**Market shrinks.** Good laptops are worth £500 to buyers and faulty ones £200; buyers believe 40% are faulty. Expected value = 0.6 × 500 + 0.4 × 200 = **£380**. Sellers of good laptops who value them at £420 withdraw, so the share of faulty laptops on sale rises.

**Moral hazard.** A bike insurer expects a 4% chance of a £600 theft claim: £24. Insured owners take less care and the chance rises to 6%: £36, up **50%**.

**Diagram.** Perceived MPB = 40 − Q, true MPB = 34 − Q, MPC = 10 + Q. Market: Q = 15. Informed: Q = 12. At Q = 15 the true benefit is £19 and the cost £25. Welfare loss = ½ × 3 × 6 = **9**.

## Diagram checklist

- Perceived MPB above true MPB: overconsumption (benefits exaggerated).
- Perceived MPB below true MPB: underconsumption (benefits underestimated, such as health checks or pension saving).
- One cost curve if there are no externalities.
- Welfare loss between the cost curve and the **true** benefit curve, from the informed output to the market output.

## Remedies and their limits

| Remedy | Works best when | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Information provision (campaigns, labels, ratings) | Information is simple and people act on it | Ignored or misread; costly |
| Regulation (disclosure, standards, licensing) | Harm is serious and quality is hard to judge | Enforcement cost; may raise prices |
| Legislation (consumer rights) | Faults appear soon after purchase | Consumers may not claim |
| Warranties and reputation | Trade is repeated and firms value their name | Weak for one-off purchases |
| Excess and no-claims discounts | Behaviour can be linked to claims | Some careless behaviour remains |

## Exam traps

- Moral hazard is about behaviour after a contract, not hidden quality before it.
- Name who has the extra information and what it is.
- The welfare loss uses the true benefit curve, not the perceived one.
- Information provision is not automatically effective: weigh it.
- An information gap can hurt honest sellers as well as buyers.
- Not all poor choices are information failure; say what information is missing.

## Self-test

1. Define asymmetric information.
2. Give one market where the seller usually knows more than the buyer.
3. Give one market where the buyer usually knows more than the seller.
4. Define moral hazard.
5. Good phones are worth £300 and faulty ones £150; half are faulty. What will an uninformed buyer pay?
6. In question 5, why might owners of good phones stop selling?
7. An insurer expects a 3% chance of a £1,000 claim. Find the expected claim.
8. If the chance rises to 5% after cover is bought, by what percentage has the expected claim risen?
9. Perceived MPB = 50 − Q, true MPB = 44 − Q, MPC = 14 + Q. Find the market and informed outputs.
10. Calculate the welfare loss in question 9.
11. Explain how an excess reduces moral hazard.
12. Give one reason why information provision may not correct an information failure.

### Answers

1. One party to a transaction has more or better information than the other.
2. Any one of: used cars, home repairs, financial products, medical treatment.
3. Insurance or loans: the buyer knows their own risk.
4. A change in behaviour after an agreement, when a person is protected from the consequences and the other party cannot observe them.
5. 0.5 × 300 + 0.5 × 150 = **£225**.
6. If they value their phones above £225, they are better off keeping them, so they withdraw.
7. 0.03 × 1,000 = **£30**.
8. New expected claim £50, a rise of 20 ÷ 30 = **66.7%**.
9. Market: 50 − Q = 14 + Q, **Q = 18**. Informed: 44 − Q = 14 + Q, **Q = 15**.
10. At Q = 18, true benefit is £26 and cost is £32. Loss = ½ × 3 × 6 = **9**.
11. The policyholder pays part of each loss, so they still bear a cost from carelessness and take more care.
12. Any one of: people ignore or misunderstand it; it is costly to provide; habits or advertising outweigh it; it reaches the wrong people.
