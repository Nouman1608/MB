## Questions

**1.** Describe how cytokinesis differs in an animal cell and in a plant cell. **[2]**

**2.** Cytokinesis is not always equal.

**(a)** State two examples of unequal cytokinesis. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why each daughter cell must still receive at least one mitochondrion. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows the DNA content of one nucleus at four stages in an organism with 2n = 6 (data supplied).

| Stage | DNA per nucleus (au) |
|---|---|
| W: before replication | 6.0 |
| X: after replication | 12.0 |
| Y: after meiosis I | 6.0 |
| Z: after meiosis II | 3.0 |

**(a)** Explain the change in DNA content between W and X. **[2]**
**(b)** State the number of chromatids in a nucleus at stage X. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify which stage could be a gamete nucleus and give a reason. **[1]**

**4.** A student examined a root-tip squash and counted 300 cells: 262 in interphase, 21 in prophase, 6 in metaphase, 3 in anaphase and 8 in telophase.

**(a)** (HL only) Calculate the mitotic index as a percentage to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how you would recognise a cell in anaphase in a micrograph. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how histones and microtubule motors contribute to chromosome behaviour in mitosis and meiosis. **[3]**

**6.** A diploid cell has four chromosomes (2n = 4). Outline what happens to the chromosomes from replication until four nuclei have formed. **[5]**

**7.** A child has Down syndrome.

**(a)** State the number of chromosomes in a body cell of the child and the reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how non-disjunction in meiosis II could have produced the gamete involved. **[2]**

**8.** A plant has 2n = 10.

**(a)** Calculate the number of chromosome combinations possible from random orientation of bivalents, ignoring crossing over. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how crossing over adds to the variation in the gametes. **[3]**

**9.** (HL only) **(a)** Give one example of cell proliferation for growth in a plant and one in an animal embryo. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how cell proliferation helps heal a skin wound. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only) A cell cycle lasts 22 hours (data supplied): G1 9 h, S 8 h, G2 4 h, and mitosis plus cytokinesis 1 h.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the cycle spent in interphase, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why DNA replication in S phase must be completed before mitosis. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe two events in interphase that show it is not a resting stage. **[2]**

**11.** (HL only) The table shows the concentrations of two cyclins (invented data, in au) in a cell. Cyclin P must reach 40 au for the cell to pass the G1 checkpoint. Cyclin R must reach 50 au for the cell to pass the checkpoint before mitosis.

| Time (h) | Cyclin P | Cyclin R |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 8 | 5 |
| 4 | 25 | 5 |
| 8 | 42 | 12 |
| 12 | 30 | 38 |
| 16 | 12 | 55 |
| 20 | 6 | 20 |

**(a)** Identify the first time shown at which the cell could pass the G1 checkpoint. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the pattern shown by cyclin P. **[2]**
**(c)** Deduce, with a reason, the earliest time shown at which the cell could pass the checkpoint before mitosis. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why the fall in cyclin concentrations is important. **[1]**

**12.** (HL only) Tissue samples were examined, with 200 cells counted in each. Tumour P had 38 cells in mitosis, tumour Q had 9 and normal tissue had 6.

**(a)** Calculate the mitotic index of tumours P and Q, as percentages. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how mutation of a tumour suppressor gene can lead to a tumour. **[2]**
**(c)** Tumour P is malignant and tumour Q is benign. Evaluate how well the data and other features support this. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** In an animal cell, a ring of actin and myosin contracts and pinches the membrane inward [1]. In a plant cell, vesicles build new membrane and new wall in sections across the middle of the cell [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "describe" answer on this topic usually gains credit only when both cell types are covered and the structures are named; "the cell splits in two" repeats the question.

**2. (a)** Oogenesis in humans [1]; budding in yeast [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Mitochondria cannot be made from scratch and arise only by division of existing ones [1]. A daughter with none could not make ATP, or could not regenerate them [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers give the reason (mitochondria come from pre-existing mitochondria) and not just "they need energy".

**3. (a)** DNA doubled from 6.0 to 12.0 au [1] because DNA replication occurred in S phase [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 12 chromatids (6 chromosomes, 2 each) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Z, because it is haploid with half the original DNA (3.0 au) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the figures from the table when explaining a change, and do not call Y a gamete just because its DNA equals W.

**4. (a)** (HL only) Cells in mitosis: 21 + 6 + 3 + 8 = 38 [1]. 38 ÷ 300 × 100 = **12.7%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Chromatids (now chromosomes) are separated into two groups [1] moving towards opposite poles, with no nuclear envelope around them [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the final answer to the requested number of significant figures and show the sum of mitotic cells; including the interphase cells in the numerator is a common slip.

**5.** DNA is wound around histones [1] and supercoiled, so chromosomes become short and compact, which stops tangling when moved [1]. Microtubule motors move chromosomes along spindle microtubules [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each structure to its role: histones go with condensation, microtubules and motors go with movement.

**6.** DNA replicates, so each of the 4 chromosomes has two chromatids [1]. In meiosis I homologous chromosomes pair as bivalents [1] and then separate, giving two cells with 2 chromosomes each [1]. In meiosis II the chromatids separate [1]. Four haploid nuclei form, each with 2 chromosomes [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Say what separates in each division and give chromosome numbers; answers that only list division names, without the pairing and separation, tend to gain little.

**7. (a)** 47 [1], because there are three copies of chromosome 21 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The two chromatids of chromosome 21 failed to separate in meiosis II [1], so one gamete received both (24 chromosomes) and fused with a normal gamete (23) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the division and what failed to separate; "chromosomes did not separate" is not specific about meiosis II.

**8. (a)** n = 5 [1]; 2⁵ = **32** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** In prophase I, non-sister chromatids of homologues exchange sections [1] at chiasmata [1], giving new combinations of alleles on one chromosome, so gametes differ from one another and from the parent chromosomes [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Place crossing over in prophase I and say it is between homologues; random orientation in metaphase I is a different source of variation.

**9. (HL only) (a)** Plant meristem [1]; early animal embryo (cleavage divisions) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cells at the wound edge divide [1] to replace lost cells and close the gap [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the type of tissue (meristem, embryo, skin) and not simply "growth".

**10. (HL only) (a)** Interphase = 9 + 8 + 4 = 21 h [1]; 21 ÷ 22 × 100 = **95.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each chromosome needs two chromatids [1] so that each daughter cell gets a full copy of the DNA [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any two: synthesis of proteins; DNA replication; growth of the cell; increase in mitochondria or chloroplasts [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Interphase includes G1, S and G2, so check you added all three before dividing.

**11. (HL only) (a)** 8 h [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Cyclin P rises from 8 au to a peak of 42 au at 8 h [1] and then falls to 6 au by 20 h [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 16 h [1], because that is the first time cyclin R is above its 50 au threshold (55 au) [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** It lets the cell be controlled again in the next cycle, since each checkpoint needs a rise to the threshold [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use values and times from the table; mention both the rise and the fall when asked for a pattern.

**12. (HL only) (a)** P: 38 ÷ 200 × 100 = **19.0%** [1]. Q: 9 ÷ 200 × 100 = **4.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A tumour suppressor gene normally restrains cell division [1]. A mutation stops it working, so division is no longer restrained and cells divide in an uncontrolled way [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Support: P has a mitotic index (19.0%) much higher than normal tissue (3.0%), consistent with fast division in a malignant tumour [1]. Q is close to normal (4.5%), consistent with slow growth in a benign tumour [1]. Limitation: mitotic index measures division rate only [1], while malignancy also means invading neighbouring tissue and forming secondary tumours, so more evidence is needed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In an "evaluate" question, give both what the data support and what they cannot show; a verdict with no limitation tends to lose credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating "cells split" for cytokinesis without naming actin and myosin or vesicles and wall.
- Leaving out the reason a daughter cell needs a mitochondrion.
- Calling a chromatid a chromosome, or forgetting that replication gives two chromatids.
- Counting interphase cells as dividing cells in a mitotic index.
- Not giving a mitotic index to the number of significant figures requested.
- Saying DNA halves in meiosis I.
- Explaining non-disjunction without saying which division failed.
- Placing crossing over in the wrong phase or confusing it with random orientation.
- Treating interphase as a resting stage.
- Calling every tumour cancer.
