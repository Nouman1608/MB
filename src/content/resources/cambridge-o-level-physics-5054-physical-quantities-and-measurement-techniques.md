This guide covers all of subtopic **1.1 Physical quantities and measurement techniques**, the first
subtopic of Topic 1, Motion, forces and energy, in Cambridge O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028
series). The broader [measurement, mass, weight and density guide](/resources/measurement-mass-weight-and-density/)
introduces the instruments; this page goes further into reading them, improving precision, and
the vector half of 1.1, including calculating and drawing resultants.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL PHYSICS 5054, subtopic 1.1**

1. Describe how to measure a variety of lengths with appropriate precision using tapes, rulers and
   micrometers (including reading the scale on an analogue micrometer)
2. Describe how to use a measuring cylinder to measure the volume of a liquid and to determine the
   volume of a solid by displacement
3. Describe how to measure a variety of time intervals using clocks and digital timers
4. Determine an average value for a small distance and for a short interval of time by measuring
   multiples (including the period of oscillation of a pendulum)
5. Understand that a scalar quantity has magnitude (size) only and that a vector quantity has
   magnitude and direction
6. Know that distance, speed, time, mass, energy and temperature are scalars
7. Know that displacement, force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field strength
   and gravitational field strength are vectors
8. Determine, by calculation or graphically, the resultant of two vectors at right angles

5054 is **not tiered**: every candidate studies all of this.

## Choosing an instrument for length

"Appropriate precision" means matching the instrument's smallest division to the size of the
length and the detail you need.

| Length to measure | Instrument | Smallest division |
|---|---|---|
| Length of a corridor or running track | Tape measure | 1 cm or 1 mm |
| Length of a pendulum string, a bench or a spring | Metre rule or 30 cm ruler | 1 mm |
| Diameter of a wire, thickness of a coin or a sheet of glass | Micrometer | 0.01 mm |

**Good technique with a ruler**

- Read the scale with your eye directly above the mark, to avoid **parallax error**.
- Measure from the zero line, not the worn end of the ruler, or subtract the starting reading.
- For a spring or pendulum, hold the rule vertical and close to the object.

## Reading an analogue micrometer

A micrometer has two scales:

- The **sleeve** (main scale) shows whole millimetres above the datum line and half millimetres
  below it.
- The **thimble** has 50 divisions; one full turn moves the spindle 0.50 mm, so each thimble
  division is **0.01 mm**.

**Reading = sleeve reading + (thimble division on the datum line × 0.01 mm)**

**Worked example.** The last visible sleeve mark is 7.5 mm (the half-millimetre mark after 7 mm
is showing), and the thimble division level with the datum line is 18.

```
reading = 7.5 mm + 18 × 0.01 mm = 7.5 + 0.18 = 7.68 mm
```

Close the jaws using the ratchet, not the thimble, so the object is not squashed, and check the
reading is zero with the jaws closed before you start. Take readings at several places on a wire
and average them, since a wire may not be perfectly round.

## Volume with a measuring cylinder

- Stand the cylinder on a level bench and read at eye level.
- For water, read the **bottom of the meniscus**.
- Choose the smallest cylinder that holds the volume: a narrow cylinder has finer divisions.

**Volume of a solid by displacement:** part-fill the cylinder, record the reading, lower the solid
in gently on a thread so it is fully submerged and no water splashes out, and record the new
reading. The solid's volume is the **difference** between the readings. The solid must sink; if it
floats, displacement in this simple form does not work.

## Measuring time intervals

- **Clocks** suit long intervals, such as minutes for a cooling experiment.
- **Stopwatches** read to 0.01 s, but a human starting and stopping one has a reaction time of a
  few tenths of a second, so the last digits are not meaningful for a single short interval.
- **Digital timers** started and stopped electronically, for example by light gates, remove
  human reaction time and suit short intervals such as a trolley passing a gate.

## Measuring multiples

When a single quantity is too small to measure precisely, measure many together and divide. The
uncertainty in the total stays about the same, so dividing spreads it over many items.

**Small distances**

- **Thickness of paper:** a stack of 200 sheets measures 19.6 mm, so one sheet is
  19.6 ÷ 200 = **0.098 mm**.
- **Diameter of a thin wire:** wind 25 turns side by side around a pencil; the coil spans
  15.0 mm, so the diameter is 15.0 ÷ 25 = **0.60 mm**.

**Short time intervals: the pendulum**

One oscillation is a complete swing from one side, across, and back to the same side. To find the
period:

1. Displace the bob through a small angle and release it.
2. Start timing as the bob passes a fixed **marker** at the centre of the swing, where it moves
   fastest, so the moment of passing is easiest to judge.
3. Time 20 oscillations, counting "zero" at the start.
4. Repeat twice more and take the mean.

**Worked example.** Three readings for 20 oscillations are 31.6 s, 31.4 s and 31.8 s.

```
mean time for 20 = (31.6 + 31.4 + 31.8) / 3 = 31.6 s
period = 31.6 / 20 = 1.58 s
```

## Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** has magnitude only. A **vector** has magnitude and direction.

| Scalars (syllabus list) | Vectors (syllabus list) |
|---|---|
| distance, speed, time, mass, energy, temperature | displacement, force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field strength, gravitational field strength |

Learn the pairs: **distance** (scalar) and **displacement** (vector); **speed** (scalar) and
**velocity** (vector); **mass** (scalar) and **weight** (vector, because it is a force acting
downwards).

## Resultant of two vectors at right angles

The **resultant** is the single vector with the same effect as the two together. When two
vectors are at right angles, they form two sides of a right-angled triangle and the resultant is
the hypotenuse.

**By calculation**

- Magnitude: resultant = √(a² + b²)
- Direction: tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent, with the angle measured from a stated reference
  direction.

**Worked example 1 (forces).** Two forces act on a point: 5.0 N east and 12.0 N north.

```
resultant = √(5.0² + 12.0²) = √(25 + 144) = √169 = 13.0 N
tan θ = 12.0 / 5.0 = 2.4  →  θ = 67.4° north of east
```

**Worked example 2 (velocities).** An aircraft flies at 80 m/s due north while a wind blows at
15 m/s due east.

```
resultant velocity = √(80² + 15²) = √6625 = 81 m/s (2 s.f.)
tan θ = 15 / 80  →  θ = 10.6° east of north
```

**By scale drawing**

1. Choose a scale, such as 1 cm to 1 N, and state it.
2. Draw the first vector as an arrow of the correct length and direction.
3. Draw the second vector starting from the **tip** of the first, at right angles.
4. Join the **tail** of the first to the **tip** of the second: this is the resultant.
5. Measure its length and convert with the scale; measure its angle with a protractor.

A scale drawing should agree with the calculation to within the precision of the ruler and
protractor; examiners accept small differences.

## Common mistakes

- Reading only the sleeve of a micrometer, or forgetting the half-millimetre mark.
- Reading the top of the meniscus, or reading the cylinder from above.
- Timing one oscillation of a pendulum instead of many.
- Adding perpendicular vectors as numbers: 5 N and 12 N at right angles give 13 N, not 17 N.
- Giving a resultant vector without a direction.
- Calling weight or gravitational field strength a scalar.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Describe** (a measurement): name the instrument, the readings taken and any calculation, in
  order.
- **Determine**: get a value from given data or a drawing, showing the steps.
- **Calculate**: show the equation, substitution and answer with a unit.
- **State**: a short answer with no explanation needed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why time from the centre of the swing?** The bob moves fastest there, so the instant it passes
the marker is sharp; at the ends it slows down and the turning point is hard to judge.

**Does measuring multiples reduce every error?** It reduces the effect of reading and reaction-time
uncertainty on each item, but not a systematic error such as a zero error.

**Can a resultant be smaller than one of the vectors?** Not for two vectors at right angles: the
hypotenuse is always longer than either side, but shorter than their sum.
