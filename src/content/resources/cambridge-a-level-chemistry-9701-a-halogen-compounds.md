This guide covers subtopic **31.1, Halogen compounds**, from Topic 31, Halogen compounds, of Cambridge
International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content and builds directly on the AS
chemistry of halogenoalkanes. The [Halogen compounds revision notes](/resources/a-chemistry-halogen-compounds-revision-notes/)
summarise the reactions and the reactivity comparison. This page goes further: it shows how to **classify** any
halogen compound you meet by where the halogen is bonded, how to **predict** what happens when a molecule
contains both kinds of C–X bond, and how to build a full-mark explanation of the chloroethane–chlorobenzene
difference. Afterwards, try the [Halogen compounds practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-halogen-compounds-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

1. **recall** the reactions by which halogenoarenes can be produced: substitution of an arene with Cl₂ or Br₂
   in the presence of a catalyst, AlCl₃ or AlBr₃, exemplified by **benzene to form chlorobenzene** and
   **methylbenzene to form 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene**;
2. **explain the difference in reactivity** between a halogenoalkane and a halogenoarene, exemplified by
   **chloroethane and chlorobenzene**.

## Halogenoalkane or halogenoarene? Look at the carbon

The class depends only on the carbon atom the halogen is bonded to:

| Halogen bonded to... | Class | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a carbon **in a benzene ring** (sp², part of the delocalised system) | **halogenoarene** (aryl halide) | chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl; 4-chloromethylbenzene |
| an **sp³ carbon**, even if a benzene ring is elsewhere in the molecule | **halogenoalkane-type** | chloroethane; (chloromethyl)benzene, C₆H₅CH₂Cl |

So (chloromethyl)benzene and 2-chloromethylbenzene have the same molecular formula, C₇H₇Cl, but behave very
differently: the first has its chlorine on the CH₂ side-chain carbon and is hydrolysed on heating with aqueous
alkali; the second has its chlorine on the ring and is not.

## Producing halogenoarenes

**From benzene:**

    C₆H₆ + Cl₂ → C₆H₅Cl + HCl          (AlCl₃ catalyst)

**From methylbenzene:** the methyl group directs to the 2- and 4-positions, so a mixture forms:

    C₆H₅CH₃ + Cl₂ → ClC₆H₄CH₃ + HCl    (AlCl₃; 2- and 4-isomers)

The mechanism is electrophilic substitution. AlCl₃ accepts a lone pair from Cl₂, generating the electrophile,
and is regenerated when the H⁺ lost from the ring combines with AlCl₄⁻. Bromine with AlBr₃ works the same way.
Conditions that must be avoided: **UV light**, which would substitute the side-chain of methylbenzene
instead, and **water**, which would react with the anhydrous catalyst.

**Counting the isomers.** In monochlorinated methylbenzene, the ring chlorine could be at position 2, 3 or 4
relative to the methyl group. Only the 2- and 4-isomers form in significant amounts because of the directing
effect. The 2-isomer has two equivalent positions available on the ring (2 and 6), the 4-isomer one.

## The reactivity difference: what is observed

| Test | Chloroethane | Chlorobenzene |
|---|---|---|
| heat under reflux with NaOH(aq) | hydrolysed to ethanol and Cl⁻ | no reaction |
| warm with AgNO₃ in aqueous ethanol | white AgCl precipitate forms (slowly) | no precipitate |

The silver nitrate test works because any Cl⁻ released by hydrolysis is precipitated as AgCl. No Cl⁻ is released
from chlorobenzene.

## The explanation, built in four links

**Worked example 1: an annotated model answer.**

*Explain why chloroethane is hydrolysed when heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide, but chlorobenzene is not.*

> In chlorobenzene, a lone pair on the chlorine atom is in a p orbital that **overlaps with the delocalised π
> system** of the ring **[1: overlap named]**. The lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring, so the C–Cl
> bond has some **double-bond character**: it is **shorter and stronger** than the C–Cl bond in chloroethane
> **[1: consequence for the bond]**. The electron-rich π system also **repels** the approaching OH⁻ nucleophile,
> and substitution at a ring carbon would **disrupt the stable delocalised ring** **[1: second factor]**. In
> chloroethane the chlorine is on an sp³ carbon, there is no such overlap, and OH⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon,
> displacing Cl⁻ by nucleophilic substitution **[1: contrast with chloroethane]**.

Two points examiners look for in particular: the **orbital** reason (p orbital, π system, delocalised lone
pair) and the **bond-strength** consequence. "Benzene is stable" on its own scores nothing.

**What not to say:** that the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is non-polar. Chlorine is still more electronegative
than carbon. The difference is bond strength and the nucleophile's approach, not the absence of a dipole.

## Worked example 2: a molecule with two C–Cl bonds

*1-chloro-4-(chloromethyl)benzene, ClC₆H₄CH₂Cl, is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Predict
the organic product.*

**Step 1: classify each chlorine.** The Cl on the ring is a halogenoarene chlorine. The Cl on the CH₂ group is
on an sp³ carbon, so it behaves like a halogenoalkane.

**Step 2: apply the reactivity difference.** OH⁻ substitutes the side-chain chlorine only.

**Product:** ClC₆H₄CH₂OH, (4-chlorophenyl)methanol, plus NaCl.

    ClC₆H₄CH₂Cl + NaOH → ClC₆H₄CH₂OH + NaCl

The ring chlorine survives. This kind of question tests whether you can apply the comparison to an
unfamiliar structure rather than recall the two named compounds.

## Consequence for synthesis

Because chlorobenzene does not react with aqueous hydroxide under these conditions, you **cannot** make phenol
by refluxing chlorobenzene with NaOH(aq). The syllabus route to phenol goes through the diazonium salt made
from phenylamine (see the [Phenol study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-phenol/)). In a
planning question, a step "chlorobenzene + NaOH(aq), reflux → phenol" will not be credited.

## Worked example 3: a quantity calculation

*(Yield illustrative.)* 15.6 g of benzene is converted into bromobenzene with a 75.0% yield. Calculate the mass of
bromobenzene. Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, Br = 79.9.

    Mr(C₆H₆) = 78.0         n = 15.6 / 78.0 = 0.200 mol
    Mr(C₆H₅Br) = 6(12.0) + 5(1.0) + 79.9 = 156.9
    theoretical mass = 0.200 × 156.9 = 31.38 g
    actual mass = 0.750 × 31.38 = 23.5 g

## Looking ahead to acyl chlorides

In Topic 33 the same comparison is extended to a third type of C–Cl bond. The ease of hydrolysis is
**acyl chloride > alkyl chloride > aryl chloride**: ethanoyl chloride is hydrolysed by cold water, chloroethane
needs heating with aqueous alkali, and chlorobenzene does not react under either set of conditions. Keep the
chlorobenzene explanation from this page ready to use as the third part of that answer.

## Common mistakes

- **Using UV light** to make 2- or 4-chloromethylbenzene. UV gives side-chain substitution.
- **Calling (chloromethyl)benzene a halogenoarene**. Its chlorine is not on the ring.
- **Giving 3-chloromethylbenzene** as a main product.
- **Explaining with electronegativity** or claiming the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is non-polar.
- **Omitting the orbital explanation**: name the p orbital on Cl and its overlap with the π system.
- **Proposing chlorobenzene → phenol with NaOH(aq)** in a synthesis.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Recall / state the reagents and conditions**: Cl₂ (or Br₂) with AlCl₃ (or AlBr₃); name both products for
  methylbenzene.
- **Explain the difference in reactivity**: overlap of Cl lone pair with the π system → stronger C–Cl bond;
  π electrons repel the nucleophile; contrast with chloroethane.
- **Predict**: classify each C–X bond before deciding which reacts.
- **Describe a test**: give the reagent, condition and observation for each compound.
