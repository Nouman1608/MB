Section 3.3.2 Theory and Methods is the second half of AQA A-level Sociology (7192) Paper 3, Crime
and Deviance with Theory and Methods. Its eleven areas are identical to 3.1.3 in Paper 1, but Paper 3
gives Theory and Methods 30 marks of extended writing rather than 10, so the theoretical debates
need more depth here. This guide develops the five theory areas: "consensus, conflict, structural
and social action theories"; "modernity and post-modernity"; "the nature of science"; "subjectivity,
objectivity and value freedom"; and "the relationship between Sociology and social policy". Because
Paper 3 pairs Theory and Methods with Crime and Deviance, the examples come from the sociology of
crime, so one body of knowledge supports both halves of the paper.

The methods half (research design, sampling and each method) is developed in the
[Theory and Methods 3.1.3 guide for Paper 1](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-theory-and-methods-3-1-3/),
and all eleven areas are summarised in the [Theory and Methods study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-theory-and-methods/).

## Two axes, not one list

Students often learn theories as a list. The specification's wording suggests two separate
questions instead:

- **Consensus or conflict?** Is society based on shared values, or on competing interests?
- **Structure or action?** Does society shape individuals from the top down, or do individuals
  create society through meaningful interaction?

| | Structural | Social action |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Functionalism | (Few clear cases; interactionists stress negotiated order rather than consensus) |
| **Conflict** | Marxism; most feminism | Labelling theory's focus on the power to define |

Placing a theory on both axes lets you criticise it precisely: functionalism is attacked by
conflict theorists for ignoring inequality, and by action theorists for treating people as puppets.
Those are different criticisms and earn separate credit.

### Applying the axes to crime

- **Functionalism (structural, consensus).** Durkheim argued that a certain amount of crime is
  normal and functional: punishing it reaffirms shared values (boundary maintenance) and some
  deviance allows change. Robert Merton (1938) explained crime as **strain** between culturally
  approved goals and the legitimate means to reach them. *Evaluation:* crime statistics show
  patterned, unequal enforcement that a consensus model struggles to explain.
- **Marxism (structural, conflict).** Law-making and enforcement serve the ruling class; crimes of
  the powerful are under-policed. Taylor, Walton and Young's *The New Criminology* (1973) combined
  Marxism with an interest in the meanings of deviant acts. *Evaluation:* deterministic, and it
  under-plays intra-class crime and victims.
- **Interactionism (action, conflict over definitions).** Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963)
  argued that deviance is created when rules are applied and labels attached. Aaron Cicourel's
  *The Social Organization of Juvenile Justice* (1968) showed police and probation officers'
  typifications shaping which young people became official delinquents. *Evaluation:* explains the
  social construction of statistics, but gives little account of why people commit the first act.
- **Feminism (mainly structural, conflict).** Frances Heidensohn argued that women's lower recorded
  offending reflects the greater social control placed on them at home, at work and in public.

The theory you prefer has methodological consequences: Durkheim and Merton work with patterns in
statistics; Becker and Cicourel need observation and the analysis of how records are made.

### Bridging structure and action

Anthony Giddens' structuration theory holds that structures are both the medium and the outcome of
action (the **duality of structure**): people draw on rules and resources, and by doing so reproduce
or change them. Use it to conclude an essay that has set structure against action.

## Modernity and post-modernity

**Modernity** describes industrial societies shaped by the Enlightenment belief that reason and
science bring progress, by capitalism, by the nation state and by stable class-based identities.
Classical sociology was itself a modern project: Comte, Durkheim and Marx each believed social
science could understand society and improve it.

**Postmodernists** argue that this confidence has gone. Lyotard's "incredulity towards
meta-narratives" means no single theory can claim to explain society; Baudrillard's hyperreality
blurs media images and reality. Identities become fluid and consumer-based.

**Late modern** theorists accept that society has changed but reject a clean break. Giddens stresses
reflexivity; Beck stresses risk. Marxists such as David Harvey (*The Condition of Postmodernity*,
1989) explain postmodern culture as a product of a more flexible capitalism.

**Why it matters for theory and method.** If meta-narratives are discredited, the large-scale
structural theories lose authority, and so does the positivist goal of discovering laws of society.
**In crime:** postmodern approaches question fixed legal definitions of crime, and Jock Young's *The
Exclusive Society* (1999) used a late-modern framework to link rising insecurity and exclusion to
crime and to more punitive responses. *Evaluation:* critics reply that persistent class, gender and
ethnic patterns in offending and victimisation are strong evidence that structure still matters.

## Is sociology a science? Going beyond the names

1. **Positivist (inductive) model.** Observe, gather data, find patterns, induce laws. Durkheim's
   *Suicide* (1897) is the classic sociological attempt.
2. **Popper's falsificationism.** No number of confirming observations proves a universal claim (one
   black swan disproves "all swans are white"), so science advances by trying to falsify bold
   hypotheses. Popper argued that Marxism became unscientific when its defenders reinterpreted it to
   fit failed predictions instead of abandoning it.
3. **Kuhn's paradigms.** Normal science works within an agreed paradigm until anomalies build up,
   triggering a crisis and a scientific revolution. Sociology, with competing perspectives, lacks a
   single paradigm.
4. **Realism.** Natural sciences such as meteorology also study **open systems** they cannot control;
   what makes them scientific is explaining underlying, unobservable causes. On this view sociology
   can be scientific without laboratory control.
5. **Interpretivist objection.** Humans act on meanings and can change their behaviour when studied,
   so causal laws are the wrong goal.

*Crime example:* official crime statistics show apparent "patterns", but Cicourel's work suggests the
patterns partly reflect how agencies process cases, which is exactly the problem positivism faces.

## Subjectivity, objectivity and value freedom

- **Classical positivism:** sociology can be objective and should guide progress.
- **Max Weber:** values legitimately shape the choice of topic and interpretation (value relevance),
  but data collection and analysis should be as objective as possible.
- **1950s American "value freedom":** Alvin Gouldner argued in 1962 that claims of neutrality turned
  sociologists into "spiritless technicians" serving whoever paid.
- **Committed sociology:** Becker argued sociologists inevitably take sides and should side with the
  underdog. Gouldner, in *The Sociologist as Partisan* (1968), criticised Becker for siding with
  deviants against petty officials while leaving the powerful system untouched.
- **Feminists and postmodernists:** all knowledge is produced from a standpoint; the aim is
  reflexivity about one's own values, not their elimination.

*Crime example:* left realists explicitly took the side of working-class victims, while labelling
theorists sided with those labelled as deviant. Both openly declared values, and their research
questions differed as a result.

## Sociology and social policy

Distinguish **social problems** (issues that cause public concern and that policy tries to fix,
such as youth crime) from **sociological problems** (any pattern a sociologist wants to explain,
including "normal" behaviour such as why most people obey the law).

| Perspective | Should sociology inform policy? | Crime-related illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Positivist / functionalist | Yes, through objective evidence and piecemeal social engineering | Evaluating which interventions reduce reoffending |
| Social democratic | Yes, to reduce inequality | Policies on poverty and exclusion as causes of crime |
| Marxist | Policy mainly serves capitalism; reform is limited | Selective enforcement of crimes of the powerful |
| Feminist | Yes, research has shaped policy on violence against women | Domestic violence policing and refuges |
| New Right / right realist | Yes, to restore control and responsibility | Wilson and Kelling's "broken windows" article (1982) and zero-tolerance policing |
| Postmodernist | Doubtful: no theory has the authority to direct policy | Scepticism about evidence-led "what works" claims |

Influence is limited by funding, electoral pressures, the ideology of the government of the day and
the way research is reported.

## Worked example: planning a 20-mark essay

*Applying material from an item and your knowledge, evaluate the contribution of social action
theories to our understanding of society.* (Original practice question.)

- **Introduction:** define social action theories (Weber, interactionism, phenomenology,
  ethnomethodology); state the structure–action debate.
- **AO1 knowledge:** Weber's verstehen and four types of action; Mead and Blumer on the self and
  meanings; Garfinkel on how members produce order.
- **AO2 application:** link each item phrase to a theory; use labelling and Cicourel as crime
  evidence.
- **AO3 evaluation:** strengths (explain meaning, change and the construction of statistics);
  weaknesses (neglect power and structure, as Marxists argue; small-scale; ethnomethodology seen as
  trivial by critics); postmodernist and structuration perspectives.
- **Conclusion:** a judgement, for example that social action theories are necessary but not
  sufficient, which is why Giddens' synthesis is attractive.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "structural" and "conflict" as the same axis.
- Name-dropping Popper or Kuhn without saying what follows for sociology.
- Treating late modernity and post-modernity as the same claim.
- Writing about policy without distinguishing social and sociological problems.
- Forgetting that methods questions can appear in Paper 3 too.

## Exam technique

"Evaluate" requires a judgement backed by argument. "Applying material from Item..." requires you to
quote or paraphrase the item and develop each reference. Keep AO1 (knowledge), AO2 (application) and
AO3 (analysis and evaluation) visible in every paragraph.
