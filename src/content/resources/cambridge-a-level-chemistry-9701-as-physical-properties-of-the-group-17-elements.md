This guide covers subtopic **11.1, Physical properties of the Group 17 elements**, from Topic 11, Group 17, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Group 17: The Halogens](/resources/as-group-17-the-halogens/) guide covers all of Topic 11. This page goes further into 11.1: the colours of the elements in each state and in solution, a step-by-step account of why volatility falls down the group, the bond strength trend with data and its one well-known exception, and how to keep the two trends apart in an answer. Condensed notes are in the [11.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-physical-properties-of-the-group-17-elements-revision-notes/), and the [11.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-physical-properties-of-the-group-17-elements-practice/) test it.

## What 11.1 asks you to do

1. Describe the **colours** and the trend in **volatility** of chlorine, bromine and iodine.
2. Describe and explain the trend in the **bond strength** of the halogen molecules.
3. Interpret the volatility of the elements in terms of **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces**.

## Colours and states

| Element | State at room temperature | Colour of the element | Colour of the vapour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | Gas | Pale yellow-green | Pale yellow-green |
| Bromine, Br₂ | Liquid | Dark red-brown | Orange-brown |
| Iodine, I₂ | Solid | Shiny grey-black | Purple (violet) |

Colour **darkens** down the group. Solid iodine sublimes on gentle warming, so a purple vapour forms without a visible liquid stage.

Colours in solution are needed later, in displacement reactions (11.2):

| Halogen | In water | In a hydrocarbon solvent such as cyclohexane |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | Very pale green, almost colourless | Very pale green, almost colourless |
| Br₂ | Yellow to orange | Orange to red-brown |
| I₂ | Yellow-brown to brown | Purple (violet) |

The hydrocarbon layer is the more useful one for telling bromine from iodine: orange against purple is easier to judge than orange against brown.

## Volatility

**Volatility** is how easily a substance turns into a vapour. A more volatile substance has a lower boiling point.

| Element | Electrons per molecule | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | 34 | −101 | −34 |
| Br₂ | 70 | −7 | 59 |
| I₂ | 106 | 114 | 184 |

Volatility **decreases** down the group: chlorine is a gas, bromine a volatile liquid, iodine a solid.

### The explanation, step by step

The halogens are simple molecular substances. When they melt or boil, the forces **between** molecules are overcome. The covalent X–X bond **within** each molecule is not broken. The only forces between the non-polar X₂ molecules are instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id) forces.

1. The electrons in a molecule are constantly moving. At any instant their distribution may be uneven, giving an **instantaneous dipole**.
2. This dipole distorts the electron cloud of a neighbouring molecule, giving an **induced dipole**.
3. The two dipoles attract each other. That attraction is the id-id force.
4. Down the group, molecules contain **more electrons** (34 → 70 → 106), and the electron cloud is larger and **more easily polarised**.
5. So larger instantaneous dipoles form and the id-id forces are **stronger**.
6. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the melting and boiling points rise and volatility falls.

**Worked example: an annotated model answer.** *Explain why iodine is a solid at room temperature but chlorine is a gas.*

> "Both are simple molecular, so only the forces between molecules are overcome on melting or boiling [1]. These are instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces [1]. I₂ has many more electrons than Cl₂ (106 compared with 34), so its electron cloud is more polarisable and the id-id forces are stronger [1]. More energy is needed to separate I₂ molecules, so iodine has a higher melting point and is a solid at room temperature [1]."

## Bond strength of the halogen molecules

The X–X bond energies (values as typically given in a data booklet, kJ mol⁻¹) are:

| Bond | F–F | Cl–Cl | Br–Br | I–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 158 | 242 | 193 | 151 |

From chlorine to iodine, bond strength **decreases**. The explanation:

- Down the group the atoms are **larger**, so the bond is **longer**.
- The shared pair of electrons is **further from both nuclei** and more shielded by inner shells.
- So the attraction between the nuclei and the bonding pair is **weaker**, and less energy is needed to break the bond.

**The fluorine exception.** The F–F bond is weaker than Cl–Cl, although fluorine atoms are smaller. Fluorine atoms are so small that, when they bond, the **lone pairs** on the two atoms are very close together. The repulsion between these lone pairs weakens the bond. The syllabus outcome names chlorine, bromine and iodine for colour and volatility, but "the halogen molecules" for bond strength, so be ready to recognise the fluorine value in data.

## Keep the two trends apart

Students often link volatility and bond strength. They are separate:

| | Volatility | Bond strength |
|---|---|---|
| What it concerns | Forces **between** molecules | The covalent bond **within** a molecule |
| Trend Cl₂ → I₂ | Decreases (boiling point rises) | Decreases |
| Reason | More electrons → stronger id-id forces | Larger atoms → longer bond, bonding pair further from nuclei |

Both decrease down the group, but for unrelated reasons. A weaker I–I bond does **not** make iodine easier to melt. Melting iodine does not break I–I bonds at all.

## Predicting astatine

Astatine is below iodine in Group 17. Extrapolating the trends gives these predictions:

- **Colour**: darker than iodine, likely black.
- **State**: a solid at room temperature, with higher melting and boiling points than iodine, because At₂ molecules would have more electrons and stronger id-id forces.
- **Bond strength**: the At–At bond would be weaker than I–I, because the atoms are larger.

These are predictions from the trends, and you should present them as such ("expected to be", "predicted to be").

## Common mistakes

- **"Iodine has a higher boiling point because the I–I bond is stronger."** The I–I bond is weaker, and it is not broken on boiling.
- **Calling the forces "van der Waals" without explaining them.** Use the syllabus term, instantaneous dipole–induced dipole, and explain how it arises.
- **Saying "bigger molecules" without saying why that matters.** Link size to the number of electrons and polarisability.
- **Getting colours mixed.** Bromine liquid is red-brown; iodine vapour is purple; iodine in a hydrocarbon solvent is purple.
- **Forgetting that the halogens are diatomic.** Write Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
- **Explaining the bond strength trend with nuclear charge.** Nuclear charge increases, but the larger distance and extra shielding dominate.

## Exam technique: command words in 11.1

- **Describe** (colours, volatility): give the colour and state for each element and the direction of the trend.
- **Explain** (bond strength): bond length, distance of the bonding pair from the nuclei, shielding, weaker attraction.
- **Interpret** (volatility): name id-id forces, link them to the number of electrons, and conclude about the energy needed to separate molecules.
- **Predict** (astatine): give the predicted property and the trend it comes from.
