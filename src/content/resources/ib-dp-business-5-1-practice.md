These questions cover section 5.1 (Introduction to operations management) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. Learn the method from the [5.1 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-5-1/) and the [5.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-5-1-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *operations management*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two inputs and one output for a car repair garage. **[3]**

**3.** A bakery sells a cake for 24.00. Bought-in ingredients and packaging cost 7.40. Calculate the value added per cake. **[1]**

**4.** Distinguish between goods and services, using one example of each. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why value added is not the same as profit. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way in which operations decisions affect a business's human resources. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

Questions 7 to 10 use the case below.

Lindqvist Bikes assembles electric bicycles from frames, motors and batteries bought from suppliers. It also runs a repair service at its workshop. Each bicycle sells for 1,600, and bought-in parts cost 920. Lindqvist sells 300 bicycles a month. Its marketing team has started promising "delivery within 48 hours" and "any colour you like". The workshop has six mechanics, who often have no work on weekday mornings but turn customers away on Saturdays.

**7.** Calculate:

**(a)** the value added per bicycle. **[1]**

**(b)** the total value added per month. **[1]**

**8.** Explain one difference between the operations of Lindqvist's assembly work and its repair service. **[2]**

**9.** Analyse the trade-offs Lindqvist's operations face because of the marketing team's promises. **[6]**

**10.** Explain the role of operations management at Lindqvist Bikes. **[6]**

**11.** Parkholme Hospital wants to reduce patient waiting times. Explain two ways its operations management could contribute. **[4]**

**12.** Grafton Joinery plans to cut costs by buying cheaper timber from a supplier with poor environmental standards. Discuss the implications of this operations decision for the business. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The planning, organising and controlling of the process that turns inputs into outputs [1], so that goods or services are produced at the quality, cost and time customers expect [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Inputs, any two: mechanics' labour, spare parts, tools and lifts, energy, the premises [1][1]. Output: repaired or serviced vehicles [1]. **[3]**

**3.** 24.00 − 7.40 = **16.60** [1] **[1]**

**4.** Goods are tangible products that can be stored, such as a packet of biscuits [1]; services are intangible, produced and consumed at the same time, such as a haircut [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Value added is price minus the cost of bought-in inputs only [1]; wages, rent, interest and other costs must still be paid from it before profit is made [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A change of production method, such as automation, changes the number and skills of staff needed [1], so the business may need to retrain workers, recruit new skills or make some roles redundant [1]. **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** 1,600 − 920 = **680** [1]

**(b)** 300 × 680 = **204,000** [1] **[2]**

**8.** Assembled bicycles are goods that can be built in advance and stored as stock [1], whereas repairs are a service produced while the customer waits or when they bring the bike in, so they cannot be stored, which is why idle weekday mornings cannot be used for Saturday demand [1]. **[2]**

**9.** "Delivery within 48 hours" requires speed and dependability [1], which means holding stock of finished bikes or parts, tying up cash and storage space [1]. "Any colour you like" requires flexibility [1], but painting to order means changeovers and waiting time, which conflicts with 48-hour delivery [1]. Meeting both promises would push up cost per unit, reducing the 680 of value added unless prices rise [1]. Operations must therefore agree with marketing which aim matters most to customers, for example offering a limited colour range in 48 hours and other colours in a longer time [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Operations transform bought-in frames, motors and batteries, plus labour, into finished bicycles worth 1,600 each [1], adding 680 of value per bike [1]. They must manage suppliers and stock so parts are available without tying up too much cash [1]. They must balance performance aims such as speed and flexibility created by marketing promises [1]. In the workshop, operations must match mechanics to demand, for example by moving some staff hours from weekday mornings to Saturdays [1], since service capacity cannot be stored [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Way 1: plan capacity and staffing to match patient arrival patterns [1], for example more staff at busy times, so fewer patients queue [1]. Way 2: redesign the patient process, such as booking systems or faster test results [1], removing delays between stages of treatment [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Lower input costs would raise value added per item and could improve profit or allow lower prices [1]. However, the decision raises an ethical issue, because the business would profit from environmental harm [1]. If customers learn of it, Grafton's reputation could suffer and sales may fall, especially among buyers who value sustainable timber [1]. Cheaper timber may also be of lower or less consistent quality, increasing waste and rework [1]. Staff may be uncomfortable with the decision, affecting morale [1]. Judgement: the saving is unlikely to be worth the risk to reputation and quality unless Grafton competes purely on price in a market that ignores sourcing; it should first look for savings by cutting waste [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

* Describing operations in general without using the case organisation.
* Calling value added profit.
* Ignoring the difference between storing goods and delivering services.
* Explaining one performance aim with no trade-off.
* Treating ethical sourcing as unrelated to operations.
