This guide goes deeper on one subtopic of Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **1.4 Business objectives**, part of AS Level topic 1, Business and its environment. A Level candidates need it too, because Paper 3 and Paper 4 assume all AS content.

The syllabus splits 1.4 into two parts:

- **1.4.1 Business objectives in the private sector and public sector**: the objectives of private-sector businesses, public-sector organisations and social enterprises; why objectives matter; corporate social responsibility (CSR) and the triple bottom line; and the relationship between mission statement, aims, objectives, strategy and tactics.
- **1.4.2 Objectives and business decisions**: the stages of decision-making and the role of objectives in them; how objectives change over time; turning objectives into targets and budgets; communicating objectives and the likely effect on the workforce; SMART objectives; and how ethics may influence objectives and activities.

## Why objectives matter: four jobs they do

Saying "objectives give direction" earns little on its own. Be precise about what an objective actually does inside a business:

1. **It is a decision rule.** When managers compare two options, the objective tells them which outcome counts as better. Without one, a cheaper option and a higher-quality option cannot be ranked.
2. **It allows delegation.** A manager can hand a department a target and let its staff choose the method, because the target defines success. This links to delegation and empowerment in later topics.
3. **It is a yardstick for control.** Actual results are compared with the objective, so managers know whether to change course.
4. **It coordinates functions.** Marketing, operations, finance and HR objectives are all derived from the same corporate objective, so the departments do not work against each other.

Objectives only do these jobs if they are clear, few and consistent.

## Objectives differ by type of organisation

The syllabus names three kinds of organisation. Go one step further and notice that objectives also vary *within* the private sector, because ownership differs.

| Organisation | Likely priority objectives | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader or small partnership | Survival, a steady income for the owner, independence | The owner's livelihood depends on it, and there are no outside shareholders to satisfy |
| Private limited company | Profit, steady growth, keeping control within the family or founders | Shares are not sold to the public, so owners can take a long-term view |
| Public limited company | Shareholder returns: profit growth, dividends, a rising share price | Shareholders can sell shares easily, and a falling share price raises takeover risk |
| Public-sector organisation | Service quality, access for all, value for money, staying within budget | Funded by taxation and answerable to government rather than shareholders |
| Social enterprise | A social or environmental purpose, with enough surplus to be financially sustainable | Profit is a means to the purpose, not the purpose itself |

The strongest answers link the objective to *who has the power to set it*.

## When objectives conflict with each other

A business rarely pursues one objective. Several common pairs pull against each other:

- **Profit against growth**: rapid expansion needs spending on new sites, staff and marketing, which can cut profit for several years.
- **Market share against profit margin**: winning share often means lower prices, so each sale earns less.
- **Short-term profit against long-term survival**: cutting training or research raises this year's profit but weakens future competitiveness.
- **Financial objectives against social and environmental objectives**: paying suppliers more or switching to cleaner energy raises costs.

The triple bottom line makes this last conflict explicit. A business reporting on **economic (financial)**, **social** and **environmental** objectives must decide what to do when the three disagree. Some firms set a minimum on one line (for example, "no fall in profit margin below 6%") and then pursue the others. Others, such as social enterprises, treat the social line as the main one and the financial line as a constraint.

### CSR: an evaluation framework

When asked to assess whether a business should adopt CSR objectives, weigh:

- **Costs**: higher input prices, monitoring and reporting costs, and fewer options (for example, being unable to switch to the cheapest supplier).
- **Benefits**: reputation, customer loyalty, easier recruitment, lower risk of fines or boycotts.
- **What the judgement depends on**: how much customers in this market value responsible behaviour, how strong the firm's finances are, and whether the CSR claims are genuine. Claims that are not backed by action can damage trust once discovered.

## From mission to tactics: a worked example

The syllabus asks for the *relationship* between the five terms, not just definitions. Each level must serve the one above it.

**Hollowmere Water Services**, a public-sector water provider:

- **Mission statement**: "Safe, reliable water for every household in the region."
- **Aim**: reduce water lost through leaking pipes.
- **Objective**: cut leakage from 22% to 15% of water supplied within four years.
- **Strategy**: replace the oldest 300 km of pipe and install pressure sensors across the network.
- **Tactics**: this year, a team of repair crews works night shifts in the three worst districts; residents are offered a free leak check.

If a night-shift scheme fails, the tactic changes but the objective stays. If funding is cut, the strategy may change and the objective may need a longer deadline. The mission should rarely change.

## Objectives in the stages of decision-making

A standard set of stages is: set objectives; gather information; identify options; evaluate options against the objective; choose and implement; review against the objective. The objective appears at the start, in the middle and at the end.

**Worked example.** Tamberlane Bakery's objective is to raise annual profit by $30 000. Two options:

| | Option A: open on Sundays | Option B: supply three local cafés |
|---|---|---|
| Extra revenue a year | $78 000 | $64 000 |
| Extra costs a year | $52 000 | $36 000 |
| Extra profit | **$26 000** | **$28 000** |

Neither option meets the $30 000 objective alone, and Option B comes closer. Two lessons follow. First, the objective, not the revenue figure, decides the ranking: Option A has more revenue but less profit. Second, the review stage might lead the bakery to combine options or revise the objective, which shows the stages are a loop, not a line.

## How objectives change over time

Changes come from inside and outside the business:

- **Internal**: the business grows past the survival stage; a new owner or chief executive arrives; a loss-making year forces a focus on cash.
- **External**: recession shifts attention from growth to survival; a new competitor forces a defence of market share; new laws on emissions make environmental objectives compulsory rather than optional.

Always name the trigger, then explain *which* objective moves up or down and why.

## Translating objectives into targets and budgets

**Worked example.** Ardwell Office Supplies has revenue of $2 000 000 and a profit margin of 8%. Its objective is a 10% margin next year, with revenue expected to stay the same.

1. Current profit = 8% × 2 000 000 = $160 000.
2. Target profit = 10% × 2 000 000 = $200 000.
3. Current total costs = 2 000 000 − 160 000 = $1 840 000.
4. Cost budget for next year = 2 000 000 − 200 000 = $1 800 000.
5. Costs must therefore fall by $40 000, about 2.2% of current costs.

Each department can then be given its share of the $40 000 saving. The objective has become a figure that a manager can be held accountable for.

## Communicating objectives to the workforce

How an objective is communicated affects how staff respond. Involving employees in setting targets tends to increase commitment, because people work harder towards goals they helped design. Targets imposed without explanation are more likely to be resisted. A further risk is that staff hit the measured target at the expense of what is not measured: a call centre judged on call length may cut calls short and lose customers.

## SMART objectives in practice

A useful exam skill is rewriting a weak objective. "Improve customer service" can become: "Reduce the average time to answer customer emails from 48 hours to 24 hours by 31 March, using the existing team." Each part now passes a test: specific (emails), measurable (hours), achievable (existing team), realistic (half the current time, not instant), time-limited (31 March).

## Ethics and objectives

Ethics can shape the **objectives** a business sets (refusing to profit from selling to children, for example) and the **activities** it uses to reach them (honest advertising, fair supplier terms). The key evaluation is whether an ethical choice is a cost or an investment. In a market where customers check how products are made, it can raise sales; in a market driven only by price, it may simply reduce margins.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Analyse why a public-sector hospital and a private clinic might have different objectives.*

> A public-sector hospital is funded by government, so its main objective is likely to be treating all patients who need care within its budget, rather than making a profit. *(Knowledge, applied to the context.)* Because it cannot turn patients away on cost grounds, it may set objectives such as shorter waiting times. *(Analysis: the chain from funding to objective.)* A private clinic depends on fees, so it must cover its costs and earn a return for its owners; this leads it to objectives such as profit and attracting patients who can pay, which may mean concentrating on a narrower range of treatments. *(Analysis: a contrasting chain.)*

## Common mistakes

- Treating the mission statement as a measurable target.
- Assuming every business aims to maximise profit.
- Listing reasons why objectives change without saying which objective changes.
- Calling an objective SMART when it has no deadline.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define** or **Identify**: precise meaning or a named item; no evaluation needed.
- **Explain**: give the reason and the effect, ideally using the case business.
- **Analyse**: build a chain of cause and effect, such as objective → decision → result for this firm.
- **Assess**, **Evaluate** or **To what extent**: weigh both sides and reach a judgement that states what it depends on.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend one course of action for the given business and support it with evidence from the case.
