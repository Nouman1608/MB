This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.6.1 Generating electricity**, statements a to f of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The whole subtopic is marked **P**, so it is assessed in the full Physics award and not in Combined Science. The specification expects you to suggest the factors that affect the size of an induced potential difference, and to explain how an **alternator** generates ac and a **dynamo** generates dc, including graphs of potential difference against time. Detailed knowledge of slip rings and split rings is not required.

The [electromagnetic induction, generators and the motor effect guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-electromagnetic-induction-generators-and-the-motor/) covers this alongside the motor effect. This page goes further on predicting the direction and size of the induced pd, on reading generator graphs, and on the chain of energy transfers in a power station. Afterwards, use the [generating electricity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-generating-electricity-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and the [generating electricity practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-generating-electricity-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | A pd is induced across a conductor when it moves relative to a magnetic field or is in a changing magnetic field: the generator effect |
| b | A pd is induced across a coil when a magnet moves into or out of it, or the coil moves relative to the magnet |
| c | In a complete circuit a current flows; its magnetic field opposes the field of the permanent magnet |
| d | Reversing the motion or the magnet's polarity reverses the induced pd and current |
| e | Induced pd increases with speed, field strength, number of turns and coil area; alternators (ac) and dynamos (dc) |
| f | Power stations use turbines to turn coils between magnets |

## The generator effect (statements a and b)

A potential difference is induced whenever a conductor and a magnetic field move **relative** to each other so that the conductor cuts across field lines, or when the field through the conductor **changes**. Nothing is induced if there is no relative movement, or if a wire moves parallel to the field lines.

Pushing a bar magnet into a coil connected to a sensitive ammeter shows the main features:

| Action | Ammeter reading |
|---|---|
| Magnet pushed in | Needle flicks one way |
| Magnet held still inside the coil | Zero |
| Magnet pulled out | Needle flicks the other way |
| Magnet pushed in faster | Bigger flick |
| South pole pushed in instead of north | Needle flicks the opposite way to the first row |
| Coil moved towards a stationary magnet | Same as pushing the magnet in |

The last row shows that only the relative motion matters. A pd is induced across the ends of the coil even if the circuit is open; a **current** only flows if the circuit is complete (statement c).

## Why the induced current opposes the magnet (statement c)

The induced current produces its own magnetic field. The specification states that this field **opposes** the field of the permanent magnet that caused it. For example, when the north pole of a magnet is pushed into a coil, the induced current makes the near end of the coil a north pole, which repels the approaching magnet. When the magnet is pulled out, the near end becomes a south pole, which attracts the magnet and resists its removal.

This has an energy meaning. Because the coil always pushes back, you must do work to move the magnet, and that work is the source of the electrical energy. If the induced field helped the motion instead, energy would appear from nowhere, which is impossible.

## Making the induced pd bigger (statement e)

The size of the induced pd increases when:

1. the **speed** of the movement increases;
2. the **strength** of the magnetic field increases;
3. the **number of turns** on the coil increases;
4. the **area** of the coil increases.

When asked to "suggest" factors, name the physical change precisely: "use a stronger magnet" or "move the magnet faster" earns marks; "use a better magnet" or "more energy" does not. Using a longer wire wound into more turns increases the number of turns; using a thicker wire does not on its own increase the pd.

## Alternators and dynamos (statement e)

In both machines a coil rotates in a magnetic field. As it turns, each side of the coil moves up through the field for half a turn and down for the other half, so the induced pd naturally reverses every half turn.

- An **alternator** connects the coil to the circuit in a way that keeps the reversals, so the output is **alternating current**. Its graph of pd against time is a smooth wave above and below zero.
- A **dynamo** reverses the connections every half turn, so the output always has the same sign: **direct current**. Its graph is a series of humps that all lie above zero, falling to zero twice each turn.

**Reading the graphs.** One complete cycle of an alternator graph corresponds to one complete turn of the coil. The pd is largest when the coil sides are cutting the field lines fastest (the coil plane is parallel to the field) and zero when the sides move along the lines (the coil plane is perpendicular to the field).

**Worked example.** An alternator coil turns 50 times per second.

```
Time for one turn (period) = 1 ÷ 50 = 0.020 s
Frequency of the ac output = 50 Hz
The coil is then turned at 100 turns per second:
Period = 1 ÷ 100 = 0.010 s, frequency = 100 Hz
The coil cuts field lines faster, so the peak pd is also larger.
```

On the new graph the waves are both **taller** and **closer together**. A common exam error is to change only one of these. For a dynamo turning faster, the humps are taller and closer together, and still never go below zero.

## Power stations (statement f)

In a power station, turbines turn wire coils between magnets. In fuel-burning and nuclear power stations the chain is:

1. Fuel is burned (or nuclear fission takes place), heating water in a boiler.
2. The water turns to high-pressure steam.
3. The steam drives a turbine, making it spin.
4. The turbine turns the generator coils between magnets, inducing a pd.

Energy is dissipated to the surroundings at each stage, mostly by heating, so the overall efficiency is well below 100%. In wind turbines and hydroelectric stations, moving air or water turns the turbine directly, with no boiler. Generators in power stations produce ac, which is then sent to the transmission system described in the next subtopic.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "the magnet makes electricity". A pd is induced by **relative movement** or a **changing** field.
- Thinking a magnet resting inside a coil induces a current.
- Forgetting that the circuit must be complete for a current, though a pd is induced either way.
- Drawing a dynamo graph that goes below zero.
- Changing only the height or only the spacing of an alternator graph when the speed changes.
- Writing "the coil's field attracts the magnet" when the magnet is pushed in.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** how a current is induced: relative motion, cutting field lines, pd induced, complete circuit, current flows.
- **Suggest** factors: list the four from the specification in their exact form.
- **Sketch** graphs: label axes (pd and time), keep the period consistent, and show how speed changes both height and spacing.
- **Describe** a power station: give the stages in order and name the energy store or transfer at each.
