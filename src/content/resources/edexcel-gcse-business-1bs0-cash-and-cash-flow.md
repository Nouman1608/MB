Appendix 3 gives:
- Net cash-flow = cash inflows − cash outflows in a given period
- Opening balance = closing balance of the previous period
- Closing balance = opening balance + net cash-flow

Appendix 2 lists cash-flow forecasts among both the calculations and the financial data you must interpret.

## Part 1: why cash is so important

Cash is needed because bills arrive on fixed dates, whatever has happened to sales.

- **Suppliers** expect payment on agreed terms. A business that pays late may lose its credit terms or its supplier.
- **Overheads** such as rent, insurance and energy must be paid on time, or the business risks losing its premises or services.
- **Employees** must be paid on payday. Late wages damage motivation and can break employment agreements.

If a business cannot pay its debts when they are due, it is **insolvent**. Creditors can then take action to recover what they are owed, which can force the business to close. This is why the specification links cash to preventing business failure.

## Part 2: cash and profit are measured differently

- **Profit** compares revenue with costs for a period. A sale counts as revenue when it is made, even if the customer pays later.
- **Cash** counts money only when it actually moves in or out of the bank or till.

Three common reasons the two differ:
1. **Customers pay later** (credit sales), so revenue is earned before the cash arrives.
2. **Large purchases** such as equipment take cash out all at once, though they are used for years.
3. **Loans and owner's capital** bring in cash but are not revenue, so they do not add to profit.

## Part 3: a forecast with credit sales

### Worked example: Fellside Signs

Fellside Signs makes signs for local businesses. Its customers pay **one month after** the sale. Materials cost 40% of each month's sales and are paid in the same month. Wages are £2,200 a month and rent is £800. December's sales were £3,000. The bank balance at the start of January is £1,500.

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales made (for reference) | 4,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 | 5,000 |
| **Cash inflows: receipts from customers** | 3,000 | 4,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 |
| Materials (40% of sales made) | 1,600 | 2,400 | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| Wages | 2,200 | 2,200 | 2,200 | 2,200 |
| Rent | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 |
| **Total cash outflows** | 4,600 | 5,400 | 6,000 | 5,000 |
| **Net cash flow** | −1,600 | −1,400 | 0 | 2,500 |
| **Opening balance** | 1,500 | −100 | −1,500 | −1,500 |
| **Closing balance** | −100 | −1,500 | −1,500 | 1,000 |

Check February: net cash flow = £4,000 − £5,400 = −£1,400. Closing balance = −£100 + (−£1,400) = −£1,500, which becomes March's opening balance.

### Profit against cash, month by month

Profit here uses the sales made and the same costs.

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Profit or loss | −600 | 600 | 1,500 | 0 |
| Net cash flow | −1,600 | −1,400 | 0 | 2,500 |

In February Fellside makes a **profit of £600** but its cash **falls by £1,400**. Sales are growing, so each month it pays for more materials while waiting for January's smaller sales to be paid. Growth itself is draining cash. In April the pattern reverses: profit is zero but cash rises by £2,500, as March's large sales are paid.

### Interpreting the forecast
- The balance is negative from January to March, at worst −£1,500. Without arrangements, Fellside could not pay its wages or rent on time.
- The cause is the one-month delay in receipts, not a lack of customers.
- Possible actions: arrange an overdraft of at least £1,500 for three months; ask customers for a deposit; offer a small discount for quick payment; ask the materials supplier for credit so that payments are made a month later.
- April's recovery shows the problem is temporary, which makes short-term finance more suitable than a long-term loan (sources are covered in 1.3.4).

## Part 4: working out missing values

Rearrange the Appendix 3 formulae:
- Cash outflows = cash inflows − net cash flow
- Cash inflows = net cash flow + cash outflows
- Net cash flow = closing balance − opening balance
- Opening balance = closing balance − net cash flow

### Worked example

| | May | June |
|---|---|---|
| Cash inflows | 5,200 | ? |
| Cash outflows | ? | 4,800 |
| Net cash flow | −300 | ? |
| Opening balance | 900 | ? |
| Closing balance | ? | 1,400 |

- May outflows = £5,200 − (−£300) = **£5,500**
- May closing balance = £900 + (−£300) = **£600**
- June opening balance = May closing = **£600**
- June net cash flow = £1,400 − £600 = **£800**
- June inflows = £800 + £4,800 = **£5,600**

Always fill in the opening balance link first: it connects one month to the next.

## Part 5: limits of a forecast

A forecast is a prediction. Sales may be lower than expected, customers may pay later than agreed, and costs can rise. Owners compare forecasts with what actually happens and update them. In an evaluation, say how reliable the figures are likely to be: a business with years of records can forecast more accurately than a start-up.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain):** Explain one reason why a profitable business may still fail. **[3]**

> A business may sell on credit, so customers pay weeks after the sale is recorded as revenue (statement). In the meantime it still has to pay suppliers, rent and wages in cash (expansion). If it cannot pay these debts when they are due it becomes insolvent and may be forced to close, even though its sales are making a profit (second expansion).

## Common mistakes

- Counting sales made, rather than cash received, as the inflow when customers pay later.
- Carrying forward the opening balance instead of the closing balance.
- Adding a negative net cash flow instead of subtracting it.
- Saying a negative closing balance means a loss.
- Recommending a long-term loan for a short, temporary gap.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Complete the table:** work across one month at a time, using the opening balance link.
- **Calculate:** show each formula with figures.
- **Identify:** the month with the lowest balance, or the cause of a change.
- **Analyse:** say what caused the cash problem and what it means for paying suppliers, overheads and employees.
- **Justify:** choose between two solutions, such as an overdraft or chasing customers for faster payment.
