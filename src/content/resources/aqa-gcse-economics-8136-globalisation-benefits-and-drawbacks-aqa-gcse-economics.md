Globalisation is the process by which the world's economies become more closely linked through trade in goods and services, flows of investment, the spread of technology and the movement of people. Section 3.2.4.4 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification asks you to describe the main features of globalisation, explain what has made it grow (new technology and the operations of multinational companies are named), weigh its benefits and drawbacks for **producers, workers and consumers** in developed countries such as the UK and in less developed countries, and recognise the **moral, ethical and sustainability considerations** when UK producers trade with other countries.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, which is assessed on Paper 2. There are no tiers. All firms and figures on this page are invented for teaching. The shorter overview of free trade and globalisation together is in the [free trade, the EU and globalisation guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-free-trade-agreements-and-globalisation/); this page goes further into globalisation alone. ## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Main features of globalisation | Describe how economies have become more interdependent, and explain the factors behind it, including new technology and multinational companies |
| Benefits and drawbacks in developed countries | Explain the effects on UK producers, workers and consumers |
| Benefits and drawbacks in less developed countries | Explain the effects on producers, workers and consumers there |
| Moral, ethical and sustainability considerations | Explain the issues that arise when UK producers trade with other countries |

Notice the structure: three groups, two kinds of country, plus ethics. Many questions are built from that grid, so an answer that names the group and the country type scores more securely than one about "people" in general.

## The main features of globalisation

- **More international trade.** A larger share of what countries produce is exported, and a larger share of what they buy is imported.
- **International supply chains.** One product is designed in one country, its parts are made in several others and it is assembled somewhere else. A smartphone or a car is rarely "made" in a single place.
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI).** Firms build factories, offices and shops abroad, or buy foreign firms.
- **Movement of capital.** Savings and investment flow quickly between countries through the financial system.
- **Movement of labour.** More people move between countries to work.
- **Global brands and shared culture.** The same products, films and services are sold in many countries.

Together these make countries **interdependent**: what happens in one economy affects others. A recession, a natural disaster or a blocked shipping route in one place can raise prices and cut output thousands of miles away.

## Why has globalisation grown?

### New technology

- **Transport.** Large container ships and standard shipping containers have cut the cost of moving each unit of goods, so it can be cheaper to make a product far away and ship it than to make it nearby.
- **Communications.** The internet and mobile networks let a firm in the UK manage a factory abroad in real time, and let consumers order from sellers anywhere.
- **Digital services.** Some services, such as software support, design or customer service, can be supplied from another country with no physical transport at all.

### The operations of multinational companies

A **multinational company (MNC)** is a firm with operations in more than one country. MNCs drive globalisation because they:

- locate each stage of production where it is cheapest or most efficient, creating supply chains across borders;
- invest in factories and offices abroad, taking capital, technology and management methods with them;
- sell global brands, spreading the same products across markets;
- move profits, staff and knowledge between their branches.

### Other factors

Lower trade barriers through free-trade agreements, the growth of large emerging economies with low labour costs, and easier movement of money between countries have all helped.

## Benefits and drawbacks for developed countries such as the UK

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| UK producers | Access to far larger markets, so they can grow and gain economies of scale; cheaper materials and components; can locate production where costs are lowest | Intense competition from low-cost foreign firms; may lose market share or close; long supply chains can be disrupted |
| UK workers | Jobs in export industries and in foreign MNCs that locate in the UK; demand for high-skill jobs in design, finance and services | Jobs lost when production moves abroad (**offshoring**), especially in manufacturing; downward pressure on wages in industries that face import competition; workers need retraining, so some face structural unemployment |
| UK consumers | Lower prices; wider choice of goods and services from around the world; access to new products sooner | Fewer locally made goods; shortages and price rises when global supply chains break; some lose income through job losses |

The key evaluation point is that the gains and losses fall on **different people**. Lower prices are spread thinly across millions of consumers, while job losses are concentrated in particular towns and industries.

## Benefits and drawbacks for less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Can sell to rich-country markets; can become suppliers to MNCs; gain technology and know-how | Local firms may be unable to compete with MNCs; dependence on a few export products or a few large buyers |
| Workers | New jobs, often paying more than farm work or informal work; training and skills | Low pay and long hours in some factories; poor safety; jobs can move on to an even cheaper country |
| Consumers | More goods and services available; incomes rise as the economy grows | Profits may flow back to the MNC's home country; local culture and local products may be displaced |

Globalisation has helped some economies grow rapidly by exporting manufactured goods, but the gains depend on whether income is spent on education, health and infrastructure, and on how fairly workers are treated.

## Moral, ethical and sustainability considerations

- **Working conditions.** A UK retailer that buys clothes from a supplier abroad may be linked to low pay, unsafe buildings or child labour further down its supply chain. Is cheap clothing worth that cost, and who is responsible for checking?
- **Exploitation of weak regulation.** Production may move to countries with weaker employment or environmental laws, so firms cut costs at the expense of workers or the environment.
- **Carbon emissions.** Shipping and flying goods around the world adds to greenhouse gas emissions, a cost that is not included in the price.
- **Resource use.** Cheap goods encourage more consumption of finite resources and more waste.
- **Fair trade.** Schemes that guarantee producers in less developed countries a minimum price and better conditions are one response; consumers pay a little more for an ethical guarantee.

These are **normative** issues: economics can show who gains and who loses, but whether the trade-off is acceptable is a value judgement. Saying so in an evaluation shows understanding.

### Worked example: should a UK firm move production abroad? (illustrative)

A UK furniture maker produces chairs at a cost of £60 each: labour £35, materials £20, other costs £5. In another country labour would cost £10 a chair, materials and other costs would be the same, and shipping to the UK would add £8.

- Cost abroad = 10 + 20 + 5 + 8 = **£43** a chair.
- Saving = 60 − 43 = **£17** a chair, which is 17 ÷ 60 × 100 = **28.3%** lower.
- On 10,000 chairs a year, the saving is 17 × 10,000 = **£170,000**.

The firm gains lower costs and could cut prices for UK consumers. UK workers in its factory lose their jobs, while workers abroad gain jobs. The decision also raises ethical questions about pay and conditions in the new factory, and longer delivery times and higher transport emissions are drawbacks. A strong answer weighs all of these rather than stopping at the cost saving.

### Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: "Evaluate whether globalisation has benefited UK workers."*

> Globalisation has created jobs for UK workers in exporting industries such as financial services, because UK firms can sell to much larger markets (**benefit, with a reason**). However, it has also led manufacturers to move production to countries with lower labour costs, so workers in some UK towns have lost their jobs and may face long-term structural unemployment because their skills do not match the new jobs (**drawback, with a chain of reasoning**). Overall, the effect depends on the worker: highly skilled workers have tended to gain, while lower-skilled workers in industries facing import competition have tended to lose, so the impact depends on how far the government helps people retrain (**judgement that names the deciding factor**).

## Common mistakes

- Defining globalisation as "trade". It is wider: investment, supply chains, technology and migration are features too.
- Writing about "people" instead of naming producers, workers or consumers.
- Assuming every effect in the UK is the same in a less developed country. Job creation abroad and job losses in the UK can be two sides of one decision.
- Treating MNCs as simply "big companies". The defining feature is operating in more than one country.
- Listing ethical issues without linking them to a decision by a UK producer.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **State / Define:** a precise sentence. "A firm with operations in more than one country" defines an MNC.
- **Explain:** a point plus a developed chain of reasoning: what happens, and why it matters for the named group.
- **Calculate:** show each step, such as the cost saving per unit, then the percentage.
- **Analyse:** use the information given, for example the extract about one firm, to show causes and effects.
- **Evaluate:** both sides, then a supported judgement. Say what the outcome depends on, such as the skills of the workers, the quality of regulation abroad or the time period.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the two causes of globalisation named in the specification?** New technology and the operations of multinational companies.

**Why do UK consumers usually gain from globalisation?** Competition from low-cost producers lowers prices and widens choice.

**Who loses from globalisation in the UK?** Mainly workers and firms in industries that cannot compete with cheaper production abroad.
