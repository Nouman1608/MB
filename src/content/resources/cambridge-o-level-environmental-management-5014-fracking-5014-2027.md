Sub-topic 1.6 is the shortest in Topic 1, with only two learning outcomes, but the second one is a
"discuss" outcome, which means extended answers that weigh several points. The
[energy resources, conservation and fracking guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-energy-resources/)
gives the definition and a table of benefits and limitations. This page explains the process step by step,
links it back to rock permeability, sorts the arguments by who they affect, and shows how to turn them into
a balanced answer.

## What the syllabus asks

- **1.6.1** Define fracking as "the extraction of natural gas or petroleum from shale rock by hydraulic
  fracturing".
- **1.6.2** Discuss the benefits and limitations of fracking.

Every word of the definition carries meaning: **natural gas or petroleum** (what is extracted), **shale
rock** (where it is), and **hydraulic fracturing** (how: using fluid under pressure to crack the rock).

## Why shale needs fracturing

Shale formed from fine mud and clay compacted on the floors of calm lakes and seas (sub-topic 1.1). Some
shales also contained the remains of marine organisms, which became gas and oil, but the gas and oil are
still held within the shale itself. Shale is **impermeable**: its pore spaces are too small and too poorly
connected for gas or oil to flow through. A conventional well drilled into it would collect very little.
Fracking creates artificial cracks so the gas or oil has a path to the well.

Compare a **conventional** reserve, where gas and oil have already moved up through permeable rock and
collected beneath an impermeable layer: a simple well can let them flow out without fracturing.

## How fracking works

1. **Drilling.** A well is drilled vertically down to the shale layer, often more than a kilometre below
   the surface, far beneath the level of ground water used for drinking.
2. **Turning horizontal.** The drill is steered to run sideways along the shale layer, so a single well
   reaches a large area of rock.
3. **Casing.** The well is lined with steel pipe surrounded by cement, to seal it off from the rock layers
   it passes through, especially aquifers.
4. **Perforating.** Small holes are made in the casing along the horizontal section.
5. **Fracturing.** Fracking fluid, mostly **water**, with **sand** and a small amount of **chemicals**, is
   pumped down at very high pressure. The pressure cracks the shale.
6. **Holding the cracks open.** The sand grains wedge into the cracks so they do not close when the
   pressure is released.
7. **Flowback and production.** Some of the fluid returns to the surface as waste water. Gas or oil then
   flows through the cracks into the well and up to the surface.

Several wells are often drilled from one site, called a well pad, and each well may be fracked in stages.

## Benefits of fracking

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Access to new reserves | Gas and oil in shale could not otherwise be extracted, so a country's usable reserves increase |
| Energy security | Producing gas at home reduces dependence on imports, which can be disrupted by conflict or price rises |
| Lower energy prices | More supply can reduce the price of gas for homes and industry |
| Jobs and income | Drilling, transport and services create employment; the government gains tax income |
| Lower carbon dioxide than coal | Burning natural gas to generate electricity releases less carbon dioxide than burning coal, so switching from coal to gas reduces emissions |
| Smaller surface footprint per well | Horizontal drilling reaches a large area of shale from one site |

## Limitations of fracking

| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Water usage | Each fracture job uses large volumes of water, which can compete with farms and homes in dry areas |
| Ground water contamination | If casing or cement fails, chemicals or methane could escape into aquifers used for drinking |
| Waste water | Flowback water contains chemicals and dissolved substances from the rock, and must be treated or disposed of safely |
| Earthquakes | Injecting fluid at high pressure can trigger small earthquakes |
| Methane leaks | Methane escaping from wells and pipes is a greenhouse gas, reducing the climate benefit over coal |
| Still a fossil fuel | Burning the gas or oil releases carbon dioxide, and the resource is finite |
| Local disruption | Lorries, noise, lighting and land used for well pads affect residents and wildlife |
| Delayed renewables | Cheap gas may reduce investment in renewable energy resources |

## Who gains and who loses?

A "discuss" answer is stronger when it shows that different groups see fracking differently.

| Group | Likely view | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| National government | Often in favour | Energy security, tax income, lower imports |
| Energy company | In favour | Profit from new reserves |
| Local job seekers | In favour | Employment in drilling and transport |
| Local residents | Often against | Traffic, noise, worry about water and earthquakes |
| Farmers | Mixed | Payments for land use, but competition for water and risk of pollution |
| Environmental groups | Against | Methane leaks, carbon dioxide, delay to renewables |

## Model "discuss" answer

*Question (original):* A country imports most of its gas and generates much of its electricity from coal.
Large shale gas reserves have been found beneath farmland. Discuss the benefits and limitations of
fracking for this country. [6]

*Model answer:* Fracking would give the country access to gas trapped in impermeable shale, reducing its
dependence on imports and protecting it from price rises or interruptions in supply. Replacing coal with
gas for electricity would lower carbon dioxide emissions, because gas releases less carbon dioxide than
coal when burned. Drilling would create jobs and tax income. However, fracking uses large amounts of water,
which would compete with the farms above the reserve, and if a well's casing failed, chemicals or methane
could contaminate the ground water the farmers use. Waste water must be treated, the high-pressure fluid
could trigger small earthquakes, and lorries and well pads would disrupt rural communities. Gas is still a
finite fossil fuel, and methane leaks reduce its advantage over coal. Overall, fracking could be a useful
short-term replacement for coal and imports if it is strictly regulated and monitored, but it should not
replace investment in renewable resources.

### What makes the answer score

- It applies the points to the country described: imports, coal and farmland.
- Each point has a reason ("because", "so", "if").
- It covers both sides before concluding.
- The conclusion depends on conditions (regulation, short-term use) rather than simply saying "good" or
  "bad".

## Common mistakes

- Defining fracking as "drilling for oil" without shale or hydraulic fracturing.
- Saying fracking extracts coal, or that it works in permeable rock.
- Calling natural gas a renewable or "clean" fuel.
- Listing limitations only, as if the question were "describe the problems".
- Writing that fracking "poisons all the water". Say what could happen and how: chemicals or methane
  escaping into aquifers if a well fails.
- Ending without a judgement.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: learn the syllabus definition exactly.
- **Describe** the process (if asked as part of a longer question): drill, turn horizontal, pump fluid at
  high pressure, cracks held open by sand, gas flows to the well.
- **Explain** why shale needs fracking: impermeable, so gas cannot flow through it.
- **Discuss**: benefits and limitations applied to the context, then a conditional judgement.
