## Section A

**1.** Explain how cutting into the base of a slope for a road reduces its stability. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how leaking water pipes can trigger mass movement. **[2]**

**3.** Outline how pinning with soil nails increases the stability of a slope. **[2]**

**4.** State **one** advantage and **one** limitation of netting as a slope strategy. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why afforestation may not prevent deep-seated slides. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** human activities that can increase the stability of a slope. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A fictional slope beside a railway is 25 m high and stands at 40°. Engineers plan to regrade it to 25°.

**(a)** Using horizontal run = height / tan(angle), calculate the horizontal run of the slope before and after regrading. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the extra horizontal distance the regraded slope will occupy. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why grading reduces mass movement, and why it may not be chosen in a densely built city. **[3]**

**8.** Four strategies were trialled on separate 100 m sections of the same fictional road cutting. Before the trial, each section had 24 rockfalls a year reaching the road. Costs are in fictional units.

| Strategy | Cost per 100 m | Time to become effective | Rockfalls reaching the road per year after 2 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pinning (rock bolts) | 900 | Immediate | 3 |
| Netting | 250 | Immediate | 9 |
| Grading | 400 | Several months | 6 |
| Afforestation | 60 | 5 to 10 years | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reduction in rockfalls reaching the road on the pinned section. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, evaluate which strategy is most suitable for this road cutting. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how loading the top of a slope can lead to slope failure. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how deforestation can reduce slope stability over several years. **[5]**

**11.** With reference to a case study, explain how human activity affected the stability of a slope. **[8]**

**12.** "Engineering strategies are more effective than afforestation in reducing mass movement." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Cutting removes material that supported the slope above (support at the toe) [1]. The slope above the cut becomes steeper, so the downslope component of weight, the shear stress, increases [1]. Fresh joints and weak layers are exposed to water and weathering, reducing strength [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Water leaking into the slope raises pore water pressure, reducing friction between particles [1], and adds weight, increasing shear stress until the slope fails [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Steel bars are drilled through the unstable soil and grouted into stable material beneath [1], tying the loose layer to the stable layer and raising its shear strength [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Advantage: relatively cheap and quick to install, protecting roads from small falls [1]. Limitation: it does not stop rock detaching, and cannot hold large failures [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Most roots reach only the top metre or two of soil [1], whereas deep-seated slides fail on slip surfaces far below the root zone [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Two from: installing drainage to lower pore water pressure [1]; building retaining walls or gabions [1]. Accept terracing, planting vegetation, regrading or removing weight from the top of the slope in place of either. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Before: 25 / tan 40° = 25 / 0.839 = 29.8 m [1]; after: 25 / tan 25° = 25 / 0.466 = 53.6 m [1].

**(b)** 53.6 - 29.8 = 23.8 m [1].

**(c)** A lower angle reduces the downslope component of weight, so shear stress falls [1]. In a city, the extra land at the top or foot of the slope may be occupied by buildings or roads [1], and buying and clearing it would be costly and disruptive [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (24 - 3) / 24 x 100 [1] = 87.5% reduction [1].

**(b)** Pinning gives the largest reduction, from 24 to 3 rockfalls, and works immediately [1], but it is the most expensive at 900 units, fifteen times the cost of afforestation [1]. Netting costs less than a third as much as pinning and works immediately, but 9 rockfalls still reach the road, because nets manage falling rock rather than stopping detachment [1]. Afforestation is cheapest but has barely reduced rockfalls after 2 years (24 to 20) and takes 5 to 10 years, so it does not suit a busy road needing immediate protection [1]. Judgement: pinning is most suitable if safety is the priority and funds are available, possibly combined with netting as a cheaper backup on less active sections [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Buildings, spoil tips, fill or water tanks add weight at the top of the slope [1], increasing the downslope component of stress [1]. Loose fill or spoil has low strength and may absorb water [1], so heavy rain can saturate it and trigger a slide or flow [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Roots that bound the soil decay over a few years after the trees are cut [1], so root cohesion is gradually lost and shear strength falls [1]. Without a canopy, interception and transpiration fall, so soils stay wetter [1] and pore water pressure is higher during rain [1]. Bare soil is exposed to rainsplash and runoff, and rills and gullies can undercut the slope [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Credit located, dated detail from a real case study.

Identifies the human activities on the slope, such as cutting, loading, deforestation or drainage changes, with location [1]. Explains how one activity increased shear stress, for example by steepening or adding weight [1]. Explains how one activity reduced shear strength, for example by raising pore water pressure or removing root cohesion [1]. Links the impacts to the physical setting, such as weak rock, deep weathering or heavy rainfall [1]. Names the type of mass movement that resulted [1]. Gives dated evidence of failures and their consequences [1]. Recognises any activities that increased stability [1]. Shows how the human and physical factors combined to cause failure [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For the view: pinning and soil nailing raise strength immediately and work on steep slopes [1]. Drainage lowers pore water pressure, tackling the most common trigger [1]. Grading reduces stress and suits slides in soil and weak rock [1]. Engineering can protect deep-seated slip surfaces that roots cannot reach [1].

Against the view: afforestation is far cheaper and needs little maintenance once established [1]. It tackles causes, increasing interception, transpiration and root cohesion, and improves landscape and habitats [1]. Engineering structures need inspection and maintenance for decades, and some are visually intrusive [1].

Judgement: engineering is more effective where protection is needed quickly or failures are deep, while afforestation suits shallow soil slopes over the long term, so combining them is usually most effective [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a human activity without explaining its effect on stress or strength.
- Using the wrong trigonometric ratio in grading calculations.
- Choosing a strategy on cost alone, ignoring effectiveness and time.
- Saying netting prevents rockfalls.
- Writing a case study answer with no named place or dated evidence.
