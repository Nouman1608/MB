## The four lenses

| Lens | Ask | Note down |
|---|---|---|
| Content | Who, where, what happens? | A one-sentence summary |
| Theme | What bigger idea? | Your argument |
| Structure | How is it shaped and how does it develop? | Stanza pattern, line lengths, the turn, the ending |
| Use of language | Which details stand out? | Five or six short quotations with effects |

## Structure prompts

- How many stanzas, and are they regular? Does a pattern break?
- Where is the turn, and what changes there: time, tone, speaker, place?
- Which lines are longest or shortest, and why?
- Where does enjambment carry the reader on, and where does an end-stopped line or caesura force a
  pause?
- How does the poem end, and does the ending echo the opening?
- Is there repetition or parallel structure, and what does it emphasise?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Speaker | The voice of the poem, not necessarily the poet |
| Turn (volta) | A shift in thought, mood or direction |
| Enjambment | A sentence running on over a line break |
| Caesura | A pause within a line |
| End-stopped line | A line that ends with a pause |
| Parallelism | Repeated grammatical structure, such as "All night... / All night..." |
| Imagery | Language that creates a picture or sensation |
| Personification | Giving human qualities to something non-human |
| Tone | The attitude of the voice: tender, bitter, wry, anxious |
| Ambiguity | A word or line open to more than one reading |

## Tentative phrasing that earns credit

- "This could suggest..." / "Perhaps the speaker..."
- "One reading is...; another is..."
- "The reader might feel..."
- "By the final stanza, this shifts to..."

Always pair a tentative idea with a quotation.

## Handling difficulty

- Start from the clear lines; return to the hard ones.
- Guess unknown words from surrounding words, or write about sound and image instead.
- In older poems, put the sentence into normal order, then ask why the poet changed it.
- If the speaker is unclear, offer two possibilities.
- For an open ending, give two readings and choose one.

## Paragraph pattern

Point about the feeling or idea → short quotation → method named precisely → effect on meaning →
link to the poem's development or ending.

## Worked mini-example

The lines below are original, written for these notes.

> *My grandmother's hands knew bread before they knew me;*
> *now they hold my face as if checking it has risen.*

**Four-lens notes:** *content*: a grandmother touching the speaker's face; *theme*: love expressed
through habit and care; *structure*: the semicolon splits past and present; *language*: the bread
metaphor running through both lines.

**Model paragraph:** The poet presents the grandmother's love through the language of her daily
work. Her hands "knew bread before they knew me", so the speaker is placed after her lifetime of
labour, perhaps with gentle humour. The semicolon then turns the poem from past to present, and the
simile "as if checking it has risen" treats the speaker like a loaf she has made, suggesting pride
and a habit of care so deep that tenderness and baking have become the same gesture.

## Exam traps

- Leaving less than 45 minutes because Section A overran.
- No comment on structure.
- One-word feelings ("sad") instead of developed ideas ("resigned but protective").
- Inventing biography for the poet.
- Line-by-line retelling instead of an argument.
- Never reaching the final stanza.

## Self-test

1. Which section of Paper 2b assesses unseen poetry?
2. Name the four features the specification lists.
3. How many marks is the unseen question worth?
4. Why does the unseen question need careful time management on Route B?
5. What is a turn in a poem?
6. What is the difference between enjambment and caesura?
7. What is parallelism? Give a made-up example.
8. Why should you always comment on the ending?
9. Give two tentative phrases.
10. What should you do with a line you do not understand?
11. Which lens supplies your argument?
12. How can the "compare" skill named in the specification help with a single poem?

### Answers

1. Section B.
2. Content, theme, structure, use of language.
3. 30.
4. It comes after the anthology question, so if Section A overruns, the unseen answer is rushed.
5. A shift in thought, mood or direction.
6. Enjambment runs a sentence over a line break; caesura is a pause in the middle of a line.
7. Repeating a grammatical pattern for emphasis, for example "We waited for the rain. / We waited for
   the call."
8. Endings often resolve or change the meaning and show the poem's final attitude.
9. Any two: "this could suggest", "perhaps", "one reading is", "the reader might feel".
10. Offer a tentative reading supported by nearby words, or comment on its sound or image.
11. Theme.
12. You can compare parts of the poem, such as the opening and ending, or two contrasting images.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many quotations should I use?**
Five or six short, well-chosen quotations, analysed closely, usually give more credit than many
long ones.

**Should I write an introduction?**
A one- or two-sentence opening that states your interpretation is enough.
