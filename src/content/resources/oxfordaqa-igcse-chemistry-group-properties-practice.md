Written for OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202), this set follows the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. It covers section 3.7.1 Group properties (statements a to f); no required practical is attached to this section. The qualification is untiered, so every question suits every candidate. Use relative atomic masses Li = 7, K = 39, Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5 and Br = 80, and a molar gas volume of 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure.

## Questions

**1.** This question is about Group 1.

**(a)** Explain why the Group 1 elements are called the alkali metals. **[1]**
**(b)** State the charge on the ion that every Group 1 element forms. **[1]**

**2.** Lithium burns in oxygen to form lithium oxide.

**(a)** Write the formula of lithium oxide. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the appearance of lithium oxide, and of the solution formed when a lithium compound such as lithium chloride dissolves in water. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe what happens to the electrons when a lithium atom forms a lithium ion. **[2]**

**3.** A teacher drops a small piece of sodium onto water in a trough, then repeats the demonstration with potassium.

**(a)** Give two observations for sodium. **[2]**
**(b)** Give one observation that is seen with potassium but not with sodium. **[1]**
**(c)** Write a balanced equation for potassium reacting with water. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe a test to show the gas produced is hydrogen. **[1]**

**4.** Halogens react with metals to form metal halides.

**(a)** Write the formulae of: (i) iron(II) chloride, (ii) barium bromide, (iii) sodium iodide. **[3]**
**(b)** Write a balanced equation for aluminium reacting with bromine. **[2]**

**5.** Use these values for four halogens.

| Halogen | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Fluorine | −220 | −188 |
| Chlorine | −101 | −34 |
| Bromine | −7 | 59 |
| Iodine | 114 | 184 |

**(a)** Give the state of bromine at 70 °C and of iodine at 25 °C. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the halogen that is a liquid at −50 °C. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the trend in melting point down Group 7. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain this trend in terms of the molecules. **[2]**

**6.** A student has three colourless solutions, P, Q and R. One is potassium chloride, one is potassium bromide and one is potassium iodide. She adds chlorine water to a sample of each, then bromine water to fresh samples.

- P: no change with chlorine water; no reaction with bromine water.
- Q: turns brown with chlorine water and with bromine water.
- R: turns yellow-orange with chlorine water; no reaction with bromine water.

**(a)** Identify P, Q and R. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why P does not react with bromine water. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the ionic equation for the reaction of Q with bromine water. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why caesium reacts more vigorously with water than sodium does. **[4]**

**8.** Fluorine is at the top of Group 7 and iodine is below bromine.

**(a)** Predict whether iodine reacts with sodium fluoride solution. Give a reason. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of electrons and energy levels, why fluorine is more reactive than iodine. **[4]**

**9.** 0.78 g of potassium reacts completely with water. The solution is made up to 250 cm³ with water.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of potassium used. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of hydrogen produced at room temperature and pressure. Give your answer in cm³. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the concentration of potassium hydroxide in the final solution, in mol/dm³. **[2]**
**(d)** State the colour of universal indicator in this solution. **[1]**

**10.** Chlorine gas is bubbled into a solution containing 0.92 g of magnesium bromide, MgBr₂.

**(a)** Explain why a reaction takes place. **[1]**
**(b)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** State what you would see. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the maximum mass of bromine that can form. **[3]**
**(e)** Calculate the minimum volume of chlorine needed at room temperature and pressure, in cm³. **[1]**

**11.** 0.70 g of lithium reacts completely with chlorine.

**(a)** Describe how the ions in lithium chloride form from lithium and chlorine atoms. Give the charge on each ion. **[3]**
**(b)** Give the balanced symbol equation for this reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of lithium chloride formed. **[2]**
**(d)** The lithium chloride is dissolved in water. Describe the solution. **[1]**

**12.** Going down Group 1 the elements become more reactive, but going down Group 7 they become less reactive. Explain both trends, and why the same change in atomic structure leads to opposite trends. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Their hydroxides dissolve in water to give alkaline solutions [1].
**(b)** +1 (a 1+ ion) [1].
*Examiner insight:* "They are reactive metals" does not answer (a); the name comes from the alkaline solutions formed by their hydroxides.

**2. (a)** Li₂O [1]
**(b)** White solid [1]; colourless solution [1].
**(c)** The atom loses its one outer electron [1], giving an ion with a 1+ charge (Li⁺) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say the electron is lost or transferred; "lithium shares an electron" describes covalent bonding and scores nothing here.

**3. (a)** Any two: floats; melts into a ball; moves around on the surface; fizzes or bubbles; gets smaller and disappears [1] [1].
**(b)** The hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame (or reacts more violently) [1].
**(c)** All four formulae right [1]; balancing right: **2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂** [1].
**(d)** Hold a lighted splint near the gas; it gives a pop [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Bubbles of hydrogen" is a conclusion, not an observation; describe what you see, such as fizzing.

**4. (a)** (i) **FeCl₂** [1] (ii) **BaBr₂** [1] (iii) **NaI** [1]
**(b)** Al, Br₂ and AlBr₃ written correctly [1]; numbers 2, 3 and 2 in place: **2Al + 3Br₂ → 2AlBr₃** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check every halide is 1−; formulae such as BaBr or AlBr show the charge has not been balanced.

**5. (a)** Bromine at 70 °C: gas [1]. Iodine at 25 °C: solid [1].
**(b)** **Chlorine** (−50 °C lies between its melting and boiling points) [1].
**(c)** Melting point increases down the group [1].
**(d)** The molecules get larger down the group [1], so the forces between molecules are stronger and need more energy to overcome [1].
*Examiner insight:* For (a), compare the temperature with both the melting point and the boiling point; 70 °C is above bromine's boiling point, so it is a gas, not a liquid.

**6. (a)** P: **potassium chloride** [1]. Q: **potassium iodide** [1]. R: **potassium bromide** [1].
**(b)** Chlorine sits above bromine in Group 7 and is more reactive, so bromine cannot push chloride out of the salt [1].
**(c)** Correct species Br₂ + I⁻ → Br⁻ + I₂ [1]; balanced **Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Start from the solution that reacts with both reagents; it must contain the least reactive halide, which is iodide.

**7.** Caesium has more occupied energy levels than sodium, so its outer electron is in a higher energy level [1]. That electron sits further out, with more inner electrons screening it from the nuclear charge [1]. So the nucleus pulls on it less strongly [1]. So caesium loses its outer electron more easily and reacts more vigorously [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give every step of the chain; leaving out "weaker attraction" breaks the link between size and reactivity.

**8. (a)** No, because iodine is less reactive than fluorine [1].
**(b)** Halogens react by gaining one electron [1]. In fluorine the outer energy level is closer to the nucleus (lower energy level) [1] and less shielded [1], so the incoming electron is attracted more strongly and gained more easily [1].
*Examiner insight:* An answer about losing electrons applies to metals and gains no credit for a halogen explanation.

**9. (a)** 0.78 ÷ 39 = **0.020 mol** [1]
**(b)** Moles of H₂ = 0.020 ÷ 2 = 0.010 mol [1]; volume = 0.010 × 24 000 = **240 cm³** [1].
**(c)** Moles of KOH = 0.020 mol; 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³ [1]; 0.020 ÷ 0.250 = **0.080 mol/dm³** [1].
**(d)** Blue or purple [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert 250 cm³ to 0.250 dm³ before dividing; dividing by 250 gives an answer one thousand times too small.

**10. (a)** Chlorine is more reactive than bromine, so it displaces bromine from its salt [1].
**(b)** Correct reactant formulae Cl₂ and MgBr₂ [1]; correct product formulae MgCl₂ and Br₂, giving **Cl₂ + MgBr₂ → MgCl₂ + Br₂** (already balanced) [1].
**(c)** The colourless solution turns yellow-orange [1].
**(d)** Mr MgBr₂ = 24 + (2 × 80) = 184 [1]; moles = 0.92 ÷ 184 = 0.0050 mol, and 1 mol MgBr₂ gives 1 mol Br₂ [1]; mass = 0.0050 × 160 = **0.80 g** [1].
**(e)** 0.0050 × 24 000 = **120 cm³** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use Mr = 160 for Br₂, not 80; bromine is formed as diatomic molecules, and that slip halves the answer.

**11. (a)** Each lithium atom loses one electron [1] and each chlorine atom gains one electron [1], forming Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions [1].
**(b)** **2Li + Cl₂ → 2LiCl** [1]
**(c)** Moles Li = 0.70 ÷ 7 = 0.10 mol, so 0.10 mol LiCl [1]; mass = 0.10 × 42.5 = **4.25 g** [1].
**(d)** Colourless [1].
*Examiner insight:* For (d) do not describe the solution as alkaline; lithium chloride is a salt, and only the hydroxide gives an alkaline solution.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each, up to 6:
- Down both groups the outer electrons are in higher energy levels (more shells) [1].
- They are further from the nucleus and more shielded, so attraction to the nucleus is weaker [1].
- Group 1 atoms react by losing their one outer electron [1].
- Weaker attraction means this electron is lost more easily, so Group 1 reactivity increases [1].
- Group 7 atoms react by gaining one electron into the outer energy level [1].
- Weaker attraction means an electron is gained less easily, so Group 7 reactivity decreases; the trends are opposite because one group loses and the other gains electrons [1].
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer treats the two groups separately and states whether each loses or gains electrons; one merged sentence rarely covers six distinct points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing Cl, Br or I as single atoms in equations, which makes balancing impossible.
- Calling the gas from Group 1 and water oxygen, or saying the metal dissolves.
- Explaining Group 7 reactivity in terms of losing electrons.
- Saying "bigger atoms" without distance, shielding and weaker attraction.
- Using an atomic mass for a diatomic element (Br = 80 instead of Br₂ = 160).
- Forgetting the cm³ to dm³ conversion in concentration and gas-volume answers.
- Writing an equation for a displacement that does not happen.
- Describing a Group 1 salt solution as alkaline; only the hydroxides are.
