**This guide covers the structural aspects AQA lists for Option 1B Aspects of Comedy:** "the
structural patterning of the text as it moves from disorder to order, incorporating rule and misrule,
how competition between characters is set up and resolved, how opposites are contrasted and
reconciled, leading to comic resolutions", together with "the use of complex plotting and
sub-plots" and "the sense that all will end well and that fortune smiles". The
[Aspects of Comedy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-1b-aspects-of-comedy-7717/)
introduces the option and the debate about who is left out of the happy ending; this page explains
how comic structure is built, so that you can analyse it as precisely as language.

## Disorder to order: the reverse of tragedy

Tragedy, in AQA's wording, moves "from order to disorder". Comedy reverses the direction: it usually
begins with an obstacle or a broken order (a law, a parent, a disguise, a misunderstanding) and ends
with a new order, often marked by marriage. That reversal is the reason an audience can laugh at
discomfort: the structure promises that the disorder is temporary. AQA's phrase "the sense that all
will end well" names this promise. Analysing *when* the audience first senses it, and how the writer
keeps it alive through moments of danger, is an effective way to write about structure.

## Rule and misrule

"Misrule" refers to a festive tradition in which ordinary authority is turned upside down for a
time; a Lord of Misrule presided over Christmas revels in some households and colleges. Comedy borrows
the pattern: a period of licence in which the usual rules are suspended, followed by a return to
rule. The critic C. L. Barber described Shakespeare's festive comedies as moving "through release to
clarification", which is a useful summary of the pattern.

- **Twelfth Night.** The title refers to the last night of the Christmas season, and the play sets
  Sir Toby's late-night revelry against Malvolio's insistence on order. Sir Toby's question to the
  steward about whether, because he is virtuous, there shall be "no more cakes and ale" stages the
  conflict between festivity and rule in one line. The audience is invited to enjoy misrule, but the
  play also asks what happens when release turns into cruelty.
- **She Stoops to Conquer.** Goldsmith's subtitle, "The Mistakes of a Night", announces a structure
  of temporary confusion. Tony Lumpkin's trick turns a gentleman's house into an "inn" for one night,
  so the rules of hospitality and rank are suspended: Marlow treats his host as a landlord. Morning
  brings discovery and the restoration of proper relations.
- **The Importance of Being Earnest.** Here misrule is a habit rather than a festival. "Bunburying",
  inventing a person or a self in order to escape obligations, lets Algernon and Jack live double
  lives. Lady Bracknell embodies rule, and her interrogation of Jack's origins sets the obstacle the
  comedy must overcome.

## Competition set up and resolved

AQA asks "how competition between characters is set up and resolved". Comic plots are full of
rivalries: suitors, wits, generations. Look for the moment each competition is announced and the
device that ends it.

- In *The Taming of the Shrew*, the play ends with a literal contest: the husbands wager on whose wife
  will come when called. The structure turns the marriage plot into a competition with a winner,
  which is why the final scene is so often debated.
- In *Emma*, Emma's matchmaking competes with the plans of others and with secrets she cannot see.
  The hidden engagement of Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax is a concealed plot running under the
  visible one; its revelation re-orders everything Emma thought she understood.
- In "The Nun's Priest's Tale", the contest is between the fox and the cockerel. Each wins by
  flattery: the fox persuades Chauntecleer to close his eyes and sing, and Chauntecleer later
  persuades the fox to open his mouth and boast. The structure is symmetrical, a trick answered by
  a trick.

## Opposites contrasted and reconciled

Comedies often pair opposites: town and country, youth and age, reason and passion, the witty and
the dull. Resolution brings them together, sometimes literally in marriage.

- *The Importance of Being Earnest* moves from Algernon's flat in town (Act 1) to the country house
  (Acts 2 and 3). The two settings stand for two kinds of pretence, and the final act gathers every
  character in one place for the revelation.
- *Twelfth Night* contrasts Orsino's self-absorbed longing and Olivia's prolonged mourning with
  Viola's active, patient love. The arrival of Sebastian, Viola's twin, allows the opposites to be
  reconciled: one figure has become two, so both Orsino and Olivia can marry.
- *Small Island* alternates sections set in 1948 with sections set "Before", and gives the narrative
  to four voices: Hortense, Gilbert, Queenie and Bernard. Opposites of nation, class and race are
  contrasted throughout; the ending reconciles some of them through an act of care, when Queenie
  gives her baby to Hortense and Gilbert, while leaving the wider prejudice in place.

## Complex plotting and sub-plots

AQA lists "the use of complex plotting and sub-plots". In comedy, complexity is often the joke:
the audience enjoys the writer's control as confusions multiply.

- **Parallel lovers.** *She Stoops to Conquer* runs the Marlow and Kate plot beside the Hastings and
  Constance plot, which turns on Mrs Hardcastle's jewels. Tony's ride, in which he drives his mother
  round in circles near her own house while she believes she is far from home, is sub-plot as farce.
- **Disguised suitors.** The Bianca plot in *The Taming of the Shrew* multiplies disguises among her
  suitors, so the sub-plot becomes a comedy of deception beside the main plot's comedy of
  confrontation.
- **The revelation that unties everything.** In *Earnest*, the handbag left at a railway station and
  Miss Prism's confession resolve the plot at a stroke; Jack discovers his name really is Ernest.
  Wilde's resolution is so neat that it mocks the conventions of plotting even as it uses them.

## Worked example: a structural paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *Twelfth Night*, misrule is more attractive than order." Discuss.

> Shakespeare structures the play so that the audience's sympathy for misrule rises and then falls.
> The late-night revel in Act 2 Scene 3 breaks out inside Olivia's house of mourning, and Orsino's
> languid music scene follows immediately, so the noise and song of Sir Toby, Sir Andrew and Feste
> come as release between two kinds of melancholy, and Malvolio's entrance to silence them makes
> order look joyless. Sir Toby's challenge about "cakes and
> ale" turns the scene into a contest between festivity and puritan restraint, and the audience is
> invited to take the revellers' side. Yet the structure does not leave the matter there. The trick
> that grows out of this scene leads to Malvolio's confinement in darkness, and by the final act the
> festive characters are bruised, quarrelling or quietly paired off. Misrule is attractive while it
> releases the household from melancholy, but the play's movement towards the final scene suggests
> that release needs an end, which is why the comedy closes with a song about the rain that "raineth
> every day" rather than a feast.

**Why it works.** It analyses the placement of scenes (AO2), sets the debate in the festive context
(AO3), weighs both sides of the view (AO5) and keeps the focus on how structure shapes the
audience's response.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the plot's confusions** instead of explaining how they are organised.
- **Assuming the resolution is total.** AQA's wording invites you to test whether every opposite is
  reconciled.
- **Treating sub-plots as filler.** Ask what each adds: contrast, farce, a second view of love.
- **Ignoring the promise of a happy ending.** Comic suspense depends on the audience trusting that
  "all will end well"; analyse how that trust is built.

## Exam technique

In Section A, place the printed passage in the comic structure first: is it a moment of misrule, a
point of maximum confusion, or the start of discovery? In Section B and Section C, plan one paragraph
each on the opening disorder, the period of misrule or competition, and the resolution, then weigh
what the ending leaves unresolved.
