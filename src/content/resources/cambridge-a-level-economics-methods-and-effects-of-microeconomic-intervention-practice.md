## Questions

**1.** Define a specific indirect tax and explain what is meant by the incidence of a tax. **[3]**

**2.** In a market for bicycle helmets, demand is Qd = 240 − 8P and supply is Qs = −80 + 24P, where P is in cu. The government imposes a specific tax of 4 cu per helmet.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium price and quantity with no tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new price paid by consumers and the new quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the burden of the tax on consumers and on producers. **[2]**
**(d)** Using elasticity values at the original equilibrium, explain why consumers bear the larger share. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how a specific tax could correct the over-consumption of a good that causes a negative externality. **[4]**

**4.** In a market for evening courses, demand is Qd = 300 − 10P and supply is Qs = −100 + 10P. The government gives producers a subsidy of 6 cu per place.

**(a)** Calculate the original equilibrium price. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the new price paid by students and the new quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the price producers receive and the total cost to the government. **[2]**
**(d)** State which group gains more and why. **[1]**

**5.** Explain two disadvantages of using a subsidy to increase consumption of a merit good. **[4]**

**6.** In a market for flats, demand is Qd = 500 − 25P and supply is Qs = −100 + 25P. The government sets a maximum rent of 8 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium rent and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the size of the shortage. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the rent tenants would be willing to pay for the number of flats actually available. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one consequence of the gap between your answers to (c) and the maximum rent. **[2]**

**7.** In a market for rice, demand is Qd = 900 − 30P and supply is Qs = −100 + 20P. The government sets a minimum price of 26 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the quantity demanded, quantity supplied and the surplus at 26 cu. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost of the government buying the whole surplus at 26 cu. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the change in consumer spending, assuming consumers buy only what they demand. **[2]**
**(e)** State one drawback of this policy. **[1]**

**8.** In a market for barley, demand is Qd = 1,500 − 5P. Supply is fixed within each year at 750 in a poor harvest, 900 in a normal harvest and 1,050 in a good harvest. A buffer stock agency sets a floor price of 105 cu and a ceiling price of 135 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the free-market price in each type of harvest. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how much the agency buys, and how much it spends, in a good harvest. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how much it sells, and how much it receives, in a poor harvest. **[2]**
**(d)** The agency enters a good year, then two poor years in a row. Explain what happens to the price in the second poor year. **[2]**

**9.** A town council runs a free health clinic at a yearly cost of 600,000 cu. It treats 12,000 patients.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per patient. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the opportunity cost of this provision. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why free provision may lead to over-use. **[2]**

**10.** Out of 25,000 adults in a district, 5,500 smoke. A warning campaign costing 30,000 cu is followed by a fall to 4,500 smokers.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the number of smokers. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in the smoking rate in percentage points. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost per person who stopped. **[1]**
**(d)** State one limitation of using information alone. **[1]**

**11.** The demand for a demerit good has a price elasticity of −0.4. The government imposes a specific tax which raises the price paid by consumers by 20%. Evaluate the effectiveness of the tax in reducing consumption of the good. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A specific tax is a fixed amount per unit sold [1]. Incidence is the way the burden is shared between consumers and producers [1], which may differ from who pays the tax to the government [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition is stronger when it says "per unit" and explains why incidence can differ from the legal payer.

**2. (a)** 240 − 8P = −80 + 24P, so 32P = 320 [1]. **P = 10 cu and Q = 160** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New supply Qs = −80 + 24(P − 4). Then 240 − 8P = −176 + 24P, so P = 13 [1]. **P = 13 cu, Q = 240 − 104 = 136** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Consumers pay 3 more: 3 × 136 = **408 cu** [1]. Producers keep 9, which is 1 less: 1 × 136 = **136 cu** [1]. Total revenue 544 cu. **[2]**
**(d)** PED = −8 × 10 ÷ 160 = −0.5 and PES = 24 × 10 ÷ 160 = 1.5 [1]. Demand is less elastic than supply, so buyers are less able to cut back and bear more of the tax [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show how the supply equation changes (P − 4). Showing the working lets a slip be seen and judged fairly.

**3.** A negative externality means the cost to society exceeds the producer's private cost [1], so the free market produces too much [1]. A tax equal to the marginal external cost shifts supply upwards to the marginal social cost [1], reducing quantity towards the social optimum [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the tax shifts supply, not demand. A limitation, such as difficulty in valuing the externality, can extend the answer.

**4. (a)** 300 − 10P = −100 + 10P, so **P = 20 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New supply Qs = −100 + 10(P + 6). Then 300 − 10P = −40 + 10P, so 20P = 340 [1]. **P = 17 cu, Q = 300 − 170 = 130** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Producers receive 17 + 6 = **23 cu** [1]. Cost = 6 × 130 = **780 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Neither: each gains 3 × 130 = 390 cu, because the demand and supply slopes are equal [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Subsidy questions often ask for the price producers receive as well. Do not stop at the price consumers pay.

**5.** Two developed points. The subsidy has an opportunity cost, since the government must raise taxes or cut other spending [1], so the money could be used on something with a higher return [1]. The benefit is hard to measure, so the subsidy may be set too high or too low [1], leaving consumption above or below the best level [1]. Producers keeping part of it as profit is another valid point. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A named disadvantage with no consequence is weaker than a developed one. Show the chain from cause to effect.

**6. (a)** 500 − 25P = −100 + 25P, so **P = 12 cu, Q = 200** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** At 8: Qd = 500 − 200 = 300 and Qs = −100 + 200 = 100 [1]. **Shortage = 200** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At 100 flats, price = (500 − 100) ÷ 25 = **16 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Tenants would pay up to 16 but the cap is 8 [1], inviting an illegal market or side payments [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the consequence to the numbers, such as the gap between 8 and 16.

**7. (a)** 900 − 30P = −100 + 20P, so **P = 20 cu, Q = 300** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** At 26: Qd = 900 − 780 = 120 and Qs = −100 + 520 = 420 [1]. **Surplus = 300** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 300 × 26 = **7,800 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Before: 20 × 300 = 6,000 cu. After: 26 × 120 = 3,120 cu [1]. Change = **−2,880 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** Any one: cost to taxpayers, storage waste, over-allocation to rice, or higher prices [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Consumer spending at the minimum price uses the quantity demanded, not the quantity supplied.

**8. (a)** Poor: (1,500 − 750) ÷ 5 = **150**. Normal: (1,500 − 900) ÷ 5 = **120** [1]. Good: (1,500 − 1,050) ÷ 5 = **90** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 105, Qd = 1,500 − 525 = 975 [1]. Buys 1,050 − 975 = **75**, spending 75 × 105 = **7,875 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At 135, Qd = 1,500 − 675 = 825 [1]. Sells 825 − 750 = **75**, receiving 75 × 135 = **10,125 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Stock after the good year is 75. The first poor year uses all of it [1]. In the second poor year there is none left, so the price rises to 150 cu, which is 15 above the ceiling [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the price result to the exhausted stock.

**9. (a)** 600,000 ÷ 12,000 = **50 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Opportunity cost is the next best use of the 600,000 cu [1], such as spending it on schools or roads, which would then not be provided [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At zero price people use it until their own marginal benefit is zero [1], which may exceed the level where marginal social benefit equals marginal cost, causing waste or queues [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name a specific alternative use in an opportunity cost answer.

**10. (a)** (4,500 − 5,500) ÷ 5,500 × 100 [1] = **−18.2%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 5,500 ÷ 25,000 = 22%. 4,500 ÷ 25,000 = 18%. Change = **−4 percentage points** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 30,000 ÷ 1,000 = **30 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Any one: slow effect, addiction overriding the message, the message being ignored, or other causes of the fall [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Label percentage change and percentage points separately, since they are different measures.

**11.** Calculation: a 20% rise in price with PED of −0.4 gives a fall in quantity of 0.4 × 20 = 8% [1]. Demand is inelastic, so buyers cut quantity proportionally less than the price increase [1]. The tax therefore only dents consumption [1], though it raises revenue because most buyers stay [1]. That revenue could fund treatment or information campaigns [1]. The tax is regressive if low-income households spend a larger share on the good [1]. Some buyers may turn to illegal supply [1]. Demand may become more elastic over time [1]. Information or regulation could add to the tax [1]. Judgement: limited effect on quantity but useful revenue, so a larger effect needs a bigger rise or other policies [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Do the arithmetic first, then use it as evidence. End with a judgement on effectiveness and the reason for it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting demand instead of supply when drawing or describing a tax or subsidy.
- Reporting the new price but not splitting it into consumer and producer shares.
- Using the wrong base for percentage changes.
- Confusing a shortage with a surplus, or a ceiling with a floor.
- Describing an effect for a price control set on the wrong side of equilibrium.
- Forgetting to multiply by quantity when asked for revenue, cost or burden.
- Listing evaluation points without a judgement.
