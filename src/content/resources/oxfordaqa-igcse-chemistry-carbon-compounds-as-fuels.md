## 3.10.1.1: crude oil and fractional distillation

Crude oil is a **mixture** of an enormous number of compounds, not chemically joined, so a physical method can separate them.

Most of those compounds are **hydrocarbons**, whose molecules contain hydrogen and carbon atoms **only**. A compound containing any other element, such as oxygen, is not a hydrocarbon.

### How continuous fractional distillation works

The aim is to split the oil into **fractions**. Each fraction holds molecules with a similar number of carbon atoms, so they have similar boiling points.

1. Crude oil is heated strongly so that most of it vaporises.
2. The vapour passes into the base of a tall **fractionating column**.
3. The column is hottest at the bottom and gets cooler towards the top. This is a temperature gradient.
4. The vapours rise. As each substance reaches a level where the temperature is below its boiling point, it **condenses** to a liquid.
5. Trays at different heights collect the liquids, which are piped out as separate fractions.
6. Large molecules (high boiling points) condense low down or leave at the base as a residue. Small molecules (low boiling points) leave the top as gases.
7. Oil goes in and fractions come out all the time, so the process is **continuous**.

The specification says you do not need the names of particular fractions or fuels. Concentrate on the process and the link between molecule size and boiling point.

## 3.10.1.2 a and b: alkanes

Most hydrocarbons in crude oil are **alkanes**. Alkanes are **saturated**, meaning they contain only single carbon–carbon covalent bonds. They form a **homologous series** with the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.

You must know three names:

| Name | Molecular formula |
|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ |
| ethane | C₂H₆ |
| propane | C₃H₈ |

Each line in a displayed formula stands for one covalent bond. Propane looks like this:

```
    H   H   H
    |   |   |
H — C — C — C — H
    |   |   |
    H   H   H
```

### Worked example 1: recognising alkanes

Which of these is an alkane: C₁₁H₂₄, C₈H₁₆, C₄H₁₀O?

- C₁₁H₂₄: n = 11, so 2n + 2 = 24. This fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, so it **is an alkane**.
- C₈H₁₆: 2n + 2 = 18, not 16. It fits CₙH₂ₙ instead, so it is not an alkane.
- C₄H₁₀O contains oxygen, so it is not a hydrocarbon at all.

## 3.10.1.2 c: properties depend on molecule size

As the number of carbon atoms in a hydrocarbon molecule increases:

| Property | Trend with larger molecules |
|---|---|
| Boiling point | **increases** |
| Viscosity (how thick and slow-flowing the liquid is) | **increases** |
| Flammability (how easily it catches fire) | **decreases** |

Larger molecules have stronger forces between them, so more energy is needed to separate them. Small molecules flow easily, ignite easily and burn with a cleaner flame, so they make good fuels. Large, viscous, hard-to-ignite molecules are less useful as fuels, which is one reason for cracking (below).

## 3.10.1.2 d and e: burning fuels

When a hydrocarbon burns, its carbon and hydrogen are **oxidised** (they combine with oxygen), and energy is released.

### Complete combustion

With plenty of oxygen, the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and the hydrogen becomes water.

### Worked example 2: balancing complete combustion of dodecane

Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, burns completely.

1. Balance carbon: 12 C gives 12CO₂.
2. Balance hydrogen: 26 H gives 13H₂O.
3. Count oxygen atoms on the right: 24 + 13 = 37, which needs 18½O₂.
4. Double everything to remove the half: **2C₁₂H₂₆ + 37O₂ → 24CO₂ + 26H₂O**

Check: 24 C, 52 H and 74 O on each side.

### Incomplete combustion

With too little oxygen, some carbon forms **carbon monoxide** (a toxic gas) or **soot** (carbon particles). Water still forms. For methane:

- 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O
- CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O

Unburnt fuel can also escape as tiny particles.

### Other products and their effects

Many fuels, including coal, contain some **sulfur**. When the fuel burns, the sulfur is oxidised: S + O₂ → SO₂. At the high temperatures inside engines and furnaces, nitrogen and oxygen from the air react to make **oxides of nitrogen**, even though the fuel contains no nitrogen.

| Product | Where it comes from | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | carbon in the fuel, complete combustion | an increase may result in climate change |
| Water vapour | hydrogen in the fuel | — |
| Carbon monoxide | carbon, incomplete combustion | toxic gas |
| Sulfur dioxide | sulfur impurity in the fuel | acid rain |
| Oxides of nitrogen | nitrogen in air, at high temperature | acid rain |
| Particulates (soot, unburnt fuel) | incomplete combustion | global dimming |

To predict products, list the elements in the fuel, then ask whether oxygen was plentiful.

## 3.10.1.3: cracking and alkenes

Fractional distillation gives more large hydrocarbons than are needed and fewer of the smaller, more useful ones. **Cracking** breaks large molecules into smaller ones.

1. The hydrocarbons are heated so they **vaporise**.
2. The vapour is then either passed over a **hot catalyst**, or mixed with **steam** and then heated very strongly.
3. **Thermal decomposition** reactions break the molecules apart.

The products include smaller **alkanes**, some of which are useful as fuels, and **alkenes**.

### Alkenes

Alkenes are **unsaturated**: some carbon–carbon bonds are **double** covalent bonds. Their general formula is **CₙH₂ₙ**. You need two names: **ethene**, C₂H₄, and **propene**, C₃H₆. In a displayed formula a double line is a double bond:

```
H       H
 \     /
  C = C
 /     \
H       H
```

That is ethene. Propene has a C=C bond and a CH₃ group on one end.

### Worked example 3: balancing a cracking equation

Nonadecane, C₁₉H₄₀, is cracked into C₁₀H₂₂, propene and one other hydrocarbon. Find the other product.

- Carbon: 19 − 10 − 3 = 6
- Hydrogen: 40 − 22 − 6 = 12
- The product is **C₆H₁₂**, which fits CₙH₂ₙ, so it is an alkene.

Equation: C₁₉H₄₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + C₃H₆ + C₆H₁₂

A single product can be found the same way: C₂₃H₄₈ → C₁₅H₃₂ + **C₈H₁₆**.

### Required practical: testing for a double bond

Alkenes react with **bromine water**, turning it from **orange to colourless**. Alkanes do not react with it, so the orange colour stays.

1. Put a small volume of the unknown liquid hydrocarbon in a test tube. For a gas, bubble it through the bromine water instead.
2. Add a few drops of bromine water.
3. Stopper the tube and shake.
4. Record the colour. Colourless means a C=C double bond is present; orange means no double bond.

Run a known alkane alongside as a comparison. For the risk assessment: bromine water is harmful and irritates skin and eyes, so wear eye protection and gloves and use small volumes; hydrocarbons are flammable, so keep them away from flames; work in a well-ventilated room. The specification's skills table lists risk assessment, collecting data, analysing data and making conclusions for this practical.

## 3.10.1.2 f: two ways to make ethanol

**Hydration of ethene.** Ethene reacts with steam:

C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH

**Phosphoric acid** is the catalyst. Typical conditions are about 300 °C and 60 to 70 atmospheres. This is a **continuous** process, which makes it efficient, but ethene comes from crude oil, a **non-renewable** resource that may run out.

**Fermentation of sugar.** Yeast converts sugar to ethanol and carbon dioxide:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

**Enzymes in yeast** are the natural catalysts. This is a **batch** process. Sugar comes from plants, so it is **renewable**.

| Feature | Hydration of ethene | Fermentation of sugar |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material | ethene, from crude oil (non-renewable) | sugar, from plants (renewable) |
| Type of process | continuous | batch |
| Labour | few workers once running | more labour to empty, clean and refill vessels |
| Rate of reaction | fast | slow |
| Conditions | high temperature and pressure, phosphoric acid catalyst | 20 °C to 35 °C, yeast |
| Purity of product | pure ethanol | dilute solution of ethanol in water |
| Energy requirements | high, to keep temperature and pressure up | low for the reaction; extra energy to separate the ethanol |

### Worked example 4: fermentation masses

What maximum masses of ethanol and carbon dioxide form from 1.26 kg of glucose? (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16)

1. Mr of C₆H₁₂O₆ = 180, so moles of glucose = 1260 ÷ 180 = 7.0 mol.
2. The ratio is 1 : 2 : 2, so 14 mol of ethanol and 14 mol of CO₂ form.
3. Ethanol: 14 × 46 = **644 g**. Carbon dioxide: 14 × 44 = **616 g**.

Check: 644 + 616 = 1260 g, so mass is conserved.

Hydration works the same way. One mole of ethene (Mr 28) gives one mole of ethanol (Mr 46), so 56 tonnes of ethene gives 56 × 46 ÷ 28 = **92 tonnes** of ethanol. No second product forms, so every atom ends up in the ethanol.

## 3.10.1.2 g: biofuels

**Biofuels**, including **biodiesel** and **ethanol**, are made from plant material. They are possible alternatives to hydrocarbon fuels. Ethanol for use as a biofuel comes from a dilute solution made by fermenting plant materials at 20 °C to 35 °C.

| Factor | Benefit | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Renewable resources | crops can be regrown each season | supply depends on harvests and weather |
| Land use | can use land unsuited to food crops | land for fuel crops may replace food crops or forest |
| Carbon footprint | plants absorb CO₂ as they grow, offsetting some of the CO₂ released on burning | farming, fertiliser, transport and distillation all use energy, so the footprint is not zero |

A balanced judgement uses both columns.

## Common errors

- Calling crude oil a compound. It is a mixture.
- Saying fractions are separated by "different melting points". Fractional distillation uses boiling points.
- Writing that the top of the column is hottest.
- Leaving water out of incomplete combustion products.
- Writing "clear" or "discoloured" instead of colourless for bromine water.
- Calling fermentation continuous or hydration a batch process.
- Calling biofuels carbon neutral with no qualification.
