This guide goes deeper on section 4.6 (Location decisions) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Its content statement, 4.6.1 Main factors which influence location decisions, lists: "factors which influence the location decisions of a manufacturing business"; "factors which influence the location decisions of a service business"; "factors that a business could consider when deciding which country to locate its operations in"; and "recommend and justify an appropriate location for a business in a given situation". The broader [quality and location guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-quality-of-goods-and-services-and-location-decisions/) lists the factors and compares site costs. This page explains *why* the deciding factor changes from one business to another, works through a transport-cost example and a revenue-against-rent example, and shows how to build a recommendation. Revise with the [location decisions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-location-decisions-revision-notes/) and practise with the [location decisions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-location-decisions-practice/).

All businesses, places and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

| Point in 4.6.1 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing location factors | Explain which factors matter most for a given manufacturer and why |

| Recommend and justify | Choose a location from the options given and defend the choice with the data |

## Find the deciding factor first

Every business considers several factors, but usually one or two dominate. Asking "what would make this business fail if it got the location wrong?" finds them.

| Business | Deciding factor | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cement works | Raw materials | Limestone is heavy and much more of it goes in than cement comes out, so moving it is expensive |
| Bakery making fresh bread for shops | Market | Bread goes stale quickly and must reach shops early each morning |
| Steel plant | Raw materials, power and transport links | Huge quantities of heavy inputs and outputs |
| Clothing shop | Customers and footfall | Sales depend on people passing and coming in |
| Software support centre | Skilled labour and communications | The service travels by phone and internet, so customers can be far away |
| Petrol station | Customers and access | Must be on a busy road, easy to drive into |

## Manufacturing businesses

The main factors for a manufacturer are the cost and availability of land, labour (skills and wage rates), nearness to raw materials, nearness to the market, transport links, power and water supplies, government grants or restrictions, and room to expand.

### Worked example: Ardwick Cement

Ardwick Cement will make 100,000 tonnes of cement a year. Each tonne of cement needs 1.5 tonnes of limestone, so it needs 150,000 tonnes of limestone a year. The quarry is 80 km from the city where the cement is sold. Transport costs $0.10 per tonne per km.

**Site Q: next to the quarry.** Limestone travels almost no distance. Cement travels 80 km to the city.

```
Cement transport = 100,000 x 80 x 0.10 = $800,000 a year
```

**Site C: next to the city.** Limestone travels 80 km. Cement travels almost no distance.

```
Limestone transport = 150,000 x 80 x 0.10 = $1,200,000 a year
```

Site Q saves $400,000 a year in transport. The reason is that the business loses weight during production: it is cheaper to move the lighter finished product than the heavier raw material. The opposite applies when the finished product is bulkier, more fragile or more perishable than its inputs, such as a bakery or a maker of large plastic water tanks. Then the business should locate near its market.

Before choosing Site Q, Ardwick should also check whether workers are available near the quarry and whether roads can take heavy lorries. A small transport saving could be lost if wages are much higher or the business has to build its own road.

## Service businesses

Service factors include nearness to customers, footfall (the number of people passing), visibility, rent, parking and public transport, competitors nearby, the availability of skilled staff, and reliable communications.

Two points are easy to miss.

- **Competitors can help.** Restaurants, jewellers and car dealers often group together because customers go to that area to compare. A new restaurant on a street full of restaurants may gain from the crowd.
- **Some services do not need a customer-facing location.** An online bank, a call centre or a firm that designs websites can locate where rent and wages are low, as long as communications are reliable.

### Worked example: Juniper Pharmacy

Juniper Pharmacy can rent one of two shops.

| | High street | Retail park |
|---|---|---|
| Rent per year | $36,000 | $22,000 |
| Expected customers per day | 180 | 120 |
| Average spend per customer | $14 | $16 |

The pharmacy opens 300 days a year.

```
High street revenue  = 180 x 14 x 300 = $756,000 a year
Retail park revenue  = 120 x 16 x 300 = $576,000 a year
Difference in revenue = $180,000
Difference in rent    = 36,000 - 22,000 = $14,000
```

The high street costs $14,000 more in rent but is expected to bring $180,000 more in revenue. Even after the extra cost of the medicines sold, the high street site is likely to earn more. The retail park's higher average spend (customers drive there and buy more at once) does not make up for fewer customers. The recommendation depends on the estimates being accurate, so Juniper should check footfall figures before signing a lease.

## Choosing a country

The factors a business could consider include:

- **Costs:** wages, land, energy and materials.
- **Access to markets:** being close to customers, and whether import tariffs or quotas make it cheaper to produce inside a market than to export to it.
- **Political stability and the law:** stable governments and clear rules lower risk; laws on employment, safety and the environment affect costs.
- **Government incentives:** grants or lower taxes for foreign businesses.
- **Infrastructure:** ports, roads, power supplies and internet.
- **Skills and language:** whether local workers have the skills needed, and whether managers can communicate with staff and customers.
- **Exchange rates:** changes in the value of a currency can alter costs and the value of profits sent home.
- **Culture:** what customers want and how business is done may differ.

### Annotated model answer

Question: *Brennock Shoes is deciding whether to build a factory in Country K, where wages are 60% lower than at home but the government has changed four times in five years. Explain one reason why Brennock might decide not to locate in Country K.*

Model answer: Country K's government has changed four times in five years, so it is politically unstable. A new government could raise taxes on foreign firms, change employment laws or even take control of foreign-owned factories. This uncertainty makes it hard for Brennock to plan and could wipe out the savings from lower wages.

Why it earns credit: it names a factor (political instability), uses the case evidence (four governments in five years), says what could happen and links back to the business's decision (losing the wage savings).

## Recommend and justify: a method

1. Identify the deciding factor for this business (use the table at the top of this page).
2. Compare the options on that factor, with numbers if the data allows.
3. Check one or two other factors that could overturn the choice.
4. Recommend one option and give the reason that decided it, plus one risk.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify / state:** name a factor, such as "nearness to raw materials".
- **Explain:** link the factor to the business: why does *this* business need to be near *that*?
- **Calculate:** transport costs, revenue or total costs per site; show units and time period.
- **Justify:** choose a location, use the deciding factor and the data, and add a risk.

## Common errors

- Listing factors without saying which matters most for the business in the question.
- Recommending the cheapest site without considering revenue, skills or access.
- Saying every service must be near its customers.
- Treating low wages as the only reason to locate abroad.
- Forgetting trade barriers: a tariff can make producing inside a market cheaper than exporting to it.
