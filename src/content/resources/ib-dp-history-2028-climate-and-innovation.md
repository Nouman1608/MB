The focused study **Climate and innovation** is one of the five focused studies in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It pairs two examples, and both must be studied: **Norse exploration (circa 982 to 1020)** and **the Aztec Empire (circa 1428 to 1469)**. It has three inquiry questions, each with three points of grounding content for each example. The study is assessed in paper 1, a source-based paper, so the aim is to know enough history to use sources well. This guide covers the history, suggests how to use the grounding content, and shows how to apply the source skills. It is the same for SL and HL.

## How the focused study is assessed

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 15 minutes and is worth 24 marks. It uses three sources (A, B and C) framed by one inquiry question drawn from the focused study. The three questions are always the same:

1. Explain how the **content** of both source A and source B can be used to answer the inquiry question (6 marks).
2. Analyse how the **context** of source C influences how it can be used to answer the inquiry question (6 marks).
3. Examine how the **perspectives** in all the sources can be used to answer the inquiry question (12 marks).

You are not asked to write about the grounding content directly. It gives you the historical context so that you can read sources with understanding. Only one of the two examples will feature in a given paper, so you must know both.

## Inquiry question 1: What factors prompted innovation?

### Norse exploration

The grounding content names population pressures and lack of arable land, advances in shipbuilding, and the roles of Erik Thorvaldsson (Erik the Red) and Leif Erikson.

The Norse who settled Iceland from about 870 found a land with limited farmland. By the late tenth century good land there was largely taken, and feuding was common. Erik the Red was outlawed from Iceland for killing in a feud, and in about 982 he sailed west, exploring the coast of Greenland for about three years. He returned to Iceland and recruited settlers, and a fleet left in about 985 or 986 to found the Eastern and Western Settlements. His name for the land, "Greenland", is often read as a recruiting slogan. His son Leif Erikson sailed west in about 1000, according to the Icelandic sagas, and reached the land he named Vinland.

Ships were central. The Norse built clinker-hulled longships, and cargo vessels (often called knarrs) that were broad enough to carry people, livestock and goods across the open Atlantic. The ships could be sailed and rowed, and their shallow draught allowed landings on beaches. These technical advances made repeated crossings possible. Historians stress that people, not ships alone, explain the move: pressure on land, ambition for status and a tradition of seafaring all mattered. The sources for these events include sagas, written down in the thirteenth century, long after the events. This matters when you assess them.

### The Aztec Empire

The grounding content names the Triple Alliance and the establishment of the Aztec Empire, imperial reforms including the Flower Wars, and the role of Moctezuma I.

In 1428 the Mexica of Tenochtitlan joined with Texcoco and Tlacopan in a Triple Alliance after defeating the Tepanec city of Azcapotzalco. This alliance, rather than a single city, formed the political core of what is called the Aztec Empire. Tenochtitlan became its dominant partner. Itzcoatl was the ruler during the alliance's founding, and Moctezuma I (about 1440 to 1469) expanded its reach. Imperial reforms under Moctezuma I included reorganising the nobility and the state. The Flower Wars (xochiyaoyotl) were ritual wars fought with neighbouring states such as Tlaxcala, in which warriors aimed to capture prisoners for sacrifice. Historians debate their purpose: some stress religion, some stress training for warriors and a display of power, and some stress political pressure on enemies.

## Inquiry question 2: How did climate conditions shape innovation?

### Norse exploration

The grounding content names the Medieval Warm Period, the emergence of new sea routes, and environmental conditions in Greenland and Vinland.

Historians use "Medieval Warm Period" for a period of generally warmer conditions in the North Atlantic region, spanning roughly the tenth to thirteenth centuries (dates vary by author). Warmer seas and less sea ice made the northern sailing routes from Iceland to Greenland more passable. The route to Greenland became a regular one, and from Greenland sailors could reach Baffin Island, Labrador and Newfoundland. In Greenland, the south-western fjords had meadows that supported cattle, sheep and goats, though only just. Vinland, according to the sagas, had good timber, grapes and rich pasture; the archaeology at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland shows a short-lived Norse base used for timber and ship repair. Treat the sagas' description of Vinland carefully and compare it with the archaeological evidence.

You should be ready to explain that climate was a condition that enabled the voyages, not the single cause. Equally, later cooling in the North Atlantic is one of the factors historians discuss when considering the decline of the Greenland settlements, though other causes (trade, contact, the loss of markets for walrus ivory) are also argued.

### The Aztec Empire

The grounding content names the environmental conditions in the Valley of Mexico, the importance of water resources, and drought including the famine of One Rabbit.

The Valley of Mexico is a high basin with a chain of shallow lakes. Tenochtitlan stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, so water was both a resource and a danger. The lake system supplied fish, birds and transport routes, but the salt content of some lakes made water management essential. The Mexica built causeways, dikes and aqueducts to manage floods and to bring fresh water to the city. The famine of One Rabbit (1454) followed a run of poor harvests caused by drought and frost, and caused deaths and migration. The reaction of the state, which opened its stores and then relied on tribute and conquest to rebuild food supplies, shows how climate shaped both policy and expansion.

## Inquiry question 3: What innovations took place?

### Norse exploration

The grounding content names new settlements in Greenland and Vinland, including L'Anse aux Meadows; food production; and relations with the Skrælingjar.

The Norse in Greenland created a farming society in the Eastern and Western Settlements, with a bishopric from the twelfth century and trade in walrus ivory and other Arctic goods. L'Anse aux Meadows, found in 1960 by Helge Ingstad and Anne Stine Ingstad, is the only securely established Norse site in North America. It had turf-walled halls and workshops and was used for a short time. Food production combined pastoral farming with hunting seals, caribou and other animals. "Skrælingjar" is the Norse term for the Indigenous peoples they met, and the sagas describe both trade and violent clashes. Remember that the Norse accounts are written from the Norse side, and that the Indigenous voices are mostly absent from the written record.

### The Aztec Empire

The grounding content names urban planning including in Tenochtitlan, food production including chinampas, and the expansion and annexation of Totonacapan.

Tenochtitlan was laid out with causeways, canals, a ceremonial centre with temples, markets and an aqueduct bringing water from Chapultepec. **Chinampas** were raised, rectangular garden plots built in shallow lake beds from layers of mud and vegetation, and enclosed by willow trees. They allowed several harvests a year and fed a large population. Expansion into Totonacapan, on the Gulf coast, gave the empire access to tropical products such as cotton, rubber and cacao. Tribute from conquered provinces brought in goods and labour. Be careful with the chronology: Moctezuma I's conquests on the Gulf coast came during and after his reign's middle decades.

## Using sources in this study

Sources for the two examples are very different, and paper 1 may use either primary or secondary sources in written, visual or multimodal form.

- **Norse sagas** (such as the Saga of the Greenlanders and Erik the Red's Saga) were composed in Iceland in the thirteenth century. Their value lies in a view of how Icelanders remembered exploration; their limitations include the time gap, literary shaping and a focus on heroes.
- **Archaeological evidence** (L'Anse aux Meadows, farm remains in Greenland) can corroborate or challenge the sagas. Be ready to compare.
- **Aztec codices** such as the Codex Mendoza (produced under Spanish rule in the 1540s) record tribute and city layout. The painter's patron and audience shaped the content.
- **Spanish accounts** of Tenochtitlan, written after the conquest of 1521, describe the city in awe, but the authors were conquerors.

For question 1, pull out specific points of content and explain how each helps answer the inquiry question. For question 2, use the origin, purpose, date and audience of source C. For question 3, state each source's perspective, then compare them: where do they agree, where do they differ, and how together do they give a fuller answer?

## Linking to the concepts

- **Cause and consequence:** compare short-term triggers (a feud, a drought year) with long-term conditions (land shortage, climate).
- **Continuity and change:** the Aztec state changed its farming and city planning, but maintained older Mesoamerican ideas of tribute and sacrifice.
- **Perspectives:** Norse, Indigenous, Mexica and Spanish voices are all present or absent.
- **Significance:** why we study L'Anse aux Meadows or the chinampas depends on what we think is important in the history of innovation.

## Common mistakes

- Treating climate as the only cause of innovation.
- Confusing the Triple Alliance with a single Aztec city.
- Stating dates for the Greenland and Vinland voyages with false precision.
- Treating the sagas as eyewitness reports.
- Writing about what happened in general and not about how a source can be used for the inquiry question.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I answer the inquiry questions in the exam?** No. They give the context. The exam asks how sources can be used to answer one inquiry question.

**Do I need to know both examples?** Yes. Only one appears in a given paper.
