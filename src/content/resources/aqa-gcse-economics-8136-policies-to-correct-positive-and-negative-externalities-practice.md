These questions cover **section 3.2.3.4 Policies to correct positive and negative externalities** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017. There are no tiers. All firms, cities and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [externality policies study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-policies-to-correct-positive-and-negative-externalities/) and the [externality policies revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-policies-to-correct-positive-and-negative-externalities-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of a positive externality? **[1]**

A: Smoke from a factory affecting nearby residents

B: Neighbours enjoying the view of a well-kept garden

C: A driver paying for fuel

D: Damage to a smoker's own health

**2.** Which **one** of the following policies is most suitable for a good that is under-consumed because of its positive externalities? **[1]**

A: An indirect tax

B: A subsidy

C: A ban

D: A minimum price

**3.** State what is meant by social cost. **[1]**

**4.** A factory's private cost is £45 per unit and each unit causes £15 of harm to local residents. It sells each unit for £55.

**(a)** Calculate the social cost per unit. **[1]**

**(b)** Using the figures, explain why the market over-produces this good. **[2]**

**(c)** State the tax per unit that would make the firm face the full social cost. **[1]**

**5.** A city introduces a £10 daily charge for driving high-emission vehicles in its centre. Daily trips by these vehicles fall from 80,000 to 62,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in daily trips. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the daily revenue from the charge. **[1]**

**6.** A home solar panel system costs £5,800. A household values its private benefit at £5,000.

**(a)** State the smallest subsidy that would make the household willing to buy the system. **[1]**

**(b)** The government pays a subsidy of £1,200 on each of 3,500 systems. Calculate the total cost of the subsidy. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why a fine may fail to reduce pollution by a firm. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain why a free market may lead to too little training of apprentices by firms. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of using regulation rather than a tax to reduce pollution. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the provision of information could reduce the consumption of a harmful good, and **one** reason why it may not work. **[3]**

**11.** A government is considering a tax on sugary snacks. Demand for the snacks is price inelastic, and low-income households spend a larger share of their income on them than high-income households.

**(a)** Explain why the tax may have only a small effect on consumption. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the tax could conflict with the objective of reducing inequality. **[2]**

**12.** A government wants to reduce litter and waste from single-use takeaway cups. It is choosing between a 25p charge on every single-use cup and a ban on single-use cups. Evaluate which policy it should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. The neighbours gain without paying; A is a negative externality and C and D are private costs. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A subsidy lowers the price, so more is consumed. **[1]**

**3.** The full cost of an activity to society: private cost plus external cost [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 45 + 15 = **£60** [1].

**(b)** The firm compares the £55 price with its private cost of £45, so it makes £10 profit a unit and keeps producing [1]; but the social cost of £60 is above the £55 price, so each unit makes society worse off and more is produced than is best for society [1].

**(c)** **£15** per unit [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** (80,000 − 62,000) ÷ 80,000 × 100 [1] = **22.5%** [1].

**(b)** 10 × 62,000 = **£620,000** [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** 5,800 − 5,000 = **£800** [1].

**(b)** 1,200 × 3,500 = **£4,200,000** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** If the fine is lower than the cost of cutting pollution, the firm may simply pay it [1]; and if the chance of being caught is low, the expected fine is even smaller, so behaviour does not change [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Training creates benefits for other firms and the wider economy, for example when trained workers later move to other employers [1]. The training firm considers only its own private benefit, which is lower than the social benefit [1], so it trains fewer apprentices than is best for society [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Advantage: regulation gives a certain result, for example a legal limit on emissions, whereas the effect of a tax depends on how firms respond [1], so it suits very harmful pollution [1]. Disadvantage: it needs inspectors and enforcement, which cost money [1], and unlike a tax it raises no revenue that could pay for cleaning up the damage [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Campaigns or warning labels show people the harm the good causes [1], so some choose to buy less of it [1]. It may not work because people may ignore the message, or habits and addiction may outweigh the information [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** With price-inelastic demand, buyers are not very responsive to a change in price [1], so the price rise reduces quantity by a smaller percentage, and the tax mainly raises revenue [1].

**(b)** Low-income households spend a larger share of their income on the snacks [1], so the tax takes a higher percentage of their income than of richer households' income: it is regressive and widens inequality [1]. **[4]**

**12.** *For the 25p charge:*

- It raises the price of each cup, so many customers switch to reusable cups [1].
- Customers who value convenience can still pay, so choice is kept [1].
- It raises revenue that could fund litter collection or recycling [1].

*For a ban:*

- It removes single-use cups entirely, so the result is certain [1].
- It needs no system for collecting a charge, though shops must be checked [1].
- But it removes choice, and it may raise costs for small cafés, which must supply washable cups [1].
- Demand may be price inelastic for some buyers, so a 25p charge may cut use only a little [1].

*Judgement:* the charge is the better first step because it cuts most use while keeping choice and raising revenue [1]; a ban would be justified if the charge did not reduce use enough, or if the cups caused severe harm that society would not accept at any price [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Refer to the actual features of the case, such as the 25p size of the charge, rather than general points about all taxes and bans.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing pollution as a cost to the firm rather than to third parties.
- Recommending a tax for a positive externality.
- Calculating the percentage fall from the new figure.
- Saying a fine works without comparing it with the cost of complying.
- Judging the policies without using the case details.
