## Section A

**1.** In which year did the UK accept the right of individual petition to the European Commission of Human Rights? A: 1951. B: 1953. C: 1966. D: 1998. **[1]**

**2.** Which Act was passed following the decision in Sunday Times v UK (1979)? A: Contempt of Court Act 1981. B: Interception of Communications Act 1985. C: Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984. D: Education (No 2) Act 1986. **[1]**

**3.** State what is meant by the UK's dualist approach to treaties. **[2]**

**4.** State the decision in Malone v Metropolitan Police Commissioner (1979) and the later decision in Malone v UK (1984). **[2]**

**5.** Identify two Strasbourg decisions against the UK before 1998 and the change in the law that followed each. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the extent to which English courts could use the European Convention on Human Rights before the Human Rights Act 1998, referring to ex parte Brind (1991). **[4]**

**7.** Explain why the decisions in R v Ministry of Defence, ex parte Smith (1996) and Smith and Grady v UK (1999) show a weakness in the pre-1998 protection of rights. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the arguments made in the 1997 white paper Rights Brought Home for incorporating the Convention. **[3]**

**9.** *In 1990 the police plant a listening device on the outside of Ravi's house without any statutory authority and record evidence used to convict him.* Suggest how Ravi's case would have been dealt with by the English courts and by the European Court of Human Rights at the time. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how the UK responded to decisions of the European Court of Human Rights before 1998, using three examples. **[6]**

**11.** *A pressure group argues in 1995 that a new government policy banning protest marches near military bases breaches Article 11 of the Convention. The policy is made under a statutory power allowing the minister to make "such directions as he considers appropriate".* Advise the group on what arguments it could make in the English courts and what other route it has. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss how effectively human rights were protected in the United Kingdom before the Human Rights Act 1998. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: 1966. **[1]**

**2.** A: Contempt of Court Act 1981. **[1]**

**3.** A treaty binds the UK in **international law** once ratified [1], but it does **not become part of domestic law** unless Parliament **incorporates** it by statute [1].

**4.** The English court held phone tapping **lawful because no law forbade it** [1]. The European Court held it **breached Article 8** because it was not in accordance with the law [1].

**5.** Any two pairs, one mark each: **Sunday Times v UK (1979)**, Contempt of Court Act 1981 [1]; **Malone v UK (1984)**, Interception of Communications Act 1985; **Dudgeon v UK (1981)**, Homosexual Offences (NI) Order 1982; **Campbell and Cosans v UK (1982)**, Education (No 2) Act 1986; **Halford v UK (1997)**, Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 [1].

**6.** The Convention was **not part of English law**, so no claim could be based on it [1]. Courts could use it to **resolve genuine ambiguity** in a statute, presuming Parliament did not intend to breach international obligations [1]. In **ex parte Brind (1991)** the House of Lords held that a minister exercising a **broad statutory discretion** did not have to follow Article 10 [1], because requiring this would incorporate the Convention **"by the back door"** [1].

**7.** In **ex parte Smith (1996)** the Court of Appeal upheld the ban on gay people in the armed forces because it was **not irrational under Wednesbury**, despite judges' doubts [1]. In **Smith and Grady v UK (1999)** the European Court found a **breach of Article 8** [1]. It also found a **breach of Article 13**, because the high irrationality threshold meant there was **no effective domestic remedy** [1]. This shows that ordinary judicial review could not test **proportionality**, so rights were not properly protected at home [1].

**8.** People should be able to **enforce their Convention rights in UK courts** [1] without the **delay and cost** of going to Strasbourg [1], and **British judges** should be able to contribute to the development of Convention law [1].

**9.** In the English courts, as in **R v Khan (1997)**, the evidence would be **admissible**, because the Convention was not part of domestic law [1] and English law had **no statutory scheme** forbidding such surveillance [1]. After exhausting domestic remedies, Ravi could **petition Strasbourg** (available since 1966) [1]. As in **Khan v UK (2000)**, the Court would probably find a **breach of Article 8**, as the surveillance was **not in accordance with the law** [1]. A Strasbourg ruling would not overturn his conviction; it could lead to just satisfaction and pressure for legislation [1].

**10.** Award two marks per example, one for the decision and one for the response, to a maximum of 6. For example: **Sunday Times v UK (1979)** found an injunction on thalidomide articles breached Article 10 [1], and Parliament passed the **Contempt of Court Act 1981** [1]. **Malone v UK (1984)** found phone tapping breached Article 8 [1], leading to the **Interception of Communications Act 1985** [1]. **Brogan v UK (1988)** found detention for over four days without a judge breached Article 5 [1], but the UK **derogated under Article 15** instead of changing the law [1].

**11.** The Convention was **not part of English law**, so the group could not sue directly for breach of Article 11 [1]. It could argue in **judicial review** that the policy was **irrational** under Wednesbury, a high threshold [1]. It could rely on the **common law** freedom to protest as a residual liberty [1]. Following **ex parte Brind**, the minister's **broad discretion** need not be exercised in line with the Convention [1], so a Convention argument would likely fail unless the statute was ambiguous [1]. After exhausting domestic remedies, the group could **petition Strasbourg** under the right of individual petition [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- Rights were **residual liberties**, protected only where the law did not intervene (Malone, 1979) [1].
- The **common law** could protect important values, as in **Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993)** [1].
- **Dualism** meant the Convention could not be enforced in English courts, and courts would not apply it to discretion (**ex parte Brind**) [1].
- **Wednesbury** review gave weak protection, shown by **ex parte Smith** and **Smith and Grady v UK** [1].
- **Strasbourg** provided an effective external check, with many cases producing legislation, such as the Contempt of Court Act 1981 [1].
- But Strasbourg claims were **slow and expensive**, and the remedy came only after domestic routes were exhausted [1].
- Governments could respond **minimally**, as with the Brogan derogation [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that protection existed but was patchy and indirect, which explains the move to the HRA 1998 [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Claiming the Convention applied in English courts before 2000.
- Misreading ex parte Brind as a pro-Convention decision.
- Giving Strasbourg decisions without the UK's response.
- Forgetting the Article 13 point in Smith and Grady.
- Writing a description with no evaluation in the essay question.
