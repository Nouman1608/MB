This guide covers outcomes **1.4.7** (characteristics of overhead expenses: fixed, semi-fixed, semi-variable and variable), **1.4.8** (allocation, apportionment and absorption of overheads), **1.4.9** (apportionment and continuous allotment of service departments), **1.4.10** (machine hour and labour hour rates) and **1.4.11** (over and under absorption of overheads). They sit in Topic 1.4, Introduction to costing, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Use it with the [introduction to costing revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-introduction-to-costing-revision-notes/) and the [overhead costs practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-overhead-costs-practice/). The absorbed overhead becomes part of the job cost in the [job costing guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-job-costing/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.4.7 | Characteristics of overhead expenses | Classify an overhead as fixed, semi-fixed, semi-variable or variable and describe how it behaves |
| 1.4.8 | Allocation, apportionment and absorption | Charge overheads to departments and then to units of output |
| 1.4.9 | Apportionment and continuous allotment of service departments | Share service department costs to production departments, including departments that serve each other |
| 1.4.10 | Machine hour and labour hour rates | Calculate the rate per hour and use it |
| 1.4.11 | Over and under absorption | Calculate the difference and say where it is shown |

An **overhead** is a cost that cannot be traced to a single unit of product. Examples are rent, factory power, supervision and depreciation. Product cost needs a fair share of every overhead, and that is the purpose of this topic.

## 1.4.7 How overheads behave

| Type | Behaviour | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | Total stays the same as activity changes within the normal range | Rent of the factory |
| **Variable** | Total changes in proportion to activity | Power used by machines |
| **Semi-fixed (stepped)** | Fixed over a range, then jumps to a new level | One supervisor for every 20 workers |
| **Semi-variable** | Has a fixed part and a variable part | Electricity with a standing charge |

**Semi-variable example.** A standing charge of 200 and 0.05 for every kilowatt hour. At 3,000 kWh the cost is 200 + 3,000 × 0.05 = 350. At 5,000 kWh it is 200 + 5,000 × 0.05 = 450.

**Semi-fixed example.** One supervisor costing 3,000 is needed for each 20 workers. With 45 workers, three supervisors are needed (45 ÷ 20 = 2.25, rounded up), so the cost is 9,000. When the numbers rise to 61 workers, a fourth supervisor takes the cost to 12,000.

## 1.4.8 Allocation, apportionment and absorption

1. **Allocation:** a cost that belongs wholly to one department is charged to it in full.
2. **Apportionment:** a cost shared by several departments is divided on a fair basis, such as floor area, number of employees, or value of machinery.
3. **Absorption:** the total overhead of a production department is charged to units of product, using a rate per machine hour, labour hour or other base.

## Worked example: Wexford Components

Wexford has two production departments, Machining and Assembly, and two service departments, Maintenance and Canteen. Service departments do not make products, so their costs must be passed to the production departments.

**Step 1: allocate and apportion the first-stage overheads**

| | Machining | Assembly | Maintenance | Canteen | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Allocated overheads | 40,000 | 25,000 | 12,000 | 8,000 | 85,000 |
| Rent 30,000 by floor area (400 : 300 : 100 : 200 square metres) | 12,000 | 9,000 | 3,000 | 6,000 | 30,000 |
| Machine insurance 10,000 by machinery value (90,000 : 45,000 : 15,000 : 0) | 6,000 | 3,000 | 1,000 | 0 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | **58,000** | **37,000** | **16,000** | **14,000** | **125,000** |

Rent: 30,000 × 400 ÷ 1,000 = 12,000 and so on. Insurance: 10,000 × 90,000 ÷ 150,000 = 6,000, and so on.

## 1.4.9 Service departments

Maintenance serves Machining 60%, Assembly 30% and Canteen 10%. The Canteen serves Machining 50%, Assembly 30% and Maintenance 20%.

If service departments did not serve each other, one step would be enough. Here they do, so use **continuous allotment** (also called repeated distribution): share each service department's cost in turn, and repeat until the amounts left are too small to matter.

| Step | Department shared | Amount | Machining | Assembly | Maintenance | Canteen |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | | | 58,000 | 37,000 | 16,000 | 14,000 |
| 1 | Maintenance | 16,000 | +9,600 | +4,800 | −16,000 | +1,600 |
| 2 | Canteen | 15,600 | +7,800 | +4,680 | +3,120 | −15,600 |
| 3 | Maintenance | 3,120 | +1,872 | +936 | −3,120 | +312 |
| 4 | Canteen | 312 | +156 | +94 | +62 | −312 |
| 5 to 7 | Remaining 62, 6 and 1 | | +41 | +21 | −62 | 0 |
| **Total** | | | **77,469** | **47,531** | **0** | **0** |

Check: 77,469 + 47,531 = 125,000, the same as the total before sharing, so no cost has been lost. Each share is rounded to whole dollars (for example 93.6 and 62.4 in step 4 become 94 and 62), and the last small steps are rounded in the same way.

**Algebraic check.** Let M be the total Maintenance cost and C the total Canteen cost after sharing between each other. M = 16,000 + 0.2C and C = 14,000 + 0.1M. Then M = 16,000 + 0.2(14,000 + 0.1M) = 18,800 + 0.02M, so M = 18,800 ÷ 0.98 = 19,183.67 and C = 15,918.37. Machining receives 0.6 × 19,183.67 + 0.5 × 15,918.37 = 11,510.20 + 7,959.18 = 19,469.38, giving 77,469.38. This matches the table. The examiner will accept either method unless the question names one.

## 1.4.10 Machine hour and labour hour rates

The **overhead absorption rate** = budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted activity.

Use a **machine hour rate** where machines do most of the work and a **labour hour rate** where work is done mainly by people.

Wexford budgets 15,500 machine hours in Machining and 9,500 direct labour hours in Assembly.

- Machining: 77,469 ÷ 15,500 = 4.998, which is **5.00 per machine hour** (rounded).
- Assembly: 47,531 ÷ 9,500 = 5.003, which is **5.00 per labour hour** (rounded).

A job using 12 machine hours in Machining and 8 labour hours in Assembly absorbs 12 × 5 + 8 × 5 = 100 of overhead.

Choose a rate that reflects what causes the overhead. A labour hour rate in a highly automated department gives a poor result, because most of the cost is machine-related. Absorption on a percentage of direct materials or direct wages is also used, but the hour-based rates are in the specification.

## 1.4.11 Over and under absorption

At the end of the period, **overhead absorbed** (actual hours × rate) is compared with **actual overhead incurred**.

- **Under-absorbed:** absorbed < actual. Too little has been charged to products, so the difference is an additional expense in the statement of profit or loss.
- **Over-absorbed:** absorbed > actual. Too much has been charged, so the difference is added back as a credit.

Wexford's actual results:

| | Machining | Assembly |
|---|---:|---:|
| Actual hours | 15,000 machine hours | 10,100 labour hours |
| Absorbed (hours × 5.00) | 75,000 | 50,500 |
| Actual overhead incurred | 79,000 | 49,600 |
| **Difference** | 4,000 under-absorbed | 900 over-absorbed |

Machining: 79,000 − 75,000 = 4,000. Assembly: 50,500 − 49,600 = 900. The net effect is an under-absorption of 3,100, which reduces profit for the year.

**Causes.** Over or under absorption arises because the rate was based on a budget. Typical reasons are: actual overheads differing from the budget, actual hours differing from the budget, or both. Here Machining worked 500 fewer hours than budgeted and spent more than budget.

The adjustment is made after the period ends. It does not change the cost already recorded on each job, but it corrects the profit figure. In practice the cost per unit is not revised, and large variances prompt a review of the budget and the rate.

## Common mistakes

- Using actual overheads to calculate the rate instead of budgeted figures.
- Sharing a service department cost to a department that does not use the service.
- Forgetting that service departments serve each other, so one distribution is not enough.
- Losing cost in the sharing, so totals before and after do not match.
- Treating under-absorption as a credit to profit, or over-absorption as a debit.
- Using the budgeted hours, not actual hours, to calculate the amount absorbed.
- Calling the same cost fixed and variable without stating the range of activity.
- Choosing a labour hour rate for a department where machines do most of the work, without comment.

## Exam technique

- Draw a table with each department as a column and each overhead as a row. Show the basis of each apportionment in the row heading.
- Check that the total of the table before and after reapportionment is the same.
- For **continuous allotment**, show at least three rounds so the method is visible.
- State clearly whether the answer is under- or over-absorbed, and whether it is added to or deducted from profit.
- **Explain** (why absorption is needed) means link the reason to a decision: for example, to find the full cost of a job so a selling price can be set.
