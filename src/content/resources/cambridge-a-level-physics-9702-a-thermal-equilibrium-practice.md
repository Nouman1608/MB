Take the specific heat capacity of water as 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. In calculations, assume no energy is exchanged with the surroundings unless told otherwise.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the direction of net thermal energy transfer between two regions, and the condition for them to be in thermal equilibrium. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why thermal equilibrium is described as a dynamic state. **[2]**

**3.** A small iron nail at 150 °C is dropped into a large tank of water at 18 °C. Explain why the nail's temperature falls a great deal while the water's temperature hardly changes. **[3]**

**4.** A metal spoon and a wooden spoon have been in the same drawer overnight. The metal spoon feels colder. Explain whether they are at different temperatures. **[2]**

**5.** Body A, at 40 °C, has five times the internal energy of body B, at 60 °C. They are placed in contact. State the direction of the net energy transfer. **[1]**

---

## Section B

**6.** An iron block of mass 0.50 kg and specific heat capacity 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, at 200 °C, is lowered into 2.0 kg of water at 15 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the final temperature. **[3]**

**(b)** State **two** assumptions made in your calculation. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the final temperature is much closer to 15 °C than to 200 °C. **[1]**

**7.** A thermometer at 22 °C is used to measure the temperature of 12 g of water at 45 °C. The part of the thermometer that changes temperature is equivalent to 8.0 g of glass of specific heat capacity 840 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the reading on the thermometer when it reaches thermal equilibrium with the water. **[3]**

**(b)** State the error in the measurement of the water's original temperature. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** change to the method that would reduce this error. **[1]**

**8.** Hot water of mass 0.25 kg at 85 °C is mixed with a mass m of cold water at 15 °C. The final temperature is 40 °C.

**(a)** Calculate m. **[2]**

**(b)** In a real experiment, the final temperature is found to be slightly lower than 40 °C. Suggest a reason. **[1]**

**9.** Blocks A and B are each placed in turn in contact with block C. In each case there is no net energy transfer.

**(a)** State what can be concluded about the temperatures of A, B and C. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how this idea allows a thermometer to compare the temperatures of two objects that are never in contact. **[2]**

**10.** Explain, in terms of molecules, why there is a net transfer of energy from a hotter region to a colder region that is in contact with it. **[3]**

**11.** Water of mass 0.30 kg at 60 °C is poured into a copper can of mass 0.15 kg at 20 °C. The specific heat capacity of copper is 385 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the final temperature of the water and can. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy transferred from the water to the can. **[2]**

**12.** A hot metal block is placed in cold water in an insulated container. Describe how the temperatures of the block and the water change with time from the moment of contact. **[3]**

---

## Answers

**1.** From the region at higher temperature to the region at lower temperature [1]; they are in thermal equilibrium when they are at the same temperature, so there is no net energy transfer [1].

**2.** Energy continues to be transferred between the regions in both directions [1], but at equal rates, so the net transfer is zero [1].

**3.** Energy flows from the nail to the water because the nail is at the higher temperature [1]. The nail has a small mass, so losing a small amount of energy produces a large fall in its temperature [1]; the water has a very large mass (and a large specific heat capacity), so gaining that same energy produces only a tiny rise in temperature [1].

**4.** They are at the same temperature (both in thermal equilibrium with the drawer) [1]; the metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from the hand faster than wood does [1].

**5.** From B to A (B is at the higher temperature, whatever the internal energies) [1].

**6. (a)** 0.50 × 450 × (200 – θ) = 2.0 × 4200 × (θ – 15) [1]; 225(200 – θ) = 8400(θ – 15), so 8625θ = 171 000 [1]; θ = 19.8 °C [1].

**(b)** Any two [1 each]: no energy exchanged with the surroundings; the container absorbs negligible energy; no water evaporates; the block and water reach a uniform final temperature.

**(c)** The water needs far more energy per kelvin (mc = 8400 J K⁻¹) than the block (225 J K⁻¹), so its temperature changes much less for the same energy [1].

**7. (a)** 0.012 × 4200 × (45 – θ) = 0.0080 × 840 × (θ – 22) [1]; 50.4(45 – θ) = 6.72(θ – 22) [1]; θ = 2416 ÷ 57.1 = 42 °C [1].

**(b)** The reading is about 3 K too low [1].

**(c)** Use a larger volume of water; or warm the thermometer close to the expected temperature first; or use a thermometer with a much smaller mass (e.g. a thermocouple) [1].

**8. (a)** 0.25 × 4200 × (85 – 40) = m × 4200 × (40 – 15) [1]; m = 0.25 × 45 ÷ 25 = 0.45 kg [1].

**(b)** Energy is lost from the hot water to the surroundings or to the container before equilibrium is reached [1].

**9. (a)** A, B and C are all at the same temperature [1].

**(b)** If each object in turn is brought into thermal equilibrium with the thermometer [1], equal readings show the objects are at the same temperature, so they would be in equilibrium with each other without being placed in contact [1].

**10.** Molecules in the hotter region have a greater mean kinetic energy [1]. Energy is passed between molecules in collisions at the boundary in both directions [1], but on average more energy passes from the higher-energy molecules to the lower-energy ones, giving a net transfer from hot to cold until the mean kinetic energies are equal [1].

**11. (a)** 0.30 × 4200 × (60 – θ) = 0.15 × 385 × (θ – 20) [1]; 1260(60 – θ) = 57.75(θ – 20) [1]; θ = 77 955 ÷ 1317.75 = 59.2 °C [1].

**(b)** Q = mcΔθ = 0.15 × 385 × (59.2 – 20) [1] = 2.3 × 10³ J [1].

**12.** The block's temperature falls and the water's temperature rises [1]; both approach the same final temperature, which lies between the two starting temperatures [1]; once they are equal there is no further net change (thermal equilibrium), with the changes becoming slower as the temperature difference becomes smaller [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "no energy transfer" instead of "no net energy transfer".
- Linking the direction of transfer to the amount of internal energy rather than temperature.
- Setting up the energy equation with a temperature difference that comes out negative.
- Final temperatures outside the range of the starting temperatures.
- Forgetting to convert grams to kilograms.
- Stating assumptions too vaguely ("no errors") rather than naming the energy losses ignored.
