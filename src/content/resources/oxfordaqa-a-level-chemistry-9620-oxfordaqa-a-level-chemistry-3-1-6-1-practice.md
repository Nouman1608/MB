These questions cover **3.1.6.1 Collision theory** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Activation energies for the lettered reactions, and for the reaction in question 10, are invented for these questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [collision theory study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-1/) or the [collision theory revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-1-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State the two conditions that collision theory, as given in this specification, says are needed for particles to react. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by a successful collision. **[1]**

**3.** Reaction X has Ea = 64 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction. **[1]**

**4.** On an enthalpy profile for reaction Y, the reactants are at 0 kJ mol⁻¹, the peak is at +210 kJ mol⁻¹ and the products are at +45 kJ mol⁻¹. State Ea and ΔH for reaction Y. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "Exothermic reactions do not need any activation energy, because they give out energy." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**6.** In a gas, each molecule collides with others many millions of times per second, yet many gas reactions take minutes or hours. Explain why. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Reaction Z is exothermic, with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Draw a labelled enthalpy profile for reaction Z. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction. **[1]**

**8.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes: 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g), ΔH = −196 kJ mol⁻¹. Without a catalyst, a bottle of hydrogen peroxide solution decomposes only very slowly at room temperature.

**(a)** Explain why the decomposition is slow, even though it is exothermic. **[3]**

**(b)** State what the value of ΔH tells you about this reaction, and one thing it does not tell you. **[2]**

**9.** Two gas reactions, P and Q, are carried out at the same temperature with the same collision frequency. Ea for P is 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and for Q is 90 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain which reaction is faster. **[3]**

**10.** An endothermic reaction has Ea(forward) = 135 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea(reverse) = 47 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for the forward reaction. **[2]**

**(b)** Draw a labelled enthalpy profile for the forward reaction, showing both activation energies and ΔH. **[3]**

**11.** Coal is mainly carbon. Burning carbon is very exothermic: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH = −394 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Explain why a heap of coal does not burn when left in air at room temperature. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why, once the coal is lit, it keeps burning without further heating. **[2]**

**12.** State whether each statement is correct. If it is incorrect, correct it.

**(a)** Every collision between reactant particles leads to a reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Activation energy is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur. **[1]**

**(c)** A reaction with ΔH = −500 kJ mol⁻¹ must be faster than one with ΔH = −50 kJ mol⁻¹. **[1]**

**(d)** For an endothermic reaction, Ea is at least as large as ΔH. **[1]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The particles must collide [1]; with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1] **[2]**

**2.** A collision with enough energy (at least Ea) that leads to a reaction [1] **[1]**

**3.** Ea(reverse) = 64 + 92 = **156 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[1]**

**4.** Ea = **210 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; ΔH = **+45 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Ea is measured from the reactants to the peak, not from the products (which would give 165 kJ mol⁻¹).

**5.** All reactions need a minimum energy to start breaking bonds in the reactants [1]; the energy given out overall (ΔH) is released only after the activation energy barrier has been overcome [1] **[2]**

**6.** Only a small fraction of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]; so most collisions are unsuccessful and the particles bounce apart unchanged [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Axes labelled enthalpy and progress of reaction [1]; products drawn 120 kJ mol⁻¹ below reactants, with ΔH labelled as a downward arrow [1]; a hump with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹ labelled from the reactants to the peak [1]

**(b)** Ea(reverse) = 50 + 120 = **170 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[4]**

**8. (a)** The activation energy for the decomposition is high [1]; at room temperature only a very small fraction of collisions have energy ≥ Ea [1]; so there are very few successful collisions per second and the rate is very low [1]

**(b)** It tells you the reaction releases 196 kJ per two moles of H₂O₂ decomposed (it is exothermic) [1]; it does not tell you the activation energy, so it does not tell you how fast the reaction is [1] **[5]**

**9.** P is faster [1]; its activation energy is lower, so at the same temperature a much larger fraction of collisions have energy ≥ Ea [1]; with the same collision frequency, P has more successful collisions per second [1] **[3]**

**10. (a)** ΔH = Ea(forward) − Ea(reverse) [1]; = 135 − 47 = **+88 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Products drawn 88 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants, with ΔH labelled [1]; Ea(forward) = 135 kJ mol⁻¹ labelled from the reactants to the peak [1]; Ea(reverse) = 47 kJ mol⁻¹ labelled from the products to the peak [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** Oxygen molecules collide with the carbon surface all the time [1]; but reaction needs collisions with at least the activation energy [1]; at room temperature almost none of the collisions have this energy, so almost none are successful [1]

**(b)** Lighting supplies energy so that some collisions reach Ea and reaction starts [1]; the reaction is exothermic, so the heat it releases gives more particles enough energy to exceed Ea, keeping it going [1] **[5]**

**12. (a)** Incorrect: only collisions with energy ≥ Ea (a small fraction) lead to reaction [1]

**(b)** Correct [1]

**(c)** Incorrect: ΔH does not determine rate; the activation energy does [1]

**(d)** Correct: the peak cannot be lower than the products [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "minimum" out of the definition of activation energy.
- Measuring Ea from the products or the axis rather than from the reactants.
- Saying particles "do not collide" when the real reason is insufficient energy.
- Linking a large negative ΔH with a high rate.
- Unlabelled profiles: no axis labels, or arrows for Ea and ΔH starting from the wrong level.
