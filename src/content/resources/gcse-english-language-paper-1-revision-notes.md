## Paper 1 at a glance

- One unseen literature fiction extract (20th or 21st century prose only — the 19th century is reserved for Paper 2).
- Reading questions move from short, contained tasks to a longer evaluative response.
- One creative writing task, linked in theme to the reading source.
- Reading and writing are equally weighted within the paper.

## Paper structure and marks

```
SECTION A: READING   40 marks (25% of GCSE)
  Q1  short form                    4 marks
  Q2  language analysis             8 marks
  Q3  structure analysis            8 marks
  Q4  extended evaluation          20 marks
  (4 + 8 + 8 + 20 = 40)

SECTION B: WRITING    40 marks (25% of GCSE)
  Content and organisation         24 marks
  Technical accuracy (AO6)         16 marks
  (24 + 16 = 40)
```

Paper 1 is worth 80 raw marks, 1 hour 45 minutes, equally weighted
against Paper 2 within the GCSE (50% each). Note Question 4's weight
in particular — at 20 of Section A's 40 marks, it is worth as much as
Questions 1–3 combined, which should shape how revision and exam time
are both allocated.

## Assessment objectives tested

```
Section A (Reading):   AO1 explicit/implicit info + interpretation
                        AO2 language and structure, using terminology
                        AO4 evaluate critically, textual references

Section B (Writing):   AO5 communicate clearly and imaginatively
                        AO6 vocabulary, sentence structures, accuracy
```

Paper 1 does **not** assess AO3 (comparing writers across texts) —
that belongs entirely to Paper 2, since Section A here works from a
single extract with nothing to compare it against.

## Reading question ladder

| Question type | What it rewards |
|---|---|
| Identify/select | Picking out explicit information or ideas directly from the text |
| Language analysis | Explaining the effect of specific word choices, imagery and techniques |
| Structure analysis | Explaining how the writer has shaped and sequenced the whole extract |
| Evaluate | Judging how successfully the writer achieves an effect, using the text as evidence |

## Reading technique

- Quote **precisely** — a single well-chosen word or short phrase beats a long, imprecise quotation.
- Always name the **technique** (metaphor, verb choice, short sentence) before explaining its effect.
- For structure questions, track the extract's **shape**: opening focus, shifts, zooming in/out, and the ending.
- For evaluation, keep returning to the specific statement or focus given in the question — don't drift into general praise.

## Creative writing

- The task is linked to the reading extract's theme, but is your own original piece — not a continuation of the extract.
- Plan before writing: a clear structure (opening hook, development, turning point, ending) outperforms a technically fluent piece with no shape.
- A varied range of sentence structures and precise vocabulary is rewarded more than length.
- Leave time to check punctuation and spelling — technical accuracy is assessed as part of writing.

## Exam traps

- Retelling the plot of the extract instead of analysing language/structure.
- Naming a technique without explaining its effect ("this is a metaphor" with no "because…").
- Writing a continuation of the source text instead of an original creative response.
- Running out of time on the writing task because too long was spent on reading questions.
- Treating Question 4 as another language-analysis question rather than the sustained, 20-mark evaluative argument it actually is — it carries as many marks as Questions 1–3 combined.
- Trying to "compare" the Paper 1 extract with anything else — AO3 (comparison) is tested only on Paper 2, since Paper 1's Section A works from a single text.
- Under-valuing technical accuracy in Section B — at 16 of 40 marks (40% of the section), spelling and punctuation errors genuinely cap the highest bands, whatever the quality of the ideas.

## Self-test

1. What are the two components tested in Paper 1?
2. Why should you always name a technique before explaining its effect?
3. What is the risk of treating the creative writing task as "continue this story"?
4. Besides content, what else is credited in the writing task?
5. How many marks is Question 4 worth, and what does that imply about revision priority?
6. Which assessment objective is tested on Paper 2 but not Paper 1, and why?

**Answers:** 1. Reading (an unseen fiction extract) and creative writing (an original piece linked in theme to the extract). 2. Because analysis marks are for the *effect* the technique creates, not simply for spotting that a technique is present — naming it is only the starting point. 3. The task asks for an original piece inspired by the theme, not a literal continuation of the source text, so writing a continuation can miss what the task is actually asking for. 4. Technical accuracy — spelling, punctuation and grammar — alongside a varied, purposeful use of vocabulary and sentence structures. 5. 20 of Section A's 40 marks — as much as Questions 1–3 combined, so it deserves proportionate revision and exam-time attention. 6. AO3 (comparing writers' ideas and perspectives across texts) — Paper 1's Section A uses only one reading source, so there is nothing to compare it against.
