This guide covers sub-topic **1.4 Resource allocation in different economic systems** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists two points: decision-making in market, planned and mixed economies (1.4.1), and resource allocation in these economic systems (1.4.2). The Topic 1 introduction adds that candidates will assess the different systems, considering their strengths and weaknesses. This guide focuses on **who makes decisions** and **what information they use**, because that is what separates the systems and drives their strengths and weaknesses. All countries and figures are invented.

## What an economic system is

An **economic system** is the set of institutions and rules through which a society answers the basic questions of 1.1.4: what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. Systems differ in two linked ways:

- **who owns the factors of production**: private individuals and firms, or the state;
- **who makes the decisions**: millions of separate buyers and sellers, or a central authority.

## 1.4.1 Decision-making in each system

### Market economy

Decisions are **decentralised**. Consumers decide what to buy given their incomes and preferences. Firms decide what to produce, and how, to earn profit. No one plans the overall outcome. Prices co-ordinate the decisions: when consumers want more of a good, its price rises, firms find it more profitable, and resources move towards it. The role of the state is limited to protecting property rights, enforcing contracts and maintaining law and order.

### Planned economy

Decisions are **centralised**. A government planning authority decides what will be produced, sets output targets for state-owned enterprises, allocates raw materials and labour between them, and sets prices and wages. Households choose among the goods the plan provides, often with rationing where demand exceeds supply.

### Mixed economy

Decisions are **shared**. Most goods are produced by private firms responding to prices, while the government owns some industries, provides some services directly (often education, health care, defence), and influences private decisions through taxes, subsidies and regulation. Every real economy is mixed; they differ in the balance.

| Decision | Market | Planned | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides what to produce? | Consumers' spending and firms' profit | Planners | Mostly consumers and firms; government for some services |
| Who decides how? | Firms, choosing the cheapest effective method | Planners and enterprise managers meeting targets | Firms, within regulations |
| Who decides for whom? | Those with income to spend | Planners, by allocation and rationing | Market incomes, adjusted by taxes, benefits and free services |
| What co-ordinates decisions? | Prices | The plan | Prices plus government action |

## 1.4.2 How resources are allocated: a worked comparison

Consider an invented country that has 1,000 thousand tonnes of steel a year to divide between two uses: making cars and making farm tractors.

**Under a plan.** Planners estimate that farms need 400 thousand tonnes' worth of tractors and allocate 600 to cars. In fact, a run of good harvests means farmers want to expand, and would buy tractors using 500. The plan delivers tractors made from 400, so there is a shortage equivalent to 100 thousand tonnes of steel in the tractor sector, and long waiting lists. Meanwhile car output exceeds what households want at the set price, and unsold cars build up. Correcting this requires the planners first to notice it, then to revise next year's plan.

**Under a market.** Farmers' extra demand raises tractor prices. Tractor makers, now earning higher profit, bid more for steel, so steel prices rise. Car makers, facing dearer steel, cut back or raise car prices. Steel moves towards tractors without anyone ordering it, and the movement continues until the extra profit from switching disappears. The information that farmers want more tractors is carried by prices.

**The lesson.** The planner needs to know every user's wants and every producer's costs; in a market, each agent needs to know only its own situation and the prices it faces. This **information problem** is the central weakness of planning. But the market answer depends on **ability to pay**: if steel prices rose sharply, a small farmer who cannot afford a tractor goes without, however great the need.

## Assessing the systems

Judge each system against several criteria, not just one.

| Criterion | Market | Planned |
|---|---|---|
| Efficiency in using resources | Strong: competition and profit push costs down | Weak: targets reward quantity, not cost or quality |
| Responsiveness to consumers | Strong: prices transmit wants quickly | Weak: plans adjust slowly |
| Innovation | Strong: profit rewards new products and methods | Weak: little reward for risk |
| Equity | Weak: output goes to those who can pay | Can be strong: basic goods shared widely |
| Provision of public goods and merit goods | Weak: public goods not provided; merit goods under-consumed | Strong: provided by the state |
| Stability of employment | Can be weak: booms and slumps | Can be strong: jobs allocated by plan |
| Ability to pursue national priorities | Limited | Strong: resources can be directed quickly |

**Worked example: a mixed economy's balance.** In invented Country Tavor, government spending is 40% of GDP. The state provides schools and hospitals free at the point of use, owns the water industry, and taxes high incomes at higher rates; everything else is produced by private firms. In invented Country Rusk, government spending is 20% of GDP, health care is mostly private, and water is supplied by regulated private firms.

- Both are mixed economies.
- Tavor places more weight on equity and access to merit goods, at the cost of higher taxes and a larger role for government decisions that may be less responsive to individual preferences.
- Rusk places more weight on market allocation, which may be more efficient and give more choice, but leaves access to health care more dependent on income.

Neither is "right": the balance reflects different value judgements (1.2.2) about efficiency and fairness.

## Why most economies are mixed

- Pure markets fail to provide public goods and under-provide merit goods, and can produce inequality that societies find unacceptable.
- Pure planning struggles with information and incentives, leading to shortages, surpluses and low quality.
- Combining the two lets prices co-ordinate most decisions while the government handles the areas markets deal with badly.

The risk in a mixed economy is that government action, too, can make poor decisions, for example through lack of information or political pressure. So the exam question is usually about the **degree** of intervention in a particular market, not which pure system is best.

## Common mistakes

- Defining systems only by ownership and forgetting who makes decisions.
- Treating "mixed" as a fixed halfway point rather than a range.
- Listing strengths and weaknesses without saying which criterion each relates to.
- Claiming planned economies are always fairer or markets always efficient.
- Ignoring the information problem when explaining why plans produce shortages and surpluses.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** for each system, say who decides, what information they use and how the three basic questions are answered.
- **Analyse:** trace how a change in consumers' wants is transmitted (prices in a market, plan revision in a planned economy).
- **Assess** or **Evaluate:** weigh at least two criteria, such as efficiency and equity, and conclude on the balance suited to the case in the question.
