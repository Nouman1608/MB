The [10.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-impacts-of-changing-trends-in-resource-consumption-revision-notes/) already define food, water and energy security, outline the water-food-energy nexus, and summarise the Aral Sea, aquifer depletion, land acquisitions and import dependence. Practise with the [10.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-10-2-practice/); the [Global resource consumption study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-global-resource-consumption-study-guide/) gives the unit overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how changing consumption affects the security of water, food and energy supplies.
- Analyse the links between the three resources, and the arguments surrounding them.
- Examine the environmental, economic, social and political impacts of consumption trends at different scales.
- Use located examples in which gains in one place or resource bring losses in another.

## Tracing a trade-off through the nexus

The nexus is most useful when you follow one decision through all three resources and ask who gains and who loses. A simple four-step method works for almost any case:

1. **Decision.** What was done to increase one resource? (A dam is built for electricity.)
2. **Direct gain.** Who benefits, and where? (Households and industry gain power.)
3. **Knock-on effect.** How are the other two resources affected? (River flow downstream changes; floodplain farming and fisheries may lose water and sediment.)
4. **Distribution.** Are the gainers and losers in the same place, country or group?

Applying the method turns a list of impacts into an argument.

## Three located examples

### A transboundary dam: the Blue Nile

Ethiopia began building the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile in 2011 and started filling its reservoir in 2020. For Ethiopia, the dam promises large amounts of hydroelectricity for a population in which many people lack reliable power, and potential electricity exports. Downstream, Egypt depends on the Nile for almost all of its fresh water and fears reduced flow, especially if the reservoir is filled quickly during a drought. Sudan stands to gain from more regular flow and reduced flooding but has also raised safety and coordination concerns. The case shows energy security for one country being viewed as a threat to water and food security for another, and why agreements on the timing of filling and release matter as much as the structure itself.

### Palm oil: consumption far away, impacts in the tropics

Palm oil is used in processed foods, cosmetics and, increasingly, biodiesel. Indonesia and Malaysia produce most of the world's supply. Demand from consumers in many countries drove the conversion of rainforest and drained peatland to plantations, causing habitat loss for species such as orangutans, large carbon emissions from burning and drying peat, and smoke haze across the region. Plantations also brought jobs and income to rural areas and export earnings to both countries. Certification schemes aim to separate sustainable supply from the rest, but their reach and enforcement are debated.

### Lithium: impacts of the energy transition

Electric vehicle batteries have raised demand for lithium. In the Salar de Atacama in northern Chile, one of the driest places on Earth, lithium is extracted by pumping brine to the surface and evaporating it in large ponds. Local Indigenous communities and scientists have raised concerns about effects on scarce water and on the wetlands and wildlife that depend on it. The example is useful because it shows that a shift to low-carbon energy changes where the impacts of consumption fall rather than removing them.

## Judging energy security: the four As

A widely used framework assesses energy security under four headings:

| Dimension | Question to ask | Example of weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Is enough energy physically there, at home or from reliable imports? | Few domestic reserves |
| Accessibility | Can it be obtained without disruption? | Dependence on one supplier, pipeline or chokepoint |
| Affordability | Can households and industry pay for it? | Price spikes, energy poverty |
| Acceptability | Is it environmentally and socially acceptable? | High emissions, local opposition to projects |

The framework helps you avoid a common error: treating a country with large reserves as secure. A country can have plenty of coal (available) that is increasingly unacceptable because of emissions, or abundant gas fields that cannot be reached during conflict.

## Worked example: virtual water in a food import

Figures are invented for a dry country, Country V.

```
Wheat imported per year          2 million tonnes = 2,000 million kg
Water needed to grow 1 kg         1,500 litres (assumed for this example)
Virtual water imported            2,000 million x 1,500 = 3,000,000 million litres
                                  = 3 x 10^12 litres
                                  = 3 cubic kilometres (1 km^3 = 10^12 litres)

Country V's renewable freshwater  4 cubic kilometres a year
Virtual water as a share          3 / 4 x 100 = 75%
```

Growing the wheat at home would require water equal to three-quarters of Country V's renewable supply. Importing it saves that water, which is why dry countries often import grain. The evaluation that follows: virtual water trade improves water security but reduces food self-sufficiency, so Country V becomes exposed to world grain prices, export bans and disruption to shipping. It also shifts pressure onto the exporting country's water resources.

## Impacts at different scales

| Scale | Example of impact |
|---|---|
| Local | Wells drying near an irrigation scheme; air pollution near a coal power station |
| National | Import bills rising when oil prices spike; export earnings from minerals |
| Regional | Haze from land clearance crossing borders; disputes over shared rivers |
| Global | Food price spikes; greenhouse gas emissions; competition for battery minerals |

Moving between scales in an answer shows that the same trend can be a gain nationally and a loss locally.

## Scarcity or distribution?

A recurring argument in this sub-topic is whether insecurity is caused by too little of a resource or by how it is shared. World food production is enough, in total, to feed everyone, yet hundreds of millions of people are undernourished, because they cannot afford or reach food. Similarly, many water-short cities lose a large share of treated water through leaking pipes, and many people without electricity live within reach of a grid they cannot pay to join. Questions that ask you to discuss the causes of insecurity reward answers that test physical scarcity against access, price, infrastructure and power before deciding which matters more in a named place.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the nexus as three separate topics rather than following one decision through all three.
- Assuming a resource-rich country is automatically secure.
- Presenting renewable energy as having no resource impacts.
- Describing impacts with no sense of which groups or places are affected.
- Calculating virtual water without converting units consistently.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse** a nexus question by breaking the decision into its effects on each resource, then linking them.
- **Examine** rewards weighing impacts at different scales and for different groups.
- **Discuss** a claim such as "Biofuels improve energy security at the expense of food security" by testing it with evidence from at least two places.
- **Evaluate** an energy policy using criteria such as the four As.
- Quote located, dated detail you are sure of; describe trends in general terms if you cannot recall figures.
