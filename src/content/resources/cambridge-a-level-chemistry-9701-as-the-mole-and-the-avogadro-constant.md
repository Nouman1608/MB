## Syllabus coverage

Subtopic 2.2 has one learning outcome: candidates should be able to **define and use the term mole in terms of the Avogadro constant**. "Use" is the larger part: almost every calculation in the course starts or ends with an amount in moles.

## The definition

**One mole of a substance is the amount that contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles of that substance.** The number of particles per mole is the **Avogadro constant**, L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ (the value given on the data sheet).

Three features of the definition matter:

- The mole is an **amount of substance**, symbol n, unit mol. It is a count, like a dozen, not a mass.
- The **particles must be specified**: atoms, molecules, ions, formula units or electrons. "One mole of oxygen" is ambiguous; one mole of O₂ molecules contains two moles of O atoms.
- L has units of mol⁻¹, because it is a number of particles *per mole*.

## Why molar mass equals relative mass

Relative masses are measured in unified atomic mass units, where 1 u = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g. How many u are there in one gram?

1 g ÷ 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 6.022 × 10²³

That number is the Avogadro constant. So if one particle has a relative mass of X (a mass of X u), then L of those particles have a mass of X grams. This is why:

**The molar mass, M, in g mol⁻¹, is numerically equal to the relative atomic, molecular or formula mass.**

| Substance | Relative mass | Molar mass |
|---|---|---|
| C (carbon) | Ar 12.0 | 12.0 g mol⁻¹ |
| H₂O | Mr 18.0 | 18.0 g mol⁻¹ |
| NaCl | relative formula mass 58.5 | 58.5 g mol⁻¹ |

The relative mass has no units; the molar mass does. Use "g mol⁻¹" whenever you write a molar mass.

## The two key relationships

    amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g mol⁻¹)        n = m ÷ M
    number of particles = amount (mol) × Avogadro constant   N = n × L

Combine them to go from a mass to a number of particles, or the reverse.

### Worked example: molecules and atoms in water

How many molecules, and how many atoms, are in 9.00 g of water?

1. n(H₂O) = 9.00 ÷ 18.0 = 0.500 mol of molecules.
2. Molecules = 0.500 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.01 × 10²³**.
3. Each molecule has 3 atoms, so atoms = 3 × 3.01 × 10²³ = **9.03 × 10²³**.
4. Hydrogen atoms alone = 2 × 3.01 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²³, one mole of H atoms.

### Worked example: ions in an ionic compound

How many ions in total are in 11.1 g of calcium chloride, CaCl₂ (relative formula mass 111.1)?

1. n(CaCl₂) = 11.1 ÷ 111.1 = 0.0999 mol of formula units.
2. Each formula unit has 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻: 3 ions.
3. Moles of ions = 3 × 0.0999 = 0.300 mol.
4. Ions = 0.300 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.80 × 10²³**.

Of these, one third are Ca²⁺ (6.02 × 10²²) and two thirds are Cl⁻.

### Worked example: the mass of one particle

Mass of one particle = molar mass ÷ L.

- One iron atom: 55.8 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = **9.27 × 10⁻²³ g**.
- One carbon-12 atom: 12.0 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g, which is 12 u, as the definition of u requires.

### Worked example: working back from a number of particles

A sample of oxygen gas contains 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules of O₂. Find its mass.

1. n(O₂) = 1.20 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.99 mol.
2. Mass = 1.99 × 32.0 = **63.8 g**.

### Worked example: counting electrons

How many electrons are in 3.40 g of ammonia, NH₃ (Mr 17.0)?

1. n(NH₃) = 3.40 ÷ 17.0 = 0.200 mol.
2. Each NH₃ molecule has 7 + 3 × 1 = 10 electrons.
3. Electrons = 0.200 × 10 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.20 × 10²⁴**.

This kind of question tests whether you can apply the mole to any particle, as the definition says.

### Worked example: elements made of molecules

Sulfur exists as S₈ molecules (Mr 256.8) and white phosphorus as P₄ molecules (Mr 124.0). How many molecules and atoms are in 8.02 g of sulfur?

1. Moles of molecules: n(S₈) = 8.02 ÷ 256.8 = 0.0312 mol, giving 0.0312 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.88 × 10²² molecules**.
2. Moles of atoms: 8 × 0.0312 = 0.250 mol, giving **1.50 × 10²³ atoms**.
3. Check: 8.02 g ÷ 32.1 g mol⁻¹ (Ar of S) also gives 0.250 mol of atoms. The number of atoms in a mass of an element does not depend on how the atoms are grouped.

## Comparing amounts

Equal masses of different substances do not contain equal numbers of particles; equal amounts in moles do.

**Worked example.** Which contains more atoms: 1.00 g of helium or 1.00 g of hydrogen gas?

- Helium is monatomic: n(He) = 1.00 ÷ 4.0 = 0.250 mol of atoms.
- Hydrogen is H₂: n(H₂) = 1.00 ÷ 2.0 = 0.500 mol of molecules = 1.00 mol of atoms.
- Hydrogen contains **four times** as many atoms.

The trap is to use 1.0 for hydrogen's molar mass, forgetting it is diatomic.

## Significant figures

Give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, or one more. The Avogadro constant (four figures) rarely limits precision; masses given to three figures usually mean a three-figure answer. Keep unrounded values in your calculator between steps.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the mole as "the mass of 6.022 × 10²³ particles". It is an amount, not a mass.
- Not specifying particles: confusing moles of O₂ with moles of O.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of ions or atoms per formula.
- Giving L without units, or writing "6.022 × 10²³ mol".
- Treating a molar mass as unitless, or a relative mass as having units.
- Dividing by L when you should multiply, which gives impossibly small numbers of particles.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: "the amount of substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant)".
- **Use**: write n = m ÷ M and N = n × L, then substitute with units.
- Label each amount with its particle: "0.500 mol H₂O molecules", "1.50 mol atoms".
- Check the size: a few grams of substance contains around 10²² to 10²⁴ particles.
