## Reading non-fiction

**Naming a technique earns nothing.** The mark is for the **effect** — specific to this word, in this text, for this audience and purpose.

Compare:

- Weak: *"The writer uses emotive language, which makes it more effective."*
- Strong: *"'Abandoned' positions the reader to see the residents as victims of neglect rather than of misfortune, quietly assigning blame without naming anyone."*

**The pattern that scores: quote → what it suggests → why the writer chose it.**

Keep quotations **short**. A two-word quotation embedded in your own sentence shows more control than a copied line.

**Reading questions progress in three stages**: literal comprehension (identifying explicit information), inference (working out implied meaning), and language/structural analysis (explaining the effect of specific word choices or organisation). Treat these as genuinely different skills — a candidate confident at identifying facts can still lose marks on an inference or analysis question if they don't shift approach accordingly.

## Features worth commenting on

- **Emotive and connotative word choice** — what associations does it carry?
- **Statistics and expert quotation** — lend authority, but consider whose.
- **Anecdote** — makes an abstract issue personal and harder to dismiss.
- **Rhetorical questions** — presuppose agreement, making disagreement feel unreasonable.
- **Direct address (*you*)** — creates involvement and implied obligation.
- **Rule of three, contrast, repetition** — structural emphasis.
- **Sentence variation** — a short sentence after long ones lands hard.

**Structure matters too:** where does the writer place the strongest point? What does the opening do, and what does the ending leave the reader with?

## Comparison questions

Compare **how** the writers achieve their effects, not just what they say.

Use a **point-by-point** structure rather than describing each text in turn — write about the same feature in both, then move on. That produces genuine comparison rather than two separate accounts.

**Comparative connectives** do real work: *whereas*, *similarly*, *by contrast*, *in the same way*. Every paragraph should refer to both texts.

## Transactional writing

Fix three things before writing: **form, audience, purpose**.

| Form | Register | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Letter** | As directed | Correct opening and sign-off |
| **Speech** | Persuasive, direct | Address the audience; rhetorical questions |
| **Article** | Engaging, semi-formal | Headline, hook, structured paragraphs |
| **Report** | Formal, impersonal | Headings, factual tone, recommendations |
| **Review** | Personal but informed | Judgement with justification |

**Use the given material but develop it** — add consequences, implications and counter-arguments in your own words. Copying from the source caps the mark.

**Deploy the same techniques you analyse in the reading section**: rhetorical questions, direct address, the rule of three, contrast, a well-placed short sentence. Using them deliberately is what lifts the writing mark.

**A transactional writing task specifies purpose, audience and form in the question itself**, and strong answers reflect all three consistently throughout, not just in an opening line. A letter to a newspaper editor needs a different register and structure from a speech to a school assembly, even where both are persuasive in purpose — the audience and form change how that purpose should actually be expressed. Briefly noting the specified purpose, audience and form before writing, and checking the finished response against that note, catches the most common way marks are lost: drifting into a generic essay-like register regardless of what form was actually asked for.

## Technical accuracy

Marks are available for accuracy, and the same errors recur:

- **Comma splice** — two complete sentences joined by a comma. Use a full stop, semicolon or conjunction.
- its / it's, their / there / they're, your / you're.
- Apostrophes for plurals.
- Inconsistent tense.
- Paragraphs that do not change with time, place, topic or speaker.

**Plan for five minutes and check for three.** Both are worth more than the extra content those minutes would have bought.

## Exam traps

- Naming techniques without effects.
- Long quotations used as padding.
- Comparing content rather than method.
- Writing about one text and then the other instead of point by point.
- Ignoring form, audience or purpose.
- Lifting from the source in transactional writing.
