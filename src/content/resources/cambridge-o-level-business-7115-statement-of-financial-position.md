This guide goes deeper into section **5.4 Statement of financial position** of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus asks for two things:

- **5.4.1** The main elements of a statement of financial position: the main classifications of assets and liabilities, using examples.
- **5.4.2** Interpret a simple statement of financial position and make deductions from it, for example how a business is financing its activities and what assets it owns, and the sale of inventories to raise finance.

The syllabus also states that **constructing statements of financial position will not be assessed**. Expect to be given one and asked to classify items, read totals, compare years and explain what the figures show. ## What the statement shows

A statement of financial position is a snapshot of a business **on one date**. It answers three questions:

1. What does the business own? (**assets**)
2. What does it owe to outsiders? (**liabilities**)
3. How much of the business belongs to the owners? (**equity**, or owners' funds)

Because everything the business owns has been paid for either by the owners or by lenders, the two sides always balance: assets − liabilities = equity.

## Classifying items: the one-year test

Assets and liabilities are split by time.

| Classification | Test | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | kept and used for more than a year | land, buildings, machinery, vehicles, computers, fixtures |
| Current assets | expected to be turned into cash, or used up, within a year | inventories, receivables (money owed by customers), cash |
| Current liabilities | must be paid within a year | payables (money owed to suppliers), bank overdraft, tax due |
| Non-current liabilities | repayable after more than a year | long-term bank loans, mortgages |
| Equity | the owners' stake | share capital, retained profit |

### Harder cases examiners like

- **The same item can change class.** A delivery van owned by a removals firm is a non-current asset. Vans held for sale by a van dealer are its inventory, a current asset. Classify by how **this** business uses the item.
- **An overdraft is a current liability**, even if the business has had one for years, because the bank can ask for it to be repaid at short notice.
- **A loan is a liability, but the cash it brings is an asset.** Taking a $50,000 loan adds $50,000 to cash and $50,000 to liabilities, so equity does not change.
- **Inventories are not cash.** They only become cash when sold, and may have to be sold at a discount.
- **Retained profit is not a bank balance.** It is part of the owners' stake and may already be spent on machinery or stock.

## Making deductions: four questions

When you are given a statement, work through these questions in order.

1. **What does it own?** Is most of its value in non-current assets (a factory, a hotel) or in current assets (a wholesaler's stock)? Does this fit the type of business?
2. **How is it financed?** Compare equity with non-current liabilities. A business funded mainly by loans has interest and repayments to meet whatever happens to profit.
3. **Can it pay short-term debts?** Compare current assets with current liabilities. Then look inside current assets: how much is cash and receivables, and how much is inventory?
4. **What has changed?** If there are two years, find the biggest changes and link them: a rise in non-current assets is often matched by a rise in loans; a rise in retained profit points to a profitable year.

## Worked example 1: comparing two years

Fennick Builders' Merchants, statements of financial position ($):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Premises | 200,000 | 200,000 |
| Vehicles | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Warehouse equipment | 0 | 70,000 |
| **Non-current assets** | **240,000** | **310,000** |
| Inventories | 50,000 | 90,000 |
| Receivables | 20,000 | 22,000 |
| Cash | 15,000 | 3,000 |
| **Current assets** | **85,000** | **115,000** |
| Payables | 30,000 | 45,000 |
| Bank overdraft | 0 | 12,000 |
| **Current liabilities** | **30,000** | **57,000** |
| Bank loan (non-current) | 60,000 | 110,000 |
| **Net assets** | **235,000** | **258,000** |
| Share capital | 150,000 | 150,000 |
| Retained profit | 85,000 | 108,000 |
| **Equity** | **235,000** | **258,000** |

**Deduction 1: what it bought and how.** Fennick bought $70,000 of warehouse equipment. Its loan rose by $50,000 and no new shares were issued, so the equipment was financed mainly by borrowing, with the rest coming from cash.

**Deduction 2: reliance on borrowing.** Long-term finance is equity plus non-current liabilities. In Year 1 the loan was 60,000 ÷ (235,000 + 60,000) = 20.3% of it. In Year 2 it is 110,000 ÷ (258,000 + 110,000) = 29.9%. Fennick now relies more on lenders.

**Deduction 3: short-term position.** Current assets still exceed current liabilities (working capital of $55,000 in Year 1 and $58,000 in Year 2), but the quality has worsened. Cash and receivables were $35,000 against $30,000 of current liabilities in Year 1; in Year 2 they are only $25,000 against $57,000. Fennick now depends on selling its inventory to pay its debts, and it has started using an overdraft.

**Deduction 4: performance.** Retained profit rose by $23,000, so the year was profitable. Profit did not stop cash falling, because the money went into equipment and stock.

The full answer to "what does this show?" combines these: a profitable business that has expanded using borrowing and has tied up cash in inventory, leaving it short of liquid funds.

## Worked example 2: selling inventories to raise finance

Fennick decides to sell $40,000 of its inventory (valued at cost) to raise cash. To sell quickly it offers a 25% discount.

- Cash raised = 40,000 × 75% = $30,000.
- Inventories fall from $90,000 to $50,000.
- The $10,000 difference is a loss, which reduces retained profit from $108,000 to $98,000.
- Fennick uses $12,000 of the cash to clear the overdraft. Cash becomes 3,000 + 30,000 − 12,000 = $21,000.

New current assets: 50,000 + 22,000 + 21,000 = $93,000. New current liabilities: $45,000. Net assets: 310,000 + 93,000 − 45,000 − 110,000 = $248,000, matching equity of 150,000 + 98,000.

**Evaluation.** Selling inventory is quick and needs no interest or new owners, and here it removes the overdraft. But selling at a discount reduces profit, and if Fennick runs low on stock it may lose builders who need materials immediately. It is a sensible short-term step if the stock was more than customers needed.

## Exam technique for the 7115 command words

- **Identify**: name the classification or the item (for example, "receivables are a current asset").
- **Define**: precise meaning plus the time test where relevant.
- **Explain**: make a deduction and say why it matters to the business.
- **Consider**: weigh what the statement shows against what it cannot show (it is one date only and gives no profit or cash flows for the period).

## Common mistakes

- Calling an overdraft a non-current liability.
- Listing a loan as an asset.
- Treating inventories or retained profit as if they were cash.
- Describing the statement line by line instead of making deductions.
- Ignoring the type of business when judging whether its assets are "too high".
- Forgetting the statement is a snapshot: the position could be very different a month later.
