## Questions

**1.** Define price elasticity of supply. **[2]**

**2.** In the fictional town of Harlow Cove, a baker raises the price of a pastry from 25 to 30 vels. Quantity supplied rises from 400 to 448 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the PES. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether supply is elastic or inelastic and give a reason. **[1]**

**3.** A fictional tile maker has a PES of 1.4. The price of tiles falls by 5%. It currently supplies 1,500 tiles a month. Calculate the new quantity supplied. **[3]**

**4.** A fictional tea farm has a PES of 0.5 and sells at 50 vels a kilogram. The farm wants to raise quantity supplied by 9%. Calculate the price needed. **[3]**

**5.** Sketch and label supply diagrams for (a) a perfectly inelastic supply curve, (b) a perfectly elastic supply curve and (c) unitary PES. Give the PES value for each. **[4]**

**6.** A fictional juice bottler has spare production lines and a cold store. Explain how each of these two determinants affects its PES. **[4]**

**7.** In a fictional market, price rises from 50 to 55 vels. Quantity supplied, starting from 1,000 units, changes as follows: 1,020 after one week, 1,090 after one year and 1,180 after five years.

**(a)** Work out the PES after one week, one year and five years. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why PES changes over time. **[3]**

**8.** In the fictional country of Nedrin, the price of both product X and product Y rises from 10 to 12 vels. Product X output rises from 300 to 312 units. Product Y output rises from 300 to 390 units.

**(a)** Calculate PES for each product. **[2]**
**(b)** Product X is cut by hand from a rare stone. Product Y is moulded in a plant with idle machines. Explain how this accounts for the figures. **[3]**

**9.** **(HL only)** Explain why the PES of primary commodities is generally lower than the PES of manufactured products. **[6]**

**10.** The table shows the fictional market for notebooks in Valdora. Demand rises from D1 to D2. S1 is the supply curve of one set of producers; S2 is that of another set in a separate fictional market with the same starting equilibrium.

| Price (vels) | Qd before (D1) | Qd after (D2) | Qs, S1 | Qs, S2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 300 | 420 | 300 | 300 |
| 11 | 280 | 400 | 320 | 310 |
| 12 | 260 | 380 | 340 | 320 |
| 13 | 240 | 360 | 360 | 330 |
| 14 | 220 | 340 | 380 | 340 |

**(a)** Identify the equilibrium price and quantity before the demand increase. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the new equilibrium price and quantity for S1 and for S2. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate PES between the old and new equilibrium for each. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the price rises more for S2. **[2]**

**11.** A small furniture workshop in the fictional town of Ashgrove makes chairs by hand. Discuss how far its supply is likely to be elastic in the short run. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** PES shows how far output reacts to a price change [1]. It is the proportionate change in output divided by the proportionate change in price, both as percentages [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show that you understand how output reacts to price; a bare formula with no meaning attached is a thinner answer.

**2. (a)** Price change = (30 − 25) / 25 × 100 = 20% [1]. Quantity change = (448 − 400) / 400 × 100 = 12% [1]. PES = 12 / 20 = **0.6** [1].
**(b)** PES is below 1, so supply is relatively inelastic [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* When a question says "calculate", show each percentage change; an unsupported answer is risky if you make a slip.

**3.** Percentage change in quantity = 1.4 × (−5%) = −7% [1]. Fall in quantity = 7% of 1,500 = 105 [1]. New quantity = 1,500 − 105 = **1,395 tiles** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the new quantity, not only the percentage change, because the question asks for the quantity.

**4.** Percentage change in price = 9 / 0.5 = 18% [1]. Price rise = 18% of 50 = 9 [1]. New price = 50 + 9 = **59 vels** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the rearranged formula so that your method is visible even if the arithmetic slips.

**5.** Axes labelled price and quantity supplied [1]. (a) A vertical line labelled S, PES = 0 [1]. (b) A horizontal line labelled S, PES = infinity [1]. (c) A straight upward line through the origin labelled S, PES = 1 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Label both axes and every curve; an unlabelled sketch shows less.

**6.** Spare production lines mean output can rise without new investment [1], so quantity supplied rises sharply when price rises and PES is higher [1]. The cold store lets the firm hold stock and release it when price rises [1], which raises PES because supply can respond immediately [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs a reason and a direction for each determinant; naming them alone does not meet the command term.

**7. (a)** Price change = 5 / 50 × 100 = 10% [1]. Week: 2% / 10% = 0.2; year: 9% / 10% = 0.9 [1]. Five years: 18% / 10% = **1.8** [1].
**(b)** Over a short time, inputs and capacity are fixed, so output cannot respond much [1]. As time passes, firms can hire, train and invest [1], so quantity supplied responds by a larger percentage than price [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), say what can be adjusted as time passes; the word "time" on its own adds nothing.

**8. (a)** Price rise = 20% for both [1]. X: 12 / 300 = 4%, so PES = 0.2. Y: 90 / 300 = 30%, so PES = **1.5** [1].
**(b)** Product X depends on a rare stone and hand skill, so the factors are scarce and not mobile [1], and PES is low (0.2) [1]. Product Y has idle machines, so output can rise quickly [1], which gives PES above 1. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to both products and to the figures you calculated; a general statement about determinants does not use the data.

**9.** Three developed reasons, two marks each (identify, then explain). Growing seasons and the time to develop a mine mean output cannot rise quickly [1], so supply responds slowly to a price rise [1]. Land and deposits are fixed and hard to switch between uses [1], so factors are immobile and PES is lower than for a factory that can switch machines [1]. Perishable goods are hard to store [1], so stocks cannot be released when the price rises, unlike most manufactured goods [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each reason to a determinant from the guide and contrast it with manufactured goods; a list of facts about farms without comparison is incomplete.

**10. (a)** Price 10 and quantity 300 [1].
**(b)** S1: price 13, quantity 360 [1]. S2: price 14, quantity 340 [1].
**(c)** S1: price +30%, quantity +20%, PES = 20 / 30 = **0.67** [1]. S2: price +40%, quantity +13.3%, PES = 13.3 / 40 = **0.33** [1]. Both are below 1, so both are inelastic [1].
**(d)** S2 is less elastic, so producers add fewer units for each price rise [1]. A larger price rise is needed to bring supply into line with the higher demand [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* State the equilibrium from the table as both a price and a quantity; giving only one is incomplete.

**11.** Credit any developed, balanced points, in a response with a judgement. A model plan follows. Short run means some inputs are fixed [1]. Handwork limits the speed at which output can rise because skilled labour is scarce and slow to train [1]. Tools and workspace may already be fully used, so there is little unused capacity [1]. Wood can be stored, so the workshop could build stock in advance, which raises PES a little [1]. Extra output may mean overtime and rising unit costs [1]. Materials might be easy to obtain, which helps [1]. A supported judgement, for example that supply is probably relatively inelastic in the short run but becomes more elastic over time, with a reason [2]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" is an AO3 command term, so an answer that only lists determinants without weighing them is unlikely to show the judgement the term asks for.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing the formula so that price is on top.
- Using the absolute change instead of the percentage change.
- Losing the negative sign on a price fall and reporting a negative PES.
- Calculating a number but not stating whether supply is elastic, inelastic or unitary.
- Sketching a diagram without axis labels or curve labels.
- Naming a determinant without showing which way it moves PES.
- Ignoring the supplied numbers when a question asks you to account for them.
- Leaving out the judgement in a "discuss" answer.
- Mixing up a movement along a supply curve with a shift of the curve.
