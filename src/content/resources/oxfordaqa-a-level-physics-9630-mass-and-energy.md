## E = mc² for every energy change

Whenever a system gains energy ΔE, its mass increases by Δm = ΔE/c². This is true for a stretched spring, a hot cup of tea or a battery being charged, not only for nuclei. The reason it is noticed only in nuclear physics is the size of the effect.

### Worked example 1: heating water

1.0 kg of water (c = 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) is heated from 20 °C to 100 °C.

ΔE = mcΔθ = 1.0 × 4180 × 80 = 3.34 × 10⁵ J
Δm = ΔE/c² = 3.34 × 10⁵ / (3.00 × 10⁸)² = **3.7 × 10⁻¹² kg**

That is a fractional change of about 4 × 10⁻¹², far below anything a balance can detect. In a nuclear reaction the fractional change is typically 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴.

## Mass defect and binding energy

A nucleus has **less mass** than the protons and neutrons that make it up. The difference is the **mass defect**:

mass defect = (Z m_p + N m_n) − m_nucleus

The **binding energy** is the energy equivalent of the mass defect: the minimum energy needed to separate the nucleus into its free nucleons. Equally, it is the energy released when the nucleus forms from free nucleons. **Binding energy per nucleon** (binding energy ÷ A) measures how tightly each nucleon is held.

### Worked example 2: helium-4

The nuclear mass of helium-4 is 4.001506 u.

Mass of free nucleons = 2 × 1.007276 + 2 × 1.008665 = 4.031882 u
Mass defect = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u
Binding energy = 0.030376 × 931.5 = **28.3 MeV**; per nucleon = 28.3/4 = **7.07 MeV**

## Atomic masses and the electron correction

Data books usually list **atomic** masses, which include the electrons. You must decide whether the electrons matter.

- **Binding energy:** subtract Z electron masses from the atomic mass to get the nuclear mass before finding the mass defect.
- **Reaction energies:** if the number of electrons is the same on both sides (the proton numbers balance), the electron masses cancel and atomic masses can be used directly.

### Worked example 3: iron-56 from its atomic mass

The atomic mass of iron-56 (Z = 26, N = 30) is 55.934936 u.

Nuclear mass = 55.934936 − 26 × 0.000549 = 55.920662 u
Free nucleons = 26 × 1.007276 + 30 × 1.008665 = 56.449126 u
Mass defect = 56.449126 − 55.920662 = 0.528464 u
Binding energy = 0.528464 × 931.5 = **492.3 MeV**; per nucleon = **8.79 MeV**

Forgetting the correction would add 26 × 0.000549 × 931.5 = 13.3 MeV of error, about 3%.

## Energy released from a mass difference

For any nuclear change, energy released = (total mass before − total mass after) × 931.5 MeV per u. A positive answer means energy is released, mostly as kinetic energy of the products.

### Worked example 4: an alpha decay

Polonium-210 decays: ²¹⁰Po → ²⁰⁶Pb + ⁴He. Atomic masses: Po-210 209.982874 u, Pb-206 205.974465 u, He-4 4.002603 u.

Electrons: 84 on the left, 82 + 2 = 84 on the right, so atomic masses can be used.
Mass difference = 209.982874 − (205.974465 + 4.002603) = 0.005806 u
Energy released = 0.005806 × 931.5 = **5.41 MeV**

The polonium nucleus starts at rest, so the alpha particle and the lead nucleus move apart with equal and opposite momentum. Kinetic energy p²/2m is shared in inverse proportion to mass, so the alpha particle takes 206/210 of the energy: **5.31 MeV**. The heavy daughter recoils with only about 0.10 MeV.

## Working from binding energies

When binding energies are given instead of masses, use

energy released = total binding energy of the products − total binding energy of the reactants

A free neutron or proton has zero binding energy.

### Worked example 5: breeding tritium

Lithium-6 absorbing a neutron gives helium-4 and tritium: ⁶Li + n → ⁴He + ³H. Binding energies: Li-6 31.99 MeV, He-4 28.30 MeV, H-3 8.48 MeV.

Energy released = (28.30 + 8.48) − (31.99 + 0) = **4.79 MeV**

This reaction is how tritium can be made for fusion fuel, which links section 3.12.2 to section 3.12.5.

## The binding energy per nucleon curve

The curve rises steeply from hydrogen-2 (about 1.1 MeV) to helium-4 (about 7.1 MeV), reaches a broad peak of about 8.8 MeV near iron-56, then falls slowly to about 7.6 MeV for uranium.

- Nuclei to the **left** of the peak can release energy by **fusion**: joining them climbs the steep slope.
- Nuclei to the **right** of the peak can release energy by **fission**: splitting them moves the fragments up the gentle slope towards the peak.
- Nuclei near the peak are the most tightly bound and cannot release energy by either process.

### Fission and fusion compared per nucleon

| Process | Energy per reaction | Nucleons involved | Energy per nucleon |
|---|---|---|---|
| D–T fusion | 17.6 MeV | 5 | 3.5 MeV |
| Uranium-235 fission | about 200 MeV | 236 | 0.85 MeV |

Each fission releases more energy, but fusion releases about four times as much per nucleon, and so per kilogram of fuel. The steep slope on the left of the curve is the reason: a small change in A there gives a large change in binding energy per nucleon.

## Common mistakes

- Subtracting the wrong way: mass defect is nucleons minus nucleus, and energy released is before minus after.
- Using atomic masses for binding energy without removing the electrons.
- Removing electrons when they already cancel in a balanced reaction.
- Multiplying u by c² with c in m s⁻¹ and then calling the answer MeV. Use 931.5 MeV per u, or convert u to kg first.
- Saying binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus that is "released when it is broken up". Breaking a nucleus up needs energy; binding energy is what must be supplied.
- Reading the curve as total binding energy. It shows binding energy **per nucleon**.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** lay out "mass before", "mass after", "difference" on separate lines with five or six decimal places in u, then convert.
- **Explain** why energy is released: refer to an increase in binding energy per nucleon, and so a decrease in mass.
- **Identify** regions on the graph: name the peak near A = 56 and say which side gives fission and which fusion.
- **Show that:** carry extra figures until the last line; rounding masses early can wipe out the small mass difference.
