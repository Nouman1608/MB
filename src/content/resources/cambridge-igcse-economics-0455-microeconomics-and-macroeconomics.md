Every country, firm and figure below is invented for practice.

## 2.1.1 Microeconomics

**Microeconomics** is the study of the decisions of individual economic agents (households, workers and firms) and of individual markets.

Microeconomic questions look at one part of the economy at a time:

- Why has the price of coffee risen?
- How many workers will a clothing factory employ?
- Will a household spend a pay rise or save it?
- Why are nurses paid less than lawyers?
- What happens to sales of buses if a tax is placed on cars?

### Micro decision makers

| Decision maker | Typical micro decisions | Where 0455 develops this |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers and households | What to buy, how much to save, how much to borrow | Demand (2.3); households (3.2) |
| Workers | Which job to take, how many hours to work | Workers (3.3) |
| Trade unions | What pay and conditions to negotiate for their members | Trade unions (3.4) |
| Firms (producers) | What and how much to produce, what price to charge, which methods to use, how many workers to hire | Supply (2.4); firms (3.5 to 3.8) |
| Government, acting in one market | A tax on one product, a subsidy for one industry, rules for one market | Mixed economic system (2.11) |

The government appears here when it acts on a particular market. A tax on sugary drinks is a micro decision because its main effect is on the price and quantity in one market.

## 2.1.2 Macroeconomics

**Macroeconomics** is the study of the economy as a whole. It looks at totals and averages for the whole country: total output, the general price level, the total number of people out of work and the country's trade with the rest of the world.

Macroeconomic questions include:

- Why has the country's total output (GDP) fallen this year?
- Why are prices in general rising faster than last year?
- How can the government reduce unemployment?
- Why is the country spending more on imports than it earns from exports?

### Macro decision makers

| Decision maker | Typical macro decisions |
|---|---|
| Government | Total spending and taxation (fiscal policy), supply-side measures, trade policy |
| Central bank | Interest rates and the money supply (monetary policy) |
| Households, firms and foreign buyers, taken together | Total spending in the economy, total saving, total exports and imports |

The government's macroeconomic aims, developed in section 4 of the syllabus, are economic growth, full employment (low unemployment), stable prices (low inflation), balance of payments stability, and the redistribution of income.

## The difference: scale, not subject

The same household or firm can appear in both branches. What changes is the scale of the question.

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Individual agents and individual markets | The whole economy |
| Price studied | The price of one product | The general price level (inflation) |
| Employment studied | Jobs and wages in one firm or one occupation | Total unemployment and the unemployment rate |
| Output studied | Output of one firm or one industry | Total output (GDP) and economic growth |
| Main decision makers | Households, workers, trade unions, firms; government in one market | Government, central bank; all households and firms together |
| Example policy | A tax on one good | A change in income tax rates or interest rates for the whole economy |

### Worked example: one event, two lenses

A drought in Country Marava (invented) cuts the wheat harvest by 25%.

**Micro analysis.**

1. In the wheat market, supply falls, so the price of wheat rises.
2. Bakeries face higher costs and may raise the price of bread.
3. Some consumers switch to rice; wheat farmers with crops left may earn more per tonne.

**Macro analysis.**

1. Wheat is 8% of Marava's total output. If the harvest falls by 25% and nothing else changes, total output falls by 0.25 × 8% = **2%**.
2. Higher food prices raise the general price level, so inflation rises.
3. If Marava imports wheat to replace the lost harvest, spending on imports rises, which affects the balance of payments.

Both analyses are correct. The question tells you which one the examiner wants: words such as "market", "firm", "consumers" or "price of" point to micro; words such as "economy", "inflation", "unemployment rate", "GDP" or "government's aims" point to macro.

### How the two branches link

- **Macro totals are built from micro decisions.** Total spending in the economy is the sum of what millions of households, firms, the government and foreign buyers decide to spend.
- **Macro conditions shape micro decisions.** When the central bank raises interest rates, each household faces a higher cost of borrowing, and each firm must decide whether an investment is still worth making.
- **Micro problems can become macro problems.** If one large industry shuts down, its job losses can raise the national unemployment rate.

### Worked example: an aggregate figure

Country Marava has three industries. Their output in a year (in millions, invented) is: farming 120, manufacturing 300, services 580.

1. Total output = 120 + 300 + 580 = **1,000 million**.
2. Services' share = 580 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **58%**.
3. If manufacturing output rises by 10% and the others are unchanged, total output rises by 30 million, which is 30 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **3%**.

Looking at manufacturing alone (a 10% rise) is micro; looking at the effect on the total (3%) is macro.

## Classifying statements: a method

1. Ask: is the statement about one market, one product, one firm or one group of workers? If so, it is micro.
2. Ask: is it about a total or average for the whole economy, or about a policy aimed at the whole economy? If so, it is macro.
3. Name the decision maker: households, workers, trade unions and firms usually point to micro; government and central bank acting on the whole economy point to macro.
4. Watch for traps. "The government taxes cigarettes" is micro (one market). "The government raises income tax" is macro (the whole economy). "Unemployment among car workers" is micro; "the national unemployment rate" is macro.

## Common mistakes

- Saying micro is about "small" things and macro about "big" things without naming individual markets versus the whole economy.
- Treating every government action as macro. A tax on one product is a micro decision.
- Forgetting the central bank as a macro decision maker.
- Classifying a statement by the size of the firm. A very large firm's pricing decision is still micro.
- Listing decision makers without saying what decisions they make.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "Microeconomics is the study of individual decision makers and individual markets"; "Macroeconomics is the study of the economy as a whole."
- **Identify:** pick out the micro or macro item from a list and give the reason in a few words.
- **Explain the difference:** set the two side by side on the same feature (price of one good versus general price level), and name a decision maker for each.
- **Analyse:** in a source-based question, trace a micro event (one industry) through to a macro outcome (national unemployment, inflation or output).
- **Calculate:** shares and contributions to totals, as in the Marava example.
