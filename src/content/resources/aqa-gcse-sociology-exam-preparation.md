AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) is assessed through two written papers, not tiered: Paper 1 (The Sociology
of Families and Education, 1h45, 100 marks, 50%) and Paper 2 (The Sociology of Crime and Deviance and
Social Stratification, 1h45, 100 marks, 50%), both also drawing on social theory and research
methodology content from across the whole course. These notes turn that structure into an exam-day
plan, alongside the
[Families and Education](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-families-education-revision-notes/) topic
revision notes and [Crime and Stratification](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-crime-and-stratification/)
study guide already on the site.

## Theory and methods are not confined to one paper

Because both papers draw on relevant social theory and research methodology content from across the
whole course, revising theory (for example, functionalism, Marxism, feminism) and methods (for
example, questionnaires, interviews, sampling) only alongside one paper's named topic under-prepares
the other. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise core sociological theories and research methods as
their own standalone strand, then practise applying that strand to both Paper 1's and Paper 2's named
topics, rather than treating theory and methods as belonging only to whichever topic they were first
taught alongside.

## Command words and how much detail they expect

**Identify**, **name** and **give** require a short, precise recall answer. **Describe** requires an
accurate account of a sociological concept, study or process without needing to explain why. **Explain**
requires a reasoned account of why a sociological pattern or process occurs, often supported by a
named theory or study. **Evaluate**, **discuss** and **to what extent** require weighing more than one
sociological perspective or piece of evidence to reach a justified judgement.

## Applying a sociological concept to an unfamiliar scenario

Higher-tariff questions frequently present a scenario or a set of data (for example, statistics on
educational attainment) and ask students to apply a named sociological concept or theory to explain
it. **Exam-preparation priority**: for every key concept and theory revised, practise applying it to
a scenario or a piece of data that was not its original textbook example, since this application step
-- not recall of the concept's definition alone -- is where the higher-tariff marks are concentrated.

## Worked practice scenario: an evaluate-style answer plan

Question: "Evaluate the functionalist explanation of the role of education in society."

```
Step 1 - state the functionalist explanation and its key claim:
Functionalists (e.g. Durkheim) argue education performs positive
functions for society -- socialising individuals into shared values,
teaching skills needed for work, and sorting/allocating people into
appropriate roles based on ability.

Step 2 - support with a named study or evidence:
Cite a named study or statistic supporting this view, e.g. evidence
that schools formally teach shared societal norms through the
curriculum and school rules.

Step 3 - evaluate using a contrasting perspective (not repetition):
Contrast with a Marxist or feminist critique -- for example, that
schools do not simply reflect merit-based sorting but instead
reproduce existing class or gender inequalities (a criticism
functionalism does not adequately address).

Step 4 - reach a judgement:
State briefly how convincing the functionalist explanation is overall,
given both the supporting evidence and the contrasting perspective's
critique.
```

This four-step structure keeps the answer from staying inside a single perspective (Step 3) and from
stopping at description (Steps 1-2) -- exactly the combination "evaluate" is testing, and the same
structure that Paper 1's and Paper 2's other perspective-based essay questions reuse.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: revise core sociological theories and research methods as a standalone
  strand, then practise applying them to both papers' named topics; practise applying key concepts to
  scenarios or data that are not the original textbook example; practise full "evaluate" answers that
  bring in a contrasting perspective before reaching a judgement.
- **During Paper 1**: be ready to draw on theory and methods content even though the paper is titled
  around Families and Education specifically.
- **During Paper 2**: be ready to draw on theory and methods content even though the paper is titled
  around Crime, Deviance and Stratification specifically.
- **On every paper**: for "evaluate"-type questions, check your answer includes a contrasting
  perspective and a final judgement, not just a one-sided explanation.

## Self-test

1. Why should theory and methods content not be revised only alongside the topic it was first taught
   with?
2. What does "evaluate" require that "describe" does not?
3. Why is applying a concept to an unfamiliar scenario a distinct skill from recalling its
   definition?
4. In the worked scenario, why does the answer bring in a Marxist or feminist critique rather than
   stopping after Step 2?

**Answers:** 1. Because both papers explicitly draw on relevant social theory and research
methodology content from across the whole course, not just the paper's own named topic. 2. A reasoned,
weighed judgement bringing in more than one sociological perspective or piece of evidence, rather than
a straightforward accurate account without explanation. 3. Because higher-tariff questions frequently
present a scenario or data set the student has not seen paired with that concept before, testing
whether the concept can be applied flexibly, not just recalled from its original textbook example. 4.
Because a strong "evaluate" answer tests AO3-style judgement, which requires weighing the
functionalist explanation against a genuinely contrasting perspective before reaching a conclusion --
stopping after describing and supporting only the functionalist view would leave the answer
one-sided and not fully evaluative.
