This guide covers subtopic **7.3 Preparation of salts** in Topic 7, Acids, bases and salts, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The [acids, bases and salts study guide](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/) gives the general four-step recipe and the solubility rules. This page turns that into full, mark-earning method descriptions for each route, explains the purpose of every step, shows how to choose reactants for a precipitation, and works through a planning question. Then revise with the [preparation of salts revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-preparation-of-salts-revision-notes/) and practise with the [preparation of salts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-preparation-of-salts-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates):

- Describe the preparation, separation and purification of soluble salts by reaction of an acid with (a) an alkali by titration, (b) excess metal, (c) excess insoluble base, (d) excess insoluble carbonate.
- Describe the general solubility rules for salts.
- Define a hydrated substance as one that is chemically combined with water, and an anhydrous substance as one containing no water.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- Describe the preparation of insoluble salts by precipitation.
- Define water of crystallisation as the water molecules present in hydrated crystals, including CuSO₄·5H₂O and CoCl₂·6H₂O.

Note the three verbs in the Core outcome: *preparation* (the reaction), *separation* (filtering), and *purification* (crystallising, washing, drying). A full-mark method covers all three.

## Choosing a method: three questions

1. **Is the salt soluble?** Use the solubility rules. If not, use precipitation (Extended).
2. **If soluble, is the other reactant soluble?** If it is an alkali, both reactants dissolve, so you cannot filter off an excess: use titration.
3. **If the other reactant is an insoluble solid** (a suitable metal, an insoluble base or an insoluble carbonate), add it in excess and filter off what is left.

**The solubility rules**

- Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble.
- Nitrates are soluble.
- Chlorides are soluble, except lead and silver.
- Sulfates are soluble, except barium, calcium and lead.
- Carbonates are insoluble, except sodium, potassium and ammonium.
- Hydroxides are insoluble, except sodium, potassium, ammonium and calcium (partially).

## Method 1: excess insoluble base

**Example: copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide.**

```
CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l)
```

1. Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker. *Warming speeds up the reaction.*
2. Add copper(II) oxide a little at a time, stirring, until some black solid remains undissolved. *Excess solid shows all the acid has reacted, so the salt will not be contaminated with acid.*
3. Filter. *Removes the excess copper(II) oxide; the blue filtrate is copper(II) sulfate solution.*
4. Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water until it is saturated. *Check by dipping in a glass rod: crystals forming on the rod as it cools show the solution is ready.*
5. Leave to cool so crystals form. *The salt is less soluble when cold.*
6. Filter off the crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water and dry between filter papers. *Washing removes the remaining solution; drying gently keeps the water of crystallisation.*

## Method 2: excess metal

**Example: zinc sulfate from zinc.**

```
Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)
```

Add zinc granules to dilute sulfuric acid until the **fizzing stops** and some zinc remains. Then filter and crystallise as in Method 1.

The metal must be suitable. Very reactive metals such as sodium and potassium react dangerously with acids; unreactive metals such as copper and silver do not react with dilute acids at all. Magnesium, zinc and iron are suitable.

## Method 3: excess insoluble carbonate

**Example: magnesium chloride from magnesium carbonate.**

```
MgCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
```

Add the carbonate until **effervescence stops** and solid remains. The end of fizzing is a useful sign that the acid is used up. Filter and crystallise.

## Method 4: titration with an alkali

**Example: sodium sulfate from sodium hydroxide.**

```
2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)
```

Both reactants are soluble, so an excess could not be filtered off. Instead the exact volumes are found:

1. Use a volumetric pipette to put 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide into a conical flask; add a few drops of indicator (for example, methyl orange).
2. Add the acid from a burette until the indicator just changes colour. Record the volume.
3. Repeat with the same volumes of alkali and acid **without the indicator**. *The indicator would contaminate the salt.*
4. Evaporate and crystallise as in Method 1. No filtering of excess solid is needed, because nothing was added in excess.

## Precipitation of insoluble salts (Supplement)

To make an insoluble salt, mix two **solutions**: one containing the salt's cation, the other its anion. The insoluble salt forms as a solid precipitate.

**Choosing reactants.** Use the solubility rules in reverse. A nitrate of the metal supplies the cation (all nitrates are soluble); a sodium or potassium salt supplies the anion (all are soluble).

**Example: barium sulfate.**

```
BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
Ba2+(aq) + SO4 2-(aq) → BaSO4(s)
```

**Example: lead(II) iodide** (a yellow precipitate).

```
Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)
```

Method:

1. Mix the two solutions; a precipitate forms.
2. Filter to collect the precipitate as the residue.
3. Wash with distilled water to remove the soluble by-product (such as sodium chloride).
4. Dry in a warm oven or between filter papers.

There is no evaporation or crystallisation: the salt was never in solution.

## Hydrated and anhydrous salts

A **hydrated** substance is chemically combined with water; an **anhydrous** substance contains no water. In hydrated crystals, the water molecules are the **water of crystallisation** (Extended), written after a dot in the formula:

| Hydrated | Colour | Anhydrous | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| CuSO₄·5H₂O | Blue | CuSO₄ | White |
| CoCl₂·6H₂O | Pink | CoCl₂ | Blue |

This is why crystals are dried gently. Strong heating drives off the water of crystallisation and changes the product.

## Worked example: planning question

*Suggest a method to prepare each salt: (a) potassium nitrate, (b) zinc chloride, (c) silver chloride. Give a reason for each choice.*

- **(a) Potassium nitrate: titration** of potassium hydroxide with nitric acid. The salt is soluble and potassium hydroxide is an alkali (soluble), so the exact volumes must be measured.
- **(b) Zinc chloride: excess zinc, zinc oxide or zinc carbonate** with hydrochloric acid. The salt is soluble and each of these solids can be added in excess and filtered off.
- **(c) Silver chloride: precipitation** (Extended), mixing silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution. Silver chloride is insoluble.

## Common mistakes

- **Not saying "in excess"** for the solid reactant, or not saying why.
- **Filtering after crystallising** instead of before. Filter off the excess solid first; then crystallise the filtrate.
- **Evaporating to dryness.** This can decompose the salt or remove water of crystallisation; heat only until saturated.
- **Leaving indicator in the titration product.**
- **Choosing an insoluble reactant for a precipitation.** Both starting materials must be in solution.
- **Using copper metal with dilute acid.** It does not react.
- **Forgetting to wash a precipitate.**

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** a preparation: number your steps, name the reagents and apparatus, and include filtering and crystallising (or washing and drying for a precipitate).
- **Explain** a step: give its purpose, e.g. "excess to make sure all the acid reacts".
- **Suggest** a method: name it and justify with solubility.
- **Define** hydrated, anhydrous and water of crystallisation using the syllabus wording.
