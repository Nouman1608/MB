## Syllabus coverage

Subtopic 1.1 has seven learning outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

1. understand that atoms are mostly empty space around a very small, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons in shells around it;
2. identify and describe protons, neutrons and electrons by relative charge and relative mass;
3. understand the terms atomic (proton) number and mass (nucleon) number;
4. describe the distribution of mass and charge within an atom;
5. describe the behaviour of beams of protons, neutrons and electrons moving at the same velocity in an electric field;
6. determine the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in atoms and ions;
7. state and explain qualitatively the variations in atomic and ionic radius across a period and down a group.

## The scale of the atom

An atom has a radius of the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m; its nucleus has a radius of the order of 10⁻¹⁵ m. The atom is therefore roughly 100 000 times wider than its nucleus. If a nucleus were the size of a marble, the atom would be about a kilometre across, and nearly all of that volume would contain only electrons.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | 1/1836 | Shells around the nucleus |

**Distribution of mass and charge.** Protons and neutrons each have about 1836 times the mass of an electron, so more than 99.9 % of an atom's mass is in the nucleus. All the positive charge is in the nucleus too. The negative charge is spread through the much larger volume occupied by the electrons. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges balance overall.

## Counting particles in atoms and ions

Use three rules for a species written as mass number, symbol and charge:

- protons = proton (atomic) number, which identifies the element;
- neutrons = mass (nucleon) number − proton number;
- electrons = protons − charge (so a 2+ ion has two fewer electrons than protons, and a 2− ion has two more).

### Worked example: simple ions

| Species | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ | 26 | 56 − 26 = 30 | 26 − 3 = 23 |
| ³²S²⁻ | 16 | 16 | 16 + 2 = 18 |
| ²⁷Al³⁺ | 13 | 14 | 10 |

### Worked example: polyatomic ions

For a polyatomic ion, add the particles of every atom, then adjust the electrons for the overall charge. Take the ammonium ion made of ¹⁴N and ¹H atoms.

1. Protons: N has 7, each H has 1, so 7 + 4 × 1 = 11.
2. Neutrons: ¹⁴N has 7, ¹H has 0, so 7.
3. Electrons: 11 protons and an overall charge of 1+, so 11 − 1 = 10.

For the hydroxide ion made of ¹⁶O and ¹H: protons 8 + 1 = 9, neutrons 8, electrons 9 + 1 = 10. Ammonium and hydroxide therefore both have 10 electrons, the same number as a neon atom.

### Worked example: identifying a species from its particles

A species has 20 neutrons, 18 electrons and a charge of 1+. Identify it.

1. A 1+ charge means one more proton than electrons: 18 + 1 = 19 protons.
2. Proton number 19 is potassium.
3. Mass number = 19 + 20 = 39, so the species is ³⁹K⁺.

## Beams in an electric field

When beams of particles moving at the same velocity pass between charged plates:

- the **direction** of deflection depends on the sign of the charge: positive particles bend towards the negative plate, negative particles towards the positive plate, and neutral particles pass straight through;
- the **size** of deflection is proportional to the charge-to-mass ratio, because the sideways force depends on charge and the resulting acceleration depends on mass.

| Particle | Charge ÷ mass (relative) | Deflection |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 ÷ 1 = +1 | Small, towards negative plate |
| Electron | −1 ÷ (1/1836) = −1836 | Very large, towards positive plate |
| Neutron | 0 | None |

The electron's deflection is about 1836 times that of the proton, in the opposite direction. Diagrams in answers should show the electron curving much more sharply.

### Worked example: comparing ions

Rank these, moving at the same velocity, by size of deflection: ¹H⁺, ²H⁺, ⁴He²⁺ and ³He⁺. Treat each mass as the mass number, because electron masses are negligible.

1. ¹H⁺: 1 ÷ 1 = 1.
2. ²H⁺: 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5.
3. ⁴He²⁺: 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5.
4. ³He⁺: 1 ÷ 3 = 0.33.

Order from most deflected: ¹H⁺, then ²H⁺ and ⁴He²⁺ equally, then ³He⁺. All bend towards the negative plate. The equal deflection of ²H⁺ and ⁴He²⁺ shows why you must use the ratio rather than mass or charge alone.

## Atomic radius: what drives the trends

Three factors decide how strongly the nucleus holds the outer electrons, and so the size of the atom:

- **nuclear charge** (number of protons): more protons pull the electrons closer;
- **number of shells**: each extra shell puts the outer electrons further out;
- **shielding** by inner shells: inner electrons repel the outer ones, reducing the attraction they feel.

| Period 3 element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius / nm (typical data values) | 0.186 | 0.160 | 0.143 | 0.117 | 0.110 | 0.104 | 0.099 |

Across Period 3 the outer electrons are all in the third shell and the inner shells (2,8) are the same, so shielding is roughly constant while nuclear charge rises from 11 to 17. The outer electrons are pulled in, and the radius falls by almost half. Argon is usually left out, because the radius measured for a noble gas is not of the same kind as that for an element that bonds.

Down a group, each element has one more shell. The rise in nuclear charge is outweighed by the greater distance and extra shielding, so the radius increases.

## Ionic radius and the isoelectronic series

Species with the same number of electrons are **isoelectronic**. Comparing them isolates the effect of nuclear charge, because the electron arrangement is identical.

| Ion | N³⁻ | O²⁻ | F⁻ | Na⁺ | Mg²⁺ | Al³⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protons | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 12 | 13 |
| Electrons | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| Ionic radius / nm (typical data values) | 0.171 | 0.140 | 0.136 | 0.095 | 0.065 | 0.050 |

All six ions have the arrangement 2,8. From N³⁻ to Al³⁺ the nuclear charge increases from 7 to 13 while the number of electrons and shells is fixed, so each ion holds the same ten electrons more tightly and is smaller.

This explains the pattern across Period 3:

- **Cations** Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ have lost the whole third shell (arrangement 2,8) and shrink across the period as nuclear charge rises.
- **Anions** P³⁻, S²⁻, Cl⁻ keep three shells (arrangement 2,8,8) and also shrink across the period: 0.212, 0.184 and 0.181 nm.
- There is a large **jump in radius** between Al³⁺ (0.050 nm) and P³⁻ (0.212 nm), because the anion has an extra occupied shell.

Down a group, ions with the same charge get larger because each has an extra shell: for example Li⁺ is smaller than Na⁺, which is smaller than K⁺.

## Common mistakes

- Subtracting the charge the wrong way: a negative ion has **more** electrons than protons.
- Forgetting that ¹H has no neutrons when counting particles in a polyatomic ion.
- Explaining deflection by mass alone: ²H⁺ and ⁴He²⁺ have different masses but deflect equally.
- Drawing electrons and protons curving by similar amounts.
- Saying shielding increases across a period; it stays roughly constant because the inner shells do not change.
- Comparing argon's radius with chlorine's as if they were measured the same way.
- Expecting radius to keep falling from Al³⁺ to P³⁻: it rises sharply because P³⁻ has an extra shell.

## Exam technique

- **State** a trend: one sentence with the direction of change.
- **Explain** a radius trend: name nuclear charge, number of shells and shielding, and say which one changes and which stays the same.
- **Describe** beam behaviour: give direction and relative size of deflection for each particle.
- **Determine** particle numbers: set out protons, neutrons and electrons separately and show the charge adjustment.
