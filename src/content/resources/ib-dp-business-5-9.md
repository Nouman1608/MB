This guide goes deeper into section 5.9, Management information systems, of the IB *Business management guide* (first assessment 2024). The section is HL only. The guide uses "management information systems" as a collective name for a set of advanced computer technologies, and its content points run from AO1 terms (such as databases and cybercrime) to AO3 issues (loyalty programmes, monitoring employees, data mining, and the benefits, risks and ethics of these technologies).

The [study guide to sections 5.8 and 5.9](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-research-and-development-hl-only-and-management-information/) defines every term and works one example each for loyalty programmes, Digital Taylorism and data mining. This page focuses on the AO3 end: how information flows into decisions, how to weigh cyber risk with numbers, what makes an AI or monitoring system fair, and how to build a balanced judgement. Condense it with the [5.9 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-5-9-revision-notes/) and practise with the [5.9 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-business-5-9-practice/).

## From data to decisions

**Data** are raw facts, such as each till transaction. **Information** is data that has been processed so it means something to a manager, such as weekly sales by store. A management information system does this conversion and puts the result in front of the person who decides.

The chain has five links, and a weakness in any one spoils the decision:

1. **Capture**, for example sensors (internet of things), tills and websites.
2. **Storage**, in databases held in data centres or in the cloud.
3. **Processing**, through data analytics, data mining or artificial intelligence.
4. **Presentation**, such as dashboards and reports.
5. **Decision**, by a manager or, increasingly, by an automated system.

Analytics is often grouped by the question it answers: what happened (descriptive), why it happened (diagnostic), what is likely to happen (predictive) and what should be done (prescriptive). These labels are common textbook terms rather than guide content, but they help you say what an MIS adds at each step.

**Big data** is usually described by its volume, the velocity at which it arrives and its variety (numbers, text, images, location). Ordinary spreadsheets cannot handle it, which is why big data relies on the critical infrastructures the guide names.

## Weighing cyber risk with numbers

Cybercrime includes hacking, ransomware, phishing and data theft. Cybersecurity is the set of measures that protect systems: staff training, access controls, encryption, backups and monitoring. Managers often weigh security spending with expected values.

### Worked example: is the security upgrade worth it?

Pellow Insurance is an invented firm. All figures are invented and in dollars.

- Estimated chance of a serious breach this year without the upgrade: 0.15.
- Estimated cost of a breach (recovery, fines, lost customers): 600,000.
- Upgrade cost: 40,000 a year, which cuts the chance of a breach to 0.05.

Expected loss without the upgrade = 0.15 × 600,000 = **90,000**.

Expected loss with the upgrade = 0.05 × 600,000 = 30,000, plus the 40,000 cost = **70,000**.

The upgrade lowers the expected yearly cost by 20,000. The numbers depend on estimates of probability and cost that are hard to make, and they leave out effects such as the loss of customer trust after a breach, which may be larger than any fine. A strong answer uses the calculation and then questions it.

## Critical infrastructures, VR, IoT and AI in context

At AO2 you need to apply each technology to the case.

| Technology | Business application | Effect to explain |
|---|---|---|
| Artificial neural networks | Forecasting demand from past sales and weather | Better stock levels, less waste |
| Data centres | Hosting a bank's customer accounts | Control over data; heavy fixed cost and energy use |
| Cloud computing | A start-up renting storage and software | Low start-up cost, easy to scale; dependence on supplier |
| Virtual reality | Training staff on dangerous equipment | Safer, repeatable training; cost of headsets |
| Internet of things | Sensors on delivery vans | Route planning and maintenance before breakdown |
| Artificial intelligence | Screening job applications | Faster decisions; risk of bias |
| Big data | A streaming service tracking viewing | Personal recommendations; privacy concerns |

## Making AI and monitoring fair

Artificial intelligence learns from past data. If past decisions were biased, for example if a firm hired fewer applicants from one group, an AI trained on them can repeat that bias at greater speed. Three questions test whether an AI decision system is acceptable:

- **Transparency.** Can the business explain why the system reached a result?
- **Accountability.** Who is responsible if the result harms someone?
- **Fairness.** Has the system been tested for unequal outcomes between groups?

The same questions apply to using data to manage and monitor employees. **Digital Taylorism** applies Taylor's scientific management, which you meet in Unit 2 motivation theory, through digital tools: tasks split into small steps, standard times set, and output tracked continuously. It can raise productivity and give clear targets. It can also cause stress, remove autonomy, and reward speed over quality or care.

A useful test for any monitoring is **proportionality**: is the data collected only what is needed for a legitimate purpose, and do employees know what is collected and why? Laws on data and privacy differ between countries, so answers should refer to legal compliance in general terms.

## Loyalty programmes and data mining at AO3

A loyalty programme has two values: the extra sales it encourages, and the data it collects. Data mining that data can reveal which customers are about to stop buying, which products are bought together, and which offers work.

Points to weigh:

- **For:** repeat purchases, richer customer profiles, targeted offers that cost less than general discounts, and a switching cost that keeps members loyal.
- **Against:** rewards reduce margin; rivals copy the scheme so the advantage fades; extra spending may come from customers who would have bought anyway; members may object to how their data is used; patterns found by mining show association, not cause.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (AO3 style):* Ravensby Contact is a call centre that plans to record every call, measure call length and rank employees weekly. Discuss this plan.

> Ranking by call length gives managers clear, up-to-date information, so slow processes and training needs are spotted quickly, which could cut costs in a business where labour is the main expense.

*Annotation:* benefit with a reason and a link to the case.

> However, this is a form of Digital Taylorism. Employees may rush calls to improve their rank, so customer problems go unsolved and complaints rise, which is the opposite of the firm's aim.

*Annotation:* a risk that uses the named concept.

> Recording every call raises privacy and trust issues. If staff do not know how recordings are used, morale and retention may fall, adding recruitment costs.

*Annotation:* ethics and a stakeholder effect.

> On balance, Ravensby should measure call quality as well as length and explain the system to staff, because monitoring that is transparent and balanced is more likely to raise performance than one based on speed alone.

*Annotation:* a judgement with conditions.

## Exam technique

- **Define (AO1):** one accurate sentence, such as "a database is an organized collection of data that can be searched and updated".
- **Explain (AO2):** name the technology, the business use and the effect on cost, revenue, quality or a stakeholder.
- **Discuss or evaluate (AO3):** benefits and risks for at least two stakeholder groups, an ethical point, and a judgement.
- Use the guide's term "Digital Taylorism" when monitoring breaks work into measured tasks.

## Common mistakes

- Treating data and information as the same thing.
- Writing only about benefits of technology, with no risks or ethics.
- Assuming a loyalty programme caused all extra spending.
- Describing AI as neutral, ignoring bias in training data.
- Forgetting that 5.9 is HL only.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is section 5.9 in the SL course?**
No. Management information systems is HL only in the IB Business Management guide, first assessment 2024.

**What is Digital Taylorism?**
The use of digital tools to apply Taylor's scientific management: breaking work into small measured tasks, setting standard times and monitoring output continuously.

**What is the difference between data and information?**
Data are raw facts. Information is data that has been processed and presented so that a manager can use it to decide.

**How should you evaluate a management information system?**
Weigh its benefits for decisions and stakeholders against risks such as cybercrime, bias and loss of privacy, discuss the ethics, and give a judgement that states the conditions.
