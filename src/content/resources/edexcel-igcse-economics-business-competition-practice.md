All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

**1.** Define monopoly. **[2]**

**2.** State two barriers to entry that can protect a monopoly. **[2]**

**3.** Total sales in the market for school notebooks in Country D are 250,000 notebooks a month. Firm X sells 90,000 notebooks and Firm Y sells 40,000 notebooks.

**(a)** Calculate the market share of Firm X. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the combined market share if Firm X takes over Firm Y. **[2]**

**4.** A workshop making wooden chairs in Country E produces 1,500 chairs a month at a total cost of 4,500. A large factory produces 15,000 chairs a month at a total cost of 30,000.

**(a)** Calculate the average cost per chair for the workshop and for the factory. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in average cost from the workshop to the factory. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why average cost falls as a firm grows. **[2]**

**5.** Four firms supply a market in Country F. Each has a unit cost of 14. Under normal rivalry the price is 20 and each firm sells 5,000 units. The firms then agree to form a cartel, fix the price at 25 and each sell 3,500 units.

**(a)** Calculate the profit of each firm under normal rivalry. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit of each firm under the cartel. **[2]**
**(c)** The cartel breaks down and a price war follows. The price falls to 17 and each firm sells 6,000 units. Calculate each firm's profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage rise in price and the percentage fall in total sales caused by the cartel. **[2]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a village florist might stay small. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which competition between firms can benefit consumers. **[4]**

**8.** A few large firms dominate the market for breakfast cereal. Explain two forms of non-price competition they might use. **[4]**

**9.** Three small bus companies on an island each charged a fare of 2.00. Together they carried 18,000 passengers a month. They merged into one company, which raised the fare to 2.60 and carried 15,000 passengers a month.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the fare. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total revenue before and after the merger. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether passengers are better off after the merger. **[2]**

**10.** A regional dairy, Firm M, plans to take over a smaller rival.

**(a)** Explain two reasons why Firm M might want to take over a competitor. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one way in which government regulation could limit the growth of Firm M. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that "a monopoly always harms consumers". **[9]**

**12.** Three firms dominate a market and begin a price war. Evaluate whether this is good for consumers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A market in which one business dominates supply [1], with little or no competition, so that it has strong control over the market [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the business dominates the market; "only one firm" alone leaves out the idea of market control that the second part of a definition needs.

**2.** Patents, which give the inventor sole rights for a set period [1]; high start-up costs, which few rivals can afford [1]. (Also accept legal barriers, high marketing budgets and technology.) **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct barriers are asked for, so naming the same idea twice in different words is unlikely to earn both credits.

**3. (a)** 90,000 / 250,000 x 100 [1] = **36%** [1].
**(b)** (90,000 + 40,000) / 250,000 x 100 [1] = **52%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division before the answer; in part (b) add the two sales figures first, then divide by the whole market.

**4. (a)** Workshop: 4,500 / 1,500 = **3.00** [1]. Factory: 30,000 / 15,000 = **2.00** [1].
**(b)** (3.00 - 2.00) / 3.00 x 100 [1] = **33.3%** [1].
**(c)** For example, the factory buys materials in bulk and gets a lower price per unit [1], which lowers the cost of each chair [1]. (Accept: specialised machinery or workers spread over more output.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the original cost, 3.00, as the base for the percentage, and for the explanation, link a cause to the lower cost per unit.

**5. (a)** (20 - 14) x 5,000 [1] = **30,000** each [1].
**(b)** (25 - 14) x 3,500 [1] = **38,500** each [1].
**(c)** (17 - 14) x 6,000 [1] = **18,000** each [1].
**(d)** Price: (25 - 20) / 20 x 100 = **25%** rise [1]. Total sales: 20,000 falls to 14,000, which is **30%** fall [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Profit is profit per unit multiplied by quantity, so show both stages; in part (d) multiply each firm's sales by four before comparing the totals.

**6.** The village has few people, so the market is small [1][1] and demand cannot support a larger shop. The florist may serve a niche, such as handmade bouquets, where customers want a personal service [1][1] that would be lost if it grew. (Also accept: lack of finance for a bigger shop, or the owner preferring a manageable workload to maximum profit.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a link to the florist's situation; "lack of money" with no explanation of what that prevents is likely to earn only part of the credit.

**7.** Lower prices, because rivals try to win customers by charging less [1][1]. Better quality, because firms improve products to keep customers [1][1]. (Also accept: more choice, and innovation as firms look for new products to get ahead.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show a chain, such as rivalry leading to lower price, rather than stating the benefit alone.

**8.** Advertising, which builds awareness of a brand without a price cut [1][1]. Branding or packaging, which makes a product look different from similar cereals [1][1]. (Also accept: improved quality or new flavours, and loyalty schemes or free gifts that encourage repeat purchase.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A price cut or a discount is price competition, so avoid it here. Tie each form to the cereal market.

**9. (a)** (2.60 - 2.00) / 2.00 x 100 [1] = **30%** rise [1].
**(b)** Before: 2.00 x 18,000 = **36,000** [1]. After: 2.60 x 15,000 = **39,000** [1].
**(c)** Passengers pay 30% more [1] and 3,000 fewer passengers travel, so they are probably worse off unless service has improved [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Revenue rising does not show that passengers gain; keep the firm's and the passengers' positions separate and give a judgement.

**10. (a)** It removes a rival, so the firm faces less competition [1] and has more control over price [1]. It adds customers, so sales and market share rise [1], and larger output may lower average cost through economies of scale [1]. (Also accept: spreading risk if the rival sells different products or in other areas.)
**(b)** For example, a regulator could block the takeover [1] because it would give the dairy too much market power and could lead to higher prices [1]. (Accept: rules that add costs which are heavy for expansion.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b) state what the regulation does and why, not just that "the government stops it".

**11.** For (up to 4): a monopoly can use economies of scale to cut average cost [1], so it may be able to charge a lower price [1]; patents and profits can fund research and new products [1]; high profits may fund better quality [1]. Against (up to 4): as a price-maker with barriers to entry, it can raise price without losing customers [1]; there are few or no alternatives, so choice is limited [1]; with no rivals it has little reason to cut waste or improve quality [1]; it may keep scale savings as profit [1]. Judgement [1]: a supported conclusion, for example that "always" is too strong because consumers gain only if cost savings are passed on or a regulator limits price. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The word "always" invites a judgement; a strong answer names the deciding factor and uses it to qualify the claim, rather than ending on a list.

**12.** For (up to 3): prices fall, so consumers can buy more for their money [1]; firms may also improve service to stand out [1]; consumers may switch to cheaper brands, getting immediate benefit [1]. Against (up to 3): profit falls, so firms may cut quality or research [1]; weaker firms may close, leaving fewer rivals [1]; with fewer rivals, the survivors may raise prices later, so the gain is short-lived [1]. Deciding factor and judgement: the effect depends on how long the war lasts and whether any firm leaves; a short war helps consumers, while a long one that ends in closures may leave them worse off [1], giving a clear conclusion [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the short run from the longer run; an answer that stops at "lower prices" misses the effect on competition afterwards.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining monopoly as "one firm" with no mention of dominance or control of the market.
- Naming a barrier to entry with no explanation of how it blocks newcomers.
- Forgetting to multiply profit per unit by the number of units sold.
- Dividing a change by the later value when the earlier value should be the base.
- Treating a cartel as a merger, or forgetting that members may cheat.
- Describing price cuts as non-price competition.
- Giving a reason why a firm stays small with no link to the firm described.
- Listing effects of a price war or monopoly with no judgement.
