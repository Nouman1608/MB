This study guide teaches sub-topic 2(a), **Level of organisation**, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (4BI1) specification, Issue 3. It has one point, **2.1: "describe the levels of organisation in organisms: organelles, cells, tissues, organs and systems"**. The point has no **B** reference, so it can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2.

The [level of organisation and biological molecules overview](/resources/edexcel-igcse-biology-4bi1-level-of-organisation-and-biological-molecules/) gives the five levels with a first example each, ordering a ribosome up to the digestive system. This page goes further: plant and animal organs taken apart tissue by tissue, the structures students most often misplace, and how to write the "describe" and "explain" answers that use this idea in later topics. Condensed notes are in the [level of organisation revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-biology-4bi1-level-of-organisation-revision-notes/), and you can test yourself with the [level of organisation practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-biology-4bi1-level-of-organisation-practice/).

## The five levels, defined precisely

Each level is built from units of the level below, and each has a definition that examiners expect almost word for word.

| Level | Definition to learn | Key word |
|---|---|---|
| Organelle | A structure inside a cell that carries out a particular function | *inside* a cell |
| Cell | The basic structural and functional unit of a living organism | *basic unit* |
| Tissue | A group of **similar** cells that work together to carry out a particular function | *similar* cells |
| Organ | A structure made of **several different** tissues that work together to carry out a particular function | *different tissues* |
| System | A group of organs that work together to carry out a particular function | *organs* together |

The two words that separate the middle levels are **similar** and **different**. A tissue is many cells of the same kind; an organ always contains more than one kind of tissue. If you can name two tissues in a structure, it is an organ.

## Why multicellular organisms are organised this way

A single-celled organism, such as an amoeba-like protoctist, carries out every life process inside one cell. In a large organism most cells are far from the outside, so one cell cannot do everything. Instead, cells become **specialised**: their structure suits one job, such as contracting, conducting impulses or absorbing water. Similar specialised cells form a tissue, tissues combine into an organ, and organs combine into a system, so that each life process has structures adapted to it. This is the link between point 2.1 and the cell specialisation work in sub-topic 2(b).

## Taking an animal organ apart: the small intestine

Applying 2.1 to a named organ is the commonest exam use.

| Level | Example in the small intestine |
|---|---|
| Organelle | Mitochondria in the epithelial cells, releasing energy for active transport |
| Cell | Epithelial cell with microvilli |
| Tissue | Epithelium lining the villi; smooth muscle in the wall; blood, flowing in capillaries |
| Organ | The small intestine itself |
| System | The digestive system: mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, with the liver and pancreas |

Note that **blood is a tissue**: it is made of cells (red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets) in plasma, working together to transport substances and defend the body. Students often call it an organ or "a liquid", and lose the mark.

## Taking a plant organ apart: the leaf

Plants have the same levels. The leaf is an organ because it contains several different tissues working together for photosynthesis.

| Tissue in the leaf | Role |
|---|---|
| Upper epidermis | Thin, transparent layer that lets light through |
| Palisade mesophyll | Column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts, where most photosynthesis happens |
| Spongy mesophyll | Loosely packed cells with air spaces for gas exchange |
| Xylem | Carries water and mineral ions into the leaf |
| Phloem | Carries sucrose and amino acids away from the leaf |
| Lower epidermis | Contains stomata, opened and closed by guard cells |

The organelles that matter here are **chloroplasts**, which absorb light energy. The leaf, stem and root are organs of the plant; together the leaves and stem form the **shoot system**, and the roots form the **root system**.

## Structures students most often misplace

| Structure | Level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus, ribosome, mitochondrion, chloroplast, vacuole | Organelle | Found inside a cell |
| Red blood cell, sperm cell, root hair cell, guard cell | Cell | A single unit |
| Blood | Tissue | Similar cells working together, suspended in plasma |
| Xylem, phloem | Tissue | Groups of similar cells forming vessels or tubes |
| Muscle, epithelium, nervous tissue | Tissue | One kind of cell |
| Skin | Organ | Contains epithelium, nervous tissue, connective tissue and blood |
| Heart | Organ | Contains cardiac muscle, connective tissue in the valves, and blood |
| Eye | Organ | Several tissues, including the retina and muscle |
| Leaf, stem, root, flower | Organ | Several tissues each |
| Circulatory, nervous, digestive, breathing systems | System | Several organs together |

A useful check: ask "could this exist inside a single cell?" (organelle), "is it one cell?" (cell), "is it many cells of one type?" (tissue), "can I name two or more tissues in it?" (organ), "is it a set of organs?" (system).

## Worked example: a model answer

*Question: Explain why the heart is described as an organ and not a tissue. [2]*

**Model answer:** The heart contains several different tissues, such as cardiac muscle tissue and connective tissue in the valves [1]. These tissues work together to carry out one function, pumping blood [1].

**Why it scores:** it names at least two tissues and links them to a shared function. "The heart is made of cells" earns nothing, because tissues and organs are both made of cells.

## Worked example: ordering and naming

*Question: The list shows structures from a plant: palisade cell, leaf, chloroplast, shoot system, palisade mesophyll. Arrange them in order of increasing level of organisation and name each level. [3]*

1. Chloroplast: inside the palisade cell, so an **organelle**.
2. Palisade cell: one **cell**.
3. Palisade mesophyll: a layer of similar palisade cells, so a **tissue**.
4. Leaf: contains palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, epidermis, xylem and phloem, so an **organ**.
5. Shoot system: leaves and stem together, so a **system**.

Answer: chloroplast → palisade cell → palisade mesophyll → leaf → shoot system.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a tissue as "a group of cells" without the word **similar**, which also fits an organ.
- Defining an organ as "made of cells" instead of "made of different tissues".
- Calling blood an organ, or calling a single red blood cell a tissue.
- Calling xylem or phloem an organ: each is a tissue inside the stem, root or leaf organ.
- Leaving out the organism itself when a question asks for the full sequence: systems together make up the organism.
- Writing "organ system" when the question uses "system", or the reverse. Both mean the same, but use the question's word.

## Exam technique for this point

The specification's command words change what earns the marks:

- **State** or **Name**: one word or phrase, such as "tissue".
- **Describe**: give an account in linked statements, such as the sequence of levels with an example at each step.
- **Explain**: a reason is required, for example why a structure counts as an organ (different tissues working together for one function).
- **Identify**: select from the information given, such as which labelled part of a diagram is an organelle.

Point 2.1 often appears inside a longer question on digestion, transport or the leaf, worth only one or two marks. Those marks are quick to gain if the definitions are exact.
