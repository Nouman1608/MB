**Depth Study B traces Germany from the revolution of November 1918 to the collapse of the Nazi state in 1945.** For Cambridge O Level History (2147) you answer three questions about it: was the Weimar Republic doomed from the start, why was Hitler able to dominate Germany by 1934, and -- in two parts -- how effectively did the Nazis control Germany and what was it like to live there? Examiners want explanation and judgement backed by precise detail.

## Where this fits in 2147

Depth Study B is one of five options; you study at least one. It is examined only in **Paper 1, Section B**, where two questions are set on each Depth Study and you answer one (parts worth 4, 6 and 10 marks). Paper 1 is worth 55% of the O Level. Related pages: [Depth Study B revision notes](/resources/o-level-world-history-depth-study-germany-1918-45-revision-notes/), [Depth Study B practice questions](/resources/o-level-world-history-depth-study-germany-1918-45-practice/) and [Depth Study A, the First World War](/resources/o-level-world-history-depth-study-first-world-war/), which ends where this one begins.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL HISTORY (2147), 2024-2026 -- DEPTH STUDY B: GERMANY, 1918-45**

- **1 Was the Weimar Republic doomed from the start?** Emerging from defeat; the impact of Versailles; recovery after 1923; Weimar's achievements. *Content:* the 1918 revolution and the Republic; Versailles and German reactions; the constitution, political divisions and the army; disorder 1919-23 (left and right, hyper-inflation, the Ruhr); the Stresemann era; Weimar culture.
- **2 Why was Hitler able to dominate Germany by 1934?** Nazi aims in the 1920s; little success before 1930; becoming Chancellor; consolidation 1933-34. *Content:* Nazi ideas and methods, the Munich Putsch, Hitler and other leaders; the Depression and the crisis of 1930-33; the Reichstag Fire and the 1933 election; the Enabling Act, the Night of the Long Knives, Hindenburg's death, removal of opposition.
- **3(a) How effectively did the Nazis control Germany, 1933-45?** Opposition; dealing with opponents; culture and mass media; persecution; was it totalitarian?
- **3(b) What was it like to live in Nazi Germany?** Young people; women and the family; who benefited; the impact of war, including the war economy and the Final Solution.

## Key question 1: Was Weimar doomed from the start?

The best way to answer is to test the word *doomed* against each period.

**1918-19: a weak birth?** The Republic was born from defeat. After the Kiel mutiny and the Kaiser's abdication on 9 November 1918, the Social Democrat Friedrich Ebert led the new government and signed the Armistice. To survive the left-wing threat, Ebert made a deal with the army's leaders (the Ebert-Groener agreement), leaving the old officer corps in place. The constitution, agreed at Weimar in 1919, was very democratic, but **proportional representation** encouraged many small parties and unstable coalitions, and **Article 48** gave the President emergency powers.

**Versailles.** Germans were not allowed to negotiate the treaty. The war-guilt clause, reparations, the 100,000-man army and lost territory were blamed on the Republic's politicians, whom nationalists called the "November criminals". This attached the Republic to defeat in many people's minds.

**1919-23: crisis.** From the left came the **Spartacist** rising (January 1919); from the right, the **Kapp Putsch** (March 1920) -- defeated only by a general strike because the army would not fire on fellow soldiers -- and hundreds of political murders, including Foreign Minister Rathenau in 1922. In January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the **Ruhr** after Germany defaulted on reparations; passive resistance and printing money produced **hyper-inflation**, ruining savers.

**1924-29: recovery.** Stresemann ended passive resistance and the new **Rentenmark** stabilised the currency. The **Dawes Plan** (1924) brought US loans; the **Young Plan** (1929) lowered reparations. Abroad, **Locarno** (1925) and League membership (1926) restored respect. Weimar culture -- Bauhaus architecture, expressionist art, cinema and cabaret -- made Berlin a cultural capital.

**Judgement:** the Republic had serious weaknesses from 1919, but it survived the crises of 1919-23 and recovered. It was not doomed; it was vulnerable to a severe economic shock -- which came in 1929.

## Key question 2: Why could Hitler dominate Germany by 1934?

**Nazi ideas in the 1920s.** The 25-point programme (1920) demanded the overturning of Versailles, union of all Germans, *Lebensraum*, and the exclusion of Jews from citizenship, with anti-communism and a strong leader at its heart. The SA gave the party a street army.

**Why so little success before 1930?** The **Munich Putsch** (November 1923) failed and Hitler was imprisoned; after his release he rebuilt the party to win votes, but the prosperity of 1924-29 meant few voters wanted extremes -- in 1928 the Nazis won only 12 Reichstag seats.

**Why did he become Chancellor?** The Depression from 1929 was the turning point: unemployment rose to about six million, and governments under Brüning used Article 48 decrees. Nazi propaganda under **Goebbels**, rallies and Hitler's speeches targeted farmers, the middle classes and the young, promising "work and bread". The party became the largest in the Reichstag in July 1932. Yet Hitler was **appointed** by President Hindenburg on 30 January 1933, after conservative politicians led by von Papen calculated they could use him.

**How did he consolidate power, 1933-34?** The **Reichstag Fire** (February 1933) allowed emergency decrees against communists; the **March 1933 election** strengthened the Nazis; the **Enabling Act** (March 1933) gave Hitler power to make laws; trade unions were dissolved and all other parties banned by July 1933. On the **Night of the Long Knives** (30 June 1934) Röhm and other SA leaders were murdered, winning the army's support. When **Hindenburg died** in August 1934 Hitler became Führer, and the army swore personal loyalty to him.

## Key question 3(a): How effectively did the Nazis control Germany?

- **Terror:** Himmler's SS, the Gestapo, informers and concentration camps; people's courts.
- **Propaganda and culture:** Goebbels controlled newspapers, radio and film; the Nuremberg rallies and the 1936 Olympics showcased the regime; "degenerate" art was banned and Jewish authors' books burned.
- **Religion:** the 1933 Concordat with the Pope; the pro-Nazi Reich Church; dissenting pastors such as Niemöller sent to camps.
- **Persecution:** Jews (the 1933 boycott, the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, Kristallnacht in 1938), Roma, disabled people, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses.
- **Opposition:** limited and divided -- communist cells, some church leaders, youth groups (Edelweiss Pirates, Swing Youth), the White Rose students, and the July 1944 bomb plot by army officers.

*Was it totalitarian?* In aim, yes; in practice control depended heavily on popular support, denunciations by ordinary Germans and the chaos of competing Nazi agencies.

## Key question 3(b): What was life like?

| Group | Experience |
|---|---|
| Young people | Hitler Youth and League of German Maidens; Nazified schools; many enthusiastic, some rebellious |
| Women | Encouraged to marry and have children; loans and medals for mothers; later needed in war work |
| Workers | Jobs through rearmament and public works; no free unions; leisure via Strength Through Joy |
| Jews and minorities | Loss of rights, property and, eventually, life |

**War** brought rationing, longer hours, foreign forced labour, the bombing of cities and, after 1943, total war under Speer's management. In occupied eastern Europe the **Final Solution** -- the planned murder of Europe's Jews, agreed at Wannsee in January 1942 -- was carried out in death camps.

## Worked example: a (b) answer

*Explain why the Nazis had little success before 1930.* **[6]**

Give two or three developed reasons: (1) the Munich Putsch failed and Hitler was in prison in 1924, leaving the party divided; (2) Stresemann's recovery -- stable currency, US loans, foreign success -- meant most voters supported moderate parties; (3) the Nazi message of crisis had little appeal while unemployment was relatively low, shown by only 12 seats in 1928.

## Common mistakes

- Describing Weimar's problems without deciding whether they made collapse inevitable.
- Forgetting that the Nazis never won a majority in a free election.
- Using "totalitarian" as a label rather than testing it.
- Leaving out the 1939-45 war years in key question 3(b).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the Holocaust in this Depth Study?**
Yes, as "the Final Solution" under the impact of the Second World War on Germany.

**What is the best single date to remember?**
30 January 1933, Hitler's appointment, because key question 2 turns on it.

**Do I need election percentages?**
Seat numbers or percentages for 1928, July 1932 and March 1933 are enough to show the trend.
