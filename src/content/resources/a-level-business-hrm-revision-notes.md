Condensed, exam-focused notes for Topic 2 of Cambridge AS & A Level Business (9609), 2026-2028 series.

## 2.1 Human resource management

- Recruitment/selection stages: job analysis → job description and person specification → advertising →
  shortlisting → interviewing → selection.
- **Internal recruitment**: faster, cheaper, candidate already familiar with the organisation, but limits
  the pool of skills to existing staff.
- **External recruitment**: brings fresh ideas and a wider skills pool, but slower, costlier, and needs
  an adjustment period.
- Training types: induction (new starters), on-the-job (while working), off-the-job (away from work) —
  be ready to recommend and justify a specific method for a specific role.

## 2.2 Motivation

- Financial methods: pay structures, bonuses, profit-sharing.
- Non-financial methods: job enrichment, delegation, recognition.
- Effectiveness depends on the workforce: a highly skilled workforce may respond better to non-financial
  motivators than pay alone.
- Common error: treating financial and non-financial motivation as interchangeable rather than weighing
  which suits a given context.

## 2.3 Management

- Management **functions**: planning, organising, coordinating, commanding, controlling.
- Management **styles**: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire.
- Matching style to situation: autocratic may suit a crisis needing fast decisions; democratic may suit a
  workforce of skilled professionals expecting consultation.
- Management skills worth naming with justification: delegation, communication, decision-making.

## How the three sub-topics connect

Recruitment decisions (2.1) determine who is available to motivate (2.2); motivation approaches interact
with the management style in place (2.3); and a manager's own style shapes which motivators are
practical to use day to day. Examiners reward answers that draw these connections explicitly — for
example, noting that a newly externally-recruited specialist may need more structured induction training
and a more directive management style initially before responding well to non-financial motivators once
settled — rather than answering 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 as three unconnected fact lists.

## Exam technique for this topic

Business at this level consistently rewards application and judgement over definitions recited in the
abstract, so for every named method, style or theory, be ready to state the specific business
circumstance that makes it the better choice, and the circumstance that makes an alternative better
instead — this applies equally to recruitment (internal vs external), motivation (financial vs
non-financial matched to workforce type), and management style (autocratic vs democratic vs
laissez-faire matched to situation). A quick self-check worth applying to any answer on this topic is
whether it explains a *consequence* for the business, not just a definition of a term — an answer that
only defines "delegation" scores lower than one that also explains why delegation matters for a
specific scenario given in the question.

## Worked example: choosing a management style for two scenarios

Consider two contrasting scenarios: a factory facing an urgent safety incident requiring an immediate,
unambiguous response, and a research team of senior specialists deciding how to redesign a product
feature. In the first scenario, an autocratic style suits the situation because speed and clarity of
direction matter more than consultation when safety and time pressure are the dominant factors — a
democratic approach that delays action to gather input could itself increase risk. In the second
scenario, a democratic (or even largely laissez-faire) style suits the situation because the specialists
already possess the expertise the decision requires, and imposing top-down direction on people better
qualified than the manager to judge the technical detail would likely produce a worse outcome and
damage morale. The pattern worth internalising is that management style should be justified by the
specific combination of time pressure, workforce expertise, and decision stakes present in a scenario,
never asserted as simply "the best" style in the abstract.

## Worked example: matching a motivator to a workforce

A supermarket with a largely part-time, lower-paid workforce and a software company with a small team of
highly-paid senior engineers illustrate why one motivation approach rarely suits every context. For the
supermarket workforce, financial incentives such as a modest bonus tied to attendance or sales targets
are likely to have a proportionally larger motivating effect, since pay increases matter more where
financial need is more pressing. For the software engineers, whose pay already comfortably covers their
financial needs, non-financial approaches — greater autonomy over how a project is completed, public
recognition of technical contribution, or opportunities for professional growth — are more likely to
sustain motivation than an equivalent-sized bonus would. Being able to generate this kind of matched,
scenario-specific reasoning on demand, rather than reciting motivation theory names alone, is what
separates strong responses on this sub-topic from weaker ones.

## Self-test

1. Name the six stages of the recruitment and selection process.
2. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of external recruitment.
3. Name the three named management functions besides planning and organising.
4. Which management style might suit a business needing fast decisions in a crisis?
5. Why might a highly skilled workforce respond better to non-financial motivators than pay alone?

**Answers:** 1. Job analysis, job description/person specification, advertising, shortlisting,
interviewing, selection. 2. Advantage: fresh ideas/wider skills pool. Disadvantage: slower, costlier,
adjustment period. 3. Coordinating, commanding, controlling. 4. Autocratic. 5. Skilled professionals
often value recognition, autonomy and job enrichment as much as or more than pay, since their needs
beyond basic financial security may already be met.
