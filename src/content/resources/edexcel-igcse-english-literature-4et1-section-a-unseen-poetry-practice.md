## Section A: short skills questions

Read the poem, then answer questions 1 to 6.

> **The Allotment**
>
> My grandfather's hands knew soil the way  
> a reader knows a page: by touch, by turning.  
> Each spring he coaxed the stubborn ground to say  
> its green, slow sentences. I watched him, learning
>
> nothing, or so I thought. Now the plot is mine.  
> The fork is heavier than it looked. The rain  
> comes when it likes. I kneel along the line  
> of onions he would have thinned, and thin them. Again,
>
> again, my hands remember what I don't.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice page.)*

**1.** Identify the simile in lines 1 to 2 and explain what it suggests about the grandfather. **[2]**

**2.** What does the word "stubborn" suggest about the ground, and about the grandfather's work? **[2]**

**3.** Explain the effect of the line break between "learning" and "nothing" (lines 4 to 5). **[3]**

**4.** How does the speaker's situation change between the first and second stanzas? Refer to the
poem in your answer. **[3]**

**5.** Comment on the effect of the final line being set apart as a stanza on its own. **[3]**

**6.** The poem uses a regular rhyme pattern in its two four-line stanzas. Explain how one example of
rhyme contributes to the meaning. **[3]**

## Section B: full essay questions

**7.** Explore how the poet presents memory and inheritance in "The Allotment". **[20]**

**8.** Read the poem below. Explore how the poet presents the experience of saying goodbye. **[20]**

> **Departures**
>
> The board flicks over, letters falling  
> like cards in a quick hand: Lagos, Dubai,  
> Toronto, Delayed. My brother checks his bag  
> for the fourth time. Our father checks his watch.
>
> No one says the word for what this is.  
> We say *passport*. We say *gate*. We say  
> *text when you land*, as if a sentence  
> could be folded small enough to pack.
>
> Then the barrier, the wave, the back  
> of his head becoming any head,  
> and the board flicks over, letters falling,  
> and finds another place to send someone.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice page.)*

**9.** Read the poem below. Explore how the poet uses structure and language to present stillness and
sudden movement. **[20]**

> **Heron**
>
> Grey as a held breath, it stands  
> where the river slows,  
> one leg tucked away like a secret,
>
> and waits.
>
> Not minutes. Not hours.  
> Something older than counting.  
> The water forgets its own reflection twice
>
> before the beak goes in—  
> one stab, one silver flicker—  
> and the stillness closes over like a lid.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice page.)*

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The simile compares his hands to "a reader" knowing "a page" (1). It suggests his gardening is
skilled, instinctive and intimate, a kind of literacy built through long familiarity (1).

**2.** "Stubborn" personifies the soil as resistant or unwilling (1), so the grandfather's work is
presented as patient persuasion ("coaxed") rather than force, showing his gentleness and skill (1).

**3.** The enjambment carries the sentence across the stanza break (1), so "learning" seems positive
until the next stanza opens on "nothing" (1); the delay creates a small shock or self-deprecating
humour, and the phrase "or so I thought" then reveals he learned more than he knew (1).

**4.** In stanza 1 the speaker is a watcher in the past tense, observing the grandfather (1). In
stanza 2 the present tense ("Now the plot is mine") shows the speaker has taken over, probably after
the grandfather's death (1). He finds the work harder and less controllable ("heavier than it looked",
the rain "comes when it likes") (1).

**5.** Isolating the line gives it emphasis and the weight of a conclusion (1). The repetition
"Again, / again" carries across the gap, suggesting repeated, habitual action (1). The paradox that his
hands "remember" what his mind does not shows the grandfather's knowledge has been inherited
physically, which is both comforting and poignant (1).

**6.** One mark for a correct example, up to two for explanation. For example: "turning/learning" (1)
links the grandfather's skilled handling with the child's education, suggesting the learning came
through watching the turning of soil (1), although the next line undercuts it (1). Or "mine/line" (1):
the rhyme ties ownership to the rows of onions, so inheriting the plot means inheriting a duty and a
routine (1-2).

### Section B: how to mark questions 7 to 9

Each essay is marked holistically on AO2. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 17-20 | Perceptive overview; sustained, precise analysis of language, form and structure; well-chosen short quotations; effects explored in detail, with alternative readings |
| 13-16 | Thorough analysis with clear effects; covers language, form and structure; quotations apt |
| 9-12 | Clear explanation of some methods; effects explained but sometimes general |
| 5-8 | Some comment on methods, often feature-spotting; some paraphrase |
| 1-4 | Mainly paraphrase or simple observations with little reference to methods |

**7. Indicative content (any well-supported reading is credited):**

- The opening simile and the language of reading ("page", "sentences") present the grandfather's
  skill as a kind of literacy the speaker must now learn.
- Contrast between the gentle verb "coaxed" and the speaker's struggle (the fork "heavier", the rain
  that "comes when it likes") shows inheritance as a burden as well as a gift.
- Structure: past tense to present tense across the stanza break marks loss and succession; the
  enjambed "learning / nothing" dramatises the speaker's underestimation of what he absorbed.
- "he would have thinned, and thin them": the conditional and the repeated verb show the speaker
  imitating his grandfather, filling the gap he left.
- Form: regular rhyme in the stanzas suggests order and continuity; the last line breaks the pattern
  and stands alone, like the speaker himself, yet "remember" asserts that the connection survives.

**8. Indicative content:**

- The simile "like cards in a quick hand" makes the departures board feel like chance or a gamble,
  hinting at the family's lack of control.
- Repeated actions ("checks his bag", "checks his watch") show anxiety channelled into small tasks.
- Stanza 2: "No one says the word" and the italicised practical words show feelings displaced into
  logistics; the metaphor of a sentence "folded small enough to pack" suggests emotion cannot be
  contained or carried.
- Stanza 3: listing ("the barrier, the wave, the back / of his head") speeds the moment up; the
  enjambment and "any head" show the brother becoming anonymous in the crowd, a painful loss of
  individuality.
- Cyclical structure: repeating "the board flicks over, letters falling" suggests the family's grief
  is one of many, and the impersonal "someone" closes the poem coldly.

**9. Indicative content:**

- Opening simile "Grey as a held breath" turns colour into suspense; "like a secret" adds concealment.
- The one-line stanza "and waits." uses isolation and white space to make the reader wait too.
- Fragments ("Not minutes. Not hours.") and caesurae slow the pace; "older than counting" lifts the
  stillness into something timeless.
- Hyperbole and personification ("forgets its own reflection twice") stretch time further.
- Sudden change in the final stanza: the dash, monosyllables and alliteration ("stab", "silver")
  quicken the rhythm to match the strike.
- The closing simile "like a lid" returns to stillness, giving a circular, contained structure; some
  candidates may note the violence hidden inside apparent calm.
