## Section A: analysing the Anthology text

You know in advance which poems and prose extracts can be set, because
Section A draws only on Part 2 of the Pearson Edexcel Anthology. That
changes revision strategy: build a short bank of **prepared observations**
for every Part 2 text — not full essays, just two or three defensible
points on language, structure and effect per text — so nothing on the
day is completely unfamiliar.

**The mark-earning pattern is unchanged from other reading questions:**
quote → what it suggests → why the writer chose it for this effect.
Naming a device (metaphor, enjambment, sibilance) without explaining
what it *does* to the reader earns AO1 credit at best, never AO2.

**For poetry specifically**, do not ignore form and structure in favour
of imagery alone. Stanza length, line breaks, rhyme (or its absence) and
where a poem's tone shifts are all structural choices examiners expect
you to notice, not just decorative facts to mention in passing.

**For prose extracts**, track narrative viewpoint and pacing: does the
extract slow down at a key moment (short sentences, present tense,
close focus) or speed past it? That pacing choice is usually where the
writer's intended effect lives.

## Section B: imaginative writing

Section B rewards **technique transfer** from Section A's reading, not
writing produced in isolation. Before the exam, identify two or three
specific techniques noticed in Anthology texts (a shift to short
sentences at a tense moment; a controlling central image; direct
address to create intimacy) that you can deliberately deploy in your own
writing.

**Plan before drafting.** With 45 minutes for 30 marks, spend roughly
five minutes sketching: opening hook, a turning point, an ending that
resolves or deliberately withholds resolution. AO4 rewards adapting
form, tone and register to the chosen prompt — that is difficult to
demonstrate in unplanned first-draft prose.

**Choose the prompt you can sustain, not the one that sounds most
ambitious.** A simple idea executed with control and a consistent voice
outscores an elaborate idea that loses coherence by paragraph three.

## Technical accuracy (AO5)

AO5 is assessed independently of AO4's content and imagination — a
vividly imagined piece with weak technical control still loses marks
here. Recurring issues:

- Tense drift within a single narrative (switching from past to present
without a deliberate reason).
- Overlong, unpunctuated sentences that lose grammatical control under
time pressure.
- A promising opening followed by a rushed, underdeveloped ending because
time ran out — evidence that planning time was skipped.

## Comparing Section A and Section B under one clock

Both sections carry equal marks (30 each) and equal recommended time
(45 minutes each). The most common timing error is over-running on
Section A's analysis — rereading and re-drafting points that were
already sufficient — which leaves Section B rushed. Practise full
90-minute timed attempts covering both sections together, not each
section in isolation, so the transition between them is rehearsed.

## Vocabulary and register in imaginative writing

A common ceiling on Section B marks is vocabulary that is accurate but
generic — "sad," "nice," "big" — where a more precise word choice would
demonstrate the range AO5 rewards. Build a small bank of precise
alternatives for commonly-needed emotional and sensory states before
the exam (unease rather than "worried," oppressive rather than "hot"),
so a wider vocabulary is available under time pressure rather than
invented on the spot.

Register should also match the imaginative task's implied audience: a
first-person reflective narrative can sustain a quieter, more
introspective register than a tense, plot-driven scene, and switching
between the two without reason reads as inconsistent rather than
varied.

## Exam traps

- Naming a poetic or narrative technique without explaining its effect.
- Treating Anthology poems as a list to summarise rather than texts to
analyse in depth.
- Writing Section B without a brief plan, producing an ending that
trails off rather than resolves.
- Applying a technique noticed in Section A mechanically, without
adapting it to fit the Section B prompt's own audience and purpose.
- Letting Section A run over time at Section B's expense.

## Self-test

1. What three-step pattern earns analysis marks on an Anthology text?
2. Why should prepared observations cover every Part 2 text, not just a
few favourites?
3. What does "technique transfer" mean in the context of Section B?
4. How long should planning take before drafting Section B, and why does
skipping it cost AO4 marks?
5. Name two common causes of lost AO5 marks.

**Answers:** 1. Quote the specific language or structural choice, state
what it suggests, then explain why the writer chose it for that
audience and effect. 2. Because Section A's question can be set on any
Part 2 text, and a candidate with no prepared response to the set text
is at a serious disadvantage compared with one who revised broadly. 3.
Deliberately applying a technique noticed and analysed in a studied
Anthology text (Section A) to your own imaginative writing (Section B),
rather than writing Section B as an unrelated task. 4. Around five
minutes; without a plan, AO4's requirement to adapt form, tone and
register to a specific purpose is hard to demonstrate in unplanned,
first-draft prose. 5. Tense drift within a narrative, and a rushed,
underdeveloped ending caused by skipping planning time — any two valid
technical-accuracy issues are acceptable.
