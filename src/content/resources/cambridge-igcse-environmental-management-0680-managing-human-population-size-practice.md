These questions cover sub-topic 7.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029): describing strategies for managing human population size and discussing their benefits and limitations. Countries and data in the sources are invented.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define an *anti-natalist* population policy. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** measures a government could use in a pro-natalist policy. **[2]**

**3.** Name the **five** strategies for managing human population size listed in the syllabus. **[3]**

**4.** State **one** way a government can control migration. **[1]**

**5.** Give **one** reason why access to contraception may not reduce the birth rate in a farming community. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how improving access to education for girls can reduce the number of children per woman. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why improving access to healthcare may increase population growth in the short term but reduce it in the long term. **[4]**

**8.** Source: *In the invented country of Venora, fertility has fallen to 1.3 children per woman. Over a quarter of the population is aged 65 or over, and hospitals report a shortage of nurses.*
(a) Using the source, explain why Venora's government is concerned about its population. **[2]**
(b) Suggest **two** strategies Venora could use, giving a reason for each. **[4]**

**9.** In the invented country of Lumaro, fertility fell from 6.0 to 4.2 children per woman after a family planning programme. Calculate the percentage fall. Show your working. **[2]**

**10.** Describe **two** problems that can result from a strict, compulsory anti-natalist policy. **[2]**

**11.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of a pro-natalist policy for a country with a falling population. **[4]**

**12.** "Control of migration is a more effective way to manage population size than policies aimed at birth rates." Discuss this statement. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A policy that encourages or requires people to have fewer children **[1]**.

**2.** Any two: payments or allowances for each child **[1]**; tax reductions for families **[1]**; paid parental leave **[1]**; subsidised or free childcare **[1]**; priority for housing. **[max 2]**

**3.** Access to contraception; population policies (pro-natalist and anti-natalist); access to education; access to healthcare; control of migration. All five for **[3]**; three or four for **[2]**; two for **[1]**.

**4.** Any one: visas or work permits; quotas on numbers; a points-based system; border controls; incentives to attract migrants. **[1]**

**5.** Any one: families want many children to work on the farm or support them in old age; cultural or religious objections to contraception; supplies or clinics are too far away. **[1]**

**6.** Girls who stay in school longer tend to marry and start families later **[1]**; education leads to better-paid jobs, so women may choose to work and have fewer children **[1]**; educated women know more about contraception and health and have more say in decisions about family size **[1]**.

**7.** Short term: healthcare and vaccination reduce deaths, especially of children **[1]**, while birth rates stay high for a time, so the population grows faster **[1]**. Long term: as more children survive, parents no longer need extra children to ensure some live to adulthood, so they choose smaller families **[1]**; clinics also provide contraception and family planning advice **[1]**.

**8.** (a) Fertility of 1.3 is well below the level that replaces each generation, so the population will shrink **[1]**; over a quarter of people are aged 65 or over, so there are fewer workers to pay for and provide care, shown by the nurse shortage **[1]**.
(b) Any two, each with a reason, 2 marks each: a pro-natalist policy such as child payments or subsidised childcare **[1]** to raise the birth rate and provide future workers **[1]**; encouraging immigration of working-age people, for example through work permits for nurses **[1]**, which fills the shortage quickly **[1]**; improving childcare and parental leave so that couples can combine work and family **[1]**, encouraging more births **[1]**. **[max 4]**

**9.** Fall = 6.0 − 4.2 = 1.8 **[1]**; 1.8 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = 30% **[1]**.

**10.** Any two: an imbalance between the numbers of males and females where families prefer sons **[1]**; a rapidly ageing population with too few young workers in later decades **[1]**; ethical concerns about personal freedom and enforcement **[1]**; resentment or evasion, such as unregistered births. **[max 2]**

**11.** Benefits: payments, tax cuts and childcare reduce the cost of raising children, which can raise births **[1]**, providing future workers and taxpayers to support the elderly **[1]**. Limitations: the policy is very expensive for the government **[1]**; many couples still choose small families because of housing costs or careers, so the effect may be small, and new births take about 20 years to join the workforce **[1]**. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 6 for a balanced discussion with a judgement. For: migration control acts quickly, as immigrants of working age can fill shortages at once **[1]**; it can target the skills a country needs, for example through a points-based system **[1]**; it can be used to raise or to limit population **[1]**. Against: immigration may cause social tension and pressure on housing and services **[1]**; it does not change the fertility of the existing population, so long-term ageing or rapid growth continues **[1]**; education, healthcare and contraception change family size permanently and improve quality of life **[1]**. Judgement: migration control is faster, but birth-related strategies give a longer-term solution, so a combination works best **[1]**. **[max 6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing pro-natalist and anti-natalist.
- Claiming healthcare reduces growth immediately.
- Suggesting an anti-natalist policy for an ageing country such as Venora.
- Answering "discuss" questions with description only, or with one side.
- Giving reasons not linked to the source when the question says "using the source".

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these population management questions from Cambridge past papers?**
No. They are original questions written for Marlbridge and do not copy past papers or their mark tariffs. Use them alongside the past papers for Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680).

**Do I need named country examples?**
The syllabus does not name examples, but an accurate one, such as China's one-child policy, strengthens a "discuss" answer if you include its drawbacks.

**Can control of migration increase a population?**
Yes. Governments can encourage working-age immigrants with work permits or points systems, as well as limit entry.
