Recall notes for section 3.2 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. Full explanations are in the [low unemployment study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics/); apply them with the [low unemployment practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain **employment** and **unemployment**.
- Explain the **Claimant Count**.
- **Calculate** the unemployment rate.
- **Analyse** recent and historical unemployment figures.
- Explain **cyclical, frictional, seasonal and structural** unemployment.
- **Evaluate** causes and consequences for **individuals, regions and the government**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Employed | In paid work, including part-time and self-employed |
| Unemployed | Without a job, available for work and actively seeking it |
| Economically inactive | Not working and not seeking work: students, retired people, full-time carers |
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Claimant Count | Number claiming benefits principally because they are unemployed |
| Unemployment rate | Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 |
| Cyclical unemployment | Caused by falling total demand in a downturn |
| Frictional unemployment | Short spells between jobs |
| Seasonal unemployment | Caused by demand changing with the time of year |
| Structural unemployment | Long-term mismatch after an industry declines or technology changes |
| Percentage point | The unit for a change between two percentages |

## Calculations

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Rate | Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 |
| Number from rate | Labour force × rate ÷ 100 |
| Change in rate | New rate − old rate, in percentage points |
| % change in the number | (new number − old number) ÷ old number × 100 |
| Cost to government | Number unemployed × (benefit + lost tax per person) |

**Key insight:** the rate can fall while the number unemployed rises, if the labour force grows faster.

## Worked reminders

**Rate and number moving apart.** Labour force 25.0 million with 1.25 million unemployed gives **5.0%**. Next year: labour force 26.0 million, 1.27 million unemployed, so **4.9%**. The number rose by 20,000 but the rate fell by about 0.1 percentage points.

**Regional comparison.** Region North: 90,000 unemployed out of 1.0 million, so **9.0%**. Region South: 120,000 out of 3.0 million, so **4.0%**. South has more unemployed people, but North has the far higher rate, so the problem is more concentrated there.

**Lost output.** If 1.25 million unemployed people could each produce £30,000 of output a year, the economy loses 1.25m × £30,000 = **£37.5bn** of output a year.

## Claimant Count: two columns

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Quick, cheap, frequent | Misses jobless people who cannot or do not claim |
| Breaks down by area and age | Rule changes alter the count without any change in jobs |
| Shows a direct cost to government | May understate true unemployment |

## The four types: spot the clue

| Clue in the case study | Type |
|---|---|
| "recession", "falling demand", "sales down across the economy" | Cyclical |
| "between jobs", "searching", "just graduated" | Frictional |
| "summer", "harvest", "Christmas", "off-season" | Seasonal |
| "industry closed", "replaced by automation", "skills no longer needed" | Structural |

Test: if demand recovers, will the job return? Yes → cyclical. No → structural.

## Consequences by group

- **Individuals:** lost income, falling living standards, debt, skills fading, health and confidence harmed, scarring from long spells.
- **Regions:** less local spending, shop closures, young people leave, fewer new firms, falling property values.
- **Government:** more benefit spending, less income tax and national insurance, lost output, opportunity cost of the extra spending.

## Evaluation factors

- Type and **length** of unemployment.
- How **concentrated** it is in one region or group.
- The age and skills of those affected.
- Whether retraining or help to move is available.
- Some frictional unemployment helps people find better-matched jobs.

## Exam traps

- Labour force, not population, goes on the bottom of the rate.
- Students and retired people are inactive, not unemployed.
- "Fell 0.4 percentage points", not "fell 0.4%".
- Automation job losses are structural.
- The Claimant Count is not a complete count of the jobless.
- Name the group: individual, region or government.

## Self-test

1. Define unemployment.
2. State what makes up the labour force.
3. A country has 28.5 million employed and 1.5 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate.
4. The rate falls from 4.2% to 3.8%. Describe the change correctly.
5. A region's labour force is 2.5 million and its unemployment rate is 6.4%. How many people are unemployed?
6. A ski instructor has no work in summer. Which type of unemployment?
7. A factory replaces its packing staff with robots. Which type?
8. Give one weakness of the Claimant Count.
9. Explain how the unemployment rate can fall while the number unemployed rises.
10. State one consequence of high unemployment for a region.
11. 50,000 people are unemployed. Each receives £4,800 in benefits and would have paid £3,600 in tax. Calculate the yearly cost to the government.
12. Why is the objective "low" unemployment rather than zero?

### Answers

1. Being **without a job while available for work and actively seeking it**.
2. The **employed plus the unemployed**.
3. Labour force = 30.0 million; 1.5 ÷ 30.0 × 100 = **5.0%**.
4. A fall of **0.4 percentage points**.
5. 2,500,000 × 0.064 = **160,000**.
6. **Seasonal.**
7. **Structural.**
8. Any one: misses those not **entitled** to claim; misses those who **choose** not to claim; changes in **benefit rules** alter it.
9. If the **labour force grows faster** than the number unemployed, the unemployed are a smaller share of it.
10. Any one: less local spending and **shop closures**; young people moving away; fewer new firms.
11. 50,000 × (4,800 + 3,600) = 50,000 × 8,400 = **£420m**.
12. Some **frictional** (and seasonal) unemployment always exists as people move between jobs, so zero is not realistic.
