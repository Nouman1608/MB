These questions cover sub-topics 1(f) Ionic bonding, 1(g) Covalent bonding and 1(h) Metallic bonding (points 1.37 to 1.54C) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). The qualification is untiered. Points 1.52C to 1.54C carry a C reference, so they are Paper 2 only; question 10 is labelled.

In the written answers below, dot-and-cross diagrams are shown in text. Brackets ( ) mark the overlap of two outer shells, where the shared pairs sit. In the exam, draw circles.

## Questions

**1.** Calcium reacts with nitrogen to form an ionic compound.

**(a)** Describe how a calcium atom forms a calcium ion. Give the electronic configuration of the ion. **[2]**
**(b)** State the charge on a nitride ion. Explain your answer in terms of electrons. **[2]**

**2.** Write the formula of each compound.

**(a)** aluminium sulfate **[1]**
**(b)** ammonium carbonate **[1]**
**(c)** iron(III) hydroxide **[1]**
**(d)** lead(II) nitrate **[1]**
**(e)** silver oxide **[1]**

**3.** Lithium reacts with nitrogen to form lithium nitride. Draw a dot-and-cross diagram to show the formation of lithium nitride from its atoms. Show outer electrons only. **[3]**

**4.** Magnesium oxide is used to line the inside of furnaces. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why magnesium oxide has a very high melting point. **[3]**

**5.** **(a)** State what is meant by a covalent bond. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of electrostatic attraction, why a covalent bond holds two atoms together. **[2]**

**6.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for each molecule. Show outer electrons only.

**(a)** nitrogen, N₂ **[2]**
**(b)** ammonia, NH₃ **[2]**
**(c)** ethene, C₂H₄ **[2]**

**7.** The table gives data for the halogens.

| Halogen | Mr | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| F₂ | 38 | −220 | −188 |
| Cl₂ | 71 | −102 | −34 |
| Br₂ | 160 | −7 | 59 |
| I₂ | 254 | 114 | 184 |

**(a)** Use the data to state the physical state of bromine at 25 °C. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the trend in boiling point from fluorine to iodine. **[3]**
**(c)** A student writes: "When iodine boils, the bonds between the iodine atoms break." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows properties of four substances, W, X, Y and Z.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts when solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| W | 2050 | no | yes |
| X | −23 | no | no |
| Y | above 3500 | no | no |
| Z | 660 | yes | yes |

**(a)** Identify the type of structure of each substance. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why X has a low melting point. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why Y has a very high melting point. **[2]**

**9.** Diamond, graphite and C60 fullerene are all forms of carbon.

**(a)** Explain why C60 fullerene is soft but diamond is very hard. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why graphite is soft and slippery, so it can be used as a lubricant. **[2]**
**(c)** Graphite conducts electricity but C60 fullerene is a poor conductor. Suggest why. **[2]**

**10.** **(Paper 2 only)** This question is about metals.

**(a)** Draw a labelled two-dimensional diagram to represent the metallic lattice in magnesium. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain metallic bonding in terms of electrostatic attractions. **[2]**
**(c)** Solid aluminium conducts electricity but solid aluminium oxide does not. Explain this difference. **[2]**

**11.** Carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature. Silicon dioxide is a solid with a very high melting point.

**(a)** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of carbon dioxide. Show outer electrons only. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature but silicon dioxide has a very high melting point. **[4]**
**(c)** Name a form of carbon that has the same type of structure as silicon dioxide, and state one physical property they share. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The calcium atom (2.8.8.2) **loses its two outer electrons** [1]. The ion is **Ca²⁺ with configuration 2.8.8** [1].
**(b)** **3−** [1]. Nitrogen (2.5) **gains three electrons** to fill its outer shell, so it has 7 protons and 10 electrons [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A charge needs both the number and the sign; "gains protons" or "loses neutrons" earns nothing because only electrons move.

**2. (a)** **Al₂(SO₄)₃** [1]
**(b)** **(NH₄)₂CO₃** [1]
**(c)** **Fe(OH)₃** [1]
**(d)** **Pb(NO₃)₂** [1]
**(e)** **Ag₂O** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Each formula is all-or-nothing: a missing bracket, as in AlSO₄ or Al₂SO₄₃, loses the mark even if the ion charges were worked out correctly.

**3.** Three lithium atoms each **lose one electron** (shown as dots), giving three **[Li]⁺** ions [1]. The nitrogen atom **gains three electrons** to give **[N]³⁻** with eight outer electrons: five crosses and three dots [1]. **Square brackets and the correct charges** on all four ions [1]. **[3]**

```
Li•   Li•   Li•   +   N (5 x)
          ->
[Li]+  [Li]+  [Li]+   [ N: 5 x + 3 • ]3-
```

*Examiner insight:* Drawing only one lithium ion is a common way to lose a mark; the number of ions drawn must match the formula Li₃N.

**4.** Magnesium oxide has a **giant ionic lattice** [1]. There are **strong electrostatic attractions between the oppositely charged Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions** [1]. There are **many** of these attractions throughout the lattice, so **a lot of energy is needed to overcome them** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Any mention of molecules or intermolecular forces in an ionic answer contradicts the structure and usually costs the structure mark.

**5. (a)** A **shared pair of electrons** between two atoms [1].
**(b)** The **shared pair of electrons** (negative) [1] is attracted to the **nuclei of both atoms** (positive) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For (b), "attraction between the atoms" does not score; name both the shared pair and the two nuclei.

**6. (a)** **Three shared pairs** between the two nitrogen atoms [1]; **one lone pair on each nitrogen** [1].

```
xx N (xxx •••) N ••
```

**(b)** **Three shared pairs**, one between N and each H [1]; **one lone pair on nitrogen** [1].

```
         xx
  H (•x) N (x•) H
         (x•)
          H
```

**(c)** **Two shared pairs between the carbon atoms** (a double bond) [1]; **one shared pair between each C–H** (each carbon bonded to two hydrogens), with no lone pairs [1]. (Left C = x, right C = o, H = •.) **[6]**

```
  H              H
 (•x)          (o•)
    C (xx oo) C
 (•x)          (o•)
  H              H
```

*Examiner insight:* The bonding pairs and the lone pairs score separate marks, so a diagram with the right shared pairs but missing lone pairs still earns one of the two marks.

**7. (a)** **Liquid**: 25 °C is above its melting point (−7 °C) and below its boiling point (59 °C) [1].
**(b)** The **relative molecular mass increases** from F₂ to I₂ [1], so the **intermolecular forces become stronger** [1], and **more energy is needed to overcome them** [1].
**(c)** Boiling only overcomes the **weak intermolecular forces between I₂ molecules** [1]. The **covalent bond within each I₂ molecule is not broken**; the vapour is still I₂ [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "the bonds get stronger" is ambiguous and does not score; the answer must say intermolecular forces.

**8. (a)** W: **giant ionic** [1]. X: **simple molecular** [1]. Y: **giant covalent** [1]. Z: **metallic** (giant metallic lattice) [1].
**(b)** Only **weak intermolecular forces** between the molecules have to be overcome [1], so **little energy** is needed [1].
**(c)** **Many strong covalent bonds** throughout the structure [1] must be **broken**, which needs **a lot of energy** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Covalent" on its own for X or Y scores nothing in (a): the mark needs simple molecular or giant covalent.

**9. (a)** C60 is made of **separate molecules** (simple molecular) [1]. Only **weak intermolecular forces** hold the molecules together, so they are easily separated [1]. In diamond each carbon is joined to **four others by strong covalent bonds** in a rigid giant three-dimensional network [1].
**(b)** Graphite has **layers** of carbon atoms held together by **weak forces between the layers** [1], so the **layers can slide over each other** [1].
**(c)** Graphite has **delocalised electrons** (one per carbon) that can move along the layers through the whole giant structure [1]. In C60 the electrons **cannot move from one molecule to the next** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), saying the covalent bonds in graphite are weak contradicts its high melting point and does not score; the weak forces are between the layers.

**10. (a)** **Regular, closely packed rows of positive ions**, labelled (for example 2+) [1]; **electrons drawn between the ions, labelled delocalised electrons** [1].

```
 (2+) e- (2+) e- (2+)
 e- (2+) e- (2+) e-
 (2+) e- (2+) e- (2+)
```

**(b)** Electrostatic attraction between the **positive metal ions** [1] and the **delocalised electrons** [1].
**(c)** Aluminium has **delocalised electrons that are free to move** through the structure and carry charge [1]. In solid aluminium oxide the **ions are held in fixed positions** in the lattice and cannot move [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An unlabelled diagram, or one labelled "atoms" or "protons", loses marks in (a); both labels, positive ions and delocalised electrons, are needed.

**11. (a)** **Two shared pairs between carbon and each oxygen** (two double bonds) [1]; **two lone pairs on each oxygen** [1]. (C = x, O = o.)

```
  oo                oo
  O  (oo xx) C (xx oo)  O
  oo                oo
```

**(b)** Carbon dioxide is **simple molecular** [1], with **weak intermolecular forces** that need little energy to overcome [1]. Silicon dioxide is **giant covalent** [1], so **many strong covalent bonds must be broken** to melt it, needing a lot of energy [1].
**(c)** **Diamond** (graphite also accepted) [1]. Shared property: **high melting point** (or solid at room temperature) [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison question needs both substances explained; a full answer about carbon dioxide alone can reach only half the marks in (b).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a charge without its sign, such as "3" for the nitride ion.
- Omitting brackets in formulae with more than one group ion.
- Drawing too few positive ions in an ionic dot-and-cross diagram.
- Leaving brackets or charges off the ions in an ionic diagram.
- Missing lone pairs on nitrogen, oxygen or a halogen in a covalent diagram.
- Writing "bonds" instead of "intermolecular forces" for simple molecular substances.
- Calling C60 a giant covalent structure.
- Writing "covalent" without saying simple molecular or giant covalent.
- In metals, describing the particles as atoms or protons instead of positive ions.
- Explaining only one substance in a "compare" question.
