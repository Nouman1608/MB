## Questions

**1.** Define aid, and distinguish bilateral aid from multilateral aid. **[3]**

**2.** Country B has GNI of 3,000 million units, a population of 9 million and receives aid of 270 million units.

**(a)** Calculate aid as a percentage of GNI. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate aid per head. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the percentage is more informative than the total when comparing countries. **[1]**

**3.** Country B receives a tied grant of 240 million units. Donor suppliers charge 25% more than world prices.

**(a)** Calculate the effective value of the grant. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the amount lost to higher prices. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one other disadvantage of tied aid for the recipient. **[2]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why a government might give aid to another country. **[4]**

**5.** Country A has GDP of 5,000 million units and domestic saving of 15% of GDP. It targets growth of 6% a year. Each extra unit of output needs 3.5 units of capital.

**(a)** Calculate the investment needed, the savings gap as a percentage of GDP, and the value of the gap. **[4]**
**(b)** Aid fills the gap, but only 60% of it is invested and the rest is spent on consumption. Calculate the growth rate that results. **[3]**

**6.** Nominal aid to Country B is 600 million in Year 1 and 630 million in Year 2. The price index of goods the aid buys rises from 100 to 108.

**(a)** Calculate real aid in Year 2 at Year 1 prices. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in real aid. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the result compared with the nominal change. **[1]**

**7.** Country C: in Year 1 (base) export and import price indices are both 100. In Year 2 the export price index is 110, the import price index is 125 and the export volume index is 130.

**(a)** Calculate the terms of trade in Year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the result means. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the income terms of trade in Year 2. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain what (c) shows that (a) does not. **[1]**

**8.** A low-income country depends on one primary export with price elasticity of demand of -0.5. A good harvest raises sales by 12%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in price and in export revenue. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain two problems this creates for the country. **[3]**

**9.** Country G exports a processed good priced at 200 made from a raw input worth 120, so value added is 80. The importing country charges an 8% tariff on the processed good and the price the buyer pays stays at 200.

**(a)** Calculate the price the exporter receives. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the new value added. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in value added. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why this pattern of tariffs may keep poor countries as raw-material exporters. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two ways in which aid could harm the economy that receives it. **[6]**

**11.** Country D has a current account deficit of 180 million. In Year 1 net private capital inflows are 210 million. In Year 2 they fall to 60 million.

**(a)** Calculate the change in reserves in Year 1. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortfall in Year 2 and state two possible results. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why portfolio inflows may be less stable than aid. **[2]**

**12.** Assess whether trade and investment, rather than aid, offer low-income countries the better route to higher living standards. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Aid is a transfer of money, goods or expertise on easy terms or free, to support development or relieve emergencies [1]. Bilateral aid goes directly from one government to another [1]. Multilateral aid is pooled by many donors and passed through an international body [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish by who delivers the aid. Saying only that one is bigger gives nothing to credit.

**2. (a)** 270/3,000 [1] x 100 = **9.0%** [1]
**(b)** 270/9 = **30 units per head** [1]
**(c)** The percentage relates aid to the size of the economy, so countries of different size can be compared [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the division before the answer so your method is visible, and say what the unit of the answer is.

**3. (a)** 240/1.25 [1] = **192 million** [1]
**(b)** 240 - 192 = **48 million** [1]
**(c)** The recipient cannot buy from the cheapest supplier [1], which limits choice and may mean poorer-suited goods and a weaker local industry [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) asks for a different disadvantage from the price premium already found in (a) and (b), so repeating it earns little.

**4.** Humanitarian: to relieve suffering after disaster or famine [1], because need is greatest where incomes are lowest and the donor values the lives saved [1]. Self-interest: aid can secure political allies or trade [1], for example tied aid that creates orders for the donor's exporters [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a short development after it. A list of motives without a link to the donor's aims stays at the level of knowledge.

**5. (a)** Investment needed = 6 x 3.5 [1] = **21% of GDP**. Gap = 21 - 15 = **6% of GDP** [1]. Value = 0.06 x 5,000 [1] = **300 million** [1]
**(b)** Invested aid = 0.6 x 6 = 3.6% of GDP [1]. Total investment = 15 + 3.6 = 18.6% [1]. Growth = 18.6/3.5 = **5.3%** (5.31) [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* State the assumption behind the model. A good answer also says that growth falls short of the target because some aid is consumed.

**6. (a)** Real aid = 630 x 100/108 [1] = **583.3 million** [1]
**(b)** (583.3 - 600)/600 [1] = **-2.8%** [1]
**(c)** Nominal aid rose 5% but real aid fell, because prices rose faster [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the Year 1 figure as the base for the percentage change. Using Year 2 as the base is a common slip.

**7. (a)** 110/125 [1] x 100 = **88.0** [1]
**(b)** Export prices have fallen relative to import prices [1], so each unit of exports buys 12% fewer imports [1].
**(c)** 88.0 x 130/100 [1] = **114.4** [1]
**(d)** Rising export volume means the country can afford more imports in total, even though each export buys less [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Interpret in words that mention both exports and imports. "The terms worsened" alone is a statement of the number, not an explanation.

**8. (a)** Price change = -12/0.5 = **-24%** [1] (linear approximation) [1]. Revenue = 1.12 x 0.76 = 0.8512 [1], a fall of **14.9%** [1]
**(b)** Export earnings are unstable when supply varies [1], which makes planning, tax revenue and investment difficult [1]. Low incomes for farmers who produce the good reduce their spending and may push them out of production [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the low elasticity to the revenue result. The size of the price fall comes from the elasticity, so refer to it.

**9. (a)** 200/1.08 = **185.19** [1]
**(b)** 185.19 - 120 = **65.19** [1]
**(c)** (65.19 - 80)/80 [1] = **-18.5%** [1]
**(d)** The tariff takes more from value added than its headline rate (8% against 18.5%) [1], because the input cost is unchanged [1]. If raw materials enter at zero tariff, selling the raw material is more profitable than processing it, so poor countries stay in primary exports [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show why a small tariff has a large effect on value added. The answer is the fixed input cost.

**10.** Dependency: government may rely on aid and tax less [1]. This weakens institutions and accountability [1], and leaves spending exposed if the aid is cut [1]. Exchange rate: a large inflow raises demand for the local currency [1], the currency appreciates, so exports become dearer [1] and exporters lose competitiveness [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each effect should be a chain with at least two linked steps. Naming "corruption" without how it happens stays at a single point.

**11. (a)** 210 - 180 = **+30 million** [1], so reserves rise by 30 million [1]
**(b)** 60 - 180 = **-120 million** shortfall [1]. Possible results: reserves fall, the currency depreciates or the government raises interest rates or cuts spending [1]
**(c)** Portfolio investors can sell quickly if confidence falls [1], whereas aid is usually a commitment spread over several years [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A sign matters here. A surplus adds to reserves and a shortfall must be financed, so state which one you have found.

**12.** Points that can be developed (the best answers weigh them and reach a judgement):
- Trade lets countries use comparative advantage and sell to larger markets [1]. Export growth raises incomes without donor decisions [1].
- Investment inflows bring capital above domestic saving and fund capacity [1]. Long-term investment can raise productive capacity [1].
- Barriers such as tariff escalation and farm subsidies limit trade gains [1]. Trade depends on rich-country policy as well [1].
- Falling terms of trade and volatile prices limit export earnings [1]. Portfolio flows can reverse suddenly [1].
- Aid reaches where trade and private investment do not, such as emergencies and basic health [1]. Aid can fund the infrastructure that makes trade and investment possible [1].
- Judgement: the answer depends on the country's size, institutions, export base and access to markets [1]. A small, fragile economy may need aid first, while a larger one may gain more from trade. A conclusion that follows from these conditions [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the routes against each other for a particular type of country. A one-sided case for trade or aid, however well developed, leaves the "assess" unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the total amount of aid without relating it to GNI or population.
- Dividing by the wrong index when finding the terms of trade.
- Using the later year as the base for a percentage change.
- Saying tied aid is "bad" without the price premium.
- Treating aid as automatically invested, so ignoring leakage into consumption.
- Describing effects of aid with no chain of reasoning.
- Forgetting that a fall in terms of trade need not mean a fall in export revenue.
- Giving trade and aid as alternatives with no conclusion about which suits which country.
