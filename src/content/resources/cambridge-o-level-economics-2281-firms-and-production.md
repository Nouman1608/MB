This guide covers section 3.6 **Firms and production** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus names three parts: the demand for factors of production, with influences that include "demand for the product, the price of different factors of production, their availability and their productivity"; labour-intensive and capital-intensive production, covering "the reasons for adopting the different forms of production and their advantages and disadvantages"; and production and productivity, meaning "the difference between, and influences on, production and productivity".

The combined [trade unions, factor demand and market structure guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-trade-unions-factor-demand-labour-and-capital/) introduces each part with a cost comparison of two methods. This page goes further: it shows how factor prices and productivity together decide what a unit of output really costs, why the same product is made by different methods in different countries, how the size of the market changes the best method, and why output per worker and output per hour can move in opposite directions. ## 3.6.1 Demand for factors of production

Firms do not want factors for their own sake. They want them to produce goods and services that can be sold, so the demand for a factor is a **derived demand**. Four influences matter.

1. **Demand for the product.** When demand for the product rises, firms need more of the factors used to make it. When a product goes out of fashion, demand for the workers and machines that make it falls.
2. **The price of the factor, and of other factors.** A higher wage makes labour dearer, so firms try to use less of it. If machines become cheaper relative to labour, firms may **substitute** capital for labour.
3. **Availability.** A firm cannot employ what it cannot find. A shortage of skilled engineers, of land in a city centre or of finance to buy machines limits how much of that factor the firm can use, and pushes it towards alternatives.
4. **Productivity.** A factor that produces more output is worth more to the firm. If training raises output per worker, each worker adds more revenue and is more worth employing.

### Worked example 1: price and productivity together

Price alone does not tell a firm which factor is cheaper. What matters is cost **per unit of output**. Two invented countries make the same shirts.

| | Country A | Country B |
|---|---|---|
| Wage per hour | $4 | $20 |
| Shirts per worker per hour | 5 | 40 |
| Labour cost per shirt | 4 ÷ 5 = $0.80 | 20 ÷ 40 = $0.50 |

Workers in Country B earn five times as much, yet labour cost per shirt is lower, because each worker uses better machinery and produces eight times as much. High wages do not always mean high costs; low productivity can make cheap labour expensive.

## 3.6.2 Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

**Labour-intensive production** uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital: hand-made crafts, hairdressing, tea picking, many personal services. **Capital-intensive production** uses a high proportion of capital (machinery and equipment) relative to labour: oil refining, car assembly, electricity generation, automated bottling.

### Reasons for adopting each method

| Reason | Favours labour-intensive when… | Favours capital-intensive when… |
|---|---|---|
| Relative factor prices | Labour is cheap compared with machines | Labour is expensive compared with machines |
| Size of the market | Output is small or demand varies | Output is large and steady |
| Nature of the product | Each item is different or needs personal service | Items are identical and made in bulk |
| Availability of finance | The firm cannot raise money for machines | Finance is available for large investment |
| Availability of skills and technology | Workers lack skills to run machines; technology is not available | Skilled technicians and suitable technology exist |

### Worked example 2: why market size matters

An invented car wash can wash cars by hand or buy an automatic machine.

- **By hand**: each worker earns $15 an hour and two workers together wash 4 cars an hour. With 4 workers it can wash 8 cars an hour at 60 ÷ 8 = $7.50 per car.
- **By machine**: running the machine costs $40 an hour and needs 1 attendant at $15. It can wash 20 cars an hour, which costs 55 ÷ 20 = $2.75 per car when it is busy.

The machine looks far cheaper, but only if there are enough customers. If only 4 cars arrive each hour, the machine still costs $55 an hour, or 55 ÷ 4 = $13.75 per car, while 2 hand-washers cost 30 ÷ 4 = $7.50 per car. A busy city-centre site should use the machine; a quiet village site should wash by hand.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Labour-intensive**
- Advantages: low set-up cost; flexible, since workers can switch tasks and staff numbers can change with demand; suits custom or personal products; provides jobs.
- Disadvantages: higher cost per unit where wages are high; output may be slower and less consistent; workers need managing and may be absent or take industrial action.

**Capital-intensive**
- Advantages: high output at low cost per unit when output is large; consistent quality; machines can run continuously; fewer staff to manage.
- Disadvantages: high set-up cost and possibly heavy borrowing; costly breakdowns; inflexible if demand changes; needs skilled maintenance; may reduce jobs in the community.

## 3.6.3 Production and productivity

**Production** is the total output of goods and services in a period. **Productivity** is output per unit of input, usually **labour productivity**: output per worker or output per hour worked.

Production can rise because more factors are used, without any rise in productivity. Productivity rises only when the same factors produce more.

### Worked example 3: per worker versus per hour

An invented workshop employs 10 workers.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Hours per worker per week | 40 | 36 |
| Total hours | 400 | 360 |
| Weekly output (units) | 2,000 | 1,980 |
| Output per worker | 200 | 198 |
| Output per hour | 5.0 | 5.5 |

Production fell by 1% and output per worker fell by 1%, but output per hour rose by 10%. Workers became more productive in each hour, and the fall in output came only from shorter hours. When a question gives hours, use output per hour to judge productivity.

### Influences on production

The quantity of factors employed (more workers, land or machines), their quality, technology, the hours worked, demand for the product, and events such as weather or power cuts.

### Influences on productivity

- **Education and training**: skilled workers produce more and make fewer mistakes.
- **Capital per worker**: better tools and machines raise output per hour, as in Country B.
- **Technology**: new methods let the same inputs make more.
- **Management and organisation**: good planning avoids delays and waste; division of labour lets workers specialise.
- **Motivation**: pay, conditions and job security affect effort.
- **Health**: healthy workers are absent less and work more effectively.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a rise in production as a rise in productivity.
- Comparing wages between countries without considering productivity.
- Saying capital-intensive methods are always better. They suit large, steady markets with available finance.
- Forgetting that the demand for a factor is derived from demand for the product.
- Listing influences on productivity that are really influences on total production, such as hiring more workers.

## Exam technique for 3.6

- **Define** productivity as output per unit of input, and say which measure you use.
- **Calculate** output per worker or per hour, and percentage changes, showing each division.
- **Explain** a choice of method using one of the five reasons, linked to the firm in the question.
- **Analyse** a change in factor demand as a chain: what changed, its effect on cost or revenue, and the firm's response.
- **Discuss** whether a firm should become more capital-intensive: weigh lower cost per unit and consistent quality against set-up costs, the size of the market, finance and the effect on jobs.
