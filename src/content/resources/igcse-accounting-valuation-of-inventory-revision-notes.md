## The three official points

1. The valuation of inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
2. How to calculate the value of inventory.
3. The effect of an incorrect valuation of inventory on gross profit, profit
   for the year, equity and asset valuation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cost | What the business paid for the units (given per unit in a question) |
| Selling price | What the units are expected to sell for |
| Costs still to pay | Repairs, repackaging, commission, delivery to the customer |
| Net realisable value (NRV) | Expected selling price less costs still to pay |
| Value per unit | The lower of cost and NRV |
| Mark-up | Profit as a percentage of cost |
| Margin | Profit as a percentage of selling price |

## Method: valuing inventory

1. List each item (or group of identical items) with its units.
2. Find NRV per unit: selling price − repair, repackaging, commission or delivery.
3. Choose the lower of cost and NRV for that item.
4. Multiply by the number of units.
5. Add the item values. Never compare the two grand totals.
6. If NRV was lower, the write-down is units × (cost − NRV).

**Why:** prudence says expected losses are recognised now. Realisation and
historic cost say expected profits are not recognised until the goods are
sold, so inventory is never shown above cost.

## Worked reminders

**Cost is lower.** Roddick Rugs holds 40 rugs that cost 18.50 each. They sell for
29.00 each with 3.50 commission. NRV = 29.00 − 3.50 = 25.50. Cost is lower, so
value = 40 × 18.50 = **740**.

**NRV is lower.** Roddick Rugs also holds 60 lamps that cost 34 each. After a
repair costing 6 per lamp they sell for 45, and delivery costs 7.

```
NRV per lamp   45 − 6 − 7           = 32
Lower          32 (NRV, not 34)
Value          60 × 32              = 1,920
Write-down     60 × (34 − 32)       =   120
```

## Method: adjusting a count taken on another date

| Count taken | Start with | Purchases since | Cost of goods sold since |
|---|---|---|---|
| After the year end | counted value | subtract | add |
| Before the year end | counted value | add | subtract |

Convert sales to cost first: cost = selling price ÷ (1 + mark-up), or
cost = selling price × (1 − margin). Goods returned by customers change the
adjustment in the opposite direction to sales. Goods returned to suppliers
change it in the opposite direction to purchases.

**Count after year end.** Counted at cost on 4 January: 12,300. Purchases
1–4 January: 2,150. Sales in that period: 3,900 at a 20% margin.

```
Cost of goods sold    3,900 × 80%   = 3,120
Inventory at 31 December  12,300 − 2,150 + 3,120 = 13,270
```

**Count before year end.** Counted at cost on 28 December: 9,860. Purchases
28–31 December: 1,740. Sales in that period: 2,800 at a 25% mark-up.

```
Cost of goods sold    2,800 ÷ 1.25  = 2,240
Inventory at 31 December  9,860 + 1,740 − 2,240 = 9,360
```

**Other corrections to a draft count.** Goods already sold and invoiced:
deduct. Goods owned but not counted: add. Counted twice: deduct one count.
Damaged or obsolete goods: reduce to NRV if that is lower than cost.

## Method: effect of an incorrect valuation

```
Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
```

| If closing inventory is | Cost of sales | Gross profit | Profit for the year | Equity | Inventory asset |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overstated | Understated | Overstated | Overstated | Overstated | Overstated |
| Understated | Overstated | Understated | Understated | Understated | Understated |

**Next year:** the wrong closing figure is the next year's opening inventory,
so next year's cost of sales is wrong by the same amount the other way, and
gross profit and profit for the year move in the **opposite** direction to
the first year. Equity at the end of the second year is correct, provided
the second closing inventory is right. Profit over the two years combined is
unchanged.

**Worked reminder.** Closing inventory is overstated by 800. Gross profit
shown is 25,800 when it should be 25,000, and profit for the year shown is
12,800 when it should be 12,000. If the opening inventory of the next year
is also 800 too high, that year's gross profit is 800 too low.

**Corrected profit.** Corrected profit = draft profit ± the change in closing
inventory (an increase in closing inventory increases profit).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cost versus NRV:** the lower is used, item by item, not in total.
- **NRV versus selling price:** NRV is selling price after deducting the
  costs still to pay.
- **Mark-up versus margin:** mark-up is on cost, margin is on selling price.
- **Closing versus opening inventory:** an error in closing inventory is in
  the following year's opening inventory.
- **Profit versus equity:** both are wrong at the end of the first year, but
  at the end of the second year only the profits are wrong, not equity.

## Quick self-test

1. Define net realisable value.
2. A unit cost 36. It will sell for 41 with 8 of selling costs. State its
   value per unit.
3. 25 units cost 12.40 each and have an NRV of 10.60 each. Calculate their value.
4. Item A: 30 units, cost 8, NRV 9. Item B: 20 units, cost 15, NRV 11.
   Calculate the inventory value item by item.
5. Closing inventory is understated by 1,500. Give the effect on this
   year's profit and the effect on next year's gross profit.
6. A count on 2 January valued inventory at 15,800. Purchases since year end
   were 2,600 and the cost of goods sold since year end was 2,100. Calculate
   inventory at 31 December.
7. Sales of 4,500 at selling price. Calculate their cost with (a) a 50% mark-up and
   (b) a 40% margin.
8. Draft profit is 14,400. Closing inventory was recorded as 8,100 and
   should be 9,300. State the corrected profit.
9. Why is inventory not valued at expected selling price?
10. A closing inventory error is made in Year 1 only. State the effect on the
    total profit of the two years combined.

### Answers

1. Expected selling price less the costs still to be paid before and when the
   goods are sold.
2. NRV = 41 − 8 = 33, which is lower than cost, so **33** per unit.
3. 25 × 10.60 = **265** (NRV is lower).
4. A: cost is lower, 30 × 8 = 240. B: NRV is lower, 20 × 11 = 220. Total
   **460** (comparing totals would wrongly give 490).
5. Profit for the year is understated by 1,500. Next year's gross profit is
   overstated by 1,500.
6. 15,800 − 2,600 + 2,100 = **15,300**.
7. (a) 4,500 ÷ 1.5 = **3,000**; (b) 4,500 × 60% = **2,700**.
8. 14,400 + (9,300 − 8,100) = 14,400 + 1,200 = **15,600**.
9. A profit is recognised only when goods are sold (realisation), and assets
   are recorded at historic cost, not at an expected price. Valuing at cost
   or NRV avoids overstating profit.
10. **No effect**: the first year's error is reversed in the second year.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing the grand totals of cost and NRV instead of choosing the lower
  figure for each item.
- Taking selling costs off cost instead of off the selling price.
- Forgetting repair costs when the selling price is the price after repair.
- Valuing at NRV when NRV is higher than cost.
- Adding purchases to a count taken after the year end (they should be
  subtracted), or the reverse.
- Using sales at selling price in a count adjustment without converting to cost.
- Applying mark-up to selling price, or margin to cost.
- Giving the effect for the first year only and omitting the next year's
  opposite effect.
- Writing "overstated" or "understated" without the amount.
- Describing equity as wrong at the end of the second year when it has
  corrected itself.
