## Questions

**1.** Explain the term *lattice enthalpy*, and write the equation, with state symbols, for the lattice enthalpy of strontium fluoride. **[3]**

**2.** Write equations, with state symbols, for each of these.

**(a)** The enthalpy change of atomisation of iodine. **[1]**
**(b)** The first ionisation energy of caesium. **[1]**
**(c)** The second ionisation energy of calcium. **[1]**
**(d)** The enthalpy change of formation of caesium iodide. **[1]**

**3.** Calculate the lattice enthalpy of caesium iodide, CsI. Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH(CsI) = −347; ΔatH(Cs) = +76; first ionisation energy of Cs = +376; ΔatH(I) = +107; first electron affinity of I = −295. **[3]**

**4.** Calculate the first electron affinity of iodine using the Born–Haber cycle for rubidium iodide, RbI. Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH(RbI) = −334; ΔatH(Rb) = +81; first ionisation energy of Rb = +403; ΔatH(I) = +107; ΔLEH(RbI) = −630. **[3]**

**5.** This question is about the Born–Haber cycle for strontium bromide, SrBr₂.

**(a)** Starting from Sr(s) + Br₂(l), give the species, with state symbols, on each level reached after: atomising strontium; atomising bromine; the first ionisation of strontium; the second ionisation of strontium; adding electrons to bromine. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔLEH(SrBr₂). Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH(SrBr₂) = −718; ΔatH(Sr) = +164; first ionisation energy of Sr = +550; second ionisation energy of Sr = +1064; ΔatH(Br) = +112; first electron affinity of Br = −325. **[3]**
**(c)** The lattice enthalpy of MgBr₂ is −2435 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why ΔLEH(SrBr₂) is less exothermic. **[2]**

**6.** Lithium iodide dissolves readily in water.

**(a)** State what is meant by the enthalpy change of hydration. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation, with state symbols, for the enthalpy change of solution of lithium iodide. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the equation, with state symbols, for the enthalpy change of hydration of the iodide ion. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the hydration of an iodide ion is exothermic. **[2]**

**7.** Calculate the enthalpy change of solution of sodium iodide, NaI. Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔLEH(NaI) = −705; ΔhydH(Na⁺) = −406; ΔhydH(I⁻) = −307. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the lattice enthalpy of lithium iodide, LiI. Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔsolH(LiI) = −63; ΔhydH(Li⁺) = −519; ΔhydH(I⁻) = −307. **[3]**

**9.** This question is about the factors that affect lattice and hydration enthalpies.

**(a)** Put CsI, NaF and NaI in order of increasingly exothermic lattice enthalpy. Explain your order. **[3]**
**(b)** Predict whether Al³⁺ or Mg²⁺ has the more exothermic enthalpy change of hydration. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**10.** A student finds the enthalpy change of solution of anhydrous lithium bromide. She dissolves 4.34 g of LiBr in 50.0 g of water in an insulated cup. The temperature rises by 10.9 K. Use Ar(Li) = 6.9 and Ar(Br) = 79.9, c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, and assume the solution has a mass of 50.0 g.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released, q, in J. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔsolH(LiBr) in kJ mol⁻¹, giving your answer to three significant figures. **[3]**
**(c)** A Born–Haber and hydration cycle gives ΔsolH(LiBr) = −48 kJ mol⁻¹. Suggest one reason why the experimental value is less exothermic. **[1]**

**11.** This question is about calcium bromide, CaBr₂.

**(a)** Calculate ΔfH(CaBr₂). Use these values, in kJ mol⁻¹: ΔatH(Ca) = +178; first ionisation energy of Ca = +590; second ionisation energy of Ca = +1145; ΔatH(Br) = +112; first electron affinity of Br = −325; ΔLEH(CaBr₂) = −2170. **[3]**
**(b)** The enthalpy change of solution of CaBr₂ is −103 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔhydH(Br⁻) = −336 kJ mol⁻¹. Draw a labelled enthalpy cycle linking CaBr₂(s), its gaseous ions and its aqueous ions, and use it to calculate ΔhydH(Ca²⁺). **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic lattice is formed [1] from its gaseous ions, under standard conditions [1]. Sr²⁺(g) + 2F⁻(g) → SrF₂(s), with correct state symbols [1]
*Examiner insight:* A definition describing the lattice being broken apart into ions describes the reverse process and does not match the H432 definition, so it is unlikely to gain the first mark.

**2. (a)** ½I₂(s) → I(g) [1]
**(b)** Cs(g) → Cs⁺(g) + e⁻ [1]
**(c)** Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻ [1]
**(d)** Cs(s) + ½I₂(s) → CsI(s) [1]
*Examiner insight:* State symbols are part of each equation here; iodine as I₂(g) in (a) or (d), or Ca(g) as the starting species in (c), would not describe the defined process.

**3.** Sum of steps before the lattice forms = 76 + 376 + 107 + (−295) = +264 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; ΔfH = 264 + ΔLEH, so ΔLEH = −347 − 264 [1] = **−611 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A positive answer for a lattice enthalpy is a signal to recheck signs; it usually comes from subtracting the electron affinity instead of adding it.

**4.** −334 = 81 + 403 + 107 + EA + (−630) [1]; EA = −334 − (81 + 403 + 107 − 630) = −334 − (−39) [1] = **−295 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Writing the full Hess' law expression first shows the method clearly, so a slip in the arithmetic still leaves the method visible to the marker.

**5. (a)** Sr(g) + Br₂(l), then Sr(g) + 2Br(g) [1]; Sr⁺(g) + e⁻ + 2Br(g), then Sr²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ + 2Br(g) [1]; Sr²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) [1]
**(b)** Steps: 164 + 550 + 1064 + 2(112) + 2(−325) [1] = +1352 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; ΔLEH = −718 − 1352 = **−2070 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Sr²⁺ has the same charge as Mg²⁺ but a larger ionic radius [1], so the attraction between Sr²⁺ and Br⁻ ions is weaker and less energy is released when the lattice forms [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), using the bromine values once instead of twice is the usual source of a wrong answer; in (c), comparing charge alone cannot explain a difference between two 2+ ions.

**6. (a)** The enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous ions dissolves in water [1]
**(b)** LiI(s) + aq → Li⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) [1]
**(c)** I⁻(g) + aq → I⁻(aq) [1]
**(d)** Water molecules are polar; the δ+ hydrogen atoms are attracted to the negative iodide ion [1]. Forming these attractions releases energy [1]
*Examiner insight:* For an anion, say that the hydrogen end of water is attracted; describing the oxygen end around a negative ion contradicts the charges.

**7.** ΔsolH = −ΔLEH + ΔhydH(Na⁺) + ΔhydH(I⁻) = 705 + (−406) + (−307) [1] = **−8 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The lattice enthalpy must be reversed for the lattice-breaking step; using −705 gives −1418, which is far too large for a solution enthalpy.

**8.** ΔsolH = −ΔLEH + ΔhydH(Li⁺) + ΔhydH(I⁻) [1]; −63 = −ΔLEH + (−519) + (−307) [1]; ΔLEH = −826 + 63 = **−763 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check the sign of the final lattice enthalpy; a value of +763 shows the rearrangement has been done the wrong way round.

**9. (a)** CsI < NaI < NaF (least to most exothermic) [1]. Na⁺ is smaller than Cs⁺, so NaI is more exothermic than CsI [1]. F⁻ is smaller than I⁻, so the ions in NaF are closest and attract most strongly, releasing the most energy [1]
**(b)** Al³⁺ [1]. It has a larger charge and a smaller radius than Mg²⁺, so it attracts the δ− oxygen atoms of water molecules more strongly and releases more energy [1]
*Examiner insight:* An order on its own gains only the first mark in (a); each comparison needs the relevant ion named and linked to the strength of attraction.

**10. (a)** q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 10.9 = **2278 J** (2280 J to three significant figures) [1]
**(b)** n(LiBr) = 4.34 ÷ 86.8 = 0.0500 mol [1]; 2.278 kJ ÷ 0.0500 = 45.6 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; temperature rose, so ΔsolH = **−45.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Energy was lost to the surroundings, so the measured temperature rise was smaller than it should have been [1]
*Examiner insight:* Converting q from J to kJ before dividing and adding the negative sign are separate steps; an answer of 45.6 without a sign, or −45 600, does not complete the calculation.

**11. (a)** Steps: 178 + 590 + 1145 + 2(112) + 2(−325) = +1487 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; ΔfH = 1487 + (−2170) [1] = **−683 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Cycle: CaBr₂(s) → Ca²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) labelled −ΔLEH; Ca²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) labelled ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) + 2ΔhydH(Br⁻); CaBr₂(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) labelled ΔsolH [1]. −103 = 2170 + ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) + 2(−336) [1]; ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) = −103 − 2170 + 672 [1] = **−1601 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), the cycle should show both ions with state symbols on every level; a cycle that shows only one Br⁻ will carry the same error into the calculation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining lattice enthalpy as lattice breaking, which reverses its sign under the H432 definition.
- Writing iodine or bromine in the wrong standard state in atomisation and formation equations.
- Using one halogen atomisation and one electron affinity for an MX₂ compound (questions 5 and 11).
- Leaving out the second ionisation energy for a Group 2 cation.
- Not reversing ΔLEH when it is used in a solution cycle (questions 7, 8 and 11).
- Counting the hydration enthalpy of a doubled anion only once.
- Explaining hydration of an anion with the oxygen end of water pointing at it.
- Giving a trend order without explaining it in terms of ionic size or charge and attraction.
- Leaving the sign off an experimental ΔsolH, or forgetting to convert J to kJ.
