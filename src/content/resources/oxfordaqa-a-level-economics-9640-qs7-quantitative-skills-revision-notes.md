These notes condense quantitative skill **QS7** from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: make calculations to convert from money to real terms. The [QS7 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs7-quantitative-skills/) has the full worked examples; the [QS7 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs7-quantitative-skills-practice/) test you. For the course overview use the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Money (nominal) value | A value measured in the prices of the year it was recorded |
| Real value | A value measured in the prices of one chosen year, so that changes in the price level are removed |
| Price index | A measure of the price level relative to a base year set at 100 |
| Base year | The year whose index is 100; not always the first year in the data |
| Deflating | Converting a money value into real terms by dividing by a price index |
| Inflation rate | The percentage change in the price index from one period to the next |
| Real wage | Money wage adjusted for the price level; a measure of purchasing power |
| Real interest rate | The nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation |
| Real GDP per head | Real GDP divided by population; adjusts for both prices and population |

## Formulas

```
real value (base-year prices)  = money value / price index x 100
real value (year-X prices)     = money value x index in year X / index in its own year
index from inflation           = previous index x (1 + inflation rate)
exact real growth              = (1 + money growth) / (1 + inflation) - 1
real interest rate (exact)     = (1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation) - 1
real interest rate (approx.)   = nominal rate - inflation
real per head growth           = (1 + money growth) / (1 + inflation) / (1 + population growth) - 1
```

## Condensed recall

- Real values let you compare purchasing power across years. Money values can rise just because prices rose.
- Choose the price year, then say it: "2,000 in year 1 prices".
- Converting into a dearer (later) year's prices makes a figure larger; into a cheaper (earlier) year's prices makes it smaller.
- The percentage change in a real series is the same whichever year's prices you use.
- With a mid-series base year, earlier years have indices below 100, so their real values exceed their money values.
- Multiply growth factors to chain inflation rates. Adding them understates the rise in prices.
- A positive inflation rate that is falling means prices still rise, only more slowly.
- A negative real interest rate transfers purchasing power from savers to borrowers.
- For living standards, deflate and then divide by population.
- Pick the index that fits the people or item: a consumer price index for wages and pensions.

## Worked reminders

- A wage of 2,400 when the index is 120 (base 100): real wage = 2,400 / 120 x 100 = 2,000 in base-year prices.
- 1,500 spent in the base year is 1,500 x 125 / 100 = 1,875 in the prices of a year with an index of 125.
- Money income up 7%, inflation 4%: exact real growth = 1.07 / 1.04 - 1 = 2.9% (approximation 3%).
- Inflation of 3%, 4% and 2.5% over three years from 100: index = 100 x 1.03 x 1.04 x 1.025 = 109.8.
- Index with 2020 = 100; 2018 index 94; a 2018 wage of 470 = 470 / 94 x 100 = 500 in 2020 prices.
- Nominal GDP up 8%, prices up 5%, population up 2%: real GDP per head = 1.08 / 1.05 / 1.02 - 1 = +0.8%.

## Exam traps

- Dividing by the index of the year you are converting into, rather than the year the value belongs to.
- Multiplying by the index when converting into base-year prices.
- Adding inflation rates across years.
- Leaving out the price year from a real value.
- Writing "real wages fell 1%" from an approximation when the question asks for exact working.
- Using nominal growth to comment on living standards.
- Forgetting population growth when the question says "per head".
- Rounding a chained index to a whole number before deflating.

## Interpreting a real-terms result

A good answer gives the money change, the real change and the reason they differ. For example: "Spending rose by 10% in money terms, but prices rose by 12%, so real spending fell by about 1.8%: the same budget buys fewer resources." Then question the deflator: an average price index may not match what a particular group buys.

## Self-test

1. A value is 880 when the price index is 110 (base year 100). Find its real value in base-year prices.
2. Express 3,000 from the base year in the prices of a year with an index of 115.
3. Money income grows by 6% and inflation is 2.5%. Find exact real growth.
4. A savings account pays 2% when inflation is 5%. Find the exact real interest rate.
5. Inflation is 4% in year 2 and 6% in year 3. With year 1 = 100, find the index in year 3.
6. A figure of 640 is recorded when the index is 108. Deflate it to base-year prices.
7. Why can a real value be larger than its money value?
8. Spending rises by 10% while prices rise by 12%. Find the exact real change.
9. Which price index is most suitable for deflating pensions?
10. The index has 2020 = 100 and the 2017 index is 90. Convert a 2017 wage of 450 into 2020 prices.
11. Does converting into 2024 prices rather than 2015 prices change the real growth rate?
12. Who gains when the real interest rate is negative, and why?

### Answers

1. 880 / 110 x 100 = 800.
2. 3,000 x 115 / 100 = 3,450.
3. 1.06 / 1.025 - 1 = 3.4%.
4. 1.02 / 1.05 - 1 = -2.9%.
5. 100 x 1.04 x 1.06 = 110.24.
6. 640 / 108 x 100 = 592.6.
7. When it is converted into the prices of a later, dearer year, or when its own year's index is below 100.
8. 1.10 / 1.12 - 1 = -1.8%.
9. A consumer price index, ideally one that reflects pensioners' spending.
10. 450 / 90 x 100 = 500.
11. No; every figure is scaled by the same proportion, so the percentage change is unchanged.
12. Borrowers, because the real value of what they owe falls over time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is "in real terms" the same as "adjusted for inflation"?**
Yes. Both mean the money value has been converted into the prices of one chosen year using a price index.

**When should I use the approximation for real growth?**
Only when the question accepts it or for a quick check. Use exact division of growth factors when a precise figure is asked for.
