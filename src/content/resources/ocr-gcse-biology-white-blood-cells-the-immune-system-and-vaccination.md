## B6.3j: Blood cells that defend the body

A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease. Blood carries cells that find and deal with pathogens, and cell fragments that seal wounds. You need to link each structure to its job.

### Phagocytes

Phagocytes (neutrophils are one example) are white blood cells that destroy pathogens by phagocytosis.

1. The phagocyte detects a pathogen and moves towards it.
2. It engulfs the pathogen, which ends up inside a vacuole in the cytoplasm.
3. Enzymes from lysosomes in the cytoplasm digest the pathogen.

Adaptations to link to function:

- A flexible, lobed nucleus and the ability to change shape let the cell squeeze out of capillaries and move through tissues to the site of infection.
- The cell surface membrane can fold around a pathogen to engulf it.
- Many lysosomes hold the digestive enzymes that break the pathogen down.

### Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes are white blood cells that make antibodies. Their nucleus is large and takes up most of the cell. Each lymphocyte has receptors on its surface that fit one antigen only. When one meets a matching antigen it divides many times, and its daughter cells make antibodies with the same specific shape.

### Platelets

Platelets are not whole cells. They are small fragments of larger cells made in the bone marrow, so they have no nucleus. When a blood vessel is cut, platelets stick to the damaged area and release chemicals. These start a series of reactions in which soluble fibrinogen is converted into insoluble fibrin. The fibrin forms a mesh of threads across the wound. Red blood cells are trapped in the mesh and a clot forms, which dries into a scab. The clot stops blood loss and stops pathogens entering. Platelets are adapted by being small, so they travel freely in the blood, and by releasing their clotting chemicals only at damaged tissue.

### Worked example 1: a blood count

A healthy adult has 7000 white blood cells and 5 000 000 red blood cells per mm³ of blood. A patient with a bacterial infection has 15 500 white blood cells per mm³.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in white blood cells.

```
increase = 15 500 - 7000 = 8500
percentage increase = 8500 / 7000 x 100 = 121.4... = 121 %
```

**(b)** Calculate how many red blood cells there are for each white blood cell in the healthy adult.

```
5 000 000 / 7000 = 714.3... = about 714
```

**(c)** Explain why the white blood cell count rises during infection. More lymphocytes are made and more phagocytes are produced, so that there are enough cells to engulf pathogens and make antibodies against them.

## B6.3k: Non-specific defences

Non-specific defences work against any pathogen. They do not need to recognise a particular microbe. They have two jobs: to stop pathogens getting in, and to destroy those that do.

| Defence | How it works |
|---|---|
| Skin | A continuous, tough barrier that most pathogens cannot cross. |
| Blood clotting and scabs | Seal cuts in the skin quickly (see platelets above). |
| Nose hairs and mucus | Hairs and sticky mucus in the nose and airways trap dust and microbes. |
| Cilia | Hair-like projections on cells lining the trachea and bronchi sweep mucus with trapped microbes up to the throat, and it is then swallowed. |
| Stomach acid | Hydrochloric acid in the stomach kills most pathogens that are swallowed with food or mucus. |
| Tears | Contain an enzyme, lysozyme, which breaks down the cell walls of some bacteria. |
| Phagocytes | Engulf and digest any pathogen that gets into the body. |

The word non-specific is the key. A phagocyte will engulf many different microbes, and stomach acid kills most of what it meets. Compare this with the next section, where each response targets one type of pathogen.

## B6.3l: The immune system

When a pathogen gets past the non-specific defences, the immune system gives a specific response.

Every pathogen has molecules on its surface called antigens. Each type of pathogen has its own antigens. This is the sequence you should be able to describe:

1. The pathogen enters the blood or tissues.
2. A lymphocyte with a receptor that matches the antigen binds to it.
3. That lymphocyte divides to form a clone of identical cells. Most become cells that secrete antibodies.
4. Antibodies are proteins whose shape is complementary to the antigen. They bind to the pathogen.
5. Antibodies can make pathogens clump together, mark them so phagocytes engulf them more easily, or neutralise toxins the pathogen has released (antitoxins).
6. Some of the clone stay in the blood as memory cells.

Memory cells are the reason you rarely catch the same disease twice. If the same antigen appears again, memory cells recognise it at once and divide quickly. You make antibodies faster, in greater amounts, and for longer. This is the secondary response. You usually destroy the pathogen before you feel ill, and you are immune.

The first time, the response is slower because few lymphocytes carry the matching receptor. You may become ill while the clone is built up. That is the primary response.

### Worked example 2: reading an antibody graph

A person is exposed to an antigen on day 0 and again on day 28. Antibody concentration (arbitrary units) was measured.

| Day | 0 | 7 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 31 | 35 | 42 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody | 0 | 2 | 10 | 6 | 3 | 25 | 90 | 75 |

**(a)** Describe the difference between the first and second responses.

- Peak of the first response is 10 units, at day 14. Peak of the second is 90 units, at day 35.
- After the first exposure it takes 14 days to reach the peak. After the second it takes 35 - 28 = 7 days.
- Antibodies are measured at 25 units only 3 days after the second exposure, but only 2 units 7 days after the first.

**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the second peak is.

```
90 / 10 = 9 times
```

**(c)** Explain the difference. Memory cells made after the first exposure recognise the antigen. They divide and make antibodies faster and in larger numbers than the first lymphocytes did.

## B6.3o: Vaccines and medicines

### Vaccination (prevention)

A vaccine contains a safe form of the antigen. It may be dead or inactive pathogens, a weakened pathogen, or parts of the pathogen that carry the antigens. It cannot cause the disease, but it still has the antigens.

1. The vaccine is injected.
2. Lymphocytes with matching receptors divide and make antibodies. This is the primary response, and it is harmless.
3. Memory cells remain in the blood.
4. If the real pathogen later infects you, memory cells give a rapid, large secondary response, and you do not become ill.

Vaccines protect before infection, so they are used for prevention. Some need booster doses to raise the level of memory cells again. Limits to remember: a vaccine is specific to one pathogen, so a measles vaccine does nothing for flu. Some pathogens, like flu viruses, change their antigens often, so the vaccine has to be updated. A few people have side effects such as a sore arm, and some vaccines do not give protection to everyone.

### Medicines (treatment)

Medicines treat people who are already ill. Learn the three named in the specification.

- **Antibiotics** kill bacteria or stop them growing. They work on bacterial structures and processes, so they do **not** work on viruses. Different antibiotics work on different bacteria.
- **Antivirals** stop viruses replicating. Viruses live inside host cells and use the cell's own machinery, so a medicine that harms the virus easily harms the host. This makes antivirals hard to develop, and each is designed for a particular virus (for example, aciclovir is used against herpes viruses).
- **Antiseptics** are applied to living tissue, such as skin or a wound, to kill microorganisms or prevent them growing, for example iodine solution or an alcohol hand gel. They are used to stop infection, not to cure one that is established.

Painkillers relieve symptoms but do not kill pathogens.

### Antibiotic resistance

Bacteria vary. A mutation may make a bacterium resistant to an antibiotic. When the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria die, and resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, so the population is now resistant. Using antibiotics only when needed, and finishing the course, slows this.

### Worked example 3: how many survive?

A population of 4 000 000 bacteria is exposed to an antibiotic that kills 99.95 % of them.

```
percentage surviving = 100 - 99.95 = 0.05 %
survivors = 0.05 / 100 x 4 000 000 = 2000
```

If these 2000 are resistant, they reproduce with little competition and the next population is resistant.

## Common errors

- Saying antibiotics kill viruses, or that taking them cures a cold.
- Saying individual bacteria "become resistant" because of the antibiotic. The resistance was already there by mutation.
- Writing that vaccines "contain antibodies". They contain antigens.
- Saying platelets are cells with a nucleus.
- Giving "kills pathogens" as an answer for a non-specific defence without saying how.
- Forgetting memory cells in an explanation of the second response.
