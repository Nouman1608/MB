## The general pattern

Benzene has a ring of delocalised electrons, which attracts an **electrophile** (an electron-pair acceptor). But because the delocalised ring is stable, benzene needs a **strong electrophile**, which has to be generated using a catalyst.

The reaction has two stages.

1. **Attack**: two electrons from the delocalised ring form a bond to the electrophile, E⁺. This breaks the ring's delocalisation and gives a positively charged **intermediate**, in which the carbon bearing E and H is sp³ and the positive charge is spread over the other five carbons.
2. **Loss of H⁺**: the C–H bond breaks, and the electrons return to the ring, restoring the delocalised system. The final product has E in place of one H.

```
C₆H₆ + E⁺ → [C₆H₆E]⁺ → C₆H₅E + H⁺
```

The restored ring is why substitution happens and not addition. The H⁺ then reacts with the anion produced when the electrophile was generated, regenerating the catalyst.

## Nitration

**Reagents**: a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid. **Conditions**: heat at about 50 °C (below 55 °C).

```
C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O        (nitrobenzene)
```

### Generating the nitronium ion

The sulfuric acid is a stronger acid than nitric acid and protonates it. The protonated nitric acid loses water to form the **nitronium ion**, NO₂⁺, the electrophile.

```
HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂NO₃⁺ + HSO₄⁻
H₂NO₃⁺ → NO₂⁺ + H₂O
overall: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻
```

You may be asked for one equation for the generation of NO₂⁺. An accepted simple version is: **HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + H₂O**.

### The mechanism

```
Step 1: arrow from the delocalised ring (the circle) to the N of NO₂⁺
        → intermediate: ring with H and NO₂ on one carbon, + inside the ring
Step 2: arrow from the C–H bond back into the ring (restoring delocalisation)
        → nitrobenzene + H⁺
Step 3: H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → H₂SO₄       (catalyst regenerated)
```

Sulfuric acid is therefore a **catalyst**: it is used up in making NO₂⁺ and regenerated in the last step.

### Why the temperature is controlled

Keeping the temperature below about 55 °C prevents further nitration to dinitrobenzene. The specification limits you to **monosubstitution**, so that is the product you describe.

### Uses

- Nitro compounds with several nitro groups are explosives, for example TNT.
- Nitrobenzene can be reduced (tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, then alkali) to **phenylamine**, a starting material for **dyes** and other products (see the Amines sub-topics).

## Friedel–Crafts acylation

**Reagents**: an acyl chloride, such as ethanoyl chloride, and a catalyst of **anhydrous aluminium chloride**, AlCl₃. **Conditions**: heat under reflux, in dry (anhydrous) conditions.

```
C₆H₆ + CH₃COCl → C₆H₅COCH₃ + HCl        (phenylethanone)
```

### Generating the acylium ion

AlCl₃ accepts a lone pair from the chlorine of the acyl chloride, and the C–Cl bond breaks to give the acylium ion, CH₃CO⁺.

```
CH₃COCl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻
```

### The mechanism

```
Step 1: arrow from the delocalised ring to the C of CH₃CO⁺
        → intermediate with H and COCH₃ on one carbon, + in the ring
Step 2: arrow from the C–H bond back into the ring → phenylethanone + H⁺
Step 3: H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl     (catalyst regenerated)
```

AlCl₃ is regenerated, so it is a catalyst. The conditions must be **dry**, because aluminium chloride reacts with water. The product is a ketone attached to the ring and is an important building block in synthesis.

## How to draw the intermediate

The intermediate has a ring of six carbons with one carbon bonded to both H and the new group. The positive charge is drawn as a **partial circle (horseshoe)** covering the five other carbons, with a + inside. Show H and E on the same carbon, pointing out of the ring. The final arrow comes from the C–H bond and goes into the ring.

## Worked example 1: nitration equation and conditions

**Question.** State the reagents and conditions, and write an equation for the formation of nitrobenzene from benzene.

Concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid, heated at about 50 °C. Equation: C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O.

## Worked example 2: yield of nitrobenzene

7.80 g of benzene (Mᵣ = 78.0) is nitrated and 8.61 g of nitrobenzene (Mᵣ = 123.0) is isolated.

```
n(benzene)           = 7.80 ÷ 78.0 = 0.100 mol
theoretical mass     = 0.100 × 123.0 = 12.3 g
percentage yield     = 8.61 ÷ 12.3 × 100 = 70.0 %
```

## Worked example 3: acylation yield

15.6 g of benzene (Mᵣ = 78.0) reacts with excess ethanoyl chloride and AlCl₃. 18.0 g of phenylethanone (Mᵣ = 120.0) is obtained.

```
n(benzene)           = 15.6 ÷ 78.0 = 0.200 mol
theoretical mass     = 0.200 × 120.0 = 24.0 g
percentage yield     = 18.0 ÷ 24.0 × 100 = 75.0 %
```

## Worked example 4: choosing between the two reactions

**Question.** Starting with benzene, what reagents would you use to make (a) a nitro compound and (b) a ketone?

(a) Concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ at about 50 °C gives nitrobenzene. (b) An acyl chloride with anhydrous AlCl₃ gives an aryl ketone, for example phenylethanone from ethanoyl chloride. In both cases, one hydrogen of the ring is replaced and the ring stays intact.

## Comparing the two reactions

| Feature | Nitration | Friedel–Crafts acylation |
|---|---|---|
| Electrophile | NO₂⁺ | RCO⁺ (acylium ion) |
| Source | HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ | RCOCl + AlCl₃ |
| Catalyst | H₂SO₄ | AlCl₃ |
| By-product | H₂O | HCl |
| Product from benzene | Nitrobenzene | A ketone (for CH₃COCl, phenylethanone) |
| Important condition | About 50 °C | Anhydrous, reflux |

## Checking a mechanism before you finish

Count the arrows. Nitration and acylation each need an arrow from the ring to the electrophile and an arrow from the C-H bond back into the ring, with a final step that regenerates the catalyst. Check that the intermediate shows H and the new group on the same carbon, and that the positive charge is spread over the ring. A mechanism with these features earns the marks.

## Common mistakes

- Starting the first arrow at the electrophile instead of at the ring.
- Drawing the intermediate with the positive charge on one carbon rather than spread around the ring.
- Missing the arrow that regenerates the ring from the C–H bond.
- Forgetting to show the catalyst is regenerated.
- Writing "nitric acid" without "concentrated", or omitting sulfuric acid.
- Giving a nitrating temperature of 100 °C or more.
- Using aqueous AlCl₃ or leaving out "anhydrous".
- Stating that the electrophile in nitration is HNO₃ or NO₂ rather than NO₂⁺.
- Calling the intermediate stable. It is a high-energy species.

## Exam technique

- **Mechanism**: ring to electrophile, then C–H bond back into the ring, then the catalyst regeneration step.
- **Generate the electrophile**: write the equation, showing the catalyst's role.
- **Conditions**: concentrated acids and about 50 °C for nitration; anhydrous AlCl₃ and reflux for acylation.
- **Explain substitution**: the delocalised ring is restored in the final step.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a catalyst needed at all?**
The ring's electron density is not high enough to attack ordinary electrophiles, so a strong electrophile is generated using a catalyst.

**What does the horseshoe in the intermediate mean?**
It shows that the positive charge is delocalised over five carbons, not located on a single atom.

**Is benzene brominated in this specification?**
The specification names nitration and acylation only. Do not spend revision time on other reactions of this type.
