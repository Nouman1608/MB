This guide covers content item 2.5.1, Organisational structures, in Theme 2 Topic 2.5 Making human resource decisions of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0), Issue 2, July 2022. The broader page on [organisational structures and job roles](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-organisational-structures-and-job-roles/) introduces the terms; this one practises the judgement the specification asks for. Every business and figure below is invented.

Companion pages: the [organisational structures revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-organisational-structures-revision-notes/) and the [organisational structures practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-organisational-structures-practice/).

## What 2.5.1 contains

The specification groups the item into three headings.

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| Different organisational structures and when each are appropriate | hierarchical and flat; centralised and decentralised |
| The importance of effective communication | the impact of insufficient or excessive communication on efficiency and motivation; barriers to effective communication |
| Different ways of working | part-time, full-time and flexible hours; permanent, temporary, and freelance contracts; the impact of technology on ways of working: efficiency, remote working |

The phrase "when each are appropriate" matters: definitions alone earn little, so match each choice to the facts of the business.

## Two separate questions about structure

A structure answers two different questions, and the specification names a pair of terms for each.

1. **How many layers of management are there?** Many layers make a hierarchical structure. Few layers make a flat one.
2. **Where are decisions made?** At the top in a centralised business. Spread out to branches, departments or teams in a decentralised one.

Because these are separate questions, any business can be described by one term from each pair. The four combinations look like this.

| Combination | Invented example | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Hierarchical and centralised | A chain of 40 pharmacies where head office sets every price and procedure | Mistakes are costly and every branch must follow the same rules |
| Hierarchical and decentralised | A national builders' merchant whose regional managers choose local stock | Many layers give control, but local conditions differ |
| Flat and centralised | A six-person design studio where the founder approves all work | The founder is close to every task and wants control of quality |
| Flat and decentralised | A software firm whose small teams choose how to build their part | Skilled staff work best when trusted |

When a question asks whether a structure is appropriate, test it against four facts from the case: the size of the business, how many sites it has, how skilled the staff are, and how costly a wrong decision would be.

## How growth changes the right structure

The specification introduces Topic 2.5 as decisions that growing a business makes necessary, so growth is the usual reason a structure stops being appropriate.

### Worked example: Fernhill Garden Centres

Fernhill began as one garden centre where the owner, now managing director, supervised everyone directly. It now has three sites and 54 staff. The managing director is comparing two structures.

| | Structure A (hierarchical) | Structure B (flatter) |
|---|---|---|
| Layers | 4: managing director, 3 site managers, 9 supervisors, 41 operational and support staff | 3: managing director, 3 site managers, 50 operational and support staff |
| Staff per supervisor or manager at the lowest level | 41 ÷ 9 = about 4.6 staff per supervisor | 50 ÷ 3 = about 16.7 staff per site manager |
| Extra cost of supervisors | 9 supervisors each paid £3,000 a year more than operational staff = £27,000 a year | None |

How to use these figures in an answer:

- Structure B saves £27,000 a year and removes one layer, so messages from the managing director reach staff more quickly and with less chance of being changed on the way.
- In Structure B each site manager looks after about 17 people as well as running the site, so problems may wait longer for attention, and there are fewer promotion steps for ambitious staff.
- Garden centres have a busy spring with many seasonal staff who need close guidance. That points towards keeping supervisors at least for the busy months.

A strong conclusion picks one structure and says which fact decided it: "Structure A is more appropriate because the seasonal staff need close supervision, and £27,000 is a small cost compared with mistakes in a busy spring." Either choice can earn full credit if it is supported by the case.

Fernhill must also decide where decisions are made. Letting site managers order plants for local demand (decentralised) is quicker than waiting for head office, while pricing could stay centralised so customers see one price everywhere. A business can centralise some decisions and decentralise others.

## Effective communication

Effective communication means the right message reaches the right people at the right time in a form they understand. The specification asks for its impact on two things: **efficiency** (getting work done with the least waste of time and resources) and **motivation** (how willing staff are to work hard).

### Spotting insufficient and excessive communication in a case

| | Insufficient communication | Excessive communication |
|---|---|---|
| Signs in a case study | Staff say they "found out last"; orders go wrong; two people do the same job; customers get different answers | Staff complain of long meetings or hundreds of emails; urgent messages are missed; people are copied in on everything |
| Effect on efficiency | Errors and repeated work waste time and materials | Time spent reading and replying is not spent on the main job |
| Effect on motivation | Staff feel ignored and undervalued | Staff feel their time is wasted and become frustrated |

The right fix is different for each. Insufficient communication needs a regular, reliable channel, such as a short daily briefing. Excessive communication needs filtering: fewer meetings, messages sent only to those affected, and a clear subject line that shows what matters.

### Barriers and how to remove them

| Barrier | Example | How a business could reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Jargon or unclear language | A technical instruction sent to new seasonal staff | Use plain words; check understanding |
| Wrong method | A complex change to a rota sent as a short text | Match the method to the message: detail in writing, sensitive news face to face |
| Too many layers | A message passes through four people before it arrives | Remove a layer or allow direct messages |
| Distance and remote working | Staff at three sites or working from home | Shared online channels and scheduled video calls |

Notice the link to structure. A hierarchical structure creates a "too many layers" barrier, so communication is one of the strongest arguments in any answer about flattening a structure.

## Different ways of working

### Hours

- **Full-time** working suits roles that need continuity, such as a manager whom customers and staff contact every day.
- **Part-time** working lets a business add staff for busy periods, such as weekends in a shop, without paying for quiet hours.
- **Flexible hours** give staff some choice over when they work. They can help a business attract parents and carers and keep staff who would otherwise leave, but rotas are harder to plan.

### Contracts

- **Permanent** contracts suit work that is needed all year. They give staff security, which supports motivation.
- **Temporary** contracts suit demand with a known end, such as a seasonal peak or cover for a staff absence.
- **Freelance** workers are self-employed and are hired for a task. They suit specialist, occasional work, such as building a website.

### Worked example: covering Fernhill's spring peak

Fernhill needs 4 extra staff for 16 weeks from March to June, each working 30 hours a week at £12.00 an hour.

- Temporary contracts: 4 × 16 × 30 × £12.00 = **£23,040**.
- Hiring 4 permanent staff for the whole year: 4 × 52 × 30 × £12.00 = **£74,880**.
- Difference: £74,880 − £23,040 = **£51,840**.

Temporary contracts are much cheaper because Fernhill does not pay for 36 quiet weeks. The drawback is that new temporary staff must be trained every spring and may not return. If the same people are willing to come back each year, the training cost falls.

### The impact of technology

The specification names two impacts.

- **Efficiency.** Online rotas, stock systems linked to tills and shared documents cut the time spent on routine tasks and reduce errors. Costs include buying the systems and training staff to use them.
- **Remote working.** Staff whose work does not need them on site can work from home. In an invented example, Fernhill's six office staff work from home three days a week, letting the business move to a smaller office that costs £4,800 a year less. Laptops cost £1,500, so the first-year saving is £3,300. Against this, managers see their team less often, and new staff learn less from colleagues.

## Exam technique for 2.5.1

The command words in Appendix 1 of the specification set what an answer needs.

- **Explain** (no context): one statement of fact plus two expansion points. For example: "A flat structure has few layers of management. This means messages pass through fewer people, so they are less likely to be distorted."
- **Outline**: two linked points placed in the business in the question.
- **Justify**: recommend one of two options using the information given, comparing both.
- **Evaluate**: reach a supported conclusion and say what the decision depends on.

### Annotated model answer

*Question (Outline):* Outline one disadvantage to Fernhill Garden Centres of moving to Structure B.

*Answer:* "Each site manager would look after about 17 staff instead of working through supervisors. This means seasonal staff in the busy spring may not get the guidance they need, so mistakes with customer orders could increase."

*Why it works:* the point uses a figure from the case, and the development is specific to a garden centre in spring.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "flat" and "decentralised" as the same thing. Layers and decision making are separate.
- Recommending more communication when the case shows staff overloaded with messages.
- Describing a freelance worker as an employee on a short contract. A freelancer is self-employed.
- Ignoring cost when recommending permanent, full-time staff for seasonal work.
- Forgetting that hours and contracts are separate choices: a permanent contract can be part-time.
