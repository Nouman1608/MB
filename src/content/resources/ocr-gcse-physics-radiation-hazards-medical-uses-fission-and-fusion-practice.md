## Questions

**1.** State the difference between contamination and irradiation. **[2]**

**2.** For each situation, state whether it is contamination or irradiation.

**(a)** A technician breathes in radioactive dust released from a damaged container. **[1]**
**(b)** A metal part is checked by placing a sealed gamma source on one side and a detector on the other. **[1]**
**(c)** A person drinks milk that contains radioactive atoms from fallout. **[1]**
**(d)** A person sits 3 m from a shielded, sealed source. **[1]**

**3.** A technician works with an alpha-emitting source.

**(a)** Explain why the risk is low while the source is sealed in a case. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the risk is much greater if a sample of the same source is swallowed. **[2]**

**4.** (Higher Tier only) Waste A has a half-life of 20 minutes. Waste B has a half-life of 24 000 years. Each sample has the same number of undecayed nuclei and emits the same type of radiation.

**(a)** State which sample has the greater activity at first, and explain why. **[2]**
**(b)** State which sample stays hazardous for longer, and explain what this means for how it is stored. **[2]**

**5.** (Higher Tier only) Radioactive material is spilt in a laboratory. The activity is 5600 Bq. The area is safe when the activity is 175 Bq or below. The half-life of the material is 4 days. Calculate how long it will be before the area is safe. **[3]**

**6.** (Higher Tier only) A smoke detector contains a small source of an alpha emitter with a half-life of about 430 years.

**(a)** Explain why a long half-life suits this use. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why an alpha emitter is suitable for a device used in homes. **[1]**

**7.** (Higher Tier only) A hospital wants a radioactive tracer to scan an organ. Four isotopes are considered.

| Isotope | Radiation | Half-life |
|---|---|---|
| P | Alpha | 6 hours |
| Q | Gamma | 6 hours |
| R | Gamma | 40 days |
| S | Gamma | 3 seconds |

**(a)** State which isotope is best and give three reasons. **[3]**
**(b)** Give one reason each for rejecting the other three isotopes. **[3]**

**8.** (Higher Tier only) A patient is injected with a tracer of activity 720 MBq. Its half-life is 8.0 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the activity of the tracer after 24 hours, assuming none leaves the body. **[3]**
**(b)** State one reason why the actual activity in the patient would be lower. **[1]**

**9.** (Higher Tier only) Radiotherapy is used to treat a tumour with gamma rays from an external source.

**(a)** Describe how the gamma rays treat the tumour. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the beam is directed at the tumour from several different directions. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the treatment has side effects. **[1]**

**10.** (Higher Tier only) Uranium-235 is used as a fuel in nuclear fission.

**(a)** State what a uranium-235 nucleus usually has to do before it splits. **[1]**
**(b)** State two things, other than the smaller nuclei, that are released when the nucleus splits. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how a chain reaction can happen. **[2]**

**11.** (Higher Tier only) In a model of a chain reaction, each fission causes an average of 3 further fissions. One fission starts the chain (generation 0).

**(a)** Calculate the number of fissions in generation 5. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total number of fissions from generation 0 to generation 5 inclusive. **[2]**
**(c)** State the average number of further fissions per fission that gives a steady release of energy. **[1]**

**12.** (Higher Tier only) Two nuclei of hydrogen-2 can fuse to form helium-3 and a neutron.

**(a)** Describe what nuclear fusion is and state what happens to some of the mass. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why fusion needs a very high temperature. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that nucleon number and proton number are both conserved in ²H + ²H → ³He + ¹n. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Contamination is the presence of radioactive atoms on or inside an object or person [1]. Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source outside, without the radioactive atoms being transferred [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that name what reaches the object (atoms or only radiation) tend to separate the two clearly; "contamination is worse" is a comparison, not a difference.

**2. (a)** Contamination, because the radioactive dust is taken into the body [1].
**(b)** Irradiation, because the source is sealed and only radiation passes through the part [1].
**(c)** Contamination, because radioactive atoms are inside the body [1].
**(d)** Irradiation, because only radiation reaches the person from the sealed source [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving the reason alongside each label is good practice, because a label alone does not show the idea of atoms being on or in the object.

**3. (a)** Alpha particles are stopped by the case, by skin and by a few centimetres of air [1]. Sealed, no radioactive atoms can get onto or into the technician [1].
**(b)** Inside the body there is no skin or air to stop the alpha particles [1]. They are strongly ionising and deposit all their energy in nearby living cells [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating that alpha is "harmless" loses the point; the stronger answer says that the risk depends on whether the source is inside or outside the body.

**4. (a)** Sample A [1]. For the same number of nuclei, a shorter half-life means the same fraction decays in less time, so the rate of decay is higher [1].
**(b)** Sample B [1]. Its activity falls very slowly, so it needs secure storage for a very long time [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer links activity to rate and hazard to duration; saying that a long half-life is "safer" without the second idea gives only half the picture.

**5.** 5600 ÷ 175 = 32 [1]. 32 = 2⁵, so five half-lives are needed [1]. Time = 5 × 4 days = **20 days** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing the ratio, then the number of half-lives, then the time makes the working easy to follow, and a halving chain 5600, 2800, 1400, 700, 350, 175 confirms it.

**6. (a)** The activity falls only very slowly [1], so the source gives a steady output for many years without needing replacement [1].
**(b)** Alpha is stopped by a few centimetres of air or by the casing, so people nearby are not irradiated [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Lasts a long time" is a weak answer; saying the activity stays almost constant explains why the detector keeps working.

**7. (a)** Isotope Q [1]. Gamma rays pass out of the body to the detector [1]. A half-life of hours is long enough for the scan but the activity falls quickly afterwards [1].
**(b)** P: alpha cannot leave the body, and would damage tissue [1]. R: the half-life is so long that the patient would stay radioactive for a long time [1]. S: it decays almost entirely in seconds, before the scan can be done [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Rejections work best when each is tied to a property in the table, so quote the radiation or half-life you are rejecting.

**8. (a)** 24 ÷ 8.0 = 3 half-lives [1]. 720 ÷ 2³ = 720 ÷ 8 [1]. Activity = **90 MBq** [1].
**(b)** The body removes some of the tracer, for example in urine [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the number of half-lives before dividing keeps the working checkable, and an answer of 90 with the unit MBq is clearer than a bare number.

**9. (a)** Gamma rays are aimed at the tumour [1]. The radiation is ionising and damages or kills cancer cells [1].
**(b)** Each beam passes through different healthy tissue, so each healthy region receives a low amount [1]. The beams all cross at the tumour, so the tumour receives a high total [1].
**(c)** Healthy cells that absorb radiation are also damaged [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For an explain question, the comparison "high at the tumour, low elsewhere" is the idea to state; "to cover the whole tumour" is a different and weaker point.

**10. (a)** It absorbs a neutron [1].
**(b)** Neutrons [1] and gamma radiation (or energy as kinetic energy of the fission products) [1].
**(c)** The neutrons released are absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei [1], which split and release more neutrons, so the process repeats [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain reaction answer needs the neutrons as the link between one fission and the next; saying only "it keeps going" does not explain the mechanism.

**11. (a)** Each generation is 3 times the previous one, so generation 5 has 3⁵ [1] = **243 fissions** [1].
**(b)** Total = 1 + 3 + 9 + 27 + 81 + 243 [1] = **364 fissions** [1].
**(c)** **1** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Listing the terms before adding them makes slips easy to spot, and noting that generation 0 counts as one fission avoids an off-by-one error.

**12. (a)** Two light nuclei combine into one heavier nucleus [1]. Some of the mass becomes energy carried by radiation [1].
**(b)** Nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other [1]. At very high temperature they move fast enough to get close enough to fuse [1].
**(c)** Nucleon number: 2 + 2 = 4 and 3 + 1 = 4 [1]. Proton number: 1 + 1 = 2 and 2 + 0 = 2 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show a "show that" by writing both totals for each quantity, because a statement that "it balances" on its own does not show the check.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an irradiated object radioactive.
- Describing contamination as radiation rather than radioactive atoms on or in the object.
- Saying alpha is harmless or always dangerous, without separating outside from inside the body.
- Treating a long half-life as safer, or a short half-life as always harmless.
- Choosing an alpha emitter or a very long-lived source as a tracer.
- Forgetting the unit in activity answers, or the power of 2 in the working.
- Describing fusion as splitting, or fission as joining.
- Leaving out the neutron that starts fission, or the neutrons that continue the chain.
- Writing a bare "it balances" for a conservation check without both totals.
