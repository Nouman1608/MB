These questions cover **3.1.5 Safety in public transport**, statements a to e, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Some questions also use the kinetic energy and work equations from section 3.2.1. Learn the content first from the [Safety in public transport study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport/) and the [Safety in public transport revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A coach travels along a straight motorway at a steady speed. The driving force is 6200 N. State the total resistive force on the coach, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**2.** State the equation that links stopping distance, thinking distance and braking distance. **[1]**

**3.** Name **two** factors that affect a driver's reaction time. **[2]**

**4.** A driver has a reaction time of 0.90 s and is travelling at 25 m/s. Calculate the thinking distance. **[2]**

**5.** A tram of mass 30 000 kg brakes with a braking force of 45 000 N. Calculate its deceleration. **[2]**

**6.** State the effect of each change on the braking distance of a bus, assuming nothing else changes:

**(a)** the road becomes icy **[1]**

**(b)** the bus's tyres are worn **[1]**

**(c)** the driver is tired. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Describe and explain the energy transfers when a moving bus brakes to a stop. **[4]**

**8.** A car of mass 1100 kg travels at 18 m/s. The driver brakes with a constant braking force of 5500 N.

**(a)** Calculate the kinetic energy of the car. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the braking distance. **[2]**

**(c)** The driver's reaction time is 0.75 s. Calculate the stopping distance. **[2]**

**9.** The table shows thinking distances and braking distances for a vehicle tested on a dry road.

| Speed (m/s) | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking distance (m) | 6.0 | 12.0 | 18.0 |
| Braking distance (m) | 7.5 | 30.0 | 67.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the reaction time used for these data. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the relationship between thinking distance and speed. **[1]**

**(c)** Show that the braking distance is proportional to the square of the speed. **[2]**

**(d)** Predict the stopping distance at 40 m/s on the same road. **[3]**

**10.** A bus driver needs to stop the bus in a shorter distance than usual because of a hazard ahead.

**(a)** Explain what must happen to the braking force. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe **two** dangers of a very large deceleration. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why passengers who are standing are at risk when the bus decelerates sharply. **[2]**

**11.** A lorry of mass 15 000 kg travels at 20 m/s.

**(a)** Calculate the braking force needed to stop the lorry in 60 m. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the deceleration this braking force produces. **[2]**

**(c)** The lorry's speed is now 10 m/s. The same braking force is used. Calculate the new braking distance without repeating the full energy calculation. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how each of the following would change the overall stopping distance of a car, stating which part of the stopping distance is affected:

**(a)** the driver has been drinking alcohol **[2]**

**(b)** the road is wet **[2]**

**(c)** the car travels faster. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** 6200 N [1]; at steady speed the resultant force is zero, so the resistive forces balance the driving force [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: tiredness, distractions, drugs, alcohol [2]. **[2]**

**4.** thinking distance = speed × reaction time = 25 × 0.90 [1] = **22.5 m** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** a = F ÷ m = 45 000 ÷ 30 000 [1] = **1.5 m/s²** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Increases [1].

**(b)** Increases [1].

**(c)** No effect on braking distance (it increases thinking distance) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The moving bus has kinetic energy [1]. Friction between the brakes and the wheels does work on the bus [1], which reduces its kinetic energy to zero [1]. The energy is transferred by heating, so the temperature of the brakes rises and energy then dissipates to the surroundings [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Ek = ½ × 1100 × 18² [1] = **178 200 J** [1].

**(b)** braking distance = 178 200 ÷ 5500 [1] = **32.4 m** [1].

**(c)** thinking distance = 18 × 0.75 = 13.5 m [1]; stopping distance = 13.5 + 32.4 = **45.9 m** [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* the most common error in (a) is squaring after multiplying by ½ × 1100, or not squaring at all; square the speed first.

**9. (a)** 6.0 ÷ 10 = **0.60 s** [1].

**(b)** Thinking distance is directly proportional to speed (it doubles when speed doubles) [1].

**(c)** From 10 m/s to 20 m/s the speed doubles and braking distance goes from 7.5 m to 30.0 m, four times larger [1]; from 10 m/s to 30 m/s the speed triples and braking distance is 67.5 m, nine times larger [1].

**(d)** Thinking distance = 40 × 0.60 = 24 m [1]; braking distance = 7.5 × 16 = 120 m [1]; stopping distance = **144 m** [1]. **[7]**

**10. (a)** The braking force must increase [1], so that the deceleration is larger and the bus stops in a shorter distance (more work done per metre to remove the same kinetic energy) [1].

**(b)** The brakes may overheat and become less effective [1]; the wheels may lock and the bus skid, so the driver loses control [1].

**(c)** The braking force acts on the bus, not directly on a standing passenger [1], so the passenger continues forwards at the original speed and may fall or be thrown against other passengers or fittings [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Ek = ½ × 15 000 × 20² = 3 000 000 J [1]; braking force × 60 = 3 000 000 [1]; braking force = **50 000 N** [1].

**(b)** a = 50 000 ÷ 15 000 [1] = **3.3 m/s²** (3.33 m/s²) [1].

**(c)** Halving the speed divides the kinetic energy by four, so the braking distance is divided by four [1]: 60 ÷ 4 = **15 m** [1]. **[7]**

**12. (a)** Alcohol increases the driver's reaction time [1], so the thinking distance and the stopping distance increase (braking distance is unchanged) [1].

**(b)** A wet road reduces the friction between tyres and road [1], so the braking distance and the stopping distance increase (thinking distance is unchanged) [1].

**(c)** Both distances increase [1]: thinking distance increases in proportion to speed, and braking distance increases with the square of the speed because there is more kinetic energy to remove [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* name the part of the stopping distance affected each time; "the car takes longer to stop" alone does not score.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Linking driver factors to braking distance.
- Assuming braking distance doubles with speed.
- Forgetting the thinking distance when a question asks for stopping distance.
- Saying energy is "lost" without naming the transfer to the brakes.
