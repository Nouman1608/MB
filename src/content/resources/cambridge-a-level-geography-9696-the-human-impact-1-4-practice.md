## Section A

**1.** Define the term "recurrence interval". **[2]**

**2.** Explain how abstraction of groundwater can affect the flow of a river. **[3]**

**3.** Outline how a diversion spillway reduces flood risk. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why urbanisation can reduce the baseflow of a river. **[2]**

**5.** State the probability of a "1 in 50 year" flood occurring in any one year, and explain why two such floods could happen in consecutive years. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how a reservoir changes the discharge of a river downstream of its dam. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The annual maximum discharges (cumecs) of the fictional River Orren over 11 years were: 95, 140, 120, 210, 88, 175, 132, 101, 160, 115, 240.

**(a)** Using T = (n + 1) / m, calculate the recurrence interval of the 175-cumec flood. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual probability of a flood of 240 cumecs or more. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** limitations of using this record to predict future flood risk. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows storm hydrographs for the same fictional basin before and after a new town was built, for storms of similar size.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Lag time (hours) | 9 | 4 |
| Peak discharge (cumecs) | 40 | 72 |
| Pre-storm baseflow (cumecs) | 6 | 4 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in peak discharge. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the changes shown in the table. **[4]**

**9.** Compare the effects of deforestation and afforestation on the stores and flows of a drainage basin. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how flood forecasts and warnings reduce the impacts of river floods, and why they do not always succeed. **[5]**

**11.** Evaluate the use of soft engineering to reduce the impacts of river floods. **[8]**

**12.** With reference to a recent river flood event you have studied, assess the relative importance of physical and human causes of the flood. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The average time interval between floods of a given size or greater [1], calculated from a long record of annual maximum discharges [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Pumping water from wells lowers the water table [1]. Less groundwater then seeps into the channel, so baseflow is reduced [1]. In dry periods river flow falls sharply and springs or small streams may dry up [1]. **[3]**

**3.** It carries excess water away from the main channel into an overflow channel or storage area [1], so the discharge passing the protected area stays below bankfull [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Impermeable surfaces stop water infiltrating and drains carry it quickly to the river [1], so less water percolates to recharge groundwater, lowering the water table that feeds baseflow [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 1 / 50 = 2% in any one year [1]. The probability is the same every year, because each year is independent [1], so one flood does not lower the chance of another the next year [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The reservoir stores floodwater and releases it gradually, so downstream flood peaks are lower [1]. Releases in dry periods can keep flows higher than before, so the annual regime is flattened [1]. Evaporation from the reservoir and abstraction for supply or irrigation can reduce total discharge downstream [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 175 cumecs is ranked 3rd of 11 [1]; T = (11 + 1) / 3 = 4 years [1].

**(b)** 240 cumecs is ranked 1st, so T = 12 / 1 = 12 years [1]; probability = 1 / 12 = about 8.3% per year [1].

**(c)** Eleven years is too short to estimate the size of rare, large floods reliably [1]; land-use change or climate change may make past discharges a poor guide to the future [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (72 - 40) / 40 x 100 [1] = an 80% increase [1].

**(b)** Roads, roofs and pavements are impermeable, so infiltration is reduced and overland flow increases [1]. Drains and gutters carry water rapidly to the river, shortening the lag time from 9 to 4 hours [1]. More water arrives at the same time, so the peak rises from 40 to 72 cumecs [1]. Less water recharges groundwater, so baseflow falls from 6 to 4 cumecs [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Deforestation removes the interception store, so more rain reaches the ground quickly [1], whereas afforestation increases interception and interception loss [1]. Deforestation reduces transpiration, leaving soils wetter [1], while afforestation increases transpiration and so creates more soil water storage capacity [1]. Deforested soils are often compacted or eroded, reducing infiltration and increasing overland flow [1], while tree roots in afforested areas raise infiltration and throughflow, though only once trees are established [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Forecasts based on rainfall radar, river gauges and models predict where and when flooding will occur [1]. Warnings give people time to move to safety, reducing deaths [1], and to move possessions, vehicles and livestock, reducing losses [1]. They may fail if forecasts are inaccurate, especially in small, flashy basins where there is little time [1], or if warnings do not reach people, are not trusted or are ignored [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Floodplain zoning keeps housing out of high-risk areas, reducing exposure at low cost [1], but it is hard to apply where towns already occupy the floodplain [1]. Drainage basin management such as tree planting and leaky dams slows runoff [1] and brings wider benefits for wildlife, soils and water quality [1]. However, its effect on very large floods is uncertain and it takes years to work [1]. Wetland conservation stores floodwater, but needs large areas of land that may be valuable for farming [1]. River restoration reconnects rivers to their floodplains and improves habitats [1]. Overall, soft engineering is sustainable and cheap but usually works best alongside warnings and some hard engineering where people are concentrated [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Credit located, dated detail from any recent river flood.

Physical causes: identifies the rainfall event, such as its duration, intensity or total, with detail [1]; explains the role of antecedent conditions such as saturated ground [1]; explains how basin characteristics such as steep slopes or impermeable rock increased runoff [1]; considers other physical factors such as snowmelt or tidal conditions where relevant [1].

Human causes: explains how land use such as urbanisation or deforestation increased runoff [1]; explains how building on the floodplain increased exposure to the flood [1]; considers the role of engineering, such as defences that failed or were overtopped, or channel changes upstream [1].

Assessment: recognises that physical causes usually trigger the flood while human causes increase its size or impact [1]; weighs the two for the named event with evidence [1]; reaches a clear, supported judgement on which mattered more [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Ranking floods from smallest to largest instead of largest to smallest.
- Saying a 1 in 100 year flood "cannot happen again for 100 years".
- Explaining urban hydrographs with no reference to the figures given.
- Listing management methods without saying how well they work.
- Writing a flood case study that describes impacts but never assesses causes.
