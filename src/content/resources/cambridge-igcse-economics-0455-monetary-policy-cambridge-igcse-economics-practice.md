## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define money supply. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is a monetary policy measure?

A: a cut in income tax

B: a rise in government spending on roads

C: a rise in the rate of interest

D: a subsidy for training

**[1]**

**3.** State **two** monetary policy measures named in the syllabus. **[2]**

**4.** A household has a variable-rate, interest-only loan of 70,000. The interest rate rises from 4% to 6%. Calculate the rise in its yearly interest payments. **[1]**

**5.** Which change would most likely help a country reduce a current account deficit?

A: a rise in the exchange rate

B: a fall in the exchange rate

C: a rise in interest rates

D: a fall in exports

**[1]**

**6.** State **one** reason why a central bank might raise interest rates. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Country R's currency changes from 1 R-dollar = 3.00 S-units to 1 R-dollar = 2.40 S-units.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the R-dollar. **[1]**

**(b)** An R-made phone costs 200 R-dollars. Calculate its price in S-units before and after the change. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how this change may affect Country R's rate of inflation. **[3]**

**8.** A firm can borrow to invest in a project costing 250,000 that is expected to earn 15,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the project's expected yearly return as a percentage of its cost. **[1]**

**(b)** The interest rate rises from 4% to 7%. Explain whether the firm is still likely to go ahead. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how an increase in the money supply may affect economic growth. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why a cut in interest rates may fail to increase spending. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse how a rise in interest rates may reduce inflation. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether monetary policy is an effective way to reduce unemployment. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The total amount of money in an economy [1]; including notes, coins and bank deposits that can be spent [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A and B are fiscal policy; D is a supply-side measure.

**3.** Any two: changes in interest rates [1]; changes in the money supply [1]; changes in the foreign exchange rate. **[2]**

**4.** 70,000 x 0.06 - 70,000 x 0.04 = 4,200 - 2,800 = **1,400** a year [1]. **[1]**

**5.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A fall makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.

**6.** To reduce inflation by cutting spending; or to raise the value of the currency [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** (2.40 - 3.00) / 3.00 x 100 = **-20%**, a depreciation of 20% [1].

**(b)** Before: 200 x 3.00 = **600** S-units [1]; after: 200 x 2.40 = **480** S-units [1].

**(c)** Imports become dearer in R-dollars [1]; firms that import raw materials and fuel face higher costs and raise their prices [1]. Cheaper exports raise foreign demand for R's goods, adding to spending and pressure on prices, so inflation is likely to rise [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 15,000 / 250,000 x 100 = **6%** [1].

**(b)** At 4%, interest is 10,000 a year, less than the 15,000 return, so the project would add 5,000 to profit [1]. At 7%, interest is 17,500, which is more than the 15,000 return [1], so the project would lose 2,500 a year and the firm is unlikely to go ahead [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A larger money supply makes credit easier to obtain and tends to lower interest rates [1]. Households borrow and spend more and firms invest more [1]. Firms raise output to meet higher demand, so real GDP rises [1]. Investment also adds to capital, raising the economy's capacity for future growth [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Low confidence: if firms expect weak sales, they will not invest even when borrowing is cheap [1], and worried households may keep saving [1]. Banks may not pass on the cut, or may refuse to lend to risky borrowers [1], so the cost and availability of loans for many households and firms does not change [1]. (Accept: fixed-rate loans; time lags; heavy existing debt, developed.) **[4]**

**11.** Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, so households buy fewer goods on credit and firms invest less [1]. Households with variable-rate loans have less left to spend, and saving becomes more attractive [1]. Lower total spending reduces pressure on firms to raise prices [1]. Higher rates may also cause the currency to appreciate [1], making imports cheaper and lowering the cost of imported materials, which reduces price rises [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Effective: lower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing, so spending and investment rise [1]; firms produce more and hire more workers [1]; a lower exchange rate makes exports more competitive, adding to demand and jobs [1].
Not effective: time lags mean the effect may come too late [1]; if confidence is low, households and firms may not borrow even at low rates [1].
Judgement: monetary policy can reduce unemployment caused by weak spending, especially when confidence is not too low, but it cannot fix unemployment caused by a lack of skills, which needs supply-side policy [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing fiscal measures (taxes and government spending) as monetary policy.
- Reversing exchange-rate effects on export and import prices.
- Comparing a project's return with the interest rate without calculating the money values.
- Explaining a measure without linking it to the named aim.
- Ending a "discuss" question without a judgement and a condition.
