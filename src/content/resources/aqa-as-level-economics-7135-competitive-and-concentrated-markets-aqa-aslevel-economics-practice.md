## Section A: short questions

**1.** State the three factors used to distinguish between market structures. **[3]**

**2.** Which one of the following is a characteristic of a perfectly competitive market? **[1]**

A: Firms sell branded, differentiated products

B: A few large firms supply most of the output

C: Firms can enter and leave the market freely

D: Each firm sets its own price

**3.** Market shares in an industry are 29%, 19%, 16%, 12% and 10% for the five largest firms, with 14% for all others.

**(a)** Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the five-firm concentration ratio. **[1]**

**4.** A market has total sales of 2,500 million. The four largest firms sell 600, 450, 300 and 250 million. Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between pure monopoly and monopoly power. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way in which advertising can increase a firm's monopoly power. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** A new chain of cafés aims to maximise its market share over its first two years. Explain two ways in which this objective is likely to affect its decisions. **[4]**

**8.** Market demand for a medicine is Qd = 1,500 - 100P, where P is the price in pounds and Qd is packs per week. In a competitive market the price would be 5. One firm gains a patent and sets the price at 8.

**(a)** Calculate the quantity bought at each price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in total consumer spending. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the patent holder's pricing leads to a misallocation of resources. **[2]**

**9.** One water company supplies a region at an average cost of 0.80 per cubic metre. If it were split into four companies, each would have an average cost of 1.10.

**(a)** Explain why average costs differ between the two arrangements. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why consumers may not gain fully from the lower cost of the single company. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why profits are likely to be lower for fruit sellers in a large street market with many stalls than for three supermarket chains that dominate grocery sales. **[6]**

**11.** Two large smartphone makers compete fiercely. Explain two ways in which this competition may benefit consumers, other than through lower prices. **[4]**

**12.** After a series of mergers, the three-firm concentration ratio in a country's cinema market has risen from 55% to 80% over ten years. Evaluate whether consumers are likely to be worse off as a result. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of firms [1], the degree of product differentiation [1] and the ease of entry into the market [1]. *Total: 3.*

**2.** **C** [1]. Free entry and exit is a characteristic of perfect competition. A and B describe concentrated markets, and D describes a price maker. *Total: 1.*

**3. (a)** 29 + 19 + 16 = **64%** [1].

**(b)** 64 + 12 + 10 = **86%** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**4.** 600 + 450 + 300 + 250 = 1,600 million [1]. CR4 = 1,600 / 2,500 = **64%** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**5.** A pure monopoly is a single firm supplying the whole market, with no close substitutes [1]. Monopoly power is the ability of a firm, even one with rivals, to raise its price above the competitive level without losing all its sales [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** Advertising builds brand loyalty, so consumers see fewer close substitutes for the product [1]. Demand becomes less price elastic, so the firm can raise its price and lose relatively few sales [1]. *Total: 2.*

**7.** Pricing: it may set prices below rivals', or offer loyalty discounts, to draw customers away from them [1], accepting low or negative profit on each drink in the short run [1]. Investment: it may open many new outlets quickly, financed by borrowing or reinvested profit [1], because more locations capture more of the market even if some sites are not yet profitable [1]. *Total: 4.*

**8. (a)** At 5: 1,500 - 500 = **1,000** packs [1]. At 8: 1,500 - 800 = **700** packs [1].

**(b)** Spending rises from 5 x 1,000 = 5,000 to 8 x 700 = 5,600, an **increase of 600** [1]. Consumers pay more in total although they receive 300 fewer packs [1].

**(c)** The price rise moves up the demand curve, so 300 packs a week that consumers valued at more than the competitive price of 5 are no longer produced [1]. Fewer resources go into the medicine than consumers want at a price reflecting cost, which is a misallocation; the firm gains higher profit at consumers' expense [1]. *Total: 6.*

**9. (a)** Water supply has very high fixed costs for pipes and treatment works [1]. One firm spreads them over all output in the region and gains technical economies of scale, so it is further down its long-run average cost curve than four smaller firms [1].

**(b)** As the only supplier it has monopoly power, so it may set a price well above 0.80 and keep the cost saving as profit [1]. Without rivals it may also face little pressure to keep costs low, so its costs could drift up over time [1]. *Total: 4.*

**10.** The street market is close to perfect competition: many stalls sell similar fruit, buyers can see every price, and setting up a stall is cheap [1]. Each stall is a price taker; a stall charging more loses customers to its neighbours [1]. If stalls earn high profits, new traders join, supply rises and prices fall until the extra profit disappears [1]. The supermarket chains face high barriers to entry, such as the cost of a national network of stores and depots [1]. With only three main rivals and strong brands, each has monopoly power and can keep prices above costs [1]. High profits are not competed away by entry, so they can persist [1]. *Total: 6.*

**11.** Product improvement: each firm tries to launch better cameras, longer battery life or new features to win customers from the other [1], so consumers get higher-quality products over time [1]. Service quality: firms compete through longer guarantees, faster repairs and software updates [1], which raise the value consumers get from the product they buy [1]. *Total: 4.*

**12.** A CR3 of 80% indicates a highly concentrated market, so the largest chains are likely to have more monopoly power [1]. The basic model predicts higher ticket prices, lower attendance and higher profits than in a competitive market, a misallocation of resources [1]. With fewer rivals, there is less pressure to keep costs down or improve facilities [1]. However, mergers may bring economies of scale, such as bulk deals with film distributors and shared marketing, which lower average costs and could allow lower prices [1]. Higher profits may fund investment in better screens and seating [1]. Cinemas also compete with streaming services and other leisure activities, which limits how far prices can rise [1]. The outcome depends on barriers to entry: if independent cinemas can still open, the threat of entry restrains prices [1]. Judgement: consumers are likely to be worse off only if barriers are high and close substitutes are weak; strong competition from streaming makes significant exploitation less likely [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding four firms when asked for a three-firm concentration ratio, or forgetting to convert sales to shares.
- Defining monopoly only as a single firm, which misses monopoly power.
- Writing that monopoly always harms consumers, with no benefit and no judgement.
- Describing competitive markets without explaining how entry lowers profits.
- Drawing theory-of-firm diagrams that AS answers do not need.
