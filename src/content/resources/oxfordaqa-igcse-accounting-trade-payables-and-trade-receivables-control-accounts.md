## 1. What a control account is

The receivables ledger holds one account for each credit customer. The payables ledger holds one account for each credit supplier. A **trade receivables ledger control account** is a single summary account that shows what all those customers owe in total. A **trade payables ledger control account** does the same for suppliers.

Because the specification calls them **memorandum records**, you should explain two points. First, they sit outside the double-entry system: the individual customer and supplier accounts are still the accounts that hold the real entries. Second, their job is checking. The closing balance of the control account is compared with the total of the individual balances. If the two agree, that is evidence the ledger is arithmetically sound. If they do not, something needs investigating.

Only credit transactions with trade customers and suppliers appear. Cash sales and cash purchases do not pass through a customer or supplier account, so they are left out.

## 2. Where each entry comes from

Each line in a control account is a total, not a single invoice. Use the books of prime entry:

| Control account line | Source of the total |
|---|---|
| Credit sales | Sales journal total |
| Sales returns | Sales returns journal total |
| Credit purchases | Purchases journal total |
| Purchases returns | Purchases returns journal total |
| Receipts from customers, payments to suppliers, cash discounts | Cash book totals |
| Irrecoverable debts, contra entries | General journal |
| Dishonoured cheques, interest charged | Bank statement or general journal |

The receivables control account is laid out like a customer account. Anything that increases what customers owe goes on the **debit** side. Anything that reduces it goes on the **credit** side. The payables control account is laid out like a supplier account, so the sides are reversed: anything that increases what you owe goes on the **credit** side, and anything that reduces it goes on the **debit** side.

## 3. Trade receivables ledger control account

**Worked example: Crestfield Traders, month of March**

Opening position: customers owe 18,400 in total (debit balances). Two customers had overpaid, leaving credit balances of 260 in total. During March:

- credit sales 61,350
- cheques and bank transfers received from customers 54,900
- cash discount allowed 1,180
- sales returns 2,360
- irrecoverable debts written off 620
- a customer's cheque for 900 was dishonoured
- interest charged on overdue accounts 140
- contra entry with the payables ledger 700 (see section 5)
- credit balances at 31 March: 340

```
Trade receivables ledger control account - Crestfield Traders
Debit side                          Credit side
Balance b/d (debit balances) 18,400  Balance b/d (credit balances)   260
Credit sales                 61,350  Bank                         54,900
Dishonoured cheque              900  Cash discount allowed         1,180
Interest on overdue accounts    140  Sales returns                 2,360
Balance c/d (credit balances)   340  Irrecoverable debts             620
                                     Contra: payables ledger         700
                                     Balance c/d (debit balances) 21,110
                             -------                              -------
                             81,130                               81,130
```

The closing debit balances are the balancing figure, found by totalling the debit side (81,130) and subtracting the other credit-side items (60,020), which gives 21,110.

Notice what each entry does:

- A **dishonoured cheque** reverses an earlier receipt, so the customer owes the money again. It goes on the debit side.
- **Interest charged on overdue accounts** is added to what the customer owes, so it is a debit. The matching credit goes to an interest income account in the general ledger.
- **Credit balances in the receivables ledger** arise when a customer has overpaid or has returned goods after paying. The business owes that customer. They are carried down on the debit side and brought down on the credit side, so that the closing debit balances figure stays accurate.

## 4. Trade payables ledger control account

**Worked example: Selwick Hardware, month of March**

Opening position: Selwick Hardware owes suppliers 12,700 (credit balances). It had overpaid one supplier by 150 (a debit balance in the payables ledger). During March:

- credit purchases 38,200
- payments to suppliers 33,640
- cash discount received 720
- purchases returns 1,540
- interest charged to Selwick Hardware by a supplier for overdue payment 85
- contra entry with the receivables ledger 500
- debit balances at 31 March: 210

```
Trade payables ledger control account - Selwick Hardware
Debit side                           Credit side
Balance b/d (debit balances)    150  Balance b/d (credit balances) 12,700
Bank                         33,640  Credit purchases              38,200
Cash discount received          720  Interest on overdue accounts      85
Purchases returns             1,540  Balance c/d (debit balances)     210
Contra: receivables ledger      500
Balance c/d (credit balances) 14,645
                             -------                               -------
                             51,195                                51,195
```

Here the credit side totals 51,195, the debit items other than the closing balance total 36,550, and the balancing figure is 14,645.

The **debit balances in the payables ledger** mirror the credit balances in the receivables ledger. They arise from an overpayment or from returning goods that were already paid for. The supplier owes the business, so these sit on the opposite side to the normal supplier balances.

## 5. Contra entries

A contra entry is used when one trader is both a customer and a supplier. Suppose Norbury Packaging owes Crestfield Traders 700 for goods bought on credit, and Crestfield Traders owes Norbury Packaging 1,150 for packaging. The two amounts can be set off against each other. The smaller amount, 700, is the contra.

The general journal records it as: debit Norbury Packaging in the payables ledger 700, credit Norbury Packaging in the receivables ledger 700. In the control accounts that means:

- credit side of the receivables control account: 700 (debts owed by customers fall)
- debit side of the payables control account: 700 (amounts owed to suppliers fall)

After the contra, Crestfield Traders still owes Norbury Packaging 450 (1,150 less 700). The contra entry must appear in both control accounts. If you put it in only one, the two control accounts will no longer agree with their ledgers.

## 6. Understanding and interpreting a control account

Once a control account is complete, compare its closing balance with the total of the list of individual balances taken from the ledger. Keep debit balances and credit balances separate in your working, as they are in the control account.

**Worked interpretation: Crestfield Traders**

The control account shows closing debit balances of 21,110. The list of individual customer debit balances adds up to 20,860. The difference is 250, and the list is lower.

Ask what could cause the gap:

- a sales invoice for 250 was entered in the sales journal total but never posted to a customer's account
- a receipt or discount of 250 was posted twice to individual accounts
- the list of balances was added up wrongly
- the sales journal total used in the control account was added up wrongly (in which case the control account, not the ledger, is wrong)

The control account tells you that an error exists. It does not say which account contains it. You must then check the individual accounts and the totals used. When the cause is found, correct the faulty record and see whether the two figures now agree.

Know the limits of this check. If a receipt of 300 is posted to the wrong customer's account, the control account and the list still agree, because the total is unchanged. If the same wrong amount is put into both the control account and the ledger, they also agree. So agreement is helpful evidence, not proof that every account is right.

A control account can also be used to find a missing figure. If you know the opening balance, closing balance and every item except credit sales, the balancing figure is credit sales. This is a common way of using the account when records are incomplete.

## Common errors

- Putting a payables control account entry on the receivables side. Check which ledger you are working in and remember that the sides are opposite.
- Leaving out the debit balances in the payables ledger or the credit balances in the receivables ledger, so the closing balance is wrong.
- Entering a contra on the same side in both accounts. It reduces both totals.
- Including cash sales or cash purchases.
- Treating the control account as part of the double entry, or forgetting to state that it is a memorandum record.
- Calling the difference between the control account and the list an error in the ledger, without allowing for an error in a journal total.
