## 4.2.2.5 Health and disease

- **Health** is the state of physical and mental well-being. It is more than just not having a disease.
- **Diseases** are major causes of ill health. They are:
  - **communicable**: caused by a pathogen and can spread between people (for example measles);
  - **non-communicable**: not caused by a pathogen and cannot spread (for example coronary heart disease).
- **Other factors** can strongly affect physical and mental health: **diet**, **stress** and **life situations**
  (such as income, housing, work and access to healthcare).

### How different types of disease interact

| Interaction | Example |
|---|---|
| Defects in the immune system make infectious diseases more likely | HIV damages the immune system, so other infections take hold more easily |
| Viruses living in cells can trigger cancers | Infection with HPV can lead to cervical cancer |
| An immune reaction first caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies | Skin rashes and asthma |
| Severe physical ill health can lead to mental illness | Depression after a long or serious illness |

A person can have several conditions at once, and one can make another more likely.

## Handling disease data

### Incidence

Incidence is the number of **new cases** of a disease in a population over a given time. Rates let you compare
populations of different sizes.

```
rate per 100 000 = (number of cases / population) x 100 000
```

**Worked reminder.** A town of 250 000 people has 340 new cases in a year. Rate = 340 / 250 000 x 100 000 =
**136 per 100 000** per year.

### Which chart?

| Data | Chart |
|---|---|
| Categories (for example cases in different countries) | Bar chart, with gaps between bars |
| Continuous data in classes (for example cases by age group) | Histogram, with no gaps between bars |
| Two variables measured on the same people or places | Scatter diagram |
| Counts in groups | Frequency table, then a bar chart or histogram |

In a histogram with **unequal class widths**, the bar height is frequency density = frequency / class width, so
the **area** of each bar shows the frequency.

### Correlation from a scatter diagram

| Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Points rise from left to right | Positive correlation |
| Points fall from left to right | Negative correlation |
| No pattern | No correlation |

A correlation shows the variables are linked. It does **not** on its own prove that one causes the other.

### Principles of sampling (epidemiological data)

- Studying a whole population is rarely possible, so scientists study a **sample**.
- The sample should be **large**, so that chance results matter less.
- It should be **representative** (similar ages, sexes, backgrounds to the population) and chosen **at random**,
  so the results are not biased.
- Results are more trustworthy when other studies **repeat** them and find the same pattern.

## 4.2.2.6 Risk factors

A **risk factor** is linked to an **increased rate** of a disease. Risk factors can be:

- aspects of a person's **lifestyle** (for example smoking, diet, exercise, alcohol);
- **substances** in the person's body or environment (for example cholesterol in the blood, or radon gas).

For some risk factors a **causal mechanism** has been proven (scientists know how the factor causes the
disease): for example, chemicals in tobacco smoke damage the DNA in lung cells, leading to lung cancer. For
others there is only a correlation so far.

**Many diseases are caused by the interaction of several factors**, so one person with a risk factor may never
get the disease, while another without it might.

### The specified links

| Risk factor | Disease or effect | Brief mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Diet high in saturated fat, smoking, lack of exercise | Cardiovascular disease | Raised blood cholesterol and damage to artery walls lead to fatty deposits; regular exercise lowers the risk |
| Obesity | Type 2 diabetes | Body cells stop responding properly to insulin |
| Alcohol | Liver damage and reduced brain function | Alcohol is toxic: it damages liver cells (cirrhosis) and slows the brain; long-term use can damage brain tissue |
| Smoking | Lung disease and lung cancer | Tar damages the airways and alveoli (for example bronchitis and emphysema) and contains carcinogens |
| Smoking and alcohol in pregnancy | Harm to the unborn baby | Smoking: carbon monoxide cuts the oxygen the blood carries, linked to low birth weight and premature birth. Alcohol: can damage the baby's development, including the brain |
| Carcinogens, including ionising radiation | Cancer | Damage DNA, so cells may divide uncontrollably. Ionising radiation includes X-rays and gamma rays |

### Lifestyle at local, national and global levels

The incidence of non-communicable diseases changes with lifestyle in a place. For example, an area where more
people smoke has more lung cancer, and rising obesity in a country brings more Type 2 diabetes. As diets and
habits change around the world, these diseases are becoming more common in many lower-income countries too.

### Human and financial costs

| Level | Human cost | Financial cost |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Pain, worse quality of life, shorter life | Lost earnings; possibly care costs |
| Local community | Families care for relatives; people leave work | Pressure on local health and care services |
| Nation | Fewer healthy workers | Cost of treatment (in the UK, mainly to the NHS); lost productivity |
| Global | Millions of early deaths | Huge treatment costs; slower economic development |

## Method in steps: interpreting risk-factor data

```
1. Read the axes, units and the size of the sample.
2. Describe the trend, quoting numbers from the graph or table.
3. Compare groups with a calculation (difference, or "how many times more likely").
4. Say whether it is a correlation; do not claim cause unless a mechanism is given.
5. Comment on the data: sample size, representative sampling, other factors that were not controlled.
```

**Worked reminder.** In a study, 24 in every 1000 smokers and 3 in every 1000 non-smokers developed a disease.
24 / 3 = 8, so smokers in this sample were **8 times** as likely to develop it. That is a strong correlation; a
causal link needs a known mechanism and repeated studies.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Health vs disease:** health is physical and mental well-being; disease is one major cause of ill health.
- **Communicable vs non-communicable:** caused by a pathogen and can spread vs cannot spread.
- **Correlation vs causation:** linked vs one proven to cause the other through a mechanism.
- **Risk factor vs cause:** a risk factor raises the rate of a disease; it does not guarantee it.
- **Bar chart vs histogram:** categories with gaps vs continuous classes with no gaps.

## Quick self-test

1. Define health.
2. Give the three "other factors" the specification lists that affect physical and mental health.
3. Give two examples of how different types of disease interact.
4. What is a risk factor? Give the two kinds.
5. Name the risk factor linked to Type 2 diabetes.
6. Give two effects of alcohol named in the specification.
7. How does smoking in pregnancy harm the unborn baby?
8. A scatter diagram shows that countries with higher sugar intake have more Type 2 diabetes. Does this prove sugar causes diabetes? Explain.
9. Give two features of a good sample for a study of a risk factor.
10. A city of 400 000 people has 1200 new cases of a disease in a year. Calculate the rate per 100 000.
11. Give one human cost and one financial cost of a non-communicable disease to a nation.

### Answers

1. The state of physical and mental well-being.
2. Diet, stress and life situations.
3. Any two: immune defects make infectious disease more likely; viruses can trigger cancers; immune reactions to a
   pathogen can trigger allergies such as rashes and asthma; severe physical ill health can lead to depression.
4. Something linked to an increased rate of a disease. Aspects of lifestyle; substances in the body or environment.
5. Obesity.
6. Damage to the liver; reduced brain function.
7. Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen carried by the mother's blood, linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
8. No. It shows a correlation. Other factors (such as obesity or exercise) may explain it, and a causal mechanism
   is needed.
9. Any two: large; representative of the population; chosen at random.
10. 1200 / 400 000 x 100 000 = **300 per 100 000**.
11. Human: early deaths or fewer healthy people able to work. Financial: cost of treatment to the health service,
    or lost productivity.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a risk factor "causes" a disease. It **increases the rate** (or risk).
- Concluding causation from a scatter diagram alone.
- Describing a trend without quoting any figures from the data.
- Comparing raw case numbers from populations of different sizes instead of rates.
- Forgetting that health includes **mental** well-being.
- Mixing up communicable and non-communicable examples.
- Drawing gaps between the bars of a histogram.
