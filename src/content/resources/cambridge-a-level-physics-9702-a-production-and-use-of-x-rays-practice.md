Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Attenuation coefficients are illustrative values: soft tissue 20 m⁻¹, bone 60 m⁻¹.

## Section A

**1.** State how the electrons in an X-ray tube are released from the cathode. **[1]**

**2.** An X-ray tube operates with an accelerating p.d. of 30 kV.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum energy of an X-ray photon produced, in joules. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the minimum wavelength of the X-rays. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the minimum wavelength of the X-rays from a tube does not change when the tube current is increased. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by contrast in an X-ray image. **[1]**

**5.** Calculate the fraction of the intensity of an X-ray beam that is transmitted through 4.0 cm of soft tissue. **[1]**

**6.** Calculate the thickness of bone that reduces the intensity of an X-ray beam to half its initial value. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An X-ray tube operates at 90 kV with a tube current of 8.0 mA.

**(a)** Calculate the number of electrons striking the target each second. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the power delivered to the target by the electron beam. **[1]**

**(c)** About 1 % of this power is emitted as X-rays. Calculate the power emitted as X-rays. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest why the anode of the tube is made to rotate. **[1]**

**8.** The operator of an X-ray tube can change the accelerating p.d. and the filament current.

**(a)** The p.d. is increased from 40 kV to 80 kV. State the effect on the minimum wavelength. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the effect of increasing the filament current on the X-ray beam, at constant p.d. **[2]**

**9.** An X-ray beam passes through a limb. Beam A passes through 10.0 cm of soft tissue only. Beam B passes through 7.0 cm of soft tissue and 3.0 cm of bone.

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the incident intensity transmitted along path A. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the fraction of the incident intensity transmitted along path B. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the ratio of the two transmitted intensities, and comment on the contrast in the image. **[1]**

**10.** Blood vessels are difficult to see on an ordinary X-ray image.

**(a)** Explain why. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how injecting a contrast medium containing iodine allows the blood vessels to be seen. **[2]**

**11.** **(a)** Describe how a CT scanner produces a three-dimensional image of an internal structure. **[4]**

**(b)** State one advantage of a CT image over a single X-ray image. **[1]**

**12.** The intensity of an X-ray beam falls to 25 % of its initial value after passing through 2.3 cm of a material. Calculate the attenuation coefficient of the material. **[2]**

**13.** Calculate the accelerating p.d. needed to produce X-rays with a minimum wavelength of 3.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** By thermionic emission from a heated filament [1].

**2. (a)** E = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 30 × 10³ = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ J [1].

**(b)** λ_min = hc/E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 4.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m [1].

**3.** The minimum wavelength is set by the maximum photon energy, which equals the kinetic energy eV of one electron [1]; increasing the current increases the number of electrons per second, but not the energy of each, which depends only on the p.d. [1].

**4.** The difference in degree of blackening (brightness) between different areas of the image [1].

**5.** I/I₀ = e^(−20 × 0.040) = 0.45 [1].

**6.** ½ = e^(−60x) [1]; x = ln 2 ÷ 60 = 0.012 m (1.2 cm) [1].

**7. (a)** 8.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.0 × 10¹⁶ [1].

**(b)** P = VI = 90 × 10³ × 8.0 × 10⁻³ = 720 W [1].

**(c)** 0.01 × 720 = 7.2 W [1].

**(d)** About 99 % of the power becomes thermal energy in the target; rotating the anode spreads the heating over a larger area so the target does not melt [1].

**8. (a)** The minimum wavelength halves [1].

**(b)** More electrons are released per second, so more electrons strike the target per second [1]; the beam has a greater intensity (more photons per second), but the photon energies and minimum wavelength are unchanged [1].

**9. (a)** e^(−20 × 0.100) = e^(−2.0) = 0.14 [1].

**(b)** I/I₀ = e^(−(20 × 0.070 + 60 × 0.030)) [1] = e^(−3.2) = 0.041 [1].

**(c)** 0.135 ÷ 0.041 = 3.3; the intensities behind the two paths are very different, so the bone gives good contrast against the soft tissue [1].

**10. (a)** Blood and the surrounding soft tissue have similar attenuation coefficients, so they transmit similar intensities and there is little contrast [1].

**(b)** Iodine has a large attenuation coefficient [1], so blood containing it absorbs X-rays much more than the surrounding tissue, giving a large difference in transmitted intensity (high contrast) [1].

**11. (a)** X-ray images of the same thin section are taken from many different angles as the source and detectors rotate around the patient [1]. A computer combines these to produce a 2D image of that section [1]. The process is repeated for further sections along an axis through the body [1]. The 2D images of the sections are combined to give a 3D image [1].

**(b)** Any one [1]: structures do not overlap as they do in a single image; the image can be viewed from any angle or as any slice; soft tissues with similar attenuation can be distinguished more easily.

**12.** 0.25 = e^(−μ × 0.023) [1]; μ = ln 4 ÷ 0.023 = 60 m⁻¹ [1].

**13.** V = hc/(eλ_min) [1] = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.1 × 10⁻¹¹) = 4.0 × 10⁴ V (40 kV) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert kilovolts to volts before using λ_min = hc/(eV).
- Linking the minimum wavelength to the tube current or the target metal.
- Using thickness in cm with an attenuation coefficient in m⁻¹.
- Multiplying attenuation coefficients for two layers instead of adding the μx terms.
- Describing contrast as the sharpness or clarity of the image.
- CT descriptions that omit "same section from different angles" or "repeated along an axis".
