Condensed notes on subtopic 7.3 Doppler effect for sound waves (Cambridge
9702, AS Topic 7). For the full explanation and worked examples, use the
[Doppler effect study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-doppler-effect-for-sound-waves/);
for the whole topic, see the [Waves revision notes](/resources/as-physics-waves-revision-notes/).

## What 7.3 asks you to do

- Understand that a source of sound moving relative to a **stationary observer** gives an observed frequency different from the source frequency
- Use f₀ = fₛv/(v ± vₛ)
- The moving-observer case is **not required**

## The formula

```
fo = fs v / (v - vs)     source moving TOWARDS observer  ->  fo > fs
fo = fs v / (v + vs)     source moving AWAY from observer ->  fo < fs

fo = observed frequency     fs = source frequency
v  = speed of sound         vs = speed of source
```

## The explanation chain (learn in order)

1. The source emits wavefronts at frequency fₛ.
2. Between emissions, the source moves towards the observer.
3. So wavefronts ahead are **closer together**: observed wavelength (v − vₛ)/fₛ is shorter.
4. The **speed of sound is unchanged** (it depends on the air).
5. f = v/λ, so the observed frequency is **higher**.
6. Behind the source, wavefronts are spread out: longer wavelength, lower frequency.

## What a stationary listener hears

| Stage | Observed frequency |
|---|---|
| Source approaching at constant speed | Constant, higher than fₛ |
| Source passing | Falls |
| Source receding at constant speed | Constant, lower than fₛ |

Loudness rising and falling is a separate effect of distance, not the Doppler effect.

## Problem types and methods

| Given | Find | Method |
|---|---|---|
| fₛ, v, vₛ | f₀ | Choose sign, substitute |
| fₛ, f₀, v | vₛ | Rearrange: v − vₛ = fₛv/f₀ (approaching) |
| f₀ approaching and f₀ receding, v | vₛ and fₛ | Divide the equations: f_approach/f_recede = (v + vₛ)/(v − vₛ) |
| Source on a circle | Max and min f₀ | vₛ = 2πr × revolutions per second; max when moving directly towards |

**Example.** A siren of frequency 450 Hz on a vehicle moving at 30 m s⁻¹; v = 340 m s⁻¹.

```
approaching: fo = 450 x 340 / 310 = 494 Hz
receding:    fo = 450 x 340 / 370 = 414 Hz
```

**Example — ratio method.** Observed 660 Hz approaching and 600 Hz receding; v = 340 m s⁻¹.

```
660 / 600 = 1.1 = (v + vs) / (v - vs)
1.1 v - 1.1 vs = v + vs      0.1 v = 2.1 vs      vs = 16.2 m/s
fs = 660 x (340 - 16.2) / 340 = 629 Hz
```

**Example — whirled source.** A 900 Hz buzzer moves in a circle of radius 0.60 m, making 3.0 revolutions per second; v = 340 m s⁻¹.

```
vs = 2 pi r x 3.0 = 2 pi x 0.60 x 3.0 = 11.3 m/s
maximum fo = 900 x 340 / (340 - 11.3) = 931 Hz
minimum fo = 900 x 340 / (340 + 11.3) = 871 Hz
```

The observed frequency cycles between these values three times per second.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to recall |
|---|---|
| Doppler effect | Change in observed frequency when a source moves relative to a stationary observer |
| Source frequency fₛ | Frequency at which the source emits; unchanged by its motion |
| Observed frequency f₀ | Frequency received by the stationary observer |
| Speed of sound v | Set by the medium; not affected by the source's speed |
| Observed wavelength | Spacing of wavefronts reaching the observer: (v ∓ vₛ)/fₛ |

## Exam traps

- v + vₛ used for an approaching source (gives a lower frequency: check the direction).
- Claiming the speed of sound changes with the source's speed.
- Claiming the source's own frequency changes.
- Describing "it gets louder" as the Doppler effect.
- Taking fₛ as the average of the two observed frequencies.
- Applying the moving-observer case, which this syllabus does not require.
- Using the angular or rotational frequency instead of the linear speed for a whirled source.

## Self-test

1. What happens to the observed frequency when a sound source moves towards a stationary observer?
2. Explain, in terms of wavefronts, why the observed frequency is higher for an approaching source.
3. A 600 Hz source approaches a stationary observer at 25 m s⁻¹ (v = 340 m s⁻¹). Find f₀.
4. The same source moves away at 25 m s⁻¹. Find f₀.
5. Find the wavelength of the sound ahead of the source in question 3.
6. Does the speed of sound reaching the observer change?
7. A 500 Hz source approaching an observer is heard at 525 Hz (v = 340 m s⁻¹). Find its speed.
8. Which Doppler case is not required by the syllabus?
9. Describe the frequency heard as a source moving in a straight line passes the observer.
10. A whistle is whirled in a horizontal circle. When is the observed frequency greatest?
11. Observed frequencies are 660 Hz approaching and 600 Hz receding (v = 340 m s⁻¹). Find the source speed.
12. Why is an increase in loudness not evidence of the Doppler effect?

**Answers:**
1. It is **higher** than the source frequency.
2. The source moves towards the observer between emitting wavefronts, so the wavefronts are closer together; the wavelength is shorter, the speed is unchanged, so the frequency is higher.
3. 600 × 340 ÷ 315 = **648 Hz**.
4. 600 × 340 ÷ 365 = **559 Hz**.
5. (340 − 25) ÷ 600 = **0.525 m**.
6. **No**; it depends only on the medium.
7. 340 − 500 × 340 ÷ 525 = **16 m s⁻¹**.
8. A stationary source with a moving observer.
9. Constant and higher while approaching, then a fall, then constant and lower while receding.
10. When the whistle's velocity is directed straight towards the observer.
11. **16 m s⁻¹** (16.2 m s⁻¹).
12. Loudness changes with distance; the Doppler effect is a change in frequency.
