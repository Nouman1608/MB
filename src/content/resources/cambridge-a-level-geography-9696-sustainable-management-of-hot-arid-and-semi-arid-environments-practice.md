## Section A

**1.** Define the term "fossil groundwater". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between top-down and bottom-up management. **[2]**

**3.** State two reasons why groundwater levels fall in many hot arid areas. **[2]**

**4.** Outline how a check dam helps recharge groundwater. **[3]**

**5.** Outline two ways shelterbelts help protect farmland in semi-arid areas. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why insecure land tenure makes sustainable management harder. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The fictional Qarnet aquifer holds 5,000 million m³ of usable water. Abstraction is 120 million m³ per year and natural recharge is 20 million m³ per year.

**(a)** Calculate the net annual loss from the aquifer. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate how many years the usable store would last at this rate. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest two ways the water could be managed more sustainably. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows changes in two fictional villages in a semi-arid area between 2005 and 2025. Village A built community water-harvesting structures in 2007; Village B did not.

| | Village A 2005 | Village A 2025 | Village B 2005 | Village B 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth to water in wells (m) | 24 | 9 | 22 | 35 |
| Months per year with water in wells | 5 | 11 | 6 | 3 |
| Households migrating seasonally for work (%) | 40 | 12 | 38 | 55 |

**(a)** Compare the changes in the two villages. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons for the differences. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how large-scale canal irrigation can cause problems in hot arid environments. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why climate change may make sustainable management of semi-arid environments more difficult. **[5]**

**11.** With reference to a case study, describe the problems of sustainable management in a hot arid or semi-arid environment. **[6]**

**12.** "Small-scale, community-based solutions are the key to managing hot arid and semi-arid environments sustainably." With reference to a case study, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Water stored in an aquifer during wetter past climates [1] that is not being replaced by present-day recharge [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Top-down management is planned and funded by governments or large agencies [1], whereas bottom-up management is led by local communities using local knowledge [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Abstraction for irrigation, towns and industry exceeds recharge [1]. Low and unreliable rainfall means recharge is small [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The dam slows and holds runoff after rain [1]. Water ponds behind it instead of flowing away [1], giving time for it to infiltrate and percolate down to recharge groundwater [1]. **[3]**

**5.** They reduce wind speed across fields [1], so less soil is lost to wind erosion and less sand blows onto crops [1]. They shade the ground and lower wind speed near the surface [1], reducing evaporation from soil and crops [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Farmers who may lose their land have little reason to invest in long-term improvements such as terraces or trees [1]. Common land may be overused because no one controls access [1]. Herders and farmers may compete for land, leading to conflict instead of shared management [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 120 - 20 = 100 million m³ per year [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 5,000 / 100 = 50 years [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Reduce abstraction through drip irrigation or growing crops that need less water [1], so that use falls towards the recharge rate [1]. Increase recharge through water harvesting such as check dams [1], so more runoff soaks into the aquifer [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** In Village A depth to water fell from 24 m to 9 m, while in Village B it rose from 22 m to 35 m [1]. Months with water rose from 5 to 11 in A but fell from 6 to 3 in B [1]. Seasonal migration fell from 40% to 12% in A but rose from 38% to 55% in B [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Water harvesting in A captured monsoon runoff and recharged groundwater [1], so water levels rose and wells held water for longer [1]. With reliable water, farmers could grow more crops and fewer needed to migrate for work [1]. In B, continued pumping without recharge lowered the water table, reducing farming and pushing more people to migrate [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Water applied in excess, with poor drainage, raises the water table [1]. This causes waterlogging of fields [1]. Capillary rise and evaporation leave salts at the surface, causing salinisation [1]. Seepage from unlined canals wastes water [1]. Schemes depend on water from outside the region, which may cause conflict with users upstream [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Higher temperatures increase evaporation and potential evapotranspiration [1], so less water is available even if rainfall is unchanged [1]. Rainfall may become more variable, with longer droughts [1]. Intense storms may increase runoff and erosion rather than recharge [1]. Strategies designed for past conditions, such as rain-fed farming calendars, may no longer work [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Credit a named hot arid or semi-arid environment with relevant detail, for example: the environment is located and identified as hot arid or semi-arid [1]; water scarcity and rainfall variability are explained [1]; land degradation such as overgrazing, fuelwood cutting or salinisation is described [1]; groundwater depletion is identified [1]; human pressures such as population growth, poverty or conflict are explained [1]; supporting data, dates or places are included [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content (the Thar Desert is used here; credit any valid case study).

*Introduction:* define sustainable management in drylands [1]; distinguish small-scale community-based from large-scale top-down solutions [1].

*Community-based solutions as key:* johads in Alwar district recharge groundwater [1]. The Arvari river began to flow for much more of the year [1]. Low cost, local materials and labour [1]. Community ownership gives people a stake in conservation [1]. They work within the water balance [1].

*Limitations of community solutions:* labour-intensive [1]. Depend on monsoon rain [1]. Suit hill areas with runoff, not the driest sandy plains [1].

*Large-scale solutions:* the Indira Gandhi Canal brought irrigation and drinking water to arid districts [1]. It caused waterlogging and salinisation [1]. Fossil water schemes such as the Great Man-Made River provide large supplies but deplete a non-renewable store [1]. Dune stabilisation by CAZRI protected roads and canals [1], though invasive *Prosopis juliflora* spread [1].

*Interaction:* large schemes can supply towns while community schemes manage local water [1]. Government support can spread community methods [1].

*Judgement:* community-based solutions are more sustainable in semi-arid areas [1]; large-scale supply may be unavoidable in the arid core and for cities [1]; a reasoned final view on "how far" [1]. **[20]**
