This guide teaches section **3.1.6 Kinetics** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry
(9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards. It covers
every point from 3.1.6.1 to 3.1.6.5: collision theory, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the effect of
temperature, the effect of concentration and pressure, and catalysts. The whole section is International AS
content, so nothing here is International A-level only. The specification assesses 3.1.6 in Unit 2
(Organic 1 and Physical 1), and the International A2 Unit 5 (practical and synoptic) covers all content.

## 3.1.6.1 Collision theory

The specification states that reactions can only occur when collisions take place between particles having
sufficient energy, and calls this energy the activation energy.

**Definition.** The **activation energy, Ea**, is the **minimum** energy that particles must have when they
collide for a reaction to occur.

On an enthalpy profile (see [energetics](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-energetics/)), Ea is the
height of the barrier from the reactants to the top of the curve.

**Why most collisions do not lead to a reaction.** In the typical gas examples at this level, most particles
have less energy than Ea, so many collisions cannot react. Energy at or above Ea is necessary but not
sufficient: the particles must also collide in a suitable orientation. Thus not every collision above Ea is
successful. The fraction below Ea depends on temperature and the barrier; it is not universal for every
reaction and condition.

Two quantities therefore control the rate:

1. the collision **frequency** (collisions per second);
2. the **fraction** of those collisions that carry energy of at least Ea.

Temperature, concentration, pressure and catalysts each act on one or both of these.

**Worked example 1.** A mixture of methane and air can sit in a closed room at 25 °C for days without
reacting, yet the same mixture burns fast once a flame is applied. Explain this.

Step 1: at 25 °C the molecules are colliding all the time, so collisions are happening.
Step 2: almost none of these collisions have energy at or above the activation energy for combustion, so
they are unsuccessful.
Step 3: the flame gives some molecules energy above Ea. The exothermic reaction then heats nearby molecules
above Ea too, so the reaction spreads.

## 3.1.6.2 The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

At a fixed temperature, gas molecules share out energy unevenly: a few are slow, most are moderate, a few
are very fast. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution plots this spread.

**Drawing it**

- Label the axes **energy** (horizontal) and **number of molecules** (vertical).
- Begin the line at **(0, 0)**, since zero-energy molecules do not exist.
- Climb to a single maximum, the **most probable energy**, then descend more slowly.
- The shape is **lopsided**: the stretched-out high-energy side drags the **mean energy** above the most
  probable value.
- Let the tail flatten towards the energy axis **without ever reaching it**, as no upper energy limit exists.
- Everything under the line stands for the **whole sample** of molecules.
- Ea is a vertical line far out in the tail. The area to the right of it represents the molecules with
  E ≥ Ea.

**Curves for different temperatures.** For the same sample at a higher temperature:

- the peak moves **to the right** (higher most probable energy);
- the peak is **lower**;
- the curve is **broader**;
- the **total area is unchanged**, because the number of molecules is unchanged.

The cooler curve is higher at low energies and the hotter curve is higher at high energies, with a single
crossing between them. Ea lies in the region where the hotter curve is on top.

**Worked example 2 (interpreting curves from a grid).** A teacher draws two distribution curves for the same
gas sample on squared paper. The total area under each curve is 1200 small squares. To the right of Ea, the
curve at T₁ encloses 18 squares and the curve at T₂ encloses 45 squares.

Step 1: proportion with E ≥ Ea at T₁ = 18 ÷ 1200 × 100 = **1.5 %**.
Step 2: proportion at T₂ = 45 ÷ 1200 × 100 = **3.75 %**.
Step 3: T₂ has the larger area beyond Ea, so **T₂ is the higher temperature**.
Step 4: the proportion with E ≥ Ea has risen by 45 ÷ 18 = **2.5 times**, so roughly 2.5 times as many
collisions can succeed, even before any change in collision frequency.

The totals are equal because the sample is the same.

## 3.1.6.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate

**Rate of reaction** is the change in the concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time.
With concentrations in mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds, the unit is **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.

```
mean rate = change in concentration ÷ time taken
```

**Worked example 3.** In the hydrolysis of 1-bromobutane by hydroxide ions,

```
CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH + Br⁻
```

the hydroxide ion concentration falls from 0.250 mol dm⁻³ to 0.190 mol dm⁻³ in 300 s. Find the mean rate.

```
change in [OH⁻] = 0.250 − 0.190 = 0.060 mol dm⁻³
mean rate       = 0.060 ÷ 300   = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
```

The equation is 1 : 1, so bromide ions form at the same rate. A rate is always quoted as positive.

### Why a small temperature rise gives a large rise in rate

Heating raises the average kinetic energy of the molecules, and this has two consequences.

1. Faster molecules meet slightly more often.
2. The whole distribution slides towards higher energy, so a **much larger proportion of molecules have
   E ≥ Ea**.

The second effect dominates. Ea sits far out in the tail of the curve. When the curve shifts right, the small
area beyond Ea grows by a large factor, so the number of successful collisions per second rises sharply.

**Worked example 4 (a simple Arrhenius estimate).** For a reaction with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, estimate the
rate-constant change from 295 K to 305 K using `k = A exp(−Ea/RT)` and assume the pre-exponential factor A
is constant:

| Quantity | Change from 295 K to 305 K |
|---|---|
| Arrhenius factor in k, with A held constant | about 1.95 times larger |
| Mean molecular speed (a simple collision-frequency comparison) | rises by about 1.7 % |

Step 1: `k₂/k₁ ≈ exp[(Ea/R)(1/295 − 1/305)] ≈ 1.95` under the stated constant-A approximation.
Step 2: the separate mean-speed comparison is `√(305/295) ≈ 1.017`.
Step 3: do **not** multiply these figures as independent corrections: the first is an Arrhenius rate-constant
estimate, while the second is only a simple collision-frequency comparison. In collision-theory language,
heating also increases the fraction of particles in the high-energy tail; the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve
explains this qualitatively here, and its tail fraction is not separately calculated by the Arrhenius ratio.

You do not need this calculation at International AS, only the explanation it supports. The numerical
Arrhenius estimate is a simple model, not a separate exact measurement of the Maxwell–Boltzmann tail.

## 3.1.6.4 Effect of concentration and pressure

**Concentration (solutions).** Making a solution more concentrated means each cm³ holds **more reactant
particles**. With less space between them, they meet **more often**, so the number of collisions reaching Ea
each second goes up and so does the rate.

**Pressure (gases).** Compressing a gas at fixed temperature leaves the same molecules in a **smaller
volume**, so their number per cm³ rises. They meet **more often**, and the rate goes up.

**What does not change.** Neither change alters the energy of the molecules. The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve
keeps its shape and position, and the **proportion** of collisions with E ≥ Ea is the same. Only the number
of collisions per second goes up.

**Worked example 5 (linking pressure to concentration).** A gas at 298 K is compressed from 100 kPa to
200 kPa. Use pV = nRT (from [amount of substance](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amount-of-substance/))
to show how the number of molecules per unit volume changes. R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

```
n/V = p/(RT)
at 100 kPa: 100 000 ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 40.4 mol m⁻³ = 0.0404 mol dm⁻³
at 200 kPa: 200 000 ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 80.8 mol m⁻³ = 0.0808 mol dm⁻³
```

Doubling the pressure doubles the amount of gas per dm³, so molecules collide more often. Their energies
are unchanged, because the temperature is the same.

## 3.1.6.5 Catalysts

**Definition (as the specification gives it).** A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a
chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount.

Catalysts work by providing an **alternative reaction route of lower activation energy**. They do not give
molecules extra energy. On an enthalpy profile, the catalysed route is a lower hump between the same
reactant and product levels, so the enthalpy change is unchanged.

A catalyst does not change the position of an equilibrium; that point belongs to 3.1.7 and is covered in
[equilibria and Le Chatelier's principle](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-equilibria-le-chateliers-principle/).

### Using a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution for a gas reaction

At a fixed temperature the catalyst does not move the curve. Only the activation energy that matters changes.

Sketch a single curve, because the temperature is fixed. Put two vertical lines in its tail: Ea for the
uncatalysed reaction, and a lower Ea(cat) **nearer the peak**. Shade the strip between the two lines. Those
molecules could not react before but can react by the catalysed route, so the successful-collision rate
rises.

**Worked example 6.** On a distribution curve with a total area of 900 squares, 2 squares lie beyond Ea for
the uncatalysed reaction and 27 squares lie beyond Ea(cat). By what factor does the catalyst increase the
proportion of molecules that can react?

Step 1: without the catalyst, 2 ÷ 900 × 100 = 0.22 % of molecules have E ≥ Ea.
Step 2: with it, 27 ÷ 900 × 100 = **3.0 %** have E ≥ Ea(cat).
Step 3: factor = 27 ÷ 2 = **13.5**. The curve and the temperature are unchanged; only the energy barrier has
moved.

## Common errors

- An activation energy definition that drops the word **minimum**.
- A hotter Maxwell–Boltzmann curve whose peak is higher, or which encloses more area than the cooler one.
- A temperature answer built only on faster, more frequent collisions; the energy fraction is the key idea.
- Crediting concentration or pressure with giving molecules extra energy. They change how **often**
  molecules meet, nothing else.
- A catalyst answer with no **alternative route**.
- A second, shifted curve drawn for a catalyst. The temperature is the same, so the curve stays put.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is activation energy?**
Activation energy, Ea, is the minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur. Leaving out the word "minimum" is a common way to lose the definition mark.

**Why does a small temperature rise cause a large increase in reaction rate?**
Heating shifts the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to higher energies, and because Ea lies far out in the tail, the small fraction of molecules with energy at or above Ea grows by a large factor. Collisions also become slightly more frequent, but the energy effect dominates.

**How does a catalyst increase the rate of a reaction?**
A catalyst provides an alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react. It does not give molecules extra energy, and the enthalpy change of the reaction is unchanged.

**Does increasing concentration change the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution?**
No. A higher concentration or pressure puts more particles in each unit of volume, so they collide more often, but their energies are unchanged. The curve and the proportion of collisions with E ≥ Ea stay the same.

**Are rate equations part of OxfordAQA 9620 kinetics?**
Not in section 3.1.6, which is International AS content assessed in Unit 2. Orders, the rate constant and Required practical 8 belong to rate equations in section 3.1.11, which is International A-level only.
