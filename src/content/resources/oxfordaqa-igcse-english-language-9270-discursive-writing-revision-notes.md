**These notes condense discursive writing for Paper 1 Section B of OxfordAQA International GCSE
English Language (9270).** The specification lists the task types as "descriptive/imaginative/
argumentative or discursive". For full explanation, a plan and a model paragraph, use the
[discursive writing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-discursive-writing/);
for all four task types, see the
[Section B composition revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-b-composition-revision-notes/).

## Discursive in one line

**Explore an issue from more than one angle, weigh the views, and reach a considered judgement.**

## Spot the task type

| Wording | Likely type |
|---|---|
| "Discuss…", "Explore the arguments for and against…" | Discursive |
| "Consider different views on…" | Discursive |
| "Argue…", "Persuade…", "Write a speech arguing…" | Argumentative |
| A statement followed by "Discuss this view" | Discursive, often ending with a judgement |

The specification's wording "argumentative and/or discursive" means some tasks blend the two.

## Three structures

1. **Block**: one side, then the other, then conclusion. Simple; weighing left to the end.
2. **Alternating**: each paragraph weighs both sides of one aspect. Usually the strongest.
3. **Thematic**: paragraphs by theme (individuals, families, society), each weighed.

## Paragraph engine

```
Topic sentence   names the aspect being weighed
View A           with a realistic example or reasoned consequence
Turn             concessive or contrasting connective
View B           with an example or consequence
Weighing line    which is stronger here, and why
```

## Weighing vocabulary

| Job | Words and phrases |
|---|---|
| Concede | although, while, admittedly, granted, it is true that |
| Contrast | however, on the other hand, conversely, yet, nevertheless |
| Qualify | to some extent, in certain cases, not always, for some people |
| Modal caution | may, might, could, tends to, is likely to |
| Evaluate | more convincing, a stronger case, the most serious objection |

## Evidence without numbers

- Realistic examples (a student with a weekend job).
- Reasoned consequences ("If…, then…, which would…").
- General knowledge you are certain of.
- **Never** invented statistics or vague "experts say".

## Conclusion options

- **Qualified view**: "On balance…, provided that…"
- **Conditional view**: "It depends on…"
- **Reframing**: "Perhaps the real question is…"

A conclusion that only says "there are good points on both sides" does not judge.

## Writing objectives in discursive writing

- **W1**: measured, engaging voice; precise vocabulary for distinctions.
- **W2**: concessive and complex sentences; a short sentence for a key judgement.
- **W3**: planned structure; aspect-led topic sentences; logical links.
- **W4**: commas after introductory clauses; accurate complex sentences.

## Five-minute plan

```
1. Task type: discursive / argumentative / blend?
2. Structure: block, alternating or thematic
3. Opening hook: image, anecdote or question
4. Three aspects, each with view A and view B
5. Judgement: qualified, conditional or reframing
```

## Weak and strong compared

- **Weak:** "Some people think phones are bad. Other people think they are good. Both are good
  points." Views are stated, not weighed, and there is no example.
- **Strong:** "Although phones keep teenagers in touch with friends, the same constant contact can
  make it harder to switch off at night; the benefit is real, but it carries a cost that is easy to
  overlook." One aspect, both sides, an example and a weighing line.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Discursive | Exploring several views in a balanced way |
| Concession | Acknowledging a point on the other side |
| Qualification | Limiting a claim to make it more accurate |
| Modal verb | A verb such as *may* or *might* expressing possibility |
| Counter-argument | A view that opposes the one being considered |
| Rebuttal | An answer to a counter-argument, more common in argument |
| Reframing | Restating the question in a more precise or interesting way |
| Measured tone | Calm, reasonable and controlled voice |

## Voice check

- Formal or semi-formal; first person allowed, especially in the conclusion.
- Rhetorical questions to pose the issue, not to bully the reader.
- Emotive language used sparingly, if at all.

## Exam traps

- Writing a one-sided argument for a "discuss" task.
- A block structure that never weighs.
- Invented statistics.
- Repeating one point in several paragraphs.
- A summary instead of a judgement at the end.
- Over-emotive language and exclamations.

## Self-test

1. What does the specification list as the task types for Paper 1 Section B?
2. Define discursive writing in one sentence.
3. Which task wordings usually signal a discursive task?
4. Name the three structures and the main risk of the block structure.
5. What does an alternating paragraph do?
6. Give three concessive connectives.
7. Why use modal verbs in discursive writing?
8. What kinds of evidence can you use without sources?
9. Why should you avoid invented statistics?
10. Give two ways to conclude with a judgement.
11. Write a concessive sentence on whether school uniform should be compulsory.
12. Which writing objective is served by aspect-led topic sentences?

## Answers

1. Descriptive, imaginative, argumentative or discursive; students choose one of three tasks.
2. Writing that explores an issue from more than one angle and reaches a considered judgement.
3. "Discuss…", "Explore the arguments for and against…", "Consider different views on…", or a
   statement followed by "Discuss this view".
4. Block, alternating and thematic; the block structure can read like two lists and leaves all the
   weighing to the conclusion.
5. It takes one aspect of the issue and weighs both sides within the same paragraph.
6. Any three: although, while, admittedly, granted, it is true that.
7. They show that you are judging evidence carefully rather than stating opinions as facts.
8. Realistic examples, reasoned consequences and general knowledge you are certain of.
9. Examiners recognise them, and they weaken the credibility of the writing.
10. Any two: a qualified view; a conditional view; a reframing of the question.
11. For example: "Although uniform can reduce pressure to wear expensive clothes, it may also limit
    students' chance to express their identity."
12. W3, organisation and cohesion.
