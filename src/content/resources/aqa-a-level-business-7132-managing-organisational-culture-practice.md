These questions cover section 3.10.2 managing organisational culture of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-organisational-culture/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-organisational-culture-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** In a small firm, all major decisions are made by the owner, and staff rarely challenge her. Which of Handy's cultures is this? **[1]**

A: Role culture

B: Power culture

C: Task culture

D: Person culture

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most typical of a role culture? **[1]**

A: Teams form around projects and then break up

B: Authority depends on rules, job titles and procedures

C: The organisation exists to serve individual professionals

D: Decisions are made by one leader at the centre

**3.** Define "organisational culture". **[2]**

**4.** A firm has 250 staff and annual labour turnover of 20%. Calculate the number of staff who leave in a year. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a task culture suits a software development company. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Orrin Services, a call-centre company with 250 staff, has a labour turnover of 20% and a culture of strict rules. Each leaver costs £6,000 to replace. Managers plan a culture change programme to cut turnover to 12%. Calculate the annual saving if the target is met. **[3]**

**7.** The programme costs £360,000 in the first year. Calculate the payback period, and explain **one** reason why the saving might not be achieved. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse **two** influences that have probably shaped Orrin's rule-based culture. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** problems Orrin might face in trying to change its culture. **[4]**

**10.** Orrin's chief executive says the new culture will be one of "trust and initiative", but continues to check every decision personally. Analyse the likely effect on the change programme. **[6]**

**11.** Two firms merge: one with a power culture, one with a role culture. Assess the problems the new business is likely to face. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that a task culture is better than a role culture for all businesses. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Power is concentrated in the owner. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Role cultures rely on defined roles, rules and procedures. **[1]**

**3.** The shared values, beliefs and attitudes [1] that shape how people behave in an organisation: "how things are done here" [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 250 × 20% [1] = **50** staff [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Software development is project-based and problems change constantly [1], so teams of experts can form around each project [1], giving the flexibility and creativity needed, and authority comes from skill, not position [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Leavers before = 250 × 20% = 50; after = 250 × 12% = 30 [1]. Fewer leavers = 20 [1]. Saving = 20 × £6,000 = **£120,000** a year [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Payback = £360,000 ÷ £120,000 [1] = **3 years** [1]. The saving might not be achieved because culture takes years to change [1], and managers may not alter their behaviour, so staff still leave; other causes of turnover such as pay may be the real problem [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Influence 1: the nature of the industry [1]. A call centre handles large numbers of similar calls [1] and must follow scripts and legal rules, which encourages a role culture [1]. Influence 2: size and structure [1]. With 250 staff and layers of supervisors, rules and procedures help control quality and performance [1], and they become the normal way of working, which reinforces the culture [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Problem 1: resistance [1]. Staff used to rules may fear taking initiative or lose the security of clear instructions [1]. Problem 2: time and cost [1]. Culture change takes years, and the programme costs money and disrupts work during the change [1]. (Credit also: subcultures; inconsistent leaders.) **[4]**

**10.** Staff watch what leaders do more than what they say [1]. If the chief executive checks every decision, the message that staff should use their initiative is not believed [1], so staff will continue to wait for approval [1]. This weakens trust in the change programme and may cause cynicism [1], wasting the money spent on training and communication [1]. To succeed, leaders must change their own behaviour, for example by delegating decisions [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Problem 1: conflict of values and styles [1]. The power-culture staff expect quick decisions from a leader; role-culture staff expect rules and procedures [1], which can cause misunderstandings and frustration [1]. Problem 2: resistance and uncertainty [1]. Staff from one firm may feel the other's culture is being imposed and fear losing status or jobs [1]. Problem 3: slower decisions or loss of speed [1]. If the role culture dominates, the entrepreneurial firm's flexibility may be lost [1]. Possible benefits: combining speed with discipline may give a stronger business. Judgement: the problems are likely to be significant in the first years [1], and the merger will succeed only if leaders agree a clear target culture and communicate it [1]. **[9]**

**12.** For: task cultures are flexible, innovative and motivating, which suits changing environments [1]. Teams use expertise and give staff autonomy, which raises job satisfaction [1]. They can respond quickly to customers [1]. Against: they are harder to control and can be costly, with duplicated work [1]. Safety-critical, regulated and routine businesses need rules and clear responsibilities, so a role culture is better [1]. Examples include airlines, hospitals and banks [1]. A task culture also depends on skilled staff who work well in teams, which not every business has [1]. Factors: the stability of the environment [1], the type of work [1], and the skills and preferences of staff [1]. Judgement: no culture is best in all conditions [1]; the right one depends on the strategy and environment, and many firms use a role culture for core processes and a task culture for innovation [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers match the culture to the business's setting and refuse to call any one type best in every case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming Handy's types without applying them to the case.
- Assuming one culture is always best.
- Forgetting that leaders' behaviour matters more than words.
- Treating culture change as quick.
- Ignoring the costs of change.
