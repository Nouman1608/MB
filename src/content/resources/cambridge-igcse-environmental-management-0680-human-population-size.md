Sub-topic 7.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029) explains why the number of people in a place changes and what happens when it grows. It has two statements: describe and explain the factors that change human populations (7.2.1), and describe the impacts of human population growth (7.2.2). The second list is long, with twelve named impacts, and links strongly to environmental topics such as water, waste and pollution. For the whole topic, see the [Human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/).

## The population equation

A population changes in only three ways: people are born, people die, and people move in or out.

**Population change = (births − deaths) + (immigrants − emigrants)**

The first bracket is **natural change**; the second is **net migration**. Every factor in 7.2.1 works through one of these.

## Factors that change populations (7.2.1)

### (a) Birth rates: children per woman

The syllabus measures births by the average number of **children per woman** (the fertility rate). A fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman is roughly enough for each generation to replace the one before; above this the population tends to grow, and below it, without migration, it eventually shrinks.

Fertility tends to be **high** where:
- many children die young, so parents have more to ensure some survive;
- children work on farms or support parents in old age;
- contraception is hard to obtain or not accepted;
- girls leave education early and marry young;
- cultural or religious traditions value large families.

Fertility tends to be **low** where:
- children are expensive to raise and educate;
- women stay longer in education and have careers;
- contraception is widely available;
- people marry and start families later;
- pensions and healthcare mean parents do not rely on children in old age.

### (b) Death rates: life expectancy

The syllabus links death rates to **life expectancy**, the average number of years a person can expect to live. Life expectancy rises and death rates fall with better healthcare and vaccination, clean water and sanitation, a reliable food supply and safer housing and working conditions. It falls with famine, war, epidemics and poverty. When death rates fall faster than birth rates, populations grow quickly.

### (c) Migration: emigration, immigration, push and pull factors

- **Emigration**: people leaving a country. **Immigration**: people arriving in a country.
- **Push factors** drive people away: unemployment, poverty, war, persecution, natural hazards, drought and lack of services.
- **Pull factors** attract people: jobs, higher wages, safety, better healthcare and education, and family already living there.

Migration changes the structure as well as the size of a population, because migrants are most often young working-age adults. This can lower the working population of the place they leave and raise it, along with future births, in the place they go to.

### (d) Rural and urban migration

Most migration within a country is from the countryside to towns and cities. Typical push factors in rural areas are low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services, and land degraded by drought or erosion. Typical pull factors in cities are factory, service and informal jobs, schools, hospitals and the perception of a better life.

### (e) Urbanisation

**Urbanisation** is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas. It happens through rural-urban migration and through natural increase in cities, which is often high because migrants are young adults who start families there. Note the word "proportion": if both rural and urban populations grow at the same rate, the total urban population grows but urbanisation does not increase.

## Impacts of population growth (7.2.2)

The syllabus lists twelve impacts. Learn them in three groups.

**Where people live**
- **Informal urban settlements**: when housing cannot be built fast enough, people build homes from scrap materials on land they do not own, often on steep slopes or floodplains, without water, sanitation or electricity.
- **Megacities**: cities of more than 10 million people, which grow as migration and natural increase continue.
- **Availability of land**: land is needed for housing, farming, industry and roads, so farmland and natural habitats are built over.

**Resources and services**
- **Availability of resources**: more demand for food, timber, minerals and fuel.
- **Energy usage**: more homes, transport and industry raise demand for electricity and fuel.
- **Water supply**: demand can exceed what rivers, reservoirs and aquifers provide.
- **Sanitation**: sewers and treatment works cannot keep pace, so sewage enters rivers and spreads disease.
- **Management of waste**: more solid waste is produced than can be collected, so it is dumped or burned.
- **Facilities and infrastructure**: schools, hospitals, roads and public transport become overcrowded.

**Environment and economy**
- **Air, land, light, noise, visual and water pollution**: all six types are named, so be ready to give one example of each, such as vehicle exhaust (air), dumped waste (land), street lighting at night (light), traffic and construction (noise), billboards or sprawling housing (visual) and untreated sewage (water).
- **Traffic congestion**: more vehicles than roads can carry, causing delays, accidents and air pollution.
- **Changes in employment opportunities**: growth can create jobs in construction, services and trade, but if the working population grows faster than jobs, unemployment and informal work increase.

## Worked example 1: population change

The data are invented. A city of 2,000,000 people recorded in one year: 60,000 births, 20,000 deaths, 15,000 immigrants and 5,000 emigrants.

1. Natural change = 60,000 − 20,000 = +40,000.
2. Net migration = 15,000 − 5,000 = +10,000.
3. Total change = 40,000 + 10,000 = +50,000.
4. Percentage growth = 50,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = 2.5% in the year.
5. Natural change provides 40,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 80% of the growth.

## Worked example 2: is the country urbanising?

The data are invented.

| Year | Total population | Urban population |
|---|---|---|
| First census | 9,000,000 | 3,600,000 |
| Second census | 12,600,000 | 6,300,000 |

1. Urban share at first census = 3,600,000 ÷ 9,000,000 × 100 = 40%.
2. Urban share at second census = 6,300,000 ÷ 12,600,000 × 100 = 50%.

The urban share rose from 40% to 50%, so the country is urbanising. Notice that the rural population also grew, from 5,400,000 to 6,300,000; urbanisation is about proportion, not about rural areas emptying.

## Common mistakes

- Saying birth rate when the question asks about children per woman, or mixing them.
- Confusing emigration (leaving) and immigration (arriving).
- Giving push factors for the destination or pull factors for the origin.
- Defining urbanisation as "cities growing" instead of an increasing proportion of people living in urban areas.
- Listing "pollution" without naming the type, when six types are named.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** impacts: name and add a detail, ideally from the source.
- **Explain** a factor: say why it raises or lowers births, deaths or migration.
- **Calculate**: separate natural change and net migration, then combine, and give percentages to the stated accuracy.
- Link impacts to the environment, for example population growth → more waste → land and water pollution.
