## Core definition and formula

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** shows how strongly output reacts when the price moves.

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| PES | %ΔQs / %ΔP: output change over price change, each in percent |
| Percentage change | (new − old) / old × 100 |
| Quantity from PES | percentage change in quantity = PES × percentage change in price |
| Price from PES | percentage change in price = percentage change in quantity / PES |
| Sign | Positive on an upward-sloping supply curve |

Keep the order straight: quantity supplied on top, price underneath.

## Method in steps

1. Write the formula down.
2. Work out the percentage change in price.
3. Work out the percentage change in quantity supplied.
4. Divide quantity change by price change.
5. State whether supply is elastic, inelastic or unitary, and give a short reason.

### Worked reminder

Price rises from 20 to 25 and quantity supplied from 160 to 200 in an illustrative market.

- Price: (25 − 20) / 20 × 100 = 25%
- Quantity: (200 − 160) / 160 × 100 = 25%
- PES = 25 / 25 = **1.0**, so supply is unitary.

### Reverse reminders

- PES 2 and price up 3%: quantity supplied rises by 2 × 3 = 6%.
- PES 0.5 and quantity needs to rise 4%: price must rise by 4 / 0.5 = 8%.

## Degrees of PES

| Value | Name | Diagram (price on vertical axis, quantity on horizontal) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical line |
| 0 to 1 | Relatively inelastic | Straight line meeting the quantity axis |
| 1 | Unitary | Straight line through the origin |
| Above 1 | Relatively elastic | Straight line meeting the price axis |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal line |

Memory aid: extend a straight supply line back. Meeting the price axis means elastic. Meeting the quantity axis means inelastic. Passing through the origin means unitary, however steep it is.

## Determinants of PES

| Determinant | Raises PES | Lowers PES |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Longer period; inputs can be adjusted | Short period; inputs are fixed |
| Mobility of factors of production | Resources move easily between uses | Resources are specialised |
| Unused capacity | Spare capacity exists | Already at full capacity |
| Ability to store | Stocks can be held and released | Perishable; costly to store |
| Rate at which costs increase | Costs rise slowly with output | Costs rise steeply with output |

How to use these in an answer: name the determinant, say whether it makes supply more or less responsive, and tie it to the product in the question. For example, "the firm is operating at full capacity, so extra output requires new machines, which lowers PES in the short run."

## HL only: primary commodities and manufactured goods

The PES of primary commodities is generally lower than that of manufactured products. Reasons to remember:

- Production takes a long time (growing seasons, tree crops, mine development).
- Land and natural deposits are fixed and hard to switch to other uses.
- Many are perishable or hard to store in quantity.
- Extra output from limited land or a single mine can bring steeply rising costs.
- Weather and geology interfere with planned output.

Manufacturers generally have more flexibility with shifts, machinery and stocks.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| PES and price elasticity of demand | PES is positive; demand elasticity is normally negative because demand slopes downward |
| Steepness and elasticity | Steepness is not PES; only a line through the origin gives exactly 1 |
| Short run and long run | Short run: at least one input is fixed. Long run: all inputs can change |
| Movement along the curve and shift of the curve | A price change moves you along S. A change in cost or technology shifts S |
| Inelastic and perfectly inelastic | Inelastic is below 1. Perfectly inelastic is exactly zero |

## Diagram checklist

- Axes labelled: Price (vertical), Quantity supplied (horizontal).
- Curve labelled S.
- For two time periods, label S (short run) and S (long run); the long-run curve is flatter.
- Say in words which PES value your diagram shows.

## Time and PES: a pattern to memorise

In a fictional market a price rise of 10% might produce these responses.

| Period | What is fixed | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate | Output and stocks | Almost no response, PES close to 0 |
| Short run | Capital, but labour and materials can change | Some response, PES below 1 |
| Long run | Nothing | Larger response, PES often above 1 |

The pattern is the reason a supply diagram can show a steep short-run curve and a flatter long-run curve for the same product.

## A model paragraph on a determinant

Use this three-part shape: claim, reason, link to the product.

"Unused capacity raises PES. A bakery with idle ovens and staff can bake extra loaves as soon as the price rises, without buying equipment. Quantity supplied therefore rises by a larger percentage than price, and supply is relatively elastic."

For a lower PES, reverse it: "A shipyard working at full capacity cannot add output without building new berths, which takes years, so PES is low in the short run."

## Quick self-test

1. Give the PES formula.
2. Price rises from 20 to 25 and quantity supplied rises from 160 to 200 (illustrative). Calculate PES.
3. PES is 2 and price rises by 3%. What is the percentage change in quantity supplied?
4. PES is 0.5. Quantity supplied must rise by 4%. By what percentage must price rise?
5. Price falls from 90 to 81 and quantity supplied falls from 500 to 470. Calculate PES.
6. Price rises from 8 to 10 and quantity supplied from 240 to 264. Calculate PES and say whether supply is elastic or inelastic.
7. A firm has PES of 3 and supplies 1,200 units. Price falls by 2%. Find the new quantity supplied.
8. A producer has PES of 1.25 and supplies 800 units. Price rises 16%. Find the new quantity.
9. Name the five determinants of PES that the guide lists.
10. HL only: give two reasons why the PES of a farm crop is generally lower than the PES of a manufactured good.
11. Which straight-line supply curve has PES of 1 at every point?
12. A firm sells 250 units at 10 and 300 at 12. A second firm sells 250 at 10 and 275 at 12. Which has the higher PES, and by how much?

### Answers

1. PES = %ΔQs / %ΔP.
2. Price +25%, quantity +25%, so PES = **1.0** (unitary).
3. 2 × 3 = **6%**.
4. 4 / 0.5 = **8%**.
5. Price: (81 − 90) / 90 = −10%. Quantity: (470 − 500) / 500 = −6%. PES = −6 / −10 = **0.6**.
6. Price +25%, quantity +10%. PES = **0.4**, so inelastic.
7. Quantity change = 3 × (−2%) = −6%. New quantity = 1,200 × 0.94 = **1,128 units**.
8. Quantity change = 1.25 × 16% = 20%. New quantity = 800 × 1.2 = **960 units**.
9. Time; mobility of factors of production; unused capacity; ability to store; rate at which costs increase.
10. Any two: long production time (growing seasons); fixed land or deposits that cannot switch use; perishability and difficulty of storage; steeply rising costs when output is pushed on limited land; weather and natural limits.
11. A straight line passing through the origin.
12. First firm: price +20%, quantity +20%, PES = 1.0. Second firm: price +20%, quantity +10%, PES = 0.5. The first firm is higher, by **0.5**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Inverting the ratio so price sits above quantity.
- Using absolute changes instead of percentage changes.
- Dropping the negative signs on a price fall, then reporting a negative PES.
- Stopping at the number when the question also wants a verdict: elastic, inelastic or unitary.
- Listing determinants without explaining the direction of the effect.
- Drawing a diagram without labelled axes or without labelling the curve.
- Believing a steep line must be inelastic, whatever the intercept.
- Writing "time" as the only determinant of PES and not saying what is fixed or adjustable in the period.
- Putting the HL only primary commodity argument in an SL answer, or leaving it out when the HL question asks for it.
- Quoting a figure for a real country or firm. Use only the data in the question.
