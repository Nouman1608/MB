Section 3.3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification covers "Accounting for depreciation and disposal of non-current assets". Its additional information asks you to identify and explain why non-current assets depreciate, to "treat depreciation as a non-cash expense and know the effect on the valuation of assets", to explain and apply the straight line and reducing balance methods, to enter depreciation in the books "including the provision for depreciation", and to "record the sale of a non-current asset in the books of account, including the use of a disposal account".

The combined [capital, revenue, depreciation and disposal guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-capital-and-revenue-items-depreciation-and-disposal-of/) introduces each method with single assets. This guide goes further: why a business picks one method, how a provision account works when a business holds several assets and sells one during the year, and how the entries flow into the statements. All figures are invented and in dollars. Use the [depreciation and disposal revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-depreciation-and-disposal-revision-notes/) to recap and the [depreciation and disposal practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-depreciation-and-disposal-practice/) to test yourself.

## What depreciation is, and what it is not

Depreciation shares the cost of a non-current asset, less any expected residual value, over the periods that benefit from using it. It applies the accruals concept: the cost is matched against the revenue the asset helps to earn. The causes you should be able to explain are:

- **wear and tear**: physical deterioration from use, such as a machine's moving parts wearing out
- **obsolescence**: newer, better or cheaper models make the asset out of date while it still works
- **passage of time**: some assets lose usefulness simply with age, or have a fixed legal life such as a lease.

Depreciation is **not** a valuation at market price. Under the historic cost concept the asset starts at cost, and net book value is cost less the total depreciation to date. A van with a net book value of 9,000 might sell for 7,000 or 11,000. Nor is depreciation a sum of money put aside to replace the asset.

## A non-cash expense

Suppose a machine is bought for 20,000 by bank transfer at the start of Year 1, with a five-year life and no residual value, using straight line.

| Year | Cash paid for the machine | Depreciation in the income statement | Net book value at year end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20,000 | 4,000 | 16,000 |
| 2 | 0 | 4,000 | 12,000 |
| 3 | 0 | 4,000 | 8,000 |

All the cash leaves in Year 1, but each year's profit carries only 4,000. From Year 2 onwards, depreciation lowers profit while the bank balance is untouched. This is one reason profit and cash differ, a link examiners use in topic 5.

## Choosing between the methods

**Straight line**: annual charge = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. Equal charges suit assets that give a similar benefit every year, such as shop fittings or office furniture.

**Reducing balance**: a fixed percentage of the net book value at the start of each year. The charge is highest in the first year and falls every year. It suits assets that lose most of their value early, such as vehicles and computers. There is a second argument: repair costs tend to rise as an asset ages, so a falling depreciation charge plus rising repairs gives a more even total cost each year.

Once chosen, the method should be used every year (the consistency concept), so that profits can be compared.

### Worked example 1: comparing the methods

Calder Design buys computer equipment for 9,600. It expects a three-year life and a residual value of 600.

Straight line: (9,600 − 600) ÷ 3 = 3,000 a year.

Reducing balance at 50%:

| Year | Opening NBV | Charge | Closing NBV |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9,600 | 4,800 | 4,800 |
| 2 | 4,800 | 2,400 | 2,400 |
| 3 | 2,400 | 1,200 | 1,200 |

Straight line leaves 600, the residual value, after three years. Reducing balance leaves 1,200, because the percentage method never brings the value exactly to a chosen figure. The rate is an estimate set so that the closing value is roughly what the business expects. Over the three years reducing balance charges 8,400 and straight line 9,000, but reducing balance charges 1,800 more than straight line in Year 1, so Year 1 profit is lower.

A quick check for reducing balance: net book value after n years = cost × (1 − rate)ⁿ. Here 9,600 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1,200.

## Part-year policies

A question will state one of two policies, and you must follow it exactly:

- **time basis**: charge depreciation for each month the asset is owned, in both the year of purchase and the year of sale
- **full year in the year of purchase, none in the year of sale**: assets held at the year end are depreciated for a full year, and assets sold during the year receive no charge for that year.

## Worked example 2: a group of assets with a disposal

Fenwick Joinery depreciates equipment at 20% a year on the cost of equipment held at the year end, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale. Its year ends on 31 December.

At 1 January Year 3: equipment at cost 50,000; provision for depreciation 18,000.

- 1 April Year 3: a saw bought on 1 January Year 1 for 10,000 was sold for 4,500, received by bank transfer.
- 1 July Year 3: a planer was bought for 14,000 by bank transfer.

Workings:

- Depreciation already charged on the saw: Years 1 and 2 at 20% of 10,000 = 2 × 2,000 = 4,000.
- Net book value of the saw: 10,000 − 4,000 = 6,000. Proceeds 4,500, so a loss on disposal of 1,500.
- Equipment held at 31 December Year 3: 50,000 − 10,000 + 14,000 = 54,000.
- Depreciation for Year 3: 20% of 54,000 = 10,800.

**Equipment account**

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance b/d | 50,000 | Disposal | 10,000 |
| Bank (planer) | 14,000 | Balance c/d | 54,000 |
| | 64,000 | | 64,000 |

**Provision for depreciation: equipment**

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disposal | 4,000 | Balance b/d | 18,000 |
| Balance c/d | 24,800 | Depreciation (income statement) | 10,800 |
| | 28,800 | | 28,800 |

**Disposal of equipment account**

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment (cost of saw) | 10,000 | Provision for depreciation | 4,000 |
| | | Bank | 4,500 |
| | | Income statement: loss on disposal | 1,500 |
| | 10,000 | | 10,000 |

Only the saw's own provision, 4,000, is transferred to the disposal account, not the whole 18,000. The remaining 14,000 belongs to the equipment still held.

**Statement of financial position extract at 31 December Year 3**

| | Cost | Provision for depreciation | Net book value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment | 54,000 | 24,800 | 29,200 |

**Income statement for Year 3**: depreciation of equipment 10,800 and loss on disposal 1,500, both among the expenses.

## Worked example 3: reducing balance and a profit on disposal

A van cost 32,000 and is depreciated at 25% reducing balance, with a full year in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale.

| Year | Charge | Closing NBV |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8,000 | 24,000 |
| 2 | 6,000 | 18,000 |
| 3 | 4,500 | 13,500 |

The van is sold early in Year 4 for 14,100. Provision = 8,000 + 6,000 + 4,500 = 18,500. Net book value = 13,500, so there is a profit on disposal of 600. In the disposal account the profit is a debit entry (it balances the account), and in the income statement it is added as other income. A profit on disposal shows that earlier depreciation was slightly more than the fall in the van's value; it is not trading revenue.

## Common mistakes

- Transferring the whole provision balance to the disposal account instead of the provision on the asset sold.
- Charging a year's depreciation on an asset sold when the policy says none in the year of sale, or forgetting the part-year charge on a time basis.
- Calculating depreciation on the opening equipment balance when the policy says "held at the year end".
- Applying the reducing balance rate to cost in later years.
- Deducting residual value before applying a reducing balance rate.
- Describing depreciation as saving cash for a replacement.

## Exam technique

- **Explain why assets depreciate**: name the cause and apply it to the asset in the question, for example obsolescence for computers.
- **Calculate**: show each year's opening value, charge and closing value in a table.
- **Prepare ledger accounts**: date or describe each entry, balance the accounts, and bring the provision down as a credit.
- **Evaluate a method**: link the pattern of charges to how the asset loses value and to the effect on reported profit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is only part of the provision moved to the disposal account?**
The provision account holds depreciation on every asset in the group. Only the depreciation on the asset sold is removed with it; the rest still relates to assets the business owns.

**Does reducing balance depreciation ever reach zero?**
No. A fixed percentage of a falling balance always leaves something, which is why the rate is chosen to leave roughly the expected residual value.

**Is a profit on disposal part of revenue?**
No. It is shown in the income statement as other income, separate from sales.
