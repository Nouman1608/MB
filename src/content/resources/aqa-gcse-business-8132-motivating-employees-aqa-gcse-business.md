## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Explain the benefits of a motivated workforce, including staff retention and productivity | Why motivation matters |
| Understand financial methods of motivation: salary, wages, commission and profit sharing | Financial methods |
| Understand non-financial methods: styles of management, training, greater responsibility and fringe benefits | Non-financial methods |

The specification states that specific motivational theories, such as Maslow, will not be examined. You gain nothing by naming a theorist. Marks come from applying practical methods to the business in the case.

## Why motivation matters

Motivation is the drive that makes an employee want to work hard and well. It matters to the business because of what it changes.

- **Productivity.** Motivated staff produce more in the same time and make fewer errors, so the cost of each unit or each customer served falls.
- **Retention.** Motivated staff are less likely to leave, so the business spends less on recruiting and training replacements and keeps experienced people.

Signs of low motivation that a case study might give you include rising absence, lateness, customer complaints, errors, and staff leaving. If the data shows these, say what they cost the business.

### Worked example: measuring the effect on productivity

Dalbury Print has 15 machine operators producing 4,500 printed boxes a week, so output per worker is 4,500 ÷ 15 = 300 boxes. After the manager introduces team targets and starts consulting operators on scheduling, weekly output rises to 4,950 boxes with the same 15 staff.

- New output per worker: 4,950 ÷ 15 = 330 boxes
- Increase: 330 − 300 = 30 boxes, which is 30 ÷ 300 × 100 = **10%**

If the weekly wage bill stays at £7,200, the wage cost per box falls from £7,200 ÷ 4,500 = £1.60 to £7,200 ÷ 4,950 = about £1.45. Higher productivity lowers costs, which can raise profit or allow lower prices.

## Financial methods

Remuneration is the payment an employee receives for their work. The specification names four forms.

### Salary

A fixed yearly amount, usually paid monthly. A salary of £27,600 is £27,600 ÷ 12 = £2,300 a month. It gives the worker security and is simple for the business to budget. It does not rise with extra effort, so it suits jobs where output is hard to measure, such as office or management work.

### Wages

Payment for the hours worked, usually weekly, at an hourly rate. A worker on £11.80 an hour for 35 hours earns £413. Wages link pay to time spent at work, which suits jobs with varying hours. They reward attendance, not effort or quality.

### Commission

A percentage of the value of sales the employee makes, often paid on top of a basic salary or wage. It motivates sales staff directly because more sales mean more pay. Risks: staff may push customers into buying unsuitable products, damaging the business's reputation, and income becomes unpredictable for the worker.

### Profit sharing

A share of the business's profit paid to employees, often as a yearly bonus. It encourages staff to think about costs and sales across the whole business. The link between one person's effort and the payout is weak, and in a year with no profit there is no reward.

### Worked example: choosing a pay system for sales staff

Carrow Kitchens sells fitted kitchens. A designer currently earns a salary of £2,400 a month. The owner proposes a lower basic salary of £1,700 plus 3% commission on sales.

| Monthly sales by designer | Commission (3%) | Total pay | Change on current £2,400 |
|---|---|---|---|
| £15,000 | £450 | £2,150 | −£250 |
| £25,000 | £750 | £2,450 | +£50 |
| £40,000 | £1,200 | £2,900 | +£500 |

The designer earns more than now only by selling more than about £23,333 a month, because (£2,400 − £1,700) ÷ 0.03 = £23,333.33. Strong sellers gain; weaker ones lose. For the business, pay costs rise only when sales rise, which protects cash in a slow month. The judgement depends on the case: commission suits a business that wants to grow sales, but kitchens are expensive, long-term purchases, so pressure selling could produce complaints.

## Non-financial methods

### Styles of management

How a manager treats staff affects how they feel about their work. A manager who tells staff exactly what to do and expects them to obey keeps control and makes quick decisions, which can suit an emergency or untrained staff. A manager who consults staff, listens to ideas and explains decisions tends to make staff feel valued, which raises motivation, although decisions take longer. The specification does not require named styles, but you may meet labels such as autocratic and democratic in textbooks.

### Training

Training shows employees that the business is investing in them, builds confidence and opens routes to promotion. It costs money and working time, and newly skilled staff might move to a better-paid job elsewhere.

### Greater responsibility

Giving an employee more authority or more varied and challenging tasks, for example letting a shop assistant manage the stock orders, builds a sense of trust and achievement. It must be matched to the person's ability: responsibility without support or training can cause stress and mistakes.

### Fringe benefits

Extras on top of pay, such as staff discounts, a company car, free meals, gym membership or private health insurance. They make the job more attractive and help retention. They cost the business money, and a benefit that staff do not value, such as gym membership for staff who do not use it, motivates little.

## Choosing the right mix

There is no single best method. Use these factors from the case:

- **The job.** Commission suits roles where individual sales can be measured. Greater responsibility suits experienced, skilled staff.
- **The workers.** Low-paid staff may value higher wages most; well-paid staff may value responsibility or recognition more.
- **The business's finances.** A business short of cash may favour non-financial methods, which can cost little.
- **The problem.** If staff leave because they see no future, training and promotion routes address the cause; a one-off bonus does not.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Analyse one way Weller's Garage could improve the motivation of its mechanics.* (The case says mechanics are well paid but complain they are never asked for their views.)

"Weller's could give mechanics greater responsibility, such as letting the senior mechanic plan the daily workload. *(method applied)* This shows the owner trusts their judgement, which addresses their complaint about not being consulted. *(link to case)* As a result, the mechanics may work harder and stay longer, so the garage avoids the cost of replacing skilled staff and keeps the experience that produces good-quality repairs." *(consequence for the business)*

The answer avoids pay, because the case says pay is not the problem.

## Common mistakes

- Naming Maslow or another theorist instead of applying a method.
- Saying money is always the best motivator.
- Confusing a salary (fixed yearly amount) with a wage (paid for hours worked).
- Calculating commission on profit rather than on the value of sales, unless the question says otherwise.
- Forgetting that fringe benefits and training cost the business money.
- Suggesting a method that does not fix the problem the case describes.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain:** a method plus its effect on the worker and the business.
- **Analyse:** a chain from method to motivation to a business consequence such as productivity, retention or costs.
- **Evaluate:** weigh benefits against cost and suitability, then decide which method is best for this business and why.
- **Calculate:** show each step and give units.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are motivation theories examined in AQA GCSE Business?**
No. The specification states that specific motivational theories, such as Maslow, will not be examined. Focus on financial and non-financial methods.

**What is the difference between commission and profit sharing?**
Commission is a percentage of the sales an individual makes. Profit sharing gives employees a share of the whole business's profit, so it rewards everyone when the business does well.

**Is a fringe benefit financial or non-financial?**
The specification lists fringe benefits with the non-financial methods, because they are not part of pay, although they still cost the business money.
