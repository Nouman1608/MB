## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the term discharge. **[1]**

**2.** Name the process by which a river breaks down its own load into smaller, rounder pieces. **[1]**

**3.** Name **two** landforms found in the upper course of a river. **[2]**

**4.** Name the landform created when a meander is cut off from the river. **[1]**

**5.** Give **one** example of soft engineering used to reduce flooding. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how the cross profile of a river valley changes between the upper course and the lower course. **[3]**

**7.** The table shows the discharge of an invented river after a rainstorm. The peak rainfall was at 06:00.

| Time | Discharge (cumecs) |
|---|---|
| 00:00 | 5 |
| 04:00 | 5 |
| 08:00 | 18 |
| 10:00 | 40 |
| 12:00 | 22 |
| 16:00 | 10 |

**(a)** State the peak discharge and the time at which it occurred. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the lag time. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** physical factor and **one** human factor that could make the lag time shorter. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how a waterfall and gorge are formed. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a meander develops and how it may become an ox-bow lake. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how levées form. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why rivers deposit sediment. **[3]**

**12.** For a named river flood management scheme in the Middle or Far East, explain why it was needed and the social, economic and environmental issues it caused. **[6]**

**13.** "Hard engineering is always better than soft engineering for managing river floods." To what extent do you agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The volume of water passing a point in the river each second (measured in cumecs) [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Attrition [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two from: interlocking spurs; waterfalls; gorges; V-shaped valley [1 each]. **[2]**

**4.** Ox-bow lake [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any one from: flood warnings and preparation; flood plain zoning; planting trees; river restoration [1]. **[1]**

**6.** In the upper course the valley is narrow and V-shaped with steep sides [1]. In the lower course the valley is very wide with a flat floor, the floodplain [1]. The channel also becomes wider and deeper downstream [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 40 cumecs at 10:00 (both the value and the time are needed) [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Lag time = time of peak discharge minus time of peak rainfall [1] = 10:00 - 06:00 = 4 hours [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Physical factor, such as steep slopes or impermeable rock, which sends water to the river quickly rather than letting it soak in [2]. Human factor, such as urbanisation or deforestation, where impermeable surfaces and drains, or loss of trees, mean less infiltration and interception, so more rapid runoff [2]. **[4]**

**8.** A river flows over a layer of hard rock lying on softer rock [1]. The soft rock is eroded faster by abrasion and hydraulic action, creating a step and a plunge pool [1]. The hard rock is undercut and eventually collapses [1]. This repeats so that the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Water flows faster on the outside of a bend, where it is deeper [1]. This erodes the outer bank by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a river cliff [1]. On the inside the water is slow and shallow, so sediment is deposited, forming a slip-off slope [1]. The meander becomes more curved over time and the neck of land between two bends narrows [1]. During a flood, the river breaks through the neck and takes the shortest route [1]. Deposition seals off the ends of the old loop, leaving an ox-bow lake [1]. **[6]**

**10.** During a flood, water overflows the channel and slows down as it spreads over the floodplain [1]. The river drops its coarsest sediment first, right next to the banks [1]. Over many floods, this builds up natural raised banks, called levées [1]. **[3]**

**11.** A river deposits sediment when it loses energy [1]. This happens when discharge falls (for example in dry weather), when the water becomes shallow, or on the inside of bends where flow is slow [1]. It also happens where the river enters a lake or the sea, or when the load is too heavy for the flow to carry [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Named scheme in the region [1]. Why it was needed, such as a history of flooding and a large population at risk or need for power [1]. Social issues, such as relocation of people or flood protection [1]. Economic issues, such as the cost, electricity generation, trade or protection of farmland [1]. Environmental issues, such as sediment trapping, loss of habitats or reduced carbon emissions [1]. Detail or evidence, for example dimensions, numbers or dates [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: dams, embankments and relief channels give a high level of protection to towns and farmland [1].
- Agree: hard engineering can be designed for the biggest floods and may also produce power or water supply [1].
- Disagree: hard engineering is expensive, may displace people and damage habitats [1].
- Disagree: it can increase flood risk downstream and can fail catastrophically [1].
- Disagree: soft engineering such as warnings, zoning and tree planting is cheaper, sustainable and works with nature [1].
- Judgement: the best approach depends on the cost, size of the population at risk and location; most effective management combines both, with a justified conclusion [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the shape of a landform without saying how it formed.
- Saying that the inside of a meander bend is eroded.
- Forgetting to quote the unit (cumecs) or the times from a hydrograph.
- Giving a lag time calculated from the wrong points.
- Describing a scheme in general terms with no name, place or detail.
- Judgements that do not weigh both types of engineering.
