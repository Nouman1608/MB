> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 1.2 follows a mine through its whole life: deciding whether to open it, choosing how to dig,
living with its impacts and dealing with the land when it closes. The
[Topic 1 rocks, ores and minerals guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-rocks-ores-and-minerals/)
lists each syllabus item; this page shows how the items connect, works through a decision with invented
data, and models the "discuss" answer the sub-topic ends with.

## What the syllabus asks

- **1.2.1** Define an ore as "rock containing minerals and metals".
- **1.2.2** Describe extraction methods: surface (opencast, open-pit, open-cut and strip mining),
  subsurface (deep mining and shaft mining) and biological (phytomining and bioleaching).
- **1.2.3** Describe eight factors affecting the decision to extract: exploration; geology; accessibility
  and terrain; quantity and quality of deposit (ore grade); climate; environmental impact assessment;
  supply and demand; cost and profit.
- **1.2.4** Describe and explain environmental, economic and social impacts: loss of habitat and
  biodiversity; air, land, noise, visual and water pollution; water usage; waste management; changes in
  employment opportunities; local and national economies; facilities and infrastructure.
- **1.2.5** Describe strategies for managing damaged landscapes: land restoration (replacement of
  overburden, soil improvement, bioremediation and tree planting) and repurposing land (landfill, lakes,
  recreation and nature reserves).
- **1.2.6** Discuss the benefits and limitations of those strategies.

## Choosing a method: depth decides

The key question is how deep the deposit lies compared with the thickness of **overburden** (the soil and
rock above it).

**Surface extraction** removes the overburden and digs the deposit out from above. The four names in the
syllabus describe variations:

- **Open-pit** mining digs a deep pit with stepped sides, widening downwards, suited to a large ore body
  that extends a long way down.
- **Opencast** and **open-cut** are names for the same basic approach of digging from an open excavation
  after removing the overburden.
- **Strip mining** removes the overburden in long strips over a shallow, near-horizontal layer, such as a
  coal seam. The overburden from each new strip can be placed in the strip just worked out.

Surface methods are cheaper, safer for workers and recover more of the deposit, but they destroy every
habitat on the site and create the largest scars.

**Subsurface extraction** reaches deposits too deep to dig from above:

- **Shaft mining** sinks a vertical shaft down to the deposit, with lifts for workers, ore and equipment.
- **Deep mining** drives tunnels outwards from the shafts along the deposit.

Less land is disturbed at the surface, but these methods are expensive and carry risks of collapse,
flooding, poor air and gas explosions.

**Biological extraction** uses living things to recover metal from low-grade ore or mine waste:

- **Phytomining**: plants that absorb metal compounds through their roots are grown on the ore or waste,
  harvested and burned; the metal is recovered from the ash.
- **Bioleaching**: bacteria release metal compounds from the ore into a solution, from which the metal is
  then extracted.

Both are slow and give small amounts of metal, but they use land that is already damaged and avoid digging
new pits.

## The decision to extract

The eight factors fall into three questions:

| Question | Factors |
|---|---|
| Is the deposit there and worth having? | Exploration; geology; quantity and quality of deposit (ore grade) |
| Can it be reached and worked? | Accessibility and terrain; climate |
| Should it be worked? | Environmental impact assessment; supply and demand; cost and profit |

**Exploration** comes first: surveys, satellite images and test drilling find out whether a deposit
exists and how big it is. **Geology** describes the rock around the deposit, which affects how safely and
cheaply it can be dug. An **environmental impact assessment** studies the likely effects on people and
the environment before permission is given; it can stop a project or attach conditions such as
restoration. **Supply and demand** sets the price: when demand rises and supply is short, a previously
unprofitable deposit may become worth working. **Cost and profit** brings the others together: a mine
opens only if expected income is greater than the costs of extraction, processing, transport and
restoration.

### Worked example: comparing two deposits

*Invented data.*

| | Deposit A | Deposit B |
|---|---|---|
| Mass of ore (tonnes) | 2 000 000 | 6 000 000 |
| Ore grade (% metal) | 1.5 | 0.4 |
| Depth | 40 m below farmland | 600 m below a forest reserve |
| Access | Main road 5 km away | No road; steep mountains |

*Metal available:*

```
A: 1.5 / 100 x 2 000 000 = 30 000 tonnes
B: 0.4 / 100 x 6 000 000 = 24 000 tonnes
```

*Judgement:* A contains more metal from a third of the ore, so less waste per tonne of metal. Its
shallow depth suits open-pit mining, and road access lowers transport costs. B would need shaft mining in
difficult terrain, and its location in a forest reserve makes an unfavourable environmental impact
assessment likely. A is the stronger choice, although open-pit mining would remove farmland, so the
assessment for A would need a restoration plan.

## Impacts: describe and explain

"Describe and explain" means each impact needs its **cause** and its **effect**.

| Impact | Cause | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Loss of habitat and biodiversity | Vegetation and soil are cleared for pits, spoil heaps and roads | Species lose food and shelter and may disappear locally |
| Air pollution | Dust from blasting, digging and lorries | Breathing problems; dust settles on crops |
| Land pollution | Spoil heaps and spilled chemicals | Land cannot be farmed |
| Noise pollution | Blasting, machinery and traffic | Disturbs residents and wildlife |
| Visual pollution | Pits, heaps and buildings | Scenery spoiled, which can reduce tourism |
| Water pollution | Rain washing through spoil and processing waste into rivers | Fish die; water unsafe to drink |
| Water usage | Processing ore uses large volumes of water | Less water for farms and homes |
| Waste management | Most of the rock mined from a low-grade ore is waste | Waste must be stored safely for many years |
| Employment | Jobs at the mine and in local businesses | Income rises, but jobs are lost when the mine closes |
| Local and national economies | Wages spent locally; taxes and export income | Money for public services; economy can depend on one product |
| Facilities and infrastructure | Roads, power, housing and services built for the mine | Benefit local people, but may decline after closure |

## Managing the land afterwards

**Land restoration** returns the site towards its earlier state:

- **Replacement of overburden** fills pits and reshapes the land.
- **Soil improvement** adds topsoil, fertiliser or organic matter so plants can grow.
- **Bioremediation** uses organisms, such as plants and microorganisms, to remove or break down pollutants.
- **Tree planting** binds the soil, creates habitat and stores carbon.

**Repurposing land** gives it a new use: **landfill** for waste, **lakes** in flooded pits, **recreation**
such as parks and water sports, and **nature reserves**.

### Model "discuss" answer

*Question (original):* Discuss the benefits and limitations of turning a worked-out open pit into a lake
for recreation. [6]

*Model answer:* Flooding the pit is cheaper than refilling it with overburden, and the lake can bring
income from water sports, fishing and tourism, replacing some jobs lost when the mine closed. A lake also
creates a wetland habitat for birds and fish, so biodiversity can increase. However, the pit's steep
sides and deep, cold water can be dangerous for swimmers, so the edges may need to be reshaped first.
Rainwater may wash pollutants from the old workings into the lake, so its water quality must be tested
and treated. Recreation can disturb the wildlife that the lake attracts, and the scheme needs investment
in paths, car parks and safety. Overall, a lake is a sensible use if the site is near a town and the
water is clean, but it does not restore the land that was lost.

## Common mistakes

- Describing only one surface method when the question asks about surface, subsurface and biological.
- Confusing phytomining (plants) with bioleaching (bacteria).
- Listing factors without saying how they affect the decision.
- Giving "pollution" as one impact. Name the type (air, land, noise, visual, water).
- Treating all impacts as negative: employment, economies and infrastructure can benefit people.
- Answering a "discuss" question with benefits only.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the syllabus wording for an ore.
- **Describe** a method: say what is removed, how the deposit is reached and what kind of deposit it suits.
- **Explain** an impact: cause, then effect, then who or what is affected.
- **Discuss**: benefits, limitations and a short judgement that depends on the situation.
