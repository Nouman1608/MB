**This practice set covers topic 4.3, Group behaviour in organisations, in Specialist Option 4 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All organisations and people are fictional.

Related: [Group behaviour study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-group-behaviour-in-organisations/) · [Group behaviour revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-group-behaviour-in-organisations-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline the storming and norming stages of group development. **[2]**

**2.** Describe **three** of Belbin's team roles. **[3]**

**3.** Explain how evaluation apprehension differs from drive theory as an explanation of social facilitation. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** of Thomas–Kilmann's conflict-handling modes, with an example of when each is useful. **[4]**

**5.** Groups in organisations can make poor decisions.

**(a)** Describe faulty decision-making in groups, including groupthink and Forsyth's cognitive errors. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate explanations of faulty decision-making in groups, including a discussion of individual and situational explanations. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** A new marketing team has five members: three generate many creative ideas, and two are good at driving the team forward. Projects are exciting but often late and full of errors.

**(a)** Using Belbin, identify the roles present. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** role the team is missing, and explain how adding it would help. **[2]**

**7.** A warehouse manager notices that staff pick orders faster when supervisors walk the floor, but make more mistakes on a complicated new returns procedure.

**(a)** Using drive theory, explain both observations. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest how the manager could reduce errors on the returns procedure. **[2]**

**8.** A researcher replicates Earley (1993) with employees from two countries.

**(a)** Explain why she should define "in-group" and "out-group" operationally. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** way to measure individual effort objectively. **[2]**

**9.** A design company is considering monitoring its staff's screens electronically.

**(a)** Using Claypoole and Szalma (2019), explain how monitoring might affect performance. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** ethical concern about electronic performance monitoring. **[2]**

**10.** Two departments in a bank argue about who should pay for new software.

**(a)** Identify the level of conflict. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** organisational cause of this conflict. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest which Thomas–Kilmann mode would be best for resolving it, and explain why. **[2]**

**11.** An employee reports that a colleague has repeatedly mocked her in meetings for six months. Using Einarsen (1999), explain whether this is person-related or work-related bullying. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each. Storming: conflict over roles, goals or leadership as members assert themselves (1). Norming: rules and roles are agreed and the group becomes cohesive (1).

**2.** One mark each for any three, with contribution: plant, creative ideas (1); monitor evaluator, judges options logically (1); co-ordinator, clarifies goals and delegates (1); implementer, turns ideas into practical plans (1); completer finisher, checks details and deadlines (1); shaper, drives the team forward (1); teamworker, supports and reduces friction (1); resource investigator, explores outside contacts (1); specialist, expert knowledge (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. Drive theory says the mere presence of others raises arousal, strengthening the dominant response (1). Evaluation apprehension says arousal rises only when people believe they are being judged (1), so presence alone, for example of blindfolded observers who cannot evaluate, should have less effect (1).

**4.** Two marks for each mode: description (1) and a suitable use (1). For example: competing, pursuing one's own position assertively (1), useful in an emergency when a fast decision is needed (1); collaborating, working together to find a solution meeting both parties' concerns (1), useful when the issue is important to both and the relationship matters (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Groupthink occurs when a cohesive group values agreement more than realistic appraisal (1). Features include self-censorship of doubts (1), an illusion of unanimity and pressure on dissenters (1), and mindguards who shield the leader from bad news (1). Forsyth classified cognitive limitations into errors of commission, such as sunk cost bias (1), and errors of omission, such as base-rate bias, and errors of imprecision, such as relying on the availability heuristic (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Individual and situational explanations*: groupthink is situational, arising from cohesion, isolation and a directive leader, whereas Forsyth's errors are cognitive limitations of individuals that are amplified in groups; a full account needs both.
- *Evidence*: groupthink was developed from case studies of historical decisions, which are rich but retrospective and selective.
- *Application*: remedies such as a devil's advocate are used in organisations.
- *Reductionism*: listing features reduces complex decision processes to a checklist.
- *Generalisation*: cohesion does not always lead to poor decisions; some cohesive teams decide well.
- *Conclusion*: useful explanations with practical value but limited experimental support.

**6.**

**(a)** One mark each: plants (creative ideas) (1); shapers (driving the team forward) (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. A completer finisher (1), who would check details and deadlines, reducing errors and late delivery (1). Credit implementer or monitor evaluator if justified.

**7.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. The presence of supervisors raises arousal (1), which increases the dominant response (1); for well-practised picking, the dominant response is correct, so speed improves, but for the new, complex procedure, the dominant response is often wrong, so errors increase (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Allow staff to practise the returns procedure without being observed until it is well learned (1), so that the correct response becomes dominant (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Without clear definitions, different researchers or settings may classify groups differently (1); operational definitions, e.g. "colleagues from the same team, whom the participant has worked with for over six months", make the study replicable and reliable (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Count the number of tasks correctly completed in a fixed time (1), recorded for each individual rather than reported by participants (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Claypoole and Szalma found that being monitored, including electronically, affected performance on a sustained-attention task compared with working unmonitored (1), consistent with social facilitation occurring through monitoring as well as physical presence (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It may invade privacy if personal activity is recorded (1), so staff should be told what is monitored and why, and their consent sought (1). Credit increased stress from constant monitoring.

**10.**

**(a)** Inter-group conflict (1).

**(b)** Competition for scarce resources, the budget (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Collaborating (1), because both departments depend on each other in the long term, so a solution that meets both concerns preserves the relationship (1). Credit compromising, e.g. splitting the cost, if justified by time pressure.

**11.** Up to 2 marks. It is person-related bullying (1), because it targets her personally through repeated mockery, rather than through her work, such as impossible deadlines or withheld information (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 3, describing social facilitation without separating the two explanations.
- In question 5(a), listing groupthink features without saying what groupthink is.
- In question 7(a), explaining only one of the two observations.
