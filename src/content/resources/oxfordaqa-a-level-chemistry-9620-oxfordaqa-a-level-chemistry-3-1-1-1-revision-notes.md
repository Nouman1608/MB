These notes condense **3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1 and able to reappear in Unit 5. For full explanations and worked examples read the [fundamental particles study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-1-1/); then try the [fundamental particles practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-1-1-practice/).

## The subtopic in four lines

1. An atom is a nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.
2. Proton: relative charge +1, relative mass 1. Neutron: 0 and 1. Electron: −1 and about 1/1840.
3. Nearly all the mass is in the nucleus; nearly all the volume is the space the electrons occupy.
4. The model of the atom changed each time new evidence could not be explained by the old one.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Small, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons |
| Proton | Particle in the nucleus with relative charge +1 and relative mass 1 |
| Neutron | Particle in the nucleus with relative charge 0 and relative mass 1 |
| Electron | Particle outside the nucleus with relative charge −1 and relative mass about 1/1840 |
| Relative charge | Charge compared with the charge on a proton, taken as +1 |
| Relative mass | Mass compared with the mass of a proton, taken as 1 |
| Neutral atom | Equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel |
| Ion | Species in which the numbers of protons and electrons differ |

## Numbers worth knowing

- Proton to electron mass ratio is about 1836, so the electron's relative mass is written 1/1836 or about 1/1840.
- The neutron is very slightly heavier than the proton, but both are given relative mass 1.
- In a carbon-12 atom, electrons make up only about 0.03% of the mass, which is why mass number ignores them.
- The charge on an electron is equal in size and opposite in sign to the charge on a proton.

## The model over time: cause and effect

| Model | Key evidence or reason for change |
|---|---|
| Dalton: solid, indivisible spheres | Explained elements combining in fixed ratios |
| Thomson (1897): electron discovered; plum pudding model | Neutral atoms containing negative electrons must also contain positive charge |
| Rutherford (1911), from Geiger and Marsden's gold foil experiment: nuclear model | Most alpha particles passed straight through (mostly empty space); a few were deflected through large angles (small, dense, positive nucleus) |
| Bohr (1913): electrons in fixed energy levels | Elements emit light only at particular frequencies |
| Chadwick (1932): neutron discovered | Nuclear masses were larger than the mass of the protons alone |
| Sub-shells and orbitals | Ionisation energy patterns need more than simple shells |

**General point to make:** a model is accepted while it explains the evidence. When new evidence contradicts a prediction, the model is revised, keeping the parts that still work.

## Using the data

**Charge of a species.** Add +1 for each proton and −1 for each electron; ignore neutrons.

- 12 protons, 12 neutrons, 10 electrons: charge +2.
- 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons: charge −2.
- 1 proton, 0 neutrons, 0 electrons: charge +1 (a hydrogen ion is a bare proton).

**Particles between charged plates (same speed).**

- Neutrons: undeflected (no charge).
- Protons: towards the negative plate.
- Electrons: towards the positive plate, deflected far more than protons (same size charge, much smaller mass).

**Mass of an ion.** Losing or gaining electrons changes the mass by a negligible amount, so an ion has effectively the same mass as its atom.

## Exam traps

- Electron relative mass "0": wrong. Use 1/1840 or 1/1836.
- Neutron charge "−1" or "negative": wrong. It is 0.
- Electrons "in the nucleus": wrong. The nucleus contains only protons and neutrons.
- Hydrogen-1 has no neutrons, so "every nucleus contains neutrons" is false.
- Gold foil answers: an observation without its conclusion rarely scores. Pair them.
- The gold foil experiment did not discover the neutron or the electron.
- Deflection answers need both direction (sign of charge) and size (mass).
- Counting neutrons when finding the charge of an ion.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. Give the relative charge and relative mass of a neutron.
2. Give the relative mass of an electron.
3. Where in an atom is almost all of its mass found?
4. A species has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. What is its overall charge?
5. Which fundamental particle is absent from a hydrogen-1 atom?
6. State the observation from the gold foil experiment that showed most of an atom is empty space.
7. State the observation that showed the positive charge is concentrated in a small nucleus.
8. Why did the discovery of the electron mean that atoms must also contain positive charge?
9. A beam of electrons and a beam of protons, at the same speed, pass between charged plates. Which beam is deflected more, and why?
10. Explain why the nucleus of a lithium-7 atom, with three protons, has a relative mass of about 7.
11. Why is the mass of a Na⁺ ion taken to be the same as the mass of a sodium atom?
12. What is the relative charge of the nucleus of an atom with 20 protons and 20 neutrons?

### Answers

1. Relative charge 0; relative mass 1.
2. About 1/1840 (1/1836 is also accepted).
3. In the nucleus.
4. 17 − 18 = −1.
5. The neutron.
6. Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil without being deflected.
7. A small number of alpha particles were deflected through large angles, some bouncing back.
8. Atoms are electrically neutral, so the negative charge of the electrons must be balanced by positive charge.
9. The electron beam, because the charges are the same size but the electron's mass is about 1840 times smaller. The beams also bend in opposite directions.
10. The nucleus also contains four neutrons, each of relative mass 1, so 3 + 4 = 7.
11. Only one electron has been lost, and an electron's mass is about 1/1840 of a proton's, so the change is negligible.
12. +20. Neutrons add no charge, and electrons are outside the nucleus.
