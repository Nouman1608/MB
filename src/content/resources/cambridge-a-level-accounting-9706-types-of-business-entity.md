This guide covers **1.1 Types of business entity**, the first subtopic of Topic 1, Financial accounting, for Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting 9706, syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content, examined in Paper 1 (multiple choice) and Paper 2 (Fundamentals of Accounting), and assumed knowledge for the A Level papers. The [business entities and accounting system guide](/resources/business-entities-and-the-accounting-system/) introduces 1.1 and 1.2 together; this guide goes further into liability, control, stakeholders and matching finance to need, with worked examples. Afterwards, use the [types of business entity revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-types-of-business-entity-revision-notes/) and the [types of business entity practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-types-of-business-entity-practice/). All names and amounts in the examples are invented, in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

The syllabus asks candidates to explore how a business may be structured "with reference to legal form, ownership, management and control", and the benefits and drawbacks of each type from the point of view of owners and other stakeholders. Section 1.1.1 lists:

- the different types of business entity: sole trader; partnership; limited company (including public limited company (plc))
- the advantages and disadvantages of these types of business entity
- sources of finance and methods of funding: loans (secured and unsecured); bank overdrafts; payment by instalments; rental/leasing as an alternative to purchase; trade credit; sources of finance for limited companies as in 1.5.4

Candidates are expected to use this understanding "to take relevant decisions". Questions on Limited Liability Partnerships will not be set.

## Comparing the three entities

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | One owner | Usually two or more partners | Shareholders |
| Management and control | Owner decides everything | Partners share decisions, as set out in the partnership agreement | Directors manage on behalf of shareholders; shareholders vote at meetings |
| Legal form | Not separate from the owner in law | Not separate from the partners in law | A separate legal entity that can own assets, sue and be sued |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited, and partners are jointly liable | Limited to the amount shareholders agreed to pay for their shares |
| Continuity | Ends if the owner dies or retires | Changes when a partner joins, leaves or dies | Continues regardless of changes in shareholders |
| Profits | All to the owner | Shared as the agreement says | Distributed as dividends at directors' discretion; rest retained |
| Raising capital | Owner's savings and borrowing | Partners' capital and borrowing | Issue of shares and debentures, as well as borrowing |
| Disclosure | Private | Private | More regulation of accounts; a plc must publish its accounts |

Note that the business entity concept treats every type of entity as separate from its owners **for accounting purposes**, even though a sole trader and a partnership are not separate in law. The difference matters for liability: the accounts keep business and private items apart, but creditors of a sole trader can still claim the owner's private assets.

## Advantages and disadvantages from different viewpoints

**Owners.** A sole trader keeps all profit and full control but carries all the risk and workload. Partners bring extra capital, skills and shared workload, but must share profit and may disagree; each partner is bound by the business decisions of the others. Shareholders in a limited company risk only their investment and can sell shares (easily, in the case of a plc), but small shareholders have little say, and directors may pursue their own interests.

**Lenders and suppliers.** Lending to a sole trader or partnership gives a claim on the owners' private assets. Lending to a limited company does not, so banks often ask for security over company assets or personal guarantees from directors of small companies.

**Employees.** Larger entities can offer more stable employment and career paths, while a sole trader's business may close on the owner's retirement.

### Worked example 1: who pays when the business fails?

Ade and Bea run a partnership. It closes owing creditors $120 000, and its assets raise only $75 000.

```
Shortfall = 120 000 − 75 000 = $45 000
```

Because liability is unlimited and joint, the creditors can claim the $45 000 from the partners' private assets. If Bea has no private assets, Ade may have to pay the whole $45 000.

Had the business been a limited company, the shareholders would lose only what they had invested in shares. The creditors would bear the $45 000 loss, unless they had security or personal guarantees.

### Worked example 2: advising on a change of entity

Maya, a sole trader, earns a steady profit and wants to expand by opening a second shop, which needs $60 000. Her friend Tom offers $30 000 if he becomes a partner. Alternatively, Maya could form a private limited company and sell shares to family members.

A balanced answer, as the syllabus expects:

- **Partnership**: Tom's capital and skills reduce the borrowing needed and share the workload; but Maya would share profits and control, and both would have unlimited, joint liability for the larger business.
- **Limited company**: liability becomes limited, which protects Maya's home if the expansion fails; but there are formation costs and reporting requirements, and family shareholders would gain votes.
- **Recommendation**: a limited company, because expansion increases the risk and limited liability protects Maya's private assets, while she can keep control by holding most of the shares.

Either recommendation can score if it is justified with reasons drawn from the situation.

## Sources of finance and methods of funding

| Source | Features | Best used for |
|---|---|---|
| Secured loan | Lender has a claim on a named asset if repayments are missed; usually a lower interest rate | Long-term needs such as premises or vehicles |
| Unsecured loan | No claim on a specific asset; usually a higher interest rate and smaller amounts | Medium-term needs where no suitable asset exists |
| Bank overdraft | Borrowing up to an agreed limit on the current account; interest only on the amount overdrawn; can be withdrawn by the bank at short notice | Short-term, fluctuating needs such as seasonal inventory |
| Payment by instalments | Asset acquired now, paid for in regular amounts over time | Equipment and vehicles when cash is limited |
| Rental/leasing | Use of an asset for regular payments without buying it; maintenance may be included | Assets that become outdated quickly or are needed only for a period |
| Trade credit | Goods received now, paid for later, often 30 days or more | Funding inventory from day to day |
| Limited company sources (1.5.4) | Ordinary share issues, rights issues and debentures | Large or long-term needs |

A **bonus issue** of shares raises no cash: it converts reserves into share capital. It appears in 1.5.4 but is not a way of funding a purchase.

The key decision principle is **matching**: short-term needs should be met from short-term sources, and long-term assets from long-term sources. Financing a van with an overdraft risks the bank withdrawing the facility before the van has paid for itself.

### Worked example 3: matching finance to need

Ravi, a sole trader, needs $6 000 for four months to buy inventory for a festival season, and $20 000 for a delivery van that will last five years.

```
Overdraft for the inventory, at 12% a year, fully used for 4 months:
interest = 6 000 × 12% × 4/12 = $240

3-year loan for the inventory, at 8% a year:
interest = 6 000 × 8% × 3 = $1 440, paid long after the need has passed
```

For the inventory, an overdraft is cheaper and more flexible because interest stops when sales repay it. For the van, a secured loan or payment by instalments matches the five-year life of the asset; leasing would suit him if he prefers not to own it or wants maintenance included.

**Trade credit has a cost too.** If a supplier offers a 2% discount for payment within 10 days on an invoice of $4 800, taking the full credit period gives up $96.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a sole trader's business is a separate legal entity. Only a company is.
- Writing "limited liability" for partnerships. Standard partners have unlimited, joint liability; LLPs are not examined.
- Treating a bonus issue as a source of cash.
- Recommending long-term borrowing for a short-term need, or an overdraft for a long-term asset.
- Listing advantages without saying whose point of view they are from.
- Giving a recommendation without balancing reasons.

## Exam technique

- **State** and **identify**: one precise feature per mark, such as "unlimited liability".
- **Explain**: give the feature and why it matters, for example, "limited liability means shareholders can lose only their investment, so it is easier to attract investors".
- **Advise**, **discuss** and **evaluate**: consider both sides, use the figures and context given, and finish with a justified recommendation, as Paper 2 expects.
