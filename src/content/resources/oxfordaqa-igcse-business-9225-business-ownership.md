This guide covers section **3.1.2 Business ownership** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand the legal structures that businesses adopt, namely **sole traders, partnerships, limited companies and multinational organisations**; to analyse the benefits and drawbacks of each in terms of **management and control, sources of finance, liability and the distribution of profits**; to explain **limited liability** and how it benefits businesses; to evaluate which structure suits a given business, from a new start-up to a larger established firm; and to distinguish **private sector** from **public sector** ownership in a mixed economy. You are not expected to know the legal process of gaining limited liability status. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Questions on this subtopic are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-business-ownership-practice/), and condensed notes are in the [Business in the Real World revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-real-world-revision-notes/).

## Four things to compare for every structure

Whatever the structure, examiners reward answers built around four ideas.

| Idea | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Management and control | Who makes the decisions, and does anyone else have a say? |
| Sources of finance | How much money can the business raise, and from where? |
| Liability | If the business cannot pay its debts, whose property is at risk? |
| Distribution of profits | Who receives the profit, and how is it shared? |

Use these four headings to compare structures and to justify a recommendation.

## Sole traders

A **sole trader** is a business owned and controlled by one person. The owner may employ other people, but only one person owns the business. Examples are a freelance electrician, a market-stall holder or a one-person hairdressing salon.

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| The owner keeps full control and makes quick decisions | The owner has **unlimited liability**: personal belongings can be used to pay business debts |
| The owner keeps all the profit | Finance is limited to the owner's savings, loans from family and friends and bank loans |
| Few legal formalities, so it is cheap and simple to start | The owner carries all the risk and may have to work very long hours |
| Easy to close down | Illness or holiday can stop the business, since one person does the key tasks |

## Partnerships

A **partnership** is owned by two or more people, called partners, who share the running of the business and the profits. Examples are a small firm of accountants or dentists.

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| More people can bring savings, so more finance | Partners may disagree, which slows decisions |
| Skills are shared, such as one partner handling sales and one finance | Partners normally have **unlimited liability**, and may be responsible for debts caused by another partner |
| Workload and risk are shared | Profits are shared between the partners |
| Still fairly simple to set up | Finance is limited compared with a large company |

**Worked example 1: sharing profit.** Ana invests $30,000 in a partnership and Ben invests $20,000. They agree to share profits in the same ratio as their investment, 3 to 2. This year the profit is $45,000.

```
Ana's share = 3/5 x $45,000 = $27,000
Ben's share = 2/5 x $45,000 = $18,000
Check: $27,000 + $18,000 = $45,000
```

If instead they had agreed to share equally, each would receive $22,500. Partners should agree the sharing arrangement before they start, because it is a common cause of disputes.

## Limited liability businesses

In a **limited company** the business is a separate legal body from its owners. The owners are **shareholders**, who put money in by buying shares. **Limited liability** means a shareholder can lose no more than the amount they invested in the company. Their home, car and savings are safe if the business cannot pay its debts.

**Worked example 2: limited liability at work.** A business fails owing $90,000 when its assets are worth $40,000, leaving a shortfall of $50,000.

- If it was a sole trader, the owner is personally responsible for the $50,000. Personal property may have to be sold.
- If it was a limited company, the owners lose their share investment, for example $5,000 each, but cannot be forced to pay the rest from their own pockets. The creditors suffer the loss.

Limited liability makes owning shares less risky, so more people are willing to invest. This is its main benefit to businesses.

### Private and public limited companies

| Feature | Private limited company | Public limited company |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the shares | Chosen people, often family and friends; shares cannot be sold to the public | Anyone can buy shares on a stock exchange |
| Finance | Raised from a small number of shareholders and loans | Can raise very large sums by selling shares to the public |
| Control | Founders usually keep control | Control can be lost if shares are bought by others |
| Information | Fewer rules about publishing accounts | Must publish detailed accounts |

You are not required to explain how a company gains limited liability status. Focus on the effects.

**Distribution of profits in a company.** Profit is shared in two ways: some is **retained** for use in the business, and the rest is paid to shareholders as a **dividend**. 

**Worked example 3: dividends.** A company makes a profit of $80,000. It keeps 25% for new equipment and pays the rest to shareholders. There are 120,000 shares.

```
Retained profit = 25% x $80,000 = $20,000
Paid out        = $80,000 - $20,000 = $60,000
Dividend per share = $60,000 / 120,000 = $0.50
A shareholder with 2,000 shares receives 2,000 x $0.50 = $1,000
```

### Benefits and drawbacks of limited companies

Benefits: limited liability; the ability to raise more finance by selling shares; a business that continues even if an owner leaves or dies; and a more serious image with lenders and suppliers. Drawbacks: more legal formalities and costs; accounts must be filed, and in public companies they are open to the public; profits are shared with shareholders; and owners may lose control as more shares are sold.

## Multinational organisations

A **multinational** is a business that operates in more than one country, often with its head office in one country and factories, shops or offices in others. It may be a large public limited company. Benefits include access to many markets, the chance to produce where costs are lower and the power to spread risk across countries. Drawbacks include the difficulty of managing across distances and cultures, the need to follow different laws, and criticism if the business is seen to pay low wages or harm the environment abroad. Its stakeholders are spread across many countries.

## Not-for-profit organisations

A **not-for-profit organisation** exists mainly to serve a social aim rather than to make money for owners. Examples are charities, community clubs and some co-operatives. Any surplus is used to carry on the work and is not paid out as profit. They often rely on donations, grants and volunteers. Their main drawbacks are uncertain funding and limited finance, and they may find it hard to match the pay offered by profit-making firms.

## Private sector and public sector

In a **mixed economy** some businesses are owned by individuals and companies and some by the government.

| Sector | Ownership | Typical aim | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Private sector | Individuals or groups of shareholders | Profit | Sole traders, partnerships, companies |
| Public sector | The state or local government | Provide services for the public, often at low or no cost | State hospitals, public schools, public transport run by the government |

Public sector organisations are funded mainly by taxes. Private sector businesses are funded by owners and lenders and must earn income to survive.

## Choosing the best structure

The specification asks you to evaluate which structure suits a given business. Use the four headings and the facts in the case.

- **A new market-stall business, one owner, $3,000 savings:** a sole trader. It is cheap and simple, and the owner wants full control. The risk is small enough to accept unlimited liability.
- **Two friends opening a dental surgery needing $120,000 of equipment:** a partnership or a private limited company. The partners need to pool finance and skills, but the large debts make limited liability attractive.
- **A well-known clothing business wanting to open 40 new shops overseas:** a public limited company. The size of finance needed, and the need to protect owners from large debts, make selling shares to the public suitable.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that a limited company "cannot lose money". Shareholders can lose their investment.
- Confusing a partnership with a limited company; most partners have unlimited liability.
- Saying sole traders work alone. They may employ staff, but they own the business alone.
- Describing the public sector as "public limited companies". They are different things.
- Naming benefits without relating them to the case study business.

## Exam technique for this section

For **describe** and **state** questions, give the key feature in a few words. For **analyse** questions, link a feature to its consequence: unlimited liability means the owner's home could be sold if debts cannot be paid, so the owner may be cautious about borrowing. For **evaluate** questions, weigh the structure against the stated needs of the business (finance, risk, control) and reach a judgement that fits the facts.
