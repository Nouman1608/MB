## Warm-up questions

**W1.** Distinguish between substantive and functional definitions of religion. **[4]**

**W2.** Explain what Durkheim meant by the *sacred*. **[2]**

**W3.** Outline **one** reason why women may show higher religiosity than men. **[2]**

**W4.** What is *liberation theology*? **[2]**

### Warm-up answers

- **W1.** Substantive definitions identify religion by its content, such as belief in a supernatural
  being (Weber); functional definitions identify it by what it does, such as creating social
  solidarity (Durkheim). 2 marks each; credit an example or problem.
- **W2.** Things set apart and forbidden, which inspire awe and represent the collective power of
  society, as opposed to the ordinary (profane).
- **W3.** For example: socialisation into caring, nurturing roles compatible with religious values;
  greater involvement in children's moral upbringing; longer life expectancy and greater
  experience of bereavement.
- **W4.** A movement within the Catholic Church, especially in Latin America from the 1960s, that
  interprets Christianity as a commitment to the poor and to social justice (Gutiérrez, 1971).

## Essay 1 (11.1)

**"It is impossible to measure religious belief accurately." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Belief is private and subjective; interpretivists argue meaning cannot be reduced to numbers.
- Operationalisation problems: belief, belonging and practice differ (Glock and Stark, 1968).
- Attendance statistics are collected differently by different organisations.
- Survey wording and social desirability change answers.
- Definitions of religion determine what is counted.

**Against:**
- Large-scale surveys using consistent questions over time allow reliable comparison of trends.
- Multiple indicators (belief, practice, experience) improve validity.
- Qualitative methods — interviews, ethnography — capture meaning.
- Methodological pluralism and triangulation can reduce error.

**Evaluation:** measurement can be reliable without being perfectly valid; the issue matters because
secularisation claims depend on it. Link to research methods concepts from Paper 1.

## Essay 2 (11.2)

**"Religion mainly functions to maintain social solidarity." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**Functionalist support:** Durkheim (1912) — sacred symbols represent society; collective worship
creates collective conscience; Malinowski — coping with uncertainty and life crises; Parsons — core
values and meaning; Bellah (1967) — civil religion integrates diverse societies; national mourning
and ceremonies.

**Marxist challenge:** Marx (1844) — religion is ideology and the opium of the people; it serves the
ruling class; legitimates inequality (divine right, caste); offers compensation.

**Other challenges:** religious conflict and sectarian violence divide societies; religious diversity
weakens a single collective conscience; feminists argue religion supports patriarchy; religion can
cause change (Weber, liberation theology).

**Evaluation:** solidarity within a group can mean conflict between groups; judge which perspective
fits small, homogeneous versus large, diverse societies.

## Essay 3 (11.3)

**"Weber's theory explains the relationship between religion and the rise of capitalism."
Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**Weber's argument (1905):** Calvinist predestination created salvation anxiety; success in a
worldly *calling* was read as a sign of election; asceticism led to saving and reinvestment, so a
"spirit of capitalism" emerged; Weber compared Northern Europe with societies where similar
economic conditions did not produce capitalism.

**Criticisms and alternatives:**
- Tawney (1926): economic change came first; Protestantism adapted to it.
- Marxists: capitalism results from changes in the mode of production.
- Capitalism developed in some Catholic regions; some Calvinist regions did not industrialise.
- Many Calvinists were not capitalists and vice versa.

**Defence:** Weber argued Calvinism was one *necessary* factor among several, not the sole cause;
his comparative method was ambitious.

**Evaluation:** distinguish between "explains" and "contributes to"; conclude on Weber's lasting
value in showing ideas can shape economic change.

## Essay 4 (11.3)

**"Religious movements have a significant influence on political struggles." Evaluate this view.**
**[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Iranian revolution (1979): religious leadership under Ayatollah Khomeini overthrew the Shah.
- Liberation theology in Latin America: clergy supported the poor against military regimes;
  Archbishop Romero's assassination (1980).
- The Evangelical movement in the USA: the Moral Majority (founded 1979) mobilised voters on moral
  issues.
- Maduro (1982): relative autonomy allows religion to oppose ruling classes.

**Against:**
- Bruce (2003): US Evangelicals gained publicity but achieved limited lasting policy change.
- Religious movements often succeed only when combined with economic or political grievances.
- Marxist view that religion usually supports the status quo.
- In secularised societies religion has less political leverage.

**Evaluation:** influence depends on whether religion is the main channel for opposition (where
other channels are blocked, as in pre-revolutionary Iran, its role grows).

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail should I give on case studies?** Enough to show you know what happened and why it
matters — usually a short paragraph, followed by analysis.

**Can I use examples from my own country?** Yes — relevant local examples of religion and politics
earn application marks.

**Which essay is most likely to need research methods?** Essay 1 — use validity, reliability and
operationalisation explicitly.
