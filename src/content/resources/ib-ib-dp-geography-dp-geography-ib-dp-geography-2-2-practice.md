## Section A

**1.** Define a storm surge. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between spring tides and neap tides. **[2]**

**3.** A storm has a central pressure of 978 hPa. Using normal pressure of 1,013 hPa and a rise of 1 cm per hPa, estimate the sea-level rise caused by low pressure alone. **[1]**

**4.** A storm wave is 4 m high and a calm-weather wave is 1 m high. How many times more energy does the storm wave carry? **[1]**

**5.** Define coastal squeeze. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows an invented sediment budget for a beach, in cubic metres per year.

| Inputs | | Outputs | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cliff erosion | 28,000 | Longshore drift out | 30,000 |
| River supply | 6,000 | Offshore storm loss | 7,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the net annual change in the beach store. **[2]**

**(b)** A port breakwater is built updrift and halves the cliff input reaching the beach. Calculate the new net change. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one consequence of this change for the beach. **[2]**

**7.** Describe and explain the differences between a summer and a winter beach profile. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why a storm surge arriving at high spring tide is more dangerous than the same surge at low neap tide. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how salt marshes can keep pace with rising sea level, and why some fail to do so. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the ways in which human activities alter physical processes at the coast. **[10]**

**11.** With reference to examples, discuss the relative importance of waves and tides in shaping coastal landforms. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A temporary rise in sea level above the predicted tide, caused by low pressure and strong winds during a storm [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Spring tides occur when the Sun, Moon and Earth are aligned, at new and full moon, giving the largest tidal range [1]; neap tides occur when the Sun and Moon are at right angles, giving the smallest range [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 1,013 - 978 = 35 hPa, so about **35 cm** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** (4 / 1) squared = **16 times** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** The loss of intertidal habitats such as salt marsh [1] when rising sea level pushes them landward against a fixed barrier such as a sea wall, so they cannot migrate inland [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Inputs = 28,000 + 6,000 = 34,000; outputs = 30,000 + 7,000 = 37,000 [1]. Net change = **-3,000 cubic metres a year** [1].

**(b)** Cliff input = 14,000, so inputs = 20,000 [1]. Net change = 20,000 - 37,000 = **-17,000 cubic metres a year** [1].

**(c)** The beach narrows and lowers [1], so waves expend less energy on the beach and more on the land behind it, increasing erosion of dunes, cliffs or defences [1]. **[6]**

**7.** In summer, constructive swell waves move sediment onshore [1], building a wide beach with a berm near the top [1]. In winter, destructive storm waves move sediment offshore [1], lowering and flattening the beach and forming an offshore bar, from which sediment can return in calm weather [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Surge height adds to the tide height [1], so at high spring tide the combined water level is at its maximum, overtopping defences and flooding land that would stay dry at low neap tide [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Marshes trap fine sediment carried in by the tide, and plants add organic matter, so the marsh surface rises [1]; if the surface rises as fast as sea level, the marsh survives [1]. Some fail where sediment supply has fallen, for example because rivers are dammed or sediment cells interrupted [1], or where defences prevent the marsh moving inland as sea level rises (coastal squeeze) [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Interrupting sediment movement:** groynes, breakwaters and ports trap longshore drift, starving beaches downdrift [1] [1].
- **Removing sediment:** beach and river sand mining lowers beaches [1]; dams inland cut sediment supply, as on the Nile after the Aswan High Dam, causing delta erosion [1].
- **Altering wave energy:** sea walls reflect energy and scour beaches [1]; removing mangroves and reefs lets larger waves reach the shore [1].
- **Changing relative sea level:** groundwater or gas extraction makes deltas subside [1].
- **Indirect, global effects:** greenhouse emissions raise sea level and warm water, bleaching reefs [1].
- **Examination:** effects are often felt away from the place of the activity, so impacts are displaced in space and delayed in time [1].
- **Conclusion:** human activity now rivals natural processes in many coastal systems, especially through sediment supply [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Waves:** supply most erosive energy through hydraulic action and abrasion, forming cliffs, platforms, caves, arches and stacks [1]; wave energy rises with the square of height, so storms dominate change [1]; refraction concentrates energy on headlands [1]; longshore drift builds spits, bars and tombolos [1].
- **Tides:** tidal range sets the vertical zone of wave attack [1]; large ranges produce wide intertidal platforms and mudflats [1]; tidal currents shape estuaries and salt marshes [1]; high tides combine with surges to flood coasts [1].
- **Interaction:** tides control where wave energy acts, so the two work together [1].
- **Conclusion:** waves are usually more important on exposed, high-energy coasts; tides are more important on sheltered, macrotidal estuaries [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Relative importance" questions need a judgement that varies with the type of coast. Name a high-energy and a low-energy example to support it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to square the height ratio in wave energy questions.
- Leaving out the sign or the units in sediment budget answers.
- Explaining a surge with low pressure only, ignoring wind set-up and timing.
- Describing human activities without saying which process they change.
