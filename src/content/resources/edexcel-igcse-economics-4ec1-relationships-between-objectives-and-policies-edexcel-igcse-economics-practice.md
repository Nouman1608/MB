## Section A

**1.** What is meant by a trade-off between macroeconomic objectives? **[2]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following policies is most likely to reduce inflation and improve the current account in the short run? **[1]**

A: A cut in the interest rate

B: A rise in the rate of income tax

C: An increase in government spending on roads

D: A cut in the rate of business tax

**3.** Explain one reason why a cut in the interest rate might lead to higher inflation. **[3]**

**4.** In Country H, clothing has a weight of 6% in the consumer price index and energy has a weight of 12%. In one year clothing prices rise by 15% and energy prices by 5%. All other prices are unchanged. Calculate the combined contribution of clothing and energy to the inflation rate. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how stricter regulation of factory emissions could slow economic growth. **[3]**

**6.** In Country J the unemployment rate rose from 6.5% to 8.0% while inflation fell from 5.0% to 2.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in each rate in percentage points. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the trade-off shown by these figures. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Country R's government raised spending and cut taxes at the start of Year 2.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 1.0% | 3.5% | 4.5% |
| Unemployment rate | 9.0% | 7.0% | 5.5% |
| Inflation rate | 1.0% | 2.5% | 5.0% |
| Current account (% of GDP) | +1.0% | -0.5% | -2.5% |

**(a)** Calculate the change in inflation, in percentage points, between Year 1 and Year 2, and between Year 2 and Year 3. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, analyse the trade-off between unemployment and inflation in Country R. **[6]**

**(c)** Explain how the data show the trade-off between inflation and the current account. **[4]**

**8.** Country T introduces a tax on carbon emissions. Before the tax, emissions were 50 million tonnes a year. They fall by 12%. Forecast growth falls from 3.0% to 2.4% and electricity prices rise.

**(a)** Calculate emissions after the tax. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain one way the tax could raise the inflation rate. **[3]**

**(c)** Assess the trade-off between economic growth and environmental protection created by the tax. **[6]**

**9.** The central bank of Country W raises its interest rate from 3% to 5%.

**(a)** Explain why this could cause the exchange rate of Country W's currency to rise. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how a rise in the exchange rate could limit any improvement in the current account caused by the higher interest rate. **[3]**

**10.** Country V has inflation of 7%, caused mainly by strong consumer spending. Unemployment is 3%, close to its lowest level for twenty years. Productivity growth has been weak for five years and firms report shortages of skilled workers.

Evaluate whether the government should use a rise in interest rates or supply-side policies to reduce inflation in Country V. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A situation where achieving one macroeconomic objective [1] makes another objective worse, for example lower unemployment coming with higher inflation [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A higher income tax reduces disposable income, so spending falls, which reduces demand-pull inflation and spending on imports. A and C raise spending; D aims to raise investment. **[1]**

**3.** A lower interest rate makes borrowing cheaper [1], so households borrow and spend more on items such as cars and houses, and firms invest more [1]. If total spending grows faster than the economy can increase output, demand-pull pressure pushes prices up [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Clothing: 0.06 x 15 = 0.9 percentage points [1]. Energy: 0.12 x 5 = 0.6 percentage points [1]. Combined contribution = 0.9 + 0.6 = **1.5 percentage points** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Firms must fit cleaner equipment or change methods, which raises their costs of production [1]. Higher costs reduce profit, so some firms cut output or delay expansion and investment [1]. Lower output and investment across many firms mean real GDP grows more slowly [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Unemployment rose by 1.5 percentage points [1]; inflation fell by 2.5 percentage points [1].

**(b)** The trade-off between unemployment and inflation: lower inflation has come with higher unemployment [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Year 1 to Year 2: 2.5 - 1.0 = 1.5 percentage points [1]. Year 2 to Year 3: 5.0 - 2.5 = 2.5 percentage points [1].

**(b)** Higher government spending and lower taxes raised total demand [1], so firms increased output and hired workers: unemployment fell from 9.0% to 7.0% and then to 5.5% [1]. As unemployment fell, inflation rose from 1.0% to 2.5% and then to 5.0% [1], because firms competed for fewer available workers and raised wages, passing the higher costs on in prices [1]. The trade-off became worse over time: in Year 2 a 2-point fall in unemployment came with a 1.5-point rise in inflation, but in Year 3 a smaller 1.5-point fall came with a larger 2.5-point rise [1]. This is because spare capacity was used up, so extra demand went increasingly into higher prices rather than more output [1].

**(c)** Inflation rose from 1.0% to 5.0% over the period [1] while the current account moved from a surplus of 1.0% of GDP to a deficit of 2.5% [1]. As prices in Country R rose, its exports became more expensive for foreign buyers, so export sales fell [1], and imports became relatively cheaper, so spending on imports rose, worsening the current account [1]. (Accept: rising incomes also increased spending on imports.) **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* In part (b) the strongest point is the comparison between the two years. Quoting the figures earns application marks; noticing that the trade-off worsens shows analysis.

**8. (a)** Fall = 50 x 0.12 = 6 million tonnes [1]. Emissions after the tax = 50 - 6 = **44 million tonnes** [1].

**(b)** The tax raises the cost of producing electricity from fossil fuels, so electricity prices rise [1]. Firms that use a lot of electricity face higher costs [1] and pass some of them on to consumers in higher prices, so the general price level rises (cost-push inflation) [1].

**(c)** Knowledge and analysis (up to 3): the tax makes pollution more expensive, so firms switch to cleaner methods or cut polluting output [1]; emissions fall by 6 million tonnes, which improves environmental protection [1]; but higher costs reduce firms' output and investment, so forecast growth falls from 3.0% to 2.4%, a fall of 0.6 percentage points [1].
Assessment (up to 3): the environmental gain is large relative to the growth cost, as emissions fall by 12% while growth is still positive [1]; the cost to growth may be short-term if firms invest in cleaner technology and energy, which could create new jobs [1]; judgement: the trade-off is real but modest, and its size depends on whether firms can switch to cheaper clean energy or simply pass on costs in prices [1]. **[11]**

**9. (a)** Higher interest rates give a better return on savings held in Country W [1], so foreign savers and investors want to deposit money there [1]. To do so they must buy Country W's currency, so demand for the currency rises and its exchange rate rises [1].

**(b)** An appreciation makes Country W's exports more expensive in foreign currencies [1], so foreign buyers purchase fewer of them, reducing export revenue [1]. Imports become cheaper in Country W's currency, so spending on imports may rise, offsetting the fall in imports caused by lower domestic spending [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Knowledge and analysis (up to 4): a rise in interest rates makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive [1], so consumer spending falls, reducing demand-pull inflation [1]. Supply-side policies such as training raise productivity and productive capacity [1], so output can grow to meet demand without the same pressure on prices [1].
Application (up to 3): the inflation is caused by strong consumer spending, so it is demand-pull, which an interest rate rise targets directly [1]. Unemployment is only 3%, so the economy has little spare capacity and the job losses from higher rates would come from a low base [1]. Weak productivity growth and skills shortages show a supply-side problem that interest rates cannot fix [1].
Evaluation (up to 3): supply-side policies take years, so they cannot bring 7% inflation down quickly [1]; an interest rate rise acts sooner but slows growth and raises unemployment, and may raise the exchange rate [1]. Judgement: an interest rate rise is the better short-run tool because the inflation is demand-pull and unemployment is low, but combining it with training spending as a policy mix would address the skills shortages that make inflation likely to return [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Use every clue in the extract. "Strong consumer spending" points to demand-pull inflation; "3%" tells you the cost in jobs; "skills shortages" justifies a supply-side element.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining one side of a trade-off only, for example how a tax cut lowers unemployment but not how it raises inflation.
- Not quoting figures from the table when the question says "using the data".
- Confusing percentage points with percentage change.
- Forgetting that environmental taxes can raise prices as well as slowing growth.
- Recommending supply-side policy for an urgent problem without weighing its time lag.
- Giving a final judgement that does not follow from the points made.
