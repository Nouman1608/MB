**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 9, "Conflict and instability in the Middle East (1896 to 2020)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The study centres on the Arab-Israeli conflict. This is a sensitive and contested subject: use accurate terms, treat the experiences and arguments of Jewish, Arab and Palestinian peoples with equal care, and judge evidence rather than taking sides. The six inquiry topics are: the origins of the conflict; the reasons for Arab-Jewish tensions to 1945; the reasons for the first Arab-Israeli war; the continuation and escalation of the conflict; its impact; and attempts to reach a peaceful settlement.

## 1. Origins of the Arab-Israeli conflict

- **Zionism and the Balfour Declaration.** Zionism grew in the late nineteenth century as a Jewish national movement, in response to persecution in Europe and the idea of national self-determination. Theodor Herzl's work and the First Zionist Congress at Basel (1897) set out its aims. In 1917 British Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour announced support for "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, provided the rights of the existing non-Jewish communities were not prejudiced.
- **Sykes-Picot and the Palestine Mandate.** In 1916 Britain and France secretly agreed spheres of influence in the Ottoman Arab lands. Britain had also made promises to Sharif Hussein of Mecca in the Hussein-McMahon correspondence. After the war, the League of Nations gave Britain the mandate for Palestine (confirmed 1922). Arabs felt their wartime expectations had been betrayed.
- **Growth of Arab nationalism.** Arab nationalists, who had supported the revolt against the Ottomans, demanded independence and opposed a Jewish national home. Palestinian Arab identity grew in response to the mandate and to Jewish immigration.
- **Increased Jewish immigration.** Waves of immigration (*aliyot*) from Europe brought land purchases, new settlements and institutions. By the 1940s the Jewish population was about a third of the total.

## 2. Reasons for Arab-Jewish tensions (to 1945)

- **British immigration policy.** Britain tried to balance two promises and changed course: the 1922 White Paper tied immigration to economic capacity, and the 1939 White Paper limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years and then required Arab consent, just as Jews were trying to flee Nazi persecution.
- **Social, political and economic factors.** Competition for land and employment; the separation of Jewish and Arab economies and institutions; disputes over holy sites, as in the 1929 riots over the Western Wall; Arab fears of losing land and political control.
- **Arab Revolt (1936 to 1939).** A general strike and rebellion against British rule and Jewish immigration, led by the Arab Higher Committee under the Mufti Haj Amin al-Husseini. Britain suppressed it harshly. The Peel Commission (1937) proposed partition, which Arab leaders rejected.
- **Responses.** Jewish groups built the *Haganah* defence force and institutions such as the Jewish Agency, while a smaller group, the Irgun, used violence. Arab leaders were divided between those who accepted compromise and those who rejected it.

**Argument to weigh:** "British policy was the main cause of Arab-Jewish tensions." Support: contradictory promises, shifting immigration policy, repression. Challenge: the two communities had competing national claims that British policy could not have reconciled.

## 3. Reasons for the First Arab-Israeli War (1948)

- **Impact of the Second World War and Jewish immigration.** The Holocaust, and the plight of survivors, increased support for a Jewish state, and illegal immigration against British restrictions grew.
- **Role of the UK and the UN.** Britain, unable to find a solution, passed the problem to the UN in 1947. The UN General Assembly voted on 29 November 1947 for the partition of Palestine into Jewish and Arab states, with Jerusalem under international control. Jews largely accepted the plan, and the Arab leadership and Arab states rejected it.
- **Creation of the state of Israel.** After months of civil war between Jewish and Arab forces, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed Israel on 14 May 1948, as the British mandate ended.
- **Role of groups and individuals.** The Arab League states (Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon) sent forces on 15 May. The war ended with armistice agreements in 1949. Israel held more territory than the UN plan had allotted; Jordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem; Egypt held Gaza. About 700,000 Palestinians left or were expelled, an event Palestinians call the *Nakba* (catastrophe).

**Perspectives.** Traditional Israeli accounts presented the war as a defence against invasion and the flight of Palestinians as largely at the urging of Arab leaders. Historians such as Benny Morris, Avi Shlaim and Ilan Pappé, sometimes called the "New Historians", used Israeli archives to show a more complex picture, including expulsions and the role of military action, though they disagree with each other on intent. Arab accounts stressed dispossession.

## 4. Continuation and escalation of the conflict

- **Military conflicts.** The Suez Crisis (1956): after Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal, Israel, Britain and France attacked Egypt, and US and Soviet pressure forced them to withdraw. The Six-Day War (June 1967): Israel's pre-emptive attack destroyed Arab air forces and took Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights. The October War (1973): Egypt and Syria attacked on Yom Kippur. Israel recovered but the war showed Israeli vulnerability and led to diplomacy.
- **Creation of the PLO and the search for support.** The Palestine Liberation Organization was formed in 1964 under Arab League sponsorship. After 1967 Fatah under Yasser Arafat took control of it. Palestinian groups fought from Jordan (until 1970), then Lebanon.
- **Israeli policies.** After 1967, settlements in the occupied territories, military rule, and operations against the PLO, including the invasion of Lebanon in 1982. UN Security Council Resolution 242 called for withdrawal from occupied territories and for recognition of all states in the area, wording that remained open to different readings.
- **Palestinian actions.** The First Intifada (1987 to 1993) was a mass uprising in the occupied territories, using strikes, demonstrations and stone throwing. Hamas was founded in 1987. The Second Intifada (from 2000) involved armed attacks and heavy Israeli military response.

## 5. Impact of the Arab-Israeli conflict

- **Political and economic impact.** Heavy military spending, the influence of the Cold War, the 1973 oil embargo, and the effect of conflict on states such as Egypt, Jordan and Lebanon.
- **Social impact.** Refugees and migration: Palestinian refugees in camps supported by UNRWA, Jewish immigration from Europe and from Arab countries.
- **Women.** Women took part in political movements, in the intifadas and in the Israeli armed forces, and bore the burdens of displacement and loss of male family members.
- **Marginalised groups.** Palestinian citizens of Israel (under military government until 1966), Palestinian refugees in neighbouring states, Mizrahi Jews and other minority groups.

## 6. Attempts to reach a peaceful settlement

- **Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty (1979).** Following the Camp David talks (1978) with US President Carter, Egypt recognised Israel and Israel returned Sinai. Sadat was assassinated in 1981, and Egypt was isolated in the Arab League for a time.
- **Oslo Peace Process.** The 1993 Declaration of Principles between Israel (Rabin) and the PLO (Arafat), mutual recognition and phased Palestinian self-rule. Rabin was assassinated in 1995 by an Israeli opponent of the process. Violence, settlement growth and failure to resolve final status issues undermined it.
- **Camp David (2000).** Talks between Barak and Arafat, with President Clinton, failed on Jerusalem, refugees and borders. Each side blamed the other, and historians disagree about the responsibility.
- **Arab Peace Initiative (2002).** A Saudi-led plan, adopted by the Arab League, offering normal relations in exchange for withdrawal to 1967 lines and a settlement for refugees. Israel did not accept it.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "The Six-Day War of 1967 was the most important turning point in the Arab-Israeli conflict." Evaluate this claim.

1. Define "turning point": a change in direction, with long-term effects.
2. For: occupation of territories, settlements, refugees, a new Arab strategy of land for peace, Resolution 242.
3. Against: 1948 created the refugee issue and the state; the 1973 war changed Arab policy and led to Camp David; Oslo changed the framework.
4. Perspectives: some historians stress 1948 as the founding event, others 1967 for the occupation.
5. Judgement: 1967 was a major turning point because it created the issues of territory still debated, but 1948 laid the base.

## Common mistakes

- Taking a side instead of analysing.
- Using the term "Palestinian" or "Israeli" for the whole period without explaining changes in identity.
- Omitting the role of Britain and the UN.
- Giving casualty or refugee figures without "about".

## Frequently asked questions

**Which people can be named in questions?** Those in the guide: David Ben-Gurion, and groups such as the Arab League, the PLO and Hamas.

**How do I deal with disputed language?** Use neutral terms, explain disputed terms (for example Nakba) and give the perspectives on them.
