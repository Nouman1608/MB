This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.4.1 Kinetic theory**, statements a to e of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. It includes the required practical *investigate cooling curves for stearic acid*, which the specification marks P (full Physics award only). The specification also says that throughout section 3.4 you should be able to **explain the shape of a temperature–time graph** for a substance heated or cooled through changes of state, so that skill runs through every section below.

For the combined overview of kinetic theory and heat transfer, see the [kinetic theory, specific heat and latent heat guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-kinetic-theory-specific-heat-and-latent-heat/). ## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Kinetic theory explains the states of matter and their properties; particles in solids, liquids and gases have different amounts of energy. Recognise, use and compare particle diagrams |
| b | Specific heat capacity: energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C; E = m c Δθ |
| c | Specific latent heat of vaporisation: energy to change 1 kg from liquid to vapour with no temperature change; E = m L_V |
| d | Specific latent heat of fusion: energy to change 1 kg from solid to liquid with no temperature change; E = m L_F |
| e | Impurities affect the melting point of a solid and the boiling point of a liquid |

## Particle models and properties (statement a)

Kinetic theory says all matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion. The state depends on how much energy the particles have compared with the attractive forces between them.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Close together, regular pattern | Close together, irregular | Far apart, random |
| Movement | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around each other | Move quickly in all directions |
| Energy of particles | Least | More | Most |
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape and volume | Takes the container's shape; fixed volume | Fills the container |
| Compressibility | Almost none | Almost none | Easily compressed |

When you **compare** diagrams, use these marking features: in a solid and a liquid the particles touch their neighbours, so the two have similar densities, while a gas diagram should show only a few particles with large gaps. A liquid diagram that leaves big gaps, or a solid drawn without a regular pattern, loses credit. Each property in the table should be explained from the arrangement: a gas compresses because there is space between particles; a liquid flows because its particles can slide past each other.

## Specific heat capacity (statement b)

> E = m c Δθ, where E is in joules, m in kilograms, c in J/kg °C and Δθ in °C

A substance with a **high** specific heat capacity needs a lot of energy for each degree of temperature rise, so it warms slowly and cools slowly. Water's high value is why it is used to carry energy in heating systems.

**Worked example 1 (finding the temperature change).** 42 000 J of energy is transferred to 2.0 kg of water (c = 4200 J/kg °C). Rearranging, Δθ = E ÷ (m c) = 42 000 ÷ (2.0 × 4200) = **5.0 °C**. If the water started at 18 °C, it ends at 23 °C.

## Specific latent heat (statements c and d)

> E = m L, with L in J/kg

During melting or boiling the temperature stays constant even though energy is still being supplied. The energy is used to overcome the forces between particles and separate them, not to make them move faster. Freezing and condensing release the same energy per kilogram. Vaporisation needs far more energy than fusion for the same substance, because the particles must be separated completely rather than just freed from fixed positions.

**Worked example 2 (three stages).** Calculate the energy needed to turn 0.20 kg of ice at −15 °C into water at 25 °C. Use c(ice) = 2100 J/kg °C, c(water) = 4200 J/kg °C and L_F = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg (values supplied for this question).

```
Warm ice to 0 °C:      E = 0.20 × 2100 × 15      =  6 300 J
Melt at 0 °C:          E = 0.20 × 3.34 × 10⁵     = 66 800 J
Warm water to 25 °C:   E = 0.20 × 4200 × 25      = 21 000 J
Total                                            = 94 100 J ≈ 9.4 × 10⁴ J
```

The melting stage needs more energy than both warming stages together. Exam questions often test whether you include all the stages and use the right specific heat capacity for each state.

## Reading a temperature–time graph

Imagine a heater of constant power warming a solid until it has boiled. The graph has five parts:

1. **Sloping line (solid warming).** Particles vibrate more; temperature rises.
2. **Flat line at the melting point.** Energy breaks the regular arrangement; temperature constant.
3. **Sloping line (liquid warming).**
4. **Flat line at the boiling point.** Energy separates the particles completely. This plateau is longer than the melting one, because L_V is larger than L_F.
5. **Sloping line (gas warming).**

With constant power, the **gradient** of a sloping section depends on the specific heat capacity: a gentler slope means a larger c. For water, the liquid section is less steep than the ice section because water's c is about twice that of ice.

**Worked example 3 (latent heat from a graph).** A 200 W heater warms 0.50 kg of a solid. The graph is flat at the melting point for 400 s. Energy supplied during melting = P t = 200 × 400 = 80 000 J, so L_F = E ÷ m = 80 000 ÷ 0.50 = **1.6 × 10⁵ J/kg**. In practice some energy heats the surroundings, so this value is an overestimate.

## Required practical: cooling curves for stearic acid

Stearic acid is melted in a boiling tube in hot water, then left to cool while its temperature is recorded at regular intervals. The curve falls quickly at first, levels off while the liquid freezes, then falls again as the solid cools.

- **Why the flat section?** As the liquid solidifies, attractive forces pull particles into fixed positions and energy is released. This release balances the energy transferred to the surroundings, so the temperature stays at the freezing point until all the acid is solid.
- **Why does the cooling slow down?** The rate of energy transfer depends on the temperature difference with the room, which gets smaller.
- **Reading the melting point.** Take the temperature of the flat section, not the starting or finishing temperature.
- **Risks.** Hot water and hot stearic acid can scald; wear eye protection and stand the tube securely.

## Impurities (statement e)

A pure substance melts and boils at fixed temperatures, giving sharp flat sections on a heating or cooling graph. Impurities change these temperatures: salt lowers the melting point of ice, which is why salt is spread on icy roads, and dissolved salt raises the boiling point of water. An impure sample changes state over a range of temperatures, so its plateau slopes slightly. If a stearic acid sample shows a sloping, lower "flat" section, it is likely to be impure.

## Common mistakes

- Saying temperature rises during melting. It stays constant until the change of state is complete.
- Using °C for mass or kJ in place of J without converting.
- Leaving out one stage of a multi-stage calculation, or using the wrong c for the state.
- Writing that particles "expand" or "melt". The particles stay the same; their arrangement and motion change.
- Explaining a plateau with "the heat is lost". The energy is being used to separate particles.
- Drawing gas particles touching, or liquid particles widely spaced.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** particle arrangement and movement separately: two ideas per state.
- **Explain** a flat section: energy is still transferred, but it overcomes forces between particles rather than increasing their kinetic energy.
- **Calculate**: write E = m c Δθ or E = m L first, convert grams to kilograms, then substitute.
- **Compare** two substances: link the size of c or L to how much energy is needed per kilogram.
- **Evaluate** a practical value: say whether energy losses make the result too high or too low, and suggest insulation or a lid.
