All examples are invented. Money is in dollars.

## 3.1.1 The five roles of marketing

| Role | In a sentence | Quick example: a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Identifying customer needs | Finding out what customers want | Notices requests for gluten-free bread |
| Satisfying customer needs | Providing products that meet those wants | Bakes a gluten-free range |
| Maintaining customer loyalty | Making customers come back | A stamp card: tenth loaf free |
| Building customer relationships | Creating trust over time | Staff learn regulars' usual orders |
| Anticipating changes in customer needs | Spotting future wants before rivals | Plans a delivery service as more people work from home |

Order of logic: identify, then satisfy, then keep (loyalty and relationships), while looking ahead (anticipate).

## 3.1.2 Market changes

**Why spending patterns change:** changes in income, tastes and fashion, the age structure of the population, technology, and relative prices.

**Why markets become more competitive:** new entrants (including from abroad), online selling widening the area a business competes in, and easy price comparison by customers.

**How businesses respond:**

- improve or develop products
- change prices
- increase or change promotion
- improve customer service and loyalty schemes
- target a different segment or a niche

### Market share

```
Market share = (sales revenue of a business / total sales revenue for the whole market) x 100
```

Worked reminder: Varrow Paints sells $1.8m in a market worth $24m.

```
1.8 / 24 x 100 = 7.5%
```

Next year Varrow sells $2.0m and the market is worth $32m.

```
2.0 / 32 x 100 = 6.25%
```

Sales up, share down: the market grew faster than Varrow did.

Working backwards: sales revenue = market share / 100 x total market sales. A 20% share of a $3m market = $600,000.

## 3.1.3 Mass and niche markets

| | Mass market | Niche market |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Large market; many customers buy similar products | Small, specialised part of a larger market |
| Example | Everyday toothpaste | Toothpaste for people with sensitive teeth |
| Advantages | High sales volume; low unit costs from large output; many places to sell | Fewer rivals; higher prices possible; loyal customers |
| Disadvantages | Strong competition; low prices; heavy promotion | Small sales; risk the niche shrinks or large firms enter; higher unit costs |
| Suits | Large businesses that can produce cheaply | Small businesses with specialist skills |

## 3.1.4 Segmentation

**Meaning:** dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics.

**The five bases in the syllabus:** age, income, location, gender, lifestyle.

| Basis | Example of a product aimed at one segment |
|---|---|
| Age | A phone with large buttons for elderly users |
| Income | A budget range and a premium range of the same food |
| Location | Rain jackets promoted in a wet region |
| Gender | Football boots designed for women |
| Lifestyle | Ready meals for busy workers |

**Advantages:** better-targeted products and promotion; less wasted advertising; can find gaps in the market; different prices for different groups.

**Disadvantages:** higher costs of separate products and campaigns; research needed; a segment may be too small; people within a segment still differ.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | Where buyers and sellers of a product come together |
| Customer loyalty | Customers choosing to buy again from the same business |
| Market share | A business's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Mass market | A large market where many customers buy similar products |
| Niche market | A small, specialised part of a larger market |
| Market segment | A group of customers with similar characteristics |
| Market segmentation | Dividing a market into segments |

## Exam traps

- Rising sales do not mean rising market share. Always compare with the market.
- Use the same measure for the business and the market (both revenue or both units).
- "Small market" is not enough for a niche: it must be *specialised*.
- A response to competition needs a drawback. A price cut can start a price war and lowers revenue per unit.
- Segment examples must match the basis: "teenagers" is age; "people who cycle to work" is lifestyle.
- In a "consider" answer, base the decision on the case: business size, its customers, the strength of rivals.

## Quick self-test

1. List the five roles of marketing named in the syllabus.
2. Give one example of how a business can maintain customer loyalty.
3. State the formula for market share.
4. A firm sells $450,000 in a market worth $3.6m. Calculate its market share.
5. A firm's sales rise but its market share falls. Explain how this can happen.
6. Give two reasons why a market may become more competitive.
7. Define a niche market.
8. State one advantage of a mass market for a large manufacturer.
9. Name the five bases of segmentation in the syllabus.
10. Which basis of segmentation is used by a holiday company targeting families with young children who like outdoor activities?
11. State one disadvantage of market segmentation.
12. A business has a 15% share of a market worth $4m. Calculate its sales revenue.

### Answers

1. Identifying customer needs, satisfying customer needs, maintaining customer loyalty, building customer relationships, anticipating changes in customer needs.
2. Any one, for example a reward or loyalty card, discounts for repeat customers, or reminders and special offers to existing customers.
3. Market share = (sales revenue of the business / total sales revenue for the whole market) x 100.
4. 450,000 / 3,600,000 x 100 = 12.5%.
5. The whole market grows by a larger percentage than the firm's sales, so rivals take more of the extra spending.
6. Any two: new businesses enter the market; firms from other countries enter; online selling lets more rivals reach the same customers; customers can compare prices easily.
7. A small, specialised part of a larger market, with customers who have particular needs.
8. Any one: high sales volume; low unit costs from producing in large quantities; many places to sell.
9. Age, income, location, gender and lifestyle.
10. Lifestyle (with age also relevant because of the young children).
11. Any one: higher costs of designing separate products or campaigns; research is needed; the segment may be too small; customers within a segment still differ.
12. 15 / 100 x 4,000,000 = $600,000.
