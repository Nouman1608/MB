## Condensed recall

### Pollutant behaviour

| Type | Examples | Main harm | Management focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biodegradable | Sewage, food waste | Oxygen use during decay | Treatment before release |
| Persistent | PCBs, many pesticides, mercury, lead, plastics | Accumulation, biomagnification, long-distance spread | Prevent use and release |
| Thermal | Power-station cooling water | Less dissolved oxygen, higher oxygen demand | Cooling towers, ponds |
| Acidic | Acid mine drainage | Low pH, dissolved toxic metals | Seal mines, treat drainage |

- **Stockholm Convention (2001)**: restricts persistent organic pollutants.

### BOD method

1. Measure dissolved oxygen at once (day 0).
2. Seal the sample in the dark at 20 °C (no photosynthesis, no air contact).
3. Measure again on day 5.
4. BOD₅ = DO day 0 − DO day 5.
5. Dilute heavily polluted samples, or oxygen runs out early and BOD is underestimated.

Short reminder: 8.8 → 2.4 mg L⁻¹ gives BOD₅ = **6.4 mg L⁻¹**.

### Load

```
load (g s⁻¹) = concentration (g m⁻³) × discharge (m³ s⁻¹)
1 mg L⁻¹ = 1 g m⁻³;  1 day = 86 400 s
```

Short reminder: 2 mg L⁻¹ × 5 m³ s⁻¹ = 10 g s⁻¹ = 864 000 g = **864 kg per day**.

### Survey design

- Control site upstream; several sites downstream.
- Same time of day (oxygen varies with photosynthesis).
- Repeat on several dates, including after rain.
- Combine direct measures with a biotic index.

### Nutrients

- **Limiting nutrient**: the one in shortest supply relative to need.
- Usually **phosphorus** in lakes and rivers; often **nitrogen** in coastal and marine waters.
- **Hypoxia**: dissolved oxygen below about 2 mg L⁻¹.
- **Dead zones**: northern Gulf of Mexico (Mississippi basin farm runoff); Baltic Sea.
- **Harmful algal blooms**: cyanobacterial toxins affect fish, animals, people and water supplies.
- **Internal loading**: phosphorus released from sediments under low oxygen delays recovery.

### Plastics

- Accumulate in gyres (Great Pacific Garbage Patch, North Pacific).
- Macroplastics: entanglement, ingestion.
- Microplastics (under 5 mm): breakdown of larger items, textile fibres, tyre wear; eaten by filter feeders and zooplankton.
- Carry adsorbed persistent chemicals.
- Prevention matters most: dispersed microplastics cannot be removed.

### Cases

- **Minamata, Japan (1950s)**: factory mercury → methylmercury → bioaccumulation and biomagnification in seafood → neurological disease. **Minamata Convention (2013)**.
- **Lake Erie**: eutrophic by the 1960s; Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (1972) cut point-source phosphorus; recovery; blooms returned from the 1990s through farm runoff of dissolved phosphorus.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biodegradable pollutant | Broken down by decomposers |
| Persistent pollutant | Breaks down very slowly, so it accumulates |
| BOD | Oxygen used by microorganisms decomposing organic matter in a sample |
| Load | Mass of pollutant delivered per unit time |
| Limiting nutrient | Nutrient whose short supply restricts growth |
| Hypoxia | Very low dissolved oxygen |
| Dead zone | Area where hypoxia kills or drives out most animal life |
| Harmful algal bloom | Bloom that produces toxins or causes severe hypoxia |
| Internal loading | Release of stored nutrients from sediments |
| Microplastic | Plastic fragment smaller than 5 mm |
| Methylmercury | Toxic, fat-soluble form of mercury that biomagnifies |
| Thermal pollution | Raising water temperature with waste heat |

## Exam traps

- BOD is the fall in oxygen, not the day-5 reading.
- Concentration is not load: multiply by discharge.
- Freshwater blooms are usually phosphorus-limited.
- Recovery after cutting inputs is slow because of internal loading.
- Thermal pollution has two effects: less oxygen dissolved and more oxygen needed.
- Minamata answers need methylmercury and biomagnification, not just "mercury in the sea".
- Point sources are easier to control than non-point sources; say why (one location, permits).

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between biodegradable and persistent pollutants, with an example of each.
2. Why must a BOD sample be kept in the dark?
3. Dissolved oxygen falls from 9.6 to 4.1 mg L⁻¹ over five days. Calculate BOD₅.
4. Why are very polluted samples diluted before measuring BOD?
5. A stream carries phosphate at 0.5 mg L⁻¹ with a discharge of 4 m³ s⁻¹. Calculate the load in kg per day.
6. Which nutrient usually limits algal growth in lakes?
7. Define hypoxia.
8. Name one large dead zone and its main nutrient source.
9. Give two sources of microplastics.
10. Explain why mercury was most harmful to people in the Minamata case.
11. Give two reasons why thermal pollution reduces fish diversity.
12. Why did harmful blooms return to Lake Erie after point sources were controlled?

### Answers

1. Biodegradable pollutants are broken down by decomposers (sewage); persistent pollutants break down very slowly and accumulate (PCBs, mercury, plastics).
2. To stop photosynthesis adding oxygen, so the fall reflects only microbial respiration.
3. 9.6 − 4.1 = **5.5 mg L⁻¹**.
4. Otherwise all the oxygen is used before day 5 and BOD is underestimated.
5. 0.5 g m⁻³ × 4 m³ s⁻¹ = 2 g s⁻¹; × 86 400 = 172 800 g = **172.8 kg per day**.
6. Phosphorus.
7. Dissolved oxygen low enough (below about 2 mg L⁻¹) to stress or kill most aquatic animals.
8. Northern Gulf of Mexico: nutrients from farmland in the Mississippi River basin. (Or the Baltic Sea.)
9. Any two: breakdown of larger plastic items; synthetic textile fibres; tyre wear; cosmetics or industrial pellets.
10. It was converted to methylmercury, which accumulated in fish and shellfish and biomagnified, so people eating seafood received the highest doses.
11. Warm water holds less oxygen; higher temperatures raise organisms' oxygen demand; cold-water species are excluded.
12. Non-point runoff of dissolved phosphorus from farmland increased, and non-point sources are harder to control.
