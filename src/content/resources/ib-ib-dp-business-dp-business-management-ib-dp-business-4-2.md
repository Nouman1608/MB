## The role of marketing planning

A marketing plan turns the organisation's objectives into marketing decisions. A typical plan answers five questions:

1. **Where are we now?** Current sales, share, customers and rivals, often summarised in a SWOT or STEEPLE analysis (tools the guide suggests for this unit).
2. **Where do we want to be?** Marketing objectives, such as a target share or sales level by a date.
3. **Who are we serving?** The target market and the position sought.
4. **How will we get there?** The marketing mix, covered in section 4.5.
5. **How will we know?** A budget, a timetable and measures to check progress.

Its role, therefore, is to coordinate decisions, allocate a limited budget, set targets that results can be compared with, and keep marketing consistent with finance, operations and HR. A plan is only as good as its assumptions, so it must be reviewed when the market changes.

## Segmentation: choosing a basis

| Basis | Divides by | Useful when |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | Age, gender, income, family size, occupation | Needs differ clearly by life stage or income |
| Geographic | Country, region, urban or rural, climate | Tastes, laws or weather vary by place |
| Psychographic | Lifestyle, values, personality, interests | Buyers choose to express identity or beliefs |
| Behavioural | Usage rate, occasion, benefits sought, loyalty | Buying habits differ more than personal traits |

### Is a segment worth targeting?

Standard tests (textbook criteria, not wording from the guide):

* **Measurable:** can its size and spending be estimated?
* **Substantial:** is it large enough to be profitable?
* **Accessible:** can the business reach it through media and distribution?
* **Distinct:** do its members respond differently from other segments?
* **Growing or stable:** is demand rising, flat or falling?

### Worked example: Saltcote Swimwear

Saltcote Swimwear is an invented business, and all figures are invented. It estimates the yearly value of four segments in its home market (money in dollars).

| Segment | Buyers per year | Average spend | Segment value | Share of total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive pool swimmers | 30,000 | 80 | 2,400,000 | 12.0% |
| Fitness swimmers | 120,000 | 45 | 5,400,000 | 26.9% |
| Holiday buyers | 400,000 | 25 | 10,000,000 | 49.9% |
| Open-water swimmers | 15,000 | 150 | 2,250,000 | 11.2% |
| **Total** | | | **20,050,000** | |

Holiday buyers are the largest segment by value, but they spend little each, buy mainly on price and fashion, and are served by large retailers. Open-water swimmers are the smallest segment, but each spends 150, they need specialist features such as thermal panels and high-visibility colours, and Saltcote's research suggests the group is growing by about 20% a year. If that continues, next year there would be about 18,000 buyers worth about 2,700,000.

Applying the tests: the open-water segment is measurable, accessible through clubs and online communities, distinct in its needs, and growing. It is not substantial for a large retailer, but it may be substantial enough for a small specialist.

## Targeting: one segment or several?

| Choice | Meaning | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated (niche) targeting | Serve one segment with one tailored offer | Small firms with specialist skills |
| Differentiated targeting | Serve several segments, each with its own offer | Larger firms with resources for several mixes |
| Undifferentiated (mass) targeting | One offer for the whole market | Products with broadly similar needs, such as basic household goods |

Saltcote's resources make concentrated targeting on open-water swimmers the logical choice, with the option to add fitness swimmers later.

## Positioning and position maps

A position map plots brands on two axes that matter to buyers. Choosing the right axes is the first skill: they must be features customers actually use to choose, which research should confirm.

For open-water swimwear, invented scores out of 10 might be:

| Brand | Price | Cold-water protection |
|---|---|---|
| Saltcote | 7 | 9 |
| Rival A (sports chain own brand) | 3 | 4 |
| Rival B (wetsuit specialist) | 9 | 10 |
| Rival C (fashion brand) | 6 | 2 |

How to use the map:

* **Find gaps.** No brand offers strong protection at a mid-to-low price. A gap is only an opportunity if customers want that combination and it can be supplied profitably.
* **Find crowding.** If several brands cluster in one area, competition there will be strong.
* **Check fit.** Saltcote sits close to Rival B but at a lower price, so its message should be "specialist protection without wetsuit prices".
* **Plan repositioning.** If research shows buyers now value sustainability more than price, the axes and the map should change.

## Niche market versus mass market

The core guide sets out the differences. For evaluation, focus on the trade-off: a niche offers higher margins and loyal customers but depends on a small base and invites larger rivals if it grows; a mass market offers volume and economies of scale but low margins and fierce competition. Saltcote's open-water segment could attract larger brands once it becomes valuable, which is the main long-term risk of a successful niche.

## The importance of a USP

A strong USP passes four tests:

1. **Unique:** rivals do not offer it, or not in the same way.
2. **Valued:** target customers care about it enough to choose or pay more.
3. **Credible:** the business can prove it, for example with tests, awards or reviews.
4. **Defensible:** it is hard to copy, perhaps through a patent, special skills or a brand reputation.

"High quality" fails the first test, because every brand claims it. Saltcote's "thermal panels tested in water at 12°C" is unique among cheaper brands, valued by open-water swimmers, and credible if test results are published. It may not be defensible for long if rivals copy the panels.

## How organisations differentiate

Differentiation is the wider strategy; the USP is the sharpest single point within it. Routes include product features, design, service, brand image, distribution, and ethical or sustainable practices. Each has a cost, so differentiation only pays if the extra price or extra sales cover it. A useful exam habit is to link each route to the target segment: open-water swimmers may value testing and safety features more than fashion.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate Saltcote's decision to target open-water swimmers.*

> The open-water segment is the smallest by value at 2,250,000, but each buyer spends 150, nearly twice the next highest average spend, and it is growing by about 20% a year. **[uses data]** Its specialist needs suit a small firm that can design features such as thermal panels, and clubs make customers easy to reach. **[segment tests applied]** However, a growing segment will attract larger brands such as Rival B, and Saltcote's thermal panels could be copied. **[risk]** Depending on one segment also leaves Saltcote exposed if the trend for open-water swimming fades. **[second risk]** On balance, the decision is sound because Saltcote has a credible USP and a clear gap on the position map. It should protect its position by building a brand among swimming clubs before larger rivals enter; if growth stalled, adding fitness swimmers would reduce the risk. **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

* Describing segmentation without saying which segment is chosen and why.
* Choosing the largest segment by default without checking spending, competition and fit.
* Using position map axes that customers do not care about.
* Treating every gap on a map as a profitable opportunity.
* Naming a vague USP such as "good quality".

## Exam technique

* **Explain**: apply the concept to the case and give a consequence.
* **Analyse** a position map: identify gaps, crowding and the business's position, and say what they mean.
* **Evaluate** or **recommend**: weigh benefits and risks of a target market or differentiation strategy and judge, with a condition.
