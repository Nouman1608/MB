These notes condense the **health problems** strand of the health and well-being context (SL and HL, first assessment 2027). The guide says to understand one or more of obesity, drug misuse or abuse, or social media addiction, through three areas: prevalence, social learning and stress. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems/) for the detail and try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems-practice/).

## The three areas at a glance

| Area | Learning objective (summarised) | Key idea | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prevalence | Factors that may explain changes over time and differences between populations | Food environment, activity, marketing, income, culture, policy, measurement | Population surveys; natural experiments such as taxes |
| Social learning | Role of social learning in one or more health problems | Modelling, vicarious reinforcement, peers, media | Birch et al. (1980); Harris, Bargh and Brownell (2009) |
| Stress | Role of stress in one or more health problems | Cortisol route and coping-behaviour route | Epel et al. (2000); Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1984) |

## Key definitions

- **Prevalence:** the proportion of a population with a health problem at a time.
- **Obesity:** excess body fat that harms health; often defined as BMI of 30 or more.
- **BMI:** weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It does not distinguish fat from muscle.
- **Drug misuse or abuse:** use that harms the user or others.
- **Social media addiction:** a contested label for compulsive and harmful use.
- **Social learning:** learning by observing models (Bandura).
- **Vicarious reinforcement:** learning from seeing a model rewarded or punished.
- **Stress:** a response to demands that exceed perceived resources.
- **Cortisol:** a stress hormone released by the HPA axis.
- **Ecological fallacy:** assuming a population pattern applies to each individual.
- **Natural experiment:** a change in the real world (such as a new law) used as a test.

## Prevalence

- **Over time:** a rapid rise in obesity cannot be explained by genes alone, so look to environmental change: cheaper energy-dense food, less activity, marketing.
- **Between populations:** income, culture, policy, access to food and healthcare, and different measurement methods.
- **Evaluate:** mostly correlational; risk of ecological fallacy; surveys may miss the poorest or most stigmatised groups; BMI is a crude measure.
- **Switch the example:** for drug misuse use price, law, availability and norms; for social media addiction use smartphone spread and platform design.
- **Concepts:** change, measurement, causality, bias (sampling).

## Social learning

- **Four conditions:** attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
- **Eating behaviour:** children copy family and peers (Birch et al., 1980); advertising works as modelling (Harris, Bargh and Brownell, 2009).
- **Drug misuse:** friends and family who smoke or vape raise the chance of starting; seeing social rewards for use.
- **Social media:** peers' likes and influencers model rewards for posting and checking.
- **Prevention:** positive role models, peer-led programmes, family meals, showing non-use as normal.
- **Evaluate:** explains clustering in groups; underplays biology; selection effect (people pick similar friends); short-term experiments.
- **Concepts:** causality, change, responsibility.

## Stress

| Route | Explanation | Evidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct (physiological) | Chronic cortisol affects appetite, fat storage and immunity | Epel et al. (2000): higher cortisol reactivity with central fat; Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1984): weaker immunity during exams | Correlational; unclear direction |
| Indirect (behavioural) | Stress leads to eating more, sleeping less, smoking, drinking or escaping online | Observational and self-report studies | Self-report; people already unwell may report more stress |

- **Individual differences:** social support buffers the effect; not everyone under stress develops the problem.
- **Reverse causation:** a health problem can itself cause stress through stigma.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement, change (stress management).

## Evidence about change

Hunt et al. (2018) asked students to limit their social media use for three weeks and compared them with a control group; loneliness and depression fell in the limited-use group. Strength: an experiment with a control. Limitations: small sample, self-report, no long follow-up.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Causality:** multiple interacting factors; experiments (advertising, limiting use) against correlational surveys.
- **Measurement:** BMI, prevalence surveys, stress scales, addiction scales.
- **Bias:** sampling bias, social desirability in self-reports, stigma.
- **Change:** which prevention strategies work at population and individual level.
- **Perspective:** biological (cortisol), cognitive (beliefs) and sociocultural (norms, media).
- **Responsibility:** advertising to children, labelling, privacy in data about health.

## A model answer plan (Section B style)

Scenario: a school finds that students whose older siblings vape are more likely to vape.
1. Name the term: social learning, modelling and vicarious reinforcement.
2. Apply to details in the scenario.
3. Add a second explanation: peer norms or stress.
4. Evaluate: similar students may choose similar friends (selection), so influence is not proved.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a list of harms rather than explanations.
- Quoting a prevalence figure and not saying how it was measured.
- Describing Bandura's doll study without applying the theory to a health problem.
- Treating stress as the sole cause.
- Forgetting to name the health problem you are using.

## Self-test

1. Why can genes not explain a rapid rise in obesity in a population?
2. What is a natural experiment?
3. Name Bandura's four conditions for social learning.
4. Explain the ecological fallacy.
5. What are the two routes from stress to a health problem?
6. State one limitation of using BMI.
7. Why might a link between stress and obesity run in both directions?

**Answers:** 1. Gene pools change slowly, so a fast change points to environmental factors. 2. A real-world change, such as a new tax, used as if it were an experiment, without random allocation. 3. Attention, retention, reproduction, motivation. 4. Assuming that a pattern found in a population applies to every individual in it. 5. A direct physiological route (such as cortisol) and an indirect behavioural route (coping through eating, smoking, drinking or screen use). 6. It does not distinguish fat from muscle or show where fat is stored. 7. Stress may lead to behaviours that cause weight gain, and weight stigma may cause stress.
