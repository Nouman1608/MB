## Section A

**1.** Define "flashbulb memory". **[2]**

**2.** Name two of Brown and Kulik's canonical categories. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by "consistency" in flashbulb memory research. **[1]**

**4.** Identify the cognitive process affected in the weapon focus effect. **[1]**

**5.** Outline the role of the placebo group in Cahill et al. (1994). **[2]**

**6.** Explain why Brown and Kulik (1977) could not measure the accuracy of their participants' memories. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how Talarico and Rubin (2003) improved on the design of Neisser and Harsch (1992). **[4]**

**8.** Invented study: the day after a severe earthquake, a researcher asks 40 students to write down how they heard the news. Eighteen months later, she asks them again and asks them to rate their confidence from 1 to 7. She finds that the average confidence rating is 6.4, and that 15 of the 40 students give accounts that match their first account on all major details.
(a) Calculate the percentage of students whose accounts matched on all major details.
(b) Explain what the results suggest about the influence of emotion on memory.
(c) Suggest one change to the design that would allow a stronger conclusion. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how the weapon focus effect could reduce the reliability of eyewitness testimony. **[3]**

**10.** Outline how Sharot et al. (2007) could be used to support Brown and Kulik's flashbulb memory theory, and give one reason for caution. **[4]**

**11.** Explain one study investigating the influence of emotion on one cognitive process. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate one theory of how emotion may affect one cognitive process. **[22]**

## Answers

**1.** A vivid, detailed and long-lasting memory **[1]** of the circumstances in which a person learned of a surprising and important event **[1]**.

**2.** Any two, one mark each: place **[1]**; informant (who told them) **[1]**. Also creditable: ongoing activity; own feelings; others' feelings; aftermath.

**3.** Whether a later account of a memory matches an earlier account of the same memory **[1]**.

**4.** Attention, which in turn affects what is encoded into memory **[1]**.

**5.** It received an inactive substance instead of propranolol **[1]**, showing the normal memory advantage for the emotional story, against which the propranolol group's lack of advantage could be compared **[1]**.

**6.** They used retrospective self-report, collected long after the events **[1]**, with no record made at the time against which participants' accounts could be checked **[1]**.

**7.** Both used a test-retest design with an initial record taken soon after the event **[1]**, but Talarico and Rubin also recorded an everyday memory from the same period as a control **[1]**. This allowed them to compare the rate of forgetting for emotional and ordinary memories **[1]**, showing similar declines in consistency but higher vividness and confidence for 9/11 memories **[1]**.

**8.** (a) 15 / 40 = 0.375, so 37.5% **[1]**.
(b) Most students (62.5%) gave accounts that changed in at least one major detail **[1]**, yet average confidence was very high **[1]**, suggesting that emotion makes memories feel certain without making them accurate **[1]**.
(c) For example, also record and retest an everyday memory from the same day **[1]**, so that forgetting of the earthquake memory can be compared with ordinary forgetting rather than assumed to be unusual **[1]**.

**9.** An arousing weapon draws the witness's attention **[1]**, so less attention is given to the perpetrator's face and other details **[1]**, which are then poorly encoded, making later identification less reliable **[1]**.

**10.** Support: people close to the World Trade Center showed greater amygdala activation when recalling 9/11, which suggests a biological basis for a special, emotion-driven memory process **[2]**. Caution: the study measured brain activity and vividness during recall, not the accuracy of the memories, so it cannot show that flashbulb memories are more accurate **[2]**.

**11.** Up to nine marks for knowledge and understanding and use of research:
- names the process and the study, for example memory and Neisser and Harsch (1992) **[1]**;
- defines flashbulb memory and the claim being tested **[1]**;
- aim: to test whether flashbulb memories are accurate **[1]**;
- procedure: questionnaire the morning after the *Challenger* explosion, repeated about two and a half years later with confidence ratings **[2]**;
- findings: many later accounts differed from the originals while confidence remained high **[2]**;
- explanation: memories are reconstructed through rehearsal and retelling **[1]**;
- link back: emotion influenced confidence rather than accuracy **[1]**.

Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987) on attention, or Talarico and Rubin (2003) on memory, is equally creditable. Evaluation is not required in a short answer, so a paragraph of criticism earns no extra marks.

**12.** Marked with the Paper 1 essay criteria:
- **A Focus:** identifies flashbulb memory theory and the claim that emotional events are recorded more accurately by a special mechanism **[2]**.
- **B Knowledge and understanding:** surprise and consequentiality; "Now Print!"; canonical categories; the difference between vividness and accuracy **[6]**.
- **C Use of research:** Brown and Kulik (1977) and Sharot et al. (2007) for the theory; Neisser and Harsch (1992) and Talarico and Rubin (2003) against it **[6]**.
- **D Critical thinking:** retrospective versus test-retest designs; the value of an everyday-memory control; single-university samples; rehearsal and media coverage as an alternative explanation; a conclusion that emotion affects confidence more than accuracy **[6]**.
- **E Clarity and organisation:** a planned argument with a clear conclusion **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 6:** saying the study was "unreliable" without explaining the missing baseline.
- **Question 8(b):** concluding that the earthquake memories were forgotten "more" than normal, which the design cannot show.
- **Question 10:** treating amygdala activation as proof of accuracy.
- **Question 9:** describing the weapon focus study without explaining the step from attention to encoding.
- **Question 12:** listing four studies without weighing them against each other.
