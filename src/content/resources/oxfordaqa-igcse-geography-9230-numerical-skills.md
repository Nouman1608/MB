This guide covers section 3.3.1.3, Numerical skills, within Section A (Geographical skills) of Unit 3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The specification says that students should be able to:

- demonstrate an understanding of **number, area and scales**, and the quantitative relationships between units;
- **design fieldwork data collection sheets** and collect data with an understanding of **accuracy, sample size and procedures, control groups and reliability**;
- understand and correctly use **proportion and ratio, magnitude and frequency**;
- **draw informed conclusions** from numerical data.

Geographical skills can be tested in all three written exams, and Paper 3 (1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks) has the most skills content. For the wider theme, see the [geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/). For the fieldwork method in more depth, see the [fieldwork data collection study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-fieldwork-data-collection/). This page goes deeper on the numbers. All calculations have been checked.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scale | The relationship between a distance on a map and the real distance |
| Ratio | A comparison of two quantities, such as 3:1 |
| Proportion | A part compared with the whole, as a fraction or percentage |
| Magnitude | The size of something, often compared in powers of ten |
| Frequency | How many times something occurs |
| Accuracy | How close a measurement is to the true value |
| Precision | How finely a measurement is made, such as to the nearest millimetre |
| Reliability | Whether repeating the method gives similar results |
| Sample size | The number of items or people measured |
| Control | A variable kept the same so that comparisons are fair |

## Units and conversions

| Quantity | Conversions |
|---|---|
| Length | 1 km = 1 000 m; 1 m = 100 cm; 1 cm = 10 mm |
| Area | 1 km² = 100 hectares (ha); 1 ha = 10 000 m²; 1 km² = 1 000 000 m² |
| Time | 1 hour = 60 minutes = 3 600 seconds |
| Discharge | Cubic metres per second (cumecs, m³/s) |

Always convert to the same unit before calculating, and write the unit with your answer.

## Scale

A map scale of **1:50 000** means that 1 cm on the map represents 50 000 cm on the ground, which is 500 m, or 0.5 km. A scale of **1:25 000** means 1 cm represents 250 m.

**From map to ground:** multiply by the scale.

- A road measures 6.4 cm on a 1:25 000 map. Real distance = 6.4 × 250 m = 1 600 m = **1.6 km**.
- A string follows a winding river and measures 38 cm on a 1:50 000 map. Real length = 38 × 0.5 km = **19 km**.

**From ground to map:** divide by the scale.

- A distance of 2.4 km on the ground at 1:50 000 is 2.4 ÷ 0.5 = **4.8 cm** on the map.

**Area.** On a 1:50 000 map, one grid square (2 cm by 2 cm) is 1 km². To estimate the area of an irregular shape, count whole squares and add half for every part-square. For example, 5 whole squares and 6 part-squares gives 5 + (6 × 0.5) = **8 km²**.

**Area of a rectangle:** length × width. A site 2.4 km by 1.5 km has an area of 3.6 km², or 360 ha.

**Population density** = population ÷ area. If 14 400 people live in 3.6 km², the density is 14 400 ÷ 3.6 = **4 000 people per km²**.

## Ratio and proportion

**Ratio.** A city has 2.4 million urban dwellers and 0.8 million rural dwellers in its region. The ratio of urban to rural is 2.4 : 0.8, which simplifies to **3 : 1**. Divide both numbers by the smallest.

**Proportion.** The urban share is 2.4 ÷ (2.4 + 0.8) = 0.75, or **75%**. A ratio of 3:1 corresponds to a proportion of three quarters.

**Sharing in a ratio.** To share a sample of 50 households between three zones in the ratio 6:3:1, divide 50 by 10 (the total parts) to get 5, then multiply: 30, 15 and 5.

Be careful about the order. "The ratio of A to B" puts A first.

## Magnitude and frequency

**Magnitude** means size. In geography you often compare sizes using powers of ten. For earthquakes, each whole number on the moment magnitude scale means ground motion about **10 times** greater and energy released about **32 times** greater. So a magnitude 7 earthquake produces 100 times (10 × 10) the ground motion of a magnitude 5, and about 1 000 times the energy.

**Frequency** is how often something happens or how many items fall in a category. A **frequency table** or tally chart records this. A **rate** compares a count to a base, such as accidents per 1 000 people, which allows fair comparisons between places of different size.

## Designing a data collection sheet

A good sheet makes it quick to record data accurately and easy to analyse.

| Feature | Why |
|---|---|
| Clear title, date, time, weather and site name | Records conditions that could affect results |
| One row per site or observation | Easy to read and compare |
| Columns for each variable, with units | Avoids mixing units |
| Space for repeat readings | Allows a mean to be calculated |
| A column for the mean or total | Speeds up analysis |
| Space for notes and anomalies | Explains odd results |

**Example: river velocity sheet.** Columns: site number, distance from source (km), width (m), depth at set intervals (cm), time for a float to travel 10 m (s), repeated three times, mean time (s), velocity (m/s).

## Accuracy, precision and procedure

- **Accuracy:** use calibrated equipment and read it carefully, at eye level.
- **Precision:** record to a sensible number of decimal places, for example the nearest centimetre.
- **Procedure:** use the same method at each site, with the same equipment, and, where possible, the same person.
- **Safety and consistency:** record the weather and time, as they can change results.

## Sample size and sampling

A larger sample is usually more representative, but takes longer. A sample that is too small may give an unrepresentative result. Sampling methods include:

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Random | Every item has an equal chance of selection, using random numbers | Choosing 30 pebbles with a random number table |
| Systematic | Choosing every nth item | 1 200 households, sample of 40: choose every 1 200 ÷ 40 = 30th |
| Stratified | The sample reflects the proportions of sub-groups | 1 000 homes in zones of 600, 300 and 100, sample of 50: choose 30, 15 and 5 |

Stratified sampling calculation: 600 ÷ 1 000 × 50 = 30; 300 ÷ 1 000 × 50 = 15; 100 ÷ 1 000 × 50 = 5. The numbers add up to 50.

## Control groups and reliability

A **control** keeps other variables the same so that you test only one factor. For example, to test whether soil moisture differs with slope angle, measure on the same day and at the same depth with the same equipment. A **control group** is an area or sample where the factor of interest is absent, used for comparison.

**Reliability** is improved by repeating measurements and calculating a mean, collecting data at several sites, using a larger sample and identifying and checking anomalies. If repeating the work gives similar results, the data are reliable. Reliable data are not necessarily accurate: a faulty instrument could give the same wrong answer every time.

## Worked example: from raw data to conclusion

A student tests the hypothesis "river velocity increases downstream" at five sites. At site 1, a float travels 10 m in times of 52 s, 48 s and 50 s. The mean time is (52 + 48 + 50) ÷ 3 = 50 s. Velocity = 10 ÷ 50 = 0.20 m/s. The mean velocities at the five sites are 0.20, 0.31, 0.38, 0.52 and 0.61 m/s.

- **Description:** velocity rises at every site, from 0.20 to 0.61 m/s.
- **Calculation:** the increase is 0.61 - 0.20 = 0.41 m/s, which is 0.41 ÷ 0.20 × 100 = **205%**.
- **Conclusion:** the data support the hypothesis, because velocity rises steadily downstream and roughly triples.
- **Evaluation:** five sites and three readings each is a small sample; the results are for one day, so repeating the study would improve reliability.

At a site with velocity 0.67 m/s (10 m in a mean time of 15 s), width 4.0 m and mean depth 0.5 m, the cross-sectional area is 4.0 × 0.5 = 2.0 m², and discharge is area × velocity = 2.0 × 0.67 = **1.3 m³/s**.

## Drawing informed conclusions

1. **State the pattern** with figures.
2. **Link it to the hypothesis** (supports, partly supports or does not support).
3. **Explain** using geographical knowledge.
4. **Mention limitations** such as small sample, anomalies or one-day data.

Use cautious language, for example "the data suggest" rather than "this proves".

## Checking your answer

Before moving on, test each result for sense. A river velocity of 25 m/s is far too fast for a stream, which suggests a mistake with units. A river length of 190 km from a 38 cm string line on a 1:50 000 map is a slip of a factor of ten. A stratified sample that adds up to more or less than the planned total has been miscalculated. Showing your working lets an examiner give credit for correct steps even when the final answer is wrong, and the habit of checking catches many errors before the exam does.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing units** (metres with kilometres) in one calculation.
- **Using the map scale the wrong way round.**
- **Giving a ratio in the wrong order.**
- **Counting part-squares as whole squares.**
- **Stating conclusions without figures.**
- **Confusing accuracy, precision and reliability.**
- **Treating a large sample as proof of cause.**
