## Questions

### Section A: knowledge of the NEA

**1.** State the number of marks for the NEA and the percentage of the International A-level it represents. **[2]**

**2.** Name the four kinds of question that the specification says can start a language investigation. **[2]**

**3.** The specification lists six sections for the investigation. Name the two that come after the Conclusion. **[2]**

**4.** State what the specification says about the choice of topic. **[2]**

### Section B: planning

**5.** A student has drafted four research questions.

- (i) How do people feel about the language of fashion?
- (ii) What are the distinctive features of the captions that a local football club uses on its social media posts about match days?
- (iii) Has English changed?
- (iv) What do people say about fashion?

**(a)** Identify which draft is the most suitable and give two reasons. **[3]**

**(b)** Identify the question type of the question you chose in part (a). **[1]**

**(c)** Explain one weakness of each of two of the other drafts. **[2]**

**6.** A student plans an attitudes-based investigation into how students feel about mixing English with another language in conversation. Plan the Method section by listing four points that it should cover. **[4]**

**7.** A student's draft contains these sections: Aim 140 words; Method 420 words; Analysis 1,530 words; Conclusion 380 words; Appendix of transcripts 910 words.

**(a)** Calculate the word count of the report, excluding data. **[1]**

**(b)** Compare it with the figure of approximately 2,500 words and say how many words are available for the student to add. **[2]**

### Section C: analysis and judgement

**8.** A student surveyed 30 students at their school about mixing English with another language. The results are shown.

| How often the student mixes languages | Number of students | Number who view mixing positively |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | 18 | 15 |
| Weekly | 7 | 3 |
| Never | 5 | 1 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the 30 students who mix languages daily. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage of daily mixers who view mixing positively, and the percentage of students who mix less than daily who view it positively. **[3]**

**(c)** Write an analysis paragraph (about 100 words) that uses the figures and suggests a link between behaviour and attitude. Include one limitation. **[6]**

**9.** Read this extract from the Analysis section of a report.

> *Participants often switched language mid-sentence. For example, in the transcript, Speaker A says "I'll meet you after, ya?" with a final particle from another language. This shows that A is bilingual. Speaker B also switches. Switching is a common feature of language.*

**(a)** Using the AO2 descriptors in the specification, say whether this extract is nearer Level 2 or Level 4, and give two reasons. **[3]**

**(b)** Rewrite the extract so that it moves up one level. **[4]**

## Answers

Marking guidance is Marlbridge's own. Award marks for the points listed or for equally valid alternatives.

**1.** 50 marks [1]; 30% [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Genre-based, function or use-based, attitudes-based and user-based [2 for all four; 1 for two or three]. **[2]**

**3.** References [1]; Appendix: Data [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Students should be encouraged to pursue an area of individual interest [1]. Any topic is acceptable provided its central focus is on an aspect of language [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Draft (ii) [1]. It is focused on a specific type of language (captions by one club) [1] and the data can be collected from public posts and analysed for features [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Genre-based [1], because it asks for the distinctive features of a type of language use. **[1]**

**(c)** Any two: (i) is vague because it does not say who the people are or how their attitudes would be gathered [1]; (iii) is far too broad, with no data or time frame, and cannot be answered in about 2,500 words [1]; (iv) is about the topic of fashion and not about language [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any four from: the nature of the data (a questionnaire, plus recorded conversation if used) [1]; the sample and why it was chosen (for example 30 students at one school) [1]; how the data were collected, including consent and anonymity [1]; the questions asked and the reason for them [1]; how the responses will be counted and grouped, and any transcription conventions [1]; the frameworks to be used, such as attitudes to language and code switching [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** 140 + 420 + 1,530 + 380 = 2,470 words [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 2,500 - 2,470 = 30 words [1]; the figure is approximately 2,500 and the overview says up to 3,000, so the student could consider adding to the analysis if more detail is needed, after checking the limit with the teacher [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** 18 / 30 x 100 = 60 % [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Daily: 15 / 18 x 100 = 83.3 % [1]. Less than daily: 12 students (7 + 5), of whom 3 + 1 = 4 view mixing positively [1]; 4 / 12 x 100 = 33.3 % [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Award marks for: quoting accurate figures [1]; comparing the groups (83.3 % against 33.3 %) [1]; a suggested link, that students who mix daily are more positive [1]; a reason, for example that using a practice often makes it feel normal or part of identity [1]; a cautious word such as "suggests" rather than "proves" [1]; one limitation, such as 30 students at one school, or that the survey cannot show cause [1]. A model: "Of the 30 students, 60 % mix languages daily, and 83.3 % of these view mixing positively, compared with only 33.3 % of the 12 students who mix less often. This suggests a link between how often students mix languages and how favourably they view it. It may be that using both languages every day makes the practice feel natural and part of identity, so it is judged more positively. However, this is a small sample from one school and the data cannot show whether behaviour shapes attitude or the reverse." **[6]**

**9. (a)** Nearer Level 2 [1]. It identifies a feature (switching) with one example [1] but gives only a general, one-dimensional meaning ("A is bilingual") and a broad statement without evidence or context [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Award marks for: an exact quotation of the switch [1]; a language term, such as code switching or a discourse particle [1]; an explanation of the function in context, for example showing closeness with a friend or marking an informal tone [1]; a link to the aim or a wider point, such as how meaning is negotiated between participants, or a limit on the example [1]. A model: "In the transcript, Speaker A ends the utterance 'I'll meet you after, ya?' with a particle borrowed from another language. This is code switching at the end of a clause, where the particle works as a tag, inviting agreement. Because the speakers are friends in an informal setting, the choice appears to signal closeness. However, one example cannot show how typical this is, so the pattern across other speakers needs to be traced." **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Topics about a subject, not about language.
- Questions that are too broad or have no data to answer them.
- Methods that leave out why the data were chosen and how they were collected.
- Percentages calculated from the wrong group.
- Descriptions of patterns without explaining meaning or context.
- Conclusions that go beyond what a small sample can show.
