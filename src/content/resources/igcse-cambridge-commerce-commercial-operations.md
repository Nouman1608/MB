This guide covers **Topic 2 Commercial Operations**, the second of six
topics in Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715), a new syllabus for exams
from 2028. Where Topic 1 introduced the basic concepts of trade,
commerce and production, Topic 2 moves into the day-to-day mechanics
of running a commercial enterprise: who owns and operates businesses,
where they locate, how retailers and wholesalers behave, what
documents change hands, and how buyers and sellers communicate.

Candidates sometimes assume that small-scale retailers are simply
being squeezed out by supermarkets and online sellers; the syllabus
explicitly asks candidates to explain why independent shops and
market stalls continue to survive, introducing its examples with "e.g."
-- personal service, flexible opening hours and additional services
provided -- as an illustrative, not closed, list. Convenience and
local knowledge of customers are further legitimate examples beyond
the syllabus's own three, not a substitute for them, and examiners
reward answers that engage with this survival, not just the growth of
large-scale retailing.

## Where this fits in 0715

Topic 2 sits directly after the introductory Topic 1 (Commerce and
production) and before Topic 3 (Globalisation of trade). It is the
first topic to look inside a commercial enterprise itself, building
the vocabulary -- sole trader, partnership, franchise, multinational,
wholesaler, retailer -- that later topics on logistics, aids to trade
and sustainability all assume candidates already know.

Every enterprise, whichever sector owns it, has to make the same basic
decisions: what legal form to take, where to locate, whether to make
or buy, and how to reach customers. Topic 2 works through each of
these decisions in turn, then closes with the paperwork and
communication that keep transactions moving once a sale has been
agreed.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE COMMERCE (0715) — TOPIC 2 COMMERCIAL OPERATIONS**

**2.1 Commercial enterprises**
- The difference between private sector enterprises (sole trader, cooperative, partnership, private limited company, public limited company) and public sector enterprises (public corporations), and why each ownership type suits different situations
- Multinational companies: their features, why they exist, and the positive and negative impacts on a country of an MNC locating there
- Franchises: their features, plus the advantages and disadvantages for both franchisor and franchisee
- Physical location factors (labour, markets, transport, power, raw materials, competition, suppliers, communication, government influence) versus ecommerce location factors (wi-fi, warehousing, technology specialists, logistics)

**2.2 Outsourcing**
- The growth of outsourcing through contract manufacturing and contract logistics, and why enterprises choose to outsource rather than produce in-house

**2.3 Retailing**
- Types of small-scale and large-scale retailers -- itinerant retailers, vending machines, independent retailers, street markets, speciality shops, online retailers, multiple chain stores, supermarkets, hypermarkets and department stores -- and why small-scale retailers often survive alongside large ones
- Retail selling techniques: branding, own brands, logos, packaging, self-service, after-sales service, barcoding, direct mail, loyalty programmes, informal credit and cash on delivery
- Digital retail trends: omnichannel retailing, cashless stores, EPoS, click and collect, self-service checkouts, subscription commerce, QR codes, VR showrooms, dropshipping, mobile apps and multi-channel retailing
- The positive and negative effects of ecommerce on traditional commerce

**2.4 Wholesaling**
- The functions and services of the wholesaler, why the wholesaler matters to both manufacturer and retailer, and why independent wholesalers survive through cash-and-carry and voluntary chains

**2.5 Trading documents**
- Home-trade documents: enquiry, quotation, order, invoice, delivery note, credit note, statement of account and receipt
- International-trade documents: certificate of origin, bill of lading, air waybill and documentary credit (letter of credit)
- Digital use of trading documents, including the stages of an online order and technologies such as digital signatures and electronic receipts

**2.6 Communication**
- Types of communication (written, oral, electronic, visual) and the methods used to deliver them (letter, telephone, face to face, videoconferencing, email, web chat/chatbots, social media, posters)
- The factors that affect choice of communication method: urgency, cost, security, distance, availability of networks and importance of the message

## How to approach it

Topic 2 rewards candidates who can classify a given business scenario correctly rather than simply reciting definitions from memory.
Paper 1's multiple-choice questions often test whether a business
scenario fits sole trader versus partnership versus limited company,
or physical versus ecommerce location factors -- practise sorting
short scenarios into the right category before trying to recall every
definition. Paper 2's structured questions frequently ask candidates
to justify a choice (which type of retailer, which document, which
communication method) against a given business situation, which means
AO2 (application) marks depend on applying the reasons above to the specific
context in the question rather than listing them in the abstract, while
the reasoned judgement or recommendation that follows is where AO3
(analysis and evaluation) is rewarded.
Build a simple table of the eight document types against what triggers
each one in a transaction (enquiry before an order, invoice after
delivery, credit note when goods are returned) -- this sequencing is a
common source of confidence in Paper 2 answers.

A worked example is a useful way to fix the ownership types in
memory: a small tailoring business run by one person who keeps all
the profit but is personally liable for any debts is a sole trader; if
two tailors join together and share both profit and liability, that
is a partnership; if the business incorporates, sells shares privately
to raise capital and its owners' liability is limited to what they
invested, it becomes a private limited company. Walking through this
progression -- from sole trader to partnership to private limited
company -- makes the "advantages and disadvantages" comparisons in
2.1 far easier to recall under exam pressure than memorising each
form in isolation.

## How do private and public sector enterprises differ? (2.1.1–2.1.2)

A **private sector** enterprise is owned by individuals or groups of investors and usually aims to make a profit. A **public sector** enterprise is owned and controlled by the government on behalf of the people. Its main aim is usually to provide a service, not to maximise profit.

| Form | Who owns it | Liability of owners | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited | Owner keeps all profit and makes every decision | Owner's personal possessions are at risk; finance is limited |
| Partnership | Two or more partners | Usually unlimited | More capital and shared skills | Disagreements; partners are liable for each other's business debts |
| Cooperative | Its members (customers, workers or producers) | Limited | Members share profits and each has one vote | Hard to raise large amounts of capital; decisions can be slow |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | Shareholders, invited by the owners | Limited | Owners risk only what they invested; family control kept | Shares cannot be sold to the public; accounts must be published |
| Public limited company (plc) | Shareholders, shares sold to the public on a stock exchange | Limited | Can raise very large sums of capital | Expensive to set up; founders can lose control to new shareholders |

**Unlimited liability** means the owner is personally responsible for all the debts of the business and could lose personal possessions such as a house or car. **Limited liability** means a shareholder can lose only the money they invested in shares.

**Public corporations** are businesses owned by the state, run by a board appointed by the government and financed partly by government funds. Governments set them up or keep them:

- to provide **essential services**, such as water, electricity or public transport, at prices people can afford;
- to keep **strategic industries**, such as defence or energy, under national control;
- to provide services in areas where a private firm would not make a profit, such as rural postal services;
- to protect jobs when a key industry is in difficulty;
- to avoid a private **monopoly** charging high prices.

**Which form is appropriate?** Match the form to the situation. A hairdresser starting alone suits a sole trader. Two accountants who want to share skills and costs suit a partnership. A family firm that wants limited liability but no outside owners suits a private limited company. A business that needs millions of dollars to build factories in several countries suits a public limited company.

## What are multinational companies and franchises? (2.1.3–2.1.4)

A **multinational company (MNC)** has its head office in one country and production or service operations in several others. MNCs exist because operating abroad lets them reach new markets, lower costs (cheaper labour, land or raw materials), avoid tariffs by producing inside a trading bloc and spread risk across countries.

| Positive impacts on the host country | Negative impacts on the host country |
|---|---|
| Creates jobs and trains local workers | Profits may be sent back to the home country |
| Brings investment and new technology | Local firms may be forced out of business |
| Raises tax revenue for the government | Workers may be paid low wages |
| Increases exports and improves the Balance of Trade | The MNC may cause pollution or use up resources |
| Wider choice of goods for consumers | The MNC may leave suddenly if costs rise elsewhere |

A **franchise** is an agreement in which the **franchisor** (the owner of an established brand) allows a **franchisee** to sell its products or use its name and business methods. The franchisee pays an initial fee and usually a **royalty** (a share of sales). For the franchisee, the advantages are a known brand, training, national advertising and a lower risk of failure; the disadvantages are the fees, strict rules on what can be sold and how, and a share of profit going to the franchisor. For the franchisor, franchising allows rapid expansion using the franchisees' capital, but it risks damage to the brand if a franchisee gives poor service.

## How do enterprises choose a location? (2.1.5)

**Physical location factors** decide where a shop, factory or office is built: labour (enough workers with the right skills), markets (nearness to customers), transport links, power supply, raw materials, competition, suppliers, communication networks and government influence (grants, tax breaks or planning rules).

**Ecommerce location factors** matter for a business that sells online: reliable **wi-fi** and internet connections, **warehousing** close to delivery routes, access to **technology specialists**, and good **logistics** for fast delivery and returns.

In an answer, choose the two or three factors that matter most for the business in the question. A bakery needs to be near its customers; a steel works needs raw materials and power; an online clothing retailer needs a warehouse near motorways and a parcel hub.

## What kinds of retailer are there? (2.3.1)

| Small-scale retailers | Large-scale retailers |
|---|---|
| Itinerant retailers (traders who move from place to place) | Multiple chain stores (ten or more branches with the same appearance) |
| Vending machines | Supermarkets (large self-service food stores) |
| Independent retailers (one owner, one or a few shops) | Hypermarkets (very large out-of-town stores with free parking) |
| Street markets | Department stores (many departments under one roof) |
| Speciality shops | Online retailers (can be any size, selling through websites and apps) |

Large-scale retailers benefit from **bulk buying**, lower prices, a wide range and the funds to advertise. **Small-scale retailers often survive** because they offer personal service, flexible opening hours and additional services such as delivery or informal credit, and because they are convenient for customers who live nearby.

## How do retailers persuade customers to buy? (2.3.2–2.3.4)

Retail **selling techniques** named in the syllabus are branding, own brands, logos, packaging, self-service, after-sales service, barcoding, direct mail, loyalty programmes, informal credit and cash on delivery. For example, a supermarket **own brand** is cheaper than a manufacturer's brand and earns the retailer a higher margin; a **loyalty programme** rewards repeat purchases and gives the retailer data about what customers buy.

**Digital retail trends** include omnichannel retailing (one connected experience across shop, website and app), cashless stores, Electronic Point of Sale (EPoS), click and collect, self-service checkouts, subscription commerce, QR codes, virtual reality showrooms, dropshipping (the seller passes the order to a supplier who ships direct to the customer), smartphone apps and multi-channel retailing (selling through several separate channels).

**Ecommerce affects traditional commerce** in both directions. Positive effects include 24/7 selling, more potential customers, higher revenue, targeted marketing and lower store operating costs. Negative effects include extra costs for delivery, staff training and larger warehouses, and the cost of maintaining a secure website.

## Which trading documents are used, and in what order? (2.5)

| Stage | Document | Sent by | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Enquiry | Buyer | Asks for details of goods, prices and terms |
| 2 | Quotation | Seller | States prices, discounts, delivery and payment terms |
| 3 | Order | Buyer | Formally requests the goods |
| 4 | Invoice | Seller | The bill: what is supplied, the amount owed and the payment terms |
| 5 | Delivery note | Seller | Goes with the goods; the buyer checks and signs it to confirm they arrived |
| 6 | Credit note | Seller | Reduces the amount owed if goods are returned, damaged or overcharged |
| 7 | Statement of account | Seller | Summarises all transactions for the period and the balance due |
| 8 | Receipt | Seller | Proof that payment has been made |

The order above follows the syllabus list; in practice the invoice may be sent just before, with or just after the goods.

For **international trade** four more documents are needed: a **certificate of origin** (states the country where the goods were made, used for customs duties and trade agreements), a **bill of lading** (a receipt and document of title for goods sent by sea), an **air waybill** (the equivalent for goods sent by air, but not a document of title), and a **documentary credit** or letter of credit (the importer's bank promises to pay the exporter once the correct documents are presented, which reduces the risk of non-payment).

**Online ordering** follows clear stages: the buyer browses the website, selects items and adds them to a basket, checks out and enters delivery details, pays securely, receives an email confirmation, can track the order, and finally receives the goods. Digital technology appears throughout: **digital signatures**, **secure access** (passwords and encryption), **cloud-based storage** of documents, **electronic receipts** and **etickets**. These save paper, time and storage space, but depend on reliable networks and good cyber security.

## How do businesses choose a method of communication? (2.6)

Communication can be **written** (letters, email), **oral** (telephone, face to face), **electronic** (email, videoconferencing, web chat, social media) or **visual** (posters, charts). Several methods fit more than one type.

| Factor | Best choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Urgency | Telephone or web chat | Immediate reply |
| Cost | Email or social media | Very cheap per message |
| Security | Face to face or encrypted email | Less risk of interception |
| Distance | Videoconferencing or email | No travel needed |
| Availability of networks | Letter or face to face | Works without an internet connection |
| Importance of the message | Letter or face to face | Gives a formal record or personal attention |

**Chatbots** answer routine customer questions on a website at any hour, but they cannot deal well with complex complaints.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a private and a public limited company?** Both have limited liability. A public limited company can sell shares to the general public through a stock exchange; a private limited company can sell shares only to people the owners invite.

**Why do governments run public corporations?** To provide essential services at affordable prices, to keep strategic industries under national control, to serve areas a private firm would not, and to prevent private monopolies.

**What is the difference between an invoice and a statement of account?** An invoice is the bill for one delivery. A statement of account summarises all invoices, credit notes and payments over a period and shows the balance still owed.

**Is an air waybill a document of title?** No. A bill of lading is a document of title, so whoever holds it can claim the goods; an air waybill is a receipt and a contract for carriage only.
