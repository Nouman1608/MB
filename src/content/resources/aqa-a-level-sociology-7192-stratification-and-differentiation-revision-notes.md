These notes condense the [Stratification and differentiation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-stratification-and-differentiation/). Read the guide first for full explanations and worked paragraphs.

They cover the Stratification and Differentiation topic of the AQA AS and A-level Sociology (7191, 7192) specification, Version 1.2 (14 October 2021), numbered 4.2.8 in the A-level subject content. It is assessed in Paper 2: Topics in Sociology (2 hours, 80 marks, 33.3% of A-level). Section B is "one from option 2: 4.2.5, 4.2.6, 4.2.7 or 4.2.8", and it is worth 40 marks of extended writing.

Use them with the [AQA A-level Sociology hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/sociology/) and [checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/sociology/). Test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-stratification-and-differentiation-practice/).

## The five content points

1. Stratification and differentiation by social class, gender, ethnicity and age.
2. Dimensions of inequality: class, status and power; differences in life-chances by social class, gender, ethnicity, age and disability.
3. The problems of defining and measuring social class; occupation, gender and social class.
4. Changes in structures of inequality, including globalisation and the transnational capitalist class, and the implications of these changes.
5. The nature, extent and significance of patterns of social mobility.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stratification | Ranking of groups in a lasting hierarchy of unequal rewards |
| Differentiation | Treating groups as different, for example by gender or age |
| Life chances | Weber's term: chances of gaining valued things such as health, education and income |
| Market situation | Weber: the skills, qualifications and property a person brings to the market |
| Status | Social honour or prestige given to a group |
| Party (power) | Organised attempts to gain power; the ability to get your way |
| Means of production | Marx: land, factories and capital used to produce goods |
| False consciousness | Marx: accepting ideas that hide exploitation |
| Polarisation | Marx's prediction that classes would split into two hostile camps |
| Contradictory class location | Wright: positions, such as managers, that share features of two classes |
| NS-SEC | National Statistics Socio-economic Classification: eight analytic classes based on employment relations |
| Conventional approach | Classifying a household by the male "head of household" |
| Individual approach | Classifying each adult by their own occupation |
| Embourgeoisement | Claim that affluent manual workers become middle class |
| Proletarianisation | Claim that routine white-collar workers are deskilled into the working class |
| Precariat | A group in insecure, short-term work (Standing; Savage) |
| Transnational capitalist class | Sklair: a capitalist class working across national borders |
| Absolute mobility | The total amount of movement between classes |
| Relative mobility | The chances of people from different origins reaching the same position |
| Elite self-recruitment | Top positions filled from the same privileged group |

## Theorists and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Davis and Moore (1945) | Functionalist theory | Higher rewards attract talent to important roles | Tumin (1953): importance is hard to measure and stratification blocks talent |
| Marx | Conflict theory | Two classes defined by the means of production; polarisation | The middle class grew instead |
| Weber | Class, status, party | Many classes based on market situation; status and power vary separately | Marxists say it hides the core owner/worker divide |
| Westergaard and Resler (1975) | Marxist | Britain remained a class society with a powerful upper class | Postmodernists say class identity has faded |
| Wright (1985) | Neo-Marxist | Contradictory class locations | Adds complexity Marx did not foresee |
| Walby (1990) | Feminist | Six structures of patriarchy; private to public patriarchy | Hakim says women's work patterns partly reflect choice |
| Hakim (2000) | Preference theory | Home-centred, adaptive and work-centred women | Choices are limited by childcare and employers |
| Rex and Tomlinson (1979) | Weberian, ethnicity | Minority ethnic underclass in Handsworth | Modood shows big differences between groups |
| Castles and Kosack (1973) | Marxist, ethnicity | Migrants as a reserve army of labour | Underplays racism as a separate factor |
| Cumming and Henry (1961) | Functionalist, age | Disengagement theory | Many older people stay active |
| Phillipson (1982) | Marxist, age | Capitalism devalues people outside paid work | Ignores wealthy older people |
| Oliver (1990) | Social model of disability | Society, not impairment, disables people | Shakespeare (2006): impairment still matters |
| Goldthorpe (1983) | Gender and class | Defends the conventional household approach | Acker (1973) and Stanworth (1984) call it sexist |
| Savage and colleagues (2013) | Great British Class Survey | Seven classes from elite to precariat using three capitals | Self-selecting web survey |
| Goldthorpe and Lockwood (1960s) | Affluent Worker | Little support for embourgeoisement in Luton | Small, specific sample |
| Braverman (1974) | Proletarianisation | Clerical work deskilled | Many white-collar workers have gained skills |
| Pakulski and Waters (1996) | Postmodern | The death of class; identity from consumption | Class still shapes life chances |
| Sklair (2001) | Globalisation | TCC with corporate, state, technical and consumerist fractions | Business elites still divided by nation |
| Glass (1954) | Mobility | Mostly short-range; elite self-recruitment | Men only |
| Goldthorpe (1980) | Oxford Mobility Study | More absolute upward mobility; relative chances still unequal | Men only |
| Marshall and colleagues (1988) | Essex study | Similar pattern, with women included | Still class-based measures |
| Saunders (1996) | Meritocracy | Ability and effort explain much mobility | Critics say ability tests reflect class |
| Blanden, Gregg and Machin (2005) | Income mobility | Fell between the 1958 and 1970 birth cohorts | Income is only one measure |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Class vs status.** Class is economic position; status is social honour. A lottery winner may gain class position without status.
- **Marx vs Weber.** Marx: two classes, ownership. Weber: many classes, market situation, plus status and party.
- **Medical vs social model of disability.** Impairment as the problem vs barriers in society as the problem.
- **Objective vs subjective class.** A scale's label vs how a person sees themselves.
- **Conventional vs individual approach.** Household head vs each adult's own job.
- **Absolute vs relative mobility.** More upward movement can happen while relative chances stay unequal.
- **Intergenerational vs intragenerational.** Compared with parents vs within one career.
- **Embourgeoisement vs proletarianisation.** Workers moving up vs white-collar workers moving down.

## Mini essay plan

*Question type: "Evaluate the view that social class is no longer the most important source of inequality in the UK."*

1. **Introduction.** Define class and stratification. Name the view (postmodern; some feminist and ethnicity theorists) and the challenge (Marxist and Weberian).
2. **For the view.** Pakulski and Waters: identity from consumption. Beck: individualisation. Walby: gender inequality is structural. Rex and Tomlinson, Modood: ethnicity shapes life chances. Disability and age create their own inequalities.
3. **Against the view.** Marmot's social gradient in health. Goldthorpe: relative mobility still unequal. Blanden and colleagues: income mobility fell. Savage: economic, cultural and social capital still cluster.
4. **Globalisation.** Sklair's TCC suggests class power has grown, not faded. Standing's precariat shows new class insecurity.
5. **Intersection.** Class, gender, ethnicity and age interact; Weber's three dimensions explain this.
6. **Conclusion.** Reach a judgement that follows from your evidence. One possible line: class remains important, but it works together with other sources of inequality.

## Quick self-test

1. Name Weber's three dimensions of inequality.
2. What did Tumin argue against Davis and Moore?
3. What is a contradictory class location, and who proposed it?
4. When did NS-SEC have its first major use?
5. Name the three types of capital in the Great British Class Survey.
6. Who argued for the conventional approach to women and class in 1983?
7. Name Sklair's four fractions of the transnational capitalist class.
8. Define relative mobility.
9. What did Blanden, Gregg and Machin find?
10. What is the social model of disability?

### Answers

1. Class, status and party (power).
2. That functional importance is hard to measure and stratification limits the discovery of talent.
3. A position, such as a manager, that shares features of two classes; Erik Olin Wright.
4. The 2001 Census.
5. Economic, cultural and social capital.
6. John Goldthorpe.
7. Corporate, state, technical and consumerist.
8. The chances of people from different class origins reaching the same position, compared with each other.
9. Income mobility fell between children born in 1958 and those born in 1970.
10. The view that people are disabled by barriers in society rather than by their impairment (Oliver).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing Marx and Weber side by side without saying where they disagree.
- Writing "Weber said there are three classes". His three dimensions are class, status and party.
- Using "life chances" loosely; it needs concrete examples such as health or education.
- Leaving out disability, even though the specification names it under life chances.
- Calling NS-SEC an income measure rather than a classification of occupations.
- Treating absolute mobility as proof of meritocracy.
- Mentioning globalisation without linking it to Sklair's transnational capitalist class.
- Listing studies with no evaluation, so analysis and evaluation marks are missed.
- Reaching a conclusion that does not follow from the evidence you gave.
