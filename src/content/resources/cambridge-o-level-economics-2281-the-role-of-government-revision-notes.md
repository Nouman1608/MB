Quick recall for section 4.1 **The role of government** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the role of government "locally, nationally and internationally". Full explanations are in the [role of government study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-government/); test yourself with the [role of government practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-government-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Local government | Councils or municipal authorities serving one town, city or district |
| National (central) government | The government of the whole country |
| International co-operation | Governments acting together through agreements or organisations |
| State-owned enterprise | A firm owned and run by the government |
| Regulation | Rules set and enforced by government on firms and consumers |
| Redistribution | Moving income from richer to poorer people through taxes and benefits |
| Public good | A good that non-payers cannot be excluded from, so markets do not provide it |
| Trade agreement | An agreement between countries on barriers to trade and trade rules |

## Why government is needed

Markets fail to provide public goods, under-provide merit goods, over-produce goods with external costs, allow monopoly power, leave unequal incomes and can be unstable. Markets also need a government to enforce property rights and contracts.

## Seven roles

1. **Provider**: public and merit goods (street lighting, schools).
2. **Producer**: state-owned enterprises.
3. **Employer**: teachers, nurses, police, officials.
4. **Buyer**: purchases from private firms (medicines, road building).
5. **Regulator**: food safety, pollution limits, competition law.
6. **Redistributor**: progressive taxes, pensions, benefits.
7. **Manager of the economy**: pursuing the macroeconomic aims.

## The three levels

| Level | Typical roles | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local | Waste collection, local roads, parks, markets, planning permission, licences | Local knowledge; quick decisions; accountable to residents | Small budgets; unequal resources between areas; spillovers beyond its borders |
| National | Law and defence; health and education systems; competition and safety law; taxes and benefits; managing the economy | Can set rules for the whole country; can tax and borrow on a large scale | Far from local needs; slower; cannot control other countries |
| International | Trade agreements; membership of international organisations; climate, pollution, fish stock and disease agreements; aid | Can deal with cross-border problems | Depends on countries agreeing and keeping to agreements |

## International organisations to know

- **World Trade Organization**: sets rules for international trade and settles trade disputes.
- **International Monetary Fund**: can lend to countries with balance of payments problems.
- **World Bank**: lends for development projects.

## Matching the level to the problem

- Problem confined to one area (blocked drains, a dangerous junction): **local**.
- Problem needing one rule everywhere, or national resources (food safety, recession, defence): **national**.
- Problem crossing borders (shared river, climate, fish stocks, trade barriers): **international**.

Many problems need more than one level working together, such as a polluted river that crosses a border.

## Worked reminder: one problem, three levels

A factory in invented Country S pollutes a river that flows on into Country T.

- **Local**: the town council inspects the site, can refuse permission to expand and warns residents about the water.
- **National**: Country S sets a legal pollution limit for all factories, fines firms that break it and funds water treatment.
- **International**: Countries S and T agree joint limits and share the cost of monitoring where the river crosses the border.

Each level does what the others cannot: the council acts fastest, the national government can make law, and only an agreement gives Country T a say.

## Chains to learn

- **Public good**: non-payers cannot be excluded → no firm can profit → government provides it, funded by taxes.
- **Monopoly**: one firm raises prices → national government uses competition law or regulates prices.
- **Recession**: output and jobs fall nationally → national government changes taxes and spending to support demand.

## Limits of government action

- Costs money raised through taxes.
- Officials may lack information or act slowly.
- State services may face little pressure to cut costs.

## Exam traps

- Answering only for the national level.
- Listing services without saying why government provides them.
- Confusing roles (4.1) with policy tools (4.3 to 4.5).
- Saying international organisations "control" governments.
- Claiming local government can solve cross-border problems.

## Quick self-test

1. Give two examples of services usually provided by local government.
2. State two ways local government is funded.
3. Why might a decision about a new local market be best made locally?
4. Give two roles of national government.
5. Why must food safety rules usually be set nationally?
6. What is a state-owned enterprise?
7. Explain the government's role as redistributor.
8. Why is a shared fish stock an international problem?
9. What does the World Trade Organization do?
10. Give one reason why government action may not always improve an outcome.
11. Name a problem that needs local, national and international action together.

### Answers

1. Any two: waste collection, local roads, parks, libraries, markets, planning permission.
2. Any two: local taxes such as property taxes; charges for services; grants from national government.
3. Local officials know local conditions and residents can hold them to account; decisions can be made quickly.
4. Any two: law and defence; health and education systems; regulation; taxes and benefits; managing the economy.
5. If rules differed between areas, firms could move to where rules are weakest; consumers everywhere need the same protection.
6. A firm owned and run by the government.
7. Using progressive taxes and benefits such as pensions to move income from richer to poorer people, reducing inequality.
8. Each country gains from catching more, but the stock is shared, so one country cutting its catch alone does not protect it; all must agree.
9. It sets rules for international trade and settles trade disputes between members.
10. It costs tax revenue; officials may lack information or act slowly; state services may lack pressure to cut costs (any one).
11. Pollution of a river that crosses a border (or the spread of disease).
