## 1.2.1 a: Factors of production

| Factor | Definition | Example in a small bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Land | All natural resources | The site, water, wheat in the ground |
| Labour | Human effort, mental and physical | Bakers, shop staff |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to make other goods | Ovens, mixers, delivery van |
| Enterprise | Organising the factors, deciding, bearing risk | The owner |

Capital means equipment, not money. Enterprise is the factor that brings the other three together.

## 1.2.1 b: Sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing natural resources | Farming, fishing, mining |
| Secondary | Making and building | Manufacturing, construction |
| Tertiary | Services | Retail, banking, education, transport |

Classify by the activity carried out, not by the firm's name.

## 1.2.1 c: Changing importance of sectors

| | Developing economy (typical) | Developed economy (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Large share of jobs, falling as the economy grows | Small share of both jobs and output |
| Secondary | Share rises during industrialisation | Share often falls over time |
| Tertiary | Grows as incomes rise | Largest share of jobs and output |

Reasons to learn:

- **Primary share falls in a developing economy:** farm machinery and better seeds need fewer workers, and as incomes rise people spend a smaller share on food and more on manufactured goods and services.
- **Secondary share falls in a developed economy:** capital replaces labour, so output can rise while jobs fall, and some goods are imported from lower-cost producers.
- **Tertiary share rises:** higher incomes increase demand for services such as healthcare, education and leisure.

Qualify with "typically". Resource-rich economies may keep a large primary output share.

### Method: reading sector data

1. Number employed = share x total employment for that year.
2. Percentage change in jobs = (new - old) / old x 100.
3. Change in a share = new share - old share, in percentage points.
4. Relative productivity (a handy comparison) = output share / employment share.

Reminder: Country D employs 10 million people, 30% in services in Year 1 and 45% in Year 2 when the total is 12 million. Services jobs: 3.0 million to 5.4 million, a rise of 80%, although the share rose by only 15 percentage points.

## 1.2.2 a: Productivity

Productivity = output / number of units of input (for labour: output per worker, or per hour).

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour productivity | total output / number of workers |
| Land productivity (yield) | output / hectares |
| Percentage change | (new - old) / old x 100 |

Reminder: a firm with 8 workers makes 960 units, so productivity is 120. With 10 workers it makes 1,150, so productivity is 115. Output rose 19.8%, yet productivity fell by 4.2%: (115 - 120) / 120 x 100 = -4.17%.

## 1.2.2 b: What affects productivity

| Factor | Ways to raise productivity | Cost or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Fertiliser, drainage, irrigation, reclamation | Expense; pollution from run-off; reclaimed land may be poor quality |
| Labour | Education and training (human capital); skilled migrants | Time and cost of training; trained staff may leave; migrants may start with low output |
| Capital | More machines per worker; new technology | Purchase price; operators need training; may reduce jobs |

Reminder on land: 50 hectares produce 200 tonnes, a yield of 4.0 tonnes per hectare. After drainage, 230 tonnes: 4.6 tonnes per hectare, a rise of 15%.

Key distinctions:

- **Quality vs quantity of labour:** training raises quality (productivity); hiring raises quantity (production).
- **Reclamation vs irrigation:** reclamation adds more land; irrigation raises output per hectare of existing land.
- **Quantity of capital vs technology:** more machines of the same type versus better machines.
- **Migration in and out:** skilled immigration raises the receiving country's average; emigration of skilled workers lowers the average in the country they leave.

## 1.2.2 c and d: Division of labour

**Definition:** splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker or group.

Method for explaining benefits (a chain): tasks split, so workers repeat one task, so skill builds and switching time falls, so output per worker rises, so unit cost falls.

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Learn a task quickly; skill grows; higher pay is possible if the firm does well | Boredom; narrow skills; harder to change job; low motivation |
| Businesses | Higher output per worker; lower unit cost; consistent quality; quick training | Low morale causes absence and turnover; production halts if a worker is missing; inflexible; errors pass along the line |

Reminder with numbers: a workshop of 3 workers (120 hours in total) makes whole shirts at 3 hours each, so 40 shirts. If the work is divided so that labour time per shirt falls to 2 hours, output is 60 shirts, up 50%. With a weekly wage bill of USD 360, labour cost per shirt falls from USD 9.00 to USD 6.00.

Evaluation points: standardised products suit division of labour; made-to-order products do not. The market must be big enough to sell extra output. Job rotation, team working and better pay can limit boredom.

## Sketch: shares of employment over time

```
Share of jobs
 |  P                              T
 |    P                         T
 |      P        S----S      T
 |         P  S              S
 |            T        (S may fall in a developed economy)
 +----------------------------------------> Time
 P = primary falls; S = secondary rises then often falls; T = tertiary rises
```

Describe it in words in the exam: the primary line slopes down, the tertiary line slopes up, and the secondary line rises first and may turn down later.

## Evaluation chains to learn

- **Training:** more training, so higher skill, so more output per hour; but it costs money and time, and trained workers may leave. Judgement: worthwhile when staff stay long enough to repay the cost.
- **Machines:** more or better capital, so more output per worker, so lower unit cost; but high purchase price, operators need training and some jobs may go. Judgement: depends on the volume sold.
- **Migration:** skilled arrivals raise average quality and fill shortages; unskilled or untrained arrivals can lower the average at first. Example: 80 workers at 40 units each plus 20 migrants at 30 units gives an average of 38. Total output still rises.
- **Falling share of a sector:** a fall in a sector's share of jobs does not prove its output has fallen. Output can rise while jobs fall if productivity rises faster.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four factors of production.
2. Classify as primary, secondary or tertiary: an oil well, a car assembly line, a hairdresser.
3. Define productivity.
4. Eight workers make 960 units a week. Calculate output per worker.
5. The firm hires 2 more workers and output rises to 1,150 units. Calculate the new output per worker and the percentage change in productivity.
6. In Country D, services employ 30% of 10 million workers in Year 1 and 45% of 12 million in Year 2. How many more people work in services, and by what percentage did services jobs rise?
7. State two ways of raising the productivity of labour.
8. Name the four ways of raising land productivity in the specification.
9. Define division of labour.
10. A machine lifts output per worker from 40 to 52 units. Calculate the percentage rise.

### Answers

1. Land, labour, capital, enterprise.
2. Primary, secondary, tertiary.
3. Output per unit of input (such as per worker) in a given time.
4. 960 / 8 = 120 units.
5. 1,150 / 10 = 115 units; (115 - 120) / 120 x 100 = -4.17%, a fall of about 4.2%.
6. 3.0 million to 5.4 million: 2.4 million more. 2.4 / 3.0 x 100 = 80%.
7. For example: education and training; skilled migration; better equipment.
8. Fertiliser, drainage, irrigation and reclamation.
9. Splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker or group.
10. (52 - 40) / 40 x 100 = 30%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that more output means higher productivity, without dividing by inputs.
- Calling money or savings "capital" instead of man-made productive goods.
- Giving percentage points when a percentage change was asked, or the reverse.
- Naming a factor without saying how it works, such as "irrigation" with no link to yield.
- Treating division of labour as all gain, or all loss, without a judgement.
- Describing a change in sector shares without using the figures given.
- Stating that the sector pattern is identical in every country.
- Placing a firm in the wrong sector, such as calling a factory tertiary.
