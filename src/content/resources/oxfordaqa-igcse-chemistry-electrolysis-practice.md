Written for OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202), this set follows the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. It covers section 3.3.2 Electrolysis (statements a to j) and the required practical on copper sulfate solution. The qualification is untiered, so every question suits every candidate. Use relative atomic masses Al = 27, O = 16 and C = 12 where needed.

## Questions

**1.** This question is about potassium bromide, an ionic compound.

**(a)** State what is meant by the term electrolyte. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why solid potassium bromide does not conduct electricity but molten potassium bromide does. **[2]**

**2.** Molten zinc iodide, ZnI₂, is electrolysed using carbon electrodes.

**(a)** Name the product at the cathode and the product at the anode. **[2]**
**(b)** Write a half equation for the reaction at each electrode. **[2]**
**(c)** State the electrode at which oxidation happens. Explain your answer in terms of electrons. **[2]**

**3.** Complete and balance each half equation.

**(a)** Cr³⁺ + ...... → Cr **[1]**
**(b)** 2Br⁻ – ...... → Br₂ **[1]**
**(c)** ......OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + ...... **[1]**

**4.** For each solution below, carbon electrodes are used. Predict what forms at each electrode.

**(a)** concentrated sodium iodide solution **[2]**
**(b)** silver nitrate solution **[2]**
**(c)** dilute lithium sulfate solution **[2]**

**5.** A student electrolyses copper sulfate solution using two carbon electrodes and a low-voltage direct-current supply. She weighs the cathode at intervals, rinsing and drying it each time. The current is kept constant.

| Time / min | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass gained by cathode / g | 0.00 | 0.20 | 0.41 | 0.60 | 0.70 | 1.00 |

**(a)** Describe what the student would see on the cathode. **[1]**
**(b)** Bubbles of gas form at the anode. Name the gas and describe a test to confirm it. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the anomalous result. Suggest one reason for it. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the mean rate of mass gain over the 25 minutes. Give the unit. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the blue colour of the solution fades during the experiment. **[1]**

**6.** A steel door hinge is to be copper plated.

**(a)** State which electrode the hinge must be. **[1]**
**(b)** Name a suitable material for the other electrode and a suitable electrolyte. **[2]**
**(c)** Write a half equation for the reaction at each electrode. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the concentration of copper ions in the electrolyte stays about the same during plating. **[2]**

**7.** State whether each change is oxidation or reduction.

**(a)** Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag **[1]**
**(b)** 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ **[1]**
**(c)** Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ **[1]**

**8.** Aluminium is manufactured by electrolysis of a molten mixture of aluminium oxide and cryolite.

**(a)** Give the reason why heating with carbon is not used to obtain aluminium from its oxide. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why cryolite is used. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the half equations for the changes at the negative and positive electrodes. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the positive electrodes have to be replaced regularly. Include a balanced equation. **[2]**
**(e)** The overall reaction is 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂. Calculate the maximum mass of aluminium that can be made from 20.4 tonnes of aluminium oxide. **[3]**
**(f)** Suggest why aluminium is more expensive to extract than iron. **[1]**

**9.** Sodium chloride solution is electrolysed using inert electrodes.

**(a)** Name the four ions present in the solution. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, using ideas about reactivity, why hydrogen forms at the cathode rather than sodium. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the solution left at the end is sodium hydroxide solution. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one use of sodium hydroxide and one use of chlorine in the chemical industry. **[2]**

**10.** A solution contains copper(II) ions, magnesium ions, nitrate ions and a low concentration of chloride ions. It is electrolysed with carbon electrodes.

**(a)** Predict the product at the cathode. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the product at the anode. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** State how the anode product would change if the chloride ion concentration were made much higher. **[1]**

**11.** In aluminium manufacture, assume that all the oxygen formed reacts with the carbon positive electrodes.

**(a)** Write the equation for the reaction of carbon with oxygen. **[1]**
**(b)** Using 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂, calculate the mass of carbon dioxide released for every 1.00 tonne of aluminium made. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A molten or dissolved ionic substance that is broken down by electrolysis [1].
**(b)** Solid: the ions are locked in the lattice and cannot travel [1]. Molten: the lattice has broken down, so ions can drift to the electrodes, carrying charge [1].
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say electrons move through the molten compound usually lose the mark; the explanation must be about ions.

**2. (a)** Cathode: zinc [1]. Anode: iodine [1].
**(b)** Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn [1]; 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ [1].
**(c)** The anode (positive electrode) [1], because iodide ions lose electrons there [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Oxidation at the anode" on its own is a statement, not an explanation; you need the words "lose electrons" to earn the second mark.

**3. (a)** Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Cr [1]
**(b)** 2Br⁻ – 2e⁻ → Br₂ [1]
**(c)** 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check the charge on each side before moving on; in part (c) both the 4 in front of OH⁻ and the 4e⁻ are needed for the equation to balance.

**4. (a)** Cathode: hydrogen [1]. Anode: iodine [1].
**(b)** Cathode: silver [1]. Anode: oxygen [1].
**(c)** Cathode: hydrogen [1]. Anode: oxygen [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming "sodium" or "lithium" at the cathode for a solution is a common error; the metal only forms if it is less reactive than hydrogen.

**5. (a)** A pink-brown (copper-coloured) coating forms [1].
**(b)** Oxygen [1]. It relights a glowing splint [1].
**(c)** The 20 minute reading (0.70 g) [1]. Suggestion: some copper flaked off during rinsing, or the cathode was not dry when weighed at another time point, or the current briefly dropped [1].
**(d)** 1.00 ÷ 25 [1] = **0.040 g/min** [1].
**(e)** Copper ions (which make the solution blue) are removed from the solution as copper is deposited on the cathode [1].
*Examiner insight:* For an anomalous result, quote the specific reading and compare it with the trend (about 0.04 g each minute predicts 0.80 g at 20 minutes); "the one that doesn't fit" is too vague.

**6. (a)** The cathode (negative electrode) [1].
**(b)** Anode: copper [1]. Electrolyte: copper sulfate solution, or any solution containing copper ions [1].
**(c)** Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ [1]. Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu [1].
**(d)** Copper ions are removed from solution at the cathode [1], and the same number are added at the copper anode as it dissolves [1].
*Examiner insight:* An electrolyte that contains no copper ions, such as sodium chloride solution, cannot plate copper, so read the electrolyte you name before you move on.

**7. (a)** Reduction [1]
**(b)** Oxidation [1]
**(c)** Oxidation [1]
*Examiner insight:* Look at which side the electrons are on: electrons on the left are being gained (reduction), electrons on the right are being lost (oxidation).

**8. (a)** Aluminium is more reactive than carbon [1].
**(b)** On its own, aluminium oxide would need a temperature above 2000 °C to melt [1]. Cryolite lowers the temperature needed, so less energy is used and costs are lower [1].
**(c)** Negative: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al [1]. Positive: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ [1].
**(d)** The carbon electrodes react with the oxygen formed and burn away [1]: C + O₂ → CO₂ [1].
**(e)** Mᵣ Al₂O₃ = 102, so amount = 20.4 ÷ 102 = 0.200 million mol (0.2 × 10⁶ mol) [1]. Moles of Al = 2 × 0.200 = 0.400 million mol [1]. Mass = 0.400 × 27 = **10.8 tonnes** [1].
**(f)** Large amounts of energy (electricity) are needed for melting and electrolysis [1].
*Examiner insight:* Saying cryolite is a catalyst, or that it "makes the reaction faster", does not answer part (b); the answer must link a lower melting temperature to saving energy.

**9. (a)** Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺ and OH⁻ [2] (all four needed for both marks; two or three correct earns one).
**(b)** Hydrogen is less reactive than sodium [1], so H⁺ ions accept electrons more easily than Na⁺ ions and hydrogen gas is released [1].
**(c)** Hydrogen ions and chloride ions are removed, leaving sodium ions and hydroxide ions in solution [1].
**(d)** Sodium hydroxide: making soap [1]. Chlorine: making bleach or plastics [1].
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), a bare "hydrogen is less reactive" without comparing it with sodium is incomplete; name both elements in the comparison.

**10. (a)** Copper [1]. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen (and magnesium is more reactive), so copper ions are discharged [1].
**(b)** Oxygen [1]. The chloride ions are at low concentration and nitrate is not discharged, so hydroxide ions lose electrons [1].
**(c)** Chlorine would form instead [1].
*Examiner insight:* At the anode this specification asks you to use the relative concentrations of the ions, so refer to the low chloride concentration rather than only listing ion types.

**11. (a)** C + O₂ → CO₂ [1]
**(b)** 3 mol O₂ gives 3 mol CO₂, so 4 mol Al goes with 3 mol CO₂; mass ratio 4 × 27 = 108 : 3 × 44 = 132 [1]. Mass of CO₂ = 1.00 × 132 ÷ 108 [1] = **1.22 tonnes** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the mole ratio taken from the equations; a correct final figure is far easier to credit when the 4 : 3 link between aluminium and carbon dioxide is written down.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using single atoms in half equations, such as I or O, instead of I₂ or O₂.
- Electrons on the wrong side of a half equation, which turns reduction into oxidation.
- Naming a reactive metal (sodium, lithium, potassium, magnesium) as a cathode product from a solution.
- Ignoring concentration at the anode, so a dilute chloride solution is wrongly said to give chlorine.
- In the practical, not giving the result of the gas test ("relights") or describing the copper only as "a solid".
- In electroplating, making the object the anode, or naming an electrolyte without ions of the plating metal.
- For aluminium, calling cryolite a catalyst, or saying the anodes "dissolve" rather than react with oxygen.
- Leaving out the unit on a rate, or losing track of tonnes and moles in reacting-mass work.
