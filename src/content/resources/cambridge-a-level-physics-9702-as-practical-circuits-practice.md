## Section A

**1.** Define the electromotive force (e.m.f.) of a source. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between e.m.f. and potential difference in terms of energy. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why a voltmeter of very high resistance connected directly across the terminals of a battery, with nothing else connected, reads almost exactly the e.m.f. of the battery. **[2]**

**4.** A cell of e.m.f. 4.5 V and internal resistance 0.90 Ω is accidentally short-circuited by a thick copper wire. Calculate the current and the rate at which thermal energy is produced inside the cell. **[2]**

**5.** A circuit diagram shows a cell, a lamp and an ammeter. The ammeter is connected in parallel with the lamp. State what is wrong with the diagram and what would happen if the circuit were built as drawn. **[2]**

**6.** Three cells, each of e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.40 Ω, are connected in series, but one of them is reversed. State the total e.m.f. and the total internal resistance. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A high-resistance voltmeter is connected across the terminals of a battery. With a 16 Ω resistor connected to the battery, the voltmeter reads 8.0 V. With a 4.0 Ω resistor instead, it reads 6.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the current in each case. **[2]**

**(b)** Determine the e.m.f. and the internal resistance of the battery. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the largest current the battery could deliver. **[1]**

**8.** A car battery has e.m.f. 12.0 V and internal resistance 0.025 Ω. When the engine is started, the starter motor draws a current of 160 A.

**(a)** Calculate the terminal p.d. of the battery while the starter motor is running. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the power wasted inside the battery. **[2]**

**(c)** The headlamps are on while the engine is started. Explain why they dim. **[2]**

**9.** An extra-high-tension supply has an e.m.f. of 3.0 kV and an internal resistance of 20 MΩ.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum current that the supply can deliver. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the supply is designed with such a large internal resistance. **[1]**

**(c)** The supply is connected to a 10 MΩ resistor. Calculate the terminal p.d. **[2]**

**10.** A battery of e.m.f. 6.0 V and internal resistance 2.0 Ω is connected to a 10 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total power supplied by the battery, the power delivered to the resistor and the power wasted in the battery. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the fraction of the energy supplied that is delivered to the resistor. **[1]**

**(d)** For each coulomb of charge that passes, state how much energy is converted to electrical energy, how much is transferred in the resistor and how much is transferred inside the battery. **[2]**

**11.** A battery with internal resistance is connected to a variable resistor. Explain how the terminal p.d. changes as the resistance of the variable resistor is reduced from a very large value to zero. **[4]**

**12.** Describe an experiment to determine the e.m.f. and internal resistance of a cell, using a variable resistor, an ammeter and a voltmeter. Include a description of the graph plotted and how the results are obtained from it. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** The energy transferred per unit charge [1] by the source in driving charge around a complete circuit [1].

**2.** E.m.f.: energy is transferred from other forms (such as chemical) to electrical energy, per unit charge [1]. P.d.: energy is transferred from electrical energy to other forms, per unit charge, as charge passes through a component [1].

**3.** The voltmeter draws almost no current [1], so the lost volts Ir are almost zero and the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir is almost equal to ε [1].

**4.** I = ε/r = 4.5 ÷ 0.90 = 5.0 A [1]; P = I²r = 5.0² × 0.90 = 22.5 W (23 W) [1].

**5.** An ammeter must be in series with the lamp [1]; drawn in parallel, its very low resistance would short-circuit the lamp, so the lamp would not light and a large current would flow through the ammeter [1].

**6.** e.m.f. = 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V [1]; internal resistance = 3 × 0.40 = 1.2 Ω [1].

**7. (a)** I₁ = 8.0 ÷ 16 = 0.50 A [1]; I₂ = 6.0 ÷ 4.0 = 1.5 A [1].

**(b)** ε = 8.0 + 0.50r and ε = 6.0 + 1.5r [1]; subtracting: 2.0 = 1.0r, so r = 2.0 Ω [1]; ε = 8.0 + 0.50 × 2.0 = 9.0 V [1].

**(c)** I_max = ε/r = 9.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4.5 A [1].

**8. (a)** V = ε − Ir = 12.0 − 160 × 0.025 [1] = 8.0 V [1].

**(b)** P = I²r = 160² × 0.025 [1] = 640 W [1].

**(c)** The headlamps are connected in parallel across the battery terminals, so their p.d. is the terminal p.d. [1]; the large current makes Ir large, so the terminal p.d. falls from about 12 V to 8.0 V and the lamps receive less power [1].

**9. (a)** I_max = 3000 ÷ 20 × 10⁶ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ A [1].

**(b)** To limit the current to a safe value if someone touches the terminals or they are short-circuited [1].

**(c)** I = 3000 ÷ (30 × 10⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ A [1]; V = IR = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 10 × 10⁶ = 1.0 × 10³ V [1].

**10. (a)** I = ε ÷ (R + r) = 6.0 ÷ 12 = 0.50 A [1].

**(b)** Total = εI = 6.0 × 0.50 = 3.0 W [1]; resistor: I²R = 0.50² × 10 = 2.5 W [1]; wasted: I²r = 0.50² × 2.0 = 0.50 W [1].

**(c)** 2.5 ÷ 3.0 = 0.83 [1].

**(d)** 6.0 J converted to electrical energy [1]; 5.0 J transferred in the resistor and 1.0 J inside the battery [1].

**11.** At very large resistance the current is almost zero, so the terminal p.d. is almost equal to the e.m.f. [1]. As the resistance is reduced, the current increases [1], so the lost volts Ir increase and the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir decreases [1]. At zero resistance the terminal p.d. is zero and the current is ε/r [1].

**12.** Any five [1 each]: connect the cell in series with the ammeter and the variable resistor, with the voltmeter across the cell's terminals; vary the resistance and record pairs of values of V and I; take at least five or six sets over a wide range of current; switch off between readings so the cell's internal resistance does not change as it warms or runs down; plot V on the vertical axis against I on the horizontal axis; draw the best-fit straight line; the e.m.f. is the intercept on the V axis; the internal resistance is minus the gradient.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining e.m.f. without "per unit charge" or without "complete circuit".
- Answering an energy distinction without saying which way the energy is converted.
- Using ε = IR with the external resistance only.
- Forgetting that a reversed cell still adds its internal resistance.
- Stating that the gradient of the V–I graph is r rather than −r.
- Explaining the headlamp effect without saying the lamps are in parallel across the terminals.
