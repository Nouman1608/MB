## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Ecosystem | Relatively self-contained, interacting community of organisms and the environment they live in and interact with |
| Niche | Role of a species in an ecosystem: where it lives, what it eats, what eats it, its interactions with biotic and abiotic factors |
| Biodiversity | Variety of ecosystems and habitats, species, and genetic variation within species |
| Species richness | Number of different species in an area |
| Relative abundance | How common each species is compared with the others (evenness) |
| Random sampling | Every part of the area has an equal chance of being sampled |
| Density | Number of individuals per unit area |
| Frequency | Percentage of quadrats containing the species |
| Percentage cover | Percentage of quadrat area covered by the species |
| Abiotic factor | Non-living factor, e.g. light, temperature, soil moisture, pH |
| Biotic factor | Living factor, e.g. predation, competition, grazing, trampling |

## Levels of biodiversity (18.2.2)

1. **Ecosystem / habitat**: number and range of ecosystems and habitats.
2. **Species**: number of species **and** their relative abundance.
3. **Genetic**: variation (alleles) within each species.

## Random sampling (18.2.3)

- **Why**: representative sample; avoids bias; allows statistical tests.
- **How**: tape-measure axes → random number coordinates → quadrat at each.
- **How many**: until the **running mean** levels off.
- **When not random**: across a gradient, use a **systematic** transect.

## Methods (18.2.4)

| Method | Use | Data |
|---|---|---|
| Frame quadrat | Plants, slow animals in uniform area | Density, frequency, % cover |
| Line transect | Change along a gradient, quick | Presence at intervals |
| Belt transect | Change along a gradient, detailed | Abundance in quadrats along the line |
| Mark-release-recapture | Mobile animals | Population estimate N |

**Lincoln index:** N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ m₂

*Worked reminder:* n₁ = 50, n₂ = 60, m₂ = 12 → N = 3000 ÷ 12 = **250**.

**Lincoln assumptions:** marks not lost and harmless; time to mix; closed population (no births, deaths or migration); equal chance of capture.

## Correlation (18.2.5)

- Coefficient from **−1** to **+1**; 0 = no correlation.
- Calculated value > critical value at p = 0.05 → **significant** correlation (reject null hypothesis).
- **Correlation ≠ causation.**

| | Pearson's (r) | Spearman's (rₛ) |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Continuous, normally distributed | Not normal, or ordinal / ranked |
| Scatter graph | Linear | Increasing or decreasing |
| Pairs | ≥ 5, ideally ≥ 10 | > 5, ideally 10–30 |

**Spearman's method in steps**

1. Rank each variable separately (tied values share the mean rank).
2. Find D = difference in ranks for each pair; square it.
3. Add the D² values.
4. rₛ = 1 − (6ΣD²) ÷ (n³ − n).
5. Compare with the critical value.

## Simpson's index (18.2.6)

**D = 1 − Σ(n ÷ N)²**

**Method in steps**

1. Find N (total individuals).
2. Divide each n by N.
3. Square each value.
4. Add the squares.
5. Subtract from 1.

*Worked reminder:* 20, 20, 10 (N = 50) → 0.16 + 0.16 + 0.04 = 0.36 → D = **0.64**.

| D | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Near 1 | High diversity: many species, evenly represented |
| Near 0 | Low diversity: few species, or one or two dominate |

## Exam traps

- Niche ≠ habitat.
- "Random sampling makes it accurate." Say **representative** and **unbiased**.
- Quadrat placed "randomly" by throwing it: this is biased; use coordinates.
- Lincoln: m₂ goes on the bottom; N must be a whole number of animals.
- Spearman: rank both variables in the same direction (smallest = 1 for each).
- Simpson: square **n ÷ N**, not n.
- "Significant" refers to the test, not to how big the effect is.

## Quick self-test

1. Define niche.
2. Name the three levels at which biodiversity can be assessed.
3. Give two reasons why random sampling is used.
4. How do you decide that enough quadrats have been sampled?
5. Which abundance measure suits a grass species, and why?
6. When should a belt transect be used rather than random quadrats?
7. n₁ = 40, n₂ = 45, m₂ = 9. Calculate N.
8. Give two assumptions of the Lincoln index.
9. State two conditions for using Pearson's linear correlation.
10. ΣD² = 18 for 10 pairs. Calculate rₛ.
11. Calculate D for a sample of 30, 10 and 10 individuals of three species.
12. Two sites have the same number of species, but D = 0.82 at site X and 0.41 at site Y. Explain the difference.

### Answers

1. The role of a species in its ecosystem: its habitat, food, predators and interactions with biotic and abiotic factors.
2. Ecosystems and habitats; species (richness and relative abundance); genetic variation within species.
3. Representative sample; avoids observer bias; allows statistical tests (any two).
4. A running mean levels off as more samples are added.
5. **Percentage cover**, because individual grass plants cannot be separated and counted.
6. When investigating how distribution changes along an environmental gradient.
7. (40 × 45) ÷ 9 = 1800 ÷ 9 = **200**.
8. Any two: marks not lost or harmful; marked animals mix fully; no births, deaths or migration; equal chance of capture.
9. Any two: continuous data; normally distributed population; linear scatter graph; at least five pairs.
10. 1 − (6 × 18) ÷ (1000 − 10) = 1 − 108 ÷ 990 = 1 − 0.109 = **0.89**.
11. N = 50: 0.36 + 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.44 → D = **0.56**.
12. Site X has individuals spread more evenly among species; site Y is dominated by one or a few species, so it is less diverse.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a method without saying how positions were chosen or how many samples were taken.
- Leaving out the critical-value comparison after calculating a coefficient.
- Writing "high D means more species" without mentioning evenness.
- Not linking abiotic readings to the same position as the abundance readings on a transect.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the 18.2 formulae given in the exam?**
Yes. The Lincoln index, Simpson's index of diversity, Pearson's linear correlation and Spearman's rank correlation formulae are provided, but you must know how to substitute into them and interpret the result.

**What is the difference between species richness and species diversity?**
Richness is only the number of species. Diversity, as measured by Simpson's index, also takes into account how evenly individuals are spread among those species.
