## The double entry rule

Every transaction has two effects. Double entry records both: one account is **debited** and another is **credited** by the same amount. Total debits therefore always equal total credits, which keeps the accounting equation (assets = capital + liabilities) in balance. The [fundamentals guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-the-fundamentals-of-accounting/) explains the equation.

| Type of account | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Assets (bank, shop fittings, customers' accounts) | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses (rent, purchases, sales returns) | Debit | Credit |
| Drawings | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities (suppliers' accounts, loans) | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Sales and other income, purchases returns | Credit | Debit |

For any transaction, ask three questions. Which two accounts change? What type is each? Does each increase or decrease? The answers give the debit and the credit.

## Posting a month of transactions

Vellacott Pottery Supplies, a new sole trader business, records these transactions in May. Posting means copying each half of the entry to its ledger account, with the date, the amount and the name of the **other** account in the details column. (In practice transactions are first listed in books of prime entry, covered in the overview guide.)

| Date | Transaction | Debit | Credit | $ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Owner pays money into the business bank account | Bank | Capital | 12,000 |
| 2 | Shop fittings bought, paid by bank transfer | Shop fittings | Bank | 2,400 |
| 4 | Goods bought on credit from Kirtle Clay | Purchases | Kirtle Clay | 3,100 |
| 7 | Goods sold on credit to Garnet Row Hotel | Garnet Row Hotel | Sales | 2,250 |
| 9 | Goods sold, money paid straight into the bank | Bank | Sales | 1,800 |
| 12 | Rent paid by bank transfer | Rent | Bank | 600 |
| 15 | Goods sent back to Kirtle Clay | Kirtle Clay | Purchases returns | 300 |
| 18 | Garnet Row Hotel sends goods back | Sales returns | Garnet Row Hotel | 250 |
| 21 | Kirtle Clay part-paid by bank transfer | Kirtle Clay | Bank | 2,000 |
| 25 | Garnet Row Hotel pays by bank transfer | Bank | Garnet Row Hotel | 1,500 |
| 28 | Owner takes money from the bank for personal use | Drawings | Bank | 400 |

Notice that a payment to a supplier is a debit in the supplier's account, because a liability is falling. A return by a customer is a credit in the customer's account, because an asset is falling.

## Balancing an account

The bank account has four payments and three receipts. To balance it:

1. Total each side (receipts $15,300, payments
$5,400).
2. The difference ($9,900) is the balance.
3. Write it on the smaller side as **Balance c/d** (carried down), so both sides total $15,300.
4. Show the same figure on the other side below the totals as **Balance b/d** (brought down), the opening figure for June.

```
Bank account (May)
Dr                              |Cr
 1 Capital             12,000   | 2 Shop fittings        2,400
 9 Sales                1,800   |12 Rent                   600
25 Garnet Row Hotel     1,500   |21 Kirtle Clay          2,000
                                |28 Drawings               400
                                |31 Balance c/d          9,900
                       15,300   |                       15,300
 1 Balance b/d          9,900
```

The cash book records bank and cash transactions first and also serves as the ledger account for them (section 2.3), so the bank account is drawn here in ledger form.

## Transfers at the year end

At the end of the financial year, income and expense accounts are closed by transferring their balances to the **statement of profit or loss**. Asset, liability and capital balances are not closed; they are carried down and reported in the **statement of financial position**.

```
Rent account                    Sales account
Dr          |Cr                 Dr          |Cr
Bank    600 |SoPL    600        SoPL  4,050 |Garnet Row Hotel  2,250
                                           |Bank              1,800
```

SoPL here stands for the statement of profit or loss account. Closing an expense account is a credit in that account, because the cost leaves it. Closing an income account is a debit, because the income leaves it. The matching entry goes on the opposite side of the statement of profit or loss account.

| Account | Balance | Goes to |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | $4,050 Cr | Statement of profit or loss |
| Sales returns | $250 Dr | Statement of profit or loss, deducted from sales |
| Purchases | $3,100 Dr | Statement of profit or loss |
| Purchases returns | $300 Cr | Statement of profit or loss, deducted from purchases |
| Rent | $600 Dr | Statement of profit or loss |
| Shop fittings | $2,400 Dr | Statement of financial position, non-current assets |
| Bank | $9,900 Dr | Statement of financial position, current assets |
| Garnet Row Hotel | $500 Dr | Statement of financial position, current assets |
| Kirtle Clay | $800 Cr | Statement of financial position, current liabilities |
| Capital | $12,000 Cr | Statement of financial position, capital |
| Drawings | $400 Dr | Deducted from capital, not from profit |

The debit balances and the credit balances both total $17,150 at 31 May, which is the check you meet in the [trial balance and verification guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-verification-of-accounting-records/). Preparing the full statements is covered in the [financial statements guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-preparation-of-financial-statements/).

## Running balance accounts

A three-column running balance account shows **Debit**, **Credit** and **Balance** columns. The entry goes in the same column as it would go on the same side of a two-sided account; the difference is that a new balance is worked out after every line, labelled Dr or Cr. There is no balancing step at the end, because the last balance is the closing balance.

```
Kirtle Clay account (purchases ledger), amounts in $
Date    Details            Debit    Credit    Balance
4 May   Purchases                     3,100    3,100 Cr
15 May  Purchases returns     300              2,800 Cr
21 May  Bank                2,000                800 Cr
```

Here the supplier's balance is a credit, because the business owes money. For Garnet Row Hotel, a customer, sales of $2,250 give
$2,250 Dr, the return of
$250 gives
$2,000 Dr, and the payment of
$1,500 leaves
$500 Dr.

## Reading an account

Interpreting means saying what a balance tells you about the business.

| Account | Debit balance | Credit balance |
|---|---|---|
| Customer (sales ledger) | The customer owes the business | The business owes the customer, for example after an overpayment |
| Supplier (purchases ledger) | The supplier owes the business, perhaps because of an overpayment | The business owes the supplier |
| Bank | Money held in the bank | Overdrawn: the business owes the bank |
| Expense | Cost for the period | Not normal; look for an error |
| Sales | Not normal; look for an error | Revenue for the period |

From Kirtle Clay's account you can also read the story: $3,100 bought,
$300 returned,
$2,000 paid, so
$800 is still owed at 31 May.

## Division of the ledger

The ledger is divided so that different types of account are kept together.

| Ledger | Holds | Examples above |
|---|---|---|
| Sales ledger | Accounts of customers who buy on credit | Garnet Row Hotel |
| Purchases ledger | Accounts of suppliers who sell on credit | Kirtle Clay |
| Nominal (general) ledger | Every account that is not a customer's or a supplier's: assets, expenses, income, capital and drawings | Shop fittings, Rent, Sales, Purchases, Capital, Drawings, the two returns accounts |

Splitting the ledger lets different staff look after customers and suppliers, and a customer's balance can be found without searching every account.

## Digital ledgers

Accounting software can keep the sales ledger, purchases ledger and nominal ledger digitally. You enter a transaction once, choose the accounts, and the software posts the debit and the credit, updates every balance and can produce reports. Benefits include speed, fewer arithmetic slips, easy searching and backup. Limitations include the cost of software and training, reliance on equipment and power, and security risks. Software cannot judge: if the wrong account is chosen, the wrong posting is made everywhere automatically, so you still need to understand debit and credit.

## Common errors

- Debiting a supplier's account to record a purchase, instead of crediting it.
- Writing the account's own name in the details column, instead of the other account.
- Leaving off the Dr or Cr label in a running balance account.
- Carrying a balance down without making both sides total the same.
- Treating every debit balance as money owed to the business, ignoring bank and supplier accounts.
- Posting returns into the sales or purchases account instead of the returns account.
- Transferring drawings to the statement of profit or loss.
