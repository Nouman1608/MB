## Core facts

- **Base** = metal oxide or metal hydroxide (3.5.1 a).
- **Alkali** = soluble hydroxide. Every alkali is a base; most bases are not alkalis.
- **Neutralisation** = acid + base → salt + water (3.5.1 b).
- **Salt name** = metal from the base + ending from the acid (3.5.1 c).
- **Ammonia** dissolves to give an alkaline solution and reacts with acids to give ammonium salts (3.5.1 d).
- **Limewater** + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water (3.5.1 e).
- **H⁺(aq)** makes a solution acidic; **OH⁻(aq)** makes it alkaline; pH 0 to 14, 7 neutral (3.5.1 f).
- **Ionic equation for neutralisation:** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) (3.5.1 g).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Base | A metal oxide or metal hydroxide that neutralises an acid |
| Alkali | A base that dissolves in water, giving OH⁻(aq) |
| Salt | Compound in which the hydrogen of an acid has been replaced by a metal ion or ammonium ion |
| Neutralisation | Reaction of an acid with a base or alkali to form a salt |
| Spectator ion | An ion present in the reaction mixture that is unchanged by the reaction |
| Ionic equation | Equation showing only the particles that actually change |
| pH | A scale from 0 to 14 measuring acidity or alkalinity |
| Universal indicator | A mixture of indicators whose colour gives an approximate pH |
| Limewater | Calcium hydroxide solution |

## Salt endings and formulae

| Acid | Ending | Negative ion |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrochloric, HCl | chloride | Cl⁻ |
| Nitric, HNO₃ | nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| Sulfuric, H₂SO₄ | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |

Charges to balance: Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺, NH₄⁺ (1+); Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ (2+); Al³⁺, Fe³⁺ (3+).

Quick formula examples:

- Zinc nitrate: Zn²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ → **Zn(NO₃)₂**
- Iron(III) sulfate: 2Fe³⁺ + 3SO₄²⁻ → **Fe₂(SO₄)₃**
- Ammonium sulfate: 2NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → **(NH₄)₂SO₄**
- Potassium chloride: **KCl**

## Balanced equations worth practising

- Fe(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + 2H₂O
- ZnO + 2HNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + H₂O
- Al₂O₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂O
- 2LiOH + H₂SO₄ → Li₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
- NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl
- Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

Balancing check: the number of H⁺ supplied by the acid equals the number of OH⁻ (or O²⁻ × 2) in the base, and each pair makes one H₂O.

## Writing the ionic equation: three steps

1. Full equation with state symbols.
2. Split aqueous ionic compounds into ions; keep H₂O(l) whole.
3. Cancel ions that are the same on both sides.

Example: LiOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → LiCl(aq) + H₂O(l). Spectators: Li⁺ and Cl⁻. Result: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

## Ammonia

- NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): reversible, so only some ammonia reacts.
- With acids: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, so no water forms.
- NH₄⁺ takes the place of a metal ion in the salt.

## pH in terms of ions

| Situation | Effect on pH |
|---|---|
| More H⁺(aq) | pH lower |
| More OH⁻(aq) | pH higher |
| Dilute an acid with water | pH rises towards 7, stays below 7 |
| Dilute an alkali with water | pH falls towards 7, stays above 7 |
| Add alkali to acid gradually | pH rises; 7 at neutralisation; above 7 with excess alkali |

Universal indicator colours (approximate): red (strongly acidic), orange/yellow (weakly acidic), green (neutral), blue (weakly alkaline), purple (strongly alkaline). Always compare with the chart.

## Exam traps

- Calling an insoluble base such as zinc oxide an alkali.
- Writing AlSO₄ or forgetting brackets in (NH₄)₂SO₄.
- Leaving K⁺ or NO₃⁻ in a final ionic equation.
- Splitting H₂O into H⁺ and OH⁻ in step 2.
- Writing water as a product of ammonia + acid.
- Saying dilution "neutralises" an acid.
- Giving universal indicator a precise value such as pH 4.6.
- Writing "chlorine" instead of "chloride" in a salt name.

## Quick self-test

1. Is magnesium oxide an alkali? Explain.
2. Name the salt formed from zinc oxide and nitric acid.
3. Give the formula of iron(III) sulfate.
4. Balance: Al(OH)₃ + H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + H₂O
5. Identify the spectator ions when sodium hydroxide solution neutralises sulfuric acid.
6. Write the ionic equation for neutralisation with state symbols.
7. Which ion makes ammonia solution alkaline, and where does it come from?
8. Give the formula of ammonium nitrate.
9. What is the solid formed when carbon dioxide reacts with limewater?
10. An acid of pH 2 is diluted with a large volume of water. Suggest a possible new pH and explain.
11. Universal indicator is blue. Which ion is in excess?
12. Why is pH from universal indicator only approximate?

### Answers

1. No. It is a base (a metal oxide) but it is insoluble in water, so it is not an alkali.
2. Zinc nitrate.
3. Fe₂(SO₄)₃.
4. 2Al(OH)₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6H₂O
5. Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻.
6. H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
7. OH⁻; ammonia reacts with water to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻.
8. NH₄NO₃.
9. Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃.
10. Any value above 2 but below 7, such as 4; the same H⁺ ions are spread through a larger volume, so their concentration falls.
11. Hydroxide ions, OH⁻.
12. The colour is matched by eye against a chart of colours, each covering a range of pH values.
