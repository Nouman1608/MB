This guide covers section **3.3.1 Production processes** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 3, Business operations. The specification asks you to be familiar with **job, batch, flow and cell production** and to understand when each is appropriate, and to consider how production can be made more efficient by **just in time (JIT) stock control** and **continuous improvement (Kaizen)**. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business Operations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-business-operations/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-production-processes-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-production-processes-practice/).

## What is production?

**Production** is the process of turning inputs (materials, labour, machinery, energy) into outputs (goods or services). The method a business chooses depends on what it makes, how many it makes, how much customers will pay and how much variety they want. The right choice keeps costs low, quality high and customers satisfied.

## Job production

In **job production** a single, one-off item is made to meet a customer's own requirements. Each job is different.

Examples: a tailor-made suit, a bridge, a wedding cake, a custom yacht.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Exactly what the customer wants, so the product is high quality | Slow, so each item is costly |
| A high price can be charged | Needs skilled, expensive workers |
| Workers may be motivated by the variety and skill needed | Little chance to use machines or lower costs by making more |
| Easy to change the design between jobs | Production is hard to plan, with uneven workloads |

Job production is appropriate when customers want a unique product and are willing to pay a high price.

## Batch production

In **batch production** a group (batch) of identical products is made together. The workers and machines then switch to the next batch, which may be a different product or a different version.

Examples: a bakery making 120 rolls, then 80 loaves, then 60 cakes; a clothing factory making 500 shirts in one size, then 500 in another; paint produced in a colour at a time.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Flexible, since the business can switch between products | Machines must be reset or cleaned between batches, wasting time |
| Lower cost per item than job production | Storage space is needed for part-finished goods between stages |
| Can respond to changing demand | Planning is more complicated than flow production |
| Can use some specialist machinery | Costs per item are higher than flow production |

Batch production is appropriate when the business makes a range of similar products in moderate quantities.

**Worked example 1: a batch bakery.** Each batch needs 30 minutes to clean and set up and 60 minutes to bake. The bakery works an 8-hour day (480 minutes). Each batch makes 120 rolls.

```
Time per batch = 30 + 60 = 90 minutes
Batches possible = 480 / 90 = 5.33, so 5 whole batches
Rolls per day   = 5 x 120 = 600
```

If the bakery cut the set-up time to 15 minutes, a batch would take 75 minutes, allowing 6 batches (6 x 75 = 450 minutes) and 720 rolls: a gain of 120 rolls without extra staff or machines.

## Flow production

In **flow production** products pass continuously along a production line, with each stage carried out by a worker or machine. The line runs 24 hours a day in many factories. The products are standard and made in very large quantities.

Examples: cars, soft drinks, breakfast cereal, bottled water, tinned food.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Very low cost per unit, since output is high | Very high set-up cost for machinery and the line |
| Fast, large-scale output | Inflexible, so it is difficult to change the product |
| Machines reduce the labour needed | Repetitive work can lower workers' motivation |
| Quality is consistent | A breakdown at one point can stop the whole line |

Flow production is appropriate when demand is high and steady and the product is standardised.

**Worked example 2: flow production costs.** Alder Cycles runs a flow line with 25 workers that makes 300 bikes per day, at a total daily cost of $18,000.

```
Productivity = 300 / 25 = 12 bikes per worker per day
Cost per bike = $18,000 / 300 = $60
```

If the line is stopped for two days by a broken machine, output falls by 600 bikes, but most of the costs, such as the workers' wages and the factory rent, still have to be paid.

## Cell production

In **cell production** the workforce is organised into small teams (cells), each of which is responsible for making a complete part of the product or a whole product. Each cell may have several multi-skilled workers who decide how to share the work.

Examples: a team that fits all the parts of a car door; a cell that assembles a complete computer.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Workers have more variety, so motivation is higher | Workers must be trained in several skills, which costs money |
| Teams take responsibility for quality | Cells may be harder to coordinate with one another |
| Faults are traced to the cell, so quality improves | Equipment must be duplicated across cells |
| More flexible than a single long production line | May not suit very high volume production |

Cell production is appropriate when a business wants to combine reasonable volumes with flexibility and higher employee involvement.

## Choosing a production method

| Method | Quantity | Variety | Cost per item | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job | One | Unique | Highest | Custom wedding dress |
| Batch | Small to medium groups | Several similar products | Medium | Bakery |
| Flow | Very large | Standard | Lowest | Soft drink bottling |
| Cell | Medium | Moderate | Medium | Team assembling a computer |

## Making production more efficient

**Efficiency** means making as much as possible from the resources available, with less waste. Two methods appear in the specification.

### Just in time (JIT)

In **JIT** stock control, materials and components arrive just as they are needed, so the business holds very little stock. This reduces the cost of storing materials and the risk of waste or damage. However, deliveries must be frequent and reliable, since late supplies can stop production. JIT is covered in more detail in section 3.3.2 on procurement.

**Worked example 3: JIT savings.** Alder Cycles holds an average of 3,000 parts in stock and it costs $2 per part per month to store them (storage, insurance and the money tied up). With JIT, average stock falls to 500 parts. More frequent deliveries cost an extra $1,500 per month.

```
Holding cost before JIT = 3,000 x $2 = $6,000
Holding cost with JIT   = 500 x $2   = $1,000
Saving on holding costs = $6,000 - $1,000 = $5,000
Net saving = $5,000 - $1,500 = $3,500 a month
```

### Continuous improvement (Kaizen)

**Kaizen** is a Japanese word meaning "change for the better". Under Kaizen, all employees look for small improvements in how work is done, every day. The improvements are small, but together they can reduce waste, speed up production and improve quality.

How it works: staff meet in small groups, suggest improvements and try them. Managers support the ideas and give recognition.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cuts waste and costs over time | Changes may be small and take a long time to show results |
| Improves quality and speed | Training and meeting time have costs |
| Staff are involved, so motivation can rise | Needs a change in culture, and some managers resist |
| Ideas come from people who do the job | Gains may be reduced if staff become tired of making suggestions |

**Worked example 4: a Kaizen suggestion.** A worker suggests moving the paint station so that frames travel a shorter distance. This saves 6 minutes per bike. With 300 bikes a day:

```
Time saved = 6 x 300 = 1,800 minutes = 30 hours a day
```

Thirty hours is the working time of nearly four workers on an 8-hour day, so the line could make more bikes or require less overtime.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing batch and flow production. Batch makes groups, then stops and changes; flow is continuous.
- Saying that job production is cheap. It is usually the most expensive per item.
- Describing JIT as having no stock at all. It means very low stock.
- Treating Kaizen as one big change. It is lots of small changes.
- Recommending flow production for a product that is changed regularly.
- Writing about the methods without referring to the product in the case.

## Exam technique for this section

For **identify** questions, name the method from the clues in the case ("made to order" points to job; "a line of machines running all day" points to flow). For **explain** questions, give an advantage or drawback with a reason. For **analyse** questions, follow the chain, for example from the quantity needed to the method chosen and then to the cost per item. For **evaluate** questions, weigh the factors that matter for the business, such as demand, variety, cost and quality, then decide on the most suitable method.
