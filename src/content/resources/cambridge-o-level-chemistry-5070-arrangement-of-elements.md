This guide covers subtopic **8.1 Arrangement of elements** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). It goes deeper than the topic overview in [Periodic Table: Groups and Trends](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/), concentrating on how to *use* the table: reading electronic configurations from position, predicting ion charges and formulae, and handling data about elements you have never studied.

## What the syllabus asks

The 5070 learning outcomes for 8.1 ask you to:

1. Describe the Periodic Table as an arrangement of elements in periods and groups, in order of increasing proton number (atomic number).
2. Describe the change from metallic to non-metallic character across a period.
3. Describe the relationship between group number and the charge of the ions formed by elements in that group.
4. Explain similarities in the chemical properties of elements in the same group in terms of their electronic configuration.
5. Explain how the position of an element can be used to predict its properties.
6. Identify trends in groups, given information about the elements.

5070 is not tiered, so every outcome applies to every candidate. Outcomes 5 and 6 are where most of the marks for this subtopic sit, because they let examiners ask about elements that are not named anywhere in the syllabus.

## Periods, groups and proton number

The elements are placed in order of **increasing proton number**. Rows are **periods**; columns are **groups**. The order is set by proton number, not by relative atomic mass. Argon (proton number 18) comes before potassium (proton number 19) even though argon's relative atomic mass (40) is larger than potassium's (39). If the elements were ordered by mass, potassium would sit among the noble gases, which its chemistry plainly contradicts.

Position and electronic configuration are two ways of saying the same thing:

- The **period number** equals the number of occupied electron shells.
- For Groups I to VII, the **group number** equals the number of electrons in the outer shell.
- Group VIII (the noble gases) has a full outer shell: eight electrons, except helium, which has two.

So phosphorus, in Period 3 and Group V, must have three shells with five electrons in the outer one: 2,8,5. Working the other way, an element with configuration 2,8,8,2 has four shells (Period 4) and two outer electrons (Group II): it is calcium.

## Metallic to non-metallic across a period

Period 3 shows the change most clearly, and it is the period examiners use most often.

| Element | Group | Character | Structure at r.t.p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | I | Metal | Giant metallic |
| Magnesium | II | Metal | Giant metallic |
| Aluminium | III | Metal | Giant metallic |
| Silicon | IV | Non-metal (with some semiconducting behaviour) | Giant covalent |
| Phosphorus | V | Non-metal | Simple molecular |
| Sulfur | VI | Non-metal | Simple molecular |
| Chlorine | VII | Non-metal | Simple molecular (Cl₂) |
| Argon | VIII | Non-metal | Single atoms |

Elements on the left have few outer electrons and lose them to form positive ions; elements towards the right gain electrons to form negative ions, or share them in covalent bonds. The oxides follow the same pattern: sodium oxide and magnesium oxide are basic, aluminium oxide is amphoteric, and the non-metal oxides such as sulfur dioxide are acidic (see [Acids, Bases and Salts](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/) for the oxide classification). An oxide type is therefore good evidence of where an element sits in its period.

## Group number and ion charge

Atoms gain or lose electrons until they reach the electronic configuration of a noble gas. The number needed depends only on the number of outer electrons, so it is fixed by the group.

| Group | Outer electrons | Usual change | Ion charge | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 1 | lose 1 | 1+ | Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ |
| II | 2 | lose 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ |
| III | 3 | lose 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| IV | 4 | usually shares | — | carbon and silicon form covalent bonds |
| V | 5 | gain 3 | 3− | N³⁻ |
| VI | 6 | gain 2 | 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻ |
| VII | 7 | gain 1 | 1− | F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻ |
| VIII | 8 (He 2) | none | — | no ions |

Transition elements sit outside this rule: they form ions of more than one charge, which is why their compound names carry Roman numerals (subtopic 8.4).

Ion charge lets you write formulae for compounds of elements you have not met. An element in Group II combines with an element in Group VII in the ratio 1 : 2 (charges 2+ and 1−), so strontium chloride is SrCl₂. A Group III element with a Group VI element gives a 2 : 3 ratio, as in Al₂O₃.

## Why a group shares chemical properties

Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons. Elements in the same group have the **same number of outer-shell electrons**, so they lose, gain or share the same number of electrons and form compounds with the same formula type. Lithium, sodium and potassium all react with water in the same way:

```
2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂
2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
2K  + 2H₂O → 2KOH  + H₂
```

The equations are identical apart from the symbol. Note what the explanation does *not* say: elements in a group do not have the same number of shells, the same number of electrons in total, or the same reactivity. They are similar, not identical, and the differences between them are the group trends.

## Predicting properties from position

Position gives two independent pieces of information. The **group** tells you the number of outer electrons, the ion charge, the formula of compounds and the general type of chemistry. The **period** combined with the across-period trend tells you how metallic the element is likely to be. Predicting is applying both.

### Worked example 1: an element named only by position

*Element X is in Group II and Period 5. Predict three of its properties.*

A strong answer reasons from position each time:

- X is a **metal**, because it is on the left of the table, in the same group as magnesium and calcium. It will conduct electricity.
- X has **two outer electrons** and forms **X²⁺ ions**, so its oxide is **XO** and its chloride **XCl₂**.
- Its oxide is **basic**, like calcium oxide, because it is a metal oxide; it will react with dilute acids to form a salt and water.
- X reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give a salt and hydrogen, as other Group II metals do.

Each point earns credit because it is linked to the position or to a named element in the same group. "X is reactive" with no reference point does not.

### Worked example 2: identifying a trend from data

*The table gives boiling points of Group VII elements. Describe the trend and predict the boiling point of astatine, the next element down.*

| Element | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Fluorine | −188 |
| Chlorine | −34 |
| Bromine | 59 |
| Iodine | 184 |

**Trend:** boiling point **increases** down the group. The rise is not even (154, 93, 125 °C between successive elements), so an exact value cannot be calculated; a sensible prediction is that astatine boils **above 184 °C**, consistent with being a solid at r.t.p. Examiners accept any value clearly above iodine's, with the trend stated. Do not invent a mathematical rule from a short, irregular data set; state the direction, then make a prediction consistent with it.

The same method works for melting point, density, atomic radius or reactivity of any group. Read the first and last values to fix the direction, check that the middle values fit, then predict.

## Common mistakes

- **Ordering by relative atomic mass.** The syllabus wording is "increasing proton number / atomic number".
- **Saying group members have "the same electrons".** Write "the same number of electrons in the outer shell".
- **Giving Group IV or Group VIII elements an ion charge.** Carbon and silicon usually bond covalently; noble gases form no ions.
- **Mixing up period and group** when reading a configuration. 2,8,3 is Period 3 (three shells) and Group III (three outer electrons): both happen to be 3, so test yourself on cases like 2,8,8,1 (Period 4, Group I).
- **Predicting without a reference element.** "Like calcium, because it is in the same group" turns a guess into a creditworthy prediction.
- **Treating hydrogen as a Group I metal.** Hydrogen is a non-metal gas; on most tables it is placed separately at the top.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the change across a period: state the starting and finishing character (metal to non-metal) and mention an intermediate element if asked for detail.
- **Explain** group similarity: always two parts, same number of outer electrons, and outer electrons are involved in reactions.
- **Predict**: give the property and the reason from position or from a named neighbour.
- **Identify** a trend from given data: state the direction in words ("increases down the group") rather than listing the numbers back.
- **Deduce** a formula: write the ion charges first, then balance them.
