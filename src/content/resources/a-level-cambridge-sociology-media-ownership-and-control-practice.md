These questions cover Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4, heading 9
*Ownership and control of media* (9.1, 9.2 and 9.3). Each Paper 4 essay is worth 35 marks and must
explore contrasting views; the warm-up questions test the key concepts first.

---

## Warm-up questions

**W1.** Define *cross-media ownership*. **[2]**

**W2.** Identify **three** news values. **[3]**

**W3.** Explain the difference between the instrumental Marxist and hegemonic (neo-Marxist) views
of the media. **[4]**

**W4.** What is meant by *agenda setting*? **[2]**

### Warm-up answers

- **W1.** When one company owns different types of media, such as newspapers, television channels
  and online platforms. 1 mark for "owning several media", 2 for specifying different types.
- **W2.** Any three from Galtung and Ruge (1965) and later work: negativity, unexpectedness,
  reference to elite persons or nations, proximity, personalisation, threshold (size), continuity.
- **W3.** Instrumental Marxists (Miliband, 1969; Herman and Chomsky, 1988) argue owners
  deliberately use the media to spread ruling-class ideology. Neo-Marxists (Glasgow University
  Media Group; Hall) argue ruling-class ideas dominate through hegemony — journalists' shared,
  common-sense assumptions — without direct orders. 2 marks each.
- **W4.** The media's power to influence which issues the public thinks are important, by choosing
  what to cover and how prominently (McCombs and Shaw, 1972).

## Essay 1 (9.1)

**"The traditional media has been undermined by the growth of the new media." Evaluate this
view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Declining newspaper sales and scheduled TV audiences; advertising revenue moving to online
  platforms.
- User-generated content and citizen journalism break the professional monopoly on news.
- Digitalisation and convergence (Jenkins, 2006) mean audiences access content on demand from many
  sources.

**Against:**
- Traditional organisations have moved online and remain among the most trusted and most shared
  sources.
- New media firms are themselves conglomerates; concentration has moved rather than disappeared
  (Bagdikian's concern about consolidation applies to the platforms).
- Television and radio remain important for older and poorer audiences, and in many countries.

**Evaluation:** distinguish between the *business model* of traditional media (clearly weakened)
and its *influence* (arguably still strong); judge across different societies.

## Essay 2 (9.2)

**"Pluralist theory provides the best explanation of media content." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**Pluralist case:** many outlets compete; owners must satisfy audiences; journalists value
professional independence; regulation and public-service broadcasting ensure balance; new media add
further diversity.

**Marxist critique:** Miliband (1969); Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model — ownership,
advertising, official sourcing, flak and ideology filter news.

**Neo-Marxist critique:** Glasgow University Media Group's *Bad News* (1976) — consistent framing of
industrial disputes from management's viewpoint, explained by hegemony.

**Postmodern view:** Baudrillard — hyperreality; fragmented audiences make "control" less
meaningful.

**Other influences:** news values, deadlines, sources, censorship — compatible with more than one
theory.

**Evaluation:** pluralism explains diversity of choice but not concentration of ownership; reach a
judgement on which theory explains most evidence.

## Essay 3 (9.2)

**"The media has a significant influence on the political process." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

- **For:** agenda setting (McCombs and Shaw's 1968 election study, published 1972); framing and
  bias in news reporting; publication of opinion polls creating bandwagon effects; targeted online
  political advertising and data use (Cambridge Analytica, 2018); censorship of opposition voices in
  some states.
- **Against:** audiences interpret messages actively (link to uses and gratifications in heading
  10); voters' class, family and community ties shape views; regulation of election coverage in many
  countries; media may follow rather than lead public opinion.
- **Evaluation:** influence may be stronger on what people think *about* than *what* they think;
  stronger where media are concentrated or state-controlled.

## Essay 4 (9.3)

**"The new media is a challenge to existing power structures." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**Digital optimist case:** citizen journalism and leaks expose abuses; social media helped organise
protest during the Arab Spring (2010–2011); Castells (1996) on network power; Jenkins (2006) on
participatory culture.

**Digital pessimist case:** Morozov's *The Net Delusion* (2011) — authoritarian states use the
internet for surveillance and propaganda; Zuboff (2019) — surveillance capitalism concentrates power
in technology corporations; Pariser (2011) — filter bubbles fragment publics; Keen (2007) —
amateur content undermines expertise.

**Identity and relationships:** new media allow marginalised groups to build communities (boyd,
2014), but may weaken intimacy (Turkle, 2011).

**Evaluation:** power may be shifting from old elites to new corporate ones rather than to
citizens; outcomes vary between democratic and authoritarian states.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the same theories in Essays 2 and 4?** Yes. Marxist and pluralist views both apply to
new media; show how each interprets platform ownership and user power.

**How do I get top-band marks?** Sustain evaluation throughout, use accurate named evidence,
apply examples from more than one society and reach a reasoned conclusion.

**Should I include the 1965 news values study in every answer?** Only where it helps — it is most
useful for questions about selection and presentation of news.
