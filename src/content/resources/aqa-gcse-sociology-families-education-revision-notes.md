## Family types

Nuclear, extended, reconstituted (step), lone-parent, same-sex, and **beanpole** — a family that is long and thin, with several living generations but few members in each, caused by longer life expectancy and lower birth rates.

**Reasons for family diversity:** rising divorce rates, secularisation, changing attitudes, women's employment, cohabitation, and later marriage.

**Reasons for the rise in divorce since the 1960s:** changes in the law (the Divorce Reform Act 1969 allowed "irretrievable breakdown", and later reforms made divorce cheaper and simpler, making it more accessible, not necessarily because marriages became less happy); secularisation and weaker stigma; **women's economic independence**, which reduces the financial trap of an unhappy marriage; and **higher expectations of marriage** itself, so couples are less tolerant of an "empty-shell" relationship.

**Consequences for family structure:** more **lone-parent families** (mostly headed by women, associated with higher poverty risk); more **reconstituted families**, creating step-relationships as people remarry or cohabit; and more **single-person households**.

## Perspectives on the family

| Perspective | View |
|---|---|
| **Functionalism** (Murdock, Parsons) | The family performs essential functions — sexual, reproductive, economic, educational; primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities |
| **Marxism** | Reproduces class inequality; inheritance of private property; a unit of consumption. **Zaretsky**: the "private" family is an illusion of separation from the economy; it cushions but props up capitalism through domestic labour and consumption |
| **Feminism** | Reproduces patriarchy; the **dual burden** and **triple shift** fall on women. **Delphy and Leonard**: the family is an economic system in which men, not capitalists as such, exploit women's work |
| **New Right** | The nuclear family is the ideal; lone parenthood is blamed for social problems |
| **Postmodernism** | Family structure is a matter of individual choice; diversity is the norm |

**Conjugal roles:** *segregated* (separate and traditional) versus *joint* (shared). The **symmetrical family** thesis (Willmott and Young) claims roles have converged, spreading via **stratified diffusion** — their principle that social changes filter downward from higher to lower social classes over time; feminists reply that evidence of the **dual burden** — paid work plus domestic work — shows symmetry is more apparent than real, and that women retain responsibility for **emotion work** as well.

## Education

**Perspectives:**

- **Functionalism** — secondary socialisation, role allocation by merit, teaching specialist skills.
- **Marxism** — the **hidden curriculum** and **correspondence principle** prepare pupils for their place in the workforce; the **myth of meritocracy** legitimises inequality.
- **Feminism** — subject choice and the hidden curriculum reproduce gender roles.

**The core disagreement is over meritocracy.** Functionalists say education sorts by ability and effort, making inequality fair; Marxists reply that the *appearance* of meritocracy is the point, since it convinces those who fail that the failure is their own.

**Class differences in achievement:**

- **Outside school:** material deprivation (housing, diet, equipment, study space), cultural deprivation (parental attitudes, language codes), **cultural capital** (middle-class knowledge and confidence that schools reward), and **Halsey**'s research on class-based inequality in achievement — the specification's named study for class as an external factor. **Ball** is also named here, on parental choice and competition between schools.
- **Inside school:** **labelling** against the "ideal pupil", the **self-fulfilling prophecy**, setting and streaming, and pro- and anti-school subcultures — **Willis**'s study of working-class boys forming a counter-school culture is the specification's named example. **Ball** appears again here too, on teacher expectations, making him the one researcher named on both sides of the internal/external divide.

**Cultural deprivation theory is criticised for blaming the victim** — it treats working-class culture as deficient rather than different, and overlooks material barriers. **Bernstein's elaborated versus restricted code** is the specific mechanism often examined: schools use an elaborated code (context-independent, grammatically complex) that middle-class pupils are already familiar with from home, putting working-class pupils using a restricted code at an immediate disadvantage in lessons and assessment.

**Gender:** girls now outperform boys, explained by feminism, changing aspirations, equal-opportunity policies, and a crisis of masculinity as traditional male manual work declines. Yet **subject choice remains gendered**, shaped by socialisation, peer pressure and career advice — the paradox that makes a good essay.

**Ethnicity:** language, family structure, ethnocentric curriculum, teacher labelling and institutional racism. Crucially, ethnic differences **overlap with class** — the two interact, and sociologists debate how much of the apparent ethnic effect is attributable to class rather than ethnicity itself.

## Research methods

**Primary vs secondary; quantitative vs qualitative.**

**Reliability** is consistency on repetition; **validity** is giving a true picture; **representativeness** is whether the sample reflects the population. Quantitative methods favour the first, qualitative the second.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Cheap, large samples, reliable | Low response rate, low validity |
| Interview | Detailed, flexible | Interviewer effect, time-consuming |
| Observation | Very valid | Hawthorne effect, unrepresentative |
| Official statistics | Cheap, large-scale | Socially constructed |

**Researching education has specific constraints** worth naming: schools are hierarchical, pupils are minors so parental consent is needed, gatekeepers control access, and classrooms are hard to observe without changing the behaviour observed.

## Exam traps

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Describing a perspective without applying it to the question.
- Using cultural deprivation uncritically.
- Treating ethnicity as independent of class.
- Claiming the symmetrical family thesis without the dual-burden reply.
- One-sided answers — evaluation needs both sides plus a judgement.

## Self-test

1. What is a beanpole family, and what caused it?
2. Distinguish the dual burden from the triple shift.
3. What is the correspondence principle?
4. Explain the self-fulfilling prophecy in education.
5. Why do girls outperform boys yet still choose different subjects?

**Answers:** 1. A family with several living generations but few members in each; caused by increased life expectancy and falling birth rates. 2. The dual burden is paid employment plus domestic labour; the triple shift adds emotion work — managing the family's feelings — on top of both. 3. Bowles and Gintis's claim that the structures and relationships of school mirror those of the workplace, preparing pupils for their future position in the labour force. 4. A teacher labels a pupil, treats them according to that label, the pupil internalises it, and their behaviour and achievement come to match it. 5. Achievement has risen through feminism, changing aspirations and equal-opportunity policies, but subject choice is still shaped by early gender socialisation, peer pressure and gendered career advice.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a beanpole family?**
A beanpole family is long and thin, with several living generations but few members in each, caused by longer life expectancy and lower birth rates.

**What is the difference between the dual burden and the triple shift?**
The dual burden is paid employment plus domestic labour. The triple shift adds emotion work, managing the feelings and relationships of family members.

**Why is cultural deprivation theory criticised?**
It is criticised for blaming the victim: it treats working-class culture as deficient rather than different and overlooks material barriers to achievement.

**Why do girls outperform boys but still choose different subjects?**
Girls' results are explained by feminism, changing aspirations, equal-opportunity policies and a crisis of masculinity, but subject choice remains gendered because of socialisation and other influences.
