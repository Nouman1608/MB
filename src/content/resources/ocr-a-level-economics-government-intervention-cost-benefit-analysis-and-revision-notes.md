## Why governments intervene

- To correct market failure: externalities, public goods, information failure, market power.
- To reduce inequity in income, wealth and access to resources.
- Every method has a cost, so always weigh benefit against cost and side effects.

## The methods at a glance

| Method | What it does | Diagram in words | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Raises cost per unit | Supply shifts up by the tax | Cuts output of a negative-externality good; raises revenue | Needs the size of the externality; regressive |
| Subsidy | Lowers cost per unit | Supply shifts down by the subsidy | Raises output of a positive-externality good | Budget cost; producers may keep much of it |
| State provision, government expenditure | Government pays and supplies | Supply becomes vertical at the quantity provided, price paid zero | Universal access; covers public goods | Opportunity cost; weak cost control |
| Buffer stock | Buys at floor, sells at ceiling | Horizontal lines at floor and ceiling prices | Steadier prices and incomes | Storage cost; stocks can run out |
| Maximum price | Price held below equilibrium | Line below equilibrium price | Affordability | Shortage, black market |
| Minimum price | Price held above equilibrium | Line above equilibrium price | Supports producers' income | Surplus |
| Public/private partnership | Private firm builds and runs; government pays | None needed | Private finance and expertise; risk transfer | Long-run cost; complex contracts |
| Legislation, regulation | Rules, standards, bans | Output limited at a set level | Certain; targeted | Enforcement cost; no incentive beyond rule |
| Tradable permits | Cap on pollution, permits traded | Fixed (vertical) supply of permits | Meets the cap at lowest cost | Cap and monitoring problems |
| Information provision | Labels, campaigns | Demand shifts (down for demerit, up for merit) | Cheap; keeps choice | Weak where behaviour is habitual |
| Competition policy | Controls collusion, abuse, mergers | Price falls, output rises with more competition | Lower prices, more choice | Slow, costly, evidence needed |

## Key calculations

**Tax incidence.** Consumers' burden = new price minus old price. Producers' burden = tax minus that rise. Revenue = tax x new quantity. Deadweight loss = 0.5 x tax x fall in quantity (for a tax on a good with no externality).

*Worked reminder.* Demand Qd = 180 - 3P, supply Qs = 2P - 20, so P = 40 cu and Q = 60. A tax of 10 cu gives Qs = 2(P - 10) - 20 = 2P - 40, so 180 - 3P = 2P - 40, P = 44 cu and Q = 48.
- Consumers pay 4 cu more (40% of the tax); producers bear 6 cu (60%).
- Revenue = 10 x 48 = 480 cu: 192 cu from consumers, 288 cu from producers.
- Deadweight loss = 0.5 x 10 x 12 = 60 cu.

**Subsidy.** Cost = subsidy per unit x new quantity. Demand Qd = 120 - 2P, supply Qs = 4P - 30: P = 25 cu, Q = 70. A subsidy of 3 cu gives Qs = 4(P + 3) - 30 = 4P - 18, so 120 - 2P = 4P - 18, P = 23 cu and Q = 74. Cost = 3 x 74 = 222 cu. Consumers pay 2 cu less (two thirds of the subsidy); producers receive 26 cu, up 1 cu.

**Maximum price.** Demand Qd = 300 - 2P, supply Qs = 3P - 100: P = 80 cu, Q = 140. A cap of 60 cu gives Qd = 180 and Qs = 80, a shortage of 100. Consumers would pay (300 - 80)/2 = 110 cu for the last unit supplied, which is the black-market incentive.

**Minimum price.** Demand Qd = 250 - 3P, supply Qs = 2P: P = 50 cu, Q = 100. A floor of 60 cu gives Qd = 70 and Qs = 120, a surplus of 50. If the government buys it, the cost is 50 x 60 = 3,000 cu.

**Buffer stock method.** (1) Find the free-market price. (2) If it breaches the band, set the price at the floor or ceiling. (3) Read off demand and supply at that price. (4) Surplus = purchase; shortage = release. (5) Cost or revenue = quantity x price.

**Tradable permits.** Two firms each emit 80 tonnes. The cap is 100, so each gets 50 permits. Abatement costs 6 cu per tonne for firm A and 10 cu for firm B, each able to abate all 80.
- No trading: each abates 30 tonnes, 30 x 6 + 30 x 10 = 480 cu.
- Trading: 60 tonnes must be abated, all by firm A at 6 cu: 360 cu. Saving 120 cu.
- Permit price lies between 6 and 10 cu. Below 6, A will not sell; above 10, B abates instead of buying.

## Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA)

**Meaning:** valuing all social costs and benefits of a project, private and external, in money, and comparing them.

| Item | Definition or formula |
|---|---|
| Present value | amount / (1 + r)^n |
| Net present value (NPV) | present value of benefits minus present value of costs |
| Decision rule | go ahead if NPV is above zero (and compare alternatives) |

*Worked reminder.* A training centre costs 300 million cu now. For four years its net benefit (benefits minus running and external costs) is 90 million cu a year.
- At 4%: 90/1.04 + 90/1.04^2 + 90/1.04^3 + 90/1.04^4 = 326.69 million. NPV = 326.69 - 300 = +26.69 million.
- At 10%: present value 285.29 million. NPV = -14.71 million.
- A single 500 million cu benefit received in year 3, discounted at 6%: 500/1.06^3 = 419.81 million.

**Uses:** schools and colleges, environmental schemes, hospitals, transport.

**Value in decisions.**
- For: includes external effects, common basis for comparison, makes trade-offs explicit.
- Against: money values for intangibles are contestable, forecasts uncertain, result sensitive to the discount rate, ignores distribution, can be used to justify decisions already made.

## Government failure

**Definition.** Intervention that fails to improve, or worsens, resource allocation, leaving welfare lower than it could be.

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| Imperfect information | Tax set above or below the true external cost |
| Distorted incentives | Short-term, vote-winning policy |
| Regulatory capture | Industry shapes its regulator |
| Unintended consequences | Rent cap shrinks housing supply |
| Cost of administration | Enforcing rules uses resources |
| Time lags | Policy takes effect after conditions change |

**Consequences.** Resources misallocated, taxpayers' money wasted, new inequity, loss of confidence in policy. A failed intervention can still beat doing nothing: always compare the outcome with the original market failure.

## Must-know distinctions

- Floor price (buffer stock buys) versus ceiling price (buffer stock sells).
- Maximum price (below equilibrium, shortage) versus minimum price (above equilibrium, surplus).
- Tax revenue versus deadweight loss: revenue is a transfer, the triangle is the welfare loss.
- Market failure (the market gets it wrong) versus government failure (the intervention gets it wrong).
- Regulation (fixed rule) versus permits (fixed total, price decided by trading).

## Evaluation checklist

1. Does the method suit this failure (externality, public good, information, market power, inequity)?
2. How elastic are demand and supply? Short run or long run?
3. Who gains and who pays?
4. What does it cost to run and enforce?
5. What are the side effects?
6. Is there a better alternative, and is the outcome still better than doing nothing?

## Quick self-test

1. Define government failure.
2. Demand Qd = 180 - 3P and supply Qs = 2P - 20. A tax of 10 cu is imposed. Find the new price and quantity.
3. Demand Qd = 300 - 2P and supply Qs = 3P - 100. A maximum price of 60 cu is set. Find the shortage.
4. Demand Qd = 250 - 3P and supply Qs = 2P. A minimum price of 60 cu is set. Find the surplus and the cost of buying it.
5. Two firms emit 80 tonnes each, the cap is 100, abatement costs 6 cu and 10 cu per tonne. How much does trading save?
6. What is the present value of 500 million cu received in three years at 6%?
7. A project has an NPV of -14.71 million cu at a 10% discount rate. What does that suggest?
8. Give two causes of government failure.
9. Why might a subsidy be an inefficient use of public money even if it raises output to the optimum?
10. State one limitation of Cost Benefit Analysis.
11. Why might a public/private partnership cost more in the long run than public borrowing?
12. Which method suits a public good, and why?

### Answers

1. Intervention that fails to improve, or makes worse, the allocation of resources.
2. New price 44 cu, quantity 48.
3. At 60 cu, Qd = 180 and Qs = 80, so the shortage is 100.
4. Qd = 70, Qs = 120, surplus 50; cost 50 x 60 = 3,000 cu.
5. No trading 480 cu, trading 360 cu, saving 120 cu.
6. 500/1.06^3 = 419.81 million cu.
7. Costs exceed benefits at that rate, so the project fails; check how the result changes at lower rates.
8. Any two of: imperfect information, distorted incentives, regulatory capture, unintended consequences, administrative costs, time lags.
9. The gain in welfare can be small next to the budget cost, because much of the subsidy pays for units that would have been bought anyway; the funding has an opportunity cost.
10. For example: intangibles are hard to value, or the result depends on the discount rate.
11. The private partner needs a profit and bears financing costs, and the contract may be hard to monitor, so payments over many years can exceed the cost of direct public borrowing.
12. State provision, financed by tax, because a public good will not be supplied by the market (non-excludable, so no one pays).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a method without saying which market failure it corrects.
- Shifting the wrong curve: taxes and subsidies move supply, information provision moves demand.
- Reporting the tax as the price rise instead of finding the new equilibrium.
- Forgetting to name the shortage or surplus as a quantity.
- Discounting benefits but not costs in a CBA, or ignoring external costs.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no judgement on which dominates.
- Writing "government failure" for any policy that has side effects, without comparing to the original failure.
- Leaving out units or the label for the diagram axes.
