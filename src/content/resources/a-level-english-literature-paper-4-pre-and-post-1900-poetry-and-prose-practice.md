Paper 4 of Cambridge International A Level Literature in English (9695) asks for two essays in two
hours: one on a pre-1900 set text (Section A) and one on a post-1900 set text (Section B), one poetry and
one prose, each out of 25 and each assessing AO1–AO5. The questions below follow the paper's (a)
essay / (b) passage pattern on texts set for examination in 2026. Section A passages are printed where
the text is out of copyright; for Section B, (b) questions name a passage for you to find in your own
copy, because the texts are in copyright. Rubric checks come first.

**Using these questions for 2027.** *Middlemarch*, Shakespeare's *Selected Sonnets* and Katherine
Mansfield's *Selected Stories* remain set for 2027. *Pride and Prejudice*, *The Wild Iris* and
*Beloved* are not on the 2027 list, so if you sit Paper 4 in 2027, adapt those questions to your own
set text (for example *Mansfield Park* in place of *Pride and Prejudice*).


## Section 0: Rubric checks

**1.** A candidate studied *Pride and Prejudice* (Section A) and *Beloved* (Section B). Explain why this
pairing is not allowed on Paper 4. **[2]**

**2.** State two differences between an (a) and a (b) question on Paper 4. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what AO5 requires and give one way of meeting it without quoting a named critic. **[3]**

## Section A: Pre-1900 Poetry and Prose

**4. Shakespeare, *Selected Sonnets***
**(b)** Discuss Shakespeare's presentation of ageing and love in the following sonnet, relating it to
your wider reading of the selection. **[25]**

> That time of year thou mayst in me behold
> When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang
> Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,
> Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.
> In me thou seest the twilight of such day
> As after sunset fadeth in the west,
> Which by and by black night doth take away,
> Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.
> In me thou seest the glowing of such fire
> That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,
> As the death-bed whereon it must expire,
> Consumed with that which it was nourished by.
> This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong,
> To love that well which thou must leave ere long.
> (Sonnet 73)

**5. George Eliot, *Middlemarch***
**(a)** "Middlemarch is a novel about failure." How far do you agree with this view of the novel? **[25]**

**6. Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice***
**(a)** Discuss the ways in which Austen presents marriage as an economic necessity for women in the
novel. **[25]**

## Section B: Post-1900 Poetry and Prose

**7. Katherine Mansfield, *Selected Stories***
**(a)** Discuss Mansfield's presentation of moments of sudden understanding in **two** stories from
your selection. **[25]**

**8. Louise Glück, *The Wild Iris***
**(a)** Discuss the ways Glück uses different speaking voices in the collection to explore the
relationship between the human and the divine. **[25]**

**9. Toni Morrison, *Beloved***
**(b)** Find the opening chapter of the novel, from its first sentence to the point where Paul D first
appears at 124. Discuss Morrison's narrative methods in this passage and their importance to the novel
as a whole. **[25]**

## Answers and indicative content

The indicative content below is a guide to the range of relevant points, not a checklist. Credit any
well-supported argument that addresses the question.

**1.** Both texts are prose [1]. Paper 4 requires one essay on a poetry set text and one on a prose
set text, from different sections [1].

**2.** Any two [1 each]: (b) prints a passage or poem from the set text on the paper, (a) does not; (b)
centres on close analysis of the printed material, (a) asks for a wider essay across the text; (b)
still expects links to the wider text.

**3.** AO5 is to "discuss and evaluate varying opinions and interpretations" [1]. It can be met by
setting out two readings of a moment, such as a sympathetic and a critical view of a character, or
two critical approaches [1], and judging which is more convincing with evidence [1].

### Question 4 (Sonnet 73)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Situates the sonnet among the poems to the young man; links to other sonnets on time and decay, such as Sonnets 60 or 65, if studied |
| AO2 | Three quatrains with shrinking time-scales (season, day, fire); the "bare ruined choirs" image, possibly evoking ruined monasteries; night as "Death's second self"; the paradox "Consumed with that which it was nourished by"; the couplet's shift from "me" to "thou" |
| AO3 | An argued view of what the couplet does: consolation, persuasion, or plea |
| AO4 | A structured argument that moves beyond line-by-line commentary and connects to the wider selection |
| AO5 | Evaluates readings: the couplet as reassurance that love grows stronger, or as a demand that the beloved love more because the speaker is dying; possible ambiguity of "leave" (the beloved must leave the speaker, or lose him) |

**Model paragraph.** "Each quatrain narrows the span of time: a season, then a single evening, then the
last minutes of a dying fire. The effect is a quickening, as if the speaker were watching his own end
draw closer while he speaks. Yet the couplet hands the decision to the listener: 'This thou perceiv'st'.
One reading finds this generous, a celebration of love sharpened by loss; another finds it quietly
coercive, since the beloved is told what to feel. The second reading seems stronger when the sonnet is
set beside others in the selection in which the speaker's confidence in his poem's power to outlast
time sits uneasily with his fear of being forgotten."

**What separates stronger answers.** Weaker answers paraphrase each image. Stronger answers explain how
the structure of three quatrains and a couplet creates the poem's argument, and they evaluate the
ambiguity of the ending instead of resolving it too quickly.

### Question 5 (*Middlemarch*)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Dorothea and Casaubon's marriage; Lydgate's compromised ambitions and marriage to Rosamond; Bulstrode's exposure; Fred Vincy and Mary Garth; the Prelude's St Theresa and the Finale |
| AO2 | Omniscient narration and its sympathetic judgements; multi-plot structure and parallels between Dorothea and Lydgate; imagery of webs and of the pier-glass; the Prelude and Finale as a frame |
| AO3 | A clear position on "how far": many lives fall short of their aims, but the novel redefines success |
| AO4 | Structured comparison of plot strands rather than plot summary |
| AO5 | Evaluates the view: failure as the novel's theme versus the Finale's claim that "the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts"; feminist readings of Dorothea's limited options |

### Question 6 (*Pride and Prejudice*)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | The entail on Longbourn; Charlotte Lucas's acceptance of Mr Collins; Mrs Bennet's urgency; Lydia and Wickham; Elizabeth's refusals |
| AO2 | The ironic opening sentence; free indirect style in Charlotte's reasoning; dialogue and narrative irony; parallel and contrasting marriages as structure |
| AO3 | A judgement on whether the novel endorses or criticises the economics of marriage |
| AO4 | Organised by idea, not by character |
| AO5 | Evaluates whether the ending (Elizabeth marries a rich man for love) resolves or evades the problem; Charlotte as pragmatic or as a warning |

### Question 7 (Mansfield, two stories)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Accurate detail from two stories, for example "The Garden Party" (Laura's visit to the dead carter's house) and "Miss Brill" (the overheard insult) |
| AO2 | Close third-person focalisation; free indirect thought; symbolic objects (the hat, the fur); open or abrupt endings; compression of the short-story form |
| AO3 | An argument about whether these moments change the characters or are quickly closed off |
| AO4 | Integrates the two stories within paragraphs where useful; comparison is not required but discussion of two stories is |
| AO5 | Evaluates readings of an ending: Laura's unfinished question as revelation or as evasion; Miss Brill's final sentence as tragic or as self-deception continuing |

### Question 8 (*The Wild Iris*)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | The three voices of the sequence: the flowers, the human speaker's prayers (matins and vespers poems), and the god who replies; range across several poems |
| AO2 | Poem titles signalling speaker; dramatic monologue; the sequence's seasonal structure; tonal shifts between plea, rebuke and resignation; garden imagery |
| AO3 | An argued view of the relationship: distance, dependence, mutual misunderstanding |
| AO4 | Clear structure tracing the conversation across the sequence |
| AO5 | Evaluates readings of the god's voice as indifferent, as parental, or as a projection of the human speaker |

Because the collection is in copyright, quote only short phrases from memory.

### Question 9 (*Beloved*, opening)

| AO | Indicative content |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Knowledge of how the opening's withheld facts (the baby ghost, Sethe's past, Sweet Home) are later revealed |
| AO2 | The house as a character; non-linear chronology; fragmented memory ("rememory"); shifting focalisation; the matter-of-fact treatment of the supernatural |
| AO3 | A view on why Morrison delays explanation |
| AO4 | Links passage detail to the novel's overall structure |
| AO5 | Evaluates readings of the ghost as literal, as psychological trauma, or as the history of slavery returning |

**What separates stronger answers.** A (b) answer at the top of the range analyses the passage closely and
connects it to later revelations; an answer that only summarises what the opening describes stays in
the lower range however accurate it is.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much quotation do I need in a closed-book exam?**
Learn short, flexible quotations and precise references for each text. Paper 4 rewards secure knowledge
(AO1) but you are not expected to quote at length.

**Can I use a critic I have read?**
Yes, if you evaluate the view. AO5 is about discussing and evaluating opinions, so explain whether the
critic's reading holds up against the text.

**Should I practise both (a) and (b) questions?**
Yes. You choose on the day, and the right choice depends on the specific question, so practise both for
each of your two texts.
