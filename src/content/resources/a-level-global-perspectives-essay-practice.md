## Section A

**1.** State the word range for the essay, and what happens to content
written beyond the upper limit. **[2]**

**2.** State what form the essay title must take, and name one type of
question that is unlikely to be appropriate. **[2]**

**3.** Name the three assessment objectives tested on this component. **[3]**

**4.** State one thing a teacher may do to support a candidate on this
component, and one thing they must not do. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A candidate proposes the essay title: "Climate change is a
serious global problem."

**(a)** Explain why this title is not suitable as it stands. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite it as a single question that would allow at least two
globally contrasting perspectives to be developed. **[2]**

**6.** A candidate develops one perspective on their essay question in
depth, then adds a second "perspective" that argues the same
underlying position in softer language.

**(a)** Explain why this does not satisfy the requirement for
"globally contrasting perspectives." **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what would need to change for the two perspectives to
be genuinely contrasting. **[2]**

**7.** A candidate lists five sources in their bibliography but does
not explain anywhere in the essay why any of them were chosen.

**(a)** Explain which assessment objective this affects, and why. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest what the candidate should have included instead. **[2]**

**8.** A candidate's essay ends with a paragraph summarising the two
perspectives already presented, without stating their own view.

**(a)** Explain why this ending is incomplete. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what AO2 specifically requires the conclusion to
contain. **[1]**

**9.** A candidate plans to develop five different perspectives across
their 1750–2000 word essay.

**(a)** Explain why this is likely to weaken the essay. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a more effective approach within the word limit. **[1]**

**10.** A candidate asks their teacher to review a draft and suggest
specific changes that would improve their mark under the mark scheme.

**(a)** Explain why the teacher cannot provide this kind of feedback. **[2]**
**(b)** State one form of support a teacher can still legitimately
provide. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** 1750–2000 words [1]; content written beyond 2000 words is not
marked at all [1].

**2.** The title must be phrased as a single question [1]; a question
focused on an exclusively local or national issue is unlikely to be
appropriate [1].

**3.** AO1, AO2 and AO3 [3].

**4.** A teacher may support planning, source citation and word-count
discipline [1]; a teacher must not give detailed advice on how a
specific draft could be improved to meet the mark scheme, or correct
or edit draft coursework directly [1].

**5. (a)** This title is a statement, not a question, and it does not
open up the possibility of genuinely contrasting perspectives — it
simply asserts a position [2].
**(b)** For example: "Should the countries most responsible for
historical carbon emissions bear greater responsibility for funding
global climate adaptation than those with lower historical emissions?"
[2, allow any single question that clearly opens up at least two
contrasting global standpoints].

**6. (a)** This does not satisfy the requirement because the two
perspectives disagree only in tone or intensity, not in the underlying
values or evidence being weighed — "globally contrasting" requires
perspectives that differ on which considerations should matter most,
not two versions of the same conclusion [2].
**(b)** The two perspectives would need to be drawn from genuinely
different standpoints — for example, differing by economic interest,
by cultural or religious position, or by which body of evidence and
values is prioritised — rather than restating the same underlying view
with softer or stronger language [2].

**7. (a)** This affects AO1, because AO1 specifically rewards
selecting and justifying sources — explaining why a particular source
was chosen and what makes it credible — not simply citing a source
without explanation [2].
**(b)** The candidate should have briefly explained, for each key
source, its authority, its standpoint, and what perspective it
represents, so a reader can judge how much weight to give it [2].

**8. (a)** This ending is incomplete because it only restates the
perspectives already presented rather than offering the candidate's
own reasoned judgement on the question, which is what the task
requires [2].
**(b)** AO2 requires the candidate to reflect on how the alternative
perspectives have affected their own viewpoint and to reach a
personal, supported conclusion on the question [1].

**9. (a)** At 1750–2000 words, developing five different perspectives
leaves too little space to develop any of them with real evidence,
resulting in perspectives that are sketched briefly rather than
argued in depth [2].
**(b)** Focus on two or three perspectives and develop each with real
evidence and detailed evaluation, rather than covering more
perspectives superficially [1].

**10. (a)** The syllabus requires the essay to be completed
independently by the candidate; a teacher giving detailed advice on
how a specific draft could be improved to meet the mark scheme, or
correcting and editing draft coursework directly, would compromise the
independence the component is designed to assess [2].
**(b)** For example, supporting the candidate with general planning,
source citation conventions, or word-count discipline [1].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

The single decision that shapes everything else in this component is
the choice of question, so it deserves real time before research
begins: a question that only really supports one side, or that
disguises a local issue as a global one, makes every later stage
harder no matter how strong the research is. Throughout drafting, keep
returning to three checks — are the perspectives genuinely drawn from
different standpoints rather than restatements of the same view, is
each source's selection explicitly justified rather than just cited,
and does the conclusion offer a personal, reasoned judgement rather
than a summary — since these three checks map directly onto where AO1
and AO2 marks are most often lost.
