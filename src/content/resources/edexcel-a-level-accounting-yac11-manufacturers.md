This guide covers outcomes **1.3.20 Manufacturing accounts with subheadings for prime cost, factory overhead, work in progress and production cost**, **1.3.21 Allocation and apportionment of costs between products and functions** and **1.3.22 Unrealised profit resulting from goods manufactured**. They close Topic 1.3, Financial statements of organisations, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Use it with the [financial statements of organisations revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-financial-statements-of-organisations-revision-notes/) and the [manufacturers practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-manufacturers-practice/). The overhead ideas continue in the [overhead costs guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-overhead-costs/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.3.20 | Manufacturing accounts with subheadings for prime cost, factory overhead, work in progress and production cost | Prepare the account and use its result in the trading account |
| 1.3.21 | Allocation and apportionment of costs between products and functions | Split costs between the factory and the office, and between products |
| 1.3.22 | Unrealised profit resulting from goods manufactured | Remove profit included in the value of unsold goods |

A trader buys goods ready for sale. A manufacturer makes them, so it needs an extra account to calculate the **cost of production** before the trading account starts. The manufacturing account deals with costs. The trading account then deals with sales and the finished goods.

## The layout of a manufacturing account

| Section | What it contains |
|---|---|
| **Direct materials** | Opening raw materials + purchases + carriage inwards − closing raw materials |
| **Direct labour** | Wages of workers who make the product |
| **Direct expenses** | Costs traceable to the product, such as royalties or hire of a special machine |
| **Prime cost** | The total of the three direct costs |
| **Factory overheads** | Indirect wages, factory power, rent and rates of the factory, depreciation of machinery |
| **Work in progress** | Add opening work in progress, deduct closing work in progress |
| **Production cost (cost of goods manufactured)** | Transferred to the trading account |

Opening and closing **work in progress** are partly finished goods. Add the opening figure because those goods were started last year, and deduct the closing figure because costs on them belong to next year's output.

## Worked example: Halden Furniture

For the year ended 30 June 2026:

| Item | $ |
|---|---:|
| Raw materials at 1 July 2025 | 8,400 |
| Raw materials purchased | 62,300 |
| Carriage inwards | 1,500 |
| Raw materials at 30 June 2026 | 9,100 |
| Direct wages | 38,500 |
| Royalties (per unit) | 2,200 |
| Indirect factory wages | 12,000 |
| Factory power | 7,400 |
| Depreciation of machinery | 6,000 |
| Work in progress at 1 July 2025 | 5,600 |
| Work in progress at 30 June 2026 | 4,900 |

The business pays rent of 18,000 and lighting of 6,000 for the whole site. The factory takes 600 square metres and the office 200. Both costs are shared by floor area, so 600 ÷ 800 = 75% is charged to the factory.

**Step 1: apportion shared costs.** Rent 18,000 × 75% = 13,500 to the factory. Lighting 6,000 × 75% = 4,500 to the factory. The other 25% (4,500 and 1,500) goes to the statement of profit or loss as expenses of the office and selling.

**Step 2: manufacturing account for the year ended 30 June 2026**

| | $ | $ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Raw materials at 1 July 2025 | 8,400 | |
| Purchases | 62,300 | |
| Carriage inwards | 1,500 | |
| | 72,200 | |
| Less raw materials at 30 June 2026 | (9,100) | |
| **Cost of raw materials used** | | 63,100 |
| Direct wages | | 38,500 |
| Royalties | | 2,200 |
| **Prime cost** | | **103,800** |
| Indirect wages | 12,000 | |
| Factory power | 7,400 | |
| Factory rent | 13,500 | |
| Factory lighting | 4,500 | |
| Depreciation of machinery | 6,000 | |
| **Factory overheads** | | 43,400 |
| | | 147,200 |
| Add work in progress at 1 July 2025 | | 5,600 |
| Less work in progress at 30 June 2026 | | (4,900) |
| **Production cost of goods completed** | | **147,900** |

Working: materials 8,400 + 62,300 + 1,500 − 9,100 = 63,100. Prime cost 63,100 + 38,500 + 2,200 = 103,800. Overheads 12,000 + 7,400 + 13,500 + 4,500 + 6,000 = 43,400. Total 103,800 + 43,400 = 147,200. After work in progress 147,200 + 5,600 − 4,900 = 147,900.

Machinery depreciation belongs here because machinery is used to make goods. Depreciation of office equipment would go to the statement of profit or loss.

**Step 3: the trading account (goods transferred at cost).** Finished goods opening inventory 14,000 and closing 16,800. Cost of sales = 14,000 + 147,900 − 16,800 = 145,100. If sales are 215,000, gross profit = 215,000 − 145,100 = **69,900**.

## Allocation and apportionment between functions and products (1.3.21)

Costs must be put in the right place for a reliable result.

- **Allocation** charges a whole cost to one department or function. Factory power is allocated to the factory.
- **Apportionment** shares a cost used by more than one function. Rent and lighting above were shared between factory and office by floor area.
- Between **products**, costs are shared on a basis that reflects the effort each product needs, such as machine hours or direct labour hours.

**Example: two products.** Halden makes Tables and Chairs. Machine hours are 3,000 for Tables and 2,000 for Chairs. The factory overheads of 43,400 are shared 3 : 2: Tables 43,400 × 3/5 = **26,040** and Chairs 43,400 × 2/5 = **17,360**. The direct costs of each product are added to find its full cost. Different bases would give different costs per product, so the basis must be stated.

## Unrealised profit on goods manufactured (1.3.22)

Some manufacturers transfer finished goods to the trading account at a **transfer price** above cost, such as cost plus a mark-up. This shows how well the factory performs compared with buying from outside suppliers. It creates a **manufacturing profit** that is only *realised* when the goods are sold. At the year end, finished goods still unsold are valued at the inflated transfer price, so the profit on them is **unrealised**. Prudence requires it to be removed.

**Example.** Halden transfers goods at cost plus 25%. Production cost is 147,900, so the transfer price is 147,900 × 125% = 184,875 and the manufacturing profit is 36,975.

Finished goods inventory at transfer price: opening 17,500 and closing 21,000.

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Sales | 215,000 |
| Opening finished goods (at transfer price) | 17,500 |
| Goods transferred from factory | 184,875 |
| Closing finished goods (at transfer price) | (21,000) |
| Cost of sales (at transfer price) | (181,375) |
| **Trading gross profit** | **33,625** |
| Add manufacturing profit | 36,975 |
| Combined gross profit before adjustment | 70,600 |

**Provision for unrealised profit.** The profit in the closing inventory is 21,000 × 25/125 = **4,200**, since a 25% mark-up means profit is 25/125 of the transfer price. The profit in the opening inventory was 17,500 × 25/125 = 3,500, and a provision for that amount existed already. The **increase** of 700 is deducted from gross profit.

Adjusted gross profit = 70,600 − 700 = **69,900**. This matches the result when goods are transferred at cost (69,900), which proves the method. In the statement of financial position, finished goods are shown at cost: 21,000 − 4,200 = 16,800.

Only the change in the provision goes to the profit calculation each year, as for the allowance for irrecoverable debts. If the provision falls, the decrease is added to gross profit.

## Common mistakes

- Adding closing raw materials or closing work in progress instead of deducting them.
- Including indirect wages in prime cost, or direct wages in factory overheads.
- Including selling or office costs, such as carriage outwards or office rent, in the manufacturing account.
- Forgetting the opening work in progress, or reversing its direction.
- Using the factory share of rent and lighting incorrectly, for example applying 600 ÷ 200 instead of 600 ÷ 800.
- Using the production cost, rather than the transfer price, to value the opening and closing finished goods when a transfer price is used.
- Calculating unrealised profit as 25% of the transfer price, instead of 25/125.
- Charging the whole provision for unrealised profit rather than the increase.

## Exam technique

- Use the exact subheadings from the specification: prime cost, factory overhead, work in progress and production cost. They help the examiner follow your account and match the mark scheme.
- Work down the account in order, writing subtotals. Examiners award marks for each subtotal.
- When a cost is shared, show the fraction first, such as 18,000 × 600 ÷ 800.
- Prove the result by another route where possible. For unrealised profit, check that the adjusted gross profit equals the gross profit using costs.
- **Explain** questions on unrealised profit need the consequence: without the adjustment, profit and inventory are overstated, which breaks the prudence concept.
