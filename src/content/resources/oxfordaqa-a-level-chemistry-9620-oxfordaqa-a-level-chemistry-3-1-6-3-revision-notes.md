These notes condense **3.1.6.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. For full explanations, read the [temperature and rate study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-3/); for exam-style questions, use the [temperature and rate practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-3-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **Rate of reaction**: change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time; unit **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
- Raising temperature **increases** the rate; lowering it **decreases** the rate.
- A **small** temperature rise can give a **large** rise in rate.
- Explanation: the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shifts to higher energy, so a **much greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea**.
- Collision frequency also rises, but only slightly: the **minor** effect.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time |
| Mean rate | Total change over an interval ÷ time for that interval |
| Relative rate | 1 ÷ time for a fixed amount of reaction; used to compare rates |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy ≥ Ea that leads to reaction |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy colliding particles need to react; unchanged by temperature |

## Calculating rates

```
mean rate = change in concentration ÷ time
relative rate = 1 ÷ time
```

Unit conversions:

- 0.42 mol dm⁻³ min⁻¹ = 0.42 ÷ 60 = 7.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
- A gas collected at 24 cm³ in 40 s: mean rate = 0.60 cm³ s⁻¹.
- A reaction taking 50 s has a relative rate of 0.020 s⁻¹; one taking 25 s has 0.040 s⁻¹, twice as fast.

## The two effects of heating

| Effect | Size | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Molecules move faster → more frequent collisions | Small (a few per cent for 10 K) | Speed rises only slightly with temperature |
| Greater proportion with energy ≥ Ea | Large | Ea is far out in the tail; shifting the curve right enlarges the area beyond Ea by a big factor |

Both increase the number of successful collisions per second; the second dominates.

## Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch for this explanation

- Two curves, same sample, labelled T₁ (lower) and T₂ (higher).
- T₂: peak lower and to the right; curves cross once; equal areas.
- Ea marked on the energy axis, to the right of both peaks.
- Area beyond Ea shaded; larger under T₂.

## Six-point explanation

1. Higher temperature, molecules have more energy on average.
2. Distribution shifts to higher energy.
3. Ea is unchanged.
4. Much greater proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Ea.
5. Much greater proportion of collisions successful; more successful collisions per second.
6. Collision frequency rises slightly too, but this is the minor effect.

## Fair testing in temperature experiments

Keep constant: amounts and concentrations of reactants, volume of solution, surface area of any solid, container. Change only the temperature, and measure it at the start of each run.

## Methods of following a reaction

| Observation | Measured with | Rate unit |
|---|---|---|
| Gas given off | Gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder | cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Mass lost as gas escapes | Balance | g s⁻¹ |
| Time for a fixed end point | Stopwatch | relative rate, s⁻¹ |

## Exam traps

- "Rate = time taken": rate is change **per** unit time.
- Negative rate for a reactant.
- Only "more collisions" or "particles move faster": no marks without the energy ≥ Ea point.
- "More molecules have the activation energy": write "energy **equal to or greater than** Ea".
- "Heating lowers Ea".
- Hotter curve with a taller peak or larger area.
- Treating 1/t as an absolute rate in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

## Self-test

1. Define rate of reaction.
2. Give the unit of rate when concentration is in mol dm⁻³ and time in s.
3. [B] rises from 0 to 0.054 mol dm⁻³ in 120 s. Calculate the mean rate.
4. Convert 0.30 mol dm⁻³ min⁻¹ into mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
5. Run 1 takes 64 s and run 2 takes 16 s to reach the same end point. How many times faster is run 2?
6. State the two effects of increasing temperature on molecules in a gas.
7. Which of the two effects is more important?
8. Why does the proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Ea increase by such a large factor?
9. Does the activation energy change when the temperature rises?
10. Why does cooling food slow spoilage?
11. Name three variables to keep constant when investigating temperature and rate.
12. Why is a rate always given as a positive number?

### Answers

1. The change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time.
2. mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
3. 0.054 ÷ 120 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
4. 0.30 ÷ 60 = **5.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
5. 64 ÷ 16 = **4 times** faster.
6. Collisions become slightly more frequent; a much greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea.
7. The greater proportion with energy ≥ Ea.
8. Ea lies far out in the tail of the distribution; when the curve shifts to higher energy, the small area beyond Ea grows by a large factor.
9. No.
10. Lower temperature means a much smaller proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Ea, so fewer successful collisions per second in the spoilage reactions.
11. Any three: concentration of reactants; volume; amount or surface area of a solid; container; method of timing.
12. Rate describes how quickly the reaction proceeds; it is the size of the change per unit time, whichever substance is followed.
