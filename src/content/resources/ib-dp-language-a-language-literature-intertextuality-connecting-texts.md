This study guide teaches **Intertextuality: connecting texts**, the third of the three syllabus
components of IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and Literature (teachers often call them
areas of exploration). It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL
2021). It covers SL and HL: the brief recommends 50 teaching hours for this component at SL and 80
at HL, the same as each of the other two components. The unit covers how texts relate to other texts,
the conventions of text types and literary forms, and comparing literary and non-literary works.

Use it with the [Intertextuality revision
notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-intertextuality-connecting-texts-revision-notes/)
and the [Intertextuality practice
questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-intertextuality-connecting-texts-practice/).
For the whole course, see the [Language and Literature course
hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-a-language-and-literature/) and the [printable
checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-a-language-and-literature/). The first two components are
covered in [Readers, writers and
texts](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-readers-writers-texts/) (how choices create
effects) and [Time and space](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-time-and-space/) (how
context shapes meaning). This guide adds a third question: how does a text mean differently because of
the texts around it?

## What this unit covers

| Part of the unit | What you must be able to do | SL/HL |
|---|---|---|
| Text-to-text links | Spot an allusion, quotation, parody, pastiche or adaptation, name its source and explain what the borrowing adds | Both |
| Conventions of text types | Say what readers expect of an advert, speech, letter, news report or blog, and whether a text follows, bends or breaks those expectations | Both |
| Conventions of literary forms | Do the same for the sonnet, the novel, the short story and drama | Both |

## What the brief says this component is for

The subject brief does not list content for each component, but its aims and assessment objectives
point straight at this one. One aim is to "develop an understanding of relationships between texts"
and of how they "open up multiple meanings". Assessment objective 1 asks you to know "features of
particular text types and literary forms". Assessment objective 2 asks you to analyse and evaluate
"relationships among different texts" and "ways in which texts may offer perspectives on human
concerns".

So the unit has three strands, each a kind of connection.

1. **Text to text.** One text points to another.
2. **Text to convention.** A text works with, or against, the expected features of its type or form.
3. **Text beside text.** You place two texts together and build an argument out of the comparison.

## Strand 1: when one text points to another

An **intertext** is any earlier text that a new text depends on for part of its meaning. The main
ways of connecting are:

- **Allusion** -- a brief, indirect reference the reader is expected to recognise. A headline that
  calls a doomed project "a modern Titanic" alludes to the ship.
- **Quotation** -- the exact words of another text, usually marked. It borrows authority or invites
  comparison.
- **Parody** -- imitation of a style or form in order to mock or criticise it. It exaggerates the
  features you already know.
- **Pastiche** -- imitation of a style that celebrates or plays with it, without mockery.
- **Adaptation** -- a text moved into a new medium or form, such as a novel into a film.
- **Response or rewriting** -- a text that answers another, often by telling the story from a
  silenced point of view.

The question is never just "what is referenced?" but "what does the reference **do** here?". An
allusion can borrow prestige, set up irony, or compress a big idea into a few words.

### Worked example: using a convention against itself

William Shakespeare, Sonnet 130 (published 1609), opens:

> My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;
> Coral is far more red than her lips' red;

**Step 1 -- name the intertext.** Love sonnets of the period often praised a beloved by comparing
her features to precious or beautiful things: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, skin like snow.
This listing of features is called a **blazon**.

**Step 2 -- say what is kept.** The poem keeps the form: fourteen lines, three quatrains and a
rhyming couplet, in iambic pentameter. The reader recognises it at once as a love sonnet.

**Step 3 -- say what is changed.** Each comparison is reversed. The eyes are "nothing like" the sun.

**Step 4 -- explain the effect.** The reversal mocks the exaggeration of conventional praise. The
closing couplet then turns: the speaker thinks his love "as rare / As any she belied with false
compare". The poem claims that honest description is the truer compliment. Its meaning depends on
the reader knowing the convention it rejects.

That four-step move -- **name, kept, changed, effect** -- works for any intertextual reference.

## Strand 2: conventions of text types and literary forms

A **convention** is a feature readers expect in a given kind of text. The brief names both **text
types** and **literary forms**. In practice, text types are non-literary (adverts, speeches,
reports, letters, blogs, infographics) and literary forms include poetry, prose fiction, drama and
forms within them such as the sonnet.

| Kind of text | Typical conventions |
|---|---|
| Print advert | Headline, image, brand name, slogan, call to action, direct address ("you") |
| News report | Headline, short lead paragraph answering who/what/where/when, quotations from sources, third person |
| Speech | Direct address, repetition, lists of three, rhetorical questions, a memorable close |
| Letter | Salutation, sign-off, first and second person, register set by the relationship |
| Sonnet | Fourteen lines, a set rhyme scheme, a turn (volta) in the argument |

A text can do three things with a convention:

- **Follow it.** The reader's expectations are met, so attention goes to the message.
- **Bend it.** A familiar feature is used in an unexpected place or way.
- **Break it.** An expected feature is missing or reversed, which draws attention.

A **hybrid text** mixes the conventions of two types or forms. Adverts often borrow the look of news
reports or fairy tales; poems borrow recipes, letters and lists. The analytical point is always the gap between the borrowed form and the
real purpose.

### Worked example: an advert in the shape of a fairy tale

*Original text written for Marlbridge (the product and brand are invented):*

> Once upon a time, in a kitchen not far from yours, there lived a kettle that took forever to boil.
> Every morning the family waited. And waited. Then one day a small blue box arrived at the door.
> Inside was the Marrowby Quickboil. Now breakfast takes five minutes, and everyone lives happily
> ever after. *Marrowby. Mornings, rewritten.*

**Conventions borrowed:** the opening "Once upon a time", the setting "not far from yours", a
problem, a magical arrival, and "happily ever after".

**Conventions of the real text type still present:** the brand name, the product name, a slogan.

**Effect:** the fairy-tale frame turns a small appliance into the hero of a story. It suggests the
product can transform a household. The phrase "not far from yours" pulls the reader into the tale.
The slogan "Mornings, rewritten" admits the borrowing: the advert is rewriting a familiar story, and
invites you to rewrite your routine.

**What a strong answer adds:** "happily ever after" promises a feeling, not a fact, so the advert
persuades without stating anything it could be held to.

## Strand 3: comparing texts across the course

The course puts literary and non-literary texts side by side. The individual oral, as the brief
describes it, asks how one literary work and one non-literary body of work "have approached a
common global issue". Paper 2 is a comparative essay on two literary works. Comparison is therefore
a skill you need in more than one place.

A good comparison needs a **point of connection** first: a shared issue, theme, technique or form.
Then you ask how each text treats it, and why they differ.

### Worked example: one issue, two text types

*Both texts are original, written for Marlbridge.*

> **Text 1 -- poem, "The Last Bus"**
> She keeps the kettle warm for no one now.
> The clock is loud. The street lamps come and go.
> At nine she walks as far as the bus stop
> to watch the last bus leave, and not be on it,
> and wave goodnight to the driver, who waves back.

> **Text 2 -- leaflet from an invented charity**
> Somewhere on your street, someone has not had a conversation all week. One hour, once a fortnight,
> can change that. Open Door Visits pairs volunteers with older neighbours for a cup of tea and a
> chat. Pick up a form at your local library today.

| Point of comparison | Text 1 (poem) | Text 2 (leaflet) |
|---|---|---|
| Shared issue | Loneliness in old age | Loneliness in old age |
| Purpose | To make the reader feel the experience | To make the reader act |
| Address | Third person ("she"); reader observes | Second person ("your"); reader is involved |
| Method | Concrete images (kettle, loud clock, bus) | Plain statement and a practical offer |
| Ending | An image of small contact ("who waves back") | An instruction ("Pick up a form") |

A model comparative sentence: *Both texts present loneliness through a missing conversation, but
the poem leaves that silence for the reader to feel in images such as the "loud" clock, while the
leaflet names it outright and turns it into a problem the reader can solve in "one hour".*

Notice the shape: a shared point, then "but", then evidence from **both** texts in the same
sentence. That is comparison woven together, not two separate descriptions.

## How this unit is assessed

The brief's assessment model has no paper for a single component, so all four assessments can draw
on this one:

- **Paper 1** (SL 1.25 hours, HL 2.25 hours; 35% at both levels) is a guided analysis of unseen
  non-literary passage or passages "from different text types". Knowing the conventions of text
  types, and noticing when a text borrows from another type, is part of reading any unseen passage
  well.
- **Paper 2** (1.75 hours at both levels; 35% SL, 25% HL) is a comparative essay on two literary
  works, answering one of four questions. Strand 3 is the core skill.
- **Individual oral** (30% SL, 20% HL) connects one literary work and one non-literary body of
  work through a common global issue.
- **HL essay** (HL only, 20%) is a 1,200--1,500 word essay on one literary work or non-literary
  body of work. You could build it on an intertextual line of inquiry, such as how a work uses
  or subverts its form.

Your teacher will share the assessment criteria for each component.

## Common errors

- **Spotting without explaining.** "This is an allusion to a fairy tale" earns little until you say
  what the fairy-tale frame does to the message.
- **Guessing sources.** If you cannot name the intertext with confidence, describe the convention
  instead ("the language of a fairy tale") rather than inventing a specific source.
- **Treating parody and pastiche as the same.** Parody mocks; pastiche imitates without mockery.
- **Listing conventions as a checklist.** Pick the convention that is bent or broken and analyse
  that.
- **Comparing block by block.** Writing all about Text 1 and then all about Text 2 leaves the
  comparison to the reader. Put both texts in the same paragraph, often the same sentence.
- **Choosing a weak point of connection.** "Both texts are about people" is too broad to argue from.
  Narrow it to a specific issue, technique or form.
