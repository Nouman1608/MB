Condensed recall for subtopic **4.3 Achieving quality production**. For worked cost calculations and an annotated recommendation, use the [Achieving quality production study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-achieving-quality-production/).

## What quality means

- Meeting customers' **expectations** and being **fit for purpose**, every time (**consistency**).
- Not the same as luxury or a high price.
- For services: reliability, waiting time, staff manners, accuracy and the customer's experience.

## Why quality matters for all businesses

| Good quality brings… | Poor quality causes… |
|---|---|
| Repeat customers and recommendations | Lost customers and bad reviews |
| A strong brand, possibly higher prices | A damaged reputation that is slow to rebuild |
| Fewer complaints and returns | Refunds, replacements and staff time on complaints |
| Reliable orders from business customers | Business customers switching supplier |
| Lower risk of legal action | Possible legal claims if products are unsafe |
| Less waste | Scrapped materials and reworking |

Faults found **after** delivery usually cost more than faults found in production.

## Quality control

- **Idea**: check products to **find** faults and remove them before customers receive them.
- **Who**: mainly inspectors.
- **When**: on arrival of materials, during production, and on finished products.
- **How**: set standards → decide to check every item or a sample → train inspectors → remove and record faulty items → report patterns.
- **In services**: supervisors check completed work; review of customer calls.
- **Strength**: fewer faulty items reach customers.
- **Weaknesses**: waste has already happened; inspection costs money; samples can miss faults; workers may leave quality to inspectors.

## Quality assurance

- **Idea**: set and meet standards at **every stage** to **prevent** faults.
- **Who**: every employee is responsible for quality.
- **How**:
  - agreed standards for each stage
  - suppliers meet set standards
  - training so workers can check their own work
  - workers allowed to stop a process when something is wrong
  - scheduled machine maintenance
  - customer feedback used to improve
  - faults analysed and processes changed.
- **Strengths**: fewer faults, less waste, lower long-run costs, workers more involved.
- **Weaknesses**: training and new systems cost money; results take time; needs commitment from all staff and managers.

## Comparing the two

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Detect faults | Prevent faults |
| Timing | Checks at stages or at the end | Throughout the process |
| Responsibility | Inspectors | All employees |
| Cost pattern | Ongoing inspection plus cost of faulty items | Higher set-up cost, lower cost of faults later |

## Calculating the cost of poor quality

```
faulty items = output × fault rate
wasted cost = faulty items found in production × cost per item
lost revenue = faulty items refunded × selling price
```

Add other measurable costs (complaint handling, replacements), then compare before and after any change, subtracting the cost of the change.

## Recommending an approach

- **Large output of identical items**: mainly quality assurance, with some final sample checks.
- **Few, high-value or one-off items**: inspecting each item may be enough.
- **Safety-critical products**: strong assurance plus final testing.
- **Untrained or new workforce**: start with control while training builds assurance.
- Always link to two facts in the case and weigh the cost.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Quality | Meeting customer expectations and being fit for purpose, consistently |
| Quality control | Checking products to find and remove faults before they reach customers |
| Quality assurance | Making sure standards are met at every stage so faults are prevented |
| Inspection | Examining products to see whether they meet the standard |
| Sample | A selection of items checked to represent the whole output |
| Defect / fault | A product or service that does not meet the standard |
| Rework | Correcting a faulty item so it can be sold |

## Exam traps

- **Quality ≠ expensive.** A cheap product can be high quality.
- **Control finds; assurance prevents.**
- **"How it is implemented"** needs steps, not just a definition.
- **Services need quality too**: apply ideas to clinics, hotels, transport.
- **Weigh the cost** of quality assurance against the savings.
- **Many businesses use both**; a recommendation can combine them if justified.

## Self-test

1. Define quality.
2. Give two benefits to a business of producing good-quality products.
3. Give two costs of poor quality.
4. What is quality control?
5. At which three stages can quality control checks take place?
6. Give one weakness of quality control.
7. What is quality assurance?
8. Give three ways a business can implement quality assurance.
9. Give one weakness of quality assurance.
10. A factory makes 5,000 items with a 3% fault rate. How many are faulty?
11. If each faulty item costs $8 to make and is scrapped, what is the wasted cost?
12. Why might a small craft workshop rely on quality control rather than quality assurance?

## Answers

1. Meeting customers' expectations and being fit for purpose, consistently.
2. Any two: repeat sales; good reputation; higher prices possible; fewer complaints and returns; fewer legal claims.
3. Any two: scrapped materials; reworking; refunds; staff time on complaints; lost customers; damaged reputation.
4. Checking products to find faults and remove faulty items before they reach customers.
5. When raw materials arrive, during production and when products are finished.
6. Any one: faults have already been made; inspection costs money; sampling may miss faults; workers may not take responsibility for quality.
7. Making sure quality standards are met at every stage of production so that faults are prevented.
8. Any three: standards for every stage; supplier standards; training workers to check their own work; allowing workers to stop a process; scheduled maintenance; using customer feedback; analysing faults.
9. Any one: costly training and systems; takes time to show results; needs commitment from everyone.
10. 5,000 × 3% = **150**.
11. 150 × $8 = **$1,200**.
12. It makes few items, so the owner can inspect each one, and the cost of introducing a full assurance system may outweigh the benefit.
