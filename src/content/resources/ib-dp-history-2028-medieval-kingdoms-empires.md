**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 1, Medieval kingdoms and empires (circa 750 to 1400), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option, which is examined on HL Paper 3. In Paper 3 you answer two essay questions, each from a different regional study, so you must be able to write an evaluative essay on this study. The guide says every question on it will require you to refer to **one example** of a medieval kingdom or empire. Suggested examples include the Carolingian Empire, the Holy Roman Empire, the North Sea Empire, Norman and Angevin England, Capetian France and the Kingdom of Poland. This guide uses the Carolingian Empire as its main case and points out where other examples would differ.

## How the study is organised

The guide gives five inquiry topics. Each one is a question about your chosen kingdom or empire.

1. Reasons for emergence and expansion.
2. Legitimization, consolidation and maintenance of authority.
3. Impact of religion.
4. Cultural and social developments.
5. Reasons for decline.

A sixth block asks you to judge the role and importance of **one leader**: in emergence and expansion, in consolidating and maintaining rule, in religion, culture and society, and in decline. Your essay must show a clear argument and weigh different views, because the Paper 3 markband rewards "a thorough and well-balanced evaluation of a range of perspectives" and a substantiated judgement.

## Reasons for emergence and expansion

The Carolingians began as the "mayors of the palace", the chief officials of the Merovingian kings of the Franks. **Charles Martel** (died 741) held real power and led armies. His son **Pippin III** deposed the last Merovingian king and was crowned in 751 with the approval of the papacy. In 756 Pippin gave the pope lands in Italy after defeating the Lombards, the so-called Donation of Pippin.

*Political and dynastic factors.* The Carolingians won because they controlled the army and the best estates, and because the Merovingian kings were weak. Frankish custom divided a kingdom between sons, which caused rivalry but also gave each heir a ready-made power base.

*Economic factors.* Conquest was profitable. Plunder, tribute and new land paid warriors and kept aristocratic families loyal. Expansion therefore fed itself: success brought rewards, and rewards bought more support.

*Social and cultural factors.* A warrior aristocracy expected to fight each summer. Shared Christian belief gave rulers a language of mission and a reason to call conquests just.

*Conflict and settlement.* **Charlemagne** (king from 768, emperor from 800, died 814) fought almost every year. He conquered the Lombard kingdom in 774, fought the Saxons in a long series of campaigns from the 770s to about 804, defeated the Avars in the 790s and set up a frontier zone against Muslim Spain. Conquered peoples were often settled under Frankish counts, bishops and monasteries. Defeated Saxon leaders were forced to accept baptism, and resistance was met with brutal force, such as the reported execution of several thousand Saxons at Verden in 782.

**Common mistake.** Do not treat expansion as only the work of one great man. Charlemagne inherited a strong army, a loyal aristocracy and papal friendship from his father and grandfather.

## Legitimization, consolidation and maintenance of authority

*Legal and political methods.* Charlemagne issued **capitularies**, written royal orders on subjects from justice to farming. He divided the empire into counties run by **counts**, who held courts, raised troops and collected dues. To check them he sent **missi dominici**, usually a bishop or abbot and a lay noble, on circuits to hear complaints and report. Oaths of loyalty were demanded of free men. Assemblies of nobles met each year, which let the king explain policy and win consent.

*Social factors.* Rule depended on the nobility. Lords received land, called benefices, in return for service. Powerful families were rewarded with offices and intermarried with the royal house. Bishops and abbots were royal officials as well as churchmen, and literate clerics wrote the documents that kept government going.

*Economic factors.* Income came from royal estates, tolls, fines and war booty, rather than regular land tax. The royal estates were run under detailed instructions in a text called the *Capitulare de villis*. Charlemagne also reformed the coinage around a silver penny (the denier), which made trade and payment easier across a wide area.

*Use of force.* The army could be summoned every year and garrisons guarded the frontiers. Rebels, including some nobles, were dealt with severely.

*Legitimization.* The coronation of Charlemagne as emperor by **Pope Leo III** in Rome on Christmas Day 800 linked the king to the Roman past and to the Church. Historians still argue about who gained more from the ceremony. Charlemagne's biographer Einhard claimed the king was unhappy about it, but this may be flattery or later tidying.

## Impact of religion

*Political impact.* Christianity gave kings a sacred role. Anointing at the coronation echoed the Old Testament kings. Rulers presented themselves as defenders of the Church, and Charlemagne's legislation treated religious duty and royal command as one.

*Religious institutions.* Monasteries and cathedrals grew rich on royal gifts and became centres of learning. The Rule of St Benedict was promoted as the standard for monks, especially under Louis the Pious with the help of Benedict of Aniane. Missionaries such as **Boniface** (died 754) had earlier organised the Church in Germany.

*Social impact.* Priests taught belief and behaviour, churches marked the calendar, and the Church ran charity and some education. Forced conversion of the Saxons, such as the harsh law code issued for Saxony, shows religion could be used as a tool of conquest.

*The role of the papacy.* The popes needed a protector against the Lombards and later against Rome's own nobility. Kings needed the pope's blessing. This was a partnership, but its terms were never agreed, and it left a long argument over whether emperors or popes were superior.

## Cultural and social developments

*Technology.* Agriculture slowly improved. The use of the heavy plough, water mills and the three-field system spread in parts of northern Europe over this period, though historians date these developments cautiously and unevenly.

*Arts and learning.* The "Carolingian Renaissance" produced schools, copied manuscripts, the clear script known as **Carolingian minuscule** and a revival of Latin learning. **Alcuin of York** led the palace school at Aachen. Palace architecture such as the chapel at Aachen drew on Roman and Byzantine models. Some historians dislike the word "renaissance", arguing it overstates how many people were touched by it.

*Women.* Noblewomen could hold property and influence politics, as empresses and abbesses did. A rare voice is **Dhuoda**, a noblewoman who wrote a handbook of advice for her son in the early 840s. Most women worked on the land and in the household, with few legal rights.

*Marginalized groups.* The unfree, including enslaved people and serfs, formed a large part of society. Jewish communities lived under royal protection in return for payment but were open to hostility. Conquered peoples such as the Saxons faced forced conversion.

## Reasons for decline

*Internal challenges.* Louis the Pious faced revolts by his own sons. After his death in 840 civil war ended in the **Treaty of Verdun** (843), which split the empire among his three sons. Further divisions followed, and Charles the Fat, who briefly reunited much of the empire, was deposed in 887.

*Economic and social challenges.* The end of conquest dried up plunder. Kings gave away land to buy loyalty, so they had less to give. Local lords gained power as people looked to them for protection.

*Political and external challenges.* Viking raiding began around 793 and grew serious in the ninth century. Magyars raided from the east and Muslim raiders from the south. Rulers who could not defend their people lost authority to local strongmen. The rule of succession mattered most: a system that split the realm among sons made weakness likely.

## The role of one leader

If you choose Charlemagne, structure your argument around the four prompts in the guide. Charlemagne drove the conquests (emergence and expansion). He built a system of counts, missi and capitularies (maintenance of rule). He promoted Christian reform and learning (religion and culture). On decline, historians divide: some blame the partition customs and his successors' weakness, others argue his system relied too heavily on his own energy and personality. A balanced essay can say that his leadership was necessary to the empire's peak but that structural weakness, shared with other early medieval states, made its fall likely.

## Different views historians have taken

- **Pirenne** argued, in work published in the 1930s, that the real break with the ancient world came when Islam disrupted Mediterranean trade, making the Carolingian world land-based. Later research has challenged his dating and his picture of trade.
- Historians such as **Janet Nelson** and **Rosamond McKitterick** have stressed the pragmatic, written and negotiated nature of Carolingian government, rather than a simple picture of a strong ruler imposing order.
- **Susan Reynolds** and others have questioned whether "feudalism" is a useful label, which affects how you describe lord and vassal relations.

Use these views by naming the argument, stating what it explains, and saying what it leaves out.

## Using other examples

If your class studied a different example, test it against the same five inquiry topics. For Norman England, think of the conquest of 1066 and the Domesday survey of 1086 (maintenance of authority by information and taxation). For Capetian France, think of how a king with a small royal domain grew in strength. For Poland, think of the role of the nobility and the Church. The questions stay the same; only the evidence changes.

## How to write the Paper 3 essay

Read the claim in the question. Say in your introduction what you will argue. Use two or three themes, such as military, religious and economic factors, and rank them. Include at least one historian's view and a counter-argument. Finish with a judgement that follows from the evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I study only one kingdom or empire?**
Yes. Every question refers to one example, so choose one and know it well.

**Is Charlemagne's reign the only thing I need to know about the Carolingians?**
No. Questions on emergence and decline need the period before and after him.

**What evidence is strongest for Carolingian authority?**
The capitularies, the records of the missi and the surviving charters show how government worked, though they show what the king wanted as much as what happened.
