These questions cover section 3.2 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Low unemployment. All places, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [low unemployment study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [low unemployment revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by being economically inactive. **[1]**

**2.** A country has 14.4 million people employed and 0.6 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one weakness of the Claimant Count as a measure of unemployment. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the type of unemployment in each case.

**(a)** Fruit pickers have no work once the harvest ends. **[1]**
**(b)** A steelworks closes because it cannot compete with cheaper imports, and its workers' skills are not needed by other local firms. **[1]**
**(c)** Building firms lay off workers because demand falls across the economy during a recession. **[1]**

**5.** Which one of the following people is counted as unemployed? **[1]**

- A. A retired teacher who does not want a job
- B. A full-time student who is not looking for work
- C. A person who lost their job last month and is applying for new ones
- D. A part-time worker who would like more hours

**6.** Explain why frictional unemployment is usually less of a concern than structural unemployment. **[2]**

**7.** A region has a labour force of 1.2 million and an unemployment rate of 7.5%. The rate falls to 6.0% with no change in the labour force. Calculate the fall in the number of people unemployed. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** The table shows data for Country W.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Labour force (millions) | 20.0 | 20.4 | 20.6 | 21.0 | 21.5 |
| Unemployed (millions) | 0.90 | 1.12 | 1.24 | 1.05 | 1.06 |

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate for each year, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the year with the highest rate and describe the trend over the five years. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the unemployment rate fell between Year 4 and Year 5 even though the number unemployed rose. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage increase in the number unemployed between Year 1 and Year 3. **[1]**

**9.** A car plant in the town of Bramford closes, and 3,000 workers lose their jobs. Each earned £32,000 a year and paid 25% of it in tax. Each now receives £5,500 a year in benefits.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly income lost by the workers. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the yearly cost to the government. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse the likely consequences of the closure for the town. **[3]**

**10.** Analyse how a fall in consumer spending can cause unemployment. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences of long-term unemployment for individuals and for the government. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the Claimant Count is a good measure of unemployment. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Not in work and not seeking work, such as full-time students, retired people or full-time carers [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Labour force = 14.4 + 0.6 = 15.0 million [1]. Rate = 0.6 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the employed (14.4 million) gives 4.2%, which is wrong.

**3.** Some unemployed people are not entitled to benefits, for example because a partner earns or they have savings, or they choose not to claim [1]. So the count understates the true number of people without work who are looking for it [1]. (Credit also: changes in benefit rules change the count without any change in joblessness.) **[2]**

**4. (a)** Seasonal [1]. **(b)** Structural [1]. **(c)** Cyclical [1]. **[3]**

**5.** **C** [1]. A and B are economically inactive; D is employed. **[1]**

**6.** Frictional unemployment lasts a short time because people are simply moving between jobs that exist [1]. Structural unemployment can last years because the jobs have gone and workers need new skills or must move, so the losses of income and output are much larger [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Before: 1,200,000 × 0.075 = 90,000; after: 1,200,000 × 0.06 = 72,000 [1]. Fall = **18,000** [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Year 1: 0.90 ÷ 20.0 = **4.5%**; Year 2: 1.12 ÷ 20.4 = **5.5%** [1]. Year 3: 1.24 ÷ 20.6 = **6.0%**; Year 4: 1.05 ÷ 21.0 = **5.0%** [1]. Year 5: 1.06 ÷ 21.5 = **4.9%** [1].
**(b)** Year 3, at 6.0% [1]. The rate rose by 1.5 percentage points from Year 1 to Year 3, then fell to below 5% by Year 5 [1].
**(c)** The labour force grew by 0.5 million while the number unemployed rose by only 10,000 [1], so the unemployed became a smaller share of the labour force [1].
**(d)** (1.24 − 0.90) ÷ 0.90 × 100 = **37.8%** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), use percentage points for changes in the rate.

**9. (a)** 3,000 × £32,000 = **£96m** [1].
**(b)** Lost tax = 25% × £96m = £24m; benefits = 3,000 × £5,500 = £16.5m [1]. Total = **£40.5m** a year [1].
**(c)** Former workers have much lower incomes, so they spend less in local shops and services [1]. Those businesses lose revenue and may cut staff or close, so unemployment spreads to other firms in Bramford [1]. Over time young people may leave to find work and new firms may avoid the town, so the decline can become long-lasting [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Households buy fewer goods and services, so firms' sales fall [1]. Firms need fewer workers to produce the lower output, so they cut hours or make workers redundant [1]. This is cyclical unemployment, and the lost incomes reduce spending further, so other firms also cut jobs [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Individuals: lower income, so living standards fall and debts may build up [1]. The longer the spell, the more skills go out of date and confidence falls, making it harder to get a job [1]. There can be effects on physical and mental health [1]. Government: benefit spending rises and income tax and national insurance receipts fall [1], so there is an opportunity cost, such as less money for schools or hospitals, or more borrowing [1]. The economy also loses the output these workers could have produced [1]. Evaluation: the impact depends on how long unemployment lasts and whether retraining is available; support can reduce the harm to individuals but adds to the government's costs [1]. Judgement: the most serious consequence is the lasting loss of skills, because it raises both the individual's and the government's costs for years after the spell begins [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For: it is quick and cheap to produce because the government already has the records [1], and it can be broken down by area, so the government can see where unemployment is concentrated [1]. Against: it misses unemployed people who are not entitled to benefits or choose not to claim [1], and changes in benefit rules change the count even when the number of jobless people is the same [1]. It may therefore understate unemployment, especially among groups less likely to claim [1]. Judgement: it is a useful, timely indicator of trends and of the cost to government, but it is not a complete measure of everyone who wants work, so it should be read alongside other evidence [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the employed or the population instead of the labour force.
- Writing "%" when "percentage points" is needed.
- Classifying automation or industry decline as cyclical.
- Ignoring the group named in the question.
- Evaluating without a judgement.
