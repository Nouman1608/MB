## What the specification asks

The content statements for 3.1.1.1 are short. Learn them as three separate jobs:

1. **Appreciate that knowledge and understanding of atomic structure has evolved over time.** You explain why a model is replaced, using evidence.
2. **Protons, neutrons and electrons: relative charge and relative mass.** You state the values and use them.
3. **An atom consists of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons.** You describe the structure and link it to charge and mass.

The specification does not name particular scientists or experiments, so any accurate historical example can support an answer.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fundamental particle | One of the three particles atoms are built from here: proton, neutron, electron |
| Nucleus | The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom; holds protons and neutrons |
| Relative charge | Charge compared with the charge on a proton (+1); no units |
| Relative mass | Mass compared with the mass of a proton (or neutron), taken as 1; no units |
| Atom | Particle with equal numbers of protons and electrons, so no overall charge |
| Ion | Particle with unequal numbers of protons and electrons, so an overall charge |
| Model | A simplified description that explains the evidence available at the time |

## The three particles

| Particle | Location | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | Outside the nucleus | −1 | 1/1840 |

Recall hooks:

- **Charge:** proton and electron are equal and opposite; the neutron carries none.
- **Mass:** proton and neutron are almost equal (the neutron is very slightly heavier); the electron is tiny but not zero. Some books give 1/1836. Use whichever value a question supplies.

## How the model changed: summary table

| Model | Evidence it was built on | Why it was replaced or refined |
|---|---|---|
| Solid, indivisible sphere | Elements combine in fixed proportions | Atoms were found to contain smaller charged particles |
| Positive sphere with electrons embedded | Discovery of the negative electron; atoms are neutral | Alpha particles fired at thin gold foil were sometimes deflected through large angles; spread-out charge cannot do that |
| Nuclear atom | Most alpha particles passed straight through; a tiny fraction bounced back | Electron positions needed a better description |
| Electrons in fixed energy levels (shells) | Explains why electrons do not spiral into the nucleus; links to spectra | Later refined into sub-shells and orbitals (3.1.1.3) |
| Nucleus of protons and neutrons | Nuclei were heavier than their protons alone could explain | Current model at this level |

**The idea examiners want.** A model is kept while it explains the evidence. When reliable, repeated experiments produce results the model cannot explain, the model is changed. Write "the new evidence did not fit the model", not "scientists were wrong".

### Reading scattering evidence in two lines

- Most alpha particles pass straight through, so most of the atom is **empty space**.
- A very few are turned back, so the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a **very small volume**.

Pair each observation with its conclusion.

## Charge: the counting rule

```
overall charge = protons − electrons
neutrons never change the charge
```

### Method in steps

1. Find the number of protons (the atomic number; reading it from the data or Periodic Table is covered in 3.1.1.2).
2. Read the charge from the symbol.
3. Electrons = protons − charge. For a 2− ion, subtracting −2 adds 2 electrons.
4. Check: the sum of relative charges equals the charge on the symbol.

**Worked reminders.**

- O²⁻: 8 protons; electrons = 8 − (−2) = **10**.
- K⁺: 19 protons; electrons = 19 − 1 = **18**.
- A particle with 35 protons and 36 electrons: charge = 35 − 36 = **1−**, so it is a bromide ion, Br⁻.
- Cu²⁺ and Cu: both have 29 protons. Only the electron count differs (27 and 29).

Change the protons and you change the element. Change the neutrons and you change the isotope. Change the electrons and you make an ion.

## Mass: where it sits

The nucleus holds over 99.9% of an atom's mass. A quick check with fluorine (9 protons, 10 neutrons, 9 electrons):

```
nucleus:    9 + 10      = 19
electrons:  9 × 1/1840  = 0.00489
electron share = 0.00489 ÷ 19.00489 × 100 = 0.0257 %
```

So mass number counts only nuclear particles, and an ion has, in practice, the same mass as its atom. The size of an atom is set by its electrons but its mass by its nucleus, so "most of the mass is in a tiny volume" is a precise statement.

## Comparing particles

State both properties for both particles. Proton vs neutron: same mass (1), different charge (+1 and 0). Proton vs electron: equal and opposite charge, very different mass. "They are similar" earns nothing.

## Exam traps

- Writing the electron's relative mass as 0. Its mass is very small, not zero.
- Dropping the minus sign on the electron's charge.
- Placing electrons in the nucleus, or neutrons outside it.
- Calling the neutron "negative".
- Using neutrons in a charge calculation.
- Saying an atom is neutral "because neutrons cancel the charge". It is neutral because protons equal electrons.
- Describing the nuclear atom as solid. It is mostly empty space.
- Listing scattering observations without the conclusion each one supports.

## Self-test

1. State the relative charge and relative mass of a neutron.
2. Where in an atom are the electrons found?
3. Why does an atom have no overall charge?
4. How many electrons are in a S²⁻ ion (16 protons)?
5. A particle has 12 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons. Give its charge.
6. An ion of charge 3+ has 10 electrons. How many protons does it have, and which element is it?
7. Which observation from alpha scattering shows that the atom is mostly empty space?
8. Which observation shows that the positive charge is concentrated in a tiny volume?
9. Give one reason why the neutron was needed in the model of the nucleus.
10. Using 1/1840 for the electron, calculate the percentage of the mass of a helium-4 atom (2 protons, 2 neutrons, 2 electrons) due to its electrons, to 3 significant figures.
11. Compare a proton and a neutron.
12. Why is the mass of Na⁺ treated as equal to the mass of a sodium atom?

### Answers

1. Charge 0, relative mass 1.
2. Outside the nucleus, in the space around it.
3. It has equal numbers of protons (+1 each) and electrons (−1 each), so the charges cancel.
4. 16 − (−2) = **18** electrons.
5. 12 − 10 = **2+** (a magnesium ion, Mg²⁺).
6. Protons = 10 + 3 = **13**, so aluminium.
7. Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil.
8. A very small fraction were deflected back through large angles.
9. Nuclei were heavier than the mass of their protons alone, so an uncharged particle of similar mass had to be present.
10. Electrons = 2 ÷ 1840 = 0.00109; atom = 4.00109; share = **0.0272%**.
11. Both have relative mass 1; the proton has charge +1 and the neutron has charge 0.
12. Losing one electron removes only 1/1840 of a unit from a mass of 23, which is negligible.
