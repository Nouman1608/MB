## Section A

**1.** Name each amide.

**(a)** C₆H₅CONH₂ **[1]**

**(b)** CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃ **[1]**

**2.** State the conditions needed to hydrolyse an amide. **[1]**

**3.** State the reagent that reduces an amide to an amine. **[1]**

**4.** Describe a simple test that shows aqueous ethylamine is basic but aqueous ethanamide is not.
Give the result for each. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** N-methylpropanamide, CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃, is to be made and then hydrolysed.

**(a)** Name the acyl chloride and the amine needed to make it. **[2]**

**(b)** Write the equation for its formation. **[1]**

**(c)** Give the products of heating it under reflux with HCl(aq). **[2]**

**(d)** Give the products of heating it under reflux with NaOH(aq). **[2]**

**6.** Propanamide and N-methylethanamide are isomers with molecular formula C₃H₇NO.

**(a)** Give the products of heating each with NaOH(aq). **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why testing the gas given off with damp red litmus paper would not distinguish them,
and suggest what could. **[2]**

**7.** Two amides are reduced with LiAlH₄.

**(a)** Give the structural formula of the product from benzamide, C₆H₅CONH₂. **[1]**

**(b)** Give the structural formula of the product from N-phenylethanamide, CH₃CONHC₆H₅. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why reduction with LiAlH₄ keeps all the carbon atoms in one molecule, but hydrolysis
does not. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why propylamine is a much stronger base than propanamide. **[3]**

**9.** Amide **B**, C₅H₁₁NO, is heated with NaOH(aq). The products are sodium propanoate and a
primary amine.

**(a)** Give the structural formula of **B**. **[1]**

**(b)** Name the primary amine. **[1]**

**(c)** Give the structural formula of the product of reducing **B** with LiAlH₄. **[1]**

**10.** 2.95 g of ethanamide is heated with excess NaOH(aq) until hydrolysis is complete. Calculate
the volume of ammonia released, measured at room conditions. **[2]**

**11.** Outline a three-step synthesis of propylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, from propanoic acid that
proceeds through an amide. Give the reagents for each step. **[3]**

**12.** Ethanoyl chloride is hydrolysed rapidly by cold water, but ethanamide needs heating under
reflux with acid or alkali. Suggest two reasons for this difference. **[2]**

**13.** Amide **C** is made from benzoyl chloride and a primary amine. **C** has Mᵣ = 135.0.

**(a)** Deduce the structural formula of **C**, showing your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Give the products of heating **C** under reflux with HCl(aq). **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Benzamide [1].

**(b)** N-ethylpropanamide [1].

**2.** Heat under reflux with aqueous acid or aqueous alkali [1].

**3.** LiAlH₄ (lithium tetrahydridoaluminate) [1].

**4.** Test each solution with universal indicator or red litmus paper [1]; ethylamine gives an
alkaline result (blue / pH above 7), ethanamide stays about neutral (pH about 7, red litmus
unchanged) [1].

**5. (a)** Propanoyl chloride [1]; methylamine [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH₂COCl + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + HCl [1].

**(c)** Propanoic acid [1]; methylammonium chloride, CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1].

**(d)** Sodium propanoate [1]; methylamine [1].

**6. (a)** Propanamide: sodium propanoate and ammonia [1]; N-methylethanamide: sodium ethanoate and
methylamine [1].

**(b)** Ammonia and methylamine are both basic gases, so both turn damp red litmus blue [1]; the
carboxylic acids released on acidifying the residues (propanoic acid or ethanoic acid) could be
identified, or the smell of the gas used (methylamine is fishy) [1].

**7. (a)** C₆H₅CH₂NH₂ [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH₂NHC₆H₅ [1].

**(c)** LiAlH₄ only converts the C=O group into CH₂, so no bond in the carbon–nitrogen skeleton
breaks [1]; hydrolysis breaks the C–N bond, splitting the amide into an acid part and a nitrogen part
[1].

**8.** In propylamine the lone pair on nitrogen is localised, and the propyl group donates electron
density to nitrogen [1]; in propanamide the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the C=O group and
the electronegative oxygen withdraws electron density [1]; so the amide lone pair is much less
available to accept a proton [1].

**9. (a)** CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃ [1].

**(b)** Ethylamine [1].

**(c)** CH₃CH₂CH₂NHCH₂CH₃ [1].

**10.** Mᵣ(CH₃CONH₂) = 59.0, so n = 2.95 / 59.0 = 0.0500 mol, and 1 mol NH₃ forms per mol of amide
[1]; volume = 0.0500 × 24.0 = 1.20 dm³ [1].

**11.** Propanoic acid with SOCl₂ (or PCl₅, or PCl₃ with heat) to give propanoyl chloride [1];
then ammonia at room temperature to give propanamide [1]; then LiAlH₄ to reduce it to propylamine
[1].

**12.** Any two [2]: in the amide, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised onto the carbonyl group, so
the carbonyl carbon is less δ+ than in the acyl chloride; chloride is a much better leaving group
than the nitrogen group of an amide; the C–N bond has partial double-bond character, making it
stronger than a single bond.

**13. (a)** The benzoyl part C₆H₅CO– has mass 105.0, and –NH– has mass 15.0, leaving 135.0 − 120.0
= 15.0 for the group on N, which is CH₃ [1]; **C** is C₆H₅CONHCH₃, N-methylbenzamide [1].

**(b)** Benzoic acid [1]; methylammonium chloride, CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the free amine as an acid hydrolysis product instead of its salt.
- Giving the free carboxylic acid as an alkaline hydrolysis product instead of its sodium salt.
- Reducing an N-substituted amide to a primary amine; the substituent on N stays.
- Forgetting heat for hydrolysis, or adding heat to the acyl chloride step.
- Explaining amide basicity without comparing it with an amine.
- Dropping a carbon when splitting an amide: always count the carbons on each side of the C–N bond.
- Treating "fishy smell" or "turns red litmus blue" as proof of ammonia: volatile amines give the same
  litmus result, so use the carboxylic acid and the molecular formula to finish the identification.
- In Mᵣ deductions, forgetting the hydrogen still on nitrogen in an N-substituted amide.
