## What section 9.1 expects

| You must be able to | What it looks like in a question |
|---|---|
| Plot bivariate data on a scatter diagram | "On the grid, complete the scatter diagram" |
| Name the type of correlation | "State the type of correlation shown" |
| Judge its strength | "Describe the correlation" (direction and strength) |
| Interpret it in context | "Interpret the correlation" or "What does the diagram show about…?" |
| Recognise the limits of correlation | "Does this show that x causes y?" |

There is no calculation of a correlation coefficient in 4040. Every judgement is made from the diagram.

## Drawing a scatter diagram

Bivariate data are pairs of values (x, y) measured on the same individual or item, such as the age and value of each car in a sample.

1. **Choose the axes.** Put the variable that might explain or influence the other on the horizontal axis. Age explains value, so age is x and value is y. If neither variable influences the other, either choice is acceptable.
2. **Choose uniform scales** that use most of the grid. The axes need not start at zero; a scale from 40 to 80 is fine if all values lie there, provided the break is clear.
3. **Plot each pair as a single point**, usually a small cross. Do not join the points.
4. **Label both axes** with the variable and units.

Points that repeat are plotted once and can be marked with a small "2" beside them, or the question's convention is followed.

## The vocabulary of correlation

Correlation describes how closely the points follow a straight-line pattern.

| Term | What the diagram shows | Sentence in context |
|---|---|---|
| Positive correlation | Points rise from left to right | "As x increases, y tends to increase" |
| Negative correlation | Points fall from left to right | "As x increases, y tends to decrease" |
| No correlation | No upward or downward pattern | "There is no linear relationship between x and y" |
| Strong | Points lie close to a straight line | Narrow band of points |
| Weak | General direction visible but points widely scattered | Wide band of points |

A full description gives both **strength** and **direction**: "strong negative correlation", not just "negative".

The word **tends** matters. Correlation describes a general pattern, not a rule that holds for every pair. In weak positive correlation it is common for an individual with a higher x to have a lower y than someone else.

### Strength is not steepness

Strength is about how tightly the points cluster round a line, not how steep that line is. A set of points lying almost exactly on a shallow line shows strong correlation; points scattered widely round a steep line show weak correlation. Changing the scale of an axis changes how steep the pattern looks but does not change the correlation at all, which is why steepness cannot be the test.

## Worked example 1: describing and interpreting

A teacher records the hours spent on revision (x) and the test mark (y) for ten students.

| Hours, x | 2 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mark, y | 31 | 38 | 45 | 43 | 52 | 18 | 58 | 61 | 60 | 68 |

Plotted, nine of the points form a narrow band rising from (2, 31) to (12, 68). The point (8, 18) lies far below the rest.

**Describe the correlation.** "Apart from one point, the diagram shows strong positive correlation."

**Interpret it.** "Students who spent more hours revising tended to score higher marks."

**Comment on the unusual point.** "The student who revised for 8 hours but scored 18 is an outlier. The student may have been ill on the day or the mark may have been recorded wrongly. It should be checked before it is used, as it weakens the apparent correlation."

Notice the three separate tasks. Describing uses the vocabulary; interpreting translates it into the context; commenting on an outlier names the point, suggests a reason and says what it does to the pattern.

## Outliers on a scatter diagram

An outlier is a point that does not fit the pattern of the others. It may be:

- a **recording or measuring error**, which should be corrected or removed;
- a **genuine but unusual** individual, which should be kept but mentioned.

A single outlier can make a strong correlation look weak, or create the appearance of correlation in data that have none. When you are asked whether a point should be removed, the answer depends on the reason: remove it only if there is evidence of an error.

## Patterns that are not straight lines

Correlation in this course describes a **linear** relationship. Some data follow a clear curve. The number of daylight hours plotted against the month of the year rises and then falls; the points fit a smooth arch, yet the correlation is close to zero because there is no single upward or downward straight-line trend. The right description is "no linear correlation, but there appears to be a curved relationship".

Data can also contain two separate clusters, for example heights and masses of a mixed group of young children and adults. Each cluster may show little correlation on its own, but together they suggest positive correlation simply because the adults are both taller and heavier. Mentioning the groups shows you have looked at the diagram rather than just its overall direction.

## Correlation and cause

Correlation shows that two variables tend to change together. It does not show that a change in one causes a change in the other. Three explanations other than cause are tested:

1. **A third variable** drives both. Across towns, the number of doctors and the number of road accidents are positively correlated because both increase with the size of the population.
2. **Coincidence.** Two quantities that both grew over the same years, such as mobile phone ownership and the height of a tree in a park, are correlated by time alone.
3. **Cause in the other direction.** A positive correlation between exercise and good health could partly be because healthier people find exercise easier.

A good answer to "Does this show that x causes y?" says no, names a likely third variable or alternative explanation in the context, and explains how it could produce the correlation.

## Worked example 2: an annotated answer

*Question:* Across 15 villages, the number of shops and the number of schools show strong positive correlation. A councillor says that opening more shops will lead to more schools. Comment on this claim.

*Strong answer:* "The claim is not justified. Correlation does not show cause [1]. Both the number of shops and the number of schools are likely to depend on the population of the village: larger villages need more of both [1]. Opening shops would not by itself increase the number of children needing schools [1]."

Each sentence earns its mark by referring to the context. A general statement such as "correlation is not causation" on its own earns at most one mark.

## Common mistakes

- Joining the points or drawing a line when only a scatter diagram is asked for.
- Giving direction without strength, or strength without direction.
- Describing strength by the steepness of the pattern.
- Writing "as x increases, y increases" without "tends to", which claims every pair follows the rule.
- Interpreting without the context words, for example "y goes up" instead of "the mark tends to rise".
- Ignoring an outlier, or removing it without a reason.
- Calling a curved pattern "no relationship".
- Claiming correlation proves cause.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** the type of correlation: two or three words, such as "weak negative correlation".
- **Describe**: strength and direction, plus mention of any outlier or cluster.
- **Interpret**: a sentence in the context using "tend to".
- **Explain** or **Comment** on a claim of cause: reject it, give a third variable or alternative, and link it to the context.
- **Plot**: accurate to half a small square; check the number of crosses equals the number of pairs.
