## Section A

**1.** Define gravitational potential at a point. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the gravitational potential near a planet is always negative. **[2]**

**3.** Mars has mass 6.42 × 10²³ kg and radius 3.39 × 10⁶ m. Calculate the gravitational potential at its surface. **[2]**

**4.** A satellite of mass 600 kg is 2.0 × 10⁷ m from the centre of the Earth.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational potential at this point. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the gravitational potential energy of the satellite and the Earth. **[1]**

**5.** The gravitational potential at the surface of a planet of radius 4.0 × 10⁶ m is –5.0 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹.

**(a)** State the potential at a height of 4.0 × 10⁶ m above the surface. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the work done to raise a 10 kg mass from the surface to this height. **[1]**

**6.** Points A and B are at distances R and 3R from the centre of a planet. Calculate the ratio of potentials ϕ_A/ϕ_B and the ratio of field strengths g_A/g_B. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A rocket carries a payload of mass 250 kg from the Earth's surface to a height of 3.0 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in gravitational potential energy of the payload. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the value given by mgΔh with g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the two answers differ. **[2]**

**8.** A satellite of mass 1200 kg is moved from a circular orbit of radius 6.8 × 10⁶ m to a geostationary orbit of radius 4.22 × 10⁷ m.

**(a)** Show that the kinetic energy of a satellite of mass m in a circular orbit of radius r is GMm/2r. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in gravitational potential energy. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the change in kinetic energy. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the total energy that must be supplied. **[1]**

**9.** A graph shows how the gravitational potential ϕ varies with distance r from the centre of a planet of radius R, for r ≥ R.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the graph. **[2]**

**(b)** At r = 1.0 × 10⁸ m the graph shows ϕ = –4.0 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. Calculate the mass of the planet. **[2]**

**10.** An object is released from rest at a distance 2R from the centre of the Earth, where R is the Earth's radius. Ignoring air resistance, calculate its speed when it reaches the Earth's surface. **[3]**

**11.** The Earth and the Moon (mass 7.35 × 10²² kg) are 3.84 × 10⁸ m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational potential at the midpoint of the line joining their centres. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the potential at the point where the resultant field is zero is not zero. **[1]**

**12.** Two small spheres, of masses 5.0 kg and 2.0 kg, have their centres 0.50 m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational potential energy of the pair. **[2]**

**(b)** State the minimum work needed to separate them to an infinite distance apart. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The work done per unit mass [1] in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point [1].

**2.** The potential is defined as zero at infinity [1]; gravity is attractive, so the field does work on a mass as it comes in from infinity and the work done on the mass by the external agent is negative, giving a negative potential [1].

**3.** ϕ = –GM/R = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.42 × 10²³ ÷ 3.39 × 10⁶ [1] = –1.26 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**4. (a)** ϕ = –GM/r = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁷ = –1.99 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** E_P = mϕ = 600 × (–1.99 × 10⁷) = –1.19 × 10¹⁰ J [1].

**5. (a)** r = 2R, so ϕ is halved: –2.5 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** W = mΔϕ = 10 × (–2.5 × 10⁷ – (–5.0 × 10⁷)) = 2.5 × 10⁸ J [1].

**6.** ϕ ∝ 1/r, so ϕ_A/ϕ_B = 3 [1]; g ∝ 1/r², so g_A/g_B = 9 [1].

**7. (a)** r₁ = 6.37 × 10⁶ m and r₂ = 9.37 × 10⁶ m [1]; ΔE_P = GMm(1/r₁ – 1/r₂) = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 250 × (1/6.37 × 10⁶ – 1/9.37 × 10⁶) [1] = 5.0 × 10⁹ J [1].

**(b)** mgΔh = 250 × 9.81 × 3.0 × 10⁶ = 7.4 × 10⁹ J [1].

**(c)** mgΔh assumes that g is constant at 9.81 N kg⁻¹ [1]; in fact g decreases with height over 3.0 × 10⁶ m, so mgΔh overestimates the energy needed [1].

**8. (a)** Gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so mv² = GMm/r [1]; E_K = ½mv² = GMm/2r [1].

**(b)** ΔE_P = GMm(1/6.8 × 10⁶ – 1/4.22 × 10⁷) [1] = +5.89 × 10¹⁰ J [1].

**(c)** ΔE_K = (GMm/2)(1/4.22 × 10⁷ – 1/6.8 × 10⁶) [1] = –2.95 × 10¹⁰ J [1].

**(d)** 5.89 × 10¹⁰ – 2.95 × 10¹⁰ = 2.9 × 10¹⁰ J [1].

**9. (a)** All values are negative (the curve lies below the r-axis) [1]; ϕ increases towards zero as r increases, steeply near the planet and more gradually further out, never reaching zero [1].

**(b)** M = –ϕr/G = 4.0 × 10⁶ × 1.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ [1] = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg [1].

**10.** Loss of E_P = gain of E_K: ½mv² = GMm(1/R – 1/2R) = GMm/2R [1]; v² = GM/R = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.37 × 10⁶ = 6.25 × 10⁷ m² s⁻² [1]; v = 7.9 × 10³ m s⁻¹ [1].

**11. (a)** Each body is 1.92 × 10⁸ m from the midpoint. ϕ_Earth = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 1.92 × 10⁸ = –2.07 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ [1]; ϕ_Moon = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²² ÷ 1.92 × 10⁸ = –2.6 × 10⁴ J kg⁻¹ [1]; total = –2.10 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** Potential is a scalar and both contributions are negative, so they add rather than cancel; only the field strengths (vectors) cancel at that point [1].

**12. (a)** E_P = –Gm₁m₂/r = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.0 × 2.0 ÷ 0.50 [1] = –1.3 × 10⁻⁹ J [1].

**(b)** 1.3 × 10⁻⁹ J [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the minus sign, or giving a positive potential.
- Using 1/r² in potential calculations.
- Forgetting "per unit mass" or "from infinity" in the definition.
- Using mgΔh for large height changes.
- Ignoring the change in kinetic energy when a question asks for the total energy needed to change orbit.
- Treating potentials from two bodies as vectors.
