This guide covers subtopic **24.2, Standard electrode potentials E⦵, standard cell potentials
E⦵<sub>cell</sub> and the Nernst equation**, from Topic 24, Electrochemistry, of Cambridge
International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Electrochemistry: Electrolysis and Electrode Potentials guide](/resources/a-electrochemistry-electrolysis-and-electrode-potentials/)
introduces the ideas; this page sets out each of the ten learning outcomes in turn, with new
worked examples on polarity, feasibility at the borderline, constructing equations and
non-standard concentrations.

## What the syllabus asks for

Candidates should be able to: define standard electrode (reduction) potential and standard cell
potential; describe the standard hydrogen electrode; describe how to measure E⦵ for metals or
non-metals in contact with their ions, and for ions of the same element in different oxidation
states; calculate E⦵<sub>cell</sub>; use it to deduce electrode polarity, the direction of
electron flow and feasibility; deduce the relative strength of oxidising and reducing agents;
construct redox equations from half-equations; predict qualitatively and, with the Nernst
equation, quantitatively how E changes with concentration; and use ΔG⦵ = −nE⦵<sub>cell</sub>F.

## Definitions

**Standard electrode (reduction) potential, E⦵:** the potential difference of a half-cell
**relative to the standard hydrogen electrode**, measured under **standard conditions**: 298 K,
1.00 mol dm⁻³ concentrations of the aqueous ions, and 101 kPa pressure for any gases. Values are
quoted for the half-equation written as a **reduction**, for example
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s), E⦵ = +0.34 V.

**Standard cell potential, E⦵<sub>cell</sub>:** the potential difference between two half-cells
under standard conditions, when no current flows:

E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = E⦵(more positive half-cell) − E⦵(less positive half-cell)

The "no current" condition is why a high-resistance voltmeter is used: if current flowed, the
concentrations would change and the reading would fall.

## The standard hydrogen electrode

The reference half-cell, assigned E⦵ = 0.00 V, consists of:

- hydrogen gas at **101 kPa** bubbled over a **platinum electrode** (coated with finely divided
  platinum to give a large surface area);
- the platinum dipping into a solution of **H⁺(aq) at 1.00 mol dm⁻³** (for example 1.00 mol dm⁻³
  HCl);
- a temperature of **298 K**.

Platinum is used because it is inert and conducts, and it provides a surface on which the
equilibrium 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) is set up.

## Measuring standard electrode potentials

Every measurement connects the test half-cell to the hydrogen electrode with a **salt bridge**
(filter paper soaked in saturated aqueous potassium nitrate) and a **high-resistance voltmeter**.
The salt bridge completes the circuit by allowing ions to move without the two solutions mixing.
What changes is the test half-cell.

**(a) A metal in contact with its ions:** a strip of the clean metal dipping into a
1.00 mol dm⁻³ solution of its ions, such as zinc in Zn²⁺(aq).

**(a) A non-metal in contact with its ions:** the non-metal cannot be the electrode, so a
platinum electrode is used. For Cl₂/Cl⁻, chlorine gas at 101 kPa is bubbled over platinum in
1.00 mol dm⁻³ Cl⁻(aq).

**(b) Ions of the same element in different oxidation states:** a platinum electrode dips into
a solution containing **both** ions at **1.00 mol dm⁻³ each**, for example Fe³⁺(aq) and
Fe²⁺(aq). If H⁺ appears in the half-equation, as for MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺, [H⁺] must also be
1.00 mol dm⁻³.

## Polarity, electron flow and the cell reaction

The half-cell with the **more positive** E⦵ is the **positive electrode**; reduction happens
there. The half-cell with the **less positive** E⦵ is the **negative electrode**; oxidation
happens there. **Electrons flow through the external circuit from the negative electrode to the
positive electrode.**

### Worked example 1

A cell is made from Mg²⁺/Mg (E⦵ = −2.38 V) and Ni²⁺/Ni (E⦵ = −0.25 V).

- E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = −0.25 − (−2.38) = **+2.13 V**
- Positive electrode: nickel. Negative electrode: magnesium.
- Electrons flow through the wire from magnesium to nickel.
- Reactions: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (negative electrode); Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni (positive electrode).
- Overall: Mg(s) + Ni²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Ni(s)

Notice that both E⦵ values are negative; polarity depends only on which is **more** positive.

## Oxidising and reducing agents from E⦵

Write all half-equations as reductions, ordered by E⦵:

- the species on the **left** of the half-equation with the **most positive** E⦵ is the
  **strongest oxidising agent** (it is most easily reduced);
- the species on the **right** of the half-equation with the **most negative** E⦵ is the
  **strongest reducing agent** (it is most easily oxidised).

From MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ (+1.52 V), Cl₂/Cl⁻ (+1.36 V), Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77 V) and I₂/I⁻ (+0.54 V): MnO₄⁻ is
the strongest oxidising agent of these, and I⁻ the strongest reducing agent.

## Feasibility and constructing equations

A reaction is **feasible** under standard conditions if E⦵<sub>cell</sub>, calculated as
E⦵(half-cell where reduction would occur) − E⦵(half-cell where oxidation would occur), is
**positive**.

### Worked example 2

Can acidified dichromate(VI) oxidise bromide ions? Chloride ions?

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ ⇌ 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O, E⦵ = +1.33 V

Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻, E⦵ = +1.07 V; Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻, E⦵ = +1.36 V

- Bromide: E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 1.33 − 1.07 = **+0.26 V**, feasible.
- Chloride: E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 1.33 − 1.36 = **−0.03 V**, not feasible under standard
  conditions.

To construct the bromide equation, multiply the bromide half-equation by 3 so that 6 electrons
are lost and gained, then add and cancel:

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Br⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 3Br₂ + 7H₂O

Check: charges are (−2 + 14 − 6) = +6 on the left and 2 × (+3) = +6 on the right.

Two limits on E⦵ predictions: a feasible reaction may be **too slow** to observe (high
activation energy), and real conditions are often **not standard**. With chloride the margin is
only 0.03 V, so a change in concentration could reverse the conclusion.

## How concentration changes E

**Qualitatively:** treat the half-equation as an equilibrium. Increasing the concentration of
the oxidised species (left side) shifts it to the right, so E becomes **more positive**.
Increasing the reduced species (or lowering the oxidised species) makes E **less positive**.

**Quantitatively**, the Nernst equation:

E = E⦵ + (0.059 ÷ z) log([oxidised species] ÷ [reduced species])

where z is the number of electrons in the half-equation. A solid metal does not appear in the
concentration term.

### Worked example 3

(a) Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, E⦵ = +0.77 V, with [Fe³⁺] = 0.100 and [Fe²⁺] = 0.0100 mol dm⁻³:

E = 0.77 + (0.059 ÷ 1) log(0.100 ÷ 0.0100) = 0.77 + 0.059 = **+0.829 V**

(b) Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag, E⦵ = +0.80 V, with [Ag⁺] = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³:

E = 0.80 + 0.059 log(0.0500) = 0.80 − 0.077 = **+0.723 V**

Joined to a standard Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell (+0.34 V), this silver half-cell gives
E<sub>cell</sub> = 0.723 − 0.34 = **+0.38 V**, less than the standard value of +0.46 V.

## Linking E⦵<sub>cell</sub> to ΔG⦵

ΔG⦵ = −nE⦵<sub>cell</sub>F

where n is the number of electrons transferred in the balanced overall equation and
F = 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹. With E in volts, ΔG comes out in J mol⁻¹.

For the cell in Worked example 1 (n = 2):
ΔG⦵ = −2 × 2.13 × 96 500 = −411 000 J mol⁻¹ = **−411 kJ mol⁻¹**

A positive E⦵<sub>cell</sub> always gives a negative ΔG⦵, so the two feasibility tests agree.

## Common mistakes

- Subtracting the wrong way round, or reversing the sign of an E⦵ when a half-equation is
  reversed and then subtracting as well.
- Multiplying E⦵ when a half-equation is multiplied; E⦵ does not change with stoichiometry.
- Describing the hydrogen electrode without all three standard conditions.
- Using a metal electrode for Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺; it must be platinum.
- Putting the solid metal into the Nernst ratio.
- Using z from the overall equation in the Nernst equation, or n from a single half-equation in
  ΔG = −nEF when the overall equation transfers more.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: include "relative to the standard hydrogen electrode" and all standard conditions.
- **Describe** apparatus: name the electrode material, solutions and concentrations, salt bridge
  and voltmeter type.
- **Deduce** polarity: state which electrode is positive, then the direction of electron flow
  in the external circuit.
- **Predict** feasibility: show the E⦵<sub>cell</sub> calculation and state the sign.
- **Construct** equations: balance electrons first and check the charge balance.
