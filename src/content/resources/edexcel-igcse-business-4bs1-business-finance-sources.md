This guide goes deeper into section 3.1 "Business finance – sources" of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. The section has three points: 3.1.1 the need for finance (short-term needs, long-term needs, to start up or expand); 3.1.2 internal sources (personal savings, retained profit, selling assets); and 3.1.3 external sources (overdraft and trade payables; loan capital, share capital, including stock market flotation for public limited companies; venture capital; crowdfunding). The [sources of finance and cash flow study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-sources-of-business-finance-and-cash-flow-forecasting/) describes each source; this page is about **choosing** between them and using the numbers a question gives you.

## Step one: identify the need

Before naming any source, classify the need using the three headings in 3.1.1.

| Need | Typical examples | What the finance must do |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term | Paying for inventory before a busy season; covering wages while waiting for customers to pay | Be quick to arrange and easy to repay within months |
| Long-term | Buying premises, machinery or vehicles | Last for years, ideally as long as the asset is used |
| Start-up | Equipment, first stock, deposit on premises before any revenue | Come from sources available without a trading record |
| Expansion | A second branch, a new product line, a larger factory | Often large sums; may come from sources linked to past profits |

Start-up is the hardest case. A new business has no retained profit and no trading record, so lenders see it as risky. That is why personal savings, family money, crowdfunding and venture capital are so common at this stage.

## Step two: check which sources are open to this business

Not every source is available to every business.

- **Retained profit** needs a profitable trading history. A start-up has none.
- **Selling assets** needs spare assets. Selling a machine still in use would harm production.
- **Share capital** is available only to companies. A sole trader or partnership must first become a limited company.
- **Stock market flotation** is available only to public limited companies, because only their shares can be sold to the general public.
- **Trade payables** depend on suppliers being willing to give credit, which is easier for an established customer.

An answer that recommends share capital for a sole trader without mentioning a change of legal form misses this point.

## Step three: weigh the five criteria

When comparing sources, five questions do most of the work. The criteria are standard teaching rather than a specification list.

1. **Cost.** Loans and overdrafts charge interest. Shares and venture capital have no interest, but owners give up part of future profits.
2. **Amount.** Personal savings and crowdfunding usually raise smaller sums; flotation can raise very large sums.
3. **Time.** Is the money needed for weeks or for years? Match short-term needs to short-term sources.
4. **Control.** Share capital and venture capital bring new owners who can influence decisions. Loans do not.
5. **Risk.** Loan repayments must be made even in a bad month, and lenders may ask for security. Personal savings put the owner's own money at risk.

## Internal sources: the trade-offs in detail

- **Personal savings.** No interest and no loss of control, and lenders see the owner's commitment, which can make a loan easier to obtain alongside it. Limit: few owners have large savings, and losing them could affect the owner's household.
- **Retained profit.** The cheapest source for an established business. Its opportunity cost is that profit kept in the business is not paid to owners, so in a company shareholders receive smaller dividends.
- **Selling assets.** Raises cash without borrowing. It suits assets that are no longer needed, such as an unused warehouse. If assets must be sold quickly, the price may be low.

## External sources: using the figures

### Worked example 1: the cost of a loan

Okafor Engineering borrows 30,000 for a new lathe. The bank's offer is 36 monthly repayments, with total repayments of 34,500.

- Interest cost = 34,500 − 30,000 = **4,500**.
- As a percentage of the amount borrowed: 4,500 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **15%** over the three years.
- Monthly repayment = 34,500 ÷ 36 = **958.33**.

The monthly figure matters most for a small business: it is a fixed cash outflow for three years, whatever sales are. The lathe should last longer than the loan, so the finance matches the long-term need.

### Worked example 2: what share capital costs in control

Wren & Ashby Ltd has 10,000 shares, all owned by the two founders. To fund expansion it issues 2,500 new shares to an investor for 50,000 (20 per share).

- Total shares after the issue: 10,000 + 2,500 = 12,500.
- Investor's share of ownership: 2,500 ÷ 12,500 × 100 = **20%**.
- Founders together now own 80%.

There is no interest and no repayment date, but 20% of future dividends now go to the investor, and the investor can vote at meetings. The founders keep control as long as they hold more than half the shares.

### Worked example 3: reading a venture capital offer

A venture capitalist offers Lumo Apps 200,000 in return for 40% of the business.

- Implied value of the whole business: 200,000 ÷ 0.40 = **500,000**.
- The founders keep 60%.

Venture capital suits a young business with high growth potential that banks consider too risky. The founders gain money and the investor's advice and contacts, but the investor expects a high return and may want a say in major decisions.

### Overdraft and trade payables

An overdraft charges interest only on the amount overdrawn. If Okafor is overdrawn by an average of 4,000 for 2 months at 1.5% a month, interest is 4,000 × 0.015 × 2 = **120**. That is cheap for a short gap, but the same rate over years would be expensive, and the bank can ask for repayment at short notice.

Trade payables are a supplier's credit: goods now, payment later. If a supplier extends credit from 30 to 60 days, the business keeps its cash for an extra month at no interest. The risk is that late payment beyond agreed terms damages the relationship, and suppliers may withdraw credit.

### Crowdfunding: the target matters

Pell Street Brewery sets a crowdfunding target of 18,000. It attracts 250 backers who pledge 40 each: 250 × 40 = 10,000, which is 8,000 short. On a scheme where the business only receives the money if the target is met, it receives nothing. Crowdfunding tests demand and builds a following, but it is uncertain and public: a failed campaign is visible to everyone, including competitors.

### Flotation

When a public limited company sells shares through a stock market, it can raise very large sums for major expansion. It also brings costs and reporting requirements, spreads ownership widely, and allows anyone to buy shares, so the original owners may lose control and a takeover becomes possible.

## Annotated model answer

*Asha runs a florist shop as a sole trader. She has 4,000 in savings and needs 32,000 to open a second shop. She wants to stay in control. Justify whether she should use a bank loan or venture capital.*

> A bank loan would let Asha keep full control, which she says she wants, because the bank does not become an owner. The shop is already trading, so she can show the bank her past sales, and her 4,000 savings show commitment. However, she must make fixed repayments plus interest even if the new shop starts slowly, which adds risk while she has unlimited liability. Venture capital needs no repayments, but an investor would take part of the business and venture capitalists usually look for fast-growing firms, which a second florist shop may not be. I recommend the bank loan, because keeping control is Asha's priority and an established shop with savings to contribute is a reasonable borrower.

Why it works: it starts from the owner's stated priority, uses facts from the case (trading record, savings, sole trader), gives a drawback of each option and ends with a decision tied to the case.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** and **State** questions often ask you to pick the source mentioned in the case; quote it.
- **Explain** one advantage of a source: statement plus two expansion points, for example "retained profit has no interest, so costs are lower, so more profit remains for growth".
- **Justify** needs a recommendation between two options using the case. **Evaluate** needs a supported conclusion, often with "it depends on".
- Always match the length of the finance to the length of the need.

## Common mistakes

- Recommending retained profit for a start-up.
- Suggesting flotation for a private limited company without saying it must become a plc.
- Treating share capital as a loan that must be repaid.
- Forgetting that the main cost of share capital is shared profit and control, not interest.
- Choosing an overdraft for a long-term need.
