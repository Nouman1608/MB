## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the business entity concept and give **one** example of it being applied. **[2]**

**2.** Name the accounting concept applied in each case.

**(a)** Depreciation is charged on a delivery van over its useful life. **[1]**

**(b)** An allowance for irrecoverable debts is created. **[1]**

**(c)** The same depreciation method is used every year. **[1]**

**3.** A business records revenue when goods are delivered, not when the customer's order is received. Which concept is being applied?

A: matching

B: prudence

C: realisation

D: consistency

**[1]**

**4.** Explain the money measurement concept, using an example. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the materiality concept depends on the size of a business. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the going concern concept. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Okoro Electricals has a draft profit of $52,700 for the year ended 31 December 2028. The following items have been found:

- the owner's son's school fees of $2,400 were paid by the business and charged to wages
- goods sold for $3,100 were recorded as revenue in December but delivered to the customer on 2 January 2029; the goods were included in closing inventory at cost
- insurance of $1,800 was paid on 1 October 2028 for 12 months and charged in full to 2028.

**(a)** Name the accounting concept that applies to each item. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[4]**

**8.** Ridgeway Florists has a draft profit of $28,900 for the year ended 30 June 2029. The following items have been found:

- vases in closing inventory cost $1,900 but can be sold for only $1,150
- a delivery van bought for $15,000 was charged to motor expenses; vans are depreciated at 25% a year on the reducing balance, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase
- commission of $600 earned in June 2029 will not be received until August and has not been recorded.

**(a)** Name the accounting concept that applies to each item. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[4]**

**(c)** State how the van will appear in the statement of financial position at 30 June 2029. **[2]**

**9.** A gift shop's closing inventory includes two lines.

| Line | Cost | Net realisable value |
|---|---|---|
| Candles | 4,000 | 5,200 |
| Picture frames | 2,500 | 1,700 |

**(a)** Calculate the value of closing inventory. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain, naming the concepts involved, why the candles are not valued at $5,200 but the frames are valued at $1,700. **[4]**

**10.** Fenby Crafts will close in three months. Its machinery has a book value of $18,000 but is expected to sell for $7,500. Its inventory cost $9,000 and is expected to raise $6,000 in a closing-down sale.

**(a)** Explain which accounting concept no longer applies. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total reduction in asset values if expected sale values are used. **[2]**

**11.** A business's profit before depreciation is $50,000 in year 2. On its usual straight line method, depreciation would be $12,000. On the reducing balance method it would be $9,000. The owner wants to change methods this year only.

**(a)** Calculate the profit for year 2 under each method. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the consistency concept matters to users of the accounts in this situation. **[3]**

**12.** A small shop with annual profit of $12,000 and a large company with annual profit of $2,000,000 each buy a printer for $400, expected to last four years. Discuss how each business might treat the printer, referring to the materiality concept. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The business is treated as separate from its owner, so only business transactions are recorded in its books [1]. Example: the owner's private spending paid by the business is recorded as drawings, not as an expense [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Matching / accruals (also accept going concern) [1].

**(b)** Prudence [1].

**(c)** Consistency [1]. **[3]**

**3.** C [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Only items that can be measured reliably in money are recorded [1]. For example, the skill of the workforce or a good reputation affects the business but is not recorded because it cannot be valued reliably [1]. **[2]**

**5.** An item is material if it would affect users' decisions [1]. The same amount, such as $500, may be significant to a small business but too small to matter to a large one, so the limit depends on the size of the business [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The business is assumed to continue trading for the foreseeable future [1], so assets are recorded at cost less depreciation and spread over their useful lives instead of at the amount they would raise if sold now [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** School fees: business entity [1]. Goods delivered in January: realisation [1]. Insurance: matching / accruals [1].

**(b)** Draft 52,700 + 2,400 school fees [1] - 3,100 revenue [1] + 1,350 insurance prepaid (1,800 x 9/12) [1] = **$53,350** [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* The policy runs from October 2028 to September 2029, so nine of its twelve months belong to 2029.

**8. (a)** Vases: prudence [1]. Van: matching / accruals (capital expenditure spread over the van's life) [1]. Commission: matching / accruals [1].

**(b)** Draft 28,900 - 750 inventory [1] + 15,000 - 3,750 depreciation = + 11,250 [1] + 600 commission [1] = **$40,000** [1].

**(c)** Non-current asset: van at cost $15,000 less accumulated depreciation $3,750 [1], net book value $11,250 [1]. **[9]**

**9. (a)** Candles at cost $4,000; frames at net realisable value $1,700 [1]. Total **$5,700** [1].

**(b)** Prudence requires that assets and profit are not overstated, so expected losses are recognised now [1]; the frames are written down to $1,700, recognising the $800 loss [1]. The candles' expected gain of $1,200 is not recorded [1], because realisation recognises revenue only when goods are sold to a customer [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Going concern [1]: the business will not continue, so assets should be shown at what they are expected to raise, not at cost less depreciation [1].

**(b)** Machinery 18,000 - 7,500 = 10,500; inventory 9,000 - 6,000 = 3,000 [1]. Total reduction **$13,500** [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Straight line: 50,000 - 12,000 = **$38,000** [1]. Reducing balance: 50,000 - 9,000 = **$41,000** [1].

**(b)** Changing method would raise profit by $3,000 with no change in trading [1]. Users comparing year 2 with year 1 would think performance had improved [1]. Consistency keeps the same method so that changes in profit reflect changes in trading, making comparisons reliable [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the small shop, $400 is 3.33% of profit, which may be large enough to matter [1], so it may record the printer as a non-current asset and depreciate it over four years [1]. For the large company, $400 is 0.02% of profit, too small to affect any user's decision [1], so it is likely to charge the printer as an expense in the year of purchase under its materiality limit [1]. **[4]**
