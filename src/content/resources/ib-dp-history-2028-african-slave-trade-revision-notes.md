**HL only.** These notes condense Regional study 3 of the Africa and the Middle East option (IB DP History, first assessment 2028), assessed in Paper 3 through two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The topic covers both the Atlantic and the East African trades. Full explanation is in the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-african-slave-trade/).

## The six inquiry topics

| Inquiry topic | Core idea |
|---|---|
| Expansion of the Atlantic trade | Plantation demand, European shipping, African suppliers, wars |
| Expansion of East African slavery | Omani and Zanzibari power, cloves, a shift away from the British-patrolled Atlantic |
| Impact of slavery in Africa | Social loss, mixed economic effects, population effects, some states grow |
| Resistance | Everyday, rebellions, escape, legal and political action |
| Economic reasons for decline | Industrialisation, legitimate commerce, labour needs, productivity |
| Reasons for abolition | Ideas, campaigns, law, colonial expansion |

## Key dates

- 1400s: Portuguese begin taking captives from Africa.
- 1526: Afonso I of Kongo writes to Portugal to protest.
- 1700s: peak of the Atlantic trade.
- 1787: Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade founded in Britain.
- 1791 to 1804: Haitian Revolution.
- 1807: British Slave Trade Act. 1808: US import ban.
- 1824: British Slave Trade Act strengthens enforcement.
- 1833: abolition of slavery in the British Empire (in force 1834).
- 1840s: Zanzibar becomes the centre of Omani rule.
- 1873: Zanzibar slave market closed. 1897: legal status of slavery ended in Zanzibar.
- 1888: slavery ends in Brazil.

## Atlantic trade: reasons for expansion

- **Maritime commerce:** better ships, trading companies, major European ports.
- **Plantations:** sugar, tobacco, coffee, cotton, rice; Indigenous labour collapsed; indentured labour limited.
- **African response:** rulers and merchants controlled supply; imports of guns, cloth, cowries, metals; states such as Dahomey, Oyo and Asante.
- **Warfare:** raids and wars produced captives; the "guns for slaves" cycle is debated.

## East Africa

- Existing Indian Ocean trade to Arabia, Persia and India.
- Omani expansion; Sayyid Said's move to Zanzibar.
- Clove plantations on Zanzibar and Pemba.
- Traders like Tippu Tip linking inland to coast.
- Pressure on Atlantic routes after 1807 pushed traders east.

## Impact in Africa

| Area | Points |
|---|---|
| Social | Families broken; fear; more women than men in some regions; more domestic slavery; militarisation |
| Economic | Imports undercut crafts; elite wealth; resources diverted; long-term effect debated |
| Demographic | Population held back, especially West Central Africa and the Bight of Benin |
| Political | Dahomey, Oyo, Asante, Niger Delta city-states gain, but dependent on the trade |

## Resistance

- **Day-to-day:** slowdowns, sabotage, religion, language, family ties.
- **Rebellions:** *Amistad* (1839), *Creole* (1841), Tacky's War (1760), Baptist War (1831), Haiti (1791 to 1804).
- **Escape:** maroons in Jamaica, Suriname, Brazil (Palmares).
- **Legal and political resistance in Africa:** Afonso I's letters; Abdul Kader of Futa Toro banning export of Muslims.

## Decline and abolition

**Economic reasons.** Industrialisation, palm oil and groundnuts, labour needs inside Africa, argued lower productivity of slave labour.

**Perspectives.** Williams (1944): the trade was ended mainly because it was no longer profitable. Drescher: still profitable in 1807, so abolition needed a moral and political movement. A middle position: economic change opened the way but the campaign made it law.

**Abolition factors.** Enlightenment and religious ideas; Clarkson, Wilberforce, Equiano; petitions and boycotts; the 1807 and 1824 Acts; naval patrols; later colonial rule used antislavery as a justification.

## Argument table

| Claim | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| African rulers were equal partners in the trade | They controlled supply and set terms | Europeans created the demand and brought violence and debt |
| The trade damaged Africa in the long term | Population loss, insecurity | Evidence is hard to measure; some states grew |
| Abolition was mainly moral | Mass campaigns | Economic and strategic interests |
| Abolition ended slavery | Fewer Atlantic voyages after 1807 | Slavery continued in Brazil, Cuba, Zanzibar and inside Africa |

## Worked example

*Question:* "Evaluate the view that resistance by enslaved people was the most important factor in ending the slave trade."

- **Define resistance:** day-to-day, rebellions and escape. State that you will weigh it against economic change and the abolitionist movement.
- **For:** Haiti, which made slavery seem unsafe to planters and inspired others; the testimony of Equiano; maroon communities proving resistance.
- **Against:** British parliamentary action in 1807 followed political campaigns; economic change shaped the choices.
- **Judgement:** resistance changed the climate and sometimes the law, but the trade ended through a combination of factors, with legislation and naval enforcement decisive in the Atlantic.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the trade as only transatlantic.
- Ignoring African agency or exaggerating it.
- Quoting numbers without "about".
- Stating that abolition in 1807 ended slavery.

## Self-test

1. Name two goods Africans received in exchange for captives.
2. Which sultanate expanded clove production using enslaved labour?
3. Which two years do the guide's British Slave Trade Acts carry?
4. Who wrote *Capitalism and Slavery*?
5. Give one example of a maroon community.
6. What did "legitimate commerce" mean?

**Answers:** 1. Any two of cloth, guns, metal goods, alcohol, cowrie shells. 2. Zanzibar. 3. 1807 and 1824. 4. Eric Williams. 5. Palmares in Brazil, or maroons in Jamaica or Suriname. 6. Trade in goods such as palm oil and groundnuts instead of people.

## Key terms

- **Middle Passage:** the sea crossing of the Atlantic trade, in which captives were held in crowded ships and many died.
- **Maroons:** communities formed by people who escaped slavery.
- **Legitimate commerce:** trade in goods such as palm oil and groundnuts, promoted as a replacement for the trade in people.
- **West Africa Squadron:** the British naval patrol from 1808 that stopped slave ships.
- **Abolition:** the legal ending of the trade (1807 in Britain) or of slavery itself (1833 in the British Empire).
- **Plantation complex:** the system of large farms, forced labour and export crops in the Americas and, later, in parts of Africa.
