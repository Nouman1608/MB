This guide covers **3.3.2 Original writing** in the AQA A-level English Language (7702) non-exam assessment: a
750-word piece of original writing (25 marks) and a 750-word commentary (25 marks). The
[Language in Action NEA guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-nea-language-in-action/) lists the areas and
example forms. This page concentrates on the process: reading a style model as a writer, turning that analysis into
craft choices, and writing a commentary that evaluates rather than narrates.

## What the specification requires

You produce one piece of original writing in one of three areas, **The Power of Persuasion**, **The Power of
Storytelling** or **The Power of Information**, with one accompanying commentary. In preparation, you study a range
of style models, then select and analyse **one style model in detail**, chosen in consultation with your supervising
teacher. The commentary lets you "consider and evaluate the style model, the writing process and the effectiveness
of the final piece of writing". The folder contains the original writing, an **annotated style model**, the
**reflective commentary** and **references**.

## What each "power" asks of a writer

| Area | What the writing must do | Language choices that typically matter |
|---|---|---|
| Persuasion | Change what a reader or listener thinks or does | Address and pronouns, rhetorical patterning, modality, evaluative lexis, evidence and anecdote, structure building to a call to action |
| Storytelling | Engage a reader in characters, events and experience | Narrative voice and viewpoint, tense, dialogue, pace through sentence structure, sensory lexis, structural shape (openings, turning points, endings) |
| Information | Inform an audience clearly and engagingly | Organisation and signposting, register suited to expertise, precise lexis, explanation through example, headings and layout |

Choose a form you can realise in 750 words: a speech, a short story, a feature article, a blog, a local history
piece, a dramatic monologue. Define the **audience** and **purpose** precisely ("readers of a regional magazine who
walk but have never visited the coast path"), because every later choice depends on them.

## Choosing a style model

A good style model is a **published text in the same form and area** as your planned piece, short enough to analyse
closely, and rich enough to teach you techniques. Read several first, then select the one that best matches your
audience and purpose. Your style model should not be one whose content you plan to borrow: you borrow technique, not
material.

## Annotating the style model at the language levels

Annotation is analysis, not highlighting. For each feature, note what it is and what it does.

| Level | Questions to ask of the style model |
|---|---|
| Graphology | How do headings, paragraph lengths, white space and images guide the reader? |
| Phonology | Does the text use sound patterning, rhythm or patterns written to be read aloud? |
| Lexis and semantics | Which semantic fields, register and evaluative words create its tone? |
| Grammar | What sentence types and lengths, tenses, voices and clause patterns recur, and where? |
| Pragmatics | What does it assume the reader knows? How does it handle politeness and implication? |
| Discourse | How is it structured: opening hook, development, turning point, close? How is cohesion created? |

## From analysis to writing

Turn each annotation into a craft decision for your piece:

```
Style model feature                     Decision for my piece
Opens with a one-line anecdote          Open my speech with a 2-sentence anecdote
in present tense                        about my grandfather, in present tense
Triadic structures at key points        Use three triples, saved for the three
                                        main turns in the argument
Shifts from "I" to "we" in the          Move from personal to collective address
final third                             before the call to action
```

Then **adapt**: your audience and purpose are not identical to the style model's, so some features must change. The
commentary rewards explaining these adaptations.

## Drafting and redrafting

The specification's preparation list includes planning, drafting and redrafting. Keep earlier drafts and note *why*
you changed things, in linguistic terms: "cut three adjectives from the opening noun phrase to quicken pace", "moved
the statistic from paragraph four to the opening to establish credibility", "changed passive to active to make the
council responsible". Those notes become commentary material.

## Writing the commentary

The commentary is **analytical and evaluative**. A reliable structure:

1. **Context**: form, audience, purpose and area, briefly.
2. **The style model**: the key techniques you identified, with terminology.
3. **Your choices**: what you adopted, what you adapted and why, linked to audience and purpose.
4. **The writing process**: significant redrafting decisions and their reasons.
5. **Evaluation**: how effectively the piece achieves its purpose, including honest limitations.

Keep connections between the two texts running throughout, rather than describing the style model and then your own
piece separately.

**Model commentary paragraph** (for a persuasive speech to a town council on keeping a library open): *"My style
model, a campaign speech, delayed its main claim until after a personal anecdote, positioning listeners as
witnesses before asking them to judge. I adopted this structure, opening with a present-tense account of a child
choosing her first library book, so that councillors would picture the service before hearing the argument.
However, the style model addressed a sympathetic rally; my audience was a council weighing costs, so I replaced its
emotive hyperbole with hedged, evidence-led declaratives ('Our figures suggest...'), and kept second-person address
for the closing appeal only. On redrafting, I changed 'the library will be closed' to 'you will close the library',
replacing an agentless passive with an active clause to make responsibility explicit. This was the most effective
choice in the piece, although the change of tone in the final paragraph may feel abrupt."*

The paragraph connects style model and piece, uses terminology, explains choices through audience, records a
redrafting decision and evaluates effectiveness.

## How the two parts are assessed

The original writing is assessed on expertise and creativity in using English (AO5): handling the form, controlling
register, positioning the audience, and writing accurately and cohesively. The commentary draws on all the
assessment objectives, including connections between your writing and the style model (AO4), which is why a
commentary that discusses only one of the two texts limits its own marks. Write the commentary with both texts in
view throughout.

## Common mistakes

- A style model in a different form from the piece, so few techniques transfer.
- Annotations that only label features ("metaphor", "list").
- A piece with no clear audience, or a form that cannot work in 750 words.
- Borrowing the style model's content or phrasing too closely.
- A commentary that narrates the process ("First I planned...") with little analysis.
- Discussing only your own piece and not the style model.
- Claiming every choice worked, with no honest evaluation.
