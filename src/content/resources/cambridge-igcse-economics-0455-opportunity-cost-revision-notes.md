These notes condense section 1.3 of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: 1.3.1 the definition of opportunity cost and 1.3.2 its influence on decisions by consumers, workers, producers and governments. All figures are invented. Full explanations are in the [opportunity cost study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-opportunity-cost/); test yourself afterwards with the [opportunity cost practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-opportunity-cost-practice/). The combined unit on [opportunity cost and production possibility curves](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-opportunity-cost-and-production-possibility-curves/) links the idea to PPC diagrams.

## 1.3.1 Core recall

- **Definition:** opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
- **Why it exists:** scarcity. Limited resources and unlimited wants force choices, and every choice gives something up.
- **Free goods** (not scarce, such as air) have zero opportunity cost. **Economic goods** (scarce) always have one.
- It is measured in what is given up: goods, time, earnings, interest, land use or public projects. It need not be money.
- It is not the money price. A free ticket still costs the time; a fee-free state school still uses resources society could have used elsewhere.

### Three checks before writing an answer

1. Have I named only one option, the one ranked second?
2. Is that option genuinely available to this decision maker?
3. Have I stated the unit (chairs, hours, schools, interest)?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made |
| Scarcity | Wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them |
| Choice | Selecting one use of scarce resources over others |
| Free good | A good with no opportunity cost because it is not scarce |
| Economic good | A scarce good that has an opportunity cost |
| Interest forgone | Interest that savings would have earned if not spent |
| Earnings forgone | Pay given up by choosing leisure, study or another job |
| Sunk cost | Money already spent that cannot be recovered; not part of a future opportunity cost |

## Calculation methods

**Cost of one product in terms of another.** Divide the quantity given up by the quantity gained.

```
A farm can grow 300 sacks of rice or 100 sacks of beans
Opportunity cost of 1 sack of beans = 300 / 100 = 3 sacks of rice
Opportunity cost of 1 sack of rice = 100 / 300 = 0.33 sacks of beans
```

**Earnings forgone.**

```
Leisure of 5 hours at a wage of 15 an hour
Opportunity cost = 5 x 15 = 75 of earnings
```

**Interest forgone.**

```
Savings of 2,500 spent; interest rate 3% a year
Interest forgone in one year = 2,500 x 0.03 = 75
```

**Public projects.**

```
A bridge costs 48 million; a clinic costs 8 million
The bridge uses 48 / 8 = 6 clinics' worth of funds
```

## 1.3.2 Decision makers

| Decision maker | Typical choice | Opportunity cost example | How it affects the decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Spend or save; product X or Y | The next best purchase, or interest forgone | If the alternative becomes cheaper or more attractive, the consumer may switch |
| Worker | Job A or B; work or leisure; work or study | Earnings forgone, or the satisfaction of leisure forgone | A higher wage raises the cost of leisure, so more hours may be worked |
| Producer | What to make with given factors; how to use profit | Profit from the next best product; rent from premises | The firm moves resources to the use that earns more than its opportunity cost |
| Government | Which project to fund; tax cut or spending | The next best project or service | It funds a project if its benefit to society exceeds that of the alternative |

### Decision rule in four steps

1. List the available options.
2. Rank them by benefit to the decision maker.
3. Identify the second-ranked option: that is the opportunity cost.
4. Choose the first option only if its benefit exceeds that opportunity cost.

## Limits (for "Discuss" answers)

- Non-money benefits (health, leisure, a cleaner environment) are hard to value.
- Future benefits are uncertain, so the forgone option is an estimate.
- People rank options differently, so the same choice has different opportunity costs for different people.
- Sunk costs should be ignored, but people often let them influence choices.

## Exam traps

- Adding all rejected options together. Only the next best counts.
- Treating the price as the opportunity cost.
- Claiming a "free" public service costs nothing.
- Writing a bare number such as "3" without the unit "sacks of rice per sack of beans".
- Dividing the wrong way round: the product you gain goes on the bottom.
- In a scenario about a government, giving a consumer's opportunity cost.
- Ending a "Discuss" answer without saying what the decision depends on.

## Quick self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. Why does a free good have no opportunity cost?
3. A student turns down a cinema trip, a concert and a shift at work (worth 40) to revise. She ranks the shift second. What is the opportunity cost of revising?
4. A machine can make 500 cups or 250 plates a day. Calculate the opportunity cost of one plate.
5. Spending savings of 1,200 at an interest rate of 5% gives up how much interest in one year?
6. A worker earning 14 an hour takes 6 hours off. Calculate the earnings forgone.
7. Give one reason why a fee-free state hospital still has an opportunity cost.
8. A government spends 30 million on a road instead of houses costing 0.15 million each. How many houses are forgone?
9. Why might a higher wage lead a worker to work more hours?
10. What is a sunk cost, and why is it not part of opportunity cost?
11. Give one reason why opportunity cost is hard to measure.
12. A firm owns its premises and could rent them out for 2,000 a month. State the opportunity cost of using the premises itself.

### Answers

1. The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
2. It is not scarce, so using more of it takes nothing away from another use.
3. The shift at work (40 of earnings), because it is ranked second. The cinema and concert are not included.
4. 500 / 250 = 2 cups per plate.
5. 1,200 x 0.05 = 60.
6. 6 x 14 = 84.
7. The doctors, buildings and equipment could have been used for other goods or services.
8. 30 / 0.15 = 200 houses.
9. The opportunity cost of leisure rises, so leisure becomes more expensive in earnings forgone.
10. Money already spent that cannot be recovered; it is the same whichever option is chosen next, so nothing is given up by the future choice.
11. Any one: non-money benefits are hard to value; future benefits are uncertain; people rank options differently.
12. The 2,000 a month of rent forgone.
