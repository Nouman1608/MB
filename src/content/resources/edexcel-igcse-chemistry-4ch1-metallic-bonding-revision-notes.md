These notes condense sub-topic 1(h) Metallic bonding (points 1.52C, 1.53C and 1.54C) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). Every point has a C reference, so the whole sub-topic is **Paper 2 only**. Model answers and a data-interpretation example are in the [metallic bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-metallic-bonding/); test yourself with the [metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-metallic-bonding-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metallic lattice | A regular arrangement of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalised electrons |
| Delocalised electrons | Outer electrons that are not attached to one atom and can move through the whole structure |
| Metallic bonding | The strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Malleable | Can be hammered or bent into shape without breaking |
| Electrical conductivity | The ability to carry an electric current |

## The structure in four lines

```
metal atoms lose their outer electrons -> positive ions
the outer electrons become delocalised -> "sea" of electrons
positive ions sit in regular layers in a giant lattice
bonding = electrostatic attraction, ions <-> delocalised electrons, in all directions
```

Charge on each ion = outer electrons per atom = delocalised electrons per ion: Na⁺ (1), Mg²⁺ (2), Al³⁺ (3). The metal is neutral overall.

## The 2-D diagram (1.52C)

Marking points to include:

- Positive ions as equal circles in regular rows.
- Charge shown on each ion (+, 2+ or 3+).
- Electrons (e⁻) between the ions, not inside them.
- Labels: "positive ion" and "delocalised electrons".

No negative ions anywhere in the diagram.

## Properties and explanations (1.54C)

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Conducts electricity (solid and liquid) | Delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge |
| Malleable | Layers of positive ions slide over each other; delocalised electrons keep attracting the ions in their new positions, so the bonding is not broken |
| Conducts heat well | Delocalised electrons move and transfer energy through the metal |
| High melting and boiling points (most metals) | Many strong attractions between ions and delocalised electrons need a lot of energy to overcome |
| Stronger bonding in Mg than Na | Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and there are two delocalised electrons per ion, so the attraction is stronger |

## Comparing structures

| Structure | Charge carriers when solid | Conducts as solid? | Conducts as liquid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metallic | Delocalised electrons | Yes | Yes |
| Giant ionic | None mobile (ions fixed) | No | Yes (ions move) |
| Simple molecular | None | No | No |
| Graphite (giant covalent) | Delocalised electrons | Yes | — |

Clue for a metal in data: conducts as a solid **and** is malleable / has a typical metal melting point. Graphite also conducts as a solid but is not a metal.

## Answer frames

- **Conductivity:** "Metals have delocalised electrons. These are free to move through the structure and carry charge."
- **Malleability:** "The positive ions are in layers. The layers slide over each other when a force is applied. The delocalised electrons still hold the ions together, so the metal changes shape without breaking."
- **Bonding:** "Strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons."

## Worked reminders

- **Counting for a diagram:** 10 magnesium ions (each Mg²⁺) need 10 × 2 = 20 delocalised electrons for the drawing to be neutral overall.
- **Same idea, different metals:** potassium (K⁺) has one delocalised electron per ion, like sodium, which fits its low melting point (63 °C).
- **Alloys (2.27C, also Paper 2 only):** atoms of a different size in the lattice disrupt the layers, so they slide less easily; this is why alloys are harder than pure metals. See the [extraction and uses of metals revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-extraction-and-uses-of-metals-revision-notes/).

## Exam traps

- "Ions move" when explaining a metal's conductivity: wrong charge carrier.
- "Free electrons" without "move" and "carry charge": incomplete.
- Negative ions drawn in the lattice.
- "Attraction between metal atoms": the attraction is between ions and electrons.
- Malleability answers that stop at "layers slide", with no reason the metal does not break.
- Mixing up malleable (bends) with ductile (drawn into wires): the specification names malleability.
- Treating every solid conductor as a metal.

## Self-test

1. State what is meant by metallic bonding.
2. What particles are found in a solid metal lattice?
3. How many delocalised electrons does each aluminium ion contribute?
4. Give two labels a 2-D metallic lattice diagram must include.
5. State what is meant by malleable.
6. Explain why solid magnesium conducts electricity.
7. Explain why molten iron conducts electricity.
8. Explain why a copper bar can be hammered flat without breaking.
9. Explain why most metals have high melting points.
10. Suggest why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.
11. Solid lead and solid lead(II) bromide are tested for conductivity. Predict and explain the results.
12. Substance X conducts as a solid and as a liquid and bends without breaking. Identify the type of bonding.

### Answers

1. The strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
2. Positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
3. Three.
4. "Positive (metal) ion" and "delocalised electron".
5. Can be hammered or bent into shape without breaking.
6. It has delocalised electrons that are free to move through the structure and carry charge.
7. The electrons are still delocalised in the liquid, so they move and carry charge.
8. The layers of copper ions slide over each other; the delocalised electrons still attract the ions, so the bonding is maintained.
9. There are many strong electrostatic attractions between positive ions and delocalised electrons, which need a lot of energy to overcome.
10. Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and each ion provides two delocalised electrons, so the attraction is stronger.
11. Lead conducts (delocalised electrons move); lead(II) bromide does not (its ions are fixed in the solid lattice).
12. Metallic.
