Cambridge A-Level Computer Science 9618 has four externally assessed papers, each 75 marks and 25% of the A Level: Paper 1 Theory Fundamentals (1 hour 30 minutes), Paper 2 Fundamental Problem-solving and Programming Skills (2 hours), Paper 3 Advanced Theory (1 hour 30 minutes) and Paper 4 Practical (2 hours 30 minutes). Calculators must not be used in any paper. The record models the 2026 syllabus, and the 2027 to 2029 syllabus sets the same four papers with the same durations, marks and weightings. ## AS Level and A Level routes

Papers 1 and 2 form the AS Level, where each is 50% of the AS, and can be a standalone qualification. Papers 3 and 4 complete the full A Level and count only towards it, each 25% of the A Level. The AS can be staged over two years, carrying forward the AS result, or all four papers can be taken together in the same series.

## Paper 1: Theory Fundamentals

Paper 1 is 90 minutes and 75 marks, 25% of the A Level. **How to spend the time:** 90 / 75 = 1.2 minutes per mark. Since no calculator is allowed, practise binary, hexadecimal and file-size arithmetic by hand, and learn precise definitions, because short-answer items reward exact technical vocabulary.

## Paper 2: Fundamental Problem-solving and Programming Skills

Paper 2 is 120 minutes and 75 marks, 25% of the A Level. **How to spend the time:** 120 / 75 = 1.6 minutes per mark. This paper is about solving problems with algorithms and code, so practise writing and tracing algorithms on paper, not just at a keyboard. Plan the structure of an answer before writing it and check it with a trace table.

## Paper 3: Advanced Theory

Paper 3 is 90 minutes and 75 marks, 25% of the A Level, and counts only towards the full A Level. **How to spend the time:** 1.2 minutes per mark again. It builds on AS ideas, so keep the AS theory fresh while adding the A Level topics, and be ready to explain not only what a technique is but why it is used.

## Paper 4: Practical

Paper 4 is 150 minutes and 75 marks, 25% of the A Level. Candidates submit complete program code and testing evidence on a computer with no internet or email access. **How to spend the time:** 150 / 75 = 2 minutes per mark. Rehearse timed programming tasks in a plain environment, and document your testing as you go rather than at the end.

## Timing and marks-per-minute

| Paper | Minutes | Marks | Minutes per mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90 | 75 | 90 / 75 = 1.2 |
| 2 | 120 | 75 | 120 / 75 = 1.6 |
| 3 | 90 | 75 | 90 / 75 = 1.2 |
| 4 | 150 | 75 | 150 / 75 = 2.0 |

Weightings: 25 x 4 = 100. Total marks: 4 x 75 = 300.

## Command words and question styles

In theory papers, "state" and "give" need a short fact, "describe" needs a sequence or features, and "explain" needs a reason. In programming papers, "write", "trace" and "complete" mean you produce code or fill in values, so accuracy of logic matters more than prose. Use the pseudocode or programming language conventions you were taught, and keep identifier names meaningful.

## Worked practice scenario: bitmap size and a trace

**Question:** (a) A bitmap image is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high, with 24 bits per pixel. Calculate the file size in bytes, ignoring any header. (b) The algorithm below is traced with the array values 4, 9, 2. State the final value of Total.

```
Total <- 0
FOR Index <- 1 TO 3
    Total <- Total + Value[Index]
NEXT Index
```

**Model answer:**

```
(a) Pixels = 800 x 600 = 480,000
    Bits   = 480,000 x 24 = 11,520,000
    Bytes  = 11,520,000 / 8 = 1,440,000 bytes

(b) Index 1: Total = 0 + 4 = 4
    Index 2: Total = 4 + 9 = 13
    Index 3: Total = 13 + 2 = 15
    Final Total = 15
```

Because calculators are not allowed, set out the long multiplication in stages as above, and say the unit at the end. In (b), a trace table with columns for Index and Total gives the same answer and earns marks for method if you make a slip.

## Planning your final weeks

Divide the time between theory and practice, since two papers test written theory and two test programming. Keep a notebook of definitions and short explanations for Papers 1 and 3, and test yourself by covering the answer. For Papers 2 and 4, write small programs every week in the language you were taught, beginning without any help and then checking against documentation afterwards. Keep a log of the mistakes you make, such as off-by-one errors in loops, because the same ones tend to recur. In the last fortnight, attempt each paper under its real time limit, and for Paper 4 practise saving, testing and evidencing your work in a computer environment with no internet access.

## Before/during exam checklist

- Confirm which papers you sit and on which route.
- Practise hand arithmetic for binary, hex and file sizes.
- Write algorithms on paper regularly.
- Keep a trace table template ready.
- For Paper 4, practise timed tasks without internet help.
- Save and document tests as you work.
- Read the question twice and underline the command word.
- Check boundary cases in loops and conditions.
