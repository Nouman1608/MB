## What respiration is (2.34)

Respiration is a set of chemical reactions in every living cell that break down nutrient molecules, mainly glucose, to release energy. The energy released is used to make **ATP**.

Three points are tested again and again:

- Living cells need ATP for energy-demanding processes, and respiration is a major source of that ATP in plants, animals, fungi and bacteria. Rates vary with cell state: dormancy and low-activity states reduce metabolic demand, and photosynthetic cells also make ATP by photophosphorylation.
- Respiration is controlled by **enzymes**, so its rate depends on temperature and pH.
- Respiration **releases** energy. It does not "make" or "produce" energy. Energy cannot be created; it is transferred from glucose to ATP. Some is lost as heat.

Do not confuse respiration with breathing. Breathing (ventilation) moves air in and out of the lungs. Respiration is the chemical process inside cells.

## ATP: the energy carrier (2.34–2.35)

ATP stands for **adenosine triphosphate**. Respiration transfers some of the chemical energy in nutrients to ATP. When ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate, energy is transferred to processes that need it; cells do not use glucose directly as the immediate energy source for most cellular work. Photosynthetic cells can also make ATP by photophosphorylation, so not every ATP molecule comes from respiration. All living cells need ATP to drive energy-requiring processes, although demand and metabolic rate vary with cell state.

Cells use the energy from ATP for:

- **muscle contraction**
- **active transport** of ions and molecules against a concentration gradient (for example, mineral ions into root hair cells)
- **building large molecules** from small ones, such as proteins from amino acids
- **cell division** and growth
- maintaining ion gradients that enable **nerve impulses**, and transmitting those impulses.

In mammals and birds, respiration releases heat that contributes to body temperature. Temperature is kept within a viable range by homeostatic control of heat production and loss; respiration alone does not hold it perfectly constant.

Cells with high aerobic energy demand, such as many muscle cells, often contain many mitochondria. This can support greater aerobic ATP production, but mitochondrion number alone does not determine the actual respiration or ATP-production rate.

## Aerobic respiration (2.36–2.37)

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen. It takes place mainly in the **mitochondria**, and it breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water.

Word equation:

```
glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
```

Balanced symbol equation:

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
```

Learn the symbol equation exactly. Note that it is the photosynthesis equation reversed, but the two processes are not the same: they happen in different organelles and are controlled by different enzymes.

### Worked example 1: checking the symbol equation is balanced

**Question.** Show that the balanced equation for aerobic respiration has the same number of each type of atom on both sides.

**Answer.** Count each element on each side.

```
Atom      Left side                  Right side
C         6 (in C₆H₁₂O₆)            6 × 1 = 6 (in 6CO₂)
H         12 (in C₆H₁₂O₆)           6 × 2 = 12 (in 6H₂O)
O         6 + (6 × 2) = 18           (6 × 2) + (6 × 1) = 18
```

Carbon 6 = 6, hydrogen 12 = 12, oxygen 18 = 18. The equation is balanced.

The common slip is forgetting the oxygen inside glucose, which gives 12 oxygen atoms on the left instead of 18.

## Anaerobic respiration (2.36, 2.38)

Anaerobic respiration happens **without oxygen**. It takes place in the cytoplasm. Glucose is only partly broken down, so much less energy is released and much less ATP is made from each glucose molecule.

**In animals** (for example, human muscle cells during hard exercise):

```
glucose → lactic acid
```

No carbon dioxide is made in animal anaerobic respiration.

**In plants** (for example, root cells in waterlogged soil) and in yeast:

```
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
```

Yeast is a fungus, not a plant, but it uses the same reaction. Anaerobic respiration in yeast is used in bread making and brewing; that application is covered in Topic 5.

Why release less energy? In anaerobic respiration much of the chemical energy stays locked up in the product. Lactic acid and ethanol still contain energy; carbon dioxide and water from aerobic respiration contain very little.

### Comparing aerobic and anaerobic respiration (2.36)

| Feature | Aerobic | Anaerobic (animals) | Anaerobic (plants and yeast) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen needed? | Yes | No | No |
| Breakdown of glucose | Complete | Incomplete | Incomplete |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Lactic acid | Ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Energy (ATP) per glucose | Large amount | Small amount | Small amount |
| Main site in cell | Mitochondria | Cytoplasm | Cytoplasm |

When a "describe the differences" question is worth 3 marks, give three separate differences, each as a comparison ("aerobic needs oxygen **but** anaerobic does not"). A list about only one type of respiration does not show a difference.

## Practical: carbon dioxide and heat from respiring organisms (2.39)

You need to know how to show that living organisms release carbon dioxide and heat when they respire. Germinating seeds (such as peas or beans) are used because they respire quickly while they grow. Small animals such as woodlice or maggots also work for the carbon dioxide test.

Two indicators are used:

| Indicator | Normal air | More carbon dioxide | Less carbon dioxide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen-carbonate indicator | Red / orange | Yellow | Purple |
| Limewater | Clear | Turns milky (cloudy) | -- |

**Method outline.**

1. Put the same volume of hydrogen-carbonate indicator in two boiling tubes.
2. Place germinating seeds on a gauze platform above the indicator in one tube, so they do not touch it.
3. Set up a **control** tube with the same mass of seeds that have been boiled (killed) and cooled, or with glass beads of the same volume.
4. Seal both tubes with a bung and leave them at the same temperature, in the dark if you are using seeds, so photosynthesis cannot take up carbon dioxide.
5. Record the colour of the indicator at intervals.

The indicator in the tube with living seeds turns from red to yellow. The control stays red. This shows the change was caused by respiration of living cells.

### Worked example 2: interpreting indicator colours

**Question.** A student sets up three sealed tubes in the dark, each containing red hydrogen-carbonate indicator: tube A has live maggots, tube B has glass beads, tube C has live maggots and is kept 10 °C warmer than A. After 20 minutes, A is orange-yellow, B is red and C is yellow. Explain the results.

**Answer.**

- Tube A: maggots respire and release carbon dioxide, which dissolves in the indicator and lowers its pH, so it changes towards yellow.
- Tube B: glass beads do not respire, so no carbon dioxide is added and the indicator stays red. This is the control; it shows the change in A is caused by the living maggots.
- Tube C: a moderate temperature increase can speed enzyme-controlled respiration while enzymes remain functional, so more CO₂ may accumulate during the same interval. The effect depends on the starting temperature; excessive heat can disrupt enzymes. Indicator colour is a qualitative, bounded proxy for CO₂ change, not a linear measurement of respiration rate.

### Showing that heat is released

**Method outline.**

1. Soak peas in water for a day so they start to germinate.
2. Boil a second batch to kill them, then cool.
3. Rinse **both** batches in dilute disinfectant to reduce surface microorganisms that could also respire and release heat; this does not guarantee sterility.
4. Put each batch in a **vacuum flask**. Its insulation reduces heat transfer to the surroundings; it does not stop heat loss entirely.
5. Plug each flask loosely with cotton wool, with a thermometer passing into the seeds. The cotton wool allows oxygen in while reducing convection and heat loss.
6. Record the temperature in each flask every day for several days.

The germinating-pea flask should show a greater temperature rise than the boiled-seed control because living seeds respire. The measured rise is the net result of heat released and heat lost to the surroundings; the insulation reduces, but does not eliminate, that loss.

### Worked example 3: processing heat data

**Question.** Germinating peas start at 21.0 °C and reach 27.0 °C after 4 days. Boiled peas start and finish at 21.0 °C. Calculate the mean rate of temperature rise of the germinating peas, and explain the difference between the flasks.

**Answer.**

```
Temperature rise = 27.0 − 21.0 = 6.0 °C
Mean rate = 6.0 ÷ 4 = 1.5 °C per day
```

The germinating peas respire and transfer some nutrient energy as heat. The insulated flask reduces heat loss, so its temperature rises; some heat still escapes. The boiled peas are non-living and do not respire, so their flask should show little or no respiration-related rise.

Always include the unit (°C per day) and use the same time unit given in the question.

### Variables and reliability

- **Independent variable**: living or dead seeds (or, in an extension, temperature or type of seed).
- **Dependent variable**: temperature rise, or the time taken for the indicator to change colour.
- **Control variables**: mass or number of seeds, type of seed, volume of indicator, starting temperature, size of flask.
- Repeat each set-up and calculate a mean to improve reliability.

## Common errors

- Writing "respiration produces energy". Write "releases energy" or "produces ATP".
- Giving carbon dioxide as a product of anaerobic respiration in human muscle.
- Leaving out carbon dioxide from the plant/yeast anaerobic equation.
- Writing O instead of O₂, or 6H₂O as H₁₂O₆, in the symbol equation.
- Saying anaerobic respiration releases "no energy". It releases less.
- Forgetting to explain why the seeds are disinfected, or why boiled seeds are used.
- Describing a colour change with no starting colour: write "from red to yellow".
