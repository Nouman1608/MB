## Why body temperature is kept constant

Mammals are **endotherms**: they keep their core body temperature at about 37 degrees Celsius whatever the temperature outside. Enzymes in the body work best at this optimum. If the temperature fell, reactions would slow down. If it rose far above the optimum, enzymes would be denatured. Temperature control is an example of homeostasis, using negative feedback.

Heat is gained from respiration in cells, especially in the liver and muscles, and from the environment on a hot day. Heat is lost from the surface of the body, mostly through the skin. Temperature control is therefore a matter of changing how much heat is lost or produced.

## The structure of the skin

You must be able to identify the following structures on a diagram of a section through the skin and state what each does.

| Structure | Function in temperature control |
|---|---|
| Hairs | Trap a layer of air when they are raised, which insulates |
| Hair erector muscles | Contract to raise the hairs; relax to lower them |
| Sweat glands | Release sweat onto the skin surface for cooling |
| Receptors | Detect changes in temperature of the skin |
| Sensory neurones | Carry impulses from the receptors to the CNS |
| Blood vessels | Arterioles that supply capillaries near the surface; their width changes the heat lost |
| Fatty tissue | Layer under the skin that insulates the body |

Receptors in the skin detect the temperature of the surroundings. There are also receptors in the brain that detect the temperature of the blood passing through it.

## Insulation

**Insulation** reduces the transfer of heat between the body and its surroundings. In mammals, there are two layers of insulation.

- **Fatty tissue** beneath the skin is a poor conductor of heat, so it slows the loss of heat from the body core. Mammals living in cold places, such as seals, have a thick layer.
- **Hair** traps a layer of still air next to the skin. Air is a poor conductor, so this slows heat loss as well.

Insulation does not produce heat. It slows down the loss of heat, so a mammal can keep warm with less energy.

## The hypothalamus and skin receptors

The **hypothalamus** is part of the brain and acts as the control centre for temperature. Its receptors monitor the temperature of the blood flowing through the brain, which reflects the core temperature. Receptors in the skin monitor the temperature at the surface. Together they report to the hypothalamus.

The loop is as follows:

1. Receptors detect a change from the set point.
2. Sensory neurones carry impulses to the hypothalamus.
3. The hypothalamus sends impulses along motor neurones to effectors.
4. The effectors change heat loss or heat production.
5. The core temperature returns to the set point.

The effectors are the sweat glands, the muscles (including the skeletal muscles that shiver and the hair erector muscles) and the smooth muscle in the walls of the arterioles.

## Responses to heat

When the core temperature rises, the aim is to lose heat.

### Sweating

The sweat glands release **sweat**, which is mostly water, onto the skin. Water **evaporates** from the skin surface. Evaporation needs energy, which is taken as heat from the skin, so the body is cooled. The more sweat, the greater the cooling. On a humid day sweat evaporates slowly, so the cooling is less effective.

### Vasodilation

The muscles in the walls of the **arterioles** that supply the skin capillaries relax, so the arterioles become wider. This is **vasodilation**. More blood flows through the capillaries near the surface of the skin. The skin looks flushed. More heat is lost from the blood by radiation and conduction to the surroundings, so the body is cooled.

### Hairs lie flat

The hair erector muscles relax, so the hairs lie flat against the skin. Less air is trapped, so less insulation is provided and heat is lost more easily.

## Responses to cold

When the core temperature falls, the aim is to conserve heat and make more.

### Vasoconstriction

The muscles in the walls of the arterioles to the skin contract and the arterioles narrow. This is **vasoconstriction**. Less blood flows through the surface capillaries, and so less heat is lost from the skin. The skin looks pale.

### Hair erector muscles contract

The hair erector muscles contract and pull the hairs upright. A thicker layer of air is trapped next to the skin, which increases insulation. This is more important in animals with thick fur, and in humans it produces goose pimples.

### Shivering

Skeletal muscles contract and relax rapidly and involuntarily. Respiration in these muscles releases heat, which warms the blood. Shivering therefore raises body temperature.

### Reduced sweating

Sweat production falls, so less cooling by evaporation occurs.

## Common confusion: vessels do not move

A frequent error is to say that the blood vessels "move closer to the skin" or "move away". The vessels do not move. Arterioles widen or narrow. The blood is diverted to or away from the surface capillaries according to the width of the arterioles. Capillaries themselves cannot change width, because their walls have no muscle.

## Worked example: a hot day and a cold day

*Compare the body's responses to a core temperature that is too high and one that is too low.*

| Effector | Hot | Cold |
|---|---|---|
| Sweat glands | More sweat | Little sweat |
| Arterioles | Vasodilation | Vasoconstriction |
| Hair erector muscles | Relax, hairs flat | Contract, hairs upright |
| Skeletal muscles | No change | Shivering |
| Effect on heat | More lost | Less lost, more made |

## Worked example: explaining a flushed face

*After running, a person's face looks red and is wet. Explain these observations.*

- Respiration in the muscles releases heat, raising the core temperature above the set point.
- Receptors send impulses to the hypothalamus.
- The hypothalamus signals the arterioles in the skin, which undergo vasodilation. More blood flows through the surface capillaries, so the face looks red and heat is lost.
- The sweat glands produce sweat. Evaporation of the sweat removes heat from the skin, so the face looks wet and the body cools.

## Interpreting data

*The table shows the rate of blood flow in the skin of the hand, in arbitrary units, for a person who moves from a warm room to a cold room.*

| Condition | Skin blood flow (units) |
|---|---|
| Warm room | 30 |
| Cold room | 6 |

The percentage decrease is (30 - 6) divided by 30 x 100 = 80 per cent. This agrees with vasoconstriction: the arterioles supplying the skin narrow, so less blood flows to the surface and less heat is lost.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the blood vessels move towards or away from the skin.
- Saying that sweating cools the body because the sweat is cold. The cooling comes from the evaporation.
- Mixing up vasodilation (wider, more heat lost) and vasoconstriction (narrower, less heat lost).
- Saying the hairs "keep out the cold". They trap air, which is a poor conductor.
- Saying that shivering is a voluntary action.
- Leaving out the hypothalamus, or saying it is a gland that releases heat.
- Writing "capillaries dilate". The arterioles change width.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name a structure on the diagram of the skin.
- **Describe**: state the role of insulation or the hypothalamus.
- **Explain**: give the cause and effect for each response, including evaporation and the width of arterioles.
