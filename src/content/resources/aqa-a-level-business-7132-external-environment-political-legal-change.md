This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.4 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: political and legal change** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the impact of changes in the political and legal environment on strategic and functional decision making**, where the environment includes a broad understanding of the scope and effects of laws related to competition, the labour market and environmental legislation
- **the impact of government policy** related to enterprise, the role of regulators, infrastructure, the environment and international trade.

All businesses and figures are invented. The guide on the social and technological environment, and the guide on economic change, cover the other parts of the external environment; this page deals only with political and legal factors.

## The political and legal environment

The **political environment** covers the decisions of governments: their taxes, spending, policies and the stability of the political system. The **legal environment** covers the rules that result: laws and the rules set by regulators. The two are linked, because policy usually becomes law. A business cannot control them. It can only prepare, comply, adapt and sometimes lobby.

Changes matter because they can create **opportunities** (new subsidies, new markets opened by trade deals) and **threats** (higher costs, restricted activities, loss of markets). They also affect both **strategic** decisions, such as where to operate, and **functional** decisions, such as pay, pricing or production methods.

## Laws on competition

Competition law aims to protect consumers by keeping markets competitive. Typical rules prevent:

- **cartels**, where firms agree prices or share markets
- **abuse of a dominant position**, such as predatory pricing to drive out rivals
- **mergers** that would substantially reduce competition, which can be investigated and blocked by a competition authority.

**Effects on decisions:**

- Strategy: a firm with a large market share may be stopped from buying a rival (affecting growth through takeover). Firms must be careful about joint ventures and price signalling.
- Marketing: pricing agreements and some exclusive deals are restricted.
- Opportunity: competition law can open markets to smaller firms by limiting dominant players.

## Laws on the labour market

These cover employment contracts, pay, working time, equality, health and safety, redundancy and union rights.

| Change | Likely effects |
|---|---|
| A rise in the minimum wage | Higher labour costs, especially in low-paid sectors; pressure on prices; may encourage automation; may raise demand if workers spend more |
| Tighter rules on working hours or contracts | Higher costs or less flexibility; may need more staff |
| Stronger equal pay or equality laws | Compliance costs and reporting; better recruitment of diverse talent |
| New health and safety duties | Training and equipment costs; fewer accidents and claims |
| Easier or harder immigration rules | Changes in labour supply and recruitment cost |

**Functional effects:** human resources must change contracts and recruitment; finance must budget for higher costs; operations may substitute capital for labour; marketing may need to reprice.

## Environmental legislation

Examples include limits on emissions, packaging and waste rules, carbon taxes or trading, and bans on certain materials or fuels.

- **Threats:** higher costs of compliance, redesign or investment; penalties for breach; loss of products that can no longer be sold.
- **Opportunities:** demand rises for cleaner products and services; firms already ahead of the rules gain a competitive advantage; subsidies for green investment.
- **Decisions affected:** product design (operations), choice of suppliers, marketing claims, capital investment, location of production.

## Government policy

### Enterprise policy

Governments support start-ups and small firms through grants, tax relief, loans, advice and simpler regulation. Lower corporation tax for small firms, or relief on research spending, raises after-tax returns. For an existing business this may be an opportunity to expand or innovate; for a large firm it may bring more competition from new rivals.

### Regulators

Regulators are independent bodies that oversee markets, especially those with limited competition, such as water, energy, telecoms and finance. They can set price limits, quality standards and rules on service, and can fine firms. Regulators can protect consumers but also limit profit: a price cap may reduce the revenue of a water company, so its investment plans may be cut or financed differently.

### Infrastructure

Roads, rail, ports, broadband and energy supply influence costs and location. Better transport links reduce delivery costs and widen markets; poor broadband limits e-commerce. A new road or rail link may justify opening a distribution centre; its cancellation may lead to relocation.

### The environment

Policy such as net-zero targets and incentives for electric vehicles affect both costs and demand. Firms must decide whether to lead or follow.

### International trade

Policy on tariffs, quotas, trade agreements and sanctions changes the cost and availability of imports and access to export markets.

- **Tariffs** raise import costs.
- **Trade deals** can lower barriers and widen markets.
- **Sanctions** may prohibit trading with some countries.
- **Different standards** on products create extra costs for exporters.

## Worked example: a rise in the minimum wage

Larkin Care Ltd runs residential care homes. Its minimum hourly wage rises from £10.00 to £10.70 (7%). It employs 60 staff at the minimum wage, each working 30 hours a week for 52 weeks, and 15 supervisors working 40 hours a week who are paid a £2 differential above the minimum wage. Operating profit is £150,000 on revenue of £2.4m.

- Extra cost for minimum-wage staff: £0.70 × 30 × 52 × 60 = **£65,520**
- Knock-on cost if the supervisors' differential is kept: £0.70 × 40 × 52 × 15 = **£21,840**
- Total extra cost: **£87,360**, which is 3.6% of revenue
- New operating profit if nothing else changes: £150,000 − £87,360 = **£62,640**, a fall of 58%

**Strategic options:**

1. **Raise fees** by about 3.6% to cover the cost. This is possible if local authorities and families will pay, but may lose some residents.
2. **Cut costs elsewhere**, for example by reducing agency staff or using technology for record keeping.
3. **Accept lower profit** to protect quality and reputation.
4. **Change the business model**, for example by moving to services with higher fees.

**Judgement.** A small percentage rise in wages had a large effect on profit because wages are such a high proportion of costs and margins are thin. The best response depends on how price-sensitive customers are, how far competitors face the same law (which would make price rises easier), and whether quality can be maintained with fewer staff. Because the rise affects every rival, it is mainly a threat to profit rather than to competitiveness.

## Political risk and planning

Political change is hard to forecast. Elections can reverse policy, laws may be passed with little notice, and trade relations can shift quickly. This is called **political risk**. It is greatest for businesses that invest heavily in fixed assets, such as factories, because they cannot easily move if the rules change, and for businesses that depend on one government decision, such as a licence, a subsidy or a contract.

Businesses respond by:

- **scenario planning**, asking what happens to costs and revenues under different policies
- **spreading risk**, for example by selling in several countries or using several suppliers
- **building flexibility**, such as shorter contracts or leased premises
- **engaging with government**, through trade associations or consultations, to influence rules before they are set
- **monitoring** proposals early, so there is time to adapt before a law takes effect.

The strategic point is that a decision which looks sound under today's rules may become costly under tomorrow's. The longer the payback period of an investment, the more political change matters.

## Assessing the impact of change

When asked how serious a change is, use these questions:

1. **Scale:** how large is the cost or revenue effect?
2. **Timing:** is it sudden, or phased in, giving time to adapt?
3. **Certainty:** is the law passed, or only proposed? Can it be reversed?
4. **Who is affected:** the whole industry, or only this business?
5. **Flexibility:** how easily can the business change prices, methods or location?
6. **Opportunity:** can the business turn compliance into a selling point?

Large, well-resourced firms can often adapt more easily, and can lobby; small firms may find compliance costs heavy. Some firms benefit: a ban on a polluting process helps the firm already using the cleaner method.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** pick one change, say what it does to costs or revenues, and trace the effect on a decision in the case. For example: higher minimum wage, higher labour cost, lower profit unless prices rise, so the pricing decision changes.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the effects, using the questions above, and state what your judgement depends on.
- Distinguish **strategic** effects (where to operate, which markets) from **functional** effects (staffing, pricing, production methods).

## Common mistakes

- Listing laws without explaining their effect on a decision.
- Treating every change as a threat. Many create opportunities.
- Confusing the roles of regulator and government.
- Ignoring how rivals are affected.
- Describing the political environment without linking it to this business.
