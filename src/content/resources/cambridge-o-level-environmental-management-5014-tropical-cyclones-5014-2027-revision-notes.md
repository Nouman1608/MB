> **Syllabus edition note.** These notes follow the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029, sub-topic 6.2.

Sub-topic 6.2 is short but precise: three numbers for formation, ten impacts and seven management
strategies, plus a discussion of their benefits and limitations.

Full explanations: [tropical cyclones study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-tropical-cyclones-5014-2027/).

| Outcome | Command | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 6.2.1 | Describe | Location and conditions for formation |
| 6.2.2 | Describe and explain | Ten impacts on people and the environment |
| 6.2.3 | Describe | Seven strategies before, during and after |
| 6.2.4 | Discuss | Benefits and limitations of those strategies |

## Names

**Tropical cyclone** = umbrella term for **storms, hurricanes and typhoons**.
- Hurricane: Atlantic and north-east Pacific.
- Typhoon: north-west Pacific.
- Cyclone: Indian Ocean and South Pacific.

## Formation: "5-30, 27, 60"

| Condition | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude | 5° to 30° N or S | Spin needs the Earth's rotation (too weak near the Equator); cooler sea beyond 30° |
| Sea temperature | At least 27 °C | Rapid evaporation; condensation releases heat that drives the storm |
| Ocean depth | At least 60 m | Deep warm layer, so the storm does not stir up cold water |

**Energy chain:** warm sea → evaporation → moist air rises → condensation releases heat → lower pressure →
more air drawn in → spiral due to Earth's rotation → eye at the centre. Over land or cold water → energy
supply cut → storm weakens.

**Season:** late summer and early autumn, when seas are warmest.

## Ten impacts, sorted by cause

| Cause | Impacts |
|---|---|
| Strong winds | Damage to buildings and infrastructure; loss of crops; livestock and habitats |
| Heavy rain | Flooding; landslides |
| Storm surge | Coastal flooding; salt water on farmland; habitat loss |
| After-effects | Evacuation of people and livestock; contamination of drinking-water supplies; water-related disease; financial losses; human health |

## Seven strategies

| Before | During | After |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring and warning | Evacuation | Rebuilding of damaged areas |
| Structure of buildings | Shelters | International aid |
| Disaster preparation (plans, drills, emergency supplies, emergency rescue teams) | | |

**Not in the 6.2 list:** land use zoning.

## Benefits and limitations

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring and warning | Days of warning possible | Path can change; remote areas missed; false alarms |
| Structure of buildings | Fewer collapses and injuries | Cost; informal housing |
| Disaster preparation | Fast, organised response | Funding; practice needed |
| Evacuation | Saves lives, especially from surge | Transport, trust; people stay to guard property |
| Shelters | Safe refuge for those who cannot leave | Overcrowding; sanitation |
| Rebuilding | Restores services; build back stronger | Slow; costly |
| International aid | Money, supplies, expertise | Delays; uneven distribution |

**Judgement lines to reuse**
- "Warning without evacuation or shelters saves few lives."
- "Before-event strategies reduce deaths most; after-event strategies reduce long-term losses."
- "Low-income countries depend more on shelters and international aid; high-income countries on
  building structure and forecasting."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tropical cyclone | Large rotating low-pressure storm formed over warm tropical oceans |
| Eye | Calm, clear centre where air sinks |
| Low pressure | Air pressure below normal; drives inflowing winds |
| Condensation | Water vapour turning to liquid, releasing heat |
| Storm surge | Rise in sea level as low pressure and wind push water onshore |
| Evacuation | Moving people and livestock away from danger |
| Cyclone shelter | Strong, often raised building for refuge |
| Infrastructure | Roads, bridges, power, water and communication systems |

## Exam traps

- "Over warm water": give 27 °C.
- "Near the Equator": give 5° to 30°.
- Forgetting the 60 m depth condition.
- Treating hurricanes and typhoons as different hazards.
- Using land use zoning as a cyclone strategy.
- Placing evacuation "after" the event; it is before or during.
- Not saying which part of the storm causes each impact.

## Self-test

1. State the three conditions in the syllabus for a tropical cyclone to form.
2. Why do tropical cyclones not form on the Equator?
3. Name the term used for a tropical cyclone in the north-west Pacific.
4. Explain how condensation helps a cyclone grow stronger.
5. Why does a cyclone weaken when it moves over land?
6. Name two impacts caused by heavy rain from a cyclone.
7. Explain how a cyclone can damage farmland near the coast for several years.
8. List the strategies used before a cyclone.
9. Give one limitation of monitoring and warning.
10. Why can shelters increase the risk of water-related disease?
11. A site is at 18° S with a sea temperature of 28 °C and depth of 45 m. Can a cyclone form? Explain.
12. Give one benefit and one limitation of international aid after a cyclone.

### Answers

1. Latitude between 5° and 30° N or S; ocean temperature of at least 27 °C; ocean depth of at least 60 m.
2. The Earth's rotation is too weak there to make the air spin.
3. Typhoon.
4. Condensation releases heat, which makes the air rise faster, lowering pressure and drawing in more
   moist air.
5. It loses its supply of warm, moist air from the sea.
6. Flooding and landslides.
7. Storm surge floods it with salt water, which makes the soil too salty for crops.
8. Monitoring and warning; structure of buildings; disaster preparation.
9. The path or strength may change, warnings may not reach remote areas, or false alarms reduce trust.
10. Many people are crowded together with limited clean water and sanitation.
11. No. Latitude and temperature are suitable, but 45 m is shallower than the 60 m needed.
12. Benefit: provides money, supplies or rescue teams the country lacks. Limitation: it can be delayed or
    not reach the worst-hit areas.
