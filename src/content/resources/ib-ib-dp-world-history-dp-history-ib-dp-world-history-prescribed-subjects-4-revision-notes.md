Prescribed subject 4 covers the civil rights movement in the United States (1954-1965) and apartheid South Africa (1948-1964). For each, revise the nature of discrimination, protests and action, and the role and significance of key actors and groups.

Related: [Rights and protest study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-4/) and [Rights and protest practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-4-practice/).

## United States: the legal and legislative sequence

| Date | Ruling or law | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1954 | *Brown v. Board of Education* | School segregation unconstitutional |
| 1955 | *Brown II* | Desegregation "with all deliberate speed", which allowed delay |
| 1956 | *Browder v. Gayle* (upheld by the Supreme Court) | Bus segregation in Montgomery unconstitutional |
| 1957 | Civil Rights Act | Civil Rights Commission created; weak on voting |
| 1964 | Twenty-fourth Amendment | Poll taxes banned in federal elections |
| 1964 | Civil Rights Act | Segregation in public places and job discrimination outlawed |
| 1965 | Voting Rights Act | Literacy tests suspended; federal examiners sent to register voters |

## United States: campaigns and what each achieved

- **Montgomery (1955-1956)**: community-wide boycott; ended bus segregation; launched King and led to the SCLC (1957).
- **Little Rock (1957)**: federal troops enforced *Brown*; showed that presidents could be forced to act.
- **Greensboro sit-ins (1960)**: student-led; spread rapidly; led to SNCC's founding.
- **Freedom Rides (1961)**: tested interstate desegregation; violence in Alabama forced federal intervention.
- **Albany (1961-1962)**: limited success because the police chief avoided public brutality, a lesson for later campaigns.
- **Birmingham (1963)**: police brutality on camera; Kennedy proposed civil rights legislation in June 1963.
- **March on Washington (1963)**: national demonstration of support.
- **Freedom Summer (1964)**: voter registration in Mississippi; murders of three workers; the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenged the official delegation.
- **Selma (1965)**: "Bloody Sunday" (7 March); Voting Rights Act passed in August.

## South Africa: the apartheid laws

| Law | Year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act | 1949 | Banned marriages across racial categories |
| Population Registration Act | 1950 | Classified every person by race |
| Group Areas Act | 1950 | Allocated residential and business areas by race |
| Suppression of Communism Act | 1950 | Wide powers to ban opponents as "communists" |
| Bantu Authorities Act | 1951 | Tribal authorities; basis for homelands |
| Bantu Education Act | 1953 | State control of Black education |
| Reservation of Separate Amenities Act | 1953 | Segregated public facilities |
| Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act | 1959 | Planned "self-governing" ethnic homelands |

## South Africa: the turn from non-violence

1. **1949**: ANC adopts the Youth League's Programme of Action (boycotts, strikes, civil disobedience).
2. **1952**: Defiance Campaign; thousands arrested; membership grows.
3. **1955**: Congress of the People adopts the Freedom Charter.
4. **1956**: women's march on the Union Buildings in Pretoria against passes; Treason Trial begins.
5. **1959**: Africanists break away to form the PAC under Robert Sobukwe.
6. **1960**: Sharpeville (21 March); state of emergency; ANC and PAC banned.
7. **1961**: South Africa becomes a republic; Treason Trial ends in acquittals; MK sabotage begins (December).
8. **1962**: Mandela arrested.
9. **1963-1964**: Rivonia raid and trial; life sentences.

## Comparison grid

| Theme | United States | South Africa |
|---|---|---|
| Legal framework | Federal constitution could be used against state laws | Parliament made discrimination the law; courts offered little redress |
| Methods | Non-violent direct action, litigation, voter registration | Petitions, defiance, boycotts, then sabotage |
| State response | Southern resistance; federal government eventually intervened | Bans, emergency powers, trials and imprisonment |
| Outside opinion | Television and Cold War image mattered to Washington | Growing condemnation after Sharpeville, little immediate effect |
| Outcome by end date | Major legislation in 1964 and 1965 | Leaders imprisoned; movement driven underground |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Jim Crow | The system of segregation laws in the US South |
| Direct action | Non-violent protest designed to force a response, such as sit-ins and marches |
| De jure / de facto segregation | Segregation by law / segregation in practice without law |
| Petty apartheid | Segregation of everyday facilities |
| Grand apartheid | Territorial separation through homelands |
| Bantustan | An ethnic "homeland" for Black South Africans |
| Pass laws | Laws requiring Black Africans to carry documents controlling movement |
| Freedom Charter | 1955 statement of a non-racial, democratic South Africa |

## Exam traps

- **Wrong organisation.** Sharpeville was a PAC protest; the Freedom Rides were organised by CORE, later joined by SNCC.
- **Overlooking the federal government.** In the US case, presidents, Congress and the courts are part of the story, not just the protesters.
- **Treating law as reality.** *Brown* did not desegregate Southern schools quickly; say how slowly change came.
- **Ending too late or too early.** US: 1954-1965. South Africa: 1948-1964.
- **Ignoring visual sources.** Photographs from Birmingham or Sharpeville are common Question 1(b) sources; their message is often about brutality and sympathy.

## Self-test

1. What did the Supreme Court rule in *Brown v. Board of Education*?
   *Segregated public schools were unconstitutional.*
2. Why did Eisenhower send troops to Little Rock in 1957?
   *To enforce the integration of Central High School against state resistance.*
3. Which organisation grew out of the 1960 sit-ins?
   *SNCC, the Student Non-violent Coordinating Committee.*
4. Which 1963 campaign led Kennedy to propose a civil rights bill?
   *Birmingham.*
5. What did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 do?
   *It suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters.*
6. Which organisation did Malcolm X leave in 1964?
   *The Nation of Islam.*
7. Name the act that classified every South African by race.
   *The Population Registration Act (1950).*
8. What is the difference between petty and grand apartheid?
   *Petty apartheid segregated everyday facilities; grand apartheid separated territory through homelands.*
9. Which organisation called the protest at Sharpeville?
   *The PAC, the Pan Africanist Congress.*
10. What was Umkhonto we Sizwe, and when did it begin operations?
    *The ANC's armed wing, which began sabotage in December 1961.*
11. Which ANC president remained committed to non-violence and received the Nobel Peace Prize?
    *Albert Luthuli.*
12. What was the outcome of the Rivonia Trial?
    *Mandela and other leaders were sentenced to life imprisonment in 1964.*
