Condensed recall for subtopic 5.1 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070. For explanations and three worked examples, use the [energetics study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-exothermic-and-endothermic-reactions/); to practise, try the [energetics practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-exothermic-and-endothermic-reactions-practice/).

## The definitions

- **Exothermic:** transfers thermal energy **to** the surroundings; the temperature of the surroundings **increases**.
- **Endothermic:** takes in thermal energy **from** the surroundings; the temperature of the surroundings **decreases**.
- **Enthalpy change, ΔH:** the transfer of thermal energy during a reaction. Negative for exothermic; positive for endothermic. Units: kJ/mol.
- **Activation energy, Ea:** the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.
- **Bond breaking** is endothermic. **Bond making** is exothermic.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Surroundings | Everything outside the reacting particles: solvent, container, thermometer, air |
| ΔH | Enthalpy change of reaction, in kJ/mol |
| Ea | Activation energy |
| Bond energy | Energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond (and released when it forms) |
| Reaction pathway diagram | Graph of energy against progress of reaction |
| Catalyst | Lowers Ea; does not change ΔH |

## Sign summary

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal energy | Given out to surroundings | Taken in from surroundings |
| Temperature of surroundings | Rises | Falls |
| ΔH | Negative | Positive |
| Products compared with reactants on a diagram | Lower | Higher |
| Bonds | More energy released making than used breaking | More energy used breaking than released making |

## Examples to know

- Exothermic: combustion, neutralisation, metal + acid, adding water to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate.
- Endothermic: thermal decomposition (e.g. of calcium carbonate), photosynthesis, citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate, removing water from hydrated copper(II) sulfate by heating.

## Pathway diagram checklist

- Axes: **energy** (vertical) and **progress of reaction** (horizontal).
- Reactant line on the left, product line on the right, both labelled with names or formulae.
- One smooth hump between them.
- **Ea:** arrow from the reactant line **up** to the top of the hump.
- **ΔH:** arrow from the reactant line to the product line; down for exothermic, up for endothermic.
- With a catalyst: a lower hump; same reactant and product lines; same ΔH.

Reading values: ΔH = product energy − reactant energy. Ea = peak energy − reactant energy.

## Bond energy method

```
ΔH = energy to break bonds (reactants) − energy released making bonds (products)
```

1. Write or picture displayed formulae.
2. List every bond broken, multiplied by coefficients; add.
3. List every bond made; add.
4. Subtract: broken − made. Give sign and units.

Counting reminders: O₂ is O=O; CO₂ has **two** C=O; H₂O has **two** O–H; CH₄ has **four** C–H; NH₃ has **three** N–H; N₂ is N≡N.

## One condensed bond energy example

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, with N≡N = 945, H–H = 436, N–H = 391 (kJ/mol):

- broken: 945 + (3 × 436) = 2253 kJ
- made: 2 × 3 × 391 = 2346 kJ (two NH₃, each with three N–H)
- ΔH = 2253 − 2346 = **−93 kJ/mol**, exothermic

The reverse reaction, 2NH₃ → N₂ + 3H₂, has ΔH = **+93 kJ/mol**: same size, opposite sign.

## Reading temperature data

- Record the starting temperature, then the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.
- Change = final − initial. A positive change (rise) means exothermic; a negative change (fall) means endothermic.
- An insulated container, such as a polystyrene cup with a lid, reduces thermal energy exchange with the air, so the change measured is closer to the true value.
- Stir the mixture so the thermometer reads the temperature of the whole solution.

## Exam traps

- A temperature rise means ΔH is **negative**, not positive.
- Ea is measured from the **reactants**, not the products.
- The ΔH arrow starts at the reactants, not at the peak.
- "Bond breaking releases energy" is wrong: it needs energy.
- An exothermic reaction can still need heating to start; that is the activation energy, not evidence that it is endothermic.
- A catalyst does not change ΔH.
- Don't forget the + sign for endothermic answers; "+11 kJ/mol" scores, "11" may not.
- Double and triple bonds have their own values; never use C–O for C=O.

## Self-test

1. Define an endothermic reaction in terms of the surroundings.
2. A reaction mixture's temperature falls from 22.0 °C to 15.5 °C. State the sign of ΔH.
3. Define activation energy.
4. Give **one** example of an exothermic reaction and **one** of an endothermic reaction.
5. On a pathway diagram, the reactants are at 80 kJ/mol, the peak at 140 kJ/mol and the products at 20 kJ/mol. Find ΔH and Ea.
6. In question 5, is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?
7. What happens to Ea and ΔH when a catalyst is added?
8. Is bond making exothermic or endothermic?
9. How many O–H bonds are made in forming 3H₂O?
10. Bonds broken total 2648 kJ; bonds made total 3466 kJ. Calculate ΔH.
11. Explain why the reaction in question 10 is exothermic, in terms of bonds.
12. Why does a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen not react until a spark is applied?

**Answers**

1. A reaction that takes in thermal energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
2. Positive (the reaction is endothermic).
3. The minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.
4. Exothermic: e.g. combustion of methane or neutralisation. Endothermic: e.g. thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate or photosynthesis.
5. ΔH = 20 − 80 = −60 kJ/mol; Ea = 140 − 80 = 60 kJ/mol.
6. Exothermic: products are lower than reactants.
7. Ea decreases; ΔH stays the same.
8. Exothermic.
9. Six.
10. 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ/mol.
11. More energy is released making the new bonds than is taken in breaking the old bonds.
12. Too few colliding particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy; the spark supplies it.
