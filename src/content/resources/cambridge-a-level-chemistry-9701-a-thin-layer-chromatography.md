This guide covers subtopic **37.1, Thin-layer chromatography**, from Topic 37, Analytical techniques,
of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The
broader [Chromatography: Thin-Layer and Gas/Liquid study guide](/resources/a-chromatography-tlc-and-glc/)
sets out the definitions; this page shows how to read a plate, how to predict which compound travels
further from its structure, and what happens to Rf values when the solvent is changed.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 37.1 has three learning outcomes:

1. describe and understand the terms: "stationary phase, for example aluminium oxide (on a solid
   support)"; "mobile phase; a polar or non-polar solvent"; "Rf value"; "solvent front and baseline"
2. interpret Rf values
3. "explain the differences in Rf values in terms of interaction with the stationary phase and of
   relative solubility in the mobile phase"

## The parts of a TLC plate

- **Stationary phase:** a thin, even layer of a solid such as **aluminium oxide** (silica is also
  common) spread on a flat **solid support** such as a glass, plastic or aluminium sheet. The surface
  of aluminium oxide is **polar**, so polar molecules are attracted to it.
- **Mobile phase:** the **solvent**, which may be **polar** (for example ethanol) or **non-polar**
  (for example hexane), or a mixture. It rises up the plate by capillary action and carries the
  sample with it.
- **Baseline:** a line near the bottom of the plate where the sample spots are placed before the
  plate is put in the solvent. It sits above the solvent level so the spots do not dissolve into the
  solvent pool.
- **Solvent front:** the furthest point the solvent reaches. It is marked as soon as the plate is
  removed, before the solvent evaporates.

## The Rf value

    Rf = distance travelled by the spot (from the baseline to the centre of the spot)
         ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front (from the baseline)

- Both distances are measured **from the baseline**, not from the bottom of the plate.
- Measure to the **centre** of the spot.
- Rf has **no units** and lies between 0 and 1.
- For a given stationary phase, mobile phase and temperature, a compound's Rf is reproducible.

## Interpreting Rf values

**Identifying a component.** Run the unknown mixture next to pure reference samples on the **same
plate**, so the conditions are identical. A spot in the mixture with the same Rf as a reference
(and the same appearance) is probably that compound.

**Stronger evidence.** Two different compounds can, by chance, have the same Rf in one solvent.
Repeating with a different mobile phase and getting matching Rf values again makes the identification
much more convincing.

**Counting components.** Each separate spot is at least one component. The number of spots is
therefore the **minimum** number of components, because two compounds with the same Rf would appear
as one spot.

**Purity.** A pure compound gives a single spot. Extra spots show impurities.

## Explaining differences in Rf values

As the solvent moves, each compound is continuously shared between two places: **adsorbed on the
stationary phase** (held still) and **dissolved in the mobile phase** (carried up). Its Rf depends on
the balance between them.

- **Stronger interaction with the stationary phase → lower Rf.** On polar aluminium oxide, compounds
  that can form hydrogen bonds or strong permanent dipole attractions with the surface are held back.
- **Greater solubility in the mobile phase → higher Rf.** A compound that dissolves well in the
  solvent spends more of its time moving.

**With a non-polar mobile phase on aluminium oxide:** polar compounds are strongly adsorbed and only
poorly soluble in the solvent, so they have **low** Rf values; non-polar compounds are weakly adsorbed
and dissolve well, so they have **high** Rf values.

**Changing to a more polar mobile phase:** polar compounds become more soluble in the mobile phase, so
their Rf values **increase**. If the solvent is too polar, all the compounds may move close to the
solvent front and separate poorly. Choosing the mobile phase is about making the components differ in
how they balance the two effects.

## Worked example 1: calculating and interpreting Rf values

**Question.** On a plate, the solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. Mixture **M** gives spots at
2.0 cm and 5.6 cm. Reference compounds give: **A** 5.6 cm, **B** 3.2 cm, **C** 2.0 cm. Identify the
components of **M**.

**Answer.**

| Spot | Distance / cm | Rf |
|---|---|---|
| **M**, lower | 2.0 | 2.0 / 8.0 = 0.25 |
| **M**, upper | 5.6 | 5.6 / 8.0 = 0.70 |
| **A** | 5.6 | 0.70 |
| **B** | 3.2 | 0.40 |
| **C** | 2.0 | 0.25 |

**M** probably contains **A** and **C**, and not **B**. It contains at least two components.

*Examiner's note:* "probably" matters. To confirm, run the plate again in a different solvent.

## Worked example 2: predicting the order of Rf values

**Question.** A mixture of ethyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOCH₂CH₃, phenylmethanol, C₆H₅CH₂OH, and benzoic acid,
C₆H₅COOH, is separated on aluminium oxide using a mainly non-polar solvent. Predict the order of Rf
values and explain it.

**Answer.** Predicted order: **ethyl benzoate (highest) > phenylmethanol > benzoic acid (lowest)**.

- Benzoic acid has a –COOH group, which forms strong hydrogen bonds with the polar surface. It is
  held most strongly and is least soluble in the non-polar solvent, so it has the lowest Rf.
- Phenylmethanol has an –OH group, which also hydrogen-bonds to the surface, but less strongly than
  –COOH, so it is intermediate.
- Ethyl benzoate has polar C=O and C–O bonds but no O–H, so it cannot donate a hydrogen bond. It is
  adsorbed least and dissolves best in the solvent, so it travels furthest.

## Worked example 3: a change of solvent

**Question.** The plate in Worked example 2 is repeated with a more polar solvent. Predict how the
Rf values change.

**Answer.** All three Rf values rise, because each compound is more soluble in the more polar mobile
phase. The polar compounds, especially benzoic acid, are affected most, so the spots move closer
together and may separate less well.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring distances from the bottom edge of the plate instead of the baseline.
- Giving an Rf greater than 1, which means the two distances were swapped.
- Quoting units for Rf.
- Saying a single spot proves only one compound is present; two compounds could share an Rf.
- Explaining Rf with only one factor. Full answers mention both stationary-phase interaction and
  solubility in the mobile phase.
- Saying polar compounds always travel less far: that depends on the polarity of the mobile phase too.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** a term: give the definition plus the syllabus example (aluminium oxide on a solid
  support; a polar or non-polar solvent).
- **Calculate** Rf: show both distances and give the answer to two significant figures, matching the
  data.
- **Interpret:** compare Rf values with references run under the same conditions, and use
  "probably" or "consistent with".
- **Explain** differences: name the intermolecular force with the stationary phase, then the
  solubility in the mobile phase, and link each to a higher or lower Rf.
