## Section A

**1.** Identify the factor of production described in each case.

**(a)** Oil deposits beneath a company's site. **[1]**

**(b)** A website a florist uses to take orders. **[1]**

**(c)** The time and skill of a call-centre adviser. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the term opportunity cost, using an example from a business. **[2]**

**3.** A furniture maker sells a bookcase for 45.00. The timber, screws and varnish it buys in cost 27.50 per bookcase. It sells 400 bookcases a year.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per bookcase. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total added value for the year. **[1]**

**4.** Outline **two** ways a hairdresser could increase the added value of a haircut. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why a business with a high added value can still make a loss. **[2]**

**6.** Leela owns a small shop. She put in her own savings, decides what stock to buy, and works behind the counter every day. Identify **two** factors of production that Leela provides, and explain each. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Calloway Bikes builds bicycles to order. Each bicycle sells for 620. The frame, wheels and components it buys in cost 410 per bicycle. It sells 85 bicycles a month. Wages and rent total 12,500 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per bicycle. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total added value per month. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the profit per month. **[1]**

**(d)** Calloway Bikes plans to offer a custom-fit service, measuring each customer so the bicycle suits their body. Explain how this could increase added value, and one risk of doing so. **[3]**

**8.** Harbour Café has 8,000 of savings to invest and can choose only one project: a new coffee machine (expected extra profit 2,600 a year), outdoor seating (3,100 a year) or a delivery bicycle (1,900 a year).

**(a)** The owner chooses the outdoor seating. Identify the opportunity cost and justify your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Consider **two** non-financial factors the owner should think about before choosing the outdoor seating. **[4]**

**9.** A government decides to spend money on a new airport. Explain what is meant by the opportunity cost of this decision. **[2]**

**10.** A car manufacturer plans to replace some of its assembly-line workers with robots.

**(a)** State which factor of production is being increased and which is being reduced. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage to the manufacturer of this change. **[4]**

**11.** A clothing shop has 200 coats left at the end of winter. The bought-in cost of each coat was 12.50. It sells them in a clearance sale at 9.00 each.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per coat and in total. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the shop might still decide to hold the sale. **[2]**

**12.** Fernley Bakery sells 2,000 loaves a month at 3.80 each. Bought-in inputs cost 1.40 per loaf. It is considering two plans:

- **Plan A**: rebrand as a premium organic bakery. Price rises to 4.60 and input cost to 1.90 per loaf. Sales are expected to fall to 1,700 loaves a month.
- **Plan B**: buy flour in bulk. Input cost falls to 1.15 per loaf. Price and sales stay the same.

**(a)** Calculate the current total monthly added value. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total monthly added value under Plan A and under Plan B. **[2]**

**(c)** Justify which plan Fernley Bakery should choose. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Land [1].

**(b)** Capital [1].

**(c)** Labour [1].

**2.** Opportunity cost is the benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made [1]. For example, if a business uses its savings to buy a new machine, the opportunity cost might be the extra profit it could have earned by spending the money on advertising instead [1].

*Tip:* any example works if it names a specific alternative that was given up.

**3. (a)** 45.00 − 27.50 = **17.50** [1].

**(b)** 17.50 × 400 = **7,000** [1].

**4.** Any two ways, each with development [2 each]:

- Improve the service, for example offering a head massage or styling advice [1], so customers are willing to pay a higher price than the extra cost [1].
- Build a stronger brand or reputation, for example through a smart salon and well-known stylists [1], allowing a premium price [1].
- Buy hair products in bulk or from a cheaper supplier [1], lowering the cost of bought-in inputs per haircut [1].

*Tip:* "Charge more" on its own earns little; say what makes customers willing to pay more.

**5.** Added value only takes away the cost of bought-in inputs [1]. Wages, rent and other overheads must still be paid out of it, and if they are larger than the added value the business makes a loss [1].

**6.** Enterprise, because she takes the risk with her savings and organises the business by deciding what to buy [1]. Labour, because she works behind the counter serving customers [1].

**7. (a)** 620 − 410 = **210** [1].

**(b)** 210 × 85 = **17,850** [1].

**(c)** 17,850 − 12,500 = **5,350** [1].

**(d)** A custom fit makes each bicycle more valuable to the customer, so Calloway Bikes can charge a higher price [1]; if the price rises by more than any extra bought-in cost, added value per bicycle increases [1]. Risk: measuring customers takes skilled staff time, raising wage costs, or customers may not pay the higher price, so sales could fall [1].

**8. (a)** The coffee machine, worth 2,600 a year [1], because it is the next best alternative to the seating; the delivery bicycle (1,900) is not counted [1].

**(b)** Any two, each identified [1] and applied to the café [1]:

- Weather: outdoor seating may be little used in cold or wet months, so the expected profit may be uncertain.
- Planning permission or permission from the landlord may be needed, which could delay or prevent the project.
- Staff time: serving extra tables may need more staff or slow service indoors.
- Customer image: an attractive outdoor area could raise the café's profile and attract new customers.

**9.** Opportunity cost is the benefit of the next best alternative given up [1]. The money spent on the airport cannot be used for something else, such as building hospitals or schools, so the benefit of that next best use is the opportunity cost [1].

**10. (a)** Capital is increased; labour is reduced [1].

**(b)** Advantage: robots can work for long hours with consistent quality [1], so output per day can rise and wage costs fall over time [1]. Disadvantage: robots are expensive to buy and maintain [1], and a breakdown can stop the assembly line, or remaining staff may lose motivation because of job losses [1].

**11. (a)** 9.00 − 12.50 = **−3.50** per coat [1]; −3.50 × 200 = **−700** in total [1].

**(b)** The coats have already been paid for, so selling them for 9.00 brings in some money rather than none [1]; it also frees storage and shop space for new stock that can be sold at a full price [1].

**12. (a)** (3.80 − 1.40) × 2,000 = 2.40 × 2,000 = **4,800** [1].

**(b)** Plan A: (4.60 − 1.90) × 1,700 = 2.70 × 1,700 = **4,590** [1]. Plan B: (3.80 − 1.15) × 2,000 = 2.65 × 2,000 = **5,300** [1].

**(c)** Plan A raises added value per loaf from 2.40 to 2.70, but because sales fall, total added value falls by 210 to 4,590 [1]. Plan B raises total added value by 500 to 5,300, with no change in price [1]. However, if the bulk flour is lower in quality, customers may notice and sales could fall; and Plan A might attract new customers over time if the organic brand becomes popular [1]. On the figures given, Plan B is the better choice because it gives the highest total added value without relying on an uncertain sales forecast [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a payment, such as wages or rent, when a factor of production is asked for.
- Including wages or rent in bought-in input costs when calculating added value.
- Judging a price rise on added value per unit without working out the total at the new sales level.
- Giving the sum of all rejected options, or the cost of the chosen option, as the opportunity cost.
- Recommending a plan without using the figures, or without saying what could make the choice wrong.
