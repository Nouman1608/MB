This guide covers section **4.3.3 Plant disease** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for first
teaching from 2016: 4.3.3.1 Detection and identification of plant diseases, and 4.3.3.2 Plant defence
responses. The whole section is **Biology only**, so it is not in Combined Science. Within it, the lists of
**signs of disease** and **ways of identifying a disease** are marked **Higher tier only**. The section is
assessed on Paper 1.

## What the specification limits you to

The specification is precise about what you need, which keeps this section manageable:

| Area | Content you need |
|---|---|
| Plant pathogens and pests | Tobacco mosaic virus (viral), black spot (fungal) and aphids (insects) only |
| Ion deficiencies | Nitrate ions (protein synthesis, so growth) and magnesium ions (chlorophyll) only |
| Signs of disease (Higher tier only) | Stunted growth, spots on leaves, areas of decay (rot), growths, malformed stems or leaves, discolouration, the presence of pests |
| Identification (Higher tier only) | Gardening manual or website; taking infected plants to a laboratory; testing kits containing monoclonal antibodies |
| Defences | Physical, chemical and mechanical responses listed in 4.3.3.2 |

Plants can be infected by viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens and damaged by insects, but in the exam you
apply ideas to other examples only when you are given information about them.

## The three named problems

### Tobacco mosaic virus (viral)

TMV affects many species, including tomatoes. It produces a **mosaic pattern of discolouration** on the leaves.
Discoloured parts of the leaf have less chlorophyll, so less light is absorbed, the rate of **photosynthesis**
falls, less glucose is made and growth is reduced.

### Black spot (fungal)

Black spot affects roses. **Purple or black spots** form on the leaves, which often **turn yellow and drop
early**. With fewer, damaged leaves, photosynthesis falls. The fungus is **spread by water or wind**, and is
treated with **fungicides** and/or by **removing and destroying affected leaves**.

### Aphids (insects)

Aphids are small insects that feed on plant sap. Large numbers weaken the plant. The **presence of pests** is
itself one of the signs of disease, so seeing aphids on a stunted plant is evidence of their effect.

## Ion deficiencies are not infections

A plant can look unhealthy without any pathogen. The specification names two ion deficiencies, and the key to
both is what the ion is **used for**.

| Ion | Used for | Deficiency | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate | Making amino acids, so proteins | **Stunted growth** | Without proteins, new cells cannot be made |
| Magnesium | Making chlorophyll | **Chlorosis** (yellowing leaves) | Less chlorophyll, so less light absorbed and less photosynthesis |

Both ions are absorbed from the soil by the roots. This links to 4.4.1.3, where glucose and nitrate ions are
used to make amino acids. Understanding deficiencies lets growers give plants the optimum conditions, for
example by adding a fertiliser containing the missing ion.

**Worked example: reasoning from evidence**

A grower sees that one bed of tomato plants has pale yellow leaves but no spots, no pests and no mosaic
pattern. Another bed is short with small leaves of normal colour. Suggest the cause in each bed.

> Bed 1: magnesium deficiency [1], because yellow leaves (chlorosis) without spots or pests point to too little
> chlorophyll rather than a pathogen [1].
> Bed 2: nitrate deficiency [1], because stunted growth with normal leaf colour suggests too little protein for
> growth, not a lack of chlorophyll [1].

Notice how each answer uses the evidence twice: once to choose the cause, once to rule out alternatives.

## 4.3.3.1 Detecting and identifying disease (Higher tier only)

**Detection** means noticing that something is wrong. The signs are: stunted growth; spots on leaves; areas of
decay (rot); growths; malformed stems or leaves; discolouration; the presence of pests.

**Identification** means finding the cause. A grower can:

1. compare the symptoms with a **gardening manual or website**;
2. take infected plants to a **laboratory** to identify the pathogen;
3. use **testing kits that contain monoclonal antibodies**, which bind only to an antigen on one particular
   pathogen.

A sign alone rarely identifies the cause. Stunted growth could come from a pathogen, aphids or nitrate
deficiency, which is why the identification steps exist.

## 4.3.3.2 Plant defence responses

Plants cannot move away from attack, so they rely on structures and chemicals.

### Physical defences (resist invasion by microorganisms)

- **Cellulose cell walls**: a strong barrier around each cell.
- **Tough waxy cuticle** on leaves: a barrier on the leaf surface.
- **Layers of dead cells** on the surface of stems, such as **bark** on trees, which **fall off**, taking any
  pathogens on them away from the plant.

### Chemical defences

- **Antibacterial chemicals**, which kill bacteria.
- **Poisons** to deter herbivores, which learn not to eat the plant or are harmed if they do.

### Mechanical adaptations

- **Thorns and hairs** deter animals from feeding.
- **Leaves which droop or curl when touched**, which can dislodge insects or make the plant look less
  appetising.
- **Mimicry to trick animals**, where part of the plant resembles something else so that animals avoid it.

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: Describe one physical and one chemical defence of plants, and explain how each protects the plant. [4]*

> A waxy cuticle covers the leaves [1]. It is a barrier, so microorganisms cannot easily get into the leaf
> tissue [1]. Some plants make poisons [1], which deter herbivores from eating them [1].

The answer pairs each defence with its effect. Listing four defences with no explanations would score two at
most.

## Links to the rest of the course

Plant disease questions often draw on other sections, so expect to combine ideas:

- **Photosynthesis (4.4.1):** every named disease reduces growth through reduced photosynthesis, and magnesium
  deficiency acts on chlorophyll, one of the four factors affecting its rate.
- **Plant tissues (4.2.3):** the waxy cuticle and the phloem, where aphids feed on sap carrying sugars, belong
  to leaf and stem structure.
- **Monoclonal antibodies (4.3.2, Higher tier only):** testing kits work because each antibody binds to one
  antigen on one pathogen.
- **Adaptations (4.7):** thorns, hairs and mimicry are also examples of adaptations that help plants survive.

## Common mistakes

- Calling magnesium or nitrate deficiency a "disease caused by a pathogen".
- Saying nitrate is needed "for chlorophyll". Nitrate is for protein; magnesium is for chlorophyll.
- Saying plant diseases "stop photosynthesis" rather than reduce it.
- Using fungicide as a treatment for a virus such as TMV.
- Describing thorns as a physical defence against microorganisms. The specification lists thorns as a
  mechanical adaptation that deters animals.
- Naming a sign of disease when a question asks for a method of identification.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Suggest** a cause of disease: use every clue in the description and say why other causes are less likely.
- **Explain** why a disease reduces growth: link damage to leaves, then less photosynthesis, then less glucose.
- **Describe** a defence: name the structure or chemical and what it does.
- **Compare** methods of identification: give paired points, for example "a test kit gives a quick result on
  site, whereas a laboratory takes longer but can identify a wider range of pathogens".
