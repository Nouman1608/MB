This guide covers subtopic **14.6 Blood glucose control** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to explain the need to control blood glucose concentration; to describe how the liver and pancreas control it, including the roles of insulin and glucagon; and to describe the signs of Type 1 diabetes (limited to increased blood glucose concentration and glucose in the urine) and its treatment by the administration of insulin.

## Why blood glucose must be controlled

Glucose is the main fuel for respiration. Every cell needs a steady supply, and the brain is especially dependent on it. The glucose in the blood comes from digested carbohydrate, and the amount varies through the day: it rises after a meal and falls during exercise or between meals. The body therefore keeps the concentration within narrow limits. This is homeostasis, with a set point and negative feedback.

### If the concentration is too low

Cells cannot respire fast enough. The brain cells are affected first, and this can lead to confusion and, if severe, loss of consciousness. Muscles run short of energy.

### If the concentration is too high

A high concentration of glucose in the blood lowers the water potential of the blood. Water can then be drawn out of cells by osmosis, which disturbs the working of the cells. In addition, if the glucose concentration is too high, not all the glucose can be reabsorbed in the kidney tubules, so glucose appears in the urine. Over a long period, high glucose can damage the body.

For an exam answer, the standard points are: low glucose means too little for respiration, and high glucose upsets the water balance of cells and is lost in the urine.

## The organs involved

| Organ | Role |
|---|---|
| Pancreas | Detects the blood glucose concentration and releases the hormones insulin and glucagon |
| Liver | The target organ for both hormones; stores glucose as glycogen and releases glucose |

**Glycogen** is an insoluble storage carbohydrate made of many glucose molecules. Because it is insoluble, it does not change the water potential of the liver cells. The liver can convert glucose to glycogen and glycogen back to glucose. Muscles store some glycogen as well, but the control of blood glucose depends on the liver.

## The hormones

| Hormone | Made by | Triggered when | Effect on the liver | Effect on blood glucose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insulin | Pancreas | Blood glucose is too high | Glucose is converted to glycogen for storage | Falls |
| Glucagon | Pancreas | Blood glucose is too low | Glycogen is converted to glucose and released into the blood | Rises |

Insulin and glucagon have opposite effects and work against each other, which keeps the concentration steady.

### Negative feedback loop: glucose too high

1. After a meal, digested carbohydrate is absorbed and the blood glucose concentration rises above the set point.
2. The pancreas detects the rise.
3. The pancreas secretes **insulin** into the blood.
4. Insulin is carried to the liver (its target organ), which converts glucose into **glycogen**.
5. The blood glucose concentration falls to the set point, and insulin secretion falls.

### Negative feedback loop: glucose too low

1. During exercise or a long gap between meals, respiration uses glucose and the blood glucose concentration falls below the set point.
2. The pancreas detects the fall.
3. The pancreas secretes **glucagon**.
4. Glucagon is carried to the liver, which converts **glycogen into glucose** and releases it into the blood.
5. The concentration rises to the set point, and glucagon secretion falls.

A good short summary is: **insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it.** A common memory aid is that insulin "puts glucose in store" and glucagon "gets glucose out".

### The role of adrenaline

Adrenaline also raises blood glucose. It is released in times of danger or excitement, and it causes the liver to release glucose, giving muscles extra fuel. It acts as an emergency boost rather than as part of the everyday control by insulin and glucagon.

## Worked example 1: explaining a graph

*The graph data show the blood glucose concentration of a healthy person after a carbohydrate-rich meal.*

| Time after the meal (minutes) | Blood glucose concentration (mg per 100 cm3) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 90 |
| 30 | 130 |
| 60 | 110 |
| 120 | 92 |

- The increase in the first 30 minutes is 40 mg per 100 cm3, which is 40 divided by 90 x 100 = 44.4 per cent.
- The rise is caused by absorption of glucose from the digested food.
- From 30 minutes the concentration falls because the pancreas releases insulin, and the liver converts glucose to glycogen.
- By 120 minutes it has almost returned to the starting level: negative feedback has restored the set point.

## Type 1 diabetes

**Type 1 diabetes** is a condition in which the pancreas does not make enough insulin. Without insulin, the liver cannot convert glucose into glycogen properly, so the blood glucose concentration stays high after a meal.

### Signs

The syllabus limits the signs to two.

1. **Increased blood glucose concentration.**
2. **Glucose in the urine.** The kidney filters glucose from the blood, but when the concentration is too high the tubules cannot reabsorb all of it, so some is lost in the urine.

### Treatment

The treatment is the **administration of insulin**, usually by injection, as insulin is a protein and would be digested if swallowed. The dose is matched to the amount of carbohydrate eaten and the activity level, so that blood glucose is brought down towards the normal range. The person also needs to pay attention to meals. Insulin treatment replaces the missing hormone, but it does not cure the cause.

### Worked example 2: data about diabetes

*Two people drink a glucose solution. The table shows their blood glucose concentration.*

| Time (minutes) | Person A (mg per 100 cm3) | Person B (mg per 100 cm3) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 88 | 150 |
| 60 | 128 | 280 |
| 180 | 90 | 245 |

- Person A has a normal response: the concentration rises and falls back to near the start, so negative feedback is working.
- Person B starts high, rises very high and stays high after 180 minutes. This pattern is consistent with Type 1 diabetes, as insulin is missing.
- For person B, the fall from 280 to 245 is only 35, which is 35 divided by 280 x 100 = 12.5 per cent. For person A the fall from 128 to 90 is 38, which is 29.7 per cent. A much smaller fall shows the weaker response.
- Glucose would be expected in the urine of person B.

## Worked example 3: exercise and fasting

*A cyclist rides for two hours without eating. Describe and explain the changes in her blood glucose control.*

1. Her muscles respire quickly, using glucose from the blood, so the blood glucose concentration begins to fall below the set point.
2. The pancreas detects the fall and releases glucagon. Insulin release is reduced.
3. Glucagon is carried to the liver, which converts stored glycogen into glucose and releases it into the blood.
4. The glucose supply keeps pace with the demand, and the blood glucose concentration stays within the normal range.
5. If she is frightened or excited, for example in a race, adrenaline also causes the liver to release glucose.

Notice that the answer names the sensor (the pancreas), the hormone, the target organ and the change in the liver. A complete answer mentions all four.

## Why two hormones are better than one

Having two hormones with opposite effects gives control in both directions. If the concentration overshoots in either direction, the opposite hormone corrects it. This is why the system keeps the concentration so steady. If the body only had a way of lowering glucose, a long fast would leave the brain short of fuel.

## Glucose in the urine

The kidney filters glucose from the blood into the nephron, and in a healthy person all the glucose is reabsorbed. In Type 1 diabetes the blood glucose concentration can be so high that the tubules cannot reabsorb all of it, so some stays in the filtrate and leaves in the urine. A simple test of the urine can therefore be used as a sign that the blood glucose is high. This is one of the two signs the syllabus lists. Use the word "sign" in your answer and give the reason in terms of reabsorption.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that insulin turns glycogen into glucose. This is glucagon.
- Writing that the pancreas stores glucose. The liver stores it, as glycogen.
- Confusing glucose, glycogen and glucagon. Check the spelling.
- Forgetting to say the liver is the target organ.
- Writing that people with Type 1 diabetes have too much insulin.
- Saying that insulin is taken as a tablet. It is injected, as it is a protein that would be digested.
- Writing that diabetics have "sugar in the blood" instead of "a high blood glucose concentration".
- Not linking glucose in the urine to the failure of reabsorption in the kidney.

## Command words

- **Explain**: give the need for control, using respiration and osmosis.
- **Describe**: give the control loops in order.
- **State**: give the signs of Type 1 diabetes.
- **Suggest**: use the idea of negative feedback to interpret new data.
