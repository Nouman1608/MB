## Questions

**1.** Trade concepts.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by international trade. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish between absolute advantage and comparative advantage. **[4]**

**2.** Using all its resources, Country P makes 36 tonnes of tea or 12 machines a month. Country R makes 20 tonnes of tea or 10 machines. Costs are constant.

**(a)** Which country has an absolute advantage? **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one machine in each country. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify each country's comparative advantage. **[2]**
**(d)** State the range of terms, in tonnes of tea per machine, that would benefit both, and explain why. **[2]**

**3.** Before trade, P makes 24 tonnes of tea and 4 machines, and R makes 10 tonnes of tea and 5 machines. Both then specialise completely in their comparative advantage good and trade at 2.5 tonnes of tea per machine, with P buying 4 machines.

**(a)** Calculate world output of each good before trade. **[1]**
**(b)** State output of each good after specialisation. **[1]**
**(c)** Show that both countries gain. **[4]**
**(d)** Explain why trade at 4 tonnes of tea per machine would not take place. **[2]**

**4.** In Country K (base year 100) the export price index is 118 and the import price index is 131.

**(a)** Calculate the terms of trade. **[2]**
**(b)** Interpret your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** Next year export prices rise 3% and import prices fall 2%. Calculate the new index. **[3]**

**5.** Country S has abundant land and scarce capital. Country T has abundant capital and scarce land.

**(a)** Explain what Heckscher-Ohlin theory predicts about the pattern of trade. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why actual trade may not follow this prediction. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a developing economy that exports mainly primary products might experience a deterioration in its terms of trade. **[6]**

**7.** In Country X the average wage is 24,000 and output per worker is 32,000.

**(a)** Calculate unit labour cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Wages rise 5% and productivity 2%. Calculate the percentage change in ULC. **[2]**
**(c)** In Country Y wages rise 2% and productivity 4%. Calculate the change in ULC. **[2]**
**(d)** Y's currency appreciates 3% against X's. Calculate the change in Y's ULC measured in X's currency. **[2]**
**(e)** One of Y's machines sells for 8,000 units of its currency. Calculate its foreign price when each unit buys 0.25 foreign units, and after the rate becomes 0.28, and comment. **[3]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the pattern of global trade has changed over time, giving a cause for each. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate comparative advantage as an explanation of global trade patterns. **[12]**

**10.** Country Z's price indices (year 1 = 100).

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Export prices | 100 | 104 | 99 | 95 |
| Import prices | 100 | 103 | 107 | 110 |

**(a)** Calculate the terms of trade for years 2, 3 and 4. **[3]**
**(b)** By what percentage did the index move between years 3 and 4? **[2]**
**(c)** Z exported 400 units in year 1. How many year 1 units of imports would the same volume buy in year 4? **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether this deterioration necessarily harms Z. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether aid or trade is more beneficial to a low-income economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The exchange of goods and services across national borders [1], through exports and imports [1].
**(b)** Absolute advantage is the ability to produce more, or use fewer resources per unit, than another country [2]. Comparative advantage is giving up fewer other goods to make a good [1], so a country can have it in a good even when it has absolute advantage in everything [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the contrast in resources used versus opportunity cost explicit; stating two definitions side by side without the distinction tends to earn less.

**2. (a)** **P**, since 36 is above 20 and 12 is above 10 [1].
**(b)** P: 36 / 12 = **3 tonnes of tea** [1]. R: 20 / 10 = **2 tonnes** [1].
**(c)** R has the comparative advantage in machines (2 is below 3) [1]. P has it in tea (1/3 of a machine against 1/2) [1].
**(d)** **Between 2 and 3 tonnes of tea per machine** [1]. Outside it, one country could make the good more cheaply at home [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the division for each country; a correct conclusion with no opportunity costs on the page leaves nothing to reward if it is wrong.

**3. (a)** Tea 24 + 10 = **34**, machines 4 + 5 = **9** [1].
**(b)** P makes 36 tonnes of tea and R makes 10 machines [1].
**(c)** P pays 4 x 2.5 = 10 tonnes, so has 26 tonnes and 4 machines, 2 tonnes more tea than before [2]. R has 10 - 4 = 6 machines and 10 tonnes, one more machine than before [2].
**(d)** P can make a machine at home for 3 tonnes [1], so it would not pay 4 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare consumption after trade with consumption before trade for each country in turn; comparing only world totals does not show that both gain.

**4. (a)** 118 / 131 x 100 [1] = **90.1** [1].
**(b)** A deterioration [1]. Each unit of exports now buys about 9.9% fewer imports than in the base year [1].
**(c)** Exports 118 x 1.03 = 121.54 and imports 131 x 0.98 = 128.38 [1]. Index = 121.54 / 128.38 x 100 [1] = **94.7** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Interpreting the index in words, with the direction and size, tends to separate a full answer from a bare number.

**5. (a)** The abundant factor is cheap [1], so goods using it intensively are cheaper to produce [1]. S exports land-intensive goods [1], T exports capital-intensive goods, and each imports the other [1].
**(b)** Countries can differ in technology, so costs are not set by factor endowments [2]. Much trade is in differentiated goods between similar countries, driven by variety and economies of scale [2].
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a short chain from the reason to why trade departs from the prediction.

**6.** Low income elasticity of demand for primary goods [1]: as world incomes rise, demand for them grows less than demand for manufactures [1], so export prices rise slowly against import prices [1]. Volatile, oversupplied markets [1]: many producers and low price elasticity of supply mean a rise in supply pushes prices down sharply [1], reducing the export index relative to imports [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each cause to the export price index specifically, not only to lower revenue.

**7. (a)** 24,000 / 32,000 = **0.75** [1].
**(b)** 1.05 / 1.02 = 1.0294 [1], so **+2.9%** [1].
**(c)** 1.02 / 1.04 = 0.9808 [1], so **-1.9%** [1].
**(d)** 0.9808 x 1.03 = 1.0102 [1], so **+1.0%** [1].
**(e)** 8,000 x 0.25 = 2,000, and 8,000 x 0.28 = 2,240 [1], a 12% rise [1]. The machine is less price competitive abroad, although a strong brand or quality could limit the loss in sales [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the ratio before converting to a percentage, and tie any comment to elasticity.

**8.** Emerging economies have a larger share of world exports [1], because rising incomes, investment and liberalisation raised their productive capacity [1]. Trade in services and global value chains have grown [1], because lower transport and communication costs let firms split production across countries [1]. Alternatively, manufactures have grown relative to primary goods [1], because incomes rose and technology advanced [1].
*Examiner insight:* A pattern without a cause, or a cause without a pattern, gives half the credit available.

**9.** Comparative advantage says countries export goods with a lower opportunity cost [1], and Heckscher-Ohlin links this to factor endowments [1]. It explains resource-based trade, such as a country with mineral deposits exporting minerals [1]. It shows trade raises output even if one country is better at everything [1]. But much trade is between similar economies in differentiated goods, caused by variety and scale economies [1]. Opportunity costs rise, so specialisation is partial [1]. Transport costs and barriers alter the gains [1]. Advantage is dynamic, built through education and technology [1]. Multinational firms and government policy affect location [1]. The model assumes full employment and ignores externalities [1]. Judgement: strong for resource-based trade, weaker for manufactures and services [1]. It is a useful but incomplete explanation that needs scale, differentiation and dynamic factors [1].
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that weighs which kind of trade the theory suits, instead of repeating the list, tends to be the strongest evaluation.

**10. (a)** Year 2: 104 / 103 x 100 = **101.0**; year 3: 99 / 107 x 100 = **92.5**; year 4: 95 / 110 x 100 = **86.4** [3].
**(b)** (95 / 110) / (99 / 107) - 1 using unrounded values [1] = **-6.7%** [1].
**(c)** 400 x 0.8636 [1] = **about 345 units** [1].
**(d)** Not necessarily. If lower export prices raise volume and the demand is elastic, revenue may hold up [1]. Cheaper imports in some sectors may offset the loss [1]. But if demand is inelastic, revenue and living standards fall [1]. Judgement: depends on elasticities and what is traded [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures you calculated in the evaluation, with the elasticity condition stated.

**11.** Aid can fund investment where saving is low [1] and pay for health and education [1]. Risks are dependency and corruption [1] and tied aid that favours donors [1]. Trade gives market access and foreign exchange [1] and technology transfer [1]. Risks are commodity dependence and volatile prices [1], and infant industries cannot compete [1]. Judgement: the answer depends on governance, the type and size of aid and trade capacity [1]. Aid is best used to build the capacity to trade, so they complement each other [1].
*Examiner insight:* Reaching a supported choice, with the conditions that decide it, scores better than a balanced list with no verdict.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing comparative advantage by output instead of opportunity cost.
- Inverting the opportunity cost ratio.
- Giving terms outside the beneficial range.
- Showing a rise in the terms of trade as always good.
- Using a nominal wage rise as a cost rise without productivity.
- Naming a pattern of trade with no cause.
- Evaluating with a list of assumptions and no judgement.
- Treating aid as one thing, without type or recipient.
