Everything else in economics grows from this section. Opportunity cost (1.3), production possibility curves (1.4) and the way markets allocate resources (Topic 2) all exist because of the problem defined here.

## Unpacking the definition word by word

The **economic problem** is that **resources are finite** while **wants are unlimited**, so **choices** must be made about how to use the resources.

- **Resources** are the inputs used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and enterprise (section 1.2). For a household, the resources that matter most are income and time.
- **Finite** means limited at any moment in time. There is only so much farmland, so many workers and so many hours in a day. The quantity can grow over time, but never without limit.
- **Wants** are the things people would like to have. They include **needs**, which are essential for survival (food, water, shelter, clothing), and everything beyond them.
- **Unlimited** means wants are never fully satisfied. When one want is met, another appears; as incomes rise, people want better versions and new products.
- **Choice** follows because not every want can be met. Every choice means giving something up, which leads straight to opportunity cost in section 1.3.

The result of finite resources and unlimited wants is **scarcity**. Scarcity is permanent and affects everyone, rich and poor, and every country.

### Scarcity is not the same as a shortage

A **shortage** is a market situation in which, at the current price, people want to buy more than is available. It is usually temporary: a price rise or extra supply can remove it. **Scarcity** cannot be removed. Even if every shop were full, the resources used to fill them could have been used for something else, and people would still want more.

## The economic problem for each decision maker

The syllabus asks for examples in four contexts. In each one, identify three things: the **limited resource**, the **competing wants**, and the **choice** that must be made.

### Consumers

A consumer's limited resources are **income** and **time**.

**Worked example.** Amara (a student) has $60 to spend this month. She wants concert tickets ($35), a pair of trainers ($45) and a video game ($30). The total wanted is 35 + 45 + 30 = $110, which is $50 more than she has. She cannot have all three. If she buys the trainers, $15 remains, which is not enough for either of the others. Her income is the binding limit.

Time is limited too: even with unlimited money, Amara has only so many evenings to go to concerts or play games.

### Workers

A worker's limited resource is **time and effort**. Each hour can be spent working (for income) or on leisure, rest, study or family.

**Example.** A nurse is offered 8 hours of overtime at the weekend. She wants the extra pay, but she also wants rest and time with her children. She must choose how to divide a fixed number of hours. A worker also faces a choice of **which job** to do: the same hours cannot be spent in two jobs at once.

### Producers

A producer's limited resources are its **land, workers, machinery and finance**.

**Worked example.** A farmer has 20 hectares. Each hectare can grow either maize or vegetables, not both. She would like to grow 15 hectares of maize to supply a mill and 10 hectares of vegetables for the market, which needs 25 hectares. She is 5 hectares short, so she must decide which crop to cut back, or whether to rent more land, which would use up some of her limited finance.

### Governments

A government's limited resources are **tax revenue, borrowing capacity and the factors of production it controls**, such as public land.

**Example.** A government can afford one large project this year: a new hospital or a new motorway. Both would benefit citizens, but building one uses the workers, materials and finance that the other would need. Unlike a consumer, a government must also weigh the wants of different groups: patients and drivers may not be the same people.

## Economic goods and free goods

An **economic good** is one that is scarce: producing or supplying it uses resources that have alternative uses, so it has an opportunity cost. Most economic goods have a price, but not all.

A **free good** is one that is not scarce: it is available in such quantity that using it does not use up resources or deprive anyone else. It has no opportunity cost, so it has no price. Examples usually given are air in an open place, sunlight and seawater in the ocean.

### A reliable test

Ask: **does supplying one more unit use resources that could have been used for something else?**

- If **yes**, it is an economic good, whatever its price to the user.
- If **no**, it is a free good.

### Cases that cause confusion

| Case | Free or economic? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Air in an open field | Free | Breathing it uses no resources and deprives no one |
| Air in a scuba diver's tank | Economic | Compressing and bottling it uses labour, machines and energy |
| Sand on a desert dune | Free, in place | Plentiful, and taking it deprives no one |
| A bag of sand from a builders' merchant | Economic | Digging, cleaning, bagging and transport use resources |
| Healthcare with no charge to the patient | Economic | Doctors, nurses and medicines are scarce; the cost is paid through taxes |
| A "free" mobile app | Economic | Programmers and servers are paid for, often through advertising |
| A free sample in a supermarket | Economic | The firm used resources to make it; it is free to the shopper only |

### How a free good can become an economic good

A good can move from free to economic when it becomes scarce. Clean air is free in an unpolluted area. In a city with heavy traffic, people may buy air purifiers, and governments spend on pollution controls: resources are now being used to obtain clean air, so it behaves as an economic good. In the same way, water from a river is effectively free while it is plentiful, but in a drought it must be rationed or treated and transported, which uses resources.

## Annotated answer to an "explain" question

*Explain why a good provided at a zero price to consumers may still be an economic good.*

> "An economic good is one that uses scarce resources to produce, so it has an opportunity cost. A school meal given free to pupils still uses food, cooks and kitchen equipment. These resources could have been used to produce something else, so society gives up an alternative. The zero price only means the cost is paid by someone other than the pupil, such as the government through taxes."

Why it works: it defines the term, applies it to a specific example, explains the opportunity cost, and separates the price to the user from the cost to society.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the economic problem with only one half: "people have unlimited wants" is not enough; finite resources must be stated too.
- Saying scarcity means a good is rare, or that only poor countries face it.
- Calling anything with a zero price a free good.
- Describing a consumer's problem only in terms of money, forgetting time.
- Writing about a producer's wants for things the owner personally wants, instead of the firm's wants for output and profit.

## Exam technique

| Command word | What it needs in 1.1 |
|---|---|
| Define | A precise meaning: both finite resources and unlimited wants, plus the need for choice |
| State or Identify | A brief answer, such as naming a free good |
| Give | An example from memory or from the source |
| Describe | The main features, such as how the problem affects workers |
| Explain | A reason supported by a chain: resource limited → wants exceed it → choice → something given up |
| Discuss | Points on more than one side, for example whether a particular good is truly free |

On Paper 1, read each multiple-choice option against the definition. Options that mention "a zero price", "rarity" or "poor countries only" are common distractors.
