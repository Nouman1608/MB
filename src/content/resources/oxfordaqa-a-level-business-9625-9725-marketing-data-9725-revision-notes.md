Figures are invented.

## Recall in one minute

- **Primary methods named:** surveys/questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observations.
- **Secondary sources named:** websites/social media, academic journals, government publications, media articles.
- **Data types:** qualitative (opinions, reasons) and quantitative (numbers).
- **Samples named:** random, stratified, quota; plus the value of sampling.
- **Influences on choice:** cost, time required, reliability of findings.
- **Interpretation:** use data in decisions and planning; read the sign and size of a correlation value; understand confidence levels and intervals (for example 95%).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary research | New data collected first-hand for a specific purpose |
| Secondary research | Data that already exists, collected for another purpose |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data that can be measured and compared |
| Qualitative data | Descriptive data on opinions, feelings and reasons |
| Population | The whole group the business wants to know about |
| Sample | The part of the population actually researched |
| Random sample | Every member of the population has an equal chance of selection |
| Stratified sample | Population divided into groups; a random sample taken from each in proportion to its size |
| Quota sample | Researchers fill set numbers from each group, choosing whom to ask |
| Correlation | The strength and direction of the relationship between two variables, from −1 to +1 |
| Confidence interval | The range within which the true population value is likely to lie |
| Confidence level | How sure we can be that the method's interval contains the true value, such as 95% |

## Primary methods: the deciding feature

| Method | Best for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Survey/questionnaire | Numbers from many people | Shallow answers; wording can bias; low response |
| Interview | Depth and follow-up questions | Slow and costly per person; interviewer influence |
| Focus group | Reasons and reactions to ideas | Few people; strong voices dominate |
| Observation | What people actually do | Does not explain why |

## Secondary sources: quick checks

- **Who** produced it, **when**, **why**, and **how big** was the sample?
- Government publications: large and trusted, but general and may be dated.
- Academic journals: rigorous, rarely specific to one firm.
- Media articles: current, may simplify or sensationalise.
- Websites/social media: fast and cheap, but views of a self-selected few.

## Value of primary versus secondary

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Fit to the question | Exact | Partial |
| Cost | Higher | Lower or free |
| Time | Slower | Faster |
| Up to date | Yes | Varies |
| Rivals can see it | No | Often yes |

Many firms start with secondary research to understand the market, then use primary research to answer the specific question.

## Sampling: method in steps (stratified)

1. Find each group's share of the population.
2. Multiply each share by the sample size.
3. Round to whole people and check the total equals the sample size.
4. Select randomly within each group.

Example: 1,500 customers: 600 aged under 30, 525 aged 30 to 49, 375 aged 50 and over; sample 60 → 24, 21 and 15.

## Reading correlation and confidence

- **Sign** gives direction; **size** gives strength. −0.9 is strong; +0.2 is weak.
- Correlation shows association, not cause.
- A narrower interval means a more precise estimate.
- **Bigger sample → narrower interval. Higher confidence level → wider interval.**
- If a target value lies inside the interval, the firm cannot be confident the true value is above (or below) it.
- If two intervals overlap, the survey cannot show which option is truly ahead.

## Exam traps

- Do not say "primary is more reliable" without conditions: reliability depends on sample size and selection.
- Do not call quota sampling random.
- Do not treat a negative correlation as weak.
- Do not say a 95% confidence level means "95% accurate".
- Link every advantage or disadvantage to the decision in the case: cost against the budget, time against the deadline.
- When recommending research, say what the data will be used for.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between primary and secondary research.
2. Give one advantage of observation over a questionnaire.
3. Which type of data does an open question produce?
4. A firm has 3,000 customers: 1,800 in the city, 900 in towns and 300 rural. Calculate a stratified sample of 50.
5. State one disadvantage of a quota sample.
6. Give one reason why a small business might rely on secondary research.
7. Describe a correlation value of −0.78.
8. A correlation of +0.84 links social media posts and website visits. Give one reason this does not prove the posts cause the visits.
9. A survey gives 52% with a 95% confidence interval of 47% to 57%. Can the firm be confident that most of the population agrees?
10. What happens to a confidence interval if the sample size is increased?
11. State the three influences on choosing a form of market research named in the specification.
12. Give one weakness of using social media comments as research.

### Answers

1. Primary research collects new data for the firm's own purpose; secondary uses existing data collected for another purpose.
2. It records what people actually do rather than what they say they do.
3. **Qualitative** data.
4. City 1,800 ÷ 3,000 × 50 = **30**; towns 900 ÷ 3,000 × 50 = **15**; rural 300 ÷ 3,000 × 50 = **5**.
5. Researchers choose whom to ask, so the sample may be biased (any valid point).
6. It is cheaper and quicker than collecting new data.
7. **Strong negative**: as one variable rises, the other tends to fall.
8. Both may be driven by a third factor, such as a seasonal event (any valid point).
9. **No**: the interval includes values below 50%, so the true figure might not be a majority.
10. It becomes **narrower** (more precise).
11. Cost, time required, reliability of findings.
12. The comments come from people who choose to post, so they may not represent typical customers.
