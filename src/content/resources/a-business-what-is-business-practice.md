## How this paper is laid out

This set follows the section layout of the **9625** International AS **Unit 1: Business and
markets** paper (written exam, 1 hour 30 minutes, 80 marks), which has three compulsory sections:
**Section A** multiple choice, short answer and data response questions worth approximately 17 marks;
**Section B** extended response questions worth approximately 27 marks; and **Section C** extended
response questions worth approximately 36 marks. Here the sections are worth exactly 17, 27 and 36
marks. The real Unit 1 paper also examines Topic 2 Marketing; questions 3, 6, 8 and 12 draw on content
outside Topic 1 (see the scope note below), and the rest are on Topic 1 What is business?, so use it
as topic practice rather than as a full mock. Extra
short-answer questions on the external environment follow the answers, outside the paper layout.

**If you follow 9725** (first International AS exams May/June 2027), its Unit 1 paper is different:
1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks, in four sections (A short answer questions, 10 marks; B calculation
questions, 10 marks; C extended response questions, 16 marks; D extended response questions,
24 marks).

**Scope note:** these questions primarily follow 9625. Questions 3, 8 and 12 draw on content the
9625 specification places in **second-year (A2)** topics (mission statements and the link from
mission to objectives sit in 3.3.1 Mission, objectives and strategy; the shareholder/stakeholder
debate, economies of scale, overtrading and gearing also sit in section 3.3, not first-year Topic 1),
and question 6 draws on **price elasticity of demand**, which is Topic 2 Marketing content, not
Topic 1. They are kept here as useful application practice but should not be treated as first-year
Topic 1 exam scope. "SMART" (question 4) and "income elasticity of demand" (question 16) are
widely-used teaching devices that explain concepts the specification does require in substance
(setting objectives; the effect of changes in incomes) — they are not themselves named, required
terms in the OxfordAQA specification, so treat them as explanatory aids rather than must-use
vocabulary.

## Section A

Answer **all** questions.

**1.** Which of the following is an **output** of the transformation process? **[1]**
A. Labour  B. Capital  C. A finished good or service  D. Enterprise

**2.** Which of the following is a **tertiary** sector business? **[1]**
A. A coal mine  B. A car factory  C. A bank  D. A fishing company

**3.** Distinguish between a mission statement, a corporate objective and a functional objective. **[3]**

**4.** State the elements of SMART objectives. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by "divorce of ownership from control" in a public limited company, and state **one** way this problem can be reduced. **[3]**

**6.** A firm sells a good with inelastic demand. Explain, referring to price elasticity of demand, whether a price rise would increase its total revenue. **[3]**

**7.** A market has the following shares: Firm W 32%, Firm X 24%, Firm Y 14%, Firm Z 9%, all other firms 21% combined. Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio and explain **one** implication of your answer for barriers to entry. **[4]**

*Section A total: 1 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 17 marks.*

## Section B

Answer **all** questions.

**8.** Distinguish between the shareholder and stakeholder approaches to business objectives, and explain **one** argument for each. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **three** reasons why a business's objectives might change over time. **[6]**

**10.** Explain **three** roles of an entrepreneur in starting a business, and **two** reasons why many start-ups fail. **[10]**

**11.** Explain **one** way a change in government policy could affect a business, other than through interest rates. **[3]**

*Section B total: 8 + 6 + 10 + 3 = 27 marks.*

## Section C

Answer **all** questions.

**12.** Evaluate whether pursuing growth is always in the interests of a business's shareholders. **[12]**

**13.** Amira runs a successful café as a sole trader and wants to open two more branches. Evaluate whether she should convert the business into a private limited company. **[12]**

**14.** Evaluate the extent to which government support determines how many new businesses start up, and succeed, in a country. **[12]**

*Section C total: 12 + 12 + 12 = 36 marks. Paper total: 17 + 27 + 36 = 80 marks.*

## Answers

**1.** C **[1]**

**2.** C **[1]**

**3.** A **mission statement** sets out the business's **overall purpose and values** in qualitative terms [1]. A **corporate objective** is a **specific, measurable, medium-term goal for the whole organisation**, such as a 10% rise in market share [1]. A **functional objective** is a **departmental target that supports the corporate objective**, such as marketing achieving 15% brand awareness growth [1].

**4.** Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic (or Relevant), Time-bound: all five [2]; three or four correct [1].

**5.** In a plc, **shareholders own the business but directors run it day to day** [1], and their objectives can diverge — directors may pursue growth, status or job security, while shareholders want strong returns [1]. This can be reduced through **share options or performance-related pay for directors**, which realigns directors' financial interests with shareholder returns [1].

**6.** Where demand is **inelastic**, a given percentage rise in price causes a **smaller percentage fall in quantity demanded** [1], so total revenue (price × quantity) **rises** [1], since the loss in volume is more than offset by the higher price per unit [1].

**7.** Four-firm concentration ratio = 32 + 24 + 14 + 9 = **79%** [1] [1] (method and answer). A ratio this high indicates a highly concentrated market dominated by a few large firms [1], which are likely to have brand recognition, resources and economies of scale that make it harder for a new firm to enter and compete [1].

**8.** The **shareholder approach** holds that the business's purpose is to **maximise returns to its owners**, since they bear the risk and own the company [1] [1]. The **stakeholder approach** holds that the business should **balance the interests of all groups affected by it** — employees, customers, suppliers, the community — not just the owners [1] [1].
Argument for shareholder: shareholders **bear the residual risk and have supplied the capital**, so directors have a legal and moral duty to act in their interests; without returns, capital will not be supplied at all [1] [1].
Argument for stakeholder: satisfying employees and customers **builds the loyalty and reputation on which long-term profits depend** [1], so the two approaches converge over a long enough horizon and ignoring stakeholders creates regulatory and reputational risk [1].

**9.** Any three, 2 marks each: **the stage of the business's life cycle** changes — survival matters most at the start, growth and then profit later [1] [1]; **the state of the economy** — in a recession a business may switch from growth to cash-flow protection [1] [1]; **a change in ownership or leadership** brings different priorities, for example a new chief executive focusing on shareholder returns [1] [1]; **competitor or technological change** may force a shift into new markets or products [1] [1].

**10. Roles:** the entrepreneur **identifies the market opportunity or gap** and develops the idea into a viable proposition [1] [1]; they **provide or raise the capital and bear the financial risk** personally [1] [1]; they **organise the other factors of production and make the key decisions** on product, price and staffing [1] [1].
**Reasons for failure:** **poor cash flow management** — even a profitable business fails if it cannot pay its bills when they fall due, often because customers pay late [1] [1]; **inadequate market research** — the entrepreneur overestimates demand or misjudges the level of competition, so sales never reach the level the plan assumed [1] [1].

**11.** For example, a government introducing **subsidies or grants** for a particular industry [1] reduces that industry's costs and can make investment or expansion more attractive [1], so a business in the industry might expand capacity, hire more staff or cut its prices to win market share [1]. Any other policy developed in the same way is credited, e.g. tighter environmental regulation that raises compliance costs and may force changes to products or processes.

**12–14 (Section C)** are marked as whole answers against levels. The level descriptors below are
Marlbridge's own guide, not OxfordAQA's published mark scheme:

- **Level 1 (1–3 marks):** relevant knowledge, with little application to the question.
- **Level 2 (4–6 marks):** knowledge applied to the question, with limited or one-sided analysis.
- **Level 3 (7–9 marks):** developed analysis of both sides, applied to the context.
- **Level 4 (10–12 marks):** balanced analysis leading to a supported judgement that explains what the answer depends on.

**Indicative content for 12:** *for growth* — growth allows the business to exploit **economies of scale**, lowering unit costs and raising margins; a larger market share brings **greater bargaining power over suppliers and more pricing power**; growth generally raises **revenue and, if margins hold, absolute profit and the share price**; it can also **spread risk across more products and markets**. *Against* — growth is often financed by **debt or new share issues**, which raises gearing or **dilutes existing shareholders' stake and earnings per share**; **diseconomies of scale** — communication and coordination problems, weaker motivation — can raise unit costs and erode the margins growth was meant to improve; rapid growth can cause **overtrading**, where the business runs out of cash despite rising sales; management attention may be diverted from the **profitable core business**. *Judgement* — growth serves shareholders only when the **return on the capital employed exceeds its cost**; it depends on **how the growth is financed, whether the market is large enough, and whether management can run a larger organisation**.

**Indicative content for 13:** *for converting* — as a sole trader Amira has **unlimited liability**, so her personal assets are at risk if the expansion fails; a private limited company gives shareholders **limited liability**; it can raise finance by **selling shares** to people she chooses and may find it easier to borrow, which helps fund two new branches; the company continues if the owner leaves. *Against* — selling shares **dilutes her ownership and control**, and profits must be shared with other shareholders; a company usually faces more legal formalities and costs, such as registration and filing accounts, which also reduces privacy; if she can fund the branches from retained profit or a loan, she may not need outside shareholders. *Judgement* — conversion is more attractive the larger the investment and the risk involved; it depends on how much finance the branches need, how far Amira values keeping full control, and how risky the expansion is.

**Indicative content for 14:** *for government support mattering most* — governments can support start-ups with grants, low-interest loans, tax incentives, advice and training, and simpler rules for registering a business, which lower the cost and risk of starting up and help new businesses survive their first years. *Against* — the specification also names **the business environment in a country** and **factors influencing the number and type of start-ups**, such as the state of the economy and consumer incomes, access to finance from banks, the degree of competition, and the skills, motivation and characteristics of entrepreneurs themselves; many start-ups fail for internal reasons such as poor cash flow management or inadequate market research, which government support cannot remove; support schemes can be costly or poorly targeted. *Judgement* — government support can raise the number of start-ups and help them survive, but whether they succeed depends more on market conditions and the entrepreneur's own planning; its importance is greatest where finance is hard to obtain or regulation is burdensome.

## Extra practice (outside the paper layout)

These short-answer questions cover more of the first-year external environment content (3.1.1.3).

**15.** Explain **two** factors, other than price, that determine the degree of competition a business faces in its market. **[4]**

**16.** During an economic downturn, sales of a discount retailer rise while sales of a luxury retailer fall. Explain this using income elasticity of demand. **[4]**

**17.** Explain **two** effects a rise in interest rates could have on a business. **[4]**

**18.** Explain **two** ways a rise in the cost of inputs could affect a business's decisions. **[4]**

**15.** Any two, 2 marks each: **the number of businesses in the market** — more competitors generally means each firm has less pricing power and must compete harder on price, quality or service [1] [1]; **the extent of barriers to entry** — low barriers (little capital needed, no patents, easy access to distribution) allow new entrants, which increases competitive pressure over time, while high barriers protect existing firms' market position [1] [1].

**16.** **Inferior goods have negative income elasticity of demand** — as incomes fall in a downturn, demand for them actually **rises**, which explains the discount retailer's growth [1] [1]. **Luxury goods have high positive income elasticity** — demand is highly sensitive to income, so it **falls sharply** when incomes fall, explaining the luxury retailer's decline [1] [1].

**17.** Any two, 2 marks each: **higher borrowing costs**, discouraging investment and expansion funded by loans [1] [1]; **reduced consumer spending**, particularly on income-elastic, expensive items such as durables and housing, since disposable income falls once higher mortgage or loan repayments are met [1] [1]; a business holding **variable-rate debt sees its own repayments rise directly**, squeezing cash flow [1] [1].

**18.** Any two, 2 marks each: **higher costs of inputs squeeze profit margins** unless the business raises its own prices, which it may be unable to do without losing customers to competitors [1] [1]; a business may **switch supplier or input**, substituting a cheaper (perhaps lower-quality) alternative, which can affect product quality and brand reputation [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing a mission statement with a corporate objective.
- Listing stakeholders instead of explaining the two approaches.
- Describing growth's benefits without addressing how it is financed.
- Not reaching a conditional judgement in a 12-mark evaluation.
