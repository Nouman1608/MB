## Section A

**1.** Define allocative efficiency. **[2]**

**2.** A firm is productively efficient when it produces the output at which:

A: price equals marginal cost

B: marginal cost equals average cost

C: marginal revenue equals marginal cost

D: price equals average revenue **[1]**

**3.** Define Pareto optimality. **[2]**

**4.** Define dynamic efficiency and give one way in which a firm might achieve it. **[2]**

**5.** A firm's average cost is 18 at 100 units, 15 at 200 units, 14 at 300 units, 14.50 at 400 units and 16 at 500 units. State the productively efficient output from these figures. **[1]**

**6.** On a production possibility curve diagram, which points are productively efficient but not necessarily allocatively efficient?

A: any point inside the curve

B: any point on the curve

C: any point outside the curve

D: only the point where the curve meets an axis **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A firm in a perfectly competitive market has a fixed cost of 30 and the following total costs.

| Output | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost | 50 | 62 | 72 | 86 | 105 | 130 |

**(a)** Calculate marginal cost and average cost at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the productively efficient output. **[1]**

**(c)** The market price is 25. Identify the output at which the firm is allocatively efficient. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the firm is not productively efficient at this output, and what is likely to happen in the long run. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows the utility of two people under four allocations of resources.

| Allocation | Person A | Person B |
|---|---|---|
| W | 5 | 5 |
| X | 7 | 5 |
| Y | 6 | 7 |
| Z | 9 | 2 |

**(a)** Identify which moves from allocation W are Pareto improvements, and explain why. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a move from X to Y is not a Pareto improvement, even though total utility rises. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two reasons for market failure, stating in each case what goes wrong with the allocation of resources. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a perfectly competitive industry is both productively and allocatively efficient in the long run. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether perfect competition is always more efficient than monopoly. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Resources are allocated so that the goods and services produced are those consumers value most, in the right quantities [1]; the condition is that price equals marginal cost [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. MC = AC at the minimum point of the AC curve.

**3.** An allocation of resources in which it is impossible to make one person better off [1] without making at least one other person worse off [1].

**4.** Improvements in production methods or products over time that lower costs or raise quality [1]; for example, investing profit in research and development or in new machinery [1].

**5.** **300 units**, where average cost is lowest at 14 [1].

**6.** **B** [1]. Points on the curve use all resources efficiently, but only one of them matches society's preferences.

**7.** **(a)** MC: 20, 12, 10, 14, 19, 25 [1]. AC: 50, 31, 24, 21.50, 21, 21.67 [1].

**(b)** **5 units**, where AC is at its minimum of 21 [1].

**(c)** **6 units**, where price (25) equals marginal cost (25) [1].

**(d)** At 6 units AC is 21.67, above the minimum of 21, because the MC of the sixth unit is above AC [1]. Price exceeds AC, so the firm earns supernormal profit: revenue 25 × 6 = 150 minus total cost 130 = 20 [1]. In the long run new firms enter, the market price falls towards minimum AC, and the firm produces about 5 units, where it is both productively and allocatively efficient [1].

**8.** **(a)** W to X and W to Y are Pareto improvements [1]: in each, at least one person gains and nobody loses. W to Z is not, because B's utility falls from 5 to 2 [1].

**(b)** Total utility rises from 12 to 13 [1], but Person A's utility falls from 7 to 6, and a Pareto improvement requires that nobody is made worse off [1].

**9.** Any two reasons, each explained, for example:

Negative externalities: the market ignores costs borne by third parties [1], so output is above the level at which marginal social cost equals marginal social benefit, and too many resources go to the good [1].

Market power: a monopoly restricts output to raise price [1], so price exceeds marginal cost and units that consumers value above their cost are not produced [1].

Public goods: free riders mean no firm can charge, so the market provides none at all.

**10.** In the long run, supernormal profit attracts entry and losses cause exit, so firms earn only normal profit, where price equals average cost [1]. Each firm maximises profit where MC = MR, and in perfect competition MR equals price, so P = MC: allocative efficiency [1]. With price equal to AC and equal to MC, MC = AC, which occurs at the minimum point of AC [1]. So each firm produces at minimum average cost: productive efficiency [1].

**11.** For perfect competition: in the long run P = MC, so it is allocatively efficient, while a monopoly sets P > MC [1]; it produces at minimum AC, while a monopoly often does not [1]; competition puts pressure on firms to keep costs down [1].

For monopoly: supernormal profit can fund research and new technology, so a monopoly may be more dynamically efficient [1]; a large firm may gain economies of scale, so its costs may be lower than those of many small firms even if it does not produce at its own minimum AC [1]; perfectly competitive firms earn only normal profit and may be unable to fund innovation [1].

Limits: perfect competition is a theoretical benchmark that few real markets meet [1].

Judgement: perfect competition is more statically efficient, but monopoly may be more efficient over time or where economies of scale are large, so the answer depends on whether static or dynamic efficiency matters more in the market concerned [1].
