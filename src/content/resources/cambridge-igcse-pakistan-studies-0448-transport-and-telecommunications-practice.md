**What do these questions practise?** Section 7 of Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448 Paper 2:
internal transport, international transport and telecommunications. Work through Section A for
recall, then attempt the explanation and evaluation items under timed conditions. For mixed
questions on trade and transport, see the
[Trade, Transport and Telecommunications practice questions](/resources/igcse-pakistan-studies-trade-transport-and-telecommunications-practice/).

## Section A: recall

**1.** Name the pass used by the road from Quetta to Chaman. **[1]**

**2.** Name the border crossing on the road from Peshawar to Afghanistan. **[1]**

**3.** Name **two** cities, other than Karachi and Lahore, that have international airports. **[2]**

**4.** Name the border town on the road and railway from Quetta to Iran. **[1]**

**5.** State **two** features of Gwadar port. **[2]**

**6.** Name **one** motorway and the **two** cities it connects. **[2]**

## Section B: explanation

**7.** Explain why the road and rail network is dense in central Punjab. **[4]**

**8.** Describe the problems of maintaining roads in Pakistan. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why air transport is important in the northern areas of Pakistan. **[3]**

**10.** Explain the factors that should be considered when choosing the site of a new international
airport. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how telecommunications can help education in rural areas. **[3]**

**12.** Explain why Port Qasim was developed when Karachi already had the port at Keamari. **[3]**

## Section C: evaluation

**13.** Evaluate the feasibility of upgrading the main Karachi–Peshawar railway line. **[6]**

**14.** "The internet has brought more benefits to Pakistan's cities than to its rural areas." Give
your views. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The Khojak Pass [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Torkham [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: Islamabad [1]; Peshawar [1]. Also credit: Quetta, Multan, Faisalabad, Sialkot. **[2]**

**4.** Taftan [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any two: it is a deep-water port that can take large ships [1]; it is in Balochistan, close to
the shipping routes of the Gulf, and has been developed with Chinese investment under CPEC [1]. Also
credit: it is far from Pakistan's main industrial areas. **[2]**

**6.** One mark for the motorway and one for both cities, for example the M-2 [1], linking Lahore
and Islamabad [1]. Also credit: the M-1 (Islamabad–Peshawar) or the M-9 (Karachi–Hyderabad). **[2]**

**7.** The land is flat, so roads and railways are cheap to build [1]. The population is dense, with
large cities such as Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranwala, so there is great demand for travel [1].
Farming and industry produce goods that must be moved to markets and ports [1]. Many routes date from the
railway building of the colonial period, so the network is already well developed [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Monsoon floods wash away road surfaces and bridges [1]. Overloaded lorries break up the
surface [1]. In the mountains, landslides and snow block roads, and repairs are costly and slow [1].
Also credit: high summer temperatures soften surfaces; there is too little money for regular
maintenance. **[3]**

**9.** Roads through the mountains are long, narrow and often blocked by landslides or snow, so
journeys can take many hours or days [1]. Flights to towns such as Gilgit and Skardu take a short
time [1], linking these areas to the rest of the country for tourists, officials and emergencies
[1]. **[3]**

**10.** A large area of flat land is needed for runways and terminals [1]. The site should be away
from high buildings, hills and dense housing, for safety and to reduce noise [1]. Good road and rail
links are needed so passengers and cargo can reach it [1], and there must be enough demand from a
large city or region [1]. Also credit: weather with few fogs or storms. **[4]**

**11.** Radio and television lessons reach villages without enough teachers [1]. The internet gives
access to online courses and materials, such as those offered by distance-learning universities [1],
so students can study without travelling to a city, which saves money and helps girls who cannot
travel far [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Keamari was congested, with no space to expand because it is surrounded by the city [1]. A new
port was needed for bulk cargo such as oil, coal and the raw materials for steel-making [1]. Port
Qasim had space for new docks and industry, and spreading traffic between two ports reduced
delays [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Feasible: the line already links Karachi, Lahore, Rawalpindi and Peshawar, where most people
and industry are [1]; faster, heavier trains would move bulky goods more cheaply than lorries and
reduce road congestion and pollution [1]; foreign investment through CPEC has been planned for the
upgrade [1]. Less feasible: the cost is very high and adds to foreign debt [1]; work would disrupt
existing services and require land in crowded areas [1]. Judgement: feasible if funding is secure,
because the benefits for freight are large [1]. **[6]**

**14.** Agree: cities have better coverage, faster connections and more reliable electricity [1];
higher incomes and literacy mean more people can use online banking, shopping and work [1]; IT
companies and call centres are based in cities [1]. Disagree: mobile phones have spread widely in
rural areas, so farmers can check prices and weather [1]; online learning and mobile banking can do
more for remote places that have few banks and schools [1]. View: a supported judgement, for example
that cities have gained more so far, but the potential benefit is greater in rural areas if the
digital divide is closed [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the Bolan Pass for the route to Afghanistan.
- Writing "the Karakoram Highway goes to Afghanistan".
- Giving one-word answers to "explain" questions.
- Evaluating with only benefits, or only problems.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the exam include a map of routes?**
Questions use resources such as maps and photographs, so practise naming routes and describing
patterns from a map.

**How should I answer a feasibility question?**
Consider cost, demand, physical difficulty, environmental effects and who benefits, then judge.
