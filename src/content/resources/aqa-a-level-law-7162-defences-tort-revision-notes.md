Condensed notes for the section 3.3 row **Defences**: "Contributory negligence", "Consent (volenti non fit injuria)" and "Defences specific to private nuisance and the rule in Rylands v Fletcher". For explanation and worked examples, use the [defences in tort study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-defences-tort/).

## Overview

```
Tort established  ->  Defence?  ->  Effect on damages
   Contributory negligence  -> PARTIAL: reduced by claimant's share
   Consent (volenti)        -> COMPLETE: nothing
   Nuisance / Rylands       -> COMPLETE
Burden: defendant, balance of probabilities
```

## Contributory negligence

**Statute:** s1(1) Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945: damages reduced as "just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility for the damage".

**Elements:**
1. Claimant failed to take reasonable care for own safety (objective).
2. That failure contributed to the **damage** (not necessarily the accident): *Froom v Butcher* (1976).

**Adjustments:**
- Children: reasonable child of that age: *Gough v Thorne* (1966); *Yachuk v Oliver Blais* (1949).
- Emergencies: agony of the moment: *Jones v Boyce* (1816).
- No 100% reduction: *Pitts v Hunt* (1990).

| Case | Fault | Reduction |
|---|---|---|
| *Froom v Butcher* (1976) | No seat belt | 25% / 15% / 0 guidance |
| *O'Connell v Jackson* (1972) | No crash helmet | 15% |
| *Owens v Brimmell* (1977) | Lift from drinking driver | 20% |
| *Sayers v Harlow UDC* (1957) | Climbing out of locked toilet | 25% |
| *Jones v Livox Quarries* (1952) | Riding on back of vehicle | One fifth |

## Consent (volenti non fit injuria)

**Requirements:**
1. Full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk.
2. Voluntary agreement to accept it, waiving any claim.

**Fails:**

| Situation | Case / provision |
|---|---|
| Employee knows of risk but has no real choice | *Smith v Baker* (1891) |
| Passenger with drinking driver | *Dann v Hamilton* (1939); s149 RTA 1988 |
| Instructor who checked insurance | *Nettleship v Weston* (1971) |
| Rescuers | *Haynes v Harwood* (1935); *Baker v TE Hopkins* (1959) |
| Duty to prevent the very act | *Reeves v Commissioner of Police* (2000) |
| Notice alone | s2(3) UCTA 1977 |

**Succeeds:**
- *ICI v Shatwell* (1965): experienced shotfirers broke safety rules.
- *Morris v Murray* (1991): flight with an obviously drunk pilot.

**Sport:** consent only to risks within the rules and conventions: *Condon v Basi* (1985).
**Occupiers:** s2(5) OLA 1957; s1(6) OLA 1984.

## Defences to private nuisance

| Defence | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Prescription | 20 years of actionable nuisance | *Sturges v Bridgman* (1879): time runs from when it became a nuisance to the claimant |
| Statutory authority | Inevitable result of authorised activity | *Allen v Gulf Oil* (1981) |

**Not defences:** coming to the nuisance (*Bliss v Hall*, 1838; *Miller v Jackson*, 1977; *Coventry v Lawrence*, 2014); public benefit (*Adams v Ursell*, 1913); planning permission (*Wheeler v JJ Saunders*, 1996).

| Defence | Case |

## Evaluation points

- **Contributory negligence** is fairer than the pre-1945 all-or-nothing rule, but percentages are fixed by judicial impression; the *Froom* guidance adds some consistency.
- **Consent** is narrow because courts protect people who face risks through economic pressure (*Smith v Baker*) or moral duty (rescuers).
- **Nuisance defences** favour the claimant: refusing a defence of coming to the nuisance protects property rights, while public benefit is handled through the remedy.
- **Rylands defences** largely re-introduce fault ideas into a "strict" tort.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Contributory negligence | Claimant's own fault contributing to their damage |
| Apportionment | Dividing responsibility between claimant and defendant |
| Volenti non fit injuria | "To one who is willing, no harm is done": consent to the risk |
| Agony of the moment | Reasonable choice made under sudden danger |
| Prescription | Right acquired by 20 years of actionable nuisance |
| Statutory authority | Statute authorises the activity causing harm |
| Act of God | Extraordinary natural event no one could foresee or guard against |
| Act of a stranger | Unforeseeable act of a third party outside the defendant's control |

## Exam traps

- Contributory negligence reduces; it never defeats the whole claim.
- Knowledge of a risk is not consent (*Smith v Baker*).
- Car passengers: s149 RTA 1988 blocks volenti.
- Prescription runs from when the activity became a nuisance to this claimant.
- Heavy but foreseeable rain is not an Act of God.

## Self-test

1. What is the effect of contributory negligence? *Damages are reduced by the claimant's share of responsibility.*
2. Which Act governs it? *The Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945.*
3. What did *Froom v Butcher* decide about causation? *The fault need only contribute to the damage, not the accident.*
4. Why was the child not at fault in *Gough v Thorne*? *A reasonable 13-year-old would rely on the lorry driver's signal.*
5. What principle comes from *Jones v Boyce*? *A reasonable choice in an emergency is not contributory negligence.*
6. State the two requirements of volenti. *Full knowledge of the risk and voluntary agreement to accept it.*
7. Why did volenti fail in *Smith v Baker*? *The worker knew of the risk but did not freely consent.*
8. Why did volenti succeed in *ICI v Shatwell*? *Experienced workers deliberately broke safety rules, freely accepting the risk.*
9. Which statute prevents volenti against car passengers? *s149 Road Traffic Act 1988.*
10. Why did prescription fail in *Sturges v Bridgman*? *There was no nuisance to the doctor until his consulting room was built.*
11. Is coming to the nuisance a defence? *No (Miller v Jackson; Coventry v Lawrence).*
12. Name two defences to *Rylands*. *Any two: Act of God, act of a stranger, consent, statutory authority, claimant's default.*
