## The homologous series

0620 Core: a family of similar compounds with **similar chemical properties** because they have the **same functional group** (11.1 outcome 4).

*(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* The full description: the **same functional group**, the **same general formula**, differing by **CH₂**, showing a gradual trend in physical properties and similar chemical properties (11.1 outcome 9).

The **functional group** is the specific atom or group of atoms responsible for a series' characteristic reactions — the –OH in alcohols, the –COOH in carboxylic acids, the C=C in alkenes. It's the functional group, not the length of the carbon chain, that determines how a compound reacts.

| Series | Functional group | General formula | Suffix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkanes | C–C single | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | -ane |
| Alkenes | C=C double | CₙH₂ₙ | -ene |
| Alcohols | –OH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | -ol |
| Carboxylic acids | –COOH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH | -oic acid |
| Esters *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* | –COO– | — | -oate |

## Stem names — count the carbons

```
1 meth-   2 eth-   3 prop-   4 but-   5 pent-   6 hex-
```

*(Background — beyond both syllabuses.)* pent- and hex-: 0620 and 5070 both limit naming to compounds
with up to four carbon atoms per molecule.

So: C₂H₅OH = ethanol · CH₃COOH = ethanoic acid (0620 Core). *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* C₃H₈ = propane · C₄H₈ = but-1-ene or but-2-ene (the unbranched alkenes): naming other unbranched compounds up to four carbons is 11.2 outcome 3.

*(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* **Locant numbers** show *where* a feature sits on a longer or branched
chain: propan-**1**-ol has the –OH on the end carbon, propan-**2**-ol
has it on the middle carbon — same molecular formula, different
compound, different physical properties.

*(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* **Esters** are named from the alcohol and acid that formed them: an
alcohol ending in **-yl**, followed by the acid's **-oate**. Ethanol +
ethanoic acid gives **ethyl ethanoate**.

## Types of formula

| Type | Ethanol example |
|---|---|
| Empirical *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* | C₂H₆O (simplest ratio) |
| Molecular | C₂H₆O |
| Structural *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* | CH₃CH₂OH |
| Displayed | Every atom and bond drawn out |

A displayed formula is what examiners expect when a question asks you to "draw the structure" — it is the most detailed of the four representations above.

In a **displayed formula**, show every C–H bond. Omitting them is a common lost mark.

**Worked example.** Use the general formula to find the molecular formula of the alkane with 5 carbon atoms.
```
CnH2n+2, with n = 5
C5H(2x5)+2 = C5H12  (pentane)
```

Using the general formula is 0620 Core (11.1 outcome 2). The name pentane is background, beyond the four-carbon naming limit.

## Isomers *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)*

**Structural isomers** — same molecular formula, different arrangement of atoms (a different structural formula).

```
C4H10:   butane        CH3CH2CH2CH3      (straight chain)
         methylpropane CH3CH(CH3)CH3     (branched)
```

Same formula, different structure, therefore different physical properties (branched isomers have lower boiling points — less surface contact between molecules). The molecular formula alone never tells you which isomer you're looking at — that's exactly why a question distinguishing between isomers always expects a structural or displayed formula, not just C₄H₁₀.

## Saturated vs unsaturated

- **Saturated** — only single C–C bonds (alkanes).
- **Unsaturated** — contains at least one carbon-carbon bond that isn't single, most commonly C=C (alkenes).

**Test:** add **bromine water**. Orange-brown → **colourless** indicates unsaturation (alkene). No change (bromine water stays orange-brown) means the compound is saturated.

This is the single most examined organic test. *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* It also underpins the difference in typical reaction type between the two series, since alkanes mostly undergo substitution while alkenes mostly undergo addition.

## Key reactions to recall

*(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Alkanes react by **substitution** (one atom replacing another), while alkenes react by **addition** (atoms adding across the double bond) — the saturated/unsaturated distinction above is exactly why.

```
alkane + halogen  --UV light-->   substitution
alkene + bromine  ---->           ADDITION (decolourises)
alkene + steam    --catalyst-->   alcohol
ethanol  --oxidation (acidified KMnO4, or bacteria)-->  ethanoic acid
alcohol + acid    ---->           ESTER + water
```

0620 Core: alkanes are generally unreactive except in combustion and substitution by chlorine
(11.4 outcome 2); the bromine water test (11.5 outcome 4); ethanol made by adding steam to ethene
(11.6 outcome 1). *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* The meaning of substitution and addition, UV light for
substitution (11.4 outcomes 3–4, 11.5 outcomes 5–6), oxidising ethanol to ethanoic acid
(11.7 outcome 2) and making an ester (11.7 outcome 3).

## Exam traps

- Alkanes are saturated and relatively unreactive; alkenes are unsaturated and reactive.
- Bromine water goes **colourless**, not "clear" — clear is not a colour.
- Displayed formulae must show **all** bonds including C–H.
- Alkene general formula is CₙH₂ₙ — no +2.
- *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Isomers must have the **same molecular formula**; different formulae are just different compounds.
- *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Confusing propan-1-ol with propan-2-ol — the locant number is part of the name, not an optional extra.
- *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Naming an ester the wrong way round — it is always **alcohol (-yl) + acid (-oate)**, in that order.

## Self-test

1. Give the general formula of the alkenes.
2. *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Name C₃H₇OH and C₃H₇COOH.
3. How would you distinguish hexane from hexene?
4. *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Draw the two structural isomers of C₄H₁₀ (describe them).
5. What defines a homologous series?
6. Use the general formula to find the molecular formula of the alkane with 6 carbon atoms.
7. *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* Name the ester formed from methanol and ethanoic acid.
8. *(0620 Extended, 5070 required)* What is the difference between propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol?

**Answers:** 1. CₙH₂ₙ. 2. Propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol (the formula C₃H₇OH fits both; it does not show where the –OH is) and butanoic acid (the carboxyl carbon counts, so C₃H₇COOH has four carbons). 3. Add bromine water: hexene decolourises it (orange-brown → colourless); hexane produces no change. 4. Butane — an unbranched chain of four carbons; methylpropane — a three-carbon chain with a CH₃ branch on the middle carbon. 5. Compounds sharing the same functional group and general formula, each differing from the next by CH₂, with a gradual trend in physical properties and similar chemical properties. (0620 Core needs only: a family of similar compounds with similar chemical properties due to the same functional group.) 6. C6H(2x6)+2 = **C₆H₁₄** (hexane; the name is background, beyond the four-carbon naming limit). 7. **Methyl ethanoate**. 8. Both are C₃H₇OH, but the –OH is on the end carbon in propan-1-ol and on the middle carbon in propan-2-ol — same molecular formula, different structure.
