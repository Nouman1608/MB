Subtopic 1.1 opens Paper 1 English Legal System and is assumed knowledge for every other 9084 paper. It has five strands: context (1.1.1), parliamentary law making (1.1.2), delegated legislation (1.1.3), statutory interpretation (1.1.4) and judicial precedent (1.1.5). This guide goes beyond the [Topic 1 overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-law-english-legal-system/) into the detail each strand lists, with supporting cases.

## 1.1.1 The legal system in context

**Families of legal systems.** *Civil law* systems rest on comprehensive codes (France, Germany). *Common law* systems, which began in England, give judicial decisions the status of law through precedent. *Customary law* draws on community practice, *religious law* on religious texts and scholarship, and *mixed systems* combine traditions, as Scotland and South Africa blend civil and common law.

**Adversarial and inquisitorial procedure.** English trials are adversarial: each side presents its own evidence and the judge is a neutral umpire. In an inquisitorial system the judge directs the investigation and questions witnesses. The adversarial model protects party autonomy but can favour the better-resourced side.

**The rule of law.** Dicey's three ideas: no punishment except for a breach of law proved in the ordinary courts; equality before the law; rights secured by the ordinary law. In *Entick v Carrington* (1765) state officers who searched a house without lawful authority were liable in trespass. Apply the principle to:

- **Law making** – laws should be made openly and be clear and prospective; retrospective criminal laws offend it.
- **The legal system** – access to independent courts, legal advice and fair trial procedures.
- **Substantive law** – rules should treat like cases alike; in *A v Secretary of State for the Home Department* (2004) the House of Lords declared indefinite detention without trial of foreign terrorist suspects incompatible with Convention rights.

**Law and morality.** Law is enforceable; morality is belief about right and wrong. They overlap (murder, theft) but diverge: adultery is immoral to many but not a crime. The Hart–Devlin debate followed the Wolfenden Report (1957): Devlin argued that society may use law to protect its shared morality, while Hart, following Mill, limited law to preventing harm to others. Judges reflect changing morality: *R v R* (1991) removed the marital exemption for rape, while *R v Brown* (1993) held that consent was no defence to injuries inflicted in sadomasochistic activity.

**Law, justice and society.** Justice may be formal (rules applied consistently), substantive (fair outcomes) or corrective (putting right a wrong); equity and appeals exist because law does not always deliver it. Law keeps order, resolves disputes, protects rights and reflects social change.

**Fault.** Most crimes need mens rea and most torts need negligence. Strict liability dispenses with fault for at least one element; *Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v Attorney-General of Hong Kong* (1985) explains when courts accept this, chiefly for regulatory matters of social concern.

## 1.1.2 Parliamentary law making

**From green paper to Royal Assent.** A *green paper* consults on options; a *white paper* sets out firm proposals. The Bill then passes First Reading, Second Reading, Committee Stage, Report Stage and Third Reading in each House, followed by Royal Assent.

**Types of Bill.** Public Bills are usually introduced by a minister; Private Members' Bills by a backbencher (the Abortion Act 1967 began as David Steel's); Private Bills by a body seeking powers for itself; Hybrid Bills are public Bills affecting particular private interests.

**The Commons and the Lords.** The elected Commons prevails; the Lords revises and delays: under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 most Bills can be passed without Lords' consent after a delay of about a year, and money Bills after one month. The Acts were used for the War Crimes Act 1991 and the Hunting Act 2004, and *R (Jackson) v Attorney General* (2005) upheld the validity of the 1949 Act.

**Parliamentary supremacy.** Parliament can make or repeal any law, cannot bind its successors, and no court can invalidate an Act. In *Pickin v British Railways Board* (1974) the House of Lords refused to inquire into how an Act had been passed. A declaration of incompatibility under s4 Human Rights Act 1998 leaves the Act in force.

**Influences on Parliament.**

*Political pressure* comes from the manifesto and the need to win votes. *Public opinion* after the Dunblane shooting (1996) contributed to the Firearms (Amendment) Act 1997, which banned most handguns. *Pressure groups* are sectional (protecting members, such as trade unions) or cause groups (promoting a belief). *Media* campaigns raise issues fast, but rushed laws such as the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991 can be badly drafted.

**The Law Commission** was set up by the Law Commissions Act 1965 as a full-time, independent body. A senior judge chairs it, with four other commissioners. It researches, consults and drafts Bills. Successes include the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 and the Fraud Act 2006; but many reports, including the draft Criminal Code, were never enacted because implementation needs government time.

## 1.1.3 Delegated legislation

- **Orders in Council** – made by the monarch and the Privy Council, used for urgent matters and, under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004, emergencies.
- **Statutory instruments** – made by ministers and government departments; the most common form.
- **Bylaws** – made by local authorities and public corporations for their area or undertaking.

**The Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006** lets ministers make orders amending primary legislation to remove or reduce burdens, subject to conditions and parliamentary procedures. Critics see a wide executive power to change Acts with limited debate.

**Parliamentary controls.** *Pre-drafting consultation* with affected bodies, often required by the enabling Act. *Affirmative resolution*: the instrument needs the approval of Parliament. *Negative resolution*: it becomes law unless annulled within a set period, so many receive little scrutiny. *Scrutiny committees*, such as the Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments and the Lords Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee, report on instruments but cannot change them.

**Court controls (judicial review).** The claimant must have *locus standi*, a sufficient interest in the matter.

- **Procedural ultra vires** – the required procedure was not followed: *Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms* (1972), where a mushroom growers' association was not consulted.
- **Substantive ultra vires** – the content goes beyond the powers granted: *R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor* (2016), where a residence test for legal aid was outside the enabling Act.
- **Unreasonableness** – a decision no reasonable body could have made, the test in *Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation* (1948); *Strickland v Hayes Borough Council* (1896) struck down a bylaw banning obscene language in private as well as public places.

## 1.1.4 Statutory interpretation

| Approach | Key case |
|---|---|
| Literal rule | *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868): impersonating a dead voter was not impersonating "a person entitled to vote" |
| Golden rule | *Adler v George* (1964): "in the vicinity of" read to include inside a prohibited place |
| Mischief rule | *Smith v Hughes* (1960): soliciting from balconies was "in a street", because the Act targeted soliciting seen by passers-by |
| Purposive approach | *R (Quintavalle) v Secretary of State for Health* (2003): embryos created by cell nuclear replacement fell within the Act's purpose |

**Rules of language.** *Ejusdem generis*: in *Powell v Kempton Park Racecourse* (1899) "house, office, room or other place" was limited to indoor places. *Expressio unius exclusio alterius*: in *Tempest v Kilner* (1846) "goods, wares and merchandise" did not include stocks and shares. *Noscitur a sociis*: in *Inland Revenue Commissioners v Frere* (1965) "interest" took its meaning from "annual interest".

**Aids.** Intrinsic aids sit in the Act: preamble, titles, headings, schedules, marginal notes, interpretation sections. Extrinsic aids are outside it: earlier Acts on the same topic, earlier case law, the historical setting, dictionaries of the time, law reform reports, international treaties and Hansard, which *Pepper v Hart* (1993) allowed where the words are ambiguous and a ministerial statement clearly resolves the point.

**EU law and the Human Rights Act 1998.** During UK membership, courts read domestic law to give effect to EU law (*Pickstone v Freemans* (1988)), encouraging purposive interpretation. Section 3 requires legislation to be read compatibly with Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so": in *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* (2004) "as his or her wife or husband" included a same-sex partner.

## 1.1.5 Judicial precedent

**Law reporting** makes *stare decisis* workable: the Incorporated Council of Law Reporting has published reports since 1865, and neutral citations identify judgments by court and number.

- **Original precedent** – a new point, as in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932).
- **Persuasive precedent** – obiter dicta, lower courts, the Privy Council and foreign courts. In *R v James and Karimi* (2006) the Court of Appeal followed the Privy Council in *Attorney-General for Jersey v Holley* (2005) rather than a House of Lords decision.

**The Supreme Court and the Practice Statement 1966.** Examples include *British Railways Board v Herrington* (1972), overruling *Addie v Dumbreck* (1929) on child trespassers, and *R v Shivpuri* (1986), overruling *Anderton v Ryan* (1985) within a year.

**The Court of Appeal and *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co* (1944).** The Civil Division is bound by its own decisions except where two of its decisions conflict, where its decision conflicts with a later House of Lords or Supreme Court decision, or where it was made *per incuriam* (without reference to a relevant authority).

**Avoidance.** Distinguishing (*Balfour v Balfour* (1919) and *Merritt v Merritt* (1970) differ because the spouses in *Merritt* had separated), overruling and reversing.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how the courts control delegated legislation.* (part (a) style)

> The courts control delegated legislation through judicial review, brought by a claimant with sufficient interest (locus standi). **[identifies mechanism]** A court may declare an instrument procedurally ultra vires where the maker failed to follow a required procedure, as in *Aylesbury Mushrooms*, where failure to consult made the order ineffective against the growers. **[rule + case + outcome]** It may be substantively ultra vires where it goes beyond the enabling Act, as in *Public Law Project*. **[second ground]** Finally, it may be struck down as unreasonable, as in *Strickland v Hayes*. **[third ground]** The courts cannot, however, review an Act of Parliament itself. **[limit linked to supremacy]**

Each ground is stated, supported and linked to its outcome, as "explain" demands.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a declaration of incompatibility as striking down an Act.
- Citing *Pepper v Hart* as permitting Hansard whenever the court wishes.
- Describing the *Young v Bristol Aeroplane* exceptions as applying to the Supreme Court.

## Exam technique

*Describe* rewards accurate features; *explain* needs reasons linking rule to effect; *assess* and *evaluate* need arguments weighed to a justified conclusion. For any rule of interpretation, state it, cite a case and show its result.
