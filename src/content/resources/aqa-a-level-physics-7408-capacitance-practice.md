These questions cover sub-section 3.7.4 Capacitance of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards): capacitance, the parallel-plate capacitor, energy stored and charge and discharge through a resistor. It is A-level only and assessed in Paper 2.

Revise from the [capacitance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-capacitance/) and [capacitance revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-capacitance-revision-notes/) first. Mixed section 3.7 questions are in the [Fields and their consequences practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-fields-and-their-consequences-practice/).

Data: ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define capacitance and the farad. **[2]**

**2.** A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area 0.10 m² separated by 0.20 mm of air. Calculate its capacitance. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how a dielectric made of polar molecules increases the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor. **[3]**

**4.** Use a graph of charge against potential difference to show that the energy stored by a capacitor is ½QV. **[2]**

**5.** State what is meant by the time constant of a capacitor-resistor circuit and show that RC has the unit of seconds. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A constant current of 25 μA charges an initially uncharged capacitor for 40 s, after which the p.d. across it is 4.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the charge stored. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the capacitance. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy stored. **[1]**

**7.** A 470 μF capacitor charged to 6.0 V discharges through a 100 kΩ resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the time constant. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the initial current. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. after 60 s. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the energy dissipated in the resistor during the first 60 s. **[2]**

**8.** In Required practical 9, a student discharges a capacitor through a 68 kΩ resistor and plots ln(V/V) against t. The line has a gradient of −0.040 s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the capacitance. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why using the gradient of this graph is better than reading the time constant from a single point on a V–t curve. **[1]**
**(c)** The student times readings with a stopwatch. Explain why a large value of RC is chosen. **[1]**

**9.** An uncharged 220 μF capacitor is connected in series with a 10 kΩ resistor to a 9.0 V supply at t = 0.

**(a)** Calculate the time constant. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the capacitor at t = 1.5 s. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the current at t = 1.5 s. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the time for the capacitor p.d. to reach 90% of 9.0 V. **[2]**

**10.** A capacitor with plates of area 0.020 m², 0.10 mm apart and filled with a dielectric, has a capacitance of 5.3 nF.

**(a)** Calculate the relative permittivity of the dielectric. **[2]**
**(b)** The capacitor is charged to 50 V and isolated. The dielectric is removed, leaving air. Calculate the new p.d. and the new energy stored. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain where the extra energy comes from. **[1]**

**11.** A bank of capacitors stores 200 J when charged to 2.0 kV.

**(a)** Calculate the total capacitance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge stored. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. when half of the energy has been delivered. **[2]**

**12.** A capacitor is charged from a battery through a resistor. Describe the shapes of the current–time and capacitor p.d.–time graphs and explain why the current decreases. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Capacitance is the charge stored per unit potential difference, C = Q/V [1]; one farad is one coulomb per volt [1].

**2.** C = Aε₀/d = 0.10 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²/2.0 × 10⁻⁴ [1] = **4.4 × 10⁻⁹ F** [1]

**3.** The polar molecules rotate in the field so their positive ends face the negative plate [1]; they produce a field that opposes the applied field, reducing the p.d. for the same charge [1]; since C = Q/V, the capacitance increases [1].

**4.** The graph is a straight line through the origin; the work done adding charge ΔQ at p.d. V is VΔQ, a strip under the graph [1]; the total is the triangle's area, ½QV [1].

**5.** RC is the time for the charge (or p.d.) of a discharging capacitor to fall to 1/e, about 37%, of its initial value [1]; Ω × F = (V A⁻¹)(C V⁻¹) = C A⁻¹ = s [1].

**6. (a)** Q = It = 25 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 = **1.0 × 10⁻³ C** [1]
**(b)** C = Q/V = **2.5 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1]
**(c)** E = ½QV = **2.0 × 10⁻³ J** [1]

**7. (a)** RC = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 × 10³ = **47 s** [1]
**(b)** I₀ = 6.0/100 × 10³ = **6.0 × 10⁻⁵ A** [1]
**(c)** V = 6.0e^(−60/47) [1] = **1.7 V** [1]
**(d)** Energy at start ½CV₀² = 8.46 × 10⁻³ J; at 60 s ½CV² = 6.6 × 10⁻⁴ J [1]; dissipated = **7.8 × 10⁻³ J** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Energy depends on V², so after 60 s only about 8% of the energy remains though about 28% of the p.d. does.

**8. (a)** Gradient = −1/RC, so RC = 25 s [1]; C = 25/68 × 10³ = **3.7 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1]
**(b)** The gradient uses all the data points, reducing the effect of random error in any single reading [1].
**(c)** The p.d. changes slowly, so the time of each reading can be measured accurately compared with reaction time [1].

**9. (a)** RC = 220 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 × 10³ = **2.2 s** [1]
**(b)** V = 9.0(1 − e^(−1.5/2.2)) [1] = **4.4 V** [1]
**(c)** I = (9.0 − 4.45)/10 × 10³ [1] = **4.6 × 10⁻⁴ A** [1]
**(d)** 0.90 = 1 − e^(−t/2.2), so t = 2.2 ln 10 [1] = **5.1 s** [1]

**10. (a)** εᵣ = Cd/(Aε₀) = 5.3 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁴/(0.020 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) [1] = **3.0** [1]
**(b)** Q = 5.3 × 10⁻⁹ × 50 = 2.65 × 10⁻⁷ C stays constant; C_air = 1.77 × 10⁻⁹ F [1]; V = Q/C = **150 V** [1]; E = ½QV = **2.0 × 10⁻⁵ J** (up from 6.6 × 10⁻⁶ J) [1]
**(c)** Work is done pulling the dielectric out against the attraction between it and the plates [1].

**11. (a)** C = 2E/V² = 2 × 200/(2.0 × 10³)² [1] = **1.0 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1]
**(b)** Q = CV = **0.20 C** [1]
**(c)** E ∝ V², so V = 2000/√2 [1] = **1.4 × 10³ V** [1]

**12.** Current falls exponentially from ε/R towards zero; capacitor p.d. rises towards ε along a curve whose gradient decreases [1]; as charge builds up, the capacitor p.d. opposes the supply, so the p.d. across the resistor (ε − V_C) falls [1]; the current, (ε − V_C)/R, therefore falls [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Prefix slips with μF, pF and kΩ.
- Using e^(−t/RC) for p.d. while charging.
- Assuming p.d. is constant after a dielectric is removed from an isolated capacitor.
- Calculating energy lost from the change in p.d. instead of the change in V².
- Giving the 50% time as RC.
