This **higher level (HL) only** set covers the regional study of Asian kingdoms and empires (750 CE to 1500 CE) from the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. The Paper 3 essay questions require you to refer to one kingdom or empire; the answers below use the Mongol Empire, and you may substitute your own example. Study first with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-asian-kingdoms-and-empires/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-asian-kingdoms-and-empires-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain two ways in which the army of one Asian empire you have studied was organised to win wars. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract, which is *constructed for practice* and is not a real quotation.

> "The empire's roads were safe because every town on them was afraid of the khan, and rich because every merchant was protected by him. Fear and profit together did the work of government." (A historian's summary, constructed for practice)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about how the empire kept control. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of this view as a full explanation of how power was maintained. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the role of religious or spiritual beliefs in the maintenance of power in one Asian kingdom or empire. **[6]**

**4.** To what extent were military factors the main reason for the emergence of one Asian kingdom or empire? **[15]**

**5.** Discuss the reasons for the decline of one Asian kingdom or empire. **[15]**

**6.** Explain how land or maritime trade routes contributed to the development of one Asian kingdom or empire. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each explained (1 mark for the point, 1 for the link to winning). For example: a decimal system of tens, hundreds, thousands and ten-thousands gave clear command and let large armies split and rejoin; promotion by merit and loyalty rather than clan rank meant capable commanders; horse archers could move fast and strike from a distance; siege engineers recruited from conquered lands let the army take walled cities. **[4]**

**2.** **(a)** The extract suggests that control rested on a mixture of fear and reward (1). Fear of punishment kept towns obedient, as in the cities that resisted and were destroyed (1). Protection of merchants and trade gave subjects a reason to accept rule (1). **[3]**
**(b)** It leaves out other tools of rule. Allow, for example: use of local officials and rulers who submitted; the yam post system that carried orders; a census and taxation; a ruling ideology of heavenly mandate; religious tolerance. Award 1 for naming a missing factor, 1 for explaining how it supported control, 1 for linking it to the claim that fear and profit were the whole explanation. **[3]**

**3.** Credit analysis, not description. Marking points (up to 6): the claim of Eternal Heaven's mandate gave the khans a belief in universal rule (up to 2); tolerance of Buddhists, Muslims, Christians and Daoists won the support of leaders of conquered peoples (up to 2); a counterpoint, for example that belief mattered less than the army, or that conversion of later khans to Islam or Buddhism sometimes divided the empire (up to 2). A candidate using Angkor can credit the devaraja and Buddhist kingship. **[6]**

**4.** A strong answer states a thesis such as: military factors were essential, but they depended on political unity and on weak neighbours. Marking points (up to 15):
- Thesis and criteria for "main reason" stated in the introduction (up to 2).
- Military factors with specific evidence: decimal organisation, mobility, siege engineers, the Khwarazm campaign of 1219 to 1221, the end of the Jin in 1234 (up to 4).
- Political factors: unity under Chinggis Khan in 1206, the keshig, alliances and marriage ties (up to 3).
- Economic and environmental factors: herding, control of routes, the debated climate hypothesis (up to 2).
- Evaluation of perspectives, for example the view that steppe conditions made a unifier likely, against views that stress the individual (up to 2).
- A judgement consistent with the argument, for example that military power was the tool but political unity the precondition (up to 2). **[15]**

**5.** A strong answer ranks causes. Marking points (up to 15):
- A clear thesis and a definition of "decline" for the chosen example (up to 2).
- Political factors: division into khanates, rivalry among princes, succession disputes (up to 3).
- Social and economic factors: heavy taxation, paper money problems, ethnic resentment in the Yuan (up to 3).
- Conflict and rebellion: Red Turban risings in the 1350s, the Ming victory of 1368 (up to 2).
- Environmental factors: floods, famine and plague (up to 2).
- Perspectives: for example, historians who stress internal weakness against those who stress environmental shocks (up to 1).
- A reasoned judgement that ranks the causes and explains the links, for example that rebellion was the trigger but earlier division had left the Yuan unable to respond (up to 2). **[15]**

**6.** Award up to 2 marks for each of three developed points, with a maximum of 6. Mongol example: the overland routes carried silver, silk and horses, and taxes on trade paid for the state (2); the yam stations and the paiza made long-distance travel and official messages possible, which tied distant regions together (2); safe passage under the Pax Mongolica encouraged envoys, merchants and the spread of technology such as paper money and gunpowder, which strengthened the empire's reach and reputation (2). Angkor example: its control of rice-growing land linked to river and sea trade with China, and Zhou Daguan's account of 1296 to 1297 describes a rich and busy capital (2). A point earns full marks only if it explains the link to development, not just the existence of trade. **[6]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Which kingdom should I use in the exam?**
Choose one before the exam and learn it in depth. Every question requires reference to one kingdom or empire, so a general answer about "Asia" will lose marks.

**How do I earn marks for perspectives in an essay?**
Name a debate, such as destruction against connection, say who held each view and why, and use it to support your judgement.
