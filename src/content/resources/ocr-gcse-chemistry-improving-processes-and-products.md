## Extracting metals with carbon (C6.1a)

Most metals occur in **ores** as compounds, often oxides. Extraction is **reduction**: removing the oxygen. Carbon sits between aluminium and zinc in the reactivity series, so:

- metals **less reactive than carbon** (zinc, iron, tin, lead, copper) can be reduced by heating the oxide with carbon;
- metals **more reactive than carbon** (aluminium, magnesium, sodium) cannot.

In the blast furnace, carbon monoxide from coke reduces iron(III) oxide: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Copper is a non-ferrous example: 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂.

**PAG C1 practical.** Heat copper(II) oxide with excess carbon powder in a covered crucible. Brown-pink specks of copper form in the black powder.

**Worked example.** A student heats 4.00 g of copper(II) oxide with carbon and recovers 2.72 g of copper. Find the percentage yield. Relative atomic masses: Cu = 63.5, O = 16.

1. Mr of CuO = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5.
2. Fraction of the mass that is copper = 63.5 ÷ 79.5. Maximum copper = 4.00 × 63.5 ÷ 79.5 = **3.19 g**.
3. Percentage yield = 2.72 ÷ 3.19 × 100 = **85.1%** (using the unrounded 3.195).

## Extracting metals by electrolysis (C6.1b)

**Why:** carbon cannot reduce the oxides of metals above it, so electrical energy forces the reduction. It costs more than carbon reduction: large currents run for long periods and the ore must be molten.

**How (aluminium):** aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite, which lowers the operating temperature and saves energy. The ions must be free to move.

- Cathode (negative): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Molten aluminium collects at the bottom.
- Anode (positive): 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻. The oxygen reacts with the hot carbon anodes to make carbon dioxide, so the anodes wear away and are replaced.

**PAG C2 practicals.** With inert electrodes:

| Solution | Cathode product | Anode product | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aqueous sodium chloride | hydrogen | chlorine | squeaky pop; damp blue litmus turns red then bleaches |
| Aqueous copper sulfate | copper | oxygen | pink-brown coating; relights a glowing splint |

**Graph skill (M4b, M4c).** A student weighs the cathode during copper sulfate electrolysis:

| Time / min | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass gained / g | 0 | 0.10 | 0.19 | 0.30 | 0.40 |

Plot time on the x-axis and draw a line of best fit through the origin. Gradient = 0.40 ÷ 16 = **0.025 g/min**, the rate at which copper is deposited.

## Biological extraction (C6.1c, Higher Tier only)

High-grade copper ores are running out. Two biological methods work on low-grade ore.

- **Bacterial extraction (bioleaching):** bacteria break down compounds in low-grade ore and produce a solution (leachate) containing copper ions. Copper is then obtained by displacement with scrap iron or by electrolysis.
- **Phytoextraction:** plants grown on the ore absorb metal ions. The crop is cut and burned, leaving ash rich in metal compounds.

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Uses ore too poor to mine conventionally | Very slow (months or years) |
| No large open-cast mine, less waste rock | Leachate can be toxic if it escapes |
| Lower energy use than smelting | Burning plants releases carbon dioxide |

An "evaluate" answer weighs both sides and ends with a judgement.

## Rate against equilibrium (C6.1d, C6.1e, C6.1f; Higher Tier only)

**Haber process:** N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, forward reaction exothermic. Conditions: about 450 °C, about 200 atmospheres, iron catalyst.

- A lower temperature would give a higher yield but too slow a rate, so 450 °C is a **compromise**.
- Four gas molecules become two, so high pressure shifts equilibrium right and raises the rate. Higher pressures need costly compression and stronger plant.
- The iron catalyst increases rate but not yield.
- Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled, so little is wasted.

**Contact process:** 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, forward reaction exothermic. Conditions: about 450 °C, pressure close to atmospheric, vanadium(V) oxide catalyst. The equilibrium already lies far to the right, so extra pressure would add cost for little gain.

**Commercial conditions (C6.1f)** balance the cost and availability of raw materials (air, natural gas, sulfur), energy costs for heating and compression, equilibrium yield and rate. A fast reaction with a modest yield plus recycling can beat a slow one with a high yield.

**Rate graphs (C6.1e, CM6.1i–iv).** On a product-against-time curve, the rate at any moment is the **gradient of the tangent**.

**Worked example.** A tangent to a volume-of-gas curve passes through (0 s, 8 cm³) and (40 s, 56 cm³). Rate = (56 − 8) ÷ (40 − 0) = **1.2 cm³/s**. In another run, doubling the concentration from 0.40 to 0.80 mol/dm³ doubles the initial rate from 0.021 to 0.042 cm³/s, so rate is **directly proportional** to concentration: a straight line through the origin on a rate–concentration graph.

## The Haber process and agriculture (C6.1g)

Ammonia from the Haber process is the starting point for most nitrogen fertilisers. Plants need nitrogen to make proteins, so fertilisers raise crop yields and let farms feed far more people than natural nitrogen sources could.

## Fertilisers (C6.1h, C6.1i, C6.1j)

**N, P and K:** compounds of these replace elements that crops remove from soil. They must be **soluble** so roots can absorb them.

**Integrated industrial production (C6.1j).** Several plants feed each other on one site:

- Air and natural gas → hydrogen and nitrogen → **ammonia** (Haber).
- Ammonia oxidised → **nitric acid**; ammonia + nitric acid → **ammonium nitrate**.
- Sulfur → **sulfuric acid** (Contact); ammonia + sulfuric acid → **ammonium sulfate**.
- Phosphate rock and mined potassium chloride supply P and K.

**Worked example.** Potassium sulfate, K₂SO₄, supplies potassium. Using K = 39, S = 32, O = 16: Mr = 78 + 32 + 64 = 174. Percentage of potassium = 78 ÷ 174 × 100 = **44.8%**.

**Laboratory synthesis (C6.1h, PAG C6).** Titrate ammonia solution with dilute sulfuric acid using an indicator to find the volume needed. Repeat with the same volumes and no indicator, then evaporate some water and leave to crystallise. Potassium sulfate can be made the same way from potassium hydroxide solution.

| Laboratory | Industry |
|---|---|
| Batch process, grams | Continuous process, tonnes |
| Dilute solutions bought in | Concentrated reactants made on site |
| Heating to evaporate water | Heat from the exothermic reaction is reused |
| Several steps by hand | Automated, few workers per tonne |

## Life-cycle assessment (C6.1k, C6.1l)

A life-cycle assessment (LCA) totals the resources used and the environmental impact at **every stage**:

1. making the materials from raw materials, and the process that makes the product;
2. the use of the product;
3. transport of the product;
4. disposal at the end of its life.

Energy use can be measured; some effects, such as landscape damage, need value judgements.

**Worked example.** Use these illustrative values. A steel drinks bottle takes 18 MJ to make and transport and 0.05 MJ to wash per use. A single-use plastic bottle takes 1.2 MJ each. After how many uses is the steel bottle better?

Steel total = 18 + 0.05n; plastic total = 1.2n. They are equal when 18 = 1.15n, n = **15.7**, so from the **16th use** steel uses less energy (18.8 MJ against 19.2 MJ).

## Recycling (C6.1m, C6.1n)

Plastic drinks bottles are washed, shredded, melted and drawn into fibres for fleece clothing. Waste glass of the wrong colour for new bottles is crushed into aggregate for road surfaces. Both are viable because the material is collected in bulk, the new use does not need high purity, and new raw materials and landfill space are saved.

Factors in recycling decisions: cost and energy of collection, transport and sorting; how easily materials separate; value of recycled against new material; scarcity of the raw material; energy saved; landfill charges.

## Alloys, corrosion and protection (C6.1o, C6.1p, C6.1q)

| Alloy | Main metals | Property | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel | iron with carbon (stainless also chromium, nickel) | strong; stainless resists corrosion | bridges, cutlery |
| Brass | copper and zinc | hard, does not rust, easy to machine | door handles, musical instruments |
| Bronze | copper and tin | hard, resists corrosion | statues, ship propellers |
| Solder | tin with lead, or tin with small amounts of copper or silver | low melting point | joining electrical components |
| Duralumin | aluminium with about 4% copper and small amounts of magnesium and manganese | strong, low density | aircraft parts |

**Corrosion** is a metal reacting with substances around it. Iron rusts only when **both oxygen and water** are present, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide, which flakes off and exposes more metal. Salt speeds rusting. Aluminium's thin oxide layer protects it; copper slowly turns green.

**Mitigation:** a **physical barrier** (paint, oil, grease, plastic coating, plating) keeps oxygen and water off. **Sacrificial protection** attaches a more reactive metal, such as zinc or magnesium, which oxidises instead of the iron. Galvanising (a zinc coating) is both, so it still protects when scratched.

## Common errors

- Saying carbon extracts aluminium. Aluminium is above carbon.
- Calling cryolite a catalyst.
- Claiming a catalyst increases the yield of ammonia.
- Giving only one condition for rusting.
- Not saying the sacrificial metal is **more reactive**.
- Missing an LCA stage, often transport or disposal.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a life-cycle assessment?**
A life-cycle assessment (LCA) totals the resources used and the environmental impact at every stage of a product's life. Energy use can be measured, but some effects, such as landscape damage, need value judgements.

**What is the difference between barrier and sacrificial protection?**
A barrier such as paint, oil, grease or plating keeps oxygen and water away from iron, but fails once scratched. Sacrificial protection attaches a more reactive metal such as zinc or magnesium, which oxidises instead of the iron; galvanising does both.

**What are bioleaching and phytoextraction?**
In bioleaching, bacteria break down compounds in low-grade ore to produce a leachate containing copper ions. In phytoextraction, plants grown on the ore absorb metal ions and are burned to leave ash rich in metal compounds; both are Higher Tier only.

**Does a catalyst increase the yield of ammonia in the Haber process?**
No. The iron catalyst increases the rate of reaction but not the equilibrium yield. Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled, so little is wasted.
