These notes condense subtopic 2.1 Individuals, populations, communities and ecosystems of IB DP
Environmental Systems and Societies (first assessment 2026). The subtopic title is provisional,
because the IB's subject brief does not list Topic 2's subtopics. Full explanations and worked data
are in the [2.1 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-2-1/).

## Condensed recall

**Levels of organisation**
- Individual → population (one species, one area, one time) → community (all populations in an
  area) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment).

**Naming and identifying**
- Binomial name: *Genus species*, genus capitalised, italicised.
- Dichotomous key: paired choices on one clear, stable feature each.
- Keys struggle with juveniles, damaged specimens and variable features.

**Tolerance and limits**
- Range of tolerance → optimum in the middle, stress at the edges, death beyond the limits.
- Limiting factor = the factor furthest from its optimum.
- Generalists: wide tolerance, widespread. Specialists: narrow tolerance, restricted, vulnerable.

**Population regulation**
- Density-dependent (competition, predation, parasitism, disease) → negative feedback → numbers held
  near K.
- Density-independent (drought, frost, fire, flood) → sudden crashes, no regulation.
- J-curve: unchecked exponential growth, then crash. S-curve: lag, exponential, transitional,
  plateau at K.
- K can change if the habitat is degraded or improved.
- Predator–prey cycles: prey peak first; predators follow after a time lag (snowshoe hare and lynx).

**Interactions**
- Competitive exclusion: identical niches cannot coexist indefinitely.
- Resource partitioning: competitors split resources and narrow realised niches.
- Mutualism, parasitism, predation, herbivory, competition: know the effect on each partner.

**Strategies and survivorship**
- r: many small offspring, little care, early maturity, unstable habitats.
- K: few large offspring, much care, late maturity, stable habitats.
- Type I survivorship: low early death, high late death. Type II: constant risk. Type III: very
  high early death.

**Sampling**
- Random: uniform areas. Systematic (transects): gradients. Stratified: distinct zones.
- Density = individuals per m². Percentage cover = share of area covered. Percentage frequency =
  share of quadrats with the species.
- Abiotic kit: light meter, thermometer or data logger, pH probe, anemometer, dissolved-oxygen
  meter.
- Reliability: more samples, consistent method. Validity: unbiased placement, full coverage.

## Method in steps: quadrat calculations

```
Density:
1. Total count ÷ number of quadrats = mean per quadrat.
2. Mean ÷ quadrat area = density per m².
3. Density × total area = population estimate.

Percentage frequency:
1. Count quadrats containing the species.
2. ÷ total quadrats × 100.
```

Mini example (invented): 15 quadrats of 0.5 m², 9 contain clover, 36 plants counted. Frequency =
9 ÷ 15 × 100 = 60%; mean = 36 ÷ 15 = 2.4 per quadrat; density = 2.4 ÷ 0.5 = 4.8 plants per m².

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Binomial nomenclature | Two-part scientific name: genus and species |
| Dichotomous key | Identification tool using paired choices |
| Range of tolerance | Span of an abiotic factor a species can survive |
| Limiting factor | Factor in shortest supply relative to need, limiting growth |
| Carrying capacity (K) | Maximum population an environment can sustainably support |
| Density-dependent factor | Effect increases with population density |
| Density-independent factor | Effect unrelated to population density |
| Competitive exclusion | One species eliminates another with an identical niche |
| Resource partitioning | Competitors divide a resource and coexist |
| r-strategist | Species investing in many offspring with little care |
| K-strategist | Species investing in few offspring with much care |
| Survivorship curve | Graph of the number surviving against age |
| Percentage frequency | Proportion of samples in which a species occurs |
| Stratified sampling | Sampling each distinct zone in proportion |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Habitat vs niche:** where a species lives vs its whole role there, including what it eats, when
  it is active and which conditions it tolerates.
- **Fundamental vs realised niche:** every condition it could occupy vs the part it actually occupies
  once competitors and predators are present.
- **Population vs community:** one species in an area vs all the species interacting there.
- **Random vs systematic sampling:** avoids observer bias in a uniform area vs follows a gradient at
  fixed intervals.
- **Percentage cover vs density:** proportion of ground covered vs number of individuals per m².

## Exam traps

- **Binomials.** *quercus Robur* loses the mark; *Quercus robur* earns it.
- **Drought is density-independent.** It affects sparse and dense populations alike.
- **K is not permanent.** Degradation lowers it; habitat improvement can raise it.
- **Frequency is not density.** A species can be in every quadrat yet sparse.
- **Match strategy to gradient.** Random quadrats cannot show zonation.
- **Describe means data.** Quote values and years when describing a curve.

## Self-test

1. Write the binomial name of the pedunculate oak correctly.
2. Give two features of a good dichotomous key.
3. What is a range of tolerance?
4. Classify frost and disease as density-dependent or density-independent.
5. How does a density-dependent factor hold a population near K?
6. Why do predator numbers peak after prey numbers?
7. What does competitive exclusion predict?
8. Give three features of an r-strategist.
9. Which survivorship curve fits a fish that lays thousands of eggs?
10. Which sampling strategy suits a sand-dune gradient?
11. Twenty quadrats; a species is in 7. Calculate percentage frequency.
12. Give one way to improve the reliability of a quadrat survey.

### Answers

1. *Quercus robur*.
2. Any two: uses one feature per choice; features easy to see; features stable with age or sex; no
   vague terms.
3. The span of values of an abiotic factor within which a species can survive.
4. Frost: density-independent. Disease: density-dependent.
5. As density rises, its effect strengthens, raising deaths or lowering births until numbers fall
   back (negative feedback).
6. Predators need time to breed after food becomes plentiful (a time lag).
7. Two species with identical niches cannot coexist indefinitely.
8. Any three: many small offspring; little parental care; early maturity; short life; colonises
   unstable habitats.
9. Type III.
10. Systematic sampling along a transect.
11. 7 ÷ 20 × 100 = **35%**.
12. More quadrats, or the same observer and method throughout.
