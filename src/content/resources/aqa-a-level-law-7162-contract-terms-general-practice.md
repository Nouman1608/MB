These questions test the section 3.4 row on contract terms: express and implied terms, and conditions, warranties and innominate terms.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a term and a representation. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** factors the courts consider in deciding whether a statement is a term. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the officious bystander test for implying a term, with an authority. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a term implied in law, giving an example. **[3]**

**5.** State the remedies available for breach of a condition and for breach of a warranty. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the court did not treat the term as a condition in *Schuler v Wickman Machine Tool Sales* (1974). **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the courts decide whether a pre-contractual statement is a term or a representation, using **three** cases. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the approach to innominate terms in *Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki* (1962). **[4]**

**9.** Explain the test for implying a term in fact, with reference to *Marks and Spencer v BNP Paribas* (2015). **[4]**

**10.** Dina buys a used van from Gus, a car dealer. Before the sale Gus tells her, "This van has done only 40,000 miles; you won't need to check it." The written contract does not mention mileage. The van has done 90,000 miles. Advise Dina whether Gus's statement is a term. **[5]**

**11.** Fable Ltd hires Ruby, a singer, for a 10-night run of a musical. The contract requires her to attend rehearsals in the week before the run. Ruby misses the first two rehearsals but performs every night. Later, a second singer, Joss, misses the first three nights through illness, and Fable Ltd replaces him. Advise Fable Ltd and the singers on classification and remedies. **[6]**

**12.** "The law on classification of terms balances certainty against fairness." Discuss. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A term is a promise that forms part of the contract, so breach gives a claim for breach of contract [1]. A representation is a statement that induced the contract but is not part of it, so if false it may give a claim for misrepresentation [1].

**2.** Any two from: the importance of the statement to the claimant (*Bannerman v White*) [1]; the relative special knowledge of the parties (*Oscar Chess*; *Dick Bentley*) [1]; the time between the statement and the contract (*Routledge v McKay*); whether the statement was included in a written contract; whether the maker discouraged checks (*Schawel v Reade*).

**3.** A term will be implied if it is so obvious that, had an officious bystander suggested it, the parties would have replied "oh, of course" [1]: MacKinnon LJ in *Shirlaw v Southern Foundries* (1939) [1].

**4.** A term implied in law is implied into all contracts of a particular type, as a matter of legal policy [1], rather than because these particular parties intended it [1]. Example: in *Liverpool City Council v Irwin* (1977) a term that the landlord would take reasonable care to maintain the common parts was implied into council tenancies of tower block flats [1].

**5.** Breach of a condition allows the innocent party to terminate the contract [1] or to affirm it, and in either case claim damages [1]. Breach of a warranty gives damages only [1].

**6.** Although the clause requiring weekly sales visits was called a "condition" [1], the parties could not have intended that a single missed visit out of many would allow termination, so the label was not conclusive [1].

**7.** The courts ask what the parties intended, judged objectively [1]. Where the claimant made clear the statement was vital, it is likely to be a term: in *Bannerman v White* (1861) a buyer said he would not buy hops treated with sulphur [1]. The maker's special knowledge matters: in *Dick Bentley v Harold Smith Motors* (1965) a dealer's statement about mileage was a term [1], while in *Oscar Chess v Williams* (1957) a private seller's statement of a car's age, taken from the logbook, was a representation [1]. Timing also matters: in *Routledge v McKay* (1954) a statement made a week before a written contract that did not include it was a representation [1]. No factor is decisive; the court weighs them together [1].

**8.** Some terms cannot be classified in advance as conditions or warranties because they can be broken in trivial or serious ways [1]. In *Hong Kong Fir* a ship's seaworthiness term was broken because the engine crew were incompetent and the ship was out of action for about 20 weeks [1]. Diplock LJ asked whether the breach deprived the charterers of substantially the whole benefit they were intended to obtain [1]. As much of the 24-month charter remained, it did not, so the charterers could not terminate and were limited to damages [1].

**9.** A term is implied in fact only to give effect to the presumed intention of the particular parties [1]. *The Moorcock* (1889) implied a term needed to give the contract business efficacy [1]. In *Marks and Spencer v BNP Paribas* (2015) the Supreme Court held that a term will be implied only if it is necessary for business efficacy or so obvious it goes without saying [1]; it is not enough that it would be reasonable or fair, and the court will not improve the bargain the parties made [1].

**10.** The question is whether the parties intended the statement to be a term, judged objectively [1]. Gus is a dealer with special knowledge compared with Dina, which points towards a term (*Dick Bentley v Harold Smith Motors*) [1]. He told her she need not check, which also suggests a term (*Schawel v Reade*) [1]. Against this, the statement is not in the written contract (*Routledge v McKay*) [1], but the dealer's expertise and the assurance are likely to outweigh this, so the statement is probably a term and Dina can claim for breach of contract [1].

**11.** **Ruby:** attending rehearsals is subsidiary to the main purpose of performing, as in *Bettini v Gye* (1876) [1]. Missing two rehearsals is a breach of warranty [1], so Fable Ltd cannot terminate but may claim damages for any loss [1]. **Joss:** missing the opening nights goes to the root of the contract, as in *Poussard v Spiers* (1876) [1]. This is a breach of condition, or at least of an innominate term with serious consequences [1], so Fable Ltd was entitled to terminate and replace him; as his absence was due to illness, frustration may also be argued, but on these facts termination is justified [1].

**12.** Conditions give certainty: parties know in advance that breach allows termination, which matters in commerce (*Bunge v Tradax*, 1981) [1]. But certainty can produce unfairness when a party terminates for a trivial breach to escape a bad bargain, as in *Arcos v Ronaasen* (1933) [1]. The innominate approach in *Hong Kong Fir* (1962) is fairer, matching the remedy to the seriousness of the breach [1], but it is less certain, since a party who terminates may later be found to have done so wrongfully [1]. The courts balance these by treating key commercial time clauses as conditions and others as innominate, and by refusing to be bound by labels (*Schuler*) [1]. Conclusion: the balance is broadly sensible, giving certainty where commerce needs it and fairness elsewhere [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating every false statement as a term.
- Relying on a "condition" label without discussing *Schuler*.
- Implying terms because they are reasonable.
- Forgetting the option to affirm after breach of a condition.
