## Floating exchange rates

An exchange rate states how much of one currency buys a unit of another. This guide quotes it as estos per nor, so a rate of 2.00 means 1 nor buys 2 estos. In a **floating** system the market for foreign exchange decides the rate through buyers and sellers of the currency, and nobody sets an official target.

### The currency market diagram

Draw the market for the nor:

```
Exchange rate
(estos per nor)
  |
  |   \               /
  |    \            /
  |     \         /
2.00 - - - - - X - - - - -
  |         /    \
  |       /        \
  |     /            \
  |   S                D
  +--------------------------
                280   Quantity of nors
                      (millions per day)
```

- The vertical axis is the price of the nor in estos. The horizontal axis is the quantity of nors traded per day.
- Demand for nors slopes downwards. A higher rate makes Norland's exports dearer to Estovians, so they need fewer nors.
- Supply of nors slopes upwards. A higher rate makes imports cheaper for Norland, so more nors are offered to buy estos.
- Equilibrium is where the curves cross. Label the rate and the quantity.

**Appreciation** is a rise in the market value of a currency under a floating system. **Depreciation** is a fall. Appreciation of the nor means depreciation of the esto, because they are two sides of one price.

**Worked example (equilibrium and a shift).** In Norland the demand for nors is Qd = 400 - 60e and the supply is Qs = 100 + 90e, where e is the rate in estos per nor and quantities are in millions per day.

1. Set Qd = Qs: 400 - 60e = 100 + 90e, so 300 = 150e and e = **2.00**.
2. Quantity: 400 - 60(2.00) = **280 million nors**.
3. Foreign demand for Norland's exports rises, and demand becomes Qd = 460 - 60e. Then 460 - 60e = 100 + 90e, so 360 = 150e and e = **2.40**.
4. Quantity: 100 + 90(2.40) = **316 million nors**.

The demand curve shifted right and the nor appreciated from 2.00 to 2.40.

### Prices in different currencies

Foreign price = home price x exchange rate (estos per nor). A Norland good costs 45 nors.

- At 2.00 it costs 45 x 2.00 = **90 estos**.
- At 2.25 it costs 45 x 2.25 = **101.25 estos**.
- At 1.80 it costs 45 x 1.80 = **81 estos**.

To price an Estovian good in nors, divide by the rate. A good costing 63 estos costs 63 / 2.00 = 31.5 nors, and 63 / 2.25 = **28 nors** after the nor appreciates.

### Calculating changes in value

Percentage change = (new - old) / old x 100.

- The nor moves from 2.00 to 2.25 estos: (2.25 - 2.00) / 2.00 x 100 = **+12.5%**.
- The esto's value in nors moves from 1/2.00 = 0.5 to 1/2.25 = 0.444, a change of **-11.1%**. The two percentages are not equal, so name the currency you are measuring.

From a series, calculate each step from the rate before it. A rate that runs 2.00, 2.20, 1.98 and 2.079 over four years rises 10%, falls 10% and rises 5%. Over the whole period it rose (2.079 - 2.00) / 2.00 x 100 = **3.95%**. A 10% rise followed by a 10% fall does not return you to the start: 2.20 falls to 1.98, not 2.00.

## What shifts demand and supply

The guide lists ten items. For each, decide whether it changes the demand for the nor or the supply of nors, then the direction.

| Factor | Effect on the nor | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign demand for exports | Demand rises, so the nor appreciates | Foreign buyers need nors to pay |
| Domestic demand for imports | Supply rises, so the nor depreciates | Norland sells nors to buy foreign currency |
| Inward FDI and portfolio investment | Demand rises | Foreign investors buy nors to buy assets |
| Outward FDI and portfolio investment | Supply rises | Norland investors sell nors to buy foreign assets |
| Remittances | Inflows raise demand; outflows raise supply | Migrants' transfers are converted across currencies |
| Speculation | Expected appreciation raises demand; expected depreciation raises supply | Traders buy what they expect to gain value |
| Relative inflation rates | Higher Norland inflation lowers demand and raises supply | Exports lose competitiveness, imports gain it |
| Relative interest rates | Higher Norland rates raise demand | Funds move to earn more, which is portfolio investment |
| Relative growth rates | Faster Norland growth raises import demand, so supply rises | Rising incomes buy more imports |
| Central bank intervention | Buying nors raises demand; selling nors raises supply | The bank trades in the market itself |

Relative growth is not one-way. Faster growth can also attract inward investment, so state which effect you argue. Speculation can be self-fulfilling: if traders expect a fall, their selling causes it.

## Consequences of exchange rate changes

The guide asks you to examine effects on five indicators and draw AD/AS curves. Take a depreciation of the nor.

- **Inflation.** Imports cost more in nors, which raises prices directly. Imported inputs raise costs, so short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) shifts left. Rising export demand adds to demand-pull pressure.
- **Growth.** Exports cost less to Estovians and imports cost more to Norland. If foreign buyers respond strongly, net exports (X - M) rise and aggregate demand (AD) shifts right, raising real GDP.
- **Unemployment.** Higher output in export and import-competing firms creates jobs, so unemployment tends to fall.
- **Current account.** The balance on goods and services tends to improve if the quantities traded respond enough to the price change. If they respond little, the import bill rises and the balance can worsen.
- **Living standards.** Imported goods and holiday abroad become dearer, which cuts real purchasing power. More jobs may offset this.

On the AD/AS diagram, draw AD1 shifting right to AD2 and, for imported costs, SRAS1 shifting left to SRAS2. Real GDP rises if the AD shift dominates, and the price level rises in either case. An appreciation does the reverse: imports are cheaper and inflation eases, but exporters lose competitiveness, growth slows and the current account may weaken. Different stakeholders gain and lose, so a good answer names them: exporters, importers, consumers, tourists, firms with foreign debts.

## Fixed exchange rates

In a **fixed** system the government sets an official rate and the central bank defends it. A **devaluation** is an official cut in the rate and a **revaluation** is an official rise. Under a floating system the same moves are depreciation and appreciation.

**Worked example (defending a peg).** Norland pegs at 2.40 estos per nor, using the demand and supply from earlier (Qd = 400 - 60e, Qs = 100 + 90e).

1. At 2.40, Qd = 400 - 60(2.40) = 256 and Qs = 100 + 90(2.40) = 316 million nors.
2. Supply exceeds demand by 60 million nors. The market rate would be 2.00, so the nor is overvalued at the peg.
3. The central bank must buy 60 million nors, paying 60 x 2.40 = **144 million estos** from its reserves.

On the diagram draw a horizontal line at 2.40 above equilibrium. The gap between S and D along that line is the quantity the bank must buy. If the peg were 1.60, the nor would be undervalued: Qd = 304 and Qs = 244, so demand exceeds supply by 60 million. The bank would sell 60 million nors and gain 60 x 1.60 = **96 million estos** of reserves.

The ways to maintain a fixed rate:

- **Buy or sell the currency** using foreign exchange reserves, as above.
- **Change interest rates.** Higher rates attract portfolio investment and raise demand for the currency. Lower rates do the opposite.
- **Restrict or tax foreign exchange transactions** where the government chooses to.

Reserves are finite. A country defending an overvalued rate runs them down, which is why it may devalue instead. **Worked devaluation:** cutting the peg from 2.40 to 2.10 is a change of (2.10 - 2.40) / 2.40 x 100 = **-12.5%**. A 45-nor good falls from 108 to 94.5 estos, also -12.5%, so exports become cheaper.

## Managed exchange rates

In a **managed** system the rate floats, but the central bank intervenes to influence it. The currency is **overvalued** when its rate is above the level the market alone would set, and **undervalued** when below.

**Worked example (smoothing a rise).** Foreign demand lifts demand to Qd = 460 - 60e, so the market rate would be 2.40. Norland's central bank wants 2.20.

1. At 2.20, Qd = 460 - 132 = 328 and Qs = 100 + 198 = 298 million nors.
2. Demand exceeds supply by 30 million nors. At 2.20 the nor is undervalued compared with the market rate of 2.40.
3. The bank sells 30 million nors and gains 30 x 2.20 = **66 million estos** of reserves.

On the diagram show the new demand curve, the market equilibrium at 2.40, the target at 2.20 and the horizontal gap between D and S. Governments may hold a currency down to protect exporters, or hold it up to limit import prices. Each has costs: reserves, inflation from the money created when the bank sells its own currency, or retaliation by trading partners.

## Fixed versus floating (HL only)

Evaluate the two systems against criteria rather than listing them.

| Criterion | Fixed | Floating |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty for trade and investment | Greater, if the peg is believed | Lower, rates move daily |
| Monetary policy | Interest rates must serve the peg | Free to target domestic goals |
| Adjustment | Needs reserves, or devaluation | Rate moves to correct imbalances |
| Reserves | Large reserves needed | Little needed |
| Speculation | A weak peg invites attack | Exposed to volatility |
| Inflation discipline | Can import a partner's low inflation | Depreciation can raise import prices |

A judgement depends on context: a small, trade-dependent economy may value stability, while a large economy with its own cycle may value policy freedom.

## Common errors

- Drawing the axis as "price" or "exchange rate" with no units or currency pair.
- Shifting the wrong curve, for example a rise in imports moving demand for the currency.
- Applying a percentage change to the wrong currency.
- Calling a market move a devaluation, or an official move a depreciation.
- Saying a depreciation always improves the current account, without any condition.
- Describing intervention without saying whether the bank buys or sells its own currency.
