**This practice set covers topic 4.5, Satisfaction at work, in Specialist Option 4 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All organisations, people and data are fictional.

Related: [Satisfaction at work study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-satisfaction-at-work/) · [Satisfaction at work revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-satisfaction-at-work-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Distinguish between job enrichment and job rotation. **[2]**

**2.** Outline **four** of Walton's conditions for quality of working life. **[4]**

**3.** Outline the four categories of employee in Blau and Boal's model. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why Herzberg's method may have produced his two-factor pattern. **[2]**

**5.** Some dissatisfied employees sabotage their organisation.

**(a)** Describe the study by Giacalone and Rosenfeld (1987). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate the study by Giacalone and Rosenfeld (1987), including a discussion of individual and situational explanations. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** At a parcel depot, workers sort parcels all day, never find out whether deliveries arrive on time, and must follow a fixed procedure for every task.

**(a)** Using Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics theory, identify **two** characteristics that are low in this job. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** change for each characteristic you identified. **[2]**

**7.** A manager calculates motivating potential scores (fictional data). Job A: skill variety 6, task identity 5, task significance 4, autonomy 2, feedback 3. Job B: skill variety 3, task identity 3, task significance 3, autonomy 5, feedback 5.

**(a)** Calculate the motivating potential score for each job. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Job B scores higher despite lower variety. **[2]**

**8.** A hotel chain uses the Job Descriptive Index with its staff.

**(a)** Explain **one** advantage of the JDI giving separate facet scores rather than one overall score. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how the hotel could improve the validity of staff responses. **[2]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to find out whether job involvement predicts absence. She asks 80 employees to complete a job-involvement questionnaire and collects their absence records for the following year.

**(a)** Identify the research method. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** strength of using absence records rather than asking employees how often they were absent. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the study cannot show that low involvement causes absence. **[2]**

**10.** Hamid's pay has just been increased, but he still finds his job dull. Using Herzberg's theory, explain why the pay rise has not made him satisfied, and suggest what would. **[3]**

**11.** Compare the JDI and the QWL approach as ways of assessing employees' experience of work. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. Job enrichment adds responsibility and control over the work, such as planning and decision-making (1); job rotation moves workers between different jobs at intervals, adding variety without extra responsibility (1).

**2.** One mark each for any four: adequate and fair compensation (1); safe and healthy working conditions (1); opportunity to use and develop capacities (1); opportunity for growth and security (1); social integration (1); constitutionalism, rights such as privacy and fair treatment (1); work and the total life space, balance with life outside work (1); social relevance of the organisation (1).

**3.** One mark each. Institutionalised stars: high job involvement and high organisational commitment (1). Lone wolves: high involvement, low commitment (1). Corporate citizens: low involvement, high commitment (1). Apathetic employees: low on both (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. People tend to attribute good events to themselves, such as their achievement, and bad events to others or the situation, such as supervision or policy (1), so the critical incident method would produce separate lists of satisfiers and dissatisfiers even if two factors did not really exist (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Aim: to investigate how employees justify sabotage (1). The sample was unionised factory workers (1). They completed a questionnaire rating how far a range of reasons would justify sabotage (1) and how justifiable four methods were: slowdowns, destructiveness, dishonesty and causing chaos (1). Workers who accepted a wider range of reasons were more ready to justify every method except dishonesty (1), which received little justification from anyone (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Individual and situational explanations*: the findings support a situational explanation, since workers justified sabotage as a response to how they were treated; yet some workers accepted far more reasons than others, which points to individual differences in attitudes; both explanations are needed.
- *Self-report*: attitudes about sabotage may not predict actual behaviour; however, asking about justification rather than personal acts may reduce social desirability bias.
- *Generalisation*: one unionised factory; workers in other industries or non-unionised workplaces may differ.
- *Quantitative data*: ratings allow comparison but miss the detail of real grievances.
- *Application*: sabotage can be treated as a warning sign of unresolved grievances.
- *Conclusion*: useful insight into reasons for sabotage, limited by its measure and sample.

**6.**

**(a)** One mark each for any two: skill variety, as the same sorting task is done all day (1); feedback, as they never learn whether deliveries arrive on time (1); autonomy, as they must follow a fixed procedure (1). Credit task identity if justified.

**(b)** One mark each, matched to the characteristics: rotate workers between sorting, loading and checking (variety) (1); display daily on-time delivery figures (feedback) (1); allow teams to decide how to organise sorting (autonomy) (1).

**7.**

**(a)** One mark each. Job A: (6 + 5 + 4) ÷ 3 × 2 × 3 = 5 × 6 = 30 (1). Job B: (3 + 3 + 3) ÷ 3 × 5 × 5 = 3 × 25 = 75 (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. In the MPS formula, autonomy and feedback multiply the score (1), so Job B's high autonomy and feedback outweigh its lower variety, identity and significance, which are only averaged (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Facet scores show which aspect, such as pay or supervision, is causing dissatisfaction (1), so the hotel knows what to improve rather than only knowing staff are unhappy (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Make the questionnaire anonymous and collected by an outside organisation (1), so staff do not fear that managers will identify them and are less likely to give socially desirable answers (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Correlational study (accept questionnaire with records, if correlation is identified) (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Records are objective (1), whereas employees may underreport their absence to appear committed, or forget days off (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. A correlation does not show cause and effect (1); a third variable such as poor health or caring responsibilities could reduce involvement and increase absence (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. Pay is a hygiene factor (1), which prevents dissatisfaction but cannot create satisfaction (1). Motivators such as more responsibility, recognition or more interesting work would increase his satisfaction (1).

**11.** Up to 3 marks. Both use employee ratings to assess the quality of work (1). The JDI focuses on satisfaction with five job facets, such as pay and co-workers (1), whereas QWL takes a broader view, including rights at work, work–life balance and the social relevance of the organisation (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 3, swapping lone wolves and corporate citizens.
- In question 7(a), multiplying all five numbers instead of averaging the first three.
- In question 10, saying pay is a motivator.
