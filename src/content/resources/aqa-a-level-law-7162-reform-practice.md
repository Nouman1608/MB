## Section A

**1.** Which Article of the Convention allows a state to denounce (withdraw from) it? A: Article 15. B: Article 34. C: Article 46. D: Article 58. **[1]**

**2.** Who chaired the Independent Human Rights Act Review that reported in December 2021? A: Lord Bingham. B: Sir Peter Gross. C: Lord Reed. D: Lady Hale. **[1]**

**3.** State what happened to the Bill of Rights Bill introduced in June 2022. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** changes made by Protocol 15 to the Convention. **[2]**

**5.** State what is meant by "entrenchment" and why the Human Rights Act 1998 is not entrenched in the usual sense. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain **two** criticisms of section 3 and section 2 of the Human Rights Act 1998 that have led to calls for reform. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **three** proposals contained in the Bill of Rights Bill 2022. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why replacing the Human Rights Act 1998 with a UK Bill of Rights would not end the influence of the European Court of Human Rights on UK law. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how the devolution settlement and the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement constrain reform of human rights protection. **[5]**

**10.** Explain the arguments for and against adding economic and social rights to UK human rights law. **[4]**

**11.** Explain the findings of the Commission on a Bill of Rights (2012) and the Independent Human Rights Act Review (2021), and what they suggest about the case for replacing the Human Rights Act 1998. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether the United Kingdom should withdraw from the European Convention on Human Rights. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** D: Article 58. **[1]**

**2.** B: Sir Peter Gross. **[1]**

**3.** It would have **repealed and replaced the Human Rights Act 1998** [1], but the government announced in **June 2023 that it would not proceed** [1].

**4.** Any two: it added **subsidiarity** and the **margin of appreciation** to the preamble; it reduced the application time limit from **six months to four**; it removed the parties' right to object to a Chamber relinquishing a case to the Grand Chamber [1] [1].

**5.** Entrenchment means a law is **protected from amendment or repeal by the ordinary legislative process**, for example by needing a special majority [1]. Because of **parliamentary sovereignty**, the HRA can be amended or repealed by an ordinary Act passed by simple majority [1].

**6.** **Section 3**: critics argue it lets judges go beyond interpretation and effectively **rewrite statutes**, as in **Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza (2004)**, where words were read into the Rent Act 1977 [1] [1]. **Section 2**: the **Ullah (2004) mirror principle** encouraged courts to keep pace with Strasbourg, which critics say gives too much weight to a foreign court over Parliament and UK judges [1], illustrated by tension over **Hirst v UK (No 2) (2005)** [1].

**7.** Any three: **repeal the HRA** and replace it; **remove the section 3 interpretive duty**; state that **UK courts need not follow Strasbourg** case law; **disregard Strasbourg interim measures**; add a **permission stage** requiring significant disadvantage; **limit positive obligations**; require **great weight to freedom of speech** [1] [1] [1].

**8.** The UK would remain a **party to the Convention** [1]. Under **Article 46** it must abide by final judgments of the Court in cases against it [1]. Individuals could still **apply to Strasbourg** once domestic remedies were exhausted, and any gap between a Bill of Rights and the Convention would be likely to produce **more adverse judgments** [1].

**9.** The **Scotland Act 1998**, **Government of Wales Act 2006** and **Northern Ireland Act 1998** make devolved legislation that is incompatible with Convention rights **outside competence** [1], so the HRA is woven into the **devolutionary settlement** [1]. Changing it would engage the **Sewel convention**, under which Westminster does not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent [1], though the convention is **not legally enforceable** [1]. The **Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement 1998** commits the UK to incorporating the Convention into Northern Ireland law with access to courts and remedies, so withdrawal or major dilution would raise serious issues there [1].

**10.** **For**: rights such as housing, health care and education matter as much to dignity as civil rights [1], and the UK has accepted them internationally in the **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights 1966** [1]. **Against**: they involve **resource allocation**, which is a matter for elected politicians rather than judges [1], and they could pull courts into **political disputes** about public spending, risking judicial legitimacy [1].

**11.** The **Commission on a Bill of Rights** reported in December 2012 that a **majority favoured a UK Bill of Rights in principle** [1] but there was **no agreement on its content** [1]. The **Independent Human Rights Act Review** reported in December 2021 that the Act was **working broadly well** and recommended only **limited changes** [1]. Together they suggest that support for replacement is **political rather than evidence-led**, and that targeted amendment may answer criticisms without repeal [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **For**: withdrawal would end Strasbourg's power over UK law and restore full **parliamentary sovereignty** over rights [1].
- **For**: disputes such as **Hirst (No 2)** and deportation cases show tension with democratic decisions [1].
- **For**: rights could still be protected by a domestic **Bill of Rights** and common law (e.g. Simms, Derbyshire) [1].
- **Against**: Article 58 allows withdrawal, but it would complicate the **devolution** statutes and the **Sewel convention** [1].
- **Against**: it would raise serious issues under the **Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement** [1].
- **Against**: individuals would lose the **external check** that produced reforms such as the Interception of Communications Act 1985 and Gender Recognition Act 2004 [1].
- **Against**: Protocol 15 and the Brighton process already give more weight to **subsidiarity** and the margin of appreciation, reducing the need for withdrawal [1].
- **Reasoned conclusion**, for example that reform within the Convention addresses the main concerns at lower constitutional cost [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that the Bill of Rights Bill became law.
- Treating replacing the HRA and withdrawing from the Convention as the same thing.
- Describing proposals without weighing them against the criticisms they address.
- Ignoring devolution and the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement.
- Giving no dates for proposals, or presenting them as current law.
