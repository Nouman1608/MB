## The texts

**Text One: "First Strokes"** *(an original memoir extract written for this page)*

> **Paragraph 1.** I learned to swim at thirty-four, in the shallow end of a council pool, beside a
> row of seven-year-olds who were better at it than me. The instructor, a patient woman called Ines,
> never once laughed. The children did, often, and I deserved it.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Fear is not dramatic. It is a small, cold voice that says *not yet*. For three weeks
> I held the rail. For three weeks Ines said, "When you are ready." I was never ready. I let go on a
> Thursday because my arm was tired.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** What happened next took four seconds and has lasted me ten years. The water held me.
> It did not care who I was or how old. I kicked, swallowed half the pool, and reached the far side,
> two metres away, like a woman crossing an ocean.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** I still swim every Thursday. I am still slow. Nobody laughs now, but I sometimes
> wish they would; it would remind me how far I have come.

**Text Two: "Let Them Fall"** *(an original opinion article written for this page)*

> **Paragraph 1.** Walk into any new playground and you will find spongy floors, rounded corners and
> climbing frames lower than a kitchen table. We have designed out the danger. We may also have designed
> out the point.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Children learn about risk the way they learn about language: by trying it, getting
> it wrong and trying again. A child who falls from a low branch learns where her limits are. A child
> who is never allowed near the tree learns only that trees are frightening.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** None of this means ignoring safety. Broken equipment must be mended; dangerous
> roads must be crossed with an adult. But a scraped knee is not a tragedy. It is a lesson that heals.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** So the next time your child stands at the top of the climbing frame, looking down
> and unsure, try something difficult. Say nothing. Wait. Let them decide.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** From paragraph 1 of Text One, give **two** reasons why the writer felt embarrassed at the pool. **[2]**

**2.** In paragraph 2 of Text One, the writer says "Fear is not dramatic." Explain in your own words
what she means. **[2]**

**3.** What can you infer about Ines from Text One? Support your answer with evidence. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why the writer finally let go of the rail, and what this suggests about courage. **[2]**

**5.** From paragraph 1 of Text Two, identify **two** features of modern playgrounds. **[2]**

**6.** In paragraph 2 of Text Two, the writer compares learning about risk to learning language.
Explain what this comparison suggests. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how paragraph 3 of Text Two changes the direction of the argument. **[3]**

**8.** Identify one example of a short sentence in Text One and explain its effect. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**9.** Analyse how the writer of Text One uses language and structure to present her experience of
learning to swim. Support your views with detailed reference to the text. **[12]**

**10.** Text Two ends with direct address to the reader.
(a) Identify **two** words or phrases in paragraph 4 that address the reader directly. **[2]**
(b) Explain how the ending tries to change the reader's behaviour. Refer to language and structure. **[4]**
Total for Question 10: **[6]**

**11.** Compare how the writers of Text One and Text Two present ideas about fear and learning.
In your answer, you should:
- compare the ideas and perspectives of the writers
- explain how the writers present these ideas and perspectives
- support your answer with detailed references to both texts. **[22]**

**12.** Plan, in note form, a comparison of the **endings** of the two texts. Give one similarity and
two differences, each with a short quotation from each text. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Any two: she was much older than the other learners (thirty-four beside seven-year-olds) (1);
the children were better swimmers than her (1); the children laughed at her (1). Maximum 2.

**2.** Fear is quiet and ordinary rather than loud or exciting (1); it shows itself as constant
hesitation or delay, telling her to wait ("not yet") (1).

**3.** Ines is patient (1), shown by the fact that she "never once laughed" or kept repeating "When
you are ready" for three weeks (1); she respects the learner's own pace and does not force her (1).
Accept other supported inferences, such as kind or encouraging. Maximum 3.

**4.** She let go because her arm was tired, not because she felt ready (1). This suggests courage
can come from an ordinary, even accidental, moment rather than a heroic decision (1).

**5.** Any two: spongy floors (1); rounded corners (1); climbing frames that are low (lower than a
kitchen table) (1). Maximum 2.

**6.** Learning about risk is natural and gradual (1), and mistakes are a necessary part of it, just
as getting words wrong is part of learning to speak (1).

**7.** It introduces a counter-argument or concession ("None of this means ignoring safety") (1). The
writer accepts some safety measures, such as mending equipment (1), which makes her main argument seem
balanced and reasonable before she returns to it with "a scraped knee is not a tragedy" (1).

**8.** One mark for a valid example, such as "I was never ready." / "The water held me." / "I am
still slow." (1). One mark for effect, for example: it isolates a key moment or admission, giving it
emphasis or a tone of honesty (1).

### Section B

**9.** Levels-marked on AO2. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not Pearson's
published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 10–12 | Perceptive analysis of both language and structure; precise, well-chosen references; effects linked to purpose |
| 7–9 | Clear explanation of a range of methods with relevant references |
| 4–6 | Some comment on methods; effects sometimes general |
| 1–3 | Mainly description or feature-spotting |

Indicative content:
- Self-deprecating humour in paragraph 1 ("better at it than me", "I deserved it") builds trust and
  makes the narrator likeable.
- Personification of fear as "a small, cold voice" makes an abstract feeling physical and familiar.
- Repetition of "For three weeks" slows the pace and conveys how long the hesitation lasted.
- The anticlimax "because my arm was tired" undercuts any expectation of a heroic moment.
- Contrast between "four seconds" and "ten years" shows the lasting effect of a brief experience.
- Hyperbole in the simile "like a woman crossing an ocean" against the plain fact "two metres away"
  creates humour and pride at once.
- Structure: chronological movement from embarrassment to fear to breakthrough, then a present-tense
  final paragraph that reflects on change; the return to laughter links ending to opening.

**10.** (a) Any two: "your child" (1); "try something difficult" (1); imperatives "Say nothing" or
"Wait" or "Let them decide" (1). Maximum 2.
(b) Up to 4 marks: the second-person address places the reader in the scene, so the argument becomes
personal (1); the image of the child "looking down and unsure" creates sympathy and tension (1); the
sequence of short imperatives slows the pace and models the waiting the writer wants (1); the final
words hand the decision to the child, so the structure enacts the argument that children should judge
risk themselves (1).

**11.** Levels-marked on AO3. Illustrative bands (Marlbridge guidance):

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 18–22 | Perceptive, sustained comparison of ideas, perspectives and methods throughout; discriminating references to both texts |
| 13–17 | Clear, developed comparison with relevant references to both texts |
| 8–12 | Some valid comparison; may be uneven between texts or ideas and methods |
| 4–7 | Basic links, mainly of content |
| 1–3 | Little comparison; texts treated separately |

Indicative content:
- Both writers present fear as something overcome through experience rather than instruction.
- Perspective: Text One is a participant's retrospective memoir; Text Two is an adult advising other
  adults about children, an observer's argument.
- Text One presents fear from the inside (personification, "not yet"); Text Two presents it from
  outside, as something adults pass on ("trees are frightening").
- Both value patience: Ines's "When you are ready" parallels Text Two's "Say nothing. Wait."
- Methods: anecdote and humour in Text One; analogy, concession and direct address in Text Two.
- Purpose: Text One reflects and entertains; Text Two persuades parents to change behaviour.
- Both end with short sentences, but Text One's ending looks back with gratitude while Text Two's
  looks forward with an instruction.

**12.** Three points, each worth 2 marks: 1 for a valid comparative point and 1 for apt quotations
from both texts. Example:
- Similarity: both use short, final sentences for emphasis, "I am still slow" and "Let them decide" (2).
- Difference: Text One reflects on the past ("how far I have come"), whereas Text Two instructs the
  reader about the future ("the next time your child stands") (2).
- Difference: Text One's tone is personal and wistful ("I sometimes wish they would"), whereas Text
  Two's is persuasive and commanding ("try something difficult") (2).
Total 6.
