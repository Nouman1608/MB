This guide covers section 3.3, Workers, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. The section has four parts: 3.3.1 factors affecting an individual's choice of occupation, 3.3.2 wage determination, 3.3.3 reasons for differences in earnings and 3.3.4 division of labour and specialisation. Part 3.3.3 also asks for the "definition, drawing and interpretation of diagrams that illustrate the effects of changes in demand and supply in the labour market". All people, firms and figures below are invented.

## 3.3.1 Choosing an occupation

**Wage factors** are the money rewards: basic pay, overtime, bonuses and commission.

**Non-wage factors** are everything else that makes a job more or less attractive:

- working hours and holidays;
- job security;
- promotion prospects and training;
- working conditions, including safety and location;
- fringe benefits, such as a pension, health insurance, a company car or subsidised meals;
- job satisfaction and the status of the work;
- the cost of training needed to enter the job.

**Worked example 1 (invented).** Leila, a qualified accountant, compares two offers.

| | Firm A (city) | Firm B (home town) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly salary | 4,200 | 3,600 |
| Monthly travel cost | 450 | 50 |
| Employer pension contribution | 0 | 216 (6% of salary) |
| Hours a week | 48 | 38 |
| Promotion prospects | Strong | Limited |

```
Firm A: 4,200 - 450       = 3,750 a month after travel
Firm B: 3,600 - 50 + 216  = 3,766 a month after travel, with pension
Pay per hour (A) = 4,200 / (48 x 52 / 12) = 4,200 / 208 = 20.19
Pay per hour (B) = 3,600 / (38 x 52 / 12) = 3,600 / 164.67 = 21.86
```

Firm A's salary is 16.7% higher, yet after travel and pension the packages are almost equal, and Firm B pays more per hour worked. Leila might still choose Firm A for its promotion prospects if she expects a much higher salary in future. The judgement depends on what she values, which is why exam answers should weigh both kinds of factor rather than assume the highest wage wins.

## 3.3.2 Wage determination

### Demand for and supply of labour

In a free labour market the wage is set where the **demand for labour** (by employers) equals the **supply of labour** (by workers).

**Demand for labour is a derived demand:** firms want workers because consumers want the goods or services they make. Demand for labour shifts when:

- demand for the product changes;
- workers' productivity changes (more productive workers are worth more to a firm);
- the price of capital that could replace workers changes.

**Supply of labour** to an occupation shifts when:

- the training or qualifications needed change;
- the non-wage attractions of the job change;
- the size of the working population changes, for example through migration;
- wages in other occupations change, drawing workers away.

### Describing a labour-market diagram in words

When asked to draw, label the vertical axis **wage rate** and the horizontal axis **quantity of labour** (or number of workers). Draw a downward-sloping demand curve, D, and an upward-sloping supply curve, S. Mark the equilibrium wage W and quantity Q where they cross. For a change, draw the new curve (D1 or S1), mark the new wage W1 and quantity Q1, and add arrows showing the direction of change.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** Online shopping grows, raising demand for delivery drivers.

| Wage per hour | Drivers demanded (before) | Drivers demanded (after) | Drivers supplied |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | 5,000 | 6,200 | 2,600 |
| 10 | 4,400 | 5,600 | 3,200 |
| 11 | 3,800 | 5,000 | 3,800 |
| 12 | 3,200 | 4,400 | 4,400 |
| 13 | 2,600 | 3,800 | 5,000 |

- Before: equilibrium at 11 an hour with 3,800 drivers.
- After: demand shifts right by 1,200 at every wage. Equilibrium becomes 12 an hour with 4,400 drivers.
- The wage rises by 1/11 x 100 = 9.1% and employment by 600 / 3,800 x 100 = 15.8%.

At the old wage of 11 there would now be a shortage of 5,000 - 3,800 = 1,200 drivers, so firms raise the wage to attract more.

### Relative bargaining power

Where workers bargain together, or have scarce skills, the wage can be pushed above the level supply and demand alone would set. Where one large employer faces many unorganised workers, the wage may be pushed lower.

### Government policy

A **national minimum wage** affects regions differently. Suppose (invented) the market wage for shop workers is 7.50 in Region North and 10.00 in Region South, and the government sets a minimum of 9.00.

- In North, the minimum is above the market wage: pay rises by 1.50 an hour (20%), but some firms may employ fewer workers.
- In South, the minimum is below the market wage, so it has no effect.

Governments also influence wages as the largest employer in many countries (setting public-sector pay), through training schemes that raise the supply of skilled labour, and through laws on equal pay.

## 3.3.3 Reasons for differences in earnings

The syllabus names four causes: changes in demand and supply, relative bargaining strengths, discrimination and government policy. Match the strongest causes to each comparison.

| Comparison | Main causes | Diagram description |
|---|---|---|
| Skilled versus unskilled | Supply of skilled labour is small (long training); demand high because output is valuable; skilled workers bargain more strongly | Skilled market: S further left and D further right, so a higher wage |
| Primary, secondary and tertiary | Productivity and the value of output per worker differ; many workers seek low-skilled farm work in some economies | Farm labour market: S far to the right, so a low wage |
| Male versus female | Discrimination reduces demand for women's labour; differences in occupations and hours; equal pay laws narrow gaps | Discrimination: D for women's labour lower than productivity justifies |
| Private versus public sector | Public pay set by government within budgets; private pay follows profit and market forces | Public pay can lag behind when the government limits spending |

**Worked example 3 (invented).** An electrician earns 22 an hour for 40 hours; a cleaner earns 9 an hour for 40 hours.

```
Weekly pay: electrician 880, cleaner 360
Gap = 880 - 360 = 520 a week
Electrician earns 880 / 360 = 2.44 times as much
```

Explanation in a chain: electricians need years of training, so few can supply this labour; every firm and household needs wiring work done safely, so demand is high; the high demand and low supply set a high wage. Many people can do cleaning with little training, so supply is large and the wage is low.

## 3.3.4 Division of labour and specialisation

**Specialisation** is concentrating on a particular product or task. **Division of labour** is the form of specialisation in which production is split into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.

**Worked example 4 (invented).** A sandwich bar has four staff. Working alone, each makes every part of a sandwich and finishes 20 an hour: 80 in total. When the work is divided (bread and spreads, fillings, wrapping, serving), the four make 128 an hour.

```
Output per worker before = 80 / 4 = 20; after = 128 / 4 = 32
Productivity rise = (32 - 20) / 20 x 100 = 60%
If each worker earns 12 an hour, labour cost per sandwich:
before 48 / 80 = 0.60; after 48 / 128 = 0.375
```

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Quickly become skilled at one task; may earn more as output rises | Repetition and boredom; narrow skills make it harder to change jobs |
| Firms | Higher productivity; lower cost per unit; workers need less training | If one stage stops, the whole process stops; bored staff may make errors |
| Economy | Higher output and living standards; more goods to trade | Workers with narrow skills face unemployment if their industry declines |

## Common mistakes

- Saying demand for labour depends on what workers want. It is derived from demand for the product.
- Shifting the curve when only the wage changes. A change in the wage is a movement along the curves.
- Claiming an earnings gap always proves discrimination.
- Giving only wage factors when a question asks why someone chose a lower-paid job.
- Treating specialisation and division of labour as unrelated ideas.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** name the cause, the curve it shifts, and the effect on wage and employment.
- **Analyse:** use a full chain with a diagram if the question allows one.
- **Discuss:** weigh, for example, gains for employed workers from a minimum wage against possible job losses, and end with a judgement.
