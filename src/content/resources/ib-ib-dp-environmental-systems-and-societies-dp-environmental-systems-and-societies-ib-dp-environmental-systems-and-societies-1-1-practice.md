## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between instrumental value and intrinsic value. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by an environmental value system. **[2]**

**3.** Using a systems approach, outline **three** inputs that could shape a person's environmental
value system. **[3]**

**4.** A coastal town debates how to respond to beach erosion. Three residents comment:

- Resident A: "Engineers can build an offshore reef from recycled materials. Problem solved, and the
  surfers will love it."
- Resident B: "Let the dunes move naturally. The shoreline belongs to the sea and its wildlife as
  much as to us."
- Resident C: "We need a coastal plan, with building banned in the highest-risk zone and a levy on
  beachfront hotels to fund dune planting."

**(a)** Identify the environmental value system of each resident. **[3]**
**(b)** Justify your answer for Resident C. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between a deep ecologist and a self-reliance soft ecologist. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the same person might hold a technocentric view on one environmental issue and an
ecocentric view on another. **[3]**

**7.** Outline how **one** named historical event changed perspectives on the environment. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a market-based economic system might shape a government's response to air
pollution. **[3]**

**9.** A logging company and a forest-dwelling community disagree about a forest. Suggest **two**
reasons why their perspectives differ. **[2]**

**10.** Outline how the recognition of a river as a legal person could change decisions about that
river. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how the media can change environmental perspectives. **[4]**

**12.** A government plans a large solar-powered desalination plant to supply a growing desert city.
Discuss how **three** different environmental value systems might respond to the plan. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Instrumental value is the value of something as a means to human ends, such as timber or
tourism income [1]; intrinsic value is value in itself, regardless of its usefulness to people [1].
**[2]**

**2.** A worldview or set of values [1] that shapes how a person or group perceives and evaluates
environmental issues and so influences their decisions [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any three, each briefly outlined: upbringing and family [1]; religion or culture [1];
education [1]; media [1]; economic circumstances such as income or job [1]; direct experience of an
environmental event [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Resident A: technocentric [1]. Resident B: ecocentric [1]. Resident C: anthropocentric
(environmental manager) [1].
**(b)** Resident C accepts limits and uses regulation (a building ban) [1] and an economic tool (a
levy) to manage the coast for long-term human benefit [1]. **[5]**
*Marking note:* "B is ecocentric because they like nature" is too vague; quote "belongs to the sea
and its wildlife", which shows intrinsic value.

**5.** A deep ecologist gives nature equal or greater value than humans and may favour radical
change to protect it [1]; a self-reliance soft ecologist favours small, local communities that meet
their own needs within local limits [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A person's values are applied to each issue in context [1]. They may trust technology where a
proven technical fix exists, such as recycling technology for waste [1], but see an irreplaceable
ecosystem, such as an ancient forest, as having intrinsic value that technology cannot replace [1].
**[3]**

**7.** A named event with its date or place [1] and the change in perspective it caused [1]. For
example, the Bhopal gas disaster (1984) led to wider debate about corporate responsibility and
industrial safety. **[2]**

**8.** A market-based system favours price signals over direct control [1], so the government might
use a tax on emissions or tradable pollution permits [1], which leave firms free to choose the
cheapest way to cut pollution [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any two: the company values the forest instrumentally for profit, while the community may value
it for food, culture and identity [1]; their economic dependence on the forest differs [1]; they
have different cultural or spiritual relationships with the land [1]; they experience the costs and
benefits of logging differently [1]. **[2]**

**10.** The river's interests could be represented in court by appointed guardians [1], so projects
that harm it, such as pollution or abstraction, could be challenged on the river's behalf [1].
**[2]**

**11.** The media spread information about events and evidence to a wide audience [1], for example
images of plastic in the ocean [1]. Repeated coverage can shift public opinion and put pressure on
governments and companies [1]. Social media can spread campaigns quickly, as with youth climate
strikes, but can also spread misinformation that hardens opposing views [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Up to 3 marks per value system: the EVS named [1], its likely response [1] and a reason
linked to its beliefs [1].
- **Technocentric:** strongly supportive [1]; technology removes the water limit and solar power
  avoids fossil-fuel emissions [1]; growth can continue [1].
- **Anthropocentric (environmental manager):** conditional support [1]; requires an environmental
  impact assessment, controls on brine discharge and water pricing to reduce waste [1]; manages the
  resource for long-term human benefit [1].
- **Ecocentric:** opposed or sceptical [1]; brine harms marine life and the plan encourages a city to
  grow beyond its local limits [1]; prefers reducing demand and living within local water supply
  [1]. **[9]**
*Marking note:* a top answer ends with a brief judgement, such as which view is likely to dominate
and why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming an EVS without quoting or paraphrasing evidence.
- Using "environmentalist" instead of a precise position on the spectrum.
- Writing about an event without stating the change in perspective.
- In discuss questions, describing each view without a reason linked to its beliefs.
