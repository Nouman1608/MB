## Questions

**1.** Define the terms risk and reward. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a quantifiable risk and an unquantifiable risk. Give one example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Brindlow Bakery is deciding whether to start a home-delivery service. It estimates the net gain over two years as £90,000 with probability 0.25, £30,000 with probability 0.5 and a loss of £40,000 with probability 0.25.

**(a)** Calculate the expected value of the delivery service. **[3]**
**(b)** State one reason why the expected value may not be a reliable guide to the decision. **[1]**

**4.** Holmsley Tools has £50,000 to spend. A marketing campaign would add £7,000 a year, a new machine attachment £6,000 a year, staff training £5,500 a year and a bank deposit £1,500 a year. Holmsley chooses the marketing campaign. Identify the opportunity cost of this decision and explain your answer. **[3]**

**5.** Wolds Dairy cannot tell whether a new organic yoghurt range will sell, nor how a hot summer will affect the supply of milk. Explain what is meant by uncertainty and distinguish between an internal and an external cause of uncertainty, using the case. **[4]**

**6.** Pyecombe Brewing earns a contribution of £1.40 per litre and has fixed costs of £150,000 a year. Its forecast sales are between 90,000 and 150,000 litres, with 120,000 as the central figure, because a supermarket has not yet decided whether to stock its beer. Analyse how this uncertainty affects the brewery's forecasting and planning. **[6]**

**7.** Brindlow Bakery is considering a second bakery in a nearby town, at a cost it could not easily recover. Evaluate two ways in which it could reduce the risk of this decision. **[7]**

**8.** Wolds Dairy has £120,000 to invest. Robotic milking would save £30,000 a year. A cheese-making line would add £27,000 a year in profit. A bank deposit would pay 2.5% a year. The owners choose robotic milking. Analyse the impact of opportunity cost on this decision. **[6]**

**9.** Hamid Ordish is leaving a job paying £42,000 a year to open a cafe, using £35,000 of savings and a £20,000 bank loan. Evaluate the specific risks Hamid faces as an entrepreneur. **[9]**

**10.** Holmsley Tools buys all its steel from one supplier and has no back-up plan. The supplier fails and production stops for three weeks. Weekly sales lost are £15,000. Last year's profit was £60,000. Evaluate the consequences of Holmsley's poor risk management for the business and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Risk is the possibility that a decision ends worse than hoped, such as a loss [1]. Reward is the benefit if the decision works, such as profit or a return on money invested [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Definitions earn credit when they refer to decisions and outcomes; a bare example with no definition usually does not.

**2.** A quantifiable risk is one where the likelihood or size of loss can be given a number from data or research [1], such as the share of deliveries damaged in transit, from past records [1]. An unquantifiable risk has no reliable basis for a number [1], such as a sudden change in customer attitudes to sugar [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* To distinguish, compare the two directly; two stand-alone definitions may miss a mark, so say what differs (whether a number can be defended).

**3. (a)** Weighted outcomes: 0.25 × 90,000 = 22,500 [1]; 0.5 × 30,000 = 15,000 and 0.25 × -40,000 = -10,000 [1]. Sum: 22,500 + 15,000 - 10,000 = **£27,500** [1].
**(b)** The probabilities are estimates, or the average hides the possible £40,000 loss [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each weighted outcome; a bare total leaves nothing to credit if one figure slips, and a negative outcome must keep its minus sign.

**4.** Identify the options ranked by value: campaign £7,000, attachment £6,000, training £5,500, deposit £1,500 [1]. The opportunity cost is the second-best use forgone, the machine attachment [1], worth **£6,000 a year** [1]. It is not the total of all rejected options, because the money can be spent only once. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that add up every rejected option, or name the chosen option, are the typical way marks are lost here; name the single next best use and its value.

**5.** Uncertainty means the business cannot know in advance what will happen, or cannot give reliable probabilities [1]. Internal cause: whether the new organic range sells, which depends on the business's own product and decisions [1]. External cause: the hot summer, which is outside its control [1] and affects milk supply [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the case for each example and say why it is internal or external; labels alone are thin.

**6.** Break-even = 150,000 ÷ 1.4 = about 107,143 litres [1]. Profit at 90,000 = 126,000 - 150,000 = -£24,000; at 120,000 = £18,000; at 150,000 = £60,000 [1]. Forecasting: one figure hides a swing from loss to a £60,000 profit, so the brewery should use a range and scenarios [1]. The central forecast is only 12,857 litres (10.7%) above break-even, so a small shortfall causes a loss [1]. Planning: it should keep a cash reserve for the low case and delay costly commitments until the supermarket decides [1], and plan production flexibly, for example by using short-term contracts [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculations earn most when they are linked to a point about forecasting or planning; numbers left unexplained rarely complete the analysis.

**7.** Method 1, pilot: open a market stall in the town first [1]. It limits the loss if demand is weak [1], but it delays entry and may give a rival time to move [1]. Method 2, market research on shopping habits and rents [1]. It reduces the chance of a poor location [1], but costs money and customers' answers may not match what they later do [1]. Judgement: a pilot suits a decision that cannot easily be reversed, because it tests demand directly and limits the loss [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation marks need a limitation tied to this decision, and a final judgement that chooses; describing methods without weighing them stays in the lower marks.

**8.** The opportunity cost is the next best alternative, the cheese line, worth £27,000 a year [1]. The deposit earns 120,000 × 0.025 = £3,000, so it ranks third [1]. Robotic milking beats the cheese line by £3,000 a year, or £15,000 over five years [1]. Opportunity cost forces the owners to compare options, not judge milking alone [1]. However, the difference is small, and the figures are estimates, so a cheese line might spread the dairy's income away from milk [1]. Judgement: robotic milking is justified on the numbers, but only narrowly, so the owners should check the forecasts and the risk of each option [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers state the forgone option and its value before commenting; adding the deposit to the cheese line is a common slip.

**9.** Financial: £35,000 of savings plus a £20,000 loan are at risk, and a failed cafe could leave debts [1], and if liability is unlimited personal assets are also exposed [1]. Income: profit is not guaranteed, so Hamid may earn well below the £42,000 salary given up [1], which is also the opportunity cost of the venture [1]. Failure: strong competition, weak demand or poor cash flow could close the cafe, with effects on family and reputation [1], [1]. Against these: if Hamid has experience, a good location and savings to cover slow months, the chance of failure is lower [1], and the reward of independence and a business that can be sold may justify the risk [1]. Judgement: the risks are large because most of the capital is personal, so the venture suits Hamid only if the household can survive without the salary for several months [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Risks should be specific to this entrepreneur and weighed against the reward; a general list of risks of starting a business gains little.

**10.** Lost sales are 3 × 15,000 = £45,000 [1], which is 75% of last year's profit [1]. Business: a severe fall in profit, a possible cash-flow problem and damaged customer relationships [1], [1]. Stakeholders: employees may lose hours or jobs [1]; customers may switch to rivals [1]; lenders may worry about repayments [1]. Limits: the loss might be temporary if the supplier is replaced quickly, and insurance might cover part of it [1]. Judgement: the consequences are serious because a single point of failure turned a supplier problem into a loss of three-quarters of a year's profit, so contingency planning and a second supplier were proportionate [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given and judge their scale against profit; naming stakeholders without saying how each is affected is a common way to stay below the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Equating opportunity cost with the sum spent, or with every option rejected.
- Leaving a minus sign out of a loss when calculating expected value.
- Giving an expected value with no comment on probabilities or the worst case.
- Using general points on uncertainty with no link to the case.
- Mixing up internal and external causes.
- Listing ways to reduce risk without judging which suits the decision.
- Describing stakeholder effects without saying which group and how.
- Closing an evaluation with a recap that never commits to a conclusion.
