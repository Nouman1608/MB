## The sources

**Internal sources** come from within the business.

- **Retained profit:** profit kept in the business rather than paid out. It is cheap and keeps control, but it takes time to build up and is not available to a new or loss-making business.
- **Sale of assets:** selling unused equipment or property raises cash quickly but may reduce capacity. **Sale and leaseback** raises cash from a property that the business then rents.
- **Better use of working capital:** collecting receivables faster or holding less inventory frees cash but may upset customers or cause stock-outs.
- **Owner's capital:** for sole traders and partners, personal savings.

**External sources** come from outside.

- **Overdraft:** flexible borrowing from a bank up to an agreed limit. It is quick and interest is paid only on the amount used, but the rate is high and the bank can withdraw it.
- **Bank loan:** a fixed sum repaid with interest over a set time. It gives certainty, but interest must be paid whatever the profit, and the bank may want security.
- **Trade credit:** delaying payment to suppliers. It is free if terms are kept, but late payment can damage relationships.
- **Factoring and invoice discounting:** a finance company advances cash against unpaid invoices for a fee.
- **Leasing:** renting an asset rather than buying. It spreads cost and avoids a large outlay, but the business does not own the asset.
- **Share issue:** selling new shares to investors. It brings in money with no repayment or interest, but dilutes ownership and may lead to dividends.
- **Venture capital and business angels:** investors who buy a share in a growing business, often bringing expertise but wanting a say and an exit.
- **Debentures:** long-term loans, usually of a company, at a fixed rate.
- **Crowdfunding:** many small investors or lenders contribute through an online platform.
- **Government grants:** money for particular aims, usually not repayable but conditional.

## Two ways to classify

| | Short-term (up to about a year) | Long-term (over about a year) |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | Reducing inventory; collecting receivables faster | Retained profit; sale of non-current assets; owner's savings |
| **External** | Overdraft; trade credit; factoring | Bank loan; share issue; debentures; leasing; venture capital; grants |

Short-term finance suits short-term needs such as paying suppliers before customers pay. Long-term finance suits assets that last for years.

## Factors affecting the choice

**Time.** The finance should match how long the money is needed (the matching principle). Using a short-term overdraft to buy a machine that lasts ten years risks the bank withdrawing support before the machine has paid for itself. Speed also matters: an overdraft is available at once, whereas a share issue takes months.

**Legal structure.** A sole trader or partnership cannot issue shares, so relies on savings, retained profit and loans. A private limited company can sell shares but only to chosen investors; a public limited company can sell to the public on a stock exchange. Limited liability makes lenders and investors more willing to lend to a company, because the owners' personal assets are not at stake, though lenders may ask for personal guarantees from small company owners.

**Quantitative factors.** These are the numbers: the amount needed, the interest rate, the total cost, the business's existing gearing, its ability to meet repayments from cash flow and the value of assets available as security.

**Qualitative factors.** These are matters that cannot easily be measured: the owners' wish to keep control, their attitude to risk, the relationship with the bank, the reputation of the investors and the flexibility of terms.

**External influences.** Interest rates, the state of the economy, the availability of credit from banks, government policy and the stock market all affect the cost and availability of finance. When interest rates are high, loans are expensive; in a recession, banks lend less.

## Worked example 1: loan or shares?

Kingsley Components needs £400,000 for a new production line. It has 500,000 shares in issue, existing non-current liabilities of £400,000 and capital employed of £1,600,000. Its operating profit is £250,000 and existing interest is £20,000. Corporation tax is 20%. The new line is expected to add £70,000 to operating profit.

**Option A: a five-year loan at 7%.** Extra interest = 7% × £400,000 = £28,000.
**Option B: issue 100,000 new shares at £4 each**, raising £400,000.

| | No investment | A: loan | B: shares |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating profit | £250,000 | £320,000 | £320,000 |
| Interest | £20,000 | £48,000 | £20,000 |
| Profit before tax | £230,000 | £272,000 | £300,000 |
| Tax at 20% | £46,000 | £54,400 | £60,000 |
| Profit after tax | £184,000 | £217,600 | £240,000 |
| Shares in issue | 500,000 | 500,000 | 600,000 |
| Earnings per share | 36.8p | 43.5p | 40.0p |
| Gearing (non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed) | 25.0% | 40.0% | 20.0% |
| Existing owners' share of the company | 100% | 100% | 83.3% |

Working for A: 320,000 − 48,000 = 272,000; tax = 54,400; profit = 217,600; earnings per share = 217,600 ÷ 500,000 = 43.52p. Gearing: (400,000 + 400,000) ÷ (1,600,000 + 400,000) = 40%.

**Evaluation.** The loan gives the higher earnings per share (43.5p against 40.0p) and keeps all control with existing owners. But it raises gearing from 25% to 40%, adds £28,000 of fixed interest each year, and the repayment has to be found whatever the profit. The share issue leaves gearing at 20% and has no repayment, but the existing owners hold 500,000 of 600,000 shares (83.3%) and the dividend must be shared with more people. Judgement: if the line's profit is reliable and the owners want to keep control, the loan is better; if demand is uncertain or the bank would set strict limits, shares are safer. The choice depends on risk, because with a loan, a profit shortfall still leaves the interest to be paid.

## Worked example 2: short-term finance

Dalby Joinery is owed £90,000 by customers who take 60 days to pay, and it needs cash now to buy timber.

- **Factoring:** the factor advances 80% of the invoices (£72,000) and charges a fee of 2% of the invoice value (£1,800).
- **Overdraft:** Dalby could borrow £72,000 at 10% a year for the same 60 days. Interest = £72,000 × 10% × 60 ÷ 365 = **£1,184**.

On cost alone the overdraft is cheaper (£1,184 against £1,800). But factoring also takes over the work of collecting payments, may cover bad debts (depending on the terms) and is not limited by the bank's credit limit. The overdraft is repayable on demand and the bank may refuse to extend it. Judgement: for a one-off need, the overdraft; for a business with a regular gap between paying suppliers and being paid by customers, factoring may save staff time worth more than the £616 difference.

## Evaluating sources for stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Concern |
|---|---|
| Owners | Control, risk and return |
| Lenders | Security and ability to repay |
| Employees | Job security; a business over-borrowed may cut staff |
| Suppliers | Prompt payment; late payment is costly |
| Government | Tax receipts, jobs |

A strong evaluation links the source to the case: its **cost**, **risk**, **control**, **speed** and **fit with the purpose and time**. Then it judges which source is most suitable and what would change the answer.

## Common mistakes

- Listing sources without saying why one is suitable for the case.
- Calling an overdraft long-term finance.
- Forgetting that a sole trader cannot issue shares.
- Saying a share issue is free: it costs ownership and dividends.
- Comparing interest costs without considering risk and control.

## Exam technique

"Distinguish" needs both terms and the difference. "Explain how factors affect the choice" needs the factor, the effect and an example. "Evaluate" needs costs and benefits of each source, links to the case and a judgement.
