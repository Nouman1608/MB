## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between primary and secondary succession, giving an example of a starting surface
for each. **[2]**

**2.** Outline **two** reasons why distinct zones of species form along an environmental gradient.
**[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between a line transect and a belt transect. **[2]**

**4.** A transect was surveyed up a mountainside.

| Altitude (m) | 200 | 600 | 1000 | 1400 | 1800 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tree cover (%) | 85 | 70 | 40 | 10 | 0 |
| Mean summer temperature (°C) | 16.0 | 13.4 | 10.8 | 8.2 | 5.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of temperature decrease per 100 m. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the change in tree cover with altitude. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the change in tree cover. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how marram grass changes conditions on a yellow dune so that other species can
establish. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why soil depth and organic matter increase during primary succession. **[3]**

**7.** Outline the stages of secondary succession on an abandoned field in a temperate climate.
**[3]**

**8.** Plots in a pasture were grazed by sheep at different densities.

| Sheep per hectare | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant species per plot | 11 | 19 | 23 | 14 | 7 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in species from 0 to 1 sheep per hectare. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the pattern shown by the data. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why a climax community should not be thought of as a fixed end point. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how heather moorland can be maintained as a plagioclimax. **[2]**

**11.** Suggest **three** abiotic factors to measure along a sand-dune transect, with the equipment
for each. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the claim that human disturbance always reduces the biodiversity of ecosystems.
**[7]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary succession begins on a surface with no soil, such as new volcanic rock or rock exposed
by a retreating glacier [1]; secondary succession begins where soil remains after the community has
been removed, such as after a fire or on abandoned farmland [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Each species can survive only within its range of tolerance, so it occupies the part of the
gradient where conditions suit it [1]; where tolerance ranges overlap, competition determines which
species dominates [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A line transect records only species touching the line at intervals [1]; a belt transect uses
quadrats placed along the line, recording abundance in a strip, so it gives more complete data [1].
**[2]**

**4. (a)** (16.0 − 5.6) / 1600 m = 0.0065 °C per m = **0.65 °C per 100 m** [1]
**(b)** Tree cover falls with altitude [1], from 85% at 200 m to 0% at 1,800 m, with the steepest
fall between 600 m (70%) and 1,400 m (10%) [1].
**(c)** Temperature falls with height, shortening the growing season [1]; above about 1,400 m it is
too cold and exposed for trees to establish, so they are replaced by low-growing vegetation [1].
**[5]**

**5.** Marram grows up through burying sand and its long roots and rhizomes bind the dune, reducing
movement [1]. As it dies and decays it adds organic matter, which increases nutrient content and
water retention [1]. Its cover provides shelter from wind and salt spray, so less tolerant species
can establish [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Pioneers such as lichens weather rock, releasing mineral particles [1]. Dead organisms and
litter add organic matter, forming humus [1]. As plant cover increases, more litter is produced and
roots and soil organisms mix it in, so soil deepens and retains more water and nutrients [1].
**[3]**

**7.** Annual weeds colonise quickly from seed [1]; perennial grasses and herbs then dominate [1];
shrubs and fast-growing trees establish, followed by slower-growing trees towards a woodland climax
[1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** (23 − 11) / 11 × 100 = **109%** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(b)** With no grazing, a few tall, competitive species dominate and exclude smaller ones [1].
Moderate grazing reduces the dominant species and opens space, so more species coexist, peaking at
1 sheep per hectare [1]. Heavy grazing damages most plants, so only a few grazing-tolerant species
survive; the pattern supports the intermediate disturbance hypothesis [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The climax depends on climate and soil, which can change [1]. Disturbances such as fire,
storms or disease periodically reset parts of the community, producing a mosaic of stages [1].
Climate change or human impact can push the ecosystem past a tipping point into a different stable
community [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Regular burning or grazing removes young trees and shrubs [1], preventing succession to
woodland and keeping heather dominant; if management stops, succession resumes [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Any three, factor with equipment [1] each: soil pH with a pH probe or indicator kit; soil
moisture with a moisture meter or by drying and weighing samples; organic matter by burning dried
samples and reweighing; wind speed with an anemometer; light intensity with a light meter; soil
temperature with a thermometer or probe. **[3]**

**12.** Award up to 7: arguments supporting the claim, up to 3; arguments against, up to 3; a
judgement [1].
- *Supporting:* clearing, ploughing and urbanisation remove habitats entirely [1]; heavy grazing or
  pollution leaves only a few tolerant species [1]; fragmentation reduces population sizes and
  blocks movement, increasing local extinction [1]; disturbance can let invasive species establish
  [1].
- *Against:* moderate disturbance can raise diversity, as the intermediate disturbance hypothesis
  predicts [1]; managed plagioclimaxes such as hay meadows or heathland support distinctive,
  species-rich communities [1]; coppicing and rotational burning create a mosaic of stages with more
  niches [1].
- *Judgement:* for example, the claim is too absolute; the effect depends on the type, intensity and
  frequency of disturbance, and moderate, traditional management can increase diversity while
  intensive or permanent change reduces it [1]. **[7]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing transect data without quoting values at named distances or heights.
- Saying pioneers "prepare the ground" without explaining how.
- Treating all disturbance as harmful.
- Forgetting competition when explaining zonation.
