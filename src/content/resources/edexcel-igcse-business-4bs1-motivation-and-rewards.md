## Why motivation matters: the three named reasons as chains

The specification names three reasons. Each becomes a mark-earning chain only when you carry it through to a business result.

- **Attracting employees.** A business known for good pay, benefits and interesting work receives more applications, so it can choose stronger candidates, so it spends less on training and gets better work from the start.
- **Retaining employees.** Motivated staff are less likely to leave, so the business avoids the cost of recruiting, selecting and inducting replacements and keeps experienced people who know its customers.
- **Productivity.** Motivated staff put in more effort and care, so output per worker rises, so the cost of each unit falls and the business can cut prices or earn more profit.

In Paper 1 (a small business of up to 49 employees), losing one experienced person can be a large share of the workforce. In Paper 2 (more than 250 employees), small changes in staff turnover or productivity add up to large sums. Use the scale in the case.

## Using the three theories in context

The specification names Herzberg, Maslow and Taylor without describing their theories, so examiners expect the standard textbook versions. The skill being tested is using them to explain what a business should do.

### Taylor: when does money-based motivation fit?

Frederick Taylor, writing on scientific management in the early twentieth century, argued that workers are mainly motivated by money and that managers should find the most efficient way to do each task and pay workers according to output.

Taylor's approach fits best where:

- output is easy to count, such as items packed or garments sewn;
- tasks are simple and repeated;
- quality can be checked easily, so speed does not hide faults.

It fits poorly where work is varied, creative or depends on teamwork, because rewarding individual output can make people ignore quality or refuse to help colleagues.

### Maslow: diagnosing the level

Abraham Maslow's hierarchy runs from physiological needs, through safety, social (belonging) and esteem needs, to self-actualisation. The useful skill is **diagnosis**: read the case for clues about which level is unmet, then match a method to it.

| Clue in the case | Unmet level | Suitable method from 2.4.2 |
|---|---|---|
| Workers cannot cover living costs | Physiological | Higher remuneration |
| Many temporary contracts, fear of job losses | Safety | Secure contracts; fringe benefits such as sick pay |
| Staff work alone and feel isolated | Social | Team working; job rotation within a team |
| Good work goes unnoticed | Esteem | Promotion; bonus linked to recognition |
| Skilled staff are bored and under-used | Self-actualisation | Job enrichment; autonomy |

### Herzberg: hygiene first, then motivators

Frederick Herzberg divided factors into **hygiene factors** (pay, working conditions, job security, company policy, supervision) and **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement). Poor hygiene factors cause dissatisfaction; putting them right removes dissatisfaction but does not create motivation. Only motivators do that.

The exam use is sequencing. If a case shows complaints about pay *and* boring work, the Herzberg answer is: fix pay first so people stop being dissatisfied, then enrich the job so they become motivated.

### Comparing the theories

| | Taylor | Maslow | Herzberg |
|---|---|---|---|
| What motivates | Money for output | Meeting the next unmet need | Motivators in the job itself |
| Role of pay | Central | Meets lower needs | Hygiene factor only |
| Best-fit work | Repetitive, measurable | Any; depends on the individual | Skilled work that can be enriched |
| Main weakness | Ignores social and esteem needs | People do not always move up in order | Pay can still motivate some workers |

## Financial methods: the calculations behind them

**Remuneration** is payment for work. A **wage** is usually paid per hour or week; a **salary** is a fixed annual amount paid monthly. A **piece rate** pays per item produced, which is Taylor's idea in practice.

### Worked example 1: salary or commission?

Mirren Kitchens offers sales staff a choice:

- **Package A:** salary of 2,100 a month.
- **Package B:** basic pay of 1,200 a month plus 4% commission on sales.

Step 1: the commission must make up the gap of 2,100 − 1,200 = 900.
Step 2: sales needed = 900 ÷ 0.04 = **22,500** a month.

A salesperson who sells 26,000 a month earns 1,200 + (0.04 × 26,000) = **2,240** on Package B, which is 140 more. In a slow month of 18,000, Package B pays 1,200 + 720 = **1,920**, which is 180 less.

Interpretation: commission rewards effort and links pay to the sales the business needs, but it moves risk onto the employee. A worker with high fixed bills (Maslow's lower levels) may prefer the security of Package A.

### Worked example 2: why fringe benefits can be good value

Kelso Retail has 80 employees. It can buy health cover through a group scheme at 45 per employee a month. An employee buying the same cover alone would pay 70 a month.

- Yearly cost to Kelso: 80 × 45 × 12 = **43,200**.
- Yearly value to the employees: 80 × 70 × 12 = **67,200**.

Employees receive 24,000 more value than the business spends, which helps both to attract and to retain staff. The limitation: an employee who already has health cover gains nothing, so a benefit only motivates those who value it.

**Bonus and promotion.** A bonus is a one-off payment for hitting a target; promotion moves someone to a more senior role with more pay and responsibility. Promotion combines a financial reward with esteem, but only a few posts are available, so others may be demotivated.

## Non-financial methods: telling them apart

- **Job rotation**: moving between tasks of a similar level. It adds variety and helps cover absence, but each task stays just as simple.
- **Job enrichment**: giving a job more responsibility and more demanding tasks, such as planning work or checking quality. It links to Herzberg's motivators and Maslow's higher levels.
- **Autonomy**: freedom to decide how, when or in what order to work. It shows trust but needs capable staff.

A quick test: rotation changes **which** task, enrichment changes **how much** the job involves, autonomy changes **who decides**.

## Annotated model answer

*Kerrow Call Centre has 260 staff. Pay is above average, but staff say the work is repetitive and they have no say in how calls are handled. A quarter of staff leave each year. Evaluate whether a pay rise or job enrichment would better reduce staff turnover.*

> A pay rise would show staff they are valued and might keep some who are tempted by other jobs. However, pay is already above average, and Herzberg would class pay as a hygiene factor: raising it further removes little dissatisfaction because there is little left. The complaint is about the work itself, which Herzberg treats as a motivator. Job enrichment, such as letting experienced staff resolve complaints without referring them to a supervisor, gives responsibility and recognition. With 260 staff and a quarter leaving, around 65 people are replaced every year, so even a small fall in turnover saves recruitment and training costs. Job enrichment is the better choice, provided staff receive training to handle the extra responsibility; otherwise errors could rise.

Why it works: uses the case's clues (pay already high, repetitive work), applies one theory accurately, calculates the scale of the problem and ends with a conditional judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a theory with no link to the business in the case.
- Saying Herzberg thought pay is unimportant. He said poor pay causes dissatisfaction.
- Muddling job rotation and job enrichment.
- Treating commission and bonus as the same: commission is a percentage of sales, a bonus is for meeting a target.
- Recommending a costly financial method for a business that the case shows is short of cash.
