## Section A

**1.** Define the term "Benioff zone". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a primary and a secondary impact of a volcanic eruption, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**3.** State two reasons why ash fallout is hazardous. **[2]**

**4.** Outline why basaltic eruptions are usually effusive. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why surface waves usually cause more damage than P waves. **[3]**

**6.** Outline two ways that buildings can be designed to withstand earthquake shaking. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** A subduction earthquake occurs under the fictional Corran Sea. The town of Velt lies 1,500 km away across ocean with a mean depth of 4,000 m. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻².

**(a)** Using speed = √(gd), calculate the tsunami speed in deep water in km per hour. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the approximate time for the tsunami to reach Velt. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why many people in Velt might still die despite this travel time. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows two fictional earthquakes.

| | Earthquake A | Earthquake B |
|---|---|---|
| Moment magnitude | 7.0 | 7.1 |
| Focus depth (km) | 12 | 35 |
| Distance of epicentre from city (km) | 20 | 80 |
| Ground beneath city | Saturated lake sediments | Bedrock |
| Deaths | 48,000 | 120 |

**(a)** Compare the two earthquakes. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons for the difference in deaths. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how monitoring can be used to forecast a volcanic eruption. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how a volcano can cause deaths at places far from its vent. **[5]**

**11.** Explain why perception of tectonic risk varies between people living in the same area. **[6]**

**12.** "The death toll from tectonic hazards depends more on vulnerability than on the size of the event." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A sloping band of earthquake foci [1] marking the subducting plate as it descends beneath the overriding plate [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A primary impact results directly from the eruption, for example roofs collapsing under ash [1]. A secondary impact results from the primary impacts, for example lost farm income after crops are buried, or lahars in later rains [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Wet ash is heavy enough to collapse roofs [1]. Fine ash damages lungs, engines and machinery, including aircraft engines [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Basaltic magma has low silica content, so it has low viscosity [1]. Gas bubbles can escape easily from runny magma [1], so pressure does not build up and lava flows out rather than exploding [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Surface waves travel along the ground surface where buildings stand [1]. They have larger amplitudes than P waves [1] and produce rolling and side-to-side ground motion that buildings resist poorly [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Base isolation places flexible bearings between foundations and the building [1], so less ground motion is passed up into the structure [1]. Cross-bracing with steel adds strength [1], so frames resist sideways forces and do not collapse [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** √(9.81 x 4,000) = √39,240 = 198 m s⁻¹ [1]. 198 x 3.6 = about 713 km per hour [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 1,500 / 713 = about 2.1 hours [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** People in Velt would not feel the earthquake, so they rely on a warning system [1]. If no warning reaches them, or warnings are not trusted, they may not move [1]. Low-lying coastal land, few evacuation routes or high buildings, and people drawn to the shore when the sea draws back, all raise deaths [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** The two have almost the same magnitude (7.0 and 7.1) [1]. Earthquake A was shallower (12 km against 35 km) and closer to the city (20 km against 80 km) [1]. Earthquake A caused 400 times as many deaths (48,000 against 120) [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** A shallow focus means less energy is lost before waves reach the surface, so shaking is stronger [1]. Being nearer the city, shaking is more intense [1]. Saturated lake sediments amplify shaking [1] and can liquefy, so buildings sink or tilt [1]. Differences in building quality, population density or emergency response may also explain the gap [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Rising magma breaks rock, producing swarms of small earthquakes that seismometers record [1]. Magma pushing upward makes the ground bulge, measured by tiltmeters, GPS or satellite radar [1]. Gas sensors detect rising sulphur dioxide as magma nears the surface [1]. Thermal sensors detect heating of the ground or crater [1]. Combining several signals over time lets scientists raise alert levels and order evacuation [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Lahars follow valleys and can travel tens of kilometres [1], especially when ash mixes with melted snow, ice or heavy rain [1]. Ash carried by wind falls over wide areas, collapsing roofs and harming health [1]. Pyroclastic flows can travel several kilometres faster than people can run [1]. Flank collapse can trigger landslides or lateral blasts that reach well beyond the summit [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Experience: people who have survived earlier small events may underestimate risk [1]. Economic ties: farmers on fertile volcanic soils or people with local jobs accept risk for income [1]. Wealth: richer people can afford insurance or safer homes, so feel less threatened [1]. Education and access to information about hazard maps vary [1]. Cultural or religious beliefs may lead to fatalism [1]. Trust in authorities or technology may make people feel safe, or distrust may make them ignore warnings [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define vulnerability [1] and the size of an event (magnitude, VEI) [1].

*Vulnerability dominant:* Haiti 2010 (Mw 7.0) killed far more than similar-sized events in wealthier countries [1]. Building quality explains much of the Turkey–Syria toll in 2023 [1]. Lack of a warning system raised the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami toll [1]. Communication failures, as at L'Aquila in 2009, increase deaths [1]. Population density and time of day matter [1].

*Size dominant:* very large events can overwhelm even well-prepared countries [1]. Large tsunami can overtop defences built for smaller events [1]. Energy rises about 32 times per magnitude unit, so size changes the area affected [1]. Large explosive eruptions affect wide areas whatever the preparation [1].

*Other factors:* depth of focus and distance [1]. Local geology, such as soft sediment and liquefaction [1]. Secondary hazards such as landslides and lahars [1].

*Interaction:* risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity, so both matter [1]. A small event in a vulnerable place can be a disaster [1].

*Judgement:* for most events vulnerability explains the difference between places [1]; size sets the upper limit for the largest events [1]; capacity to respond, such as rescue and medical care, also shapes the final toll [1]; a reasoned overall view on "how far" [1]. **[20]**
