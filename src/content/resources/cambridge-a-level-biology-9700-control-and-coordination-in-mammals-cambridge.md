## Two coordination systems

| Feature | Endocrine system | Nervous system |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Hormone (chemical) | Action potentials (electrical), with chemical transmission at synapses |
| Route | Blood, to the whole body | Neurones, to specific cells |
| Speed | Slower | Very fast |
| Duration of effect | Often long-lasting | Usually short |
| Target | Any cell with the right receptor | Only the cells the neurone connects to |

Both systems use **chemical messengers that bind to specific receptors**, and both rely on cell signalling. The three hormones named in the syllabus show the range of endocrine targets: **ADH** (posterior pituitary → collecting duct cells), **glucagon** (α cells → liver cells) and **insulin** (β cells → muscle and liver cells). Endocrine glands are **ductless**: they secrete straight into the blood.

## Neurones and receptors

- **Sensory neurone**: carries impulses from a receptor to the central nervous system. Its cell body lies just outside the spinal cord, part-way along the fibre.
- **Motor neurone**: carries impulses from the central nervous system to an effector. Its cell body is inside the central nervous system, with many dendrites, and a long axon reaching the muscle or gland.
- **Intermediate neurones** (relay neurones) connect sensory and motor neurones within the central nervous system.

A **sensory receptor cell** detects one type of stimulus and converts its energy into a change in membrane potential, called a **receptor potential**. If the receptor potential reaches **threshold**, action potentials are set up in the sensory neurone. A stronger stimulus does not give bigger action potentials; it gives **more frequent** ones.

### The taste bud chemoreceptor (outcome 15.1.5)

Using salt (sodium chloride) as the stimulus:

1. Na⁺ ions from the food diffuse into the chemoreceptor cell through **sodium ion channels** in its membrane.
2. The inside of the cell becomes less negative: it is **depolarised** (a receptor potential).
3. The depolarisation opens **voltage-gated calcium ion channels**; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
4. Ca²⁺ causes vesicles of **neurotransmitter** to fuse with the membrane and release their contents.
5. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the **sensory neurone**, which depolarises; if threshold is reached, an **action potential** is generated and travels to the brain.

## Resting potential and action potential

**Resting potential** (about −70 mV) is maintained by:

- **Sodium–potassium pumps**, which use ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
- A membrane that is **more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺**, so K⁺ diffuses out through open channels more than Na⁺ leaks in.
- Large negatively charged molecules, such as proteins, that stay inside the cell.

**Action potential**, in order:

1. A stimulus opens some **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels**; Na⁺ diffuses in.
2. If **threshold** is reached, many more Na⁺ channels open; the inside becomes positive (about +30 mV): **depolarisation**.
3. Na⁺ channels close; **voltage-gated K⁺ channels open**; K⁺ diffuses out: **repolarisation**.
4. So many K⁺ ions leave that the potential briefly overshoots below −70 mV: **hyperpolarisation**.
5. K⁺ channels close, and the sodium–potassium pumps and leak channels restore the resting potential.

**Refractory period.** For a short time after an action potential, the Na⁺ channels cannot reopen, so a new action potential cannot be generated. This means impulses are **separate**, travel in **one direction only** (the region behind is refractory), and have a **maximum frequency**.

## Saltatory conduction

In a myelinated neurone, **Schwann cells** wrap the axon in **myelin**, which insulates it. Ions can cross the membrane only at the **nodes of Ranvier**, the gaps between Schwann cells. Local circuits of current flow from one node to the next, so the action potential "jumps" from node to node instead of travelling continuously. Fewer patches of membrane need to depolarise, so transmission is much **faster**.

### Worked example 1: conduction velocity

Two recording electrodes are 60 mm apart on a myelinated axon. An action potential reaches the second electrode 0.80 ms after the first. On a non-myelinated axon of similar diameter, the same distance takes 30 ms.

```
myelinated:      0.060 m ÷ 0.00080 s = 75 m s⁻¹
non-myelinated:  0.060 m ÷ 0.030 s   = 2.0 m s⁻¹
ratio:           75 ÷ 2.0            = 37.5 times faster
```

Convert mm to m and ms to s **before** dividing.

## The cholinergic synapse

**Structure**: the **presynaptic knob** contains many mitochondria and vesicles of **acetylcholine (ACh)**; a narrow **synaptic cleft**; the **postsynaptic membrane** has ACh receptors that are also sodium ion channels; **acetylcholinesterase** is in the cleft.

**Function**:

1. An action potential arrives and depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
2. **Voltage-gated calcium ion channels** open; **Ca²⁺ diffuses into** the knob.
3. Ca²⁺ causes vesicles to move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing ACh by **exocytosis**.
4. ACh **diffuses across** the cleft and **binds to receptors** on the postsynaptic membrane.
5. The receptor channels open; Na⁺ enters and depolarises the postsynaptic membrane. If threshold is reached, an action potential starts.
6. **Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses** ACh to acetate and choline, so the signal stops. Choline is taken back into the presynaptic knob and recycled into ACh, using ATP.

Synapses make transmission **one-way**, because only the presynaptic side releases ACh and only the postsynaptic side has receptors.

### Worked example 2: predicting drug effects

*A drug inhibits acetylcholinesterase. Predict its effect at a neuromuscular junction.* ACh is not broken down, so it stays bound to the receptors; the postsynaptic membrane stays depolarised and the muscle fibre keeps being stimulated, so the muscle may stay contracted. Contrast a drug that **blocks the receptors**: ACh cannot bind, no depolarisation occurs and the muscle does not contract.

## From nerve to muscle

1. At the **neuromuscular junction**, ACh released by the motor neurone depolarises the **sarcolemma** (muscle cell membrane).
2. The depolarisation spreads down the **T-tubules**, which carry it deep into the fibre.
3. This causes the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** to release **Ca²⁺** into the sarcoplasm.

**Sarcomere ultrastructure** (from Z line to Z line):

- **Thin filaments (actin)** are attached to the Z lines.
- **Thick filaments (myosin)** sit in the middle, held at the M line.
- **A band**: the length of the myosin filaments (dark).
- **I band**: actin only (light), spanning the Z line.
- **H zone**: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.

**Sliding filament model**:

1. Ca²⁺ binds to **troponin**, which changes shape and moves **tropomyosin** away from the myosin-binding sites on actin.
2. **Myosin heads** bind to actin, forming **cross-bridges**.
3. The heads tilt (the power stroke), pulling actin towards the M line; ADP and phosphate are released.
4. **ATP** binds to each head, causing it to **detach** from actin; hydrolysis of the ATP returns the head to its original position, ready to bind again.
5. When stimulation stops, Ca²⁺ is **actively pumped** back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, tropomyosin covers the binding sites again and the muscle relaxes.

### Worked example 3: band lengths during contraction

In a model sarcomere, myosin filaments are 1.6 µm long and each actin filament extends 1.0 µm from its Z line.

| | Relaxed | Contracted |
|---|---|---|
| Sarcomere length | 2.4 µm | 2.0 µm |
| A band (= myosin length) | 1.6 µm | 1.6 µm |
| I band (sarcomere − A band) | 0.8 µm | 0.4 µm |
| H zone (sarcomere − 2 × actin) | 0.4 µm | 0 µm |

The A band stays the same because the filaments themselves do not shorten; the I band and H zone shrink because the filaments **overlap** more.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the action potential is "an electrical current flowing along the axon". It is a wave of **depolarisation** caused by ion movements.
- Writing that the sodium–potassium pump causes depolarisation. Depolarisation is Na⁺ **diffusing in** through voltage-gated channels.
- Stating that myelin speeds up ion movement. It **prevents** ion movement except at nodes.
- Forgetting the role of **Ca²⁺** at the synapse, or putting Ca²⁺ on the postsynaptic side.
- Writing that ATP is needed to form cross-bridges. ATP is needed to **break** them (detachment) and to re-cock the head, and to pump Ca²⁺ back.
- Saying actin or myosin filaments shorten during contraction.

## Exam technique

- **Compare** (nervous and endocrine): give similarities as well as differences, in matched pairs.
- **Describe the sequence** (taste bud, synapse, contraction): number your steps; each usually earns a mark.
- **Explain** membrane potential changes: name the ion, the channel type and the direction of movement for every change.
- **Data** on nerve impulses: read voltages with signs and units; convert units before calculating speeds or frequencies.
