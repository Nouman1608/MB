This guide covers **Paper 2 – Listening**, the second compulsory paper
in Cambridge IGCSE Urdu as a Second Language (0539), 2025-2027 series.
Where Paper 1 tests reading and writing across six exercises, Paper 2
is entirely a listening paper, worth 33% of the qualification, and
tests how well candidates understand spoken Urdu delivered through
recorded audio.

The audio material for each examination series is only released to
centres through the Cambridge Handbook rather than being publicly
available in advance, so practice for Paper 2 has to rely on
general listening materials -- Urdu-language news broadcasts,
interviews, radio dialogues -- of a similar style and register to
the exercise types above, rather than on retired listening scripts
alone, since no official past-paper audio is published outside the
secure Cambridge Handbook distribution to registered centres.

## Where this fits in 0539

All candidates take Paper 1 (67%) and Paper 2 (33%); centres can
additionally enter candidates for the separately-endorsed, optional
Component 5 Speaking, which is not counted toward the overall
qualification grade. Paper 2 is approximately 35–45 minutes long and
worth 30 marks, considerably shorter than Paper 1's two hours, but it
draws on a distinct skill -- listening comprehension -- that needs its
own dedicated practice rather than simply extending reading revision.
Because Paper 2 carries only 30 of the qualification's overall marks
against Paper 1's 60, candidates sometimes under-prepare for it
relative to its actual weighting, even though 33% of the final grade
is a substantial share to leave under-practised.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE URDU AS A SECOND LANGUAGE (0539) — PAPER 2 LISTENING**

- **Format**: a written paper of approximately 35–45 minutes, 30 marks, in which candidates hear spoken material for each exercise twice, with pauses built into the recording to give time to read questions and write answers. All questions and responses are in Urdu, and dictionaries may not be used.
- **What is tested**: listening comprehension of spoken material such as dialogues, interviews, conversations, news items, public announcements and talks, assessed against four listening assessment objectives (L1–L4): understanding factual information and ideas, identifying and selecting relevant details, identifying ideas, opinions and attitudes and the connections between them, and showing awareness of what is implied but not directly stated -- gist, purpose and intention.

**Exercise 1 – Short answer questions** (8 marks): candidates hear a series of short spoken texts, such as a travel announcement, a recorded phone message or a brief dialogue, and write short answers to eight questions. Assesses L1 and L2.

**Exercise 2 – Gap-fill sentences** (8 marks): candidates hear a longer spoken text, such as a conversation, interview, monologue or formal talk, and complete gaps in notes or sentences printed on the question paper. Assesses L1 and L2.

**Exercise 3 – Multiple matching** (6 marks): candidates hear six short extracts and match each speaker to the appropriate content. Assesses L3 and L4.

**Exercise 4 – Multiple-choice questions** (8 marks): candidates hear a discussion between two speakers and answer multiple-choice comprehension questions. Assesses L1 to L4.

## How to approach it

The four exercises move from short, literal comprehension toward
longer, more inferential listening, which is a useful structure to
mirror in practice sessions. Exercises 1 and 2 mostly test whether
candidates caught specific, stated facts -- a time, a place, a name,
a reason -- so practising note-taking while listening, rather than
trying to write full answers only after the recording ends, helps
candidates keep pace with the first hearing and confirm details on
the second. Common vocabulary worth over-preparing includes numbers,
times, dates and place names, since these are the details Exercises
1 and 2 most often test and the easiest to mishear under exam
pressure. Exercise 3's multiple matching tests whether candidates
can distinguish between six different speakers' viewpoints on a
similar topic, which rewards listening for tone and opinion words
rather than just topic keywords, since several speakers may mention
the same general subject while holding different views on it.
Exercise 4 is the most demanding exercise, since L4 (awareness of
what is implied but not stated) requires candidates to infer meaning
beyond the literal words used -- for example, recognising sarcasm,
hesitation or a change of mind in a discussion between two speakers,
or noticing when a speaker's stated opinion shifts partway through
their turn rather than staying fixed from start to finish.
Because every exercise is heard exactly twice, candidates should use
the first hearing to get the overall gist and locate roughly where
each answer sits, then use the second hearing to fill in and check
precise details, rather than trying to write a complete, final answer
during the first playback.

Paper 2 sits alongside a separately-endorsed optional Component 5
Speaking test, approximately 10–12 minutes, assessed on a scale of 1
(high) to 5 (low) rather than contributing marks to the overall
qualification grade. Centres choosing to enter candidates for
Component 5 conduct and record the test in Urdu before the main
examination series, and while it is graded and reported separately
from, and does not affect, the Paper 1/Paper 2 grade, the spoken fluency it develops -- recognising register,
responding naturally to questions, following a conversation's turns
-- overlaps closely with the listening skills Paper 2 tests, so
candidates preparing for Component 5 often find their Paper 2
listening comprehension improves alongside it, and vice versa.
