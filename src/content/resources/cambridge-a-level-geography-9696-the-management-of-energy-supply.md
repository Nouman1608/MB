This guide covers section 12.2 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. It contains two case studies. Candidates "must study one country's overall electrical energy strategy showing some of the issues of changes in demand for and supply of electricity, in power production and its location, and evaluate the success of the overall strategy", and "one named located scheme to produce electricity (e.g. a power station), showing some of the issues of changes in demand and supply, in power production and its location, and evaluate the success of the scheme". It is part of Topic 12, Environmental management, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 12 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-environmental-management/) works through Germany's strategy and the Three Gorges Dam. This page shows how to structure both case studies, then gives the United Kingdom and Hinkley Point C as a second pair. Condense it with the [12.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-energy-supply-revision-notes/) and test it with the [12.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-energy-supply-practice/).

## The shared checklist

Both case studies use the same four headings from the syllabus. Plan each one under them.

| Heading | Country strategy | Named scheme |
|---|---|---|
| Changes in demand | How national electricity demand has grown, fallen or shifted | What demand the scheme is meant to meet |
| Changes in supply | How the mix of sources has changed | How the scheme changes national or regional supply |
| Power production and its location | Where generation used to be and where it is now | Why this site was chosen |
| Evaluation | Success of the overall strategy | Success of the scheme |

Keep the two case studies distinct. A named scheme can sit inside your country strategy, but each needs its own evaluation.

## Case study 1: the United Kingdom's electricity strategy

### Changes in demand

UK electricity demand has fallen since the mid-2000s, despite population growth. Reasons include more efficient lighting and appliances, the decline of heavy industry and higher prices. Future demand is expected to rise again as heating and transport are electrified with heat pumps and electric vehicles.

### The strategy

- The **Climate Change Act 2008** set a legally binding target to cut greenhouse gas emissions by 80% by 2050; in 2019 this was changed to net zero by 2050.
- A **carbon price floor** from 2013 made coal generation more expensive.
- **Contracts for Difference**, introduced through electricity market reform in the 2010s, guarantee generators a fixed "strike price" for their electricity, paying the difference when market prices are lower. They support offshore wind and new nuclear.
- **Interconnectors** link the UK grid to other countries; the North Sea Link to Norway began operating in 2021.

### Changes in supply and location

- **Coal:** once dominant, coal fell rapidly in the 2010s. The large coal stations were inland, along rivers such as the Trent, near former coalfields and with cooling water. The last, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in 2024.
- **Gas:** became the largest single source for much of the period, using flexible plants that can follow demand.
- **Offshore wind:** grew into a major source, with large wind farms in the North Sea, such as the Hornsea and Dogger Bank projects, connected to the grid at coastal sites.
- **Nuclear:** the ageing fleet is closing; Hinkley Point B closed in 2022. New stations are being built or planned at existing nuclear sites.
- **Biomass:** Drax power station in North Yorkshire converted most of its coal units to burn imported wood pellets.

Production has therefore moved from inland river valleys near coalfields to the coast and offshore.

### Issues

- **Grid constraints:** much wind generation is in Scotland and the North Sea, while demand is greatest in the south of England. When lines are full, wind farms are paid to switch off.
- **Intermittency:** low-wind periods still need gas, storage or imports.
- **Prices and security:** gas prices rose sharply in 2021–22, raising electricity bills, because gas sets the price for much of the market.
- **Biomass controversy:** critics question whether burning imported wood is truly low-carbon.
- **Nuclear delays:** replacement stations have been slow and costly to build (see case study 2).

### Evaluation of the strategy

- **Emissions:** a clear success. Removing coal sharply reduced carbon emissions from electricity.
- **Security:** mixed. Dependence on gas and imports exposed consumers to price shocks.
- **Affordability:** weaker. Bills rose, and constraint payments and subsidies add costs.
- **Location:** the shift to offshore wind has created a mismatch between where power is produced and where it is used, which the grid is still catching up with.
- **Overall:** successful in decarbonising electricity, less successful in keeping it cheap and in building the grid and nuclear capacity on time.

## Case study 2: Hinkley Point C, Somerset

### Location and purpose

Hinkley Point C is a nuclear power station under construction on the coast of the Bristol Channel in Somerset, next to the older Hinkley Point A and B stations. It is being built by EDF, with China General Nuclear as a minority partner, and has two large reactors with a combined capacity of about 3.2 GW. It is intended to supply around 7% of the UK's electricity for about 60 years.

**Why this site?**

- an existing nuclear site, with local acceptance and land already in energy use;
- coastal, with large volumes of cooling water from the Bristol Channel;
- an existing grid connection point, though it needed reinforcing;
- firm rock foundations and a site that can be protected from flooding.

### Demand and supply

The station is designed to provide steady baseload electricity as older nuclear and coal stations close, and to complement variable wind and solar. It was supported by a Contract for Difference with a strike price of £92.50 per MWh in 2012 prices, for 35 years.

### Issues

- **Cost and time:** construction began in the late 2010s; the original target of operation in the mid-2020s has been pushed back into the next decade, and cost estimates have risen substantially.
- **Price to consumers:** critics argue the strike price is high compared with the falling cost of offshore wind.
- **Grid connection:** a new high-voltage line was built across Somerset, using a new design of pylon to reduce visual impact, after objections to the route.
- **Marine environment:** the cooling-water intakes will draw in fish; plans for fish-protection measures have been disputed.
- **Waste and risk:** long-term storage of spent fuel and public concern about accidents.
- **Local impacts:** thousands of construction jobs and spending in Bridgwater, but also traffic, housing pressure and disruption.

### Evaluation of the scheme

- **Supply goal:** if completed, it will provide large-scale, low-carbon, reliable electricity, filling a gap left by closing stations.
- **Cost:** weak so far. Delays and rising costs reduce value for money, and later than planned completion means older plant or gas must fill the gap.
- **Location:** a well-chosen site in physical terms, but the grid connection created local conflict.
- **Viewpoints:** government and EDF see long-term security; consumer groups see an expensive contract; local businesses see jobs; environmental groups see marine and waste risks.
- **Judgement:** a scheme whose success depends on timescale. In the short term it is over budget and late; over 60 years its low-carbon output may justify it.

## How to evaluate energy case studies

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Did it change supply as intended? |
| Emissions | Did carbon emissions fall? |
| Security | Is supply reliable and diverse? |
| Affordability | What has happened to prices and public costs? |
| Timeliness | Was it delivered on time? |
| Distribution | Who gained and who bore the costs, locally and nationally? |

Comparing your two strategies helps: the UK, like Germany, has largely removed coal, but kept and is renewing nuclear power, whereas Germany closed its reactors. That contrast supports a judgement about which approach balanced emissions and security better.

## Common mistakes

- Using one case study for both requirements; the syllabus asks for a strategy and a scheme.
- Describing the energy mix without its location.
- Ignoring demand: the syllabus asks for changes in demand as well as supply.
- Judging a scheme still under construction as a success or failure without saying "so far" or "if completed".
- Quoting figures you are unsure of; precise but wrong numbers cost credibility.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the strategy: use the four headings in the shared checklist.
- **Explain** the location: give two or three site factors and link them to the type of generation.
- **Evaluate**: apply at least three criteria and vary the judgement by group and timescale; conclude clearly.
