## Structure

### Outside

- Two thin-walled **atria** at the top; two thick-walled **ventricles** below, making up most of the heart.
- **Coronary arteries** branch over the surface from the base of the aorta. Heart muscle respires constantly and needs its own oxygen supply; blood inside the chambers does not supply the wall.
- Large vessels at the top: aorta, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins and venae cavae.

### Inside

| Structure | Position | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Septum | Between left and right sides | Keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart |
| Tricuspid valve | Right atrium → right ventricle | Atrioventricular valve; stops backflow into the atrium |
| Bicuspid (mitral) valve | Left atrium → left ventricle | As above, on the left |
| Tendinous cords and papillary muscles | From valve flaps to ventricle wall | Stop the atrioventricular valves turning inside out when ventricles contract |
| Semilunar valves | Base of aorta and pulmonary artery | Stop blood flowing back into the ventricles |

A useful check when labelling a diagram: the heart is drawn as if you are facing the person, so the **left** side of the heart is on the **right** of the page.

## Wall thickness explained

Thickness reflects the pressure each chamber must produce.

- **Atria vs ventricles**: an atrium only has to push blood a few centimetres into the ventricle below, which is already filling passively. Little force is needed, so the wall is thin. Ventricles must push blood out of the heart and round a whole circulation, so they need more cardiac muscle.
- **Left vs right ventricle**: both pump the same volume per beat. The left pumps into the systemic circulation, which is long and has high resistance, so it needs a much higher pressure and has a much thicker wall. The right pumps only to the lungs, which are close; a high pressure there would damage the delicate alveolar capillaries and push fluid into the alveoli.

## The cardiac cycle through pressure

Valves are passive. They open or close only because of a **difference in pressure** on their two sides. Learn two rules and you can interpret any graph:

- An **atrioventricular valve** is open when atrial pressure > ventricular pressure, and closed otherwise.
- A **semilunar valve** is open when ventricular pressure > arterial pressure, and closed otherwise.

So every valve event happens at a point where two pressure lines **cross**.

### The cycle in order (left side)

1. **Atrial systole**: atrial pressure rises slightly, pushing the last blood into the relaxed ventricle. AV valve open; semilunar valve closed.
2. **Ventricular systole begins**: ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, so the **AV valve closes**. Both valves are now shut, and pressure rises steeply with no change in volume.
3. **Ejection**: ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, so the **semilunar valve opens**. Blood enters the aorta and aortic pressure rises with ventricular pressure.
4. **Ventricular diastole begins**: the ventricle relaxes and its pressure falls below aortic pressure, so the **semilunar valve closes**. Aortic pressure stays fairly high because its elastic wall recoils.
5. **Filling**: ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, so the **AV valve opens**. Blood returning from the veins flows through the atrium into the ventricle.

The right side follows the same pattern at **lower** pressures.

### Worked example 1: timing a cycle

In one heart, the pressure lines cross at these times (s) during one cycle starting at atrial systole:

| Event | Time / s |
|---|---|
| Ventricular pressure rises above atrial | 0.10 |
| Ventricular pressure rises above aortic | 0.16 |
| Ventricular pressure falls below aortic | 0.40 |
| Ventricular pressure falls below atrial | 0.48 |
| Next atrial systole begins | 0.80 |

```
AV valve closes 0.10 s; semilunar opens 0.16 s
semilunar closes 0.40 s; AV opens 0.48 s
time both valves closed = (0.16 − 0.10) + (0.48 − 0.40) = 0.06 + 0.08 = 0.14 s
blood ejected for 0.40 − 0.16 = 0.24 s, which is 0.24 ÷ 0.80 × 100 = 30 % of the cycle
heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75 beats per minute
```

Questions often ask "when is the ventricle neither filling nor emptying?": these are the two intervals with both valves closed.

## Control: SAN, AVN and Purkyne tissue

Cardiac muscle is **myogenic**: it contracts without nervous stimulation. The heartbeat is coordinated by specialised tissue.

1. The **sinoatrial node (SAN)**, in the wall of the right atrium, sets the rhythm. It sends out a **wave of excitation** at regular intervals.
2. The wave spreads across the walls of **both atria**, so they contract together (atrial systole).
3. A layer of **non-conducting tissue** separates atria from ventricles, so the wave cannot pass straight into the ventricle walls.
4. The only route is the **atrioventricular node (AVN)**, in the septum. It passes the wave on after a **short delay**. The delay gives the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract.
5. The wave travels down **Purkyne tissue** in the septum to the **apex** (bottom tip) of the ventricles, then up through the ventricle walls.
6. The ventricles therefore contract **from the bottom upwards**, squeezing blood up towards the aorta and pulmonary artery, like squeezing a tube from the closed end.

The syllabus does not expect nervous or hormonal control of heart rate.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain the roles of the SAN, AVN and Purkyne tissue in the cardiac cycle.* **[5]**

> The SAN acts as the pacemaker, sending out waves of excitation **(1)** that spread across both atria so they contract **(1)**. Non-conducting tissue stops the wave reaching the ventricles directly, so it passes through the AVN, which delays it **(1)**, allowing the atria to empty fully before the ventricles contract **(1)**. Purkyne tissue carries the wave to the apex, so the ventricles contract from the apex upwards, pushing blood into the arteries **(1)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying valves open "to let blood through" without comparing pressures.
- Placing the bicuspid valve on the right.
- Explaining the left ventricle's thicker wall by "it pumps more blood". Both ventricles pump the same volume; the left produces a higher **pressure**.
- Saying the SAN "sends nerve impulses". It sends waves of excitation (electrical activity) through muscle.
- Leaving out the non-conducting tissue, which is why the AVN matters.
- Writing that ventricles contract "from the top down".
- Saying tendinous cords open and close the valves; they only stop them inverting.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** (structure): name structures with their positions.
- **Explain** (wall thickness): link thickness to pressure needed and distance or resistance.
- **Describe** (cardiac cycle): sequence of events, each valve linked to a pressure comparison.
- **Explain** (SAN, AVN, Purkyne): wave → atria contract → delay → why → apex upwards → why.
- **Calculate**: heart rate = 60 ÷ time for one cycle in seconds.
