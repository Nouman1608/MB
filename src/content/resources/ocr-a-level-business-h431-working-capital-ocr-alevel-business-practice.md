## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by working capital. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by the working capital cycle. **[3]**

**3.** A business has current assets of £75,000 and current liabilities of £43,000. Calculate its working capital and its current ratio. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a current liability?

A: Inventory.

B: Money owed to suppliers.

C: Money owed by customers.

D: A machine. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why working capital is not the same as cash. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Bowden Bakery has revenue of £584,000 and cost of sales of £365,000 for the year. At the year end it has inventory of £18,000, receivables of £48,000, cash of £9,000, payables of £25,000, an overdraft of £12,000 and tax due of £6,000.

**(a)** Calculate the working capital. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate inventory days, receivable days and payable days. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the length of the working capital cycle. **[2]**

**(d)** Bowden cuts receivable days to 20 days. Calculate the new level of receivables and the cash released. **[3]**

**(e)** Evaluate whether Bowden should delay paying suppliers by 10 days as a way of improving cash. **[6]**

**7.** Revenue and cost of sales at Bowden Bakery both rise by 10% and the days stay the same. Calculate the new net amount tied up (inventory plus receivables less payables) and the increase. Explain what this shows about growth. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how a manufacturer's working capital cycle could be lengthened by a decision to hold more stock and offer customers longer credit. **[4]**

**9.** A supermarket chain has a low or negative working capital figure but trades successfully. Explain why. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the importance of managing working capital to a rapidly growing business and its stakeholders. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Working capital is the money available to a business to meet its day-to-day running costs [1]; it equals current assets minus current liabilities [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The working capital cycle is the length of time between a business paying for materials or stock [1] and receiving cash from customers for the goods sold [1]. It passes through stock, production, finished goods and receivables before cash returns [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Working capital = £75,000 − £43,000 = **£32,000** [1] [1]. Current ratio = £75,000 ÷ £43,000 = **1.74** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Working capital includes inventory and receivables, which have to be sold or collected before they become cash [1], so a business may have positive working capital but too little cash to pay a bill today [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Current assets = 18,000 + 48,000 + 9,000 = £75,000 [1]. Current liabilities = 25,000 + 12,000 + 6,000 = £43,000 [1]. Working capital = **£32,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Inventory days = 18,000 ÷ 365,000 × 365 = **18 days** [1]. Receivable days = 48,000 ÷ 584,000 × 365 = **30 days** [1]. Payable days = 25,000 ÷ 365,000 × 365 = **25 days** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** 18 + 30 − 25 [1] = **23 days** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Receivables at 20 days = 20 ÷ 365 × £584,000 [1] = **£32,000** [1]. Cash released = £48,000 − £32,000 = **£16,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(e)** For: 10 more days means payables rise from £25,000 to £35,000 (35 ÷ 365 × £365,000), so £10,000 of cash is kept in the business [1]; it costs nothing directly, and may avoid an overdraft [1]; the cycle falls from 23 to 13 days [1].
Against: suppliers may be unhappy and could cut credit, raise prices or stop supply [1]; Bowden may lose early-payment discounts [1]; as a small business it may depend on a few key suppliers [1].
Judgement: delay only if it is within the agreed terms or negotiated with the supplier; otherwise faster collection from customers is a safer way to release cash [1]. (Maximum 6 marks.) **[6]**

**7.** New revenue = £584,000 × 1.1 = £642,400; new cost of sales = £365,000 × 1.1 = £401,500 [1]. Inventory at 18 days = £19,800; receivables at 30 days = £52,800; payables at 25 days = £27,500 [1] [1]. Net amount = 19,800 + 52,800 − 27,500 = **£45,100** [1]. Before growth: 18,000 + 48,000 − 25,000 = £41,000, so the increase is **£4,100** [1]. This shows that a growing business needs more working capital simply to maintain the same days, so profitable growth can create a cash shortage [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Holding more stock lengthens the inventory stage, because materials and finished goods stay in the business longer before being sold [1] [1]. Offering customers longer credit increases receivable days, so cash comes back later [1]. Both mean the business pays out cash earlier than it receives it, so the cycle is longer and more finance is needed [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A supermarket sells mainly for cash, so it has few receivables [1]. It sells stock quickly, so inventory days are low [1]. It buys on credit from suppliers and takes 30 days or more to pay, so it receives cash from customers before it pays [1]. The cycle is very short or even negative, so it needs little working capital, and customers' money finances part of its operations [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Importance for the business: growth means more inventory and credit, so cash is needed to fund the cycle [1]; if cash runs out, the business may be unable to pay suppliers or wages even though profitable (over-trading) [1]; a shorter cycle reduces the need for borrowing and its cost [1].
Stakeholders: suppliers need prompt payment to keep supplying [1]; lenders look at working capital before lending more [1]; employees depend on wages being paid [1]; owners may have to put in more capital [1].
Evaluation: managing the cycle must be balanced against sales, since refusing credit or holding too little stock may reduce growth [1]. Judgement: managing working capital is crucial for a fast-growing business, because growth is the point at which it is under most strain [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using non-current assets or long-term loans in the calculation.
- Adding payable days instead of subtracting them.
- Using the wrong base: receivables use revenue; inventory and payables use cost of sales.
- Treating working capital as cash.
- Forgetting that growth increases working capital needs.
