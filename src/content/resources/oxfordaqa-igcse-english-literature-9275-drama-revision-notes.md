## Section B at a glance

- Paper 1, Section B: one question on your set play, **30 marks**.
- Paper 1: 1 hour 30 minutes, **closed book**, both routes.
- Choice: **essay-style** or **passage-based** (extract printed).
- About **45 minutes**, with five minutes of planning.
- Set plays come from a prescribed list that can change: check the list for your exam year.

## The one big idea

A play is written to be **performed**. Every answer should show that you understand it as something
an audience watches, not just a script on a page. Write "the audience", and explain what they see,
hear and feel at a given moment.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Stage direction | The playwright's instruction about action, tone, set or sound | Direct evidence of intended effect |
| Dialogue | Characters' speech to each other | Shows power, conflict and relationships |
| Soliloquy | A character speaks thoughts alone on stage | Gives the audience private access |
| Aside | A remark the audience hears but other characters do not | Creates complicity or dramatic irony |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows something a character does not | Builds tension or sympathy |
| Climax | The point of highest tension | Anchor for a paragraph on structure |
| Resolution | How the conflict ends, or fails to | Shapes the audience's final judgement |
| Exposition | Information given early to set up the situation | Shows how the playwright establishes conflict |
| Foil | A character who contrasts with another | Highlights the other's qualities |
| Pause / silence | A marked break in speech | Lets unspoken feeling register |
| Entrance / exit | Arrival or departure on stage | Interrupts, shifts power or ends a moment |
| Symbolic prop | An object given repeated attention | A visible symbol the audience remembers |

## Staging note: the extra step in drama

For every key quotation in your bank, add one line on performance:

- **How** might it be said? (tone, volume, pace)
- **Who** else is on stage, and how might they react?
- **What** do the stage directions require at this point?

## Two question types

| Passage-based | Essay-style |
|---|---|
| Analyse the printed extract closely | Range across the whole play from memory |
| Do what the wording asks: extract only, or extract and the wider play | Build the answer around development and structure |
| Comment on staging in the extract | Use staging notes from your quotation bank |

## Paragraph pattern

**Claim → short quotation or stage direction → dramatic method → effect on the audience → link to
the question or the play's structure.**

## Exam traps

- Writing about "the reader" and ignoring performance.
- Describing a film or production instead of the playwright's choices.
- Ignoring silent or watching characters.
- Quoting long speeches instead of the key words.
- Running out of time because Section A overran.
- Context with no link to a dramatic choice.

## Self-test

1. How many marks is Section B worth?
2. What is the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?
3. Give two things a staging note might record.
4. Why is dramatic irony useful for analysing tension?
5. Why should you write "the audience" rather than "the reader"?
6. What should you do first in a passage-based question?

**Answers:** 1. 30 marks, half of Paper 1. 2. A soliloquy is spoken alone on stage; an aside is a
remark heard by the audience but not by the other characters present. 3. Any two: tone or pace of
delivery; who else is on stage and their reaction; what the stage directions require. 4. The
audience knows more than a character, so they anticipate consequences and feel tension or dread.
5. It shows you understand the text as a play written for performance, which is part of analysing
the playwright's methods. 6. Read the wording carefully, then analyse the printed extract closely
before moving outward, if the question asks you to refer to the rest of the play.
