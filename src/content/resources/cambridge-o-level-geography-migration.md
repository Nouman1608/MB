This guide covers **sub-topic 1.2 Migration** of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026
(Version 3). For the wider picture of Theme 1, see the
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/).
Here the focus is on the classifications, causes and impacts you need for this one sub-topic, and
on building a case study that can carry a long answer.

## What 1.2 asks you to do

You need to be able to:

- explain and give reasons for migration using **push and pull factors**;
- recognise **internal** movements, especially **rural–urban migration**, and **international**
  movements;
- distinguish **voluntary** from **involuntary (forced)** migration;
- explain the **impacts of migration** on both the **source area** (where migrants leave) and the
  **receiving area** (where they arrive);
- use at least one named **international migration** as a case study.

Migration is also the third component of population change, so it links back to
[1.1 Population Dynamics](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-population-dynamics/), and
rural–urban migration is the main driver of urbanisation later in Theme 1.

## Key idea 1: classifying a migration

Every migration can be described along several lines at once. Practise labelling examples on all of
them.

| Way of classifying | Categories | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Distance and borders | Internal (within a country) / international (between countries) | Rural Sindh to Karachi is internal |
| Choice | Voluntary / involuntary (forced) | A worker seeking a better job / a family fleeing war |
| Time | Temporary or seasonal / permanent | Fruit pickers who return each year / settlers who stay |
| Direction within a country | Rural–urban / urban–rural / urban–urban | Counter-urbanisation is urban to rural |

Some groups have legal names you should use precisely:

- **Refugee**: a person forced to leave their **country** because of a well-founded fear of
  persecution, conflict or violence.
- **Asylum seeker**: a person who has applied for protection as a refugee and is waiting for a
  decision.
- **Internally displaced person (IDP)**: someone forced from their home who has **not crossed an
  international border**.
- **Economic migrant**: someone who moves voluntarily, mainly for work or higher income.

## Key idea 2: push factors, pull factors and obstacles

**Push factors** are conditions that encourage people to leave; **pull factors** are attractions
of the destination. Strong answers group them:

| Group | Push (source) | Pull (destination) |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low farm incomes, land shortage | Jobs, higher wages, remittance potential |
| Social | Poor schools and health care | Better services, family already there |
| Political | Conflict, persecution, instability | Safety, political freedom |
| Environmental | Drought, flooding, desertification | Reliable water, a less hazardous location |

People do not respond to push and pull factors automatically. **Intervening obstacles** such as
the cost of travel, distance, immigration laws, language and lack of information stop many people
from moving. This explains why migrants are often **young adults**: they have fewer ties, are more
able to find work and are more willing to take the risk. In many flows migrants are
disproportionately men, at least at first.

Remember that pull factors are **perceived**. A rural migrant may expect a well-paid formal job
in the city and end up in the informal sector; the image of the destination is often more
attractive than the reality.

## Key idea 3: impacts on source and receiving areas

Impacts are economic, social, demographic and cultural, and they can be positive or negative for
**both** places. The table below gives the structure examiners reward.

| | Source area | Receiving area |
|---|---|---|
| Economic + | **Remittances** support families and local spending; less unemployment | Fills labour shortages, often in jobs locals avoid; migrants pay taxes |
| Economic − | **Brain drain** of skilled workers such as doctors and engineers | Pressure on housing, school places and health services; some wage competition |
| Demographic | Loses young adults, so birth rate falls and the population ages; gender imbalance if mostly men leave | Gains young adults, which can raise the birth rate and slow ageing |
| Social and cultural | Families separated; women may take on more farm and household work | Cultural diversity; but possible tension or discrimination, and language barriers |

**Return migration** can bring skills, savings and new ideas back to the source area, which is
worth one extra developed point in an evaluation.

## Case study material

Use one well-developed case study rather than three thin ones. Two well-documented examples are
summarised here; add your own detail from class notes.

**Voluntary, international: Pakistan to the Gulf states.** Large numbers of Pakistani workers have
moved to countries such as Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, mostly on temporary contracts
in construction, transport and services.
- *Push:* limited well-paid work, especially in rural areas, and a fast-growing workforce.
- *Pull:* higher wages and demand for labour created by oil wealth and building programmes.
- *Source impacts:* remittances are a major source of income for many households and for the
  national economy; husbands and fathers are away for long periods.
- *Receiving impacts:* labour for major projects; but migrant workers often have limited rights,
  and many are housed in separate labour camps.

**Forced, international: refugees from the Syrian civil war (from 2011).** Millions of Syrians left
the country, with neighbouring **Turkey** receiving the largest number; many others went to
Lebanon and Jordan, and a smaller share travelled on to Europe, notably to Germany in 2015.
- *Push:* armed conflict, bombing of towns, collapse of services and persecution.
- *Pull:* safety, nearness (Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan), and for Europe, asylum systems and family
  links.
- *Source impacts:* loss of a large part of the working-age population and of skilled workers,
  making reconstruction harder.
- *Receiving impacts:* pressure on housing, schools and health care in border regions and refugee
  camps; humanitarian costs; in the longer term, some refugees fill labour shortages.

## Worked example: reading a migration table

A town recorded 3,600 arrivals and 5,100 departures in one year. Its population is 150,000.

- Net migration = 3,600 − 5,100 = **−1,500**.
- As a rate: −1,500 ÷ 150,000 × 1,000 = **−10 per 1,000**.

If most of those leaving are aged 18–35, you can **suggest** consequences: a falling birth rate in a
few years, an ageing population, labour shortages on farms and less demand for local services.
These data are illustrative.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that refugees "choose" to move for a better life. That describes voluntary migration.
- Calling an internally displaced person a refugee.
- Giving impacts for the receiving country only. Always cover the source area as well.
- Listing push and pull factors with no link to a named flow.
- Writing "brain drain" without saying which skilled workers leave and what the source loses.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **state** a type of migration: use the exact term (internal, forced, seasonal).
- **Explain** reasons: one factor, then "so" or "because", then the effect on the decision to
  move.
- **Describe** a flow on a map: give the direction, the source and destination regions, and the
  relative size if the arrows are scaled.
- **Suggest**: apply ideas to an unfamiliar flow in the question; credit is given for sensible
  reasoning.
- **Case study** parts: name the source and receiving countries, give dates and specific impacts on
  each side.
