## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Unlimited wants set against limited resources. The fundamental economic problem |
| Choice | Selecting one use of scarce resources, which rules out others |
| Opportunity cost | The sacrifice made by a choice, valued as the best option passed over |
| What to produce | Which goods and services, in what quantities |
| How to produce | Which method, for example labour-intensive or capital-intensive |
| For whom to produce | How output is shared among people |
| Social science | A study of human behaviour that uses scientific methods |
| Positive statement | A statement of fact that can be tested against evidence |
| Normative statement | A value judgement about what ought to be |
| Ceteris paribus | All other things being equal |
| Short run | One or more factors of production cannot be changed |
| Long run | All factors can vary |
| Very long run | Technology and the quality of factors can also change |

## 1.1 Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

### Scarcity (1.1.1)

- The root of all economic problems. It exists for rich and poor, and for time as well as money.
- Scarcity is permanent. A shortage is a market event at one price. Do not mix them up.

### Choices (1.1.2)

| Decision-maker | Typical choice | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | What to buy, how long to work | Income and time |
| Firm | What to produce, what to invest in | Finance and available inputs |
| Government | Health, education or defence spending | Tax revenue and borrowing |

### Opportunity cost (1.1.3)

Method:

1. Write down the options that were really open.
2. Order them from most to least valuable.
3. Pick the highest-ranked option you did not take. Do not sum the rest.
4. Give the answer in sensible units: goods, money or time.

Worked reminder (a ratio): a farm can grow 50 tonnes of wheat or 20 tonnes of barley.

```
Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of barley = 50 / 20 = 2.5 tonnes of wheat
Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat  = 20 / 50 = 0.4 tonnes of barley
Grow 8 tonnes of barley: wheat forgone = 8 x 2.5 = 20, wheat left = 30
```

Worked reminder (a budget): a government has 90 million. A bridge costs 30 million and a clinic costs 6 million.

```
Opportunity cost of 1 bridge = 30 / 6 = 5 clinics
Share of the budget in one bridge = 30 / 90 = 33.3%
```

Worked reminder (a firm): 50,000 can fund a machine adding 6,000 a year or a van adding 4,500 a year. Choosing the machine has an opportunity cost of 4,500 a year, which is smaller than the 6,000 gained.

Evaluation points: benefits are hard to value in money terms, benefits arrive at different times, and the next best alternative depends on which options are considered.

### What, how and for whom (1.1.4)

- What: driven in a market by what buyers will pay for, and in a government by priorities.
- How: depends on relative input prices and technology. A firm picks the cheapest method for the output it wants.
- For whom: a market shares output by ability to pay, which raises questions of fairness (normative).

Worked reminder (how): 150 units a day.

```
Method A: 3 workers x 70 + machine 90 = 300 -> 300 / 150 = 2.00 per unit
Method B: 6 workers x 70 + tools 30  = 450 -> 450 / 150 = 3.00 per unit
Choose A. Break-even point depends on the wage.
```

If A used 3 workers at wage w and B used 6 workers at wage w, the cost gap would be 3w - 60. Method A would be cheaper whenever 3w > 60, so when w exceeds 20.

How different economic systems answer these questions is section 1.4. See the [basic ideas revision notes](/resources/a-level-economics-basic-ideas-revision-notes/).

## 1.2 Economic methodology

### Economics as a social science (1.2.1)

- Scientific method: observe, build a model, form a hypothesis, test it against data, refine or reject.
- Social, because it studies human behaviour in society.
- Limits: few controlled experiments, behaviour changes with expectations, and values enter policy advice.
- Judgement: it uses scientific methods but cannot match the precision of the natural sciences.

### Positive and normative (1.2.2)

| Positive | Normative |
|---|---|
| Testable with evidence | Rests on a value judgement |
| Can be true or false | Cannot be proved true or false by evidence alone |
| "Prices rose 4% last year" | "Prices should be controlled" |
| "A subsidy raises output" | "The subsidy is unfair" |

Tips:

- A false statement can still be positive.
- Watch for should, ought, fair, too much, better.
- Split mixed sentences into a positive part and a normative part.

### Ceteris paribus (1.2.3)

- Change one variable, hold the rest constant, and observe the separate effect.
- It simplifies, so state what else might change when you evaluate.

Worked reminder: price falls from 5 to 4 and sales rise from 300 to 330.

```
Price change = (4 - 5) / 5 = -20%
Sales change = (330 - 300) / 300 = +10%
```

If a rival also raised its price in the same week, the 10% rise may not come from the price cut alone.

### Time period (1.2.4)

| Period | What can change | Typical response of a firm |
|---|---|---|
| Short run | One or more factors cannot change | Change labour or materials |
| Long run | All factors variable | Change plant size or location |
| Very long run | Technology and factor quality | Adopt new methods, retrain the workforce |

Periods differ by industry. They are defined by what can change, not by months or years.

## Chains of reasoning to reuse

- Scarcity to choice: wants exceed resources, so decision-makers must choose, so every choice has an opportunity cost, so a good decision compares the benefit gained with the best benefit forgone.
- Input prices to method: a rise in the wage makes labour-intensive methods dearer, so a profit-seeking firm moves towards capital, so the answer to "how to produce" changes, and the answer to "for whom" may change as jobs and incomes shift.
- Time to flexibility: in the short run a firm can only change the variable factors, so its response to higher demand is limited, so prices or waiting times may rise until the long run allows more capacity.
- Evaluation of a model: state the assumption (ceteris paribus), say what could break it, then judge how much the result changes.

## Command words in this topic

- Define: give a precise meaning in one or two lines.
- Explain: give a reason, then link it to the point asked.
- Distinguish: state the difference in both directions, and give a short example of each.
- Analyse: build a chain of cause and effect.
- Evaluate: weigh the strengths and limits, then reach a supported judgement.

## Must-know distinctions

- Scarcity is not a shortage.
- Opportunity cost is the next best alternative, not all alternatives and not just money spent.
- Positive is about testability, not truth.
- Ceteris paribus is an assumption, not a claim that other things never change.
- Short, long and very long run are about flexibility, not calendar time.

## Quick self-test

1. Define scarcity.
2. A tailor makes 24 shirts or 6 coats a day. What is the opportunity cost of one coat?
3. Choose positive or normative: "Wages in Town X rose 3% last year."
4. Choose positive or normative: "Wages in Town X should rise faster."
5. A government has 90 million. A bridge costs 30 million and a clinic 6 million. How many clinics equal one bridge?
6. A firm picks project P (gain 8,000), ahead of project Q (gain 6,500) and project R (gain 5,000). State its opportunity cost.
7. State the three questions of resource allocation.
8. Explain why a lower price might not be the only cause of a rise in sales.
9. Give the difference between the short run and the long run.
10. Name two reasons economics differs from physics.

### Answers

1. Wants are unlimited but resources are limited.
2. 24 / 6 = 4 shirts.
3. Positive.
4. Normative.
5. 30 / 6 = 5 clinics.
6. 6,500, from project Q.
7. What, how and for whom to produce.
8. Other factors, such as income, tastes or rivals' prices, may have changed at the same time. Ceteris paribus fails in practice.
9. In the short run some factors cannot be changed. In the long run every factor can be changed.
10. Economists rarely run controlled experiments, and human behaviour changes with expectations and values.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "what you give up" without "next best".
- Adding together several alternatives to find opportunity cost.
- Calling scarcity a shortage.
- Using "should" in a prediction and wrongly labelling it normative, or the reverse.
- Defining positive as "true" and normative as "false".
- Defining ceteris paribus but not applying it to a stated pair of variables.
- Treating the short run as a fixed number of months.
- Stopping at description in an evaluation question and offering no judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is scarcity called the fundamental economic problem?**
Scarcity is the gap between unlimited wants and limited resources. Because of it, individuals, firms and governments must choose, and every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

**What is the difference between scarcity and a shortage?**
A shortage is a market event in which buyers want more than sellers supply at the current price. Scarcity is permanent and applies to everyone, even when a market clears.

**What do the command words define, explain and evaluate ask for?**
Define asks for a precise meaning in one or two lines. Explain asks for a reason linked to the point in the question. Evaluate asks you to weigh strengths and limits and reach a supported judgement.

**What is the difference between the long run and the very long run?**
In the long run all factors of production can vary. In the very long run technology and the quality of factors can also change.
