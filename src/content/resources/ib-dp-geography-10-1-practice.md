## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between water withdrawal and water consumption. **[2]**

**2.** Define the energy ladder. **[1]**

**3.** State the three terms in the IPAT identity. **[1]**

**4.** Outline why a country can have high total energy use but low energy use per person. **[2]**

**5.** Using the rule of 70, estimate the doubling time of demand growing at 5 per cent a year. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows data for Country N.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Population (millions) | 50 | 80 |
| Water use per person (cubic metres a year) | 400 | 500 |

**(a)** Calculate total water use in Year 1 and Year 2, in billions of cubic metres. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate how many times total water use increased. **[1]**

**(c)** Using your answers, identify whether population or use per person contributed more to the increase. Show your reasoning. **[2]**

**7.** A household survey records the main cooking fuel used by 400 households in a city district: wood 60, charcoal 140, bottled gas 160, electricity 40.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of households using bottled gas or electricity. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons why many households might use more than one fuel. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why rising incomes in emerging economies increase demand for land and water. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the difference between extensification and intensification as ways of meeting rising food demand. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the relative importance of population growth and rising affluence in explaining global trends in resource consumption. **[10]**

**11.** To what extent does the ecological footprint provide a useful measure of global trends in consumption? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Withdrawal is the total volume of water taken from rivers, lakes or aquifers [1]; consumption is the part of that water not returned to the local system, because it evaporates, is transpired or is built into products [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A model of how households move from collected biomass towards cleaner purchased fuels and electricity as their incomes rise [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Population, Affluence (consumption per person) and Technology (impact per unit of consumption) [1]. **[1]**

**4.** A large population multiplies even a small amount of use per person into a large total [1], while energy use per person stays low if many people have low incomes or limited access to modern energy [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 70 / 5 = **14 years** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Year 1: 50 million x 400 = 20,000 million = **20 billion cubic metres** [1]. Year 2: 80 million x 500 = 40,000 million = **40 billion cubic metres** [1].

**(b)** 40 / 20 = **2 times** (doubled) [1].

**(c)** Population increased 80 / 50 = 1.6 times, while use per person increased 500 / 400 = 1.25 times [1], so population growth contributed more to the increase (1.6 x 1.25 = 2.0) [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** (160 + 40) / 400 x 100 = **50%** [1].

**(b)** The supply of gas or electricity may be unreliable, so households keep charcoal or wood as a back-up [1]; cleaner fuels may be too expensive for every meal, or cooking traditions may favour charcoal for some dishes [1]. **[3]**

**8.** As incomes rise, diets shift from staple grains towards meat and dairy products [1]. Livestock need far more land and water per unit of food energy than crops eaten directly, because animals are fed on grain and grazing [1]. Higher incomes also bring more use of water in homes and in manufacturing goods for consumers, so demand rises for both resources [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Extensification means increasing output by bringing more land into production [1], for example by clearing forest or ploughing grassland, which can cause deforestation and habitat loss [1]. Intensification means increasing output from the same area of land [1], using improved seeds, fertiliser, irrigation or machinery, which can raise yields but may deplete water and degrade soils [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Uses the IPAT identity or a clear equivalent to separate population from consumption per person [1].
- Global population growth increases total demand for food, water and energy [1].
- Population growth is now fastest in low-income regions, where consumption per person is low, so its effect on total demand is smaller than its size suggests [1].
- Rising affluence, especially among the new global middle class in Asia, has increased consumption per person sharply [1].
- Diet change shows affluence at work: meat and dairy demand has risen faster than population [1].
- Energy demand per person rises with car ownership, appliances and air travel [1].
- Technology can reduce impact per unit, for example more efficient appliances, so it modifies both factors [1].
- Scale matters: in some countries population growth dominates; in others, rising incomes [1].
- Uses data or located examples accurately, such as China's rising meat consumption since the 1980s [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that affluence is now the stronger global driver, while population matters most in specific regions [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines the ecological footprint as the productive land and water needed to supply consumption and absorb waste [1].
- Strength: it combines many types of consumption into one comparable unit, the global hectare [1].
- Strength: comparing footprint with biocapacity shows ecological deficit and overshoot over time [1].
- Strength: it allows comparison between countries and highlights unequal consumption per person [1].
- Limitation: it is a modelled estimate that depends on assumptions about yields and conversion factors [1].
- Limitation: it does not capture every impact, such as some forms of pollution or water depletion [1].
- Limitation: national averages hide inequality in consumption within countries [1].
- Other measures, such as water and carbon footprints, or simple per-capita use of energy, add detail it lacks [1].
- Uses an accurate example, such as humanity being in ecological deficit since the early 1970s according to the Global Footprint Network [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that it is useful for showing overall trends and inequality but should be combined with resource-specific measures [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* For a measure, "to what extent" needs you to say what it shows well, what it misses, and how far those gaps matter for the question asked.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying millions by per-capita figures and losing track of units.
- Saying population "caused" growth without comparing it with the change per person.
- Treating withdrawal and consumption of water as identical.
- Evaluating a measure without naming a specific strength and a specific limitation.
