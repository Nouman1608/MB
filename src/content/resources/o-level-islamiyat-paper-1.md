This guide covers **Paper 1**, one of two compulsory papers for
Cambridge O Level Islamiyat 2058, 2026–2027 series. Confirmed
independently against 2058's own syllabus PDF: it shares an
identical Paper 1 / Paper 2 four-topic structure with Cambridge IGCSE
Islamiyat 0493. (A version 2 syllabus for the 2028 series has since
been published; this page follows the current 2026-2027 document.)

## Where this fits in 2058

Paper 1 covers the Qur'an and the life of the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh),
while Paper 2 (not covered in this guide) covers the Hadiths, the
Rightly Guided Caliphs, and the Articles of Faith and Pillars of Islam.
Together the two papers form the whole of 2058 — there is no numbered
sub-topic list beyond these four per paper, but the syllabus also
prescribes fifteen named passages from the Qur'an in an appendix, from
which Question 1's choices are set, so revision should cover the
appendix passages directly rather than relying on the sub-topic list
alone.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL ISLAMIYAT 2058 — PAPER 1**

- 1.1 Major Themes of the Qur'an — the central themes and teachings
running through the Qur'an
- 1.2 The History and Importance of the Qur'an — how the Qur'an was
revealed and compiled, and its significance
- 1.3 The Life and Importance of the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) — the key
events of his life and their significance for Muslims
- 1.4 The First Islamic Community — the Prophet's wives; his
descendants, including his children, grandchildren and the line
recognised among Shia Muslims as Imams; his leading Companions,
including the Ten Blessed Companions, his Scribes and the major
figures of his ministry; the Emigrants and the Helpers; and the four
Rightly Guided Caliphs during his lifetime

## The paper's structure

Paper 1 runs for 1½ hours and is worth 50 marks, made up of **five set
questions**. Candidates must answer **Question 1 and Question 2**,
which are compulsory, plus **two further questions chosen from
Questions 3 to 5**. Question 1 (worth 8 marks) sets three short
passages on the major themes of the Qur'an and offers a choice of
**any two** to write on; Questions 2 to 5 are essay-length, each worth
14 marks and drawn respectively from the history and importance of the
Qur'an, the life and importance of the Prophet (pbuh), and the first
Islamic community. Because the two compulsory questions cover 1.1 and
1.2 specifically, a candidate cannot simply avoid a weaker sub-topic if
it happens to fall in that compulsory pair — which is why breadth
across all four sub-topics, not just depth in two or three, is the
safer preparation strategy.

Marks are also split by assessment objective: **AO1 (Recall, select
and present)** makes up 68% of the qualification, covering the
knowledge-based part of each question, while **AO2 (Understanding)**
makes up the remaining 32%, rewarding candidates who go beyond
description to explain significance and reasoning. The published mark
scheme describes the highest AO1 band as one that is "likely to quote
Qur'an verses and Hadiths to support points made" (or other relevant
quotations), against "may" quote for the band below — so a top-band
answer characteristically includes supporting reference, though this
is a description of what such answers tend to do, not an absolute
rule against fact-only answers.

## Key content across the four sub-topics

The Qur'an was **revealed over 23 years (610–632 CE)**, beginning in
the Cave of Hira, through the angel Jibril, and consists of **114
surahs** — generally shorter Makkan surahs concerned with tawhid, the
afterlife and moral conduct, and generally longer Madinan surahs
concerned with law, community and social order. It was **compiled**
under Abu Bakr, after the Battle of Yamama had killed many of those
who had memorised it, and later **standardised** under Uthman, who
distributed official copies and had variant texts destroyed — two
distinct stages that examiners expect to see kept separate, not
conflated into one event.

The Prophet's life (1.3) and the first Islamic community (1.4) are
best revised as a connected timeline rather than as isolated facts:
the first revelation in 610, the Hijra to Madinah in 622 that marks
the start of the Islamic calendar, the Constitution of Madinah
establishing Muslims, Jews and other groups as a single community
under the Prophet's arbitration, the pairing of the Muhajirun with the
Ansar as a practical response to the migrants' destitution, the
Battles of Badr, Uhud and the Trench, and the Farewell Sermon setting
out equality, the sanctity of life and property, and the abolition of
usury and blood feuds. Placing these events in relation to one
another, rather than recalling them in isolation, is exactly what
Questions 3 and 4 most often ask candidates to do.

## How to approach it

As with the IGCSE variant of this syllabus, each paper mixes a
compulsory question with a choice from the remainder, so breadth of
preparation across all four sub-topics matters more than depth in only
one or two — a candidate weak on one sub-topic risks being forced into
unfamiliar territory if that is where the compulsory question falls.
Answers are strongest when they combine accurate factual recall — dates,
names, sequence of events — with an explanation of significance, since
1.2 and 1.3 explicitly ask for an understanding of importance, not just
narrative retelling. Build a simple timeline of the Prophet's life and
the early community (1.3 and 1.4) as a revision anchor, since many exam
questions ask candidates to place events in relation to one another.
For 1.1 and 1.2, practise linking a named theme of the Qur'an to a
specific example of its teaching rather than listing themes as abstract
labels — vague, unsupported statements are a common way marks are lost
even when the underlying knowledge is correct. As with any two-paper
qualification examined across a defined series, confirm your own
centre's exact entry pattern and paper timetable directly with your
school rather than assuming it matches another qualification's.
