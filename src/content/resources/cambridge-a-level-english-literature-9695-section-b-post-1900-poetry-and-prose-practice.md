## Section A — Knowledge and terms

**1.** A candidate answers on *Jude the Obscure* in Section A. State what form their Section B text must
be and give **one** 2026 example. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the effect of **first-person present-tense** narration compared with retrospective
narration. **[2]**

**3.** Define **allegory** and explain why a novelist might leave a setting unnamed. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why it matters, in a (b) answer, that a set poem belongs to a **sequence**. **[2]**

## Section B — Modern passages and evaluation

**5.** Read this **original** passage.

> I am writing this in the kitchen because the light is better. That is what I tell myself. The
> kitchen is also where the telephone is, and the telephone has not rung for nine days, and I am not
> waiting for it. I am writing. I move the cup. I move it back.

Explain how the writer uses **first-person narration and sentence structure** to reveal what the
narrator will not admit. **[6]**

**6.** Read this **original** passage.

> Afterwards, nobody agreed on the order of things. Some said the soldiers came first and then the
> fire; some said the fire, and then the soldiers, who had come to see it. The child, who was there,
> said only that the river was very loud that night, louder than it had ever been.

Explain how the passage uses **non-linear and multiple perspectives** to present a traumatic event.
**[5]**

**7.** Read this **original** poem from an imagined sequence spoken by a tree.

> **The Oak Speaks**
>
> You carved your names in me
> the summer you were sure.
> I grew around them,
> as I grow around everything:
> the nail, the wire, the year
> the river came.
> Now the letters are wide
> and strange, and you
> would not know them.
> I would.

Explain how the poet uses **voice and line breaks** to present time and memory. **[6]**

**8.** Using the poem in Question 7, explain how an **ecocritical** reading might interpret it, and test
that reading against the poem. **[4]**

**9.** A candidate writes: "A postcolonial critic would say this novel is about colonialism." Explain why
this earns little AO5 credit, and write a stronger evaluated point using a general example. **[4]**

**10.** A candidate's (b) answer on a fragmented novel spends two paragraphs explaining what "really
happened" before the passage. Explain the weakness and suggest a better approach. **[3]**

**11.** Explain **three** features of a strong (a) essay on a modern poetry collection built as a
sequence. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A **poetry** text [1]; for 2026, for example, Glück's *The Wild Iris*, Okara's selected poems or
Trethewey's *Native Guard* [1].

**2.** Present-tense narration **removes hindsight**, so the narrator does not yet know how events will
turn out [1]; the reader shares that **partial understanding** and uncertainty, whereas retrospective
narration can judge and foreshadow [1].

**3.** Allegory is a narrative whose **characters, places or events stand for wider ideas** [1]. An
unnamed setting prevents the story being tied to **one place or regime**, so its concerns apply more
widely [1].

**4.** Poems in a sequence **answer and develop one another** [1], so placing the printed poem in its
sequence shows **knowledge of the whole text** (AO1) and may reveal meanings the poem alone does not
carry [1].

**5.** "That is what I tell myself" **undermines the narrator's own explanation** immediately [1] [1].
The long sentence about the telephone accumulates facts with repeated "and", **building towards the
denial** "I am not waiting for it" [1], which the reader does not believe [1]. The short sentences at
the end ("I move the cup. I move it back.") present **restless, pointless action** [1], revealing anxious
waiting through behaviour rather than statement [1].

**6.** The opening "nobody agreed on the order of things" signals that the event **resists a single
account** [1]. The two versions **reverse the order** of soldiers and fire [1], so cause and blame remain
uncertain [1]. The child's testimony turns to an **apparently unrelated sensory detail** [1], suggesting
trauma displaced into memory of sound rather than told directly [1].

**7.** The tree's **persona** gives a long, non-human perspective on time [1]. Short lines and the break
after "I grew around them" isolate the act of **absorbing** what is done to it [1]. The list "the nail,
the wire, the year / the river came" places human damage and natural disaster on **one timescale** [1].
The enjambment across "you / would not know them" **delays the human failure of memory** [1], and the
isolated final line "I would." sets the **tree's memory against human forgetting** [1] [1].

**8.** An ecocritical reading would see the poem as presenting the **natural world as a witness** to
human damage, with a memory longer than ours [1] [1]. The poem supports this, as the tree "grow[s]
around" nails and wire, **absorbing harm** [1]; but the tone is not simply accusing, since it also keeps
the lovers' names with something like **tenderness**, so the reading needs qualifying [1].

**9.** It states an obvious view **without testing or judging** it, and the vague "a postcolonial critic"
does no work [1] [1]. A stronger point: "A postcolonial reading sees the officials' fear of the 'enemy' as
the empire inventing an other to justify itself; the novel supports this when the threat never
materialises, though it also presents individual guilt, so the reading explains the politics better
than the characters." [1] [1] (One mark for testing against the text, one for a judgement.)

**10.** It **fills the gaps** the novel deliberately leaves and **delays analysis** of the passage [1]
[1]. A better approach analyses **how** the passage withholds or reveals information and what effect this
delay has on the reader [1].

**11.** Use the **structure of the sequence** as evidence, such as how poems answer one another or how
voices change across the collection [1] [1]. Select **poems from across the collection** and show
development [1] [1]. Weave in **evaluated AO5**, testing a critical approach against close analysis of
form and line [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Inventing explanations for gaps a modern text deliberately leaves.
- Treating a persona as the poet.
- Writing on one poem with no reference to its sequence.
- Naming a critical approach without testing it on the text.
