This guide goes deeper into section 3.9.1 Exothermic and endothermic reactions (statements a to d) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The course is untiered. It focuses on the reasoning examiners test: deciding the direction of energy transfer from evidence, giving ΔH the right sign, and applying the ideas to everyday products and reversible reactions. ## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.9.1 a | Energy is transferred to or from the surroundings; ΔH conventions, positive for endothermic and negative for exothermic |
| 3.9.1 b | Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings: combustion, many oxidations, neutralisation; uses include self-heating cans and hand warmers |
| 3.9.1 c | Endothermic reactions take in energy from the surroundings: thermal decompositions; some sports injury packs |
| 3.9.1 d | Reversible reactions, A + B ⇌ C + D; hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water, endothermic one way and exothermic the other |

## The system and the surroundings

Every energy question depends on separating two things:

- **The system:** the reacting chemicals.
- **The surroundings:** everything else, including the water the chemicals are dissolved in, the container, the thermometer and the air.

Energy is conserved. If the system loses energy, the surroundings gain exactly that amount, and the reverse.

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Energy moves | From system to surroundings | From surroundings to system |
| Thermometer in the mixture | Rises | Falls |
| ΔH | **Negative** | **Positive** |
| Energy of products compared with reactants | Lower | Higher |

ΔH describes the **system**. That is why a reaction that makes the thermometer rise has a negative ΔH: the chemicals have lost energy.

## Classifying reactions from evidence

### Worked example 1: a set of class results

The table shows **invented results** from a class experiment. Each reaction was carried out in a polystyrene cup.

| Reaction | Start / °C | End / °C |
|---|---|---|
| A: magnesium ribbon + dilute hydrochloric acid | 20.0 | 34.5 |
| B: citric acid solution + sodium hydrogencarbonate | 20.0 | 14.0 |
| C: sodium hydroxide solution + dilute hydrochloric acid | 20.0 | 26.5 |
| D: zinc powder + copper(II) sulfate solution | 20.0 | 29.0 |

*Classify each reaction and give the sign of ΔH.*

| Reaction | Temperature change | Type | ΔH |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | +14.5 °C | Exothermic | Negative |
| B | −6.0 °C | Endothermic | Positive |
| C | +6.5 °C | Exothermic (a neutralisation) | Negative |
| D | +9.0 °C | Exothermic | Negative |

**Exam point:** the temperature change only tells you the direction of energy transfer. Comparing the sizes of different temperature rises fairly needs the same amounts of reactants and the same volume of solution; that is the calculation work in 3.9.2.

## Exothermic reactions (3.9.1 b)

The three families named in the specification:

- **Combustion:** fuels burning in oxygen, such as methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
- **Oxidation:** many reactions with oxygen, including slow ones such as iron rusting.
- **Neutralisation:** acid + alkali, H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

### "But you have to light it"

Combustion needs a flame or spark to start, yet it is exothermic. The flame provides the **activation energy** (3.8.1 b), the minimum energy particles need to react. Once started, the reaction releases far more energy than it took to start, so it keeps itself going. Needing energy to **start** is not the same as taking in energy **overall**.

### Everyday uses

| Product | How it works | Design point |
|---|---|---|
| Self-heating can | Two substances are kept apart until a button breaks the barrier; they react exothermically and heat the drink | The reactants must not mix until needed |
| Hand warmer | An exothermic reaction (for example, iron powder slowly oxidising when the packet is opened to air) warms the hands | Slow reaction gives steady warmth over a long time |

## Endothermic reactions (3.9.1 c)

- **Thermal decomposition:** breaking down a compound by heating. Carbonates of magnesium, copper, zinc, calcium and lithium decompose this way, for example CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂. The reaction **stops when heating stops**, which is strong evidence that it needs a continuous energy supply.
- **Sports injury packs:** squeezing the pack lets the contents mix and take in energy, so the pack goes cold and cools the injury.

Endothermic reactions in solution make the solution feel cold because they take energy from the water around the particles.

## Reversible reactions and energy (3.9.1 d)

If a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic in the other, and **the same amount of energy** is transferred each way.

The specification's example:

hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water

- Heating the blue crystals drives off water and leaves a white powder: **endothermic** in the forward direction.
- Adding water to the white powder turns it blue and it gets hot: **exothermic** in the reverse direction.

### Worked example 2: the sign flips, the size does not

The thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate has ΔH = +178 kJ/mol:

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

*State ΔH for the reverse reaction, CaO(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s), and say what type it is.*

ΔH = **−178 kJ/mol**. The reverse is **exothermic**: the same amount of energy, transferred in the opposite direction.

## Worked example 3: choosing a reaction for a product

The table gives ΔH values for three reactions, labelled J, K and L.

| Reaction | ΔH / kJ/mol |
|---|---|
| J | −64 |
| K | +26 |
| L | −890 |

*Which reaction could be used in a cold pack, and which two are exothermic?*

- **K** is the only reaction with a positive ΔH, so it is the only one that takes in energy from the surroundings and could cool an injury.
- **J** and **L** have negative ΔH values, so both are exothermic. L transfers far more energy per mole, but a product such as a hand warmer also needs a safe, steady temperature, so the largest value is not automatically the best choice.

## Common mistakes

- Giving ΔH a positive sign because "the temperature went up".
- Saying an endothermic reaction "makes heat" or "absorbs cold".
- Calling combustion endothermic because it needs a match.
- Saying the two directions of a reversible reaction transfer different amounts of energy.
- Getting the copper sulfate colours the wrong way round: hydrated is **blue**, anhydrous is **white**.
- Describing energy transfer without saying **to** or **from the surroundings**.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define** exothermic or endothermic: use the specification's wording about the surroundings.
- **Give an example:** name a specific reaction (for example, burning methane; thermal decomposition of copper carbonate).
- **Explain how you know:** cite the evidence (the thermometer reading) and link it to the direction of energy transfer.
- **Suggest** a use: match the type to the job (exothermic to warm, endothermic to cool).
