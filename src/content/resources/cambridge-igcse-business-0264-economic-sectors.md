This guide goes deeper on section 1.2 (Economic sectors) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus lists one content statement, 1.2.1 Types of economic sector, with two points: "primary, secondary and tertiary sectors" and "private and public sectors". That sounds short, but these two classifications are used all through the course, and case studies often expect you to classify a business before you can say anything else about it. The broader [business activity and economic sectors guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-business-activity-and-economic-sectors/) introduces both ideas alongside section 1.1. This page concentrates on harder classification cases, the links between sectors, and how to write about ownership in answers. Recall the key points with the [economic sectors revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-economic-sectors-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [economic sectors practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-economic-sectors-practice/).

All businesses and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Content point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors | Say which sector a business works in, using evidence from what it does, and explain how the sectors depend on one another |
| Private and public sectors | Say who owns and controls an organisation, and explain how that affects its aims and the way it is funded |

The two classifications answer different questions. The first asks *what kind of work* is done. The second asks *who owns and controls* the organisation. Every organisation has one answer to each, so a full classification always has two parts.

## Classifying by type of activity

### The three tests

Use the main activity described in the data and ask three questions in order.

1. Does it take a natural resource from the land or sea, or grow or rear it? If yes, it is **primary**. Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying and drilling for oil all pass this test.
2. Does it turn materials into a different product, or build something? If yes, it is **secondary**. Manufacturing, processing, assembly and construction belong here.
3. Does it provide a service rather than a physical product of its own making? If yes, it is **tertiary**. Retail, transport, banking, insurance, education, health care, tourism and repairs are tertiary.

### Cases that catch candidates out

| Business | Correct sector | Why it is easy to get wrong |
|---|---|---|
| House-building firm | Secondary | It "provides" homes, but building is a secondary activity |
| Bakery that bakes on site and sells over the counter | Secondary and tertiary | It makes bread and also sells it; name both if the data shows both |
| Car repair garage | Tertiary | It works with physical goods but it repairs them; it does not make them |
| Fish farm | Primary | Rearing fish is a primary activity, even though it happens in tanks |
| Delivery company carrying factory goods | Tertiary | It moves goods; transport is a service |
| Fruit grower that also makes and bottles juice | Primary and secondary | Growing is primary, pressing and bottling is secondary |

The rule from these cases: classify by **what the business does**, not by what it handles. A shop handles goods but it does not make them, so it is tertiary.

### How the sectors depend on each other

The sectors are linked in a chain of production. Each stage needs the others, and each adds value.

Worked example: **Tamberlin Cocoa.** A grower harvests cocoa beans and sells them for $2.80 a kilogram (primary). A chocolate maker turns one kilogram of beans into chocolate bars that it sells to shops for $9.50 (secondary). Shops sell those bars to the public for $14.00 (tertiary).

| Stage | Sector | Value of the product at this stage | Value added at this stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beans harvested | Primary | $2.80 | $2.80 |
| Bars made | Secondary | $9.50 | $9.50 - $2.80 = $6.70 |
| Bars sold to the public | Tertiary | $14.00 | $14.00 - $9.50 = $4.50 |

The figures simplify reality (the grower has its own bought-in inputs, such as seed and fertiliser), but they show that every sector adds value. The pattern of dependence runs both ways.

- The chocolate maker depends on the primary sector for its main material. A poor harvest raises its costs.
- The grower depends on the secondary sector to buy its crop. If the maker cuts output, the grower sells less.
- Both depend on tertiary businesses: transport firms to move beans and bars, banks to provide loans, shops to reach customers.

This is useful in "explain" answers. A problem in one sector rarely stays there. For example, a strike by lorry drivers (tertiary) can leave beans unsold on farms (primary) and stop production at the factory (secondary).

## Classifying by ownership: private and public sectors

### What decides the sector

| Feature | Private sector | Public sector |
|---|---|---|
| Owned by | Individuals or groups of individuals, such as sole traders, partners or shareholders | Government, national or local |
| Controlled by | The owners, or managers who answer to them | Government ministers, local councils or managers they appoint |
| Usual main aim | Profit, though survival, growth and social aims are possible | A service for the public, often without aiming for profit |
| Main sources of money | Owners' investment, profit, loans, sales revenue | Mainly government funding from taxes, though some charge for services |
| Who gains if it does well | The owners | The public, through better services or lower taxes |

Ownership is the test. Size is not. A small village clinic run by the local council is public sector; a huge shareholder-owned bank is private sector.

### Why it matters in an answer

The sector affects how an organisation behaves. A privately owned bus company may stop running a route that makes a loss. A bus service run by a local council may keep the route because elderly residents rely on it, and pay for it from local taxes. Neither is "right"; they have different aims. Questions often ask you to explain this kind of difference, so name the aim and then show its effect on a decision.

Private and public organisations can also work together. A government may pay a private firm to build a school, or a council may pay a private company to collect rubbish. The school-building firm remains private sector; the school, once run by the government, is public sector.

### Combining both classifications

| Organisation | Activity sector | Ownership sector |
|---|---|---|
| Government-owned oil-drilling company | Primary | Public |
| Family-owned furniture workshop | Secondary | Private |
| State university | Tertiary | Public |
| Shareholder-owned mobile network | Tertiary | Private |
| Council-run road-building department | Secondary | Public |

## Annotated model answer

Question: *Rossmere Water is owned by the national government. It takes water from a lake, cleans it in a treatment works and pipes it to homes. Explain why it is difficult to place Rossmere Water in only one activity sector.*

Model answer: Rossmere Water carries out more than one kind of activity. Taking water from the lake is primary, because it extracts a natural resource. Cleaning the water in a treatment works is secondary, because it processes the raw water into a different, usable product. Supplying it to homes is a service, which is tertiary. So it works in all three sectors, and the best answer names its main activities rather than forcing a single label.

Why it earns credit: each sector is named, linked to a specific activity in the data, and followed by a reason. Note that the ownership information (national government, so public sector) is not needed for this question. Using every piece of data is not the goal; answering the question is.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **identify**, **state**, **define**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify / state:** a single label is enough, such as "tertiary". If a question asks for both classifications, give both.
- **Define:** say what decides the sector. "The public sector is the part of the economy owned and controlled by government" is precise; "the public sector helps people" is not.
- **Outline:** set out the main points briefly, such as the activity and the sector it gives.
- **Explain:** link the label to the business's actual activity or owner, then say why that matters. Use the data: "it builds the houses itself, which is construction, so it is secondary".
- **Consider / justify:** weigh both sides before deciding, for example whether a service should be run by a private firm or by government, using the aims of each.

## Common errors

- Putting construction in the tertiary sector because builders "provide a service". Building is secondary.
- Classifying a repair or transport business as secondary because it works with physical goods.
- Defining the public sector as "businesses that serve the public". Private shops serve the public too; ownership by government is the test.
- Saying a large organisation must be public sector, or a small one must be private.
- Giving only one classification when a question asks about both type of activity and ownership.
- Writing that public sector organisations never charge or never need to control costs. Many charge fees, and all have budgets.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is construction primary, secondary or tertiary?**
Construction is secondary. It takes materials such as bricks, steel and concrete and builds something new from them, which is the same kind of activity as manufacturing.

**Can a business be in more than one sector?**
Yes. A fruit farm that also bottles juice is primary and secondary, and if it runs a farm shop it is tertiary too. Classify by the main activity, and name the others if the data shows them.

**Does a public sector organisation ever make a profit?**
It can earn more than it spends, for example from fees, but profit is not usually its main aim. Its main aim is normally to provide a service to the public.
