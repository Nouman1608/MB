When you have revised, try the [approaches practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-scientific-approaches-in-psychology-practice/). The [9685 course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/) and [9685 checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/) list every unit.

## The four approaches in one line each

- **Behaviourist:** all behaviour is learned through association (classical) or consequences (operant); study only what can be observed.
- **Social learning theory:** we also learn by watching others, and mental (mediational) processes decide whether we copy.
- **Cognitive:** behaviour comes from internal mental processes, which are inferred from behaviour and described with models.
- **Biological:** behaviour has a physical basis in genes, brain structures and neurochemistry, shaped by evolution.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Unconditioned stimulus (UCS) | Triggers a reflex response without learning |
| Conditioned stimulus (CS) | A once-neutral stimulus that now triggers the response after pairing with the UCS |
| Conditioned response (CR) | The learned response to the CS |
| Extinction | The CR fades when the CS keeps appearing without the UCS |
| Spontaneous recovery | An extinguished CR returns after a rest |
| Stimulus generalisation | A stimulus similar to the CS also triggers the CR |
| Positive reinforcement | A pleasant consequence is added, so the behaviour increases |
| Negative reinforcement | An unpleasant stimulus is removed or avoided, so the behaviour increases |
| Punishment | An unpleasant consequence, so the behaviour decreases |
| Variable ratio schedule | Reward after an unpredictable number of responses; most resistant to extinction |
| Modelling | A model shows a behaviour that an observer may copy |
| Imitation | Copying the model's behaviour |
| Identification | Feeling similar to, or wanting to be like, a model |
| Vicarious reinforcement | Learning from the rewards or punishments another person receives |
| Mediational processes | Attention, retention, reproduction, motivation |
| Schema | A mental framework of beliefs and expectations built from experience |
| Theoretical model | A description of a mental process as stages or parts, such as the multi-store model |
| Computer model | Treating the mind like a computer (input, processing, storage, output), or simulating thought with a program |
| Cognitive neuroscience | Study of how brain structures and activity support mental processes |
| Genotype | A person's genetic make-up |
| Phenotype | How the genotype is expressed in observable traits, shaped by the environment |
| Concordance | The extent to which both twins in a pair share a trait |
| Neurotransmitter | A chemical, such as serotonin or dopamine, that carries signals across a synapse |
| Natural selection | Traits that aid survival and reproduction are passed on more often |

## Studies at a glance

| Who | What they did | Key finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pavlov | Paired neutral sounds (metronomes, whistles, tuning forks) with food for dogs | Sound alone came to produce salivation | Objective and controlled, but dogs' reflexes may not generalise to complex human behaviour |
| Skinner (*The Behavior of Organisms*, 1938) | Rats in a box pressed a lever for food, or to switch off a mild electric current | Behaviour followed by reward, or by removal of something unpleasant, increased | Strong control, but ignores thought and expectation |
| Ferster and Skinner (1957) | Studied schedules of reinforcement | Variable ratio responding lasts longest without reward | Explains persistent behaviour such as repeated checking |
| Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961) | 72 nursery children (37 to 69 months) saw an aggressive, non-aggressive or no model with a Bobo doll | Aggressive model led to more copied aggression; same-sex models had more influence | Demand characteristics: the doll is designed to be hit |
| Bandura (1965) | Model rewarded, punished or no consequence; then children offered rewards to copy | Punished-model group copied less, until offered rewards | Shows learning and performance are different |
| Bartlett (*Remembering*, 1932) | English participants recalled a Native American tale several times | Unfamiliar details dropped or changed to fit their culture | Supports schema, but procedure was loosely controlled |
| Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968) | Proposed the multi-store model | Sensory register, short-term and long-term stores | Clear theoretical model, but oversimplified |
| Maguire et al. (2000) | Structural MRI of London taxi drivers and controls | Larger posterior hippocampi; volume correlated with time driving | Correlation: cannot show cause on its own |
| Buss (1989) | Mate preferences of 10,047 people in 37 cultures | Men valued youth; women preferred older partners | Cross-cultural, but cannot test ancestral environments |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Classical vs operant conditioning.** Classical links two stimuli and works on reflexes. Operant links a behaviour with its consequence and works on voluntary behaviour.
- **Negative reinforcement vs punishment.** Negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant and makes behaviour *more* likely. Punishment makes it *less* likely.
- **Behaviourism vs SLT.** Behaviourism needs direct reinforcement and ignores the mind. SLT allows learning by observation and adds mediational processes.
- **Learning vs performance.** Bandura (1965) showed a behaviour can be learned but not shown until there is a reason to perform it.
- **Theoretical vs computer models.** A theoretical model describes the parts of a process. A computer model uses the computer as an analogy or simulation.
- **Cognitive approach vs cognitive neuroscience.** The first infers processes from behaviour. The second links them to brain activity.
- **Genotype vs phenotype.** Genotype is inherited and fixed; phenotype is how it shows, after interaction with the environment.
- **MZ vs DZ twins.** MZ twins share all their genes; DZ twins share about half on average.

## Mini essay plan

*Discuss the cognitive approach in psychology, including the emergence of cognitive neuroscience. [16]*

1. **Knowledge.** Define the approach: internal mental processes inferred from behaviour. Explain schema with Bartlett (1932). Describe one theoretical model (multi-store) and the computer analogy.
2. **Knowledge.** Outline cognitive neuroscience: brain imaging links processes to areas; Broca (1861) as an early root; Maguire et al. (2000) as modern evidence.
3. **Evaluation (strength).** Controlled experiments make it scientific and replicable; cognitive neuroscience adds objective, physical evidence.
4. **Evaluation (strength).** Practical value: schema research helps explain errors in eyewitness memory.
5. **Evaluation (limitation).** Inference means models can be abstract and hard to falsify; the computer analogy ignores emotion.
6. **Evaluation (limitation).** Artificial tasks reduce ecological validity; scans show correlation, not cause.
7. **Synoptic link.** Contrast with behaviourism, which rejects the study of mental processes, and with the biological approach, which cognitive neuroscience partly joins.
8. **Judgement.** The approach became more scientific once mental processes could be linked to brain activity, but it works best alongside emotional and social explanations.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the difference between a neutral stimulus and a conditioned stimulus?
2. Define spontaneous recovery.
3. Give one example of negative reinforcement.
4. Which schedule of reinforcement is most resistant to extinction?
5. Name the four mediational processes in order.
6. What is identification?
7. What did Bandura (1965) show about vicarious reinforcement?
8. Why are mental processes said to be inferred?
9. What did Maguire et al. (2000) find?
10. Define phenotype.
11. What would MZ concordance of 100% suggest, and why is it not found?
12. What evidence did Buss (1989) use to support an evolutionary explanation?

### Answers

1. A neutral stimulus produces no response before conditioning; it becomes a conditioned stimulus once pairing with the UCS makes it trigger the response alone.
2. The return of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period.
3. Taking a painkiller to remove a headache, which makes taking painkillers more likely in future.
4. Variable ratio.
5. Attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
6. Feeling similar to a model or wanting to be like them, which makes imitation more likely.
7. Children who saw a model punished copied less aggression, but all groups copied when offered rewards, so reinforcement affected performance, not learning.
8. They cannot be seen directly, so they are worked out from measured behaviour such as recall scores or reaction times.
9. Taxi drivers had larger posterior hippocampi than controls, and volume correlated with time spent driving a taxi.
10. How a person's genotype is expressed in observable traits and behaviour, shaped by the environment.
11. That the trait is entirely genetic; it is not found, so environment also plays a part.
12. Consistent sex differences in mate preferences across 37 cultures.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving "bell" as the UCS. Food is the UCS; the sound is the NS before conditioning and the CS after it.
- Using "negative reinforcement" to mean punishment.
- Describing Skinner's box without saying what was reinforced and how.
- Listing mediational processes with no link to the scenario in an application question.
- Describing the Bobo doll study but forgetting its conditions or its finding on same-sex models.
- Writing that cognitive psychologists "see" mental processes.
- Saying brain scans prove a brain area causes a behaviour.
- Using twin data as proof of a purely genetic cause.
- Evaluating with stock phrases that name no study or approach.
- Missing synoptic chances: Unit 1 and Unit 2 studies can count as evidence in Unit 4.
