This guide covers **Paper 2 Family, Education and Crime**, for
Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495, Version 3, 2025–2027 series. Paper 2
has three questions, of which candidates answer two of its three
topics; Marlbridge's Paper 1 guide covers Research Methods, Identity
and Inequality — the methods and perspectives introduced there
(functionalism, Marxism, feminism, and the strengths and limitations of
each) are assumed knowledge across every topic in Paper 2.

## Where this fits in 0495

Paper 1 and Paper 2 are each worth 50% of the qualification. Where
Paper 1 builds the sociological toolkit — research methods and the
major theoretical perspectives — Paper 2 applies that toolkit to three
substantive areas of social life: the family, education, and crime and
deviance. Every Paper 2 topic follows the same internal pattern:
factual content first (what different family types, school types or
crime types actually look like), then perspectives (how functionalists,
Marxists and feminists interpret that content), then change and
explanation (why patterns are shifting, and what explains inequality
within them).

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE SOCIOLOGY 0495 — PAPER 2: FAMILY, EDUCATION AND CRIME**

- **4. Family** — different family structures (nuclear, extended,
  reconstituted, lone parent, empty nest, childless) and variations by
  culture, class and ethnicity; sociological views on family diversity;
  alternatives to the family (single-person and shared households,
  friends as family); variations in marriage; the functionalist,
  Marxist and feminist views of the family's role; how family life is
  changing (demographic factors, industrialisation and urbanisation,
  trends in marriage/divorce/cohabitation, alternatives to marriage,
  and changing conjugal, child and grandparent roles)
- **5. Education** — formal, informal and hidden curricula; different
  types of schools and alternative approaches to education (online
  learning, homeschooling, vocational learning); the functionalist,
  Marxist and feminist views of education's roles and functions;
  patterns and explanations of differences in educational achievement
  by social class, ethnicity, gender and globally
- **6. Crime, deviance and social control** — the difference between
  crime and deviance and their relativity across societies and time;
  formal and informal agencies of social control and their
  effectiveness; different types of crime (violent, property,
  white-collar/corporate, expressive, instrumental, gang, green)

## Family (Topic 4)

Start with the factual base — the six named family structures and how
they vary by culture, class and ethnicity — before moving to the
perspectives. The New Right and Postmodernist views on family diversity
are a common source of confusion: the New Right treats family diversity
as a sign of dysfunction and favours the traditional nuclear family,
while Postmodernists treat diversity positively, as evidence of
individual choice. On the three major perspectives, learn one concrete
mechanism per view — functionalism's "warm bath" theory of emotional
stabilisation, Marxism's family-as-unit-of-consumption argument, and
feminism's joint-versus-segregated conjugal roles and domestic division
of labour — since exam questions reward specific concepts over generic
"the family benefits society" statements. For changing family life,
connect causes to effects directly: falling birth rates and rising life
expectancy explain an ageing population, which in turn explains
emerging roles like the "sandwich generation" and "boomerang children."

## Education (Topic 5)

The hidden curriculum — the norms, values, punctuality and conformity
that schools teach without naming them as lessons — is the concept
Sociology examiners return to most often across this topic, so be able
to give examples beyond the syllabus's own (e.g. how school uniform
policies teach conformity) rather than reciting the definition alone.
As with Family, each perspective on education's role should be learned
with its specific mechanism: functionalism's meritocracy and role
allocation, Marxism's reproduction of class inequality through cultural
capital and the myth of meritocracy, and feminism's patriarchal
curriculum and gendered subject choice. For explanations of
achievement differences (5.3), practise sorting explanations by type —
material (e.g. access to digital technology), cultural (e.g. cultural
capital, linguistic factors) and in-school (e.g. labelling, pro- and
anti-school subcultures) — since exam questions often ask candidates to
categorise or compare these explanation types directly.

## Crime, deviance and social control (Topic 6)

The crime/deviance distinction is foundational and frequently tested:
crime is behaviour that breaks the law, deviance is behaviour that
breaks social norms, and the two overlap but are not identical — some
deviant behaviour is not criminal, and both are relative across
societies, cultures and time periods. Formal social control (police,
courts, armed forces, government, penal system) and informal social
control (family, education, workplace, peer group, media, religion)
should be learned as a paired list, since questions often ask
candidates to compare how effectively each type of agency prevents
crime and deviance for a given scenario. The seven named crime types
(violent, property, white-collar/corporate, expressive, instrumental,
gang, green) give a ready vocabulary for classifying crimes described
in exam stimulus material — practise identifying which type a described
crime belongs to before analysing it further.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the New Right and Postmodernist views on family
  diversity** — one treats diversity as dysfunctional, the other as
  positive.
- **Describing the hidden curriculum only in the syllabus's own words**
  rather than applying it to a concrete school example.
- **Treating crime and deviance as synonyms** instead of explaining
  their overlap and relativity.
- **Answering perspective questions generically** ("Marxists think
  education benefits the rich") without naming the specific mechanism
  — cultural capital, the myth of meritocracy, material factors — that
  the syllabus specifies.

## How to approach it

Because candidates answer only two of Paper 2's three topics, decide
early which two you will prepare in depth rather than spreading equal
revision time across all three — but keep at least a working knowledge
of the third, since Question 1 patterns can vary by series.  Within
whichever topics you choose, build a single three-column table per
topic — functionalist / Marxist / feminist — and fill in each cell with
one specific mechanism rather than a general summary, since this table
transfers directly into exam answers across Family, Education and
(where perspectives are examined) Crime.
