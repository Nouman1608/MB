They cover section 3.4.3 Identification of ions (statements a to f) and the flame test required practical. There are no tiers, so you should be able to attempt all of it. ## Questions

**1.** State the flame test colour given by a compound of:

**(a)** barium **[1]**
**(b)** lithium **[1]**
**(c)** potassium **[1]**

**2.** Describe how to carry out a flame test to identify the metal ion in a small sample of an unknown solid. **[4]**

**3.** A student flame-tests a sample labelled "potassium chloride" and sees only a strong yellow flame. She had used the same wire loop, without cleaning it, for a sodium salt a moment earlier.

**(a)** Suggest why the flame was yellow. **[1]**
**(b)** Give **two** changes to her method that would make the result reliable. **[2]**

**4.** Sodium hydroxide solution is added drop by drop, then in excess, to three solutions.

| Solution | Observation |
|---|---|
| J | blue precipitate, which stays in excess |
| K | white precipitate, which stays in excess |
| L | green precipitate, which stays in excess |

**(a)** Identify the metal ion in J and in L. **[2]**
**(b)** K could contain either of two metal ions. Name both, and describe a test that would decide between them. **[3]**

**5.** Scale scraped from the inside of a kettle is thought to contain a carbonate.

**(a)** Describe a test for carbonate ions, giving the result you would expect. **[3]**
**(b)** Name the white solid that forms in the test. **[1]**

**6.** Separate solutions of lithium iodide, barium chloride and calcium bromide are each acidified with dilute nitric acid. Silver nitrate solution is then added to each.

**(a)** Give the colour of the precipitate in each tube. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why an acid is added before the silver nitrate. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why hydrochloric acid would not be a suitable acid for this test. **[1]**

**7.** A packet of bath salts is labelled as magnesium sulfate.

**(a)** Describe a test to show that sulfate ions are present, and give the result. **[2]**
**(b)** State why dilute hydrochloric acid is added in this test. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why dilute sulfuric acid could not be used in its place. **[1]**

**8.** A solution containing 1.74 g of potassium sulfate, K₂SO₄, is acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid. Excess barium chloride solution is added.

K₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2KCl(aq)

Calculate the mass of barium sulfate precipitate formed. Relative atomic masses: K = 39, S = 32, O = 16, Ba = 137. **[3]**

**9.** A solution contains 0.0250 mol of a sodium halide. Excess silver nitrate solution, acidified with dilute nitric acid, is added. The precipitate is filtered, washed with distilled water and dried. Its mass is 4.70 g.

**(a)** Calculate the relative formula mass of the silver halide. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the halide ion. Relative atomic masses: Ag = 108, Cl = 35.5, Br = 80, I = 127. **[1]**
**(c)** State the colour of the precipitate. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the precipitate is washed before it is dried and weighed. **[1]**

**10.** Two solids, X and Y, are tested. Fresh portions of solution are used for each test.

| Test | X | Y |
|---|---|---|
| Flame test | green | no characteristic colour |
| Sodium hydroxide solution, then excess | no precipitate | white precipitate, dissolves in excess |
| Dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution | cream precipitate | white precipitate |

**(a)** Name solid X and write its formula. **[3]**
**(b)** Name solid Y and write its formula. **[3]**
**(c)** Write a balanced equation, with state symbols, for the reaction when a little sodium hydroxide solution is added to a solution of Y. **[2]**

**11.** A technician has four unlabelled bottles. Each holds one of these white solids: lithium carbonate, calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate, sodium iodide.

**(a)** Describe a series of tests that would identify the contents of each bottle. Give the expected result for each solid. **[6]**
**(b)** 0.555 g of the lithium carbonate reacts completely with excess dilute hydrochloric acid:

Li₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2LiCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

Relative atomic masses: Li = 7, C = 12, O = 16. Take the molar gas volume at room temperature and pressure as 24 dm³. Calculate, in cm³, the volume of gas released. **[3]**

**12.** A sample of well water may contain chloride ions and sulfate ions. A student adds dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride solution to a portion of the water and sees a white precipitate. She then adds dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution to the **same** test tube, sees a further white precipitate, and concludes that chloride ions are present in the water.

**(a)** Explain why her conclusion about chloride ions is not valid. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how she should test the water for chloride ions instead. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Green [1]
**(b)** Crimson [1]
**(c)** Lilac [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use the exact colour words; "red" describes calcium, so write crimson for lithium to make the answer unambiguous.

**2.** Clean a nichrome wire loop: dip it in dilute hydrochloric acid and heat it, repeating until it no longer colours the flame [1]. Dip the loop in the acid, then into the solid so some sticks to it [1]. Hold the loop in the edge of a blue (non-luminous) Bunsen flame [1]. Observe the flame colour and match it to a known colour, for example crimson for lithium [1].
*Examiner insight:* The cleaning step is the point most often missing; say how you check the wire is clean (no colour in the flame), not just "clean it".

**3. (a)** Sodium compound left on the uncleaned wire gave a yellow flame, which hid the pale lilac colour of potassium [1].
**(b)** Clean the wire in acid and heat it until it gives no colour before each test [1]. Use a separate loop for each sample, or repeat the test to check that the colour is the same [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Be more careful" is too vague to credit; each improvement should name a specific action that removes the sodium contamination.

**4. (a)** J: copper(II), Cu²⁺ [1]. L: iron(II), Fe²⁺ [1].
**(b)** Calcium or magnesium ions [1]. Carry out a flame test on the solid [1]: a red flame shows calcium; no characteristic colour shows magnesium [1].
*Examiner insight:* Aluminium is ruled out because its precipitate would dissolve; more sodium hydroxide cannot separate the other two.

**5. (a)** Add dilute hydrochloric acid to the scale [1]. Bubbles of gas (fizzing) are seen [1]. Pass the gas through limewater: it turns cloudy white, showing carbon dioxide [1].
**(b)** Calcium carbonate [1].
*Examiner insight:* The limewater result is what proves the gas is carbon dioxide; "it fizzes" on its own does not identify a carbonate.

**6. (a)** Lithium iodide: yellow [1]. Barium chloride: white [1]. Calcium bromide: cream [1].
**(b)** Carbonate ions would react with silver ions too and give a misleading precipitate; the acid destroys them first [1].
**(c)** Hydrochloric acid brings in its own chloride ions, so white silver chloride would appear whatever the sample contained [1].
*Examiner insight:* The precipitate colour depends only on the halide ion; the metal in each compound is a distractor and does not change the answer.

**7. (a)** Dissolve some bath salts, add dilute hydrochloric acid, followed by barium chloride solution [1]. Result: a white precipitate [1].
**(b)** It reacts with any carbonate ions, which could otherwise form white barium carbonate and be mistaken for a positive result [1].
**(c)** Sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions, so a white precipitate would form even if the bath salts had none [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name both reagents in the right order; a description that gives barium chloride alone leaves out the acid that makes the result trustworthy.

**8.** Mr of K₂SO₄ = 2(39) + 32 + 4(16) = 174, so moles = 1.74 ÷ 174 = 0.0100 mol [1]. The mole ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.0100 mol of BaSO₄ forms; Mr of BaSO₄ = 137 + 32 + 64 = 233 [1]. Mass = 0.0100 × 233 = **2.33 g** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Write the 1 : 1 ratio from the equation; set-out working is far easier to credit than a bare number.

**9. (a)** Mr = mass ÷ moles [1] = 4.70 ÷ 0.0250 = **188** [1].
**(b)** 188 − 108 = 80, which is bromine, so the ion is **bromide, Br⁻** [1].
**(c)** Cream [1].
**(d)** To remove soluble substances (such as sodium nitrate and excess silver nitrate) that would add to the mass [1].
*Examiner insight:* State that one mole of sodium halide gives one mole of silver halide; that is why the precipitate is 0.0250 mol.

**10. (a)** Green flame: barium ion, Ba²⁺ [1]. Cream precipitate: bromide ion, Br⁻ [1]. **Barium bromide, BaBr₂** [1].
**(b)** White hydroxide that dissolves in excess: aluminium ion, Al³⁺ [1]. White silver halide: chloride ion, Cl⁻ [1]. **Aluminium chloride, AlCl₃** [1].
**(c)** AlCl₃(aq) + 3NaOH(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s) + 3NaCl(aq): correct formulae [1]; balanced with correct state symbols [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the evidence for each ion as well as the name; the formula must balance the charges, so BaBr and AlCl are not acceptable.

**11. (a)** One valid plan (other correct plans gain credit):
Flame test on each solid. Crimson flame: lithium carbonate [1]. Red flame: calcium chloride [1]. Yellow flame: sodium iodide [1]. No characteristic colour: magnesium sulfate [1].
Confirm the negative ions on fresh portions: lithium carbonate fizzes with dilute acid and the gas turns limewater cloudy white [1]; magnesium sulfate solution, acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid, turns cloudy white when barium chloride is added, or sodium iodide gives a yellow precipitate with dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate [1].
**(b)** Mr of Li₂CO₃ = 2(7) + 12 + 3(16) = 74; moles = 0.555 ÷ 74 = 0.00750 mol [1]. Moles of CO₂ = 0.00750 mol (1 : 1 ratio) [1]. Volume = 0.00750 × 24 000 = **180 cm³** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In a planning answer, tie every test to a named bottle and its expected result; a bare list of tests identifies nothing.

**12. (a)** The barium chloride and hydrochloric acid she added both contain chloride ions [1]. These chloride ions would form a white silver chloride precipitate even if the water had none [1].
**(b)** Take a fresh portion of the water [1]. Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution; a white precipitate shows chloride ions [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Use a new sample" is only half the answer; a full answer also gives the correct reagents, in order, and the expected observation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "red" for lithium or "crimson" for calcium.
- Leaving out how the flame test wire is cleaned and checked.
- Calling every white hydroxide precipitate "aluminium" without the excess sodium hydroxide result.
- Using hydrochloric acid with silver nitrate, or sulfuric acid with barium chloride.
- Running a halide test on a portion that already contains chloride from earlier reagents.
- Formulae with unbalanced charges when naming the identified compound.
