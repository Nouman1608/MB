Section 10.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *The impact of the media on
behaviour*, closes heading 10 of Paper 4. The syllabus lists four points: arguments and evidence
about the extent to which violent media leads to violent behaviour; the impact of the media on
crime, including deviance amplification and moral panics; ways in which the media might have a
positive impact on human behaviour; and ways in which people may be affected by media
sensationalism and stereotyping. Heading 10 links this to studies that illustrate the impact of the
media, so named research is essential.

The [media representation and media effects guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-representation-and-effects/)
introduces the main studies. This page adds further evidence, explains the mechanisms proposed and
shows how to weigh research methods. Use it with the

## Does violent media lead to violent behaviour?

### Proposed mechanisms

| Mechanism | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Imitation** | People, especially children, copy violent acts they see |
| **Desensitisation** | Repeated exposure makes violence seem normal and reduces emotional response to it |
| **Catharsis** | Watching violence releases aggressive feelings, so it may *reduce* violence |
| **Sensitisation** | Seeing the consequences of violence may make viewers more opposed to it |
| **Fear** | Heavy exposure makes people more afraid rather than more aggressive (Gerbner) |

### Evidence suggesting a link

- **William Belson's *Television Violence and the Adolescent Boy* (1978)** interviewed a large
  sample of London boys and found that those who had watched more television violence reported
  committing more serious violent acts. It is a survey of correlation, however, and depends on
  boys' honest recall.
- **Laboratory experiments** following Bandura showed short-term imitation of aggression against
  toys.
- **Video games** have been the focus of more recent research on whether interactive violence has
  stronger effects than watching.

### Evidence against a simple link

- **Seymour Feshbach and Robert Singer (1971)** studied boys in residential institutions and found
  that those shown a diet of aggressive television were *less* aggressive than those shown
  non-violent programmes, which they interpreted as catharsis.
- **Ann Hagell and Tim Newburn's *Young Offenders and the Media* (1994)** found that young
  offenders showed no particular preference for violent programmes and had less access to
  television and video at home than other young people.
- **Natural experiments** such as the arrival of television on St Helena (covered in the overview
  page) found no rise in anti-social behaviour.

### Why the debate is hard to settle

- **Defining violence:** cartoon slapstick, news footage and realistic drama differ.
- **Direction of cause:** aggressive people may choose violent media.
- **Artificiality:** laboratory aggression against toys is not real-world violence.
- **Time scale:** short-term arousal is easier to measure than long-term change.
- **Other variables:** family, peers, poverty and neighbourhood all affect violence.

### Moral panics about violent media

Concern about violent media is itself a sociological topic. The UK panic about "video nasties" in
the early 1980s contributed to the Video Recordings Act 1984, which introduced certification of
videos. Martin Barker's edited collection *The Video Nasties* (1984) argued that the campaign
rested on very little evidence of harm.

## The impact of the media on crime

### Over-representation and news values for crime

Media coverage gives a distorted picture of crime. Marcus Felson (*Crime and Everyday Life*, 1998)
called the belief that crime is mostly dramatic and violent the **dramatic fallacy**: most real
crime is minor property crime. Yvonne Jewkes (*Media and Crime*, 2004) identified news values that
shape crime reporting, including **threshold** (a crime must be serious enough to report),
**predictability**, **simplification**, **individualism**, **risk**, **sex**, **celebrity**,
**proximity**, **violence**, **spectacle** and **children**. Coverage therefore over-represents
violent, sexual and unusual crimes and individual offenders over social causes.

### Deviance amplification

Leslie Wilkins described the **deviance amplification spiral**, which can be set out as steps:

1. An initial act of deviance is reported in exaggerated terms.
2. The public become concerned and the group is labelled.
3. Police and authorities respond with tighter control.
4. The group feels persecuted, becomes more isolated and its deviance increases.
5. Increased deviance is reported, confirming the original fears.

Jock Young's study of drug users in Notting Hill (*The Drugtakers*, 1971) shows the spiral in
practice: police attention made drug use more central to the group's identity.

### Other ways the media may affect crime

- **Copycat crime:** offenders imitating methods reported in the media.
- **Fear of crime:** heavy media exposure linked to exaggerated fear of victimisation.
- **Glamorisation:** representations of criminal lifestyles as exciting or profitable.
- **Relative deprivation:** left realists such as Jock Young argued that media images of wealth and
  consumption increase feelings of deprivation, which can motivate crime.
- **Moral panics:** shape policing priorities and new laws.

## Positive impacts on behaviour

- **Educational programmes:** *Sesame Street*, launched in the USA in 1969, was designed to support
  early literacy and numeracy for disadvantaged children and has been widely evaluated.
- **Public health and safety campaigns:** campaigns on drink-driving, smoking or vaccination aim to
  change behaviour directly.
- **Charity and solidarity:** Live Aid (1985) used global broadcasting to raise money for famine
  relief in Ethiopia; social media challenges such as the Ice Bucket Challenge (2014) raised funds
  for motor neurone disease research.
- **Social movements:** #BlackLivesMatter, which began in 2013, shows media being used to mobilise
  campaigns against injustice.
- **Pro-social modelling:** representations of tolerance, cooperation and diversity may encourage
  empathy and reduce prejudice, the reverse of the imitation mechanism.

## Sensationalism and stereotyping

- **Sensationalism** exaggerates rare dangers, raising fear and supporting harsher responses.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy:** groups stereotyped as deviant may be treated with suspicion by
  police, employers and teachers, and may come to act in line with the label.
- **Stereotype threat:** psychologists Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995) found that people
  reminded of a negative stereotype about their group can perform worse on tests, suggesting that
  stereotypes affect behaviour directly.
- **Body image:** Anne Becker's research in Fiji, where television became widely available in the
  mid-1990s, found increased concern with weight and disordered eating attitudes among adolescent
  girls in the years after its arrival, which the girls themselves linked to images of Western
  television characters.
- **Prejudice:** sensational coverage of crimes by members of a minority can increase hostility
  towards the whole group.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that violent media cause violent behaviour.*

> Belson (1978) found that boys who watched more television violence reported more serious violent
> acts, which supports the view. **[AO1]** However, his survey shows correlation: boys who were
> already aggressive may have chosen more violent programmes. **[AO3: methodological evaluation]**
> Hagell and Newburn (1994) found that young offenders had no special taste for violent
> programmes, and Feshbach and Singer (1971) even found reduced aggression after violent
> viewing. **[AO3: counter-evidence]** The evidence therefore suggests that violent media may
> affect some individuals in some contexts, but cannot be said to cause violence in general.
> **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Treating Bandura as proof of real-world violence.
- Ignoring the methods behind each study; evaluation of 10.3 is largely methodological.
- Confusing a moral panic (the reaction) with deviance amplification (the spiral it can cause).
- Writing nothing on positive effects, which the syllabus names.
- Discussing stereotyping without showing how it affects behaviour.

## Exam technique

- For **explain** questions, name the mechanism (imitation, desensitisation, amplification) and
  develop it with a study.
- For **evaluate** questions, set studies supporting effects against those that do not, then weigh
  their methods.
- Link to 10.2: Belson and Bandura fit direct-effects thinking; Hagell and Newburn and the St
  Helena study support indirect models; Becker's Fiji research supports cultural effects.
