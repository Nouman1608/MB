Only **adult** haemoglobin is named, so you need one curve and how it moves.

## Haemoglobin and oxygen

Haemoglobin has four polypeptide chains, each with a **haem group** containing an iron ion. Each haem binds one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin carries up to **four** oxygen molecules. The binding is reversible:

```
Hb + 4O₂ ⇌ HbO₈ (oxyhaemoglobin)
```

**Percentage saturation** is the proportion of haemoglobin's binding sites occupied by oxygen. **Partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂)**, in kPa, measures how much oxygen is available; it is high in the alveoli and low in respiring tissue.

## The oxygen dissociation curve

The table gives values of the kind you would read from a curve for adult haemoglobin. They are rounded for practice.

| pO₂ / kPa | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturation / % | 8 | 25 | 45 | 60 | 72 | 80 | 90 | 95 | 98 |

### Describing it

The curve is **S-shaped (sigmoid)**: shallow at low pO₂, very steep between about 2 and 6 kPa, then levelling off close to 100 % at high pO₂.

### Explaining it

- At very low pO₂, it is **difficult for the first oxygen molecule to bind**, so saturation rises slowly.
- Binding of the first oxygen **changes the shape** (tertiary and quaternary structure) of the haemoglobin so that the other haem groups bind oxygen **more easily**. This is why the middle section is steep: a small rise in pO₂ gives a large rise in saturation.
- At high pO₂, most sites are already full, so there are few left to fill and the curve **flattens**.

"Describe" wants the shape; "explain" wants the changing affinity. Put both in when the question says "describe and explain".

## Why the shape matters (outcome 8.2.5)

**In the lungs**, pO₂ is high (about 13 kPa in this table). The curve is flat there, so haemoglobin is almost fully saturated, and even if pO₂ falls a little it still loads nearly fully.

**In respiring tissues**, pO₂ is low and lies on the **steep** part. A small drop in pO₂, caused by faster respiration, causes a large fall in saturation, so a lot of extra oxygen is released exactly where it is being used.

### Worked example 1: oxygen delivered at rest and in exercise

Use the table. Assume that fully saturated blood carries 20 cm³ of oxygen per 100 cm³ of blood (a value given for this example). Blood leaves the lungs at 13 kPa. Muscle pO₂ is 5 kPa at rest and 2 kPa during exercise.

```
leaving lungs: 98 % saturated → 0.98 × 20 = 19.6 cm³
at rest (5 kPa): 72 % → 0.72 × 20 = 14.4 cm³ still bound
   released = 19.6 − 14.4 = 5.2 cm³ per 100 cm³
in exercise (2 kPa): 25 % → 0.25 × 20 = 5.0 cm³ still bound
   released = 19.6 − 5.0 = 14.6 cm³ per 100 cm³
ratio = 14.6 ÷ 5.2 = 2.8
```

A fall of only 3 kPa in the muscle releases almost three times as much oxygen from each 100 cm³ of blood. That is the importance of the steep section.

## Carbon dioxide transport

### In the red blood cell (respiring tissue)

1. Carbon dioxide diffuses from respiring cells, through the plasma, into red blood cells.
2. **Carbonic anhydrase** catalyses its combination with water: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃. Without the enzyme this reaction is far too slow.
3. Carbonic acid dissociates: H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
4. The H⁺ combine with haemoglobin to form **haemoglobinic acid (HHb)**. This has two effects: it makes oxyhaemoglobin give up its oxygen, and it removes free H⁺ so the pH of the blood does not fall far (haemoglobin acts as a buffer).
5. Some carbon dioxide combines directly with the amine groups of haemoglobin, forming **carbaminohaemoglobin**.

### The chloride shift

Hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) diffuse out of the red blood cell into the plasma, through a transport protein in the membrane. To keep the charge balanced, **chloride ions (Cl⁻) diffuse in** from the plasma.

**Importance**: it keeps the inside of the cell electrically neutral while HCO₃⁻ leaves, so HCO₃⁻ can **keep moving out**. HCO₃⁻ does not build up inside the cell, so carbonic anhydrase can keep converting carbon dioxide, and the cell can keep taking up carbon dioxide from the tissues.

### The role of plasma

- Most carbon dioxide is carried in the plasma as **hydrogencarbonate ions** in solution, having been made in the red blood cells.
- A small amount travels as carbon dioxide **dissolved** directly in the plasma.

### In the lungs, everything reverses

pCO₂ in the alveoli is low, so carbon dioxide diffuses out of the plasma. HCO₃⁻ re-enter red blood cells (and Cl⁻ leave), combine with H⁺ released from haemoglobinic acid, and carbonic anhydrase converts the carbonic acid back to carbon dioxide and water. Carbaminohaemoglobin also releases its carbon dioxide. With the H⁺ removed, haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen rises and it loads oxygen.

## The Bohr shift

**Describe**: at higher partial pressures of carbon dioxide, the dissociation curve moves **to the right**. At any pO₂, haemoglobin is less saturated.

**Explain**: more carbon dioxide gives more H⁺ (via carbonic anhydrase). H⁺ bind to haemoglobin to form haemoglobinic acid, which changes its shape and **reduces its affinity for oxygen**, so oxygen is released.

**Importance**: in actively respiring tissue, where carbon dioxide is high, haemoglobin unloads **more** oxygen than it would otherwise. In the lungs, where carbon dioxide is low, the curve is to the left, so haemoglobin loads oxygen readily.

### Worked example 2: how much of the carried oxygen is released?

At a tissue pO₂ of 4 kPa, saturation is 60 % at normal pCO₂ and 46 % at high pCO₂. Blood arrives 98 % saturated. Calculate the percentage of the oxygen carried that is released in each case.

```
normal:  (98 − 60) ÷ 98 × 100 = 38.8 %
high CO₂: (98 − 46) ÷ 98 × 100 = 53.1 %
```

The extra carbon dioxide from respiration makes the blood give up over half of its oxygen rather than under two-fifths. This is the kind of figure that earns the "importance" mark when the question supplies data.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how carbon dioxide produced in a respiring muscle increases the oxygen released by haemoglobin.* **[4]**

> Carbon dioxide diffuses into red blood cells and carbonic anhydrase catalyses its reaction with water to form carbonic acid **(1)**. This dissociates into H⁺ and hydrogencarbonate ions **(1)**. H⁺ combine with haemoglobin to form haemoglobinic acid, which reduces haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen **(1)**, so oxyhaemoglobin dissociates and more oxygen is released to the muscle cells: the Bohr shift **(1)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying carbon dioxide "binds to the haem group". Carbaminohaemoglobin forms with **amine groups** of the protein, not the iron.
- Describing the Bohr shift as "more carbon dioxide binds, so less oxygen binds".
- Saying the chloride shift moves Cl⁻ out of the red blood cell in tissues.
- Writing "the curve shifts down" instead of "to the right".
- Explaining the S-shape without the idea of changing affinity after the first oxygen binds.
- Forgetting that most carbon dioxide is carried as HCO₃⁻ in the plasma.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** (curve or Bohr shift): shape and direction, with values if data are given.
- **Explain** (curve): the change in affinity as each oxygen binds.
- **Explain the importance**: link the curve position or shape to loading in the lungs and unloading in tissues, with a consequence for the cells.
- **Calculate**: read values carefully from the axes and show each subtraction.
