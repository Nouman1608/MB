This guide covers subtopic **13.3, Shapes of organic molecules; σ and π bonds**, from Topic 13 of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The
broader [Introduction to Organic Chemistry study guide](/resources/organic-mechanisms-an-introduction/)
gives the one-paragraph summary; this page works through the four learning outcomes in detail, with
examples you can apply to any molecule an examiner gives you.

## What the syllabus asks for

The four outcomes in 13.3 are:

1. describe organic molecules as straight-chained, branched or cyclic
2. describe and explain the shape of, and bond angles in, molecules containing sp, sp² and sp³
   hybridised atoms
3. describe the arrangement of σ and π bonds in molecules containing sp, sp² and sp³ hybridised atoms
4. understand and use the term **planar**, for example for ethene

Everything here builds on the shapes-of-molecules work in
[Chemical Bonding: Shapes and Intermolecular Forces](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-shapes-and-intermolecular-forces/).
The new step is applying it atom by atom along a carbon chain.

## Straight-chained, branched and cyclic

- A **straight-chained** molecule has every carbon joined in one continuous chain with no carbon
  bonded to more than two other carbons, as in hexane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃.
- A **branched** molecule has at least one carbon bonded to three or four other carbons, so a side
  chain comes off the main chain, as in 2-methylpentane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃.
- A **cyclic** molecule has carbons joined in a ring, as in cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, or cyclohexene, C₆H₁₀.

"Straight" describes how the atoms are connected, not the real shape. Each carbon in hexane is
tetrahedral, so the chain is actually a zig-zag; that is why skeletal formulae are drawn as zig-zag
lines. Note also that a cyclic alkane has two fewer hydrogens than the open-chain alkane with the same
number of carbons: cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, has the same molecular formula as hex-1-ene.

## Hybridisation: where the shapes come from

A carbon atom has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p². To form four bonds, one 2s electron is promoted to the
empty 2p orbital, giving four unpaired electrons. The 2s orbital then mixes (hybridises) with some or all
of the 2p orbitals. The number of p orbitals used decides the shape.

| Hybridisation | Orbitals mixed | Hybrid orbitals | Unhybridised p orbitals | Shape around the atom | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sp³ | 2s + three 2p | four sp³ | none | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| sp² | 2s + two 2p | three sp² | one | trigonal planar | 120° |
| sp | 2s + one 2p | two sp | two | linear | 180° |

The hybrid orbitals repel each other and spread out as far apart as possible, which is the same idea as
electron-pair repulsion: four regions give a tetrahedron, three give a triangle, two give a line.

**A quick way to assign hybridisation to a carbon:** count the atoms bonded to it.

- four atoms bonded (only single bonds): **sp³**
- three atoms bonded (one double bond): **sp²**
- two atoms bonded (one triple bond, or two double bonds): **sp**

The same idea works for other atoms. In a nitrile, the nitrogen of C≡N is sp. In a carbonyl group,
the carbon of C=O is sp², so the carbonyl carbon and the three atoms attached to it lie in one plane at
about 120°.

## σ and π bonds

A **σ (sigma) bond** forms by end-on (head-on) overlap of two orbitals, so the electron density lies
along the line joining the two nuclei. Every single bond is a σ bond. Overlap of hybrid orbitals with
each other, or with hydrogen 1s orbitals, always gives σ bonds.

A **π (pi) bond** forms by sideways overlap of two parallel unhybridised p orbitals on neighbouring
atoms. The electron density lies in two regions, one above and one below the line joining the nuclei,
with none along the line itself.

| Bond | σ bonds | π bonds |
|---|---|---|
| single, C–C | 1 | 0 |
| double, C=C or C=O | 1 | 1 |
| triple, C≡C or C≡N | 1 | 2 |

In a triple bond the two π bonds come from the two unhybridised p orbitals on each sp atom; they lie at
right angles to each other, so the electron density forms a cylinder around the σ bond.

**Why the π bond matters for reactivity.** Sideways overlap is less effective than end-on overlap, so a
π bond is weaker than a σ bond. The data booklet gives average bond energies of 350 kJ mol⁻¹ for C–C and
610 kJ mol⁻¹ for C=C. The double bond is therefore not twice as strong as a single bond: the π bond adds
only about 610 − 350 = 260 kJ mol⁻¹. The π electrons are also exposed above and below the plane, so
electrophiles attack them. This is why alkenes undergo addition while alkanes are unreactive (see the
[Alkenes study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-alkenes/)).

## Planar molecules

A molecule (or part of a molecule) is **planar** when all of its atoms lie in a single flat plane.

**Ethene, C₂H₄.** Both carbons are sp². Each carbon uses its three sp² orbitals to make σ bonds to two
hydrogens and to the other carbon, all at 120° in one plane. The unhybridised p orbitals stand
perpendicular to that plane and overlap sideways to make the π bond. The p orbitals can only overlap
when they are parallel, which forces both CH₂ ends into the same plane. So all six atoms are coplanar.

Planarity does not spread across sp³ atoms. In propene, CH₂=CH–CH₃, the two sp² carbons, the three
hydrogens on them and the carbon of the CH₃ group all lie in one plane, but the CH₃ carbon is sp³, so
at least two of its hydrogens must sit out of that plane. Propene as a whole is **not** planar.

**Ethyne, C₂H₂.** Both carbons are sp, so H–C≡C–H is linear: all four atoms lie on one straight line.

## Worked example 1: assigning hybridisation, angles and bonds

**Question.** Propenenitrile has the structure CH₂=CH–C≡N.
(a) State the hybridisation of each carbon atom. (b) Give the bond angles around each carbon.
(c) Count the σ and π bonds. (d) State whether the molecule is planar.

**Answer.**

(a) Label the carbons C1 (CH₂), C2 (CH) and C3 (the C of C≡N). C1 is bonded to three atoms (two H and
C2), so it is **sp²**. C2 is bonded to three atoms (H, C1 and C3), so it is **sp²**. C3 is bonded to
two atoms (C2 and N), so it is **sp**.

(b) Angles around C1 and C2 are about **120°**; the C2–C3–N angle is **180°**.

(c) Bonds: two C1–H, one C1=C2, one C2–H, one C2–C3, one C3≡N. Every bond contains one σ bond, so there
are 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = **6 σ bonds**. The C=C contributes one π bond and the C≡N contributes two, so
there are **3 π bonds**.

(d) **Yes.** The two sp² carbons hold their attached atoms in one plane, and the linear C–C≡N unit
points along a line that lies in that same plane. No sp³ atom is present to push atoms out of the
plane.

## Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain why all the atoms in ethene lie in the same plane. [3]

**Model answer.** Each carbon atom is sp² hybridised and forms three σ bonds at 120° in a trigonal
planar arrangement **[1]**. Each carbon has one unhybridised p orbital at right angles to this plane,
and these overlap sideways to form the π bond **[1]**. Sideways overlap is only possible when the two p
orbitals are parallel, so the two CH₂ groups are held in the same plane **[1]**.

*Why it scores:* the answer names the hybridisation, links the 120° σ framework to the trigonal planar
shape, and explains the role of the π bond. Simply writing "because it has a double bond" earns nothing.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying a double bond is two σ bonds.** It is one σ and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ and two π.
- **Forgetting the C–H bonds when counting σ bonds.** Count every line in the displayed formula.
- **Calling a whole molecule planar because it contains C=C.** Only the C=C carbons and the four atoms
  bonded to them are forced into a plane; any sp³ carbon breaks planarity.
- **Drawing the π bond between the nuclei.** The π electron density is above and below the σ bond axis.
- **Giving 109° or 107° for a carbon with a double bond.** An sp² carbon is 120°; the 107° and 104.5°
  values belong to atoms with lone pairs, such as N in an amine or O in an alcohol.
- **Treating "straight-chained" as literally straight.** sp³ carbons give a zig-zag chain.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the shape: name it and give the angle, for example "trigonal planar, 120°".
- **Explain** the shape: add the reason, the number of hybrid orbitals or bonding regions and their
  repulsion, and for planarity, the parallel p orbitals.
- **State** or **Identify** hybridisation: one word per atom (sp, sp² or sp³) is enough; do not pad.
- **Deduce** the number of σ and π bonds: show the count by bond type so a single slip still earns
  method credit.
