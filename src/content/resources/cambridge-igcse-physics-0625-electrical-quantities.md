This guide covers one subtopic of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **4.2 Electrical quantities**, in five parts: electric charge, current, e.m.f. and p.d., resistance, and electrical energy and power. Core content is for every candidate; content marked **Extended only** is Supplement material, examined on Papers 2 and 4. The [Electricity and magnetism study guide](/resources/igcse-physics-0625-electricity-and-magnetism/) describes the charging, conductor-testing and resistance experiments; this page concentrates on how the quantities connect and on choosing the right equation.

## The quantities and how they connect

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charge | Q | coulomb, C | Amount of electricity carried by particles such as electrons |
| Current | I | ampere, A | Rate of flow of charge |
| e.m.f. | E | volt, V | Work done by a source moving unit charge round a complete circuit |
| p.d. | V | volt, V | Work done by unit charge passing through a component |
| Resistance | R | ohm, Ω | How much a component opposes current; R = V/I |
| Power | P | watt, W | Energy transferred per second; P = IV |
| Energy | E | joule, J (or kW h) | E = IVt |

One way to keep them straight: **current** says how much charge passes each second; **p.d.** says how much energy each coulomb transfers; multiply them and you get energy per second, which is **power**. That is why P = IV, and why E = IVt.

**Extended only.** The links are written as equations: I = Q/t (so Q = It), and E = W/Q for a source, V = W/Q for a component (so W = QV). One volt is one joule per coulomb.

## Charge and electric fields

Charges are positive or negative; like charges repel and unlike charges attract. When an insulator is rubbed, **only electrons move**: the material that gains electrons becomes negative, the one that loses them becomes positive. Positive charges never move in this process. In metals, some electrons are **free** to move between the atoms, which makes metals conductors; in insulators the electrons are held in place.

**Electric fields (Extended only).** An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. Its direction at a point is the direction of the force on a **positive** charge there. Three patterns to draw:

- **Isolated positive point charge:** straight lines pointing radially **outwards**. (For a negative point charge, radially inwards.)
- **Charged conducting sphere:** the same radial pattern, starting at the surface, at right angles to it.
- **Two oppositely charged parallel plates:** straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines from the positive plate to the negative plate (end effects are not examined).

## Current, ammeters and voltmeters

Current in a metal is a flow of free electrons. **Direct current (d.c.)** flows one way only; **alternating current (a.c.)** repeatedly reverses. **Extended only:** conventional current is drawn from + to −, although the electrons actually move from − to +.

An ammeter goes **in series**; a voltmeter goes **in parallel** across the component. On a multi-range meter, start on the highest range and switch down until the reading uses as much of the scale (or as many digits) as possible without overloading. A reading of 0.25 A on a 0–10 A range is hard to read precisely; the same current on a 0–1 A range is read far more precisely.

## Resistance

R = V/I. Rearranged: V = IR and I = V/R. A lamp with 6.0 V across it and 0.50 A through it has R = 6.0 / 0.50 = **12 Ω**. If a resistance is given in kΩ, convert first: 2.2 kΩ = 2200 Ω.

For a metal wire at constant temperature, a **longer** wire has more resistance and a **thicker** wire (larger cross-sectional area) has less. **Extended only:** R is directly proportional to length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.

### Worked example 1 (Extended)

2.0 m of a wire with cross-sectional area 0.50 mm² has a resistance of 3.4 Ω. Find the resistance of 5.0 m of the same metal with area 1.0 mm².

```
length × (5.0 / 2.0):   3.4 × 2.5 = 8.5 Ω
area × (1.0 / 0.50):    8.5 ÷ 2   = 4.25 Ω  →  4.3 Ω
```

Treat each change as a separate scale factor; it avoids mixing up "proportional" and "inversely proportional".

**I–V graphs (Extended only).** The key is what the **gradient** tells you. With I on the vertical axis, a steeper line means a smaller resistance.

- **Resistor at constant temperature:** straight line through the origin, same in both directions; R constant (I directly proportional to V).
- **Filament lamp:** curve through the origin that becomes **less steep** at higher p.d. As the current rises, the filament gets hotter and its resistance increases. The curve has the same shape for negative p.d.
- **Diode:** almost no current for any reverse p.d. (very high resistance); in the forward direction, almost no current until a small threshold p.d., then the current rises steeply (low resistance).

When asked to **explain** the lamp's curve, you need both points: the temperature of the filament increases, and therefore its resistance increases.

## Electrical energy and power

A circuit transfers energy **from** the source (a cell or the mains) **to** the components and then to the **surroundings**, for example by heating and light.

- P = IV (watts)
- E = IVt (joules, with t in seconds)
- One **kilowatt-hour** is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour. For cost: energy (kW h) = power (kW) × time (h); cost = energy × price per kW h.

### Worked example 2

A 2.2 kW kettle runs on a 230 V supply for 3.0 minutes. Electricity costs 28 cents per kW h. Find the current, the energy in joules, the energy in kW h and the cost.

```
I = P / V = 2200 / 230 = 9.6 A
E = P t = 2200 × (3.0 × 60) = 396 000 J  (4.0 × 10⁵ J)
energy in kW h = 2.2 kW × (3.0 / 60) h = 0.11 kW h
cost = 0.11 × 28 = 3.1 cents
```

Two unit systems appear here. For joules use watts and seconds; for kW h use kilowatts and hours. Mixing them (kW × seconds) is the most common error in cost questions.

## Common mistakes

- Saying positive charges move when a rod is charged by rubbing.
- Defining p.d. as "the voltage across a component" (a circular definition): use work done per unit charge.
- Leaving minutes or hours in E = IVt when joules are needed.
- Treating "proportional to area" as direct rather than inverse.
- Saying the filament lamp's line curves because "resistance changes", without saying it increases due to heating.
- Drawing electric field arrows pointing towards a positive charge.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** use the syllabus wording, for example "e.m.f. is the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit".
- **Calculate:** write the equation in symbols, substitute with units converted, and give the unit.
- **Sketch** an I–V graph: label axes with quantities and units, pass through the origin, and show the correct shape for negative values.
- **Explain** a graph: link the gradient to resistance and give the physical reason.
- **Describe** an experiment: include the circuit (ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel), how readings are varied, and the calculation.
