assessment 2026). The [1.3 Sustainability study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-1-3/)

subtopic but not its learning outcomes, so check the scope with your teacher.

## Condensed recall

**Definitions**
- Sustainability: use of resources that allows natural replacement and the long-term survival of
  the system.
- Sustainable development (Brundtland, 1987): meeting present needs without compromising future
  generations' ability to meet theirs.
- Three pillars: environmental, social, economic. All three must hold.

**Natural capital and income**
- Natural capital = the stock (forest, aquifer, fish stock, soil).
- Natural income = the yield that can be taken without reducing the stock.
- Sustainable use: harvest ≤ natural income.

**Ecosystem services**
- Provisioning (food, timber, water), regulating (flood control, pollination, climate), cultural
  (recreation, spiritual), supporting (soil formation, nutrient cycling).

**Ecological footprint**
- Components: carbon, cropland, grazing land, forest products, fishing grounds, built-up land.
- EF > biocapacity → deficit; EF < biocapacity → reserve.
- EF ÷ biocapacity = how many times the country's own capacity is used.
- Limits: averages; omits toxins and biodiversity; assumption-dependent; imports shift impact.

**Carbon footprint**
- Total emissions in t CO₂e; activity × emission factor, summed.
- Direct (fuel, electricity) and indirect (goods, food, services).

**Water footprint**
- Green (soil rainwater), blue (withdrawn surface or groundwater), grey (dilution of pollutants).

**Frameworks**
- Planetary boundaries: nine processes (Rockström and colleagues, 2009); six assessed as crossed in a
  2023 update.
- Doughnut (Raworth): social foundation inside, ecological ceiling outside; Amsterdam strategy
  (2020).
- SDGs: 17 goals adopted 2015, targets for 2030; goals can conflict.
- Circular economy: design out waste; reuse, repair, remanufacture before recycling; regenerate
  nature.

**Environmental justice**
- Fair distribution of environmental benefits and harms, and fair participation in decisions.

**EIA sequence**
- Screening → scoping → baseline → prediction → mitigation → report and consultation → decision →
  monitoring.
- Limits: short baselines, uncertain predictions, developer-funded, cumulative impacts missed,
  economic interests may dominate.

## Method in steps: footprint questions

```
1. Check whether figures are per person or national totals.
2. Add components to get EF (gha per person).
3. Deficit or reserve = EF − biocapacity (state which).
4. Total = per-person value × population.
5. Ratio = EF ÷ biocapacity ("uses x times its own capacity").
6. Largest component = the most effective target for policy.
7. Interpret: imports, averages and missing impacts.
```

Mini example (invented): EF 2.0 gha per person, biocapacity 2.5 gha per person, population 4 million.
Reserve = 0.5 gha per person, or 2.0 million gha in total; ratio = 2.0 ÷ 2.5 = 0.8. The country uses
80% of its own capacity, but check whether it imports large amounts of food or goods before calling
it sustainable.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural capital | Natural resources that can produce a flow of goods and services |
| Natural income | The yield from natural capital that can be used sustainably |
| Biocapacity | Capacity of an area to provide resources and absorb wastes |
| Ecological footprint | Area of productive land and water needed to support a population |
| Global hectare (gha) | Hectare with world-average biological productivity |
| Ecological deficit | Footprint exceeds biocapacity |
| Carbon footprint | Total greenhouse gas emissions caused, in t CO₂e |
| Water footprint | Total fresh water used to produce goods and services consumed |
| Planetary boundaries | Limits on nine Earth-system processes defining a safe operating space |
| Doughnut economics | Model combining a social foundation with an ecological ceiling |
| Circular economy | Economy that keeps materials in use and designs out waste |
| Environmental justice | Fair sharing of environmental harms, benefits and decision-making |
| EIA | Assessment of a project's likely environmental impacts before approval |
| Mitigation (EIA) | Measures to avoid, reduce or offset predicted impacts |

## Exam traps

- **Per person versus total.** Do not compare a national total EF with biocapacity per person.
- **Units every time.** gha, gha per person, t CO₂e, m³.
- **Low footprint is not proof of sustainability.** Imports may move impacts abroad.
- **Recycling is not the whole circular economy.** Reuse and repair keep more value.
- **Name the pillar.** "It is unsustainable" needs which pillar fails and why.
- **EIA evaluation.** Stages alone describe; strengths and limits evaluate.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish sustainability from sustainable development.
2. A fish stock grows by 900 t a year and 1,200 t are caught. Is this sustainable? Why?
3. Name four components of an ecological footprint.
4. Country S: EF 3.0 gha per person, biocapacity 4.5 gha per person. Deficit or reserve, and by how
   much?
5. Give two limitations of the ecological footprint.
6. A car travels 5,000 km at 0.2 kg CO₂e per km. Calculate its carbon footprint in tonnes.
7. Distinguish blue water from green water.
8. What are the two rings of the doughnut model?
9. How many SDGs are there, and when were they adopted?
10. Give one conflict between two SDGs.
11. List the first three stages of an EIA.
12. Define environmental justice.

### Answers

1. Sustainability is a property of a system using resources within natural replacement;
   sustainable development is development meeting present needs without compromising future
   generations.
2. No: the catch (1,200 t) exceeds natural income (900 t), so the stock falls by 300 t a year.
3. Any four: carbon, cropland, grazing land, forest products, fishing grounds, built-up land.
4. Reserve of 4.5 − 3.0 = **1.5 gha per person**.
5. Any two: averages hide differences; omits pollution and biodiversity loss; depends on yield
   assumptions; imports shift impacts elsewhere.
6. 5000 × 0.2 = 1000 kg = **1.0 t CO₂e**.
7. Blue water is withdrawn from rivers, lakes or aquifers; green water is rainwater held in soil and
   used by plants.
8. An inner social foundation and an outer ecological ceiling.
9. 17, adopted in 2015.
10. Economic growth (decent work and growth) can increase emissions, conflicting with climate action.
11. Screening, scoping, baseline study.
12. The fair distribution of environmental benefits and harms, with fair participation in decisions.
