This guide covers section **3.5.3 The purpose and methods of market research** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 5, Marketing. The specification asks you to understand **why businesses conduct market research**, for example to identify market opportunities and to get a better insight into customers and competitors; the difference between **qualitative and quantitative** and between **primary and secondary** research; the **benefits and drawbacks of research techniques (including e-commerce)** and how to select the best method for a given business; how to **interpret and use findings** to make decisions; how to **manipulate and interpret data from tables and charts**; and **why research might produce inaccurate results** that misinform a business. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Marketing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-marketing/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-purpose-and-methods-of-market-research-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-purpose-and-methods-of-market-research-practice/).

## What is market research?

**Market research** is the process of collecting, recording and analysing information about a market, its customers and its competitors. It helps businesses to make decisions with evidence rather than guesses.

### The purpose of market research

| Purpose | Example |
|---|---|
| Identify market opportunities | Finding that many customers want a product no one supplies |
| Understand customers | Finding out what customers like, what they will pay and how they buy |
| Understand competitors | Learning rivals' prices, products, strengths and weaknesses |
| Reduce risk | Testing demand before spending money on a launch |
| Measure market size and trends | Seeing whether the market is growing or shrinking |
| Support requests for finance | A bank is more likely to lend if research shows demand |
| Check marketing success | Measuring whether an advertising campaign changed attitudes or sales |

## Types of research

### Primary and secondary research

**Primary research** (field research) is new information collected by the business itself, or by someone it pays, for its own purpose. **Secondary research** (desk research) uses information that already exists, collected by someone else.

| | Primary research | Secondary research |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Directly from customers or the market | Existing sources: government statistics, trade reports, newspapers, websites, past sales records |
| Fits the business's needs | Yes, designed to answer its own questions | Not always: collected for another purpose |
| Up to date | Yes | May be out of date |
| Cost and time | Often more expensive and slower | Usually cheaper and quicker |
| Examples | Surveys, interviews, focus groups, test marketing | Reports, internal sales data, online articles |

### Quantitative and qualitative research

**Quantitative research** collects numerical data that can be counted and analysed statistically, for example "62% of customers prefer the larger pack". **Qualitative research** collects opinions, feelings and reasons, for example why customers prefer it.

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| Type of information | Numbers, percentages | Opinions, motives, attitudes |
| Typical methods | Questionnaires with tick boxes, sales data | Interviews, focus groups, open questions |
| Strength | Easy to analyse and compare; large samples | Gives depth and reasons |
| Weakness | May not explain why | Hard to analyse; small samples; may be influenced by the interviewer |

Many businesses use both: numbers to show what is happening and opinions to explain why.

## Methods of market research

| Method | Description | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surveys and questionnaires | A set of questions given to a sample of people | Quantitative data from many people; fairly cheap | Low response rate; people may not tell the truth; careful design needed |
| Interviews | Face-to-face or telephone questions | Detailed answers; can explain the question | Expensive and slow; interviewer bias |
| Focus groups | A small group discusses a product guided by a leader | Reveals feelings and reasons | Small group, not representative; strong voices dominate |
| Observation | Watching how customers behave | Shows what people actually do | Does not explain why |
| Test marketing | Selling a product in a limited area first | Real sales evidence before a full launch | Costly; rivals can see the product |
| Sales and loyalty-card data | Using records of what customers have bought | Cheap, accurate and quantitative | Shows past behaviour only, and only of existing customers |
| Online research (e-commerce) | Online surveys, website analytics, customer reviews, social media data | Fast, cheap, wide reach; shows real behaviour such as clicks and purchases | Only reaches online users; privacy concerns; reviews may be biased |
| Secondary sources | Government statistics, trade reports, competitor websites | Cheap, wide coverage | May be old or not specific |

### Selecting the best method

The best method depends on the business and the question.

- **Small business with a small budget:** online surveys, social media polls, observation or secondary data.
- **Large business launching a new product:** large-scale surveys, focus groups and test marketing.
- **Need for reasons and attitudes:** focus groups or interviews.
- **Need for numbers across a large population:** questionnaires or online analytics.
- **Online retailer:** website analytics and customer reviews already provide a lot of data.

## Sampling

It is rarely possible to ask everyone, so businesses ask a **sample**: a smaller group chosen from the target market. The sample must be **representative**, matching the target market in age, gender, location and so on. A larger sample usually gives more reliable results, but costs more.

**Worked example 1: scaling up a sample.** A business has 4,000 customers and surveys 200 of them, which is 200 / 4,000 x 100 = 5%. In the survey 62% say they would buy a new product.

```
Estimated buyers = 62% x 4,000 = 2,480 customers
```

This is only an estimate. People often say they will buy and then do not, so the real number is likely to be lower.

## Interpreting data from tables and charts

The specification asks you to manipulate and interpret data. You may be given a table, bar chart, pie chart or line graph and asked to calculate percentages, totals or changes, and to draw a conclusion.

**Worked example 2: reading a table.** A company surveys 250 people about which of four designs they prefer.

| Design | Number choosing it | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 40% |
| B | 85 | 34% |
| C | 40 | 16% |
| D | 25 | 10% |
| **Total** | **250** | **100%** |

```
Percentage for A = 100 / 250 x 100 = 40%
A and B together = (100 + 85) / 250 x 100 = 74%
```

Design A is the most popular, but 60% of respondents did not choose it, so the business should consider A and B together, or investigate why customers prefer B. The two designs together make up 74% of choices.

**Worked example 3: grouped spending data.** The same 250 people were asked how much they spend a week on snacks.

| Weekly spend | Number of people | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Under $5 | 30 | 12% |
| $5 to $9.99 | 90 | 36% |
| $10 to $14.99 | 80 | 32% |
| $15 or more | 50 | 20% |

```
Most common group (modal class) = $5 to $9.99 (90 people)
Spending $10 or more = (80 + 50) / 250 x 100 = 52%
```

A business could use this to set the price and size of its products: more than half of the sample spend $10 or more a week.

When interpreting data, say what the numbers show, then what the business should do, and note what the data cannot tell you.

## Using the findings to make decisions

Findings can help businesses to:

- decide whether to launch a new product
- set prices that customers will accept
- choose the best promotion and distribution
- adapt products to different segments
- decide where to open a new shop.

Research should support the decision, not make it automatically. Managers must also consider cost, competitors and their own judgement.

## Why research can be inaccurate

Poor research can mislead a business into costly mistakes.

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Sample too small | Results may not reflect the whole market |
| Sample not representative | Asking only young people, or people in one area, gives biased results |
| Leading or unclear questions | "Don't you agree our product is excellent?" pushes people towards one answer |
| Respondents not truthful | People may say what they think the researcher wants to hear, or say they will buy when they will not |
| Out-of-date data | Secondary data may no longer be true |
| Interviewer bias | The way an interviewer asks or reacts may change answers |
| Poor analysis | Mistakes in calculation or reading of data |
| Low response rate | The people who reply may be different from those who do not |

To improve accuracy, a business can use a larger and more representative sample, pilot its questionnaire, avoid leading questions, check results against other sources and use more than one method.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing primary with qualitative. Primary/secondary is about where the data comes from; qualitative/quantitative is about what kind of data it is.
- Saying that primary research is always better. It is more costly and slower.
- Giving a method without a reason why it suits the business.
- Treating survey results as certain, when they are estimates from a sample.
- Forgetting that e-commerce data only covers people who use the website.
- Calculating a percentage by dividing by the wrong total.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** and **state** questions, give the key feature: "primary research is collected first-hand". For **explain** questions, give a benefit or drawback with a reason that fits the business. For **calculate** questions, show the sum and use the correct total. For **select the best method** questions, match the method to the business's budget, size and the information needed. For **evaluate** questions, weigh the reliability of the results against the cost, and say how the findings should be used.
