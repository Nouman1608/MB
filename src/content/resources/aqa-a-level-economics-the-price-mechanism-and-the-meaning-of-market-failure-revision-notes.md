## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Price mechanism | Prices, set by demand and supply, coordinating decisions in a market economy |
| Rationing function | Price limits demand to the available supply |
| Incentive function | Price and profit changes reward a change in behaviour |
| Signalling function | Price changes carry information to buyers and sellers |
| Impersonal allocation | Allocation by willingness and ability to pay, regardless of identity or need |
| Market failure | A market leads to a misallocation of resources |
| Complete failure | A missing market: it does not exist |
| Partial failure | The market exists but misallocates |

## The three functions: a chain to write

1. Demand rises, so there is a shortage at the old price.
2. Price rises, which **signals** scarcity to everyone.
3. Higher price **rations**: some buyers drop out.
4. Higher price and profit **incentivise** more supply and new entry.
5. A new equilibrium forms at a higher price and quantity.

Reverse every step for a fall in demand.

**Method for equilibrium shifts.** Set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then substitute for Q. Test the old price in the new equations to find the shortage or surplus.

**Worked reminder.** Qd = 500 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 100. Then 50P = 600, so P = 12 and Q = 260. Demand falls to Qd = 440 − 20P: 50P = 540, so P = 10.80 (a 10% fall) and Q = 224. At the old price of 12, Qd = 200 and Qs = 260, so the surplus is 60.

## What, how and for whom

| Question | Market answer |
|---|---|
| What | Goods with rising prices and profits attract resources |
| How | Firms pick the lowest-cost method at current input prices |
| For whom | Those with willingness and ability to pay |

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No central planner or information overload | Ignores need when income is unequal |
| Profit rewards efficiency and innovation | External costs and benefits ignored |
| Consumer sovereignty | Public goods not provided |
| Low administration cost | Monopoly power |
| Fast response to change | Merit and demerit goods mispriced |

## Evaluating "the price mechanism is impersonal"

- **Supports the view:** buyers are treated alike; no favouritism; a price is quick to apply.
- **Challenges the view:** allocation tracks income; a high price may reflect wealth, not need; markets still involve firms with power over price.
- **Judgement:** matters most for necessities with no substitutes and where income differences are large.

## Describing the diagram in words

In a demand and supply diagram, the vertical axis shows price and the horizontal axis shows quantity. The market settles at the point where the demand curve (sloping down) meets the supply curve (sloping up). Mark that point and read off its price and quantity.

| Event | Shift | At the old price | Price then |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand rises | Demand curve moves right | Shortage (Qd above Qs) | Rises |
| Demand falls | Demand curve moves left | Surplus (Qs above Qd) | Falls |
| Supply rises | Supply curve moves right | Surplus | Falls |
| Supply falls | Supply curve moves left | Shortage | Rises |

After a shift, movements along the other curve do the rationing (along demand) and the incentive work (along supply). That is the link between the diagram and the three functions.

**Worked reminder (both sides moving).** Take the market with Qd = 500 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 100 again. Suppose supply rises by 50 at every price: Qs = 30P − 50. Then 500 − 20P = 30P − 50, so 50P = 550, P = 11 and Q = 280. Price falls from 12 to 11 and quantity rises from 260 to 280. At the old price of 12, Qs = 310 and Qd = 260, a surplus of 50.

## Extending markets into new areas

- **Possible gains:** more supply, shorter queues, price information.
- **Possible costs:** payment can crowd out duty or altruism, change what the activity means, attract buyers or sellers with hidden information, and exclude those on low incomes.
- **Blood example:** a payment may raise the quantity offered but alter donor motives and quality; compare cost per usable unit, not just units collected.
- **Judgement:** depends on how far the activity is valued for its own sake, how safe the product is and whether quality can be checked.

## Causes of market failure, with examples

| Cause | Failure | Complete or partial | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public goods | No market; free riding | Complete | Flood defences |
| Negative externality | Overproduction | Partial | Traffic congestion |
| Positive externality | Underproduction | Partial | Training workers |
| Merit goods | Under-consumption | Partial | Health checks |
| Demerit goods | Over-consumption | Partial | Alcohol |
| Monopoly, imperfections | Price above cost, immobile factors, information gaps | Partial | A single supplier |
| Inequality | Distribution follows income | Partial | Unaffordable essentials |

A numerical reminder of a missing market: three households would pay £25, £18 and £12 for a service costing £40. Total willingness to pay is 25 + 18 + 12 = £55. Net benefit is 55 − 40 = **£15**, but free riding stops a market forming.

A reminder of partial failure: marginal private benefit 50 − 0.2Q, marginal private cost 10 + 0.2Q. Market output: 40 = 0.4Q, so Q = 100. With an external cost of £8, marginal social cost is 18 + 0.2Q: 32 = 0.4Q, so Q = 80. The market overproduces by 20, and the welfare loss is ½ × 8 × 20 = **£80**.

## Building a judgement

A good judgement names the condition on which the conclusion depends. Use this order in an extended answer.

1. State the claim in the question in your own words.
2. Give one or two points in support, each as a chain of cause and effect.
3. Give one or two points against, also as chains.
4. Weigh them: which matters more, for whom, in the short or long run?
5. Finish with a conclusion that says "it depends on" a named factor, then commits to the more likely case.

**Conditions worth naming.** How large the income gap is. Whether the good is a necessity with no close substitute. How big the third-party effects are. Whether information about quality is easy to check. Whether the government can cheaply support those on low incomes. Whether people value the activity for its own sake, as with giving.

**Linking the two sub-sections.** The first half of the topic explains how markets succeed. The second half asks when they fail. A strong answer uses the same language in both: a market fails when price signals do not reflect full costs and benefits, when no price exists, or when the result is judged unacceptable because of who can pay.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three functions of price.
2. Qd = 500 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 100. Find equilibrium.
3. Demand falls to Qd = 440 − 20P. Find the surplus at the old price.
4. A cost of £12 for a service is paid by two households with monthly incomes of £2,400 and £800. Express each as a percentage of income.
5. State the difference between complete and partial market failure.
6. Why can a private firm not sell a pure public good easily?
7. Give one reason a market for a donated product might reduce supply.
8. Is monopoly a partial or a complete failure? Explain.
9. Name the cause of market failure when allocation follows income rather than need.
10. Willingness to pay of £25, £18 and £12 and a cost of £40: find the net benefit.

### Answers

1. Rationing, incentive and signalling.
2. P = £12, Q = 260.
3. The surplus is 60 (Qd = 200, Qs = 260 at £12).
4. 12 ÷ 2,400 = 0.5%; 12 ÷ 800 = 1.5%.
5. Complete: no market exists. Partial: a market exists but misallocates.
6. People cannot be excluded, so many will free ride and revenue cannot be collected.
7. Payment may crowd out intrinsic motives such as duty.
8. Partial: the market exists, but price is above marginal cost and output is too low.
9. Inequality in the distribution of income and wealth.
10. £55 − £40 = £15.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a price change without naming the function it performs.
- Forgetting to test the old price when asked for a shortage or surplus.
- Treating "impersonal" as only good or only bad.
- Calling every unfair outcome market failure without showing misallocation.
- Using "no market" and "small market" as if they meant the same thing.
- Writing about the blood example from opinion rather than incentives and nature of the activity.
- Giving causes of market failure with no example.
- Dropping the units from a welfare-loss answer.
