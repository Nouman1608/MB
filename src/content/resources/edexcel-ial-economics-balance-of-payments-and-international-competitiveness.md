## 1. The accounts (1a)

The balance of payments records transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period.

| Account | Records | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Current | Payments for goods and services, income, transfers | Goods (visible trade); services (invisible trade); primary income (wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad less paid abroad); secondary income (transfers such as workers' remittances and aid) |
| Capital | Small transfers of capital | Debt forgiveness, assets moved by migrants |
| Financial | Cross-border investment and lending | Foreign direct investment (FDI), portfolio investment in shares and bonds, bank loans and deposits, reserves |

Trade in goods plus trade in services is the balance of trade in goods and services. Here a capital inflow counts as positive. Some published tables use the opposite sign for the financial account, so check the convention. Because each transaction has two sides, the three accounts plus net errors and omissions sum to zero.

**Worked example 1.** Country A (billion currency units, cu; GDP 1,100).

```
Goods:      exports 410 - imports 470 = -60
Services:   exports 160 - imports 120 = +40
Primary income (net)                   -25
Secondary income (net)                 -10
Current account                        -55   (-55/1,100 = -5.0% of GDP)
Capital account                         +3
Financial: FDI +30, portfolio +35, other -15 = +50
Net errors and omissions                +2
Total: -55 + 3 + 50 + 2 = 0
```

Country A pays for its deficit by net borrowing and by selling assets to foreigners. A "balance of payments deficit" in everyday use normally means the current account.

## 2. Causes of deficits and surpluses (1b)

Deficit causes:
- **Fast domestic demand growth.** Rising incomes raise imports. The effect is larger when the income elasticity of demand for imports is high.
- **Low competitiveness.** High unit labour costs or poor quality push consumers towards imports and away from exports.
- **A high exchange rate,** which raises export prices abroad and lowers import prices at home.
- **Structural weaknesses,** such as too few export industries, or reliance on a commodity whose world price falls.
- **Net income outflows,** when past borrowing leaves large interest and profit payments abroad.

Surplus causes are the reverse: strong competitiveness, a low exchange rate, high saving and weak domestic demand, or export earnings from resources.

**Worked example 2.** Country D imports 200 and exports 190 (cu billion), a balance of -10. Real GDP rises 3% and the income elasticity of demand for imports is 2.5. Foreign incomes rise 3% and the income elasticity of demand for D's exports is 1.5.

```
Imports: +2.5 × 3% = +7.5%  → 200 × 1.075 = 215
Exports: +1.5 × 3% = +4.5%  → 190 × 1.045 = 198.55
Balance: 198.55 - 215 = -16.45  (deficit widens by 6.45)
```

Evaluation: a deficit caused by a temporary boom may reverse when growth slows. A structural deficit caused by weak competitiveness will not.

## 3. Reducing an imbalance (1c)

| Type | Measure | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Expenditure-reducing | Higher taxes, lower government spending, higher interest rates to cut incomes and imports | Costs output and jobs; slow if firms and households are unresponsive |
| Expenditure-switching | Lower exchange rate; tariffs, quotas or subsidies | Retaliation, higher costs for firms using imports, breaks trade agreements |
| Supply-side | Better education, investment, infrastructure | Slow; uncertain; costly |

**Worked example 3.** Country C wants imports to fall by 12. The marginal propensity to import is 0.25.

```
Fall in income needed = 12 ÷ 0.25 = 48
Share of GDP of 1,000 = 4.8%
With a multiplier of 2.5, spending must be cut by 48 ÷ 2.5 = 19.2
```

The correction is costly: output falls 4.8% to remove 12 of imports. This is why expenditure-reducing policy is a weak tool for large deficits, and why supply-side policy is preferred for a lasting fix. For a surplus country, the reverse (higher spending, a stronger currency) is the correction.

## 4. Global imbalances (1d)

Global trade imbalances arise when some countries run large, persistent surpluses and others matching deficits. Surplus countries lend to deficit countries through the financial account.

Why they matter:
- Deficit countries accumulate foreign liabilities, so more income flows abroad.
- Heavy reliance on foreign lenders risks capital flight, a fall in the currency and sudden austerity if confidence goes.
- Surplus countries may hold weak domestic demand, which depresses world demand.
- Tensions rise, with pressure for protection and competitive depreciation.
- Surplus countries bear the risk that deficit countries cannot repay.

Chain of reasoning: persistent deficit → rising foreign debt → larger income outflows → lenders doubt repayment → capital flight and currency fall → higher import prices and inflation → forced spending cuts and slower growth. Evaluation: the risk depends on what the borrowing finances (productive investment can raise future exports), on the size of the deficit relative to GDP, and on whether debts are in the home currency.

**Worked example 4.** Country A starts with net external liabilities of 300 and runs a deficit of 55 a year for 5 years, financed by borrowing.

```
Liabilities after 5 years: 300 + 5 × 55 = 575
Interest at 4%: before 300 × 0.04 = 12; after 575 × 0.04 = 23
```

Rising interest payments widen the primary income deficit, which makes the deficit harder to shrink. If nominal GDP grows 4% a year and the deficit stays at 5.0% of GDP (55 ÷ 1,100), liabilities converge on 5.0 ÷ 4 = 125% of GDP.

## 5. Measuring competitiveness (3a)

International competitiveness is the ability to sell goods and services abroad and resist imports at home. Three measures:

- **Relative productivity:** output per worker (or per hour) in one country compared with another.
- **Relative unit labour costs (ULC):** labour cost per unit of output, compared in a common currency. ULC = total labour cost ÷ output. A rise relative to rivals means worsening competitiveness.
- **Relative export prices:** an index of one country's export prices divided by rivals', in a common currency.

**Worked example 5.** Country A: output 900 with 30 million workers. Country B: output 720 with 27 million.

```
Output per worker A = 900 ÷ 30 = 30
Output per worker B = 720 ÷ 27 = 26.67
Relative productivity = 30 ÷ 26.67 = 112.5% (A is 12.5% higher)
```

**Worked example 6.** In Country A wages per unit of labour rise 6% and productivity rises 2%. In Country B wages rise 3% and productivity 2.5%.

```
ULC A: 1.06 ÷ 1.02 = 1.0392 → +3.92%
ULC B: 1.03 ÷ 1.025 = 1.0049 → +0.49%
Relative ULC = 1.0392 ÷ 1.0049 = 1.0342 → A worse off by 3.4%
If A's currency falls 4%: 1.0342 × 0.96 = 0.9928 → 0.7% better
```

A depreciation can offset poor cost performance, but only temporarily if wages then rise to compensate.

**Worked example 7.** Country A's machine exports sell for 5,000 cu. At 1 cu = 2.0 foreign currency units (fc) the foreign price is 5,000 × 2.0 = 10,000 fc. A rival sells at 9,500 fc. If A's currency falls to 1 cu = 1.8 fc, the foreign price becomes 5,000 × 1.8 = 9,000 fc, so A now undercuts the rival by 500 fc, a 5.3% lower price (500 ÷ 9,500). Non-price factors still decide whether buyers switch.

## 6. Factors influencing competitiveness (3b)

| Factor | Link |
|---|---|
| Productivity | Higher output per worker lowers ULC at a given wage |
| Quality of human capital | Skills raise productivity and the ability to make higher-value goods |
| Exchange rate | A higher rate raises export prices abroad |
| Wage and non-wage costs | Pay, employer contributions, energy and land costs raise unit costs |
| Regulations | Compliance costs and delays raise costs; weak rules can reduce quality |
| Infrastructure | Reliable transport, power and broadband cut delivery times and costs |
| Non-price factors | Design, reliability, branding, after-sales service and innovation allow sales at higher prices |

## 7. Measures to increase competitiveness (3c)

| Measure | Chain of reasoning | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Education and training | Better skills → higher productivity → lower ULC | Long time lag; cost; skills may emigrate |
| Investment incentives | More capital per worker → output per worker up | Cost to taxpayers; some investment would have happened anyway |
| Privatisation and deregulation | Competition and lower costs → efficiency | Monopoly risk; short-term job losses |
| Reducing the exchange rate | Lower foreign-currency export prices | Dearer imports, inflation, retaliation; effect depends on elasticities |
| Trade liberalisation | Cheaper inputs and more competition | Some industries collapse; adjustment costs |

Judgement: policies that raise productivity are slow but durable; a lower exchange rate is quick but fades if costs then rise.

## 8. Why competitiveness matters (3d)

Advantages: more exports and output, a smaller deficit, more jobs, FDI attracted, higher incomes and a lower risk of currency crises.

Problems of uncompetitiveness: a persistent deficit, falling output and unemployment in traded sectors, lower FDI, weaker growth, and pressure on the currency.

Evaluation: competitiveness can be built on low wages, which may lower living standards. A strong currency can reflect competitiveness, not harm it.

## Common errors

- Calling the capital and financial accounts "the capital account" only.
- Treating a deficit as bad in every case: borrowing for productive investment can be sound.
- Comparing ULC without a common currency.
- Confusing relative productivity with growth in productivity.
- Writing "export prices fall" when only the foreign-currency price changes.
- Listing measures with no evaluation.
