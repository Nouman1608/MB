This guide covers the "Evidence" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for 2026-2028. The syllabus says: "The research report must be evidence-based." The matching assessment points are "use and assess sources to support arguments and perspectives (AO1)" and "make judgements which are supported by evidence (AO1)". For the component as a whole, read the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/) first. This page goes further into how to choose, assess and use evidence.

## What the syllabus says about evidence

"Evidence is likely to be primary evidence and/or secondary evidence." The syllabus defines them as follows.

- **Primary evidence** is "first-hand information and can come from a range of sources, such as historical documents, literary texts, artefacts and data gathered from scientific observation, as well as interviews and surveys".
- **Secondary evidence** "includes material such as the arguments and opinions of specialists which reflect on or synthesise primary evidence".

It then adds a requirement: "Candidates should be aware of and explore the strengths and potential weaknesses of all types of evidence." That sentence is the basis of the whole page: you are not only expected to use evidence, but to **assess** it.

| | Primary evidence | Secondary evidence |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | first-hand information | specialists' arguments and opinions about primary evidence |
| Examples | historical documents, literary texts, artefacts, scientific observation data, interviews, surveys | journal articles, books by experts, literature reviews, analysis reports |
| Usual strength | close to the original situation; you control what you collect (if you gather it yourself) | expert interpretation; wide coverage; saves you collecting everything |
| Usual weakness | small samples, limited scope, possible bias in collection | may repeat the assumptions or choices of the author; may be dated |

The terms depend on **use**. A newspaper article is secondary evidence if you use it to learn what happened, but primary evidence if your question is about how newspapers report the event. Say how you are using each source.

## Types of evidence and what they can show

| Type | Typical source | What it can show | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | statistics, surveys, experiments | patterns across many cases; size of an effect | may not explain why; definitions can differ |
| Qualitative | interviews, focus groups, texts | meaning, reasons, experience | small samples; hard to generalise |
| Documentary | reports, laws, archives | what was decided or stated | may be selective or official |
| Observational | field notes, scientific observation | what happened, in place | observer effects; limited time |
| Expert opinion | specialist analysis | informed judgement | may reflect the specialist's perspective |

The syllabus notes that different perspectives are supported by different kinds of evidence. In its own example, the argument for industrialisation based on efficiency "might be backed up by quantitative evidence of rates of production and profitability", whereas the argument against "might be supported by observational evidence of its impact on the natural environment". When you compare perspectives, you are therefore often comparing **types** of evidence as well as conclusions.

## How to assess a source

Use the same questions for every source. They are general research points, not syllabus wording.

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Who** produced it? | the author's expertise, affiliation and interest |
| **When and where**? | the date, and the context in which it was written (provenance) |
| **Why** was it produced? | the purpose: to inform, persuade, sell, advise |
| **How** was the evidence gathered? | the method; size and type of sample; how terms were defined |
| **How well does it fit** my question? | relevance to the debate and the perspectives |
| **Can I check it**? | whether other sources agree (corroboration) |
| **What is missing**? | the points of view or data it leaves out |

Aim to give a **short reasoned judgement** about each key source: not just "this source is reliable", but "this source is useful for X because Y, but limited for Z because W".

## Worked example: two sources compared (fictional)

*Research question: Does remote working benefit city centres?*

**Source A:** a report by a property company, published last year, based on a survey of 2,000 office workers in one large city, showing that 62% spend less in the city centre when working from home.

**Source B:** an academic article reviewing 14 studies from five countries, published four years ago, finding mixed effects on city-centre spending.

| Question | Source A | Source B |
|---|---|---|
| Who | a property company with an interest in office space | university researchers |
| When and where | last year; one city | four years ago; five countries |
| Why | to inform clients about office demand | to review research |
| How | survey of 2,000 workers; self-reported spending | review of 14 studies; method varies by study |
| Fit | recent and directly about spending, but one city | wider coverage, but older and "mixed" |
| Checking | consistent with the direction of some studies in B | partly confirmed by A |
| Missing | spending by residents and tourists | recent changes after the studies |

**Judgement:** Source A is up to date and clear but comes from an interested party and covers one city, so it should be used as evidence for *workers' reported spending*, not for all city centres. Source B is broader and independent but older, so it supports the claim that effects *vary*. Together they support a cautious conclusion that remote working reduces some spending, with effects depending on the city.

## Using evidence in a paragraph

A reliable pattern for each paragraph is **Claim, Evidence, Assessment, Link** (CEAL).

1. **Claim:** state the point.
2. **Evidence:** give the evidence, with a reference.
3. **Assessment:** say how strong the evidence is and what it cannot show.
4. **Link:** return to the research question or the perspective.

*Example:* "One perspective argues that remote working lowers city-centre spending. A survey of 2,000 office workers found that 62% reported spending less (Property Report, 2025). The survey measures reported behaviour in one city, and the company has an interest in office demand, so the finding is best seen as indicative. It supports the perspective for that city, but a wider review is needed before generalising."

Each citation in the text should match an entry in the bibliography. The syllabus requires that "full bibliographical references must be given for any quotations".

## When evidence conflicts or is missing

- **Conflict.** Do not hide it. Ask whether the sources measured the same thing, used the same definitions or covered the same period. A conflict that is explained is a finding.
- **Gaps.** Say what evidence you could not find, and how that limits your conclusion. The syllabus expects reflection on conclusions (AO2).
- **Weak evidence for one side.** Report this honestly. Do not choose a side because it has more evidence available, without checking its quality.

## Evidence and judgement

The criterion "make judgements which are supported by evidence" means your conclusion should follow from the evidence you have assessed. A good judgement:

- names the strongest evidence for each perspective;
- explains why one body of evidence is weightier (for example, more recent, more representative or better documented);
- states how certain the conclusion is.

## Common mistakes

- **Using only secondary evidence**, when the question could be strengthened with a small amount of primary evidence, such as a text, a data set or a short interview.
- **Using sources only on one side** of the debate.
- **Describing the source, not assessing it.** "The report was published by an organisation" is a description. "The organisation has an interest in the result, so the finding may be selective" is an assessment.
- **Treating a website or a statistic as automatically reliable** because it looks official.
- **Confusing primary and secondary** without explaining how the source is used.
- **Citing without referencing**, or referencing without citing.
- **Overclaiming** from a small sample or a single study.

## Checklist for your evidence

- Each perspective has at least two substantial sources.
- There is a mix of evidence types where possible.
- Each key source has a reasoned assessment.
- Conflicting evidence is explained.
- Gaps are stated.
- Every citation is in the bibliography.
