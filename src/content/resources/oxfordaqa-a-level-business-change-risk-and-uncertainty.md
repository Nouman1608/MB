## What 3.4.6 asks of you

| Content heading | You need to be able to | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Change | Explain internal and external reasons for change; weigh its advantages and disadvantages for stakeholders; explain barriers, including Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for employee resistance; manage change using Lewin's force field analysis and Kotter and Schlesinger's six ways of overcoming resistance | International A-level only |
| Risk and uncertainty | Explain why assessing and planning for risk and uncertainty matters; identify financial, strategic, operational, compliance, reputational and cybersecurity risk; weigh seven methods of managing risk and two of managing uncertainty; explain four ways of developing an agile business | International A-level only |

**Assessment.** Questions on 3.4.6 appear in the Unit 4: Business strategy paper, a 1 hour 45 minute written exam. The paper carries 80 marks, counts for 30% of the International A-level and is built around two case studies, each followed by five short answer and extended response questions.

[Implementing a strategy](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-implementing-a-strategy/) names staff resistance and strategic drift as reasons strategies fail; this topic shows how to manage them.

## 1. Why businesses change

**Velmoray Textiles** weaves cotton fabric for clothing brands. Over two years it has replaced half its looms with automated machines, closed a warehouse and started selling direct to small designers online.

**Internal reasons** start inside the business:
- a new chief executive or owner with different priorities;
- new objectives or a new strategy, such as growth into a new market;
- poor performance (falling profit, rising costs, high labour turnover) that forces action;
- growth that outstrips the current structure, systems or premises;
- a decision to adopt new technology or processes.

**External reasons** start outside it:
- **technology:** cheaper automation or new sales channels;
- **competitors:** a rival cuts prices or launches a better product;
- **economic change:** wage rates, interest rates, exchange rates;
- **legal and political change:** new safety, employment or environmental rules;
- **social change:** customers' tastes and buying habits shift.

At Velmoray, rising wage costs (external) and a new owner wanting faster delivery (internal) drove automation. Say which type each trigger is and why it forces change *now*.

## 2. Change and its stakeholders

Change creates winners and losers, and the same group can gain in one way and lose in another.

| Stakeholder | Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | New skills, more interesting work, job security if the firm becomes more competitive | Job losses, stress, retraining burden, loss of familiar teams |
| Managers | Chance to lead, better results to report | Heavier workload, blame if the change fails |
| Shareholders/owners | Higher long-term profit and value | Short-term costs cut profit and dividends |
| Customers | Better products, lower prices, faster service | Disruption while systems change, loss of a product they liked |
| Suppliers | Bigger or longer orders if the firm grows | Lost contracts if the firm switches supplier or brings work in-house |
| Local community | New investment and skilled jobs | Redundancies, closure of a site |

**Velmoray:** designers gain faster samples; loom operators fear redundancy; shareholders see profit dip in the year the machines are bought. State *which* stakeholder, *what* changes for them and *why* interests conflict.

## 3. Barriers to change

Barriers include cost, lack of skills, an entrenched culture and a lack of management time. The specification names **Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for employee resistance to change**:

| Reason | Meaning | Example at Velmoray |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-interest** | Staff expect to lose something they value: pay, status, a job, overtime | Senior weavers lose overtime and the respect that came with running the old looms |
| **Misunderstanding and lack of trust** | Staff do not understand the change or doubt managers' motives | A rumour spreads that the warehouse closure is the first step to selling the business |
| **Inertia** | Staff prefer familiar routines; change feels uncomfortable even with no clear loss | Office staff keep using paper order sheets alongside the new online system |
| **Different assessments of the situation** | Staff judge the costs and benefits differently from managers | Supervisors believe the automated machines will cause more quality faults than they solve |

Identifying the *reason* matters, because the right response depends on it.

## 4. Managing change

### Lewin's force field analysis

**Force field analysis** lists the **driving forces** pushing for a change and the **restraining forces** holding it back. Managers often give each force a strength score (for example 1 = weak, 5 = strong). Change is more likely when driving forces outweigh restraining forces. Managers then plan to **strengthen driving forces** and, usually more effectively, **weaken restraining forces**.

**Worked example 1: Velmoray's automation decision**

| Driving forces | Score | Restraining forces | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rising wage costs | 4 | Fear of job losses | 4 |
| Buyers want faster delivery | 5 | Capital cost of machines | 4 |
| Falling price of automated looms | 3 | Skills gap among operators | 2 |
| **Total** | **12** | **Total** | **10** |

Step 1. Total driving forces: 4 + 5 + 3 = **12**.
Step 2. Total restraining forces: 4 + 4 + 2 = **10**.
Step 3. Compare: driving forces lead by 12 − 10 = **2**, so the change should go ahead, but the margin is narrow.
Step 4. Plan action on restraining forces. A guarantee of retraining rather than redundancy for current operators could cut fear of job losses from 4 to 2, and a training course could cut the skills gap from 2 to 1. Restraining forces then total 2 + 4 + 1 = **7**, a lead of 12 − 7 = **5**.

**Value and limits.** It makes managers list every force and plan how to tackle each. But scores are subjective, so two managers may reach different totals, and forces shift over time.

### Kotter and Schlesinger's six ways of overcoming resistance

| Method | What it involves | Best when | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Education** | Explain the reasons for change and share the facts | Resistance comes from misunderstanding | Slow if many people are involved |
| **Participation** | Involve staff in designing the change | Staff have useful knowledge or could block the change | Time-consuming; staff may design a weaker change |
| **Facilitation** | Support: training, counselling, time off to adjust | Resistance comes from fear or a skills gap | Costly, and may still fail |
| **Negotiation** | Offer incentives, e.g. bonuses or early retirement terms | A group will clearly lose and has power to resist | Expensive; others may demand a deal too |
| **Manipulation** | Selective information or giving a resister a token role (co-option) | Other methods are too slow or too costly | Destroys trust if discovered |
| **Coercion** | Force: threats of job loss, transfer or dismissal | Speed is essential and managers have power | Resentment, lower motivation, possible staff loss |

Match the method to the reason for resistance. Velmoray's rumour about a sale (misunderstanding and lack of trust) calls for education, whereas the senior weavers' lost overtime (self-interest) may need negotiation, such as a retained-pay period while they retrain.

## 5. Risk and uncertainty

**Risk** is the chance that an outcome differs from the plan, where the possible outcomes can be identified and, to some extent, estimated. **Uncertainty** describes situations where outcomes cannot be predicted or measured with confidence, such as a sudden change of government policy or a new technology that has not yet appeared.

**Why assessing and planning matters:** it protects cash and profit, keeps operations running, helps a firm meet legal duties, reassures lenders, investors and customers, and lets managers respond quickly instead of improvising under pressure. Too much caution has a cost too: a firm that avoids every risk may miss profitable opportunities.

### Types of risk

**Quillhaven Logistics** runs a fleet of delivery vans for online retailers.

| Type | Meaning | At Quillhaven |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | Borrowing, profit or liquidity problems | Van loans at variable interest rates; retailers paying 60 days late squeeze cash |
| **Strategic** | Big decisions such as entering new markets or launching new products | Starting refrigerated deliveries for grocers, where it has no experience |
| **Operational** | Disruption in production or supply chains | A breakdown at its only depot halts deliveries |
| **Compliance** | Failure to meet legal or regulatory requirements | Drivers exceeding legal working hours |
| **Reputational** | Damage from negative publicity or customer dissatisfaction | Videos of parcels being thrown over gates shared online |
| **Cybersecurity** | Attacks on IT systems and data | Ransomware locks the route-planning system and customer address data |

### Methods of managing risk

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Market research** | Tests demand before a launch, cutting strategic risk | Costs money and time; results can be misleading |
| **Contingency and crisis management plans** | A ready response limits damage and speeds recovery | Takes time to write and update; may not fit the crisis that occurs |
| **Insurance** | Transfers the cost of insurable losses to an insurer | Premiums are a regular cost; some risks (e.g. a failed launch) cannot be insured |
| **Diversifying products, markets or suppliers** | Spreads risk, so one failure does less harm | Loses bulk discounts; stretches management into unfamiliar areas |
| **Training** | Reduces errors, accidents and compliance breaches | Costly; trained staff may leave |
| **Forecasting** | Spots cash shortages or demand changes early | Based on past data, so less reliable when conditions change |
| **Cybersecurity measures** | Firewalls, backups, staff awareness reduce attacks and recovery time | Ongoing cost; no system is fully secure |

**Worked example 2: matching methods at Quillhaven**

- Late-paying customers (financial): a cash-flow **forecast** shows when an overdraft is needed, and a [sales forecast](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-analysing-future-sales/) helps size the fleet.
- Refrigerated deliveries (strategic): **market research** with grocers before buying chilled vans.
- Single depot (operational): a **contingency plan** to hire space at a partner depot within 24 hours.
- Drivers' hours (compliance): **training** plus tracking software.
- Ransomware (cybersecurity): offline **backups** and staff training on suspicious emails.

**Judgement.** No single method covers everything; the mix depends on each risk's likelihood and impact, and on what the firm can afford.

## 6. Managing uncertainty

Uncertain events cannot be predicted, so the specification names two broader approaches.

**Scenario planning** means describing a few plausible futures (for example "fuel prices double" or "a large retailer brings deliveries in-house") and deciding in advance how the business would respond to each. *Advantages:* managers think beyond a single forecast, and responses are ready. *Disadvantages:* time-consuming, and the future that arrives may not match any scenario.

**An agile business** can sense change and respond quickly. *Advantages:* it adapts to whatever happens rather than betting on one forecast, and can seize new opportunities. *Disadvantages:* frequent change can unsettle staff and customers, and it can lack the cost efficiency of tightly planned operations.

### Ways of developing an agile business

**Tormead Bikes** builds e-bikes and wants to react faster to new battery technology.

- **Flat organisational structure:** fewer levels of hierarchy, so information and decisions move faster. Tormead removes a management layer between the workshop and the directors.
- **Empowerment:** staff are given authority to make decisions in their own area. Workshop teams can change a component supplier within an agreed budget.
- **Transformational leadership:** leaders set an inspiring vision, encourage new ideas and develop staff, so people welcome change rather than resisting it.
- **Cross-functional teams:** staff from design, operations, marketing and finance work together, so new ideas are tested quickly from every angle.

Structure and empowerment also appear in the [human resources guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-human-resources/); here, link them to speed of response.

## Common errors

- Listing reasons for change without saying whether each is internal or external.
- Naming Kotter and Schlesinger's methods without matching each to the cause of resistance.
- Treating force field scores as objective facts rather than managers' judgements.
- Describing a "risk" that is really uncertainty, or naming the wrong type of risk.
- Recommending insurance for a risk that cannot be insured, such as a product flop.
- Saying a flat structure creates agility without explaining *how* it speeds decisions.
