This guide covers section 3.1.1.2, Tectonic hazards, in Section A (The challenge of natural hazards) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. It has three key ideas: "Earthquakes and volcanic eruptions are the result of physical processes"; "The effects of, and responses to, earthquakes and volcanoes vary between areas of contrasting levels of wealth"; and "Management can reduce the effects of a tectonic hazard." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). This page goes deeper on the tectonic content and gives two worked examples of contrasting wealth.

## What the specification lists

- Plate tectonics theory.
- The global distribution of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions and their relationship to plate margins.
- Physical processes at constructive, destructive and conservative margins that lead to earthquakes and volcanic activity.
- Primary and secondary effects; immediate and long-term responses.
- **Named examples** showing how effects and responses vary between **two areas of contrasting levels of wealth**.
- Reasons why people continue to live in areas at risk.
- How monitoring, prediction, protection and planning can reduce risks.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Plate tectonics | The theory that the Earth's crust is broken into moving plates |
| Plate margin | A boundary between two plates |
| Focus | The point underground where an earthquake starts |
| Epicentre | The point on the surface directly above the focus |
| Magma | Molten rock beneath the surface; called lava once it reaches the surface |
| Subduction | One plate sinking beneath another |
| Primary effect | Damage caused directly by the hazard |
| Secondary effect | Damage that follows as a result of the primary effects |
| Immediate response | Action in the hours and days after the event |
| Long-term response | Action over months and years |

## Plate tectonics theory

The Earth's outer layer, the lithosphere, is broken into around a dozen large plates and many smaller ones. They move slowly, a few centimetres a year, on the softer, hotter mantle below. Convection currents in the mantle, and the pulling of dense plate edges as they sink (slab pull), are thought to drive this movement. There are two types of crust. **Oceanic crust** is thin and dense. **Continental crust** is thicker and lighter. When plates meet, the denser oceanic plate usually sinks beneath the lighter continental plate.

## Global distribution

Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate margins, which form narrow belts across the world. The Pacific "Ring of Fire" surrounds the Pacific Ocean and includes the Andes, Japan, the Philippines, New Zealand and the west coast of North America. Another belt runs through the middle of the Atlantic Ocean (the Mid-Atlantic Ridge) and through Iceland. A third runs through the Mediterranean and Himalayas. Some volcanoes occur away from margins at "hotspots", such as Hawaii, but the specification focuses on the link to plate margins.

When you describe a distribution, say where the hazards occur (belts, oceans, continents), how they are arranged (in lines, not randomly) and give one or two named places.

## Processes at plate margins

### Constructive margins

Plates move **apart**. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new crust. Volcanoes here are usually gentle, with runny lava, and form shield-shaped cones. Earthquakes are small to moderate and shallow. Examples are the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland and the East African Rift Valley.

### Destructive margins

Plates move **together**. The denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the continental plate. As it sinks, friction and pressure build up. When they release suddenly, powerful earthquakes occur, some at great depth. The sinking plate melts, and the magma rises to form steep-sided **composite volcanoes**, which erupt explosively with thick, sticky lava and ash. An ocean trench forms where the plate sinks. Examples are the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate (the Andes) and the Pacific Plate beneath Japan.

### Conservative margins

Plates slide **past each other**, either in different directions or in the same direction at different speeds. Plates get stuck. Pressure builds up and is then released as an earthquake. There is no new crust created or destroyed, so there are **no volcanoes**. Earthquakes can be very strong and shallow. The San Andreas Fault in California is the classic example.

| Margin | Plate movement | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constructive | Apart | Frequent but small, shallow | Frequent, gentle, runny lava |
| Destructive | Together (subduction) | Powerful, shallow to deep | Explosive composite volcanoes |
| Conservative | Slide past | Can be very strong, shallow | None |

## Primary and secondary effects

**Earthquakes.** Primary effects: ground shaking, building and bridge collapse, cracks in roads, deaths and injuries from falling debris. Secondary effects: fires from broken gas pipes, landslides, liquefaction (shaking turns waterlogged soil into a liquid so that buildings sink), tsunamis if the focus is under the sea, disease where water supplies are contaminated, loss of jobs, homelessness and disruption to power and transport.

**Volcanoes.** Primary effects: lava flows, ash fall, pyroclastic flows (fast, hot clouds of gas and ash), toxic gases, volcanic bombs. Secondary effects: lahars (mudflows from melted snow or rain on ash), crop and livestock loss, flights grounded by ash clouds, climate cooling after very large eruptions, and loss of income.

**Immediate responses** are in the first hours and days: search and rescue, first aid, emergency shelter, food, water, evacuation. **Long-term responses** take months and years: rebuilding homes and infrastructure, restoring services and jobs, improving building rules, reducing future risk.

## Contrasting wealth: two named examples

The specification requires **two** named examples from areas of contrasting wealth. This guide uses the Haiti earthquake of 2010 (a low-income country) and the Christchurch earthquake of 2011 in New Zealand (a high-income country). You may have studied different examples, such as Nepal in 2015 or Japan in 2011; the same method applies. The facts below are approximate, and your teacher may use different figures.

### Haiti, January 2010 (low-income country)

- **Event:** a magnitude 7.0 earthquake at a shallow depth, with its epicentre close to Port-au-Prince, the capital, on a conservative plate boundary.
- **Primary effects:** estimates of the death toll range from about 100,000 to over 300,000, and about 1.5 million people lost their homes. Many buildings were poorly constructed with little steel reinforcement. Government buildings, hospitals and the port were badly damaged.
- **Secondary effects:** large camps of displaced people, disruption to water and sanitation, and a cholera outbreak from October 2010 that caused many thousands of deaths. Roads were blocked by rubble.
- **Immediate response:** international search teams and aid arrived within days, but the port and airport were damaged and congested, which slowed deliveries. Local people did much of the early rescue with little equipment.
- **Long-term response:** reconstruction was slow. Large numbers of people lived in temporary camps for years. The country relied heavily on aid and non-governmental organisations.

### Christchurch, February 2011 (high-income country)

- **Event:** a magnitude 6.3 earthquake, shallow (about 5 km), with its epicentre a few kilometres from the city centre. It followed a larger but less damaging earthquake in September 2010 that struck further away.
- **Primary effects:** 185 deaths, with the largest number in the collapse of one office building. Many older brick buildings in the centre were damaged.
- **Secondary effects:** widespread liquefaction in the east of the city flooded streets with silt and damaged homes. Rock falls hit some suburbs. Power and water supplies were interrupted.
- **Immediate response:** national and international urban search and rescue teams were mobilised quickly, hospitals and the army were available, and temporary housing and portable toilets were supplied.
- **Long-term response:** most losses were insured. A government agency was set up to lead recovery, large parts of the central city and some of the worst-hit residential land were cleared, and building codes and engineering practice were revised. The rebuild cost tens of billions of New Zealand dollars and took many years.

### Making the comparison

The earthquakes were of similar scale and both were shallow and close to a city, so the hazard does not explain the difference in deaths. The reasons are human. Christchurch had stricter building codes, more emergency services, better communication, insurance and a wealthy national government. Haiti had weak construction, a dense and poor population, limited services and little insurance. Wealth also shaped the long-term response: Christchurch rebuilt with its own funds and insurance, while Haiti relied on aid.

## Why people continue to live in risky areas

- **Fertile volcanic soil** supports farming.
- **Geothermal energy** and minerals provide power and jobs.
- **Tourism** based on volcanoes or scenery provides income.
- **Jobs, family and community ties** keep people where they are.
- **Poverty** means people cannot afford to move.
- **Low perceived risk**, because events are rare and the last one may be many years ago.
- **Trust in prediction and protection**, in areas where monitoring and building standards are good.

## Management: monitoring, prediction, protection and planning

**Monitoring.** Seismometers detect earth movements. For volcanoes, scientists measure gas emissions, ground swelling with tiltmeters and satellite data, small earthquakes, and temperature changes.

**Prediction.** Volcanic eruptions can often be forecast days to weeks ahead from warning signs, though not exactly. Earthquakes cannot yet be predicted with useful accuracy. Scientists can only give long-term probabilities for a region.

**Protection.** Earthquake-resistant buildings use steel frames, cross-bracing, base isolation (rubber or steel pads) and shock absorbers. Lower, lighter roofs reduce injuries. Automatic shut-off valves cut gas lines. For volcanoes, barriers or channels can divert lava, and some areas use exclusion zones.

**Planning.** Hazard maps show risk zones. Land-use planning stops important buildings being built in the riskiest places. Evacuation routes, drills in schools, emergency kits and public education improve response. Emergency services can be pre-positioned.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how effects and responses to tectonic hazards vary between two areas of contrasting wealth. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "Comparing Haiti in 2010 and Christchurch in 2011, the effects were much worse in the poorer area. Haiti's weak buildings collapsed on a large scale, so the death toll was extremely high and many people lost their homes. Christchurch had strict building codes, so there were far fewer deaths, although liquefaction caused serious damage. Responses also differed. In Haiti, damage to the port and roads slowed the arrival of aid, and recovery took years because the government had few resources and relied on outside help. In Christchurch, the emergency services responded quickly, most losses were insured and the government funded a recovery agency, so rebuilding started sooner. So wealth affects how well buildings stand up, how quickly help arrives and how well a place recovers."

The answer names both places, gives both effects and responses, and ends with a linking sentence.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying volcanoes occur on conservative margins.** They do not.
- **Mixing up focus and epicentre.** The focus is underground; the epicentre is on the surface.
- **Writing about primary effects when asked for responses.** Responses are what people do, not what the hazard did.
- **Using a rich or poor example without evidence.** Name the place, give a number or detail, and link it to wealth.
- **Saying earthquakes can be predicted.** They can be monitored and forecast only in terms of long-term likelihood.
- **Assuming that magnitude alone explains the death toll.** Depth, distance from people, building quality and time of day matter too.
