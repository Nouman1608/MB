Key question 4.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *What are the different types of
families?* It opens the Family question on Paper 2, and its vocabulary returns in almost every part of
that question: you cannot discuss the role of the family (4.2) or explain how family life is changing
(4.3) without naming structures accurately. A list of family types is the starting point; this guide
shows how to judge those types, how to use the diversity debate, and how to avoid the definitional
slips that cost marks.

The syllabus adds that other family and marriage types, such as polygamous and same-sex families, may
be taught where appropriate to the local context. That content is not assessed, but you may use it to
support an answer where relevant.

## Family or household?

Before comparing structures, separate two terms that candidates often blur.

- A **household** is a person living alone, or a group of people living at the same address and
  sharing living arrangements. They need not be related.
- A **family** is a group linked by kinship: blood, marriage, adoption or, in some definitions, a
  long-term partnership. Its members need not live together.

George Murdock (1949) defined the family as a social group characterised by common residence,
economic cooperation and reproduction, and claimed that the nuclear family is found in every society.
His definition is useful because it is precise, but it is also easy to criticise: it excludes
childless couples, lone parents and relatives who live apart, all of which appear in the 4.1.1 list.
When a question asks you to define a family type, the household/family distinction is often what
makes a definition precise. A *single person household*, for example, is a household but not a
family, which is why the syllabus lists it under alternatives to the family.

## Judging family structures: "strong for whom?"

The syllabus asks for strengths and limitations of each structure. Many answers give one advantage
and one disadvantage without saying who gains or loses. A stronger method is to judge each structure
from the position of different members.

| Structure | Children | Women | Older relatives | Society |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nuclear | Close attention from two parents | May carry most domestic work | Can be isolated from the household | Mobile workforce (a functionalist claim) |
| Horizontal extended | Many carers and role models | Childcare shared among adults | Respect and a clear role | Kin support reduces demands on the state |
| Beanpole | Close ties with grandparents | Middle-generation women may care in both directions | Support from adult children | Fewer siblings and cousins to share care |
| Reconstituted | A second stable home after divorce | Possible conflict over step-parenting | Step-grandparent ties may be weak | Shows family life continuing after divorce |
| Lone parent | Escape from a conflict-filled home | Higher risk of poverty and the dual burden | May be relied on for childcare | New Right see a weak base for socialisation |

Using this grid, you can turn a simple point into a developed one: "The extended family can share
childcare, which allows mothers to take paid work, but the same arrangement may limit privacy for
the couple and give older relatives authority over parenting decisions."

## Variations and diversity (4.1.2)

**Cross-cultural.** Murdock's claim of a universal nuclear family has been challenged by studies of
societies where children were raised without a co-resident father. Kathleen Gough's work on the
Nayar of southern India and Melford Spiro's study of the Israeli kibbutz (*Children of the Kibbutz*,
1958), where children were raised in communal children's houses, are the examples usually used. Both
show that the functions Murdock gave to the nuclear family can be carried out by other arrangements.

**Social class.** Michael Young and Peter Willmott's *Family and Kinship in East London* (1957)
described working-class families in Bethnal Green where married daughters lived close to their
mothers and saw them daily. Middle-class families are more often geographically mobile for careers,
which makes the **modified extended family** more typical: relatives keep in touch by phone, visits
and money rather than living nearby.

**Ethnicity.** Migrant families may keep extended-family traditions, such as the joint family in
South Asian communities, because kin networks provide support in a new country. Over generations,
some families adapt to the patterns of the society they live in, so ethnicity interacts with class and
with time since migration.

## The diversity debate (4.1.3)

The **New Right** treat the married, two-parent nuclear family as the best structure for raising
children. Charles Murray argued that welfare benefits made it possible for women to raise children
without a male breadwinner, and linked lone parenthood to boys growing up without a male role model.
In New Right terms, a **dysfunctional family** is one that fails to socialise children into
conformity and self-reliance.

**Postmodernists** see diversity as a sign of choice. There is no single "normal" family, and people
build relationships that suit them. Judith Stacey described a **divorce-extended family**, in which
people stay connected through former marriages, for example a woman remaining close to her former
mother-in-law.

The third bullet, **the extent of diversity and the dominance of the nuclear family**, asks you to
weigh these views. Census figures give a snapshot of households on one day, so they show many
single-person and lone-parent households. Robert Chester's point was that most people still pass
through a nuclear family at some stage of their life. Diversity in a snapshot and the dominance of
the nuclear family over a lifetime can both be true.

## Alternatives to the family (4.1.4)

- **Single person households** have grown for different reasons in different groups: older widowed
  people living longer, young adults living alone while they establish careers, and people living
  alone after divorce.
- **Shared households** are groups of unrelated people, such as students or young workers sharing
  rent. They give companionship and lower costs without kinship obligations.
- **Friends as family** describes friendships that carry the emotional support and care once expected
  from relatives. Postmodernists see this as chosen intimacy; critics note friendships rarely carry
  the legal and lifelong obligations of kinship.

## Types of marriage (4.1.5)

- **Arranged marriage**: families select or introduce partners, and the couple consents. It must not
  be confused with **forced marriage**, where one or both partners do not consent; this is a crime in
  many countries.
- **Love marriage**: partners choose each other on the basis of romantic attraction. Functionalist
  Ronald Fletcher argued that people now expect more from marriage, so they are more likely to leave a
  marriage that fails to meet those expectations.
- **Empty shell marriage**: the couple stay together in name only, for reasons such as children,
  finances, religion or reputation. Divorce statistics do not count these, so marriage may look more
  stable than it is.
- **Monogamy** is marriage to one partner at a time. **Serial monogamy** is a sequence of such
  marriages, which is one route into the reconstituted family.

## Worked example: a definition and an explanation

**Question:** Define the term *reconstituted family*.

**Model answer:** A family in which at least one adult has a child or children from a previous
relationship, formed when a lone parent marries or cohabits with a new partner.

*Why it works:* it names the defining feature (children from a previous relationship) and how the
family forms. "A step-family" alone is a synonym, not a definition.

**Question:** Explain why the nuclear family may not be universal.

**Annotated answer:** "Murdock claimed the nuclear family exists in all societies **[states the view]**.
However, in the kibbutz children lived in communal houses and were raised by trained carers, so key
socialisation tasks were done outside the nuclear unit **[evidence and mechanism]**. In many societies
today children are raised in lone-parent or extended households, so the nuclear family is one
arrangement among several rather than a universal one **[applies to the present]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating household and family as the same thing.
- Calling every arranged marriage forced, or assuming that love marriages are always more stable.
- Mixing up **beanpole** (many generations, few members in each) with **horizontal** extended (more
  members of the same generation).
- Assuming lone-parent families are always headed by mothers, or that they are always poor.
- Describing the New Right and postmodernist views without saying which side each takes on whether
  diversity is good.
- Quoting census figures as proof the nuclear family has disappeared, without the life-course
  argument.

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Define**: one or two precise sentences with the defining feature, not an example alone.
- **Give / Identify**: a short, accurate answer, such as naming two alternatives to the family.
- **Explain**: give a point, the reason or mechanism behind it, and an example. "Explain three
  limitations" needs three separate, developed points.
- **Discuss**: write in depth and in a structured way, for instance setting out the postmodernist
  view, supporting it with Stacey, then considering criticisms.
- **Evaluate / Assess**: weigh both sides and finish with a judgement, for example on how dominant
  the nuclear family still is.
