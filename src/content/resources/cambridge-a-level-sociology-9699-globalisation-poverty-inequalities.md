Section 8.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks whether globalisation narrows or widens the gap between rich and poor societies. The syllabus lists three strands: debates about the impact of globalisation on life chances in developing countries, in relation to education, income and health; the role of transnational organisations in tackling global inequalities and the extent to which they have been successful; and sociological explanations for global inequalities, including capitalism, colonialism, post-colonialism and patriarchy. It links to the key concepts of Power, control and resistance and Inequality and opportunity, and is examined in Paper 4 Section A.

The [global inequality, migration and crime guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-contemporary-issues/) introduces Rostow, Frank, Rana Plaza, the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals, Sachs, Moyo, Wallerstein and Mies. This page adds tools for measuring life chances, further evidence in each of the three areas the syllabus names, and a wider range of explanations so that essays can compare theories rather than list them.

## Measuring life chances

Life chances are the opportunities a person has to achieve a good life: education, income, health and long life. Measurement matters, because the same country can look successful or unsuccessful depending on the indicator.

- **Income per head** shows average economic output but hides distribution.
- The **Human Development Index**, published by the United Nations Development Programme since 1990, combines life expectancy, education and income, reflecting Amartya Sen's argument that development means expanding people's capabilities, not just raising income.
- **Inequality within countries** can rise even as average incomes grow, so an essay should ask *whose* life chances changed.

Terminology is itself debated. "Developing countries", "less economically developed countries" and "Global South" carry different assumptions; modernisation theory's language of "developing" implies a single path towards Western societies.

## Life chances: education

**Optimistic evidence.** Several countries abolished primary school fees and saw enrolment rise sharply, including Malawi in 1994, Uganda in 1997 and Kenya in 2003. Global goals focused attention and aid on universal primary schooling.

**Critical evidence.** Rapid enrolment often outpaced teachers and classrooms, so quality fell. In the 1980s and 1990s, structural adjustment programmes attached to loans were associated with spending cuts and the introduction of user fees in some countries, which critics argued pushed poorer children out of school. Girls' access remains more limited in some regions, which links to patriarchy as an explanation.

## Life chances: income

**Optimistic evidence.** Countries that integrated into global trade, notably China after the economic reforms that began in the late 1970s, saw very large reductions in extreme poverty. Microfinance pioneered by Muhammad Yunus's Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, recognised with the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006, extended small loans to poor people, especially women. Remittances from migrants support many households.

**Critical evidence.** Export processing zones can offer low pay and insecure work; critics of microfinance argue some borrowers fell into cycles of debt. Gains have been concentrated in some regions, while countries dependent on a single commodity remain vulnerable to price changes.

**Paul Collier**, in *The Bottom Billion* (2007), argued that a group of the poorest countries are caught in traps — conflict, dependence on natural resources, being landlocked with poor neighbours, and bad governance — that global integration alone does not solve.

## Life chances: health

**Optimistic evidence.** The World Health Organization's campaign led to smallpox being declared eradicated in 1980. Global partnerships such as Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance (launched 2000), and the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (2002) expanded vaccination and treatment.

**Critical evidence.** Patent rules under global trade agreements were blamed for keeping HIV medicines expensive; the Doha Declaration (2001) affirmed that countries could prioritise public health in applying these rules. Brain drain of doctors and nurses weakens health systems, and relocated industry can bring pollution.

**Analytical point.** Health shows that globalisation's effects depend on *which* global forces dominate: cooperation through health partnerships improves life chances, while market rules can restrict them.

## How successful are transnational organisations?

| Organisation or initiative | Claimed success | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **IMF and World Bank lending** | Finance for infrastructure and stability | Structural adjustment conditions linked to cuts; Joseph Stiglitz, *Globalization and Its Discontents* (2002), criticised the IMF's policies |
| **Highly Indebted Poor Countries initiative (1996)** | Debt relief freed funds for services | Relief came with conditions; slow to arrive |
| **Jubilee 2000 campaign** | Global civil society pressure for debt cancellation | Limited to debt; did not change trade rules |
| **Global health partnerships** | Large-scale vaccination and treatment | Dependent on donor priorities |
| **NGOs** | Local projects, emergency relief | Small scale; may bypass local government |

**Judging success.** Ask three questions: success measured by what indicator; success for whom; and success compared with what alternative. A strong essay distinguishes organisations rather than treating "transnational organisations" as one actor.

## Explaining global inequalities

**Capitalism.** World-systems theory (Wallerstein) places countries in a core, semi-periphery and periphery; inequality is built into a global division of labour. Dependency theory (Frank) sees underdevelopment as produced by integration on unequal terms.

**Colonialism.** Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) argued that colonial rule extracted resources and labour and reshaped African economies around exports to Europe, leaving them dependent.

**Post-colonialism.** Kwame Nkrumah, in *Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism* (1965), argued that formally independent states remained economically controlled from outside. Post-colonial analysis adds cultural dimensions: former colonial powers' languages, institutions and ideas continue to shape development policy.

**Patriarchy.** Ester Boserup's *Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970) showed that development projects often ignored women's agricultural work and directed resources to men. Diana Pearce's concept of the **feminisation of poverty** (1978) describes women's over-representation among the poor. Mies adds that global capitalism depends on women's unpaid and low-paid labour.

**Neo-liberal and modernisation explanations.** These locate the causes of poverty mainly inside poor countries — weak institutions, conflict, lack of investment — and see global integration as the solution. Collier's traps belong broadly here, although he supports targeted outside help.

## Comparing explanations

| Explanation | Cause located | Solution implied |
|---|---|---|
| Modernisation / neo-liberal | Internal barriers | More integration and investment |
| Dependency / world-systems | Structure of global capitalism | Delinking or changing the system |
| Colonial / post-colonial | History and continuing external control | Ending neo-colonial relationships |
| Feminist | Patriarchy within and across societies | Gender-focused development |

## Worked paragraph

> Dependency theorists argue that globalisation reproduces inequality because poor countries are integrated on unequal terms. Rodney's account of colonialism shows how African economies were shaped around exports to Europe, a pattern that post-colonial writers such as Nkrumah argued continued after independence. However, China's large reduction in extreme poverty after its late-1970s reforms suggests integration into global trade can raise living standards. A balanced judgement is that outcomes depend on the terms of integration and on the state's capacity to manage it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "developing countries" as one group.
- Using averages without asking about distribution within countries.
- Forgetting the syllabus's three areas — education, income and health — in a life-chances essay.
- Listing explanations without comparing their causes and implied solutions.
