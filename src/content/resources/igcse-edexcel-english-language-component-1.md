This guide covers **Component 1 – Non-fiction Texts and Transactional
Writing**, for Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Language A
(4EA1), Issue 6 (2023). Language syllabuses are organised around named
components and skills rather than a numbered content taxonomy, since
the subject matter of any given exam's texts changes each series.

## Where this fits in 4EA1

Component 1 pairs reading and writing around the same broad genre —
non-fiction — testing reading comprehension and analysis skills in
Section A, then transactional (purpose-driven, real-world) writing
skills in Section B. It sits alongside Component 2 (Poetry and Prose
Texts and Imaginative Writing, not covered in this guide), which pairs
literary reading with creative writing instead. Confirm with your
teacher the exact weighting between the two components for your own
exam series before deciding how to split revision time.

## Syllabus coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LANGUAGE A (4EA1) —
COMPONENT 1**

- 1.1 Section A: Non-fiction Texts — reading unseen non-fiction
extracts and answering questions testing comprehension, inference, and
analysis of a writer's language, structure and viewpoint
- 1.2 Section B: Transactional Writing — writing for a specified
real-world purpose and audience (such as a letter, article, speech or
report), assessed on both content/organisation and technical accuracy

## How to approach it

Section A rewards precision over volume — quoting a short, well-chosen
piece of textual evidence and explaining its specific effect
consistently scores higher than a longer answer that only paraphrases
the text or quotes without analysing the language choice itself.
Practising with unseen non-fiction extracts under timed conditions is
the most direct way to build this skill, since Component 1's texts are
by definition unfamiliar on the day. For Section B, transactional
writing is graded on how precisely a response matches its stated
purpose, audience and form — a letter that reads like a generic essay,
or a speech that ignores its audience, loses marks regardless of how
well-written the prose is in the abstract. Building a mental checklist
of purpose, audience and form for each piece of practice writing, and
reviewing it before submitting an answer, is a simple habit that
directly targets how this section is actually marked.

Both sections are timed within the same paper, so practising complete Component 1 papers under exam conditions — not just isolated Section A or Section B exercises — is what actually builds the pacing and switching-between-skills that the real exam demands.
