## Market orientation and the uses of research

A **market-orientated** business finds out what customers want **before** it designs and sells a product. A **product-orientated** business concentrates on making a product it believes is good and then tries to sell it. Market orientation reduces the risk of launching products nobody buys.

Market research information helps a business to:

- find out whether there is enough demand before launching a product
- learn what features, prices and packaging customers prefer
- identify which segments to target and where they buy
- discover what competitors offer and charge
- understand why sales are falling or customers are complaining
- judge whether an advertising campaign has worked.

## Primary and secondary research

| | Primary (field) research | Secondary (desk) research |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | New data collected by or for the business for its own purpose | Data that already exists, collected by someone else or for another purpose |
| Benefits | Up to date; answers the business's exact questions; competitors do not have it | Quick and often cheap; gives an overview of the whole market; useful before primary research |
| Limitations | Expensive and slow; needs skill to design; results depend on the sample | May be out of date; collected for another purpose; competitors can use it too; commercial reports can be costly |

### Methods of primary research

| Method | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Postal questionnaire | Reaches a wide area cheaply; respondents answer in their own time | Low response rate; slow; no chance to explain unclear questions |
| Online survey | Very cheap; fast; results analysed automatically | Only reaches people online; some may answer carelessly or more than once |
| Interview (face-to-face or by phone) | Interviewer can explain questions and ask follow-up questions | Time-consuming and costly; interviewer may influence answers |
| Focus group | A small group discusses a product in depth; reveals reasons and feelings | Few people, so may not represent the market; strong voices can dominate |
| Observation | Shows what people actually do, such as how many pass a shop | Shows behaviour, not the reasons for it |

### Methods of secondary research

- **Online sources**: competitors' websites, online reviews, trade association pages.
- **Government sources**: population census figures, income and spending statistics, trade data.
- **Commercial market research reports**: detailed studies of a market, bought from research agencies.
- **Internal records**: the business's own sales, customer and complaint data.
- **Newspapers and magazines**: articles on market trends.

## Designing a questionnaire

Accurate primary data starts with good questions.

| Type | Example (invented) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Closed question | "How often do you buy bottled water? Daily / Weekly / Monthly / Never" | Easy to answer and to count; limits detail |
| Open question | "What would make you buy our water instead of your usual brand?" | Gives reasons and new ideas; harder to analyse |
| Leading question (avoid) | "Don't you agree our water tastes better?" | Pushes the respondent towards an answer, so data is biased |

A good questionnaire is short, uses clear language, puts easy questions first and avoids questions that are personal without reason.

## The need for sampling

A business cannot usually ask every possible customer (the whole **population** of its target market) because it would take too long and cost too much. Instead it asks a **sample**, a smaller group chosen to represent the population.

- The sample should **represent** the target market: a survey about a new toy should ask parents, not only teenagers.
- A **larger sample** is generally more reliable but costs more.
- A sample chosen only from people who are easy to reach, such as those in one shop on one day, may give a misleading picture.

## Factors influencing accuracy

| Factor | Why it affects accuracy |
|---|---|
| Sample size | A very small sample may not reflect the market |
| Who is in the sample | If some customer groups are left out, results are biased |
| Question design | Leading or unclear questions produce misleading answers |
| Honesty of respondents | People may give answers they think are expected |
| Interviewer influence | Tone or wording can push answers one way |
| Response rate | If few reply, those who do may not be typical |
| Age of secondary data | Old data may no longer describe the market |
| Original purpose of secondary data | It may not match the business's exact question |

## Analysing results and drawing conclusions

**Worked example (invented data).** Fernhill Bakery plans a new wholemeal range. It surveys 250 customers chosen across its three shops on different days of the week.

**Question 1: "Would you buy a wholemeal loaf priced at $2.20?"**

| Answer | Number |
|---|---|
| Definitely | 60 |
| Probably | 90 |
| Probably not | 70 |
| Definitely not | 30 |

```
Definitely or probably: (60 + 90) ÷ 250 × 100 = 60%
Definitely only: 60 ÷ 250 × 100 = 24%
```

**Question 2: average rating of a free sample, 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent), by age group.**

| Age group | Under 30 | 30–50 | Over 50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average rating | 2.8 | 3.9 | 4.4 |

**Conclusions a business can act on:**

- 60% say they would at least probably buy, which suggests reasonable demand, but only 24% are definite. "Probably" answers often overstate real buying, so the bakery should start with a small range or a trial period.
- The rating rises with age, from 2.8 to 4.4, so promotion should target customers over 30, and the product may need changing if younger customers matter.
- The sample covered all three shops and different days, which improves its accuracy, but it includes only existing customers, not people who shop elsewhere.

When describing a chart, state the **trend**, quote **figures** from it, compare **groups**, and say what the business should **do**. Avoid claiming more than the data shows: a survey of 250 people does not prove what every customer will do.

## Common mistakes

- Saying secondary research is always cheap; commercial reports can be expensive.
- Describing a method without a benefit and limitation linked to the business.
- Confusing a sample with the population.
- Copying numbers from a chart without drawing a conclusion.
- Treating survey answers as certain future sales.

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Identify / State**: name a method or an accuracy factor.
- **Calculate**: show the percentage from the table with working.
- **Explain**: give the method's benefit or limitation and why it matters for this business.
- **Consider / Justify**: compare two methods for the case and recommend one, or judge how far the data supports a decision.
