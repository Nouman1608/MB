## Section A

**1.** Define the term *meritocracy*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *correspondence principle*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** roles of education identified by functionalists. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term *role allocation*. **[2]**

**5.** Give **two** material factors that may disadvantage working-class pupils in education. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** examples of gendered subject choice. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** ways in which functionalists argue education benefits society. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** ways in which Marxists argue the hidden curriculum serves capitalism. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** limitations of the functionalist view of education. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss the feminist view that teacher expectations and peer groups reinforce gender inequality in education.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss how different sociological perspectives interpret setting by ability.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that education mainly serves the interests of the ruling class.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** A system in which rewards, such as qualifications and jobs, are based on ability and effort (1) rather than on family background, class, gender or ethnicity (1).

**2. [2]** Bowles and Gintis's idea that the relationships and rules of school (the hidden curriculum) (1) mirror those of the capitalist workplace, preparing pupils to be obedient workers (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: socialisation; social control; economic; selective (role allocation).

**4. [2]** The process of sorting people, through education and qualifications (1), into the jobs that best match their abilities, with the most able going into the most important roles (1). Credit Davis and Moore.

**5. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: limited access to digital technology or the internet; lack of quiet study space; inability to pay for tutoring, trips or books; poor diet or health; needing to work part-time or care for siblings.

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each, with gender stated: more boys choosing physics, engineering or computing; more girls choosing subjects such as health and social care, languages or the arts; boys choosing technical vocational courses and girls choosing caring courses.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three ways, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Socialisation and social solidarity**: Durkheim argued schools pass on shared culture, so children feel part of society.
- **Bridge to wider society**: Parsons argued school teaches universalistic standards and achieved status, preparing children for adult life.
- **Role allocation**: exams sort pupils by ability into appropriate jobs (Davis and Moore), using talent efficiently.
- **Economic role**: schools teach the skills the economy needs, including through vocational courses.
- **Social control**: rules and sanctions teach conformity to norms.
- **Social mobility**: able pupils from any background can rise through qualifications.

**8. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **Obedience to authority**: obeying teachers prepares pupils to obey managers without question.
- **Extrinsic rewards**: working for grades rather than interest prepares pupils to work for wages in jobs they find unrewarding.
- **Acceptance of hierarchy**: the school's hierarchy of head, teachers and pupils mirrors the workplace hierarchy.
- **Fragmentation**: learning split into separate subjects mirrors work split into small, repetitive tasks.
- **Punctuality and discipline**: bells and timetables prepare a disciplined workforce.

**9. [6]** Three limitations, 1 + 1 each:
- **Not meritocratic**: achievement gaps by class, ethnicity and gender show background still matters.
- **Ignores conflict and resistance**: pupils form anti-school subcultures (Willis), so they do not all accept shared values.
- **Whose values?** Marxists argue the values taught are those of the ruling class, not a true consensus.
- **Ignores gender inequality**: feminists point to gendered subject choice and male power in schools.
- **Qualifications and jobs do not always match**: many people are over-qualified or unable to find jobs that match their education.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- **Teacher expectations**: teachers may expect boys to be confident and girls to be neat and quiet, affecting how pupils are treated and which subjects they are encouraged to take.
- **Classroom attention**: Spender (1982) found boys dominated classroom talk and received more teacher attention.
- **Subject advice**: careers guidance may steer girls and boys into gender-typed courses.
- **Peer group policing**: teasing and labels pressure girls who are assertive and boys who are studious or caring, enforcing gender norms (informal social control).
- **Role models**: few women teaching physics or leading schools reinforces stereotypes.
- Evaluation: girls' high achievement in many countries suggests expectations have changed; boys can also be disadvantaged by peer pressure against studying.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Functionalist**: setting matches teaching to ability, so each pupil learns at a suitable pace and talent is identified for role allocation.
- **Marxist**: working-class pupils are more often placed in lower sets, where expectations and exam entry levels may be lower; setting legitimates class inequality as a difference in ability.
- **Interactionist and labelling**: placement in a low set can work as a label, producing a self-fulfilling prophecy and anti-school subcultures.
- **Feminist**: sets and subject options may reflect gendered expectations.
- Evaluation: the effect depends on whether pupils can move between sets and on how decisions are made.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Althusser: education is an ideological state apparatus passing on ruling-class ideas.
- Bowles and Gintis: the correspondence principle prepares an obedient workforce; the myth of meritocracy legitimates inequality.
- Bourdieu: schools reward middle-class cultural capital.
- Material inequalities, such as access to digital technology and private tutoring, favour wealthier families.
- Private and selective schools give the children of the wealthy privileged routes to top jobs.
*Against the view:*
- Functionalists: education benefits society as a whole through socialisation, skills and role allocation.
- Many working-class pupils achieve upward social mobility through education.
- Willis: pupils resist school values, so ideological control is incomplete.
- Feminists: gender inequality, not only class, shapes education; men of all classes may benefit.
- Policies such as free schooling and compensatory programmes aim to reduce class inequality.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that education benefits wider society but class advantage strongly shapes who benefits most.
