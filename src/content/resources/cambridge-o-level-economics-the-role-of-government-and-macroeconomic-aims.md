## 4.1.1 The role of government

A government makes rules, raises money and spends it on behalf of a society. Its role differs with the level at which it acts.

| Level | What it does | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Provides services that serve one community and controls how local land is used | A district council licenses market stalls, maintains drains and grants or refuses permission to build |
| National | Makes laws, runs courts, police and defence, taxes and spends, funds or provides services such as schooling, regulates firms and pursues the macroeconomic aims | A national government sets a minimum standard for food safety and pays for vaccination programmes |
| International | Agrees rules and joint action with other governments, makes trade agreements, shares costs of cross-border problems, gives and receives aid | Neighbouring governments agree a limit on fishing in shared waters |

### Why governments are needed

Free markets leave gaps. Some goods, such as flood defences, would be under-supplied because non-payers cannot be kept out. Some activities harm third parties. Incomes can be very unequal. Output and jobs can swing up and down. The role of government is to fill these gaps, while the macroeconomic aims set out what it tries to achieve for the economy as a whole.

### Why three levels

The level should match the scale of the problem.

```
Blocked local drains            -> local: the council knows and can fix it
Falling national output         -> national: only the national government sets tax and spending
Shared fish stock being depleted -> international: one country acting alone cannot stop others
```

Chain of reasoning for "explain why an international agreement is needed to protect a shared fish stock": each country gains from catching more now; the cost of a shrinking stock falls on all of them; if one country limits its catch, others simply take more; so only a joint limit with monitoring protects the stock. A good answer moves through four steps: the cause, its effect, why the smaller unit cannot cure it, and what joint action adds.

## 4.2.1 The macroeconomic aims

These aims are about the economy as a whole rather than any single market. A **criterion** is the measurable standard a government sets so it can tell whether an aim has been met.

| Aim | Meaning | Example criterion (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | A sustained rise in real output | Real GDP to rise by 3% to 5% a year |
| Full employment / low unemployment | Almost everyone willing and able to work has a job | Unemployment rate below 4.5% |
| Stable prices / low inflation | A low, predictable rise in the general price level | Annual inflation between 1% and 3% |
| Balance of payments stability | Money flowing out is not persistently far above or below money flowing in | Current account within 3% of GDP, deficit or surplus |
| Redistribution of income | Narrowing the gap between high and low incomes | A smaller income share for the richest fifth |

### Worked example 1: economic growth

Country Q's nominal GDP is 640 in year 1 and 707.2 in year 2 (invented units). The price index rises from 100 to 104.

```
Nominal growth = (707.2 - 640) / 640 x 100 = 10.5%
Real GDP, year 2 = 707.2 / 1.04 = 680
Real growth = (680 - 640) / 640 x 100 = 6.25%
```

The criterion is 3% to 5%, so real growth of 6.25% exceeds it. Judging by the nominal 10.5% would mislead, because part of that rise is only higher prices. Growth is judged on real output, not on money values.

### Worked example 2: the unemployment rate

In year 1 Country Q has 9,120 thousand people in work and 480 thousand unemployed. In year 3 the figures are 9,300 and 420.

```
Year 1: labour force = 9,120 + 480 = 9,600; rate = 480 / 9,600 x 100 = 5.0%
Year 3: labour force = 9,300 + 420 = 9,720; rate = 420 / 9,720 x 100 = 4.32%
```

Year 1 misses the below-4.5% criterion; year 3 meets it. Always divide by the labour force, not by those in work. Full employment still allows some unemployment, since people keep moving between jobs.

### Worked example 3: the inflation rate

A price index is 112.0 in one year and 115.36 in the next.

```
Inflation = (115.36 - 112.0) / 112.0 x 100 = 3.0%
Purchasing power of 100 = 100 / 1.03 = 97.09
```

Prices are still rising, so 3% is not "no change". It is the upper limit of the 1% to 3% band, which means the aim is only just achieved. The aim is low and predictable inflation, not zero.

### Worked example 4: balance of payments

Country R's current account for a year (invented units, GDP 500):

| Item | Balance |
|---|---|
| Trade in goods | +15 |
| Trade in services | -4 |
| Net primary income | +2 |
| Net secondary income | -3 |

```
Current account = 15 - 4 + 2 - 3 = +10
As a share of GDP = 10 / 500 x 100 = 2.0%
```

A surplus of 2.0% is inside the 3% criterion. Stability does not demand a perfect balance. A large, persistent deficit is the worry, because it must be paid for by borrowing or selling assets. A large surplus can also be a concern, because it means partners are running deficits with the country.

### Worked example 5: redistribution

Country W charges no tax on the first 9,000 of income, 15% on the slice from 9,000 to 27,000 and 30% on the slice above 27,000 (invented).

```
Person J earns 21,000: tax = 12,000 x 15% = 1,800; average rate 8.57%; income after tax 19,200
Person K earns 63,000: tax = (18,000 x 15%) + (36,000 x 30%) = 2,700 + 10,800 = 13,500; average rate 21.43%; after tax 49,500
Before tax K earns 63,000 / 21,000 = 3.0 times J; after tax 49,500 / 19,200 = 2.58 times
```

The gap narrows. Spending the revenue on benefits and services for low earners narrows it further.

### Reasons behind the choice of aims

Governments choose aims because each links to the welfare of citizens, but the choice and the criteria vary with circumstances.

- **Living standards.** Growth and jobs raise incomes; stable prices protect what incomes buy; redistribution reduces poverty.
- **Current problems.** Heavy unemployment pushes jobs up the list; a big, lasting deficit pushes the external balance up it.
- **Stage of development and resources.** A poorer country may stress growth; a richer one may stress stability.
- **Public support.** Voters care about jobs and prices, so governments rank those aims highly.
- **Measurability.** A numerical criterion lets the government, and voters, check whether the aim was met.

## 4.2.2 Conflicts between aims

Pursuing one aim can make another harder, so governments must trade off or pick priorities. For each conflict, write a chain of cause and effect, then evaluate.

### Full employment versus stable prices

```
Higher demand -> firms produce more and hire -> unemployment falls
-> labour becomes scarce, wages rise -> costs rise
-> firms raise prices; strong demand lets them -> inflation rises
```

Illustration (invented): Country S has a labour force of 4,000 thousand. Unemployment falls from 9% to 6%, so jobless numbers drop from 360 to 240 thousand, but inflation rises from 2.0% to 5.5%.

Evaluation points:
- The conflict is sharper near full capacity, when output cannot rise to meet demand.
- It is weaker with spare capacity, where output can rise without higher prices.
- It may not arise if unemployment falls because of better skills or lower costs, which raise supply.
- In the short run it may be accepted; in the long run, persistent inflation harms savers and competitiveness.

### Economic growth versus balance of payments stability

Country T's GDP rises from 800 to 856 (7%). Imports are 22% of GDP and exports are 170.

```
Imports before = 0.22 x 800 = 176; balance = 170 - 176 = -6
Imports after  = 0.22 x 856 = 188.32; balance = 170 - 188.32 = -18.32
If exports also rise 7%: 170 x 1.07 = 181.9; balance = 181.9 - 188.32 = -6.42
```

Growth with flat exports about triples the deficit. If growth is export-led, the deficit barely changes. Evaluation: the conflict is smaller when growth comes from exports, when imports are machinery that raises future output, or when the country starts in surplus.

### Full employment versus balance of payments stability

Suppose 100,000 new workers each earn 15,000 a year. Extra income is 1,500 million. If households spend 28% of extra income on imports, imports rise by 420 million. Higher demand may also raise domestic prices, making exports dearer abroad. Both effects worsen the current account. The conflict is weaker if the new jobs are in export firms or in firms that replace imports.

## Common errors

- Treating full employment as zero unemployment, or stable prices as no price change.
- Using nominal GDP when real growth is asked for.
- Dividing unemployed people by those in work.
- Asserting a conflict ("growth is bad for the balance of payments") without a chain of cause and effect.
- Listing aims with no reason for choosing them.
- Ending a discussion without a supported judgement about which effect is larger and why.
