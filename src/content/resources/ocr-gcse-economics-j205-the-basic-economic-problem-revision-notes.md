These notes condense section 1.2, The basic economic problem, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. For worked examples and a model evaluation, read the [1.2 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem/); then try the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Resources are limited relative to the wants they could satisfy |
| Unlimited wants | People always want more goods and services; new wants replace satisfied ones |
| Economic problem | Scarce resources and unlimited wants force choices about what, how and for whom to produce |
| Allocation of resources | How factors of production are shared between different uses |
| Opportunity cost | The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made |
| Non-renewable resource | Cannot be replaced once used, such as oil |
| Renewable resource | Can regrow or be replaced, such as forests, if not overused |
| Economic sustainability | Income, jobs and output can be maintained over time |
| Social sustainability | Well-being, fairness and communities are supported now and in future |
| Environmental sustainability | Natural resources and the climate are protected for the future |

## Scarcity in four lines

- Applies to individuals (time, income), firms (budgets, space), governments (tax revenue, land) and whole economies.
- Wants grow as incomes rise, so scarcity never ends.
- Scarcity is permanent; a shortage is a temporary lack of one product in one market.
- Using non-renewable resources now leaves less for the future: the link to sustainability.

## The economic problem

| Question | Example decision |
|---|---|
| What to produce? | A hospital or a motorway |
| How to produce? | Labour-intensive or capital-intensive; low-carbon or cheapest materials |
| For whom? | Those willing and able to pay, or those in greatest need |
| How to allocate resources? | Through markets and prices, the government, or a mix |

**Markets versus government:**

- Markets allocate by price: quick to respond to what people want, but people on low incomes may go without.
- Government allocates by need: can be fairer, but without price rationing there may be queues or waiting lists.

## Opportunity cost: method

```
1. List the options the decision-maker could really choose.
2. Rank them (or value them).
3. Identify the one chosen.
4. Opportunity cost = the single next best option, with its value if given.
```

**Worked reminder.** A council has £2 million. Option values to residents: library refurbishment £2.4m of benefit, new pitches £2.1m, a car park £1.5m. It chooses the library. Opportunity cost: **the pitches**, valued at £2.1m of benefit.

- Time has an opportunity cost: an hour of revision may cost an hour of paid work.
- Goods free to the user (parks, state schooling) still have an opportunity cost to society.
- Producers face opportunity cost too: a factory line used for one product cannot make another.

## Evaluating a choice: the five-step frame

1. State the choice and its opportunity cost.
2. Benefits, and who gains.
3. Costs, and who loses.
4. Short run against long run.
5. Judgement: "On balance... because... It depends on..."

Use all three types of sustainability where relevant, and say which one each point refers to.

**Worked reminder.** A firm spends £36,000 a year on greener packaging; its profit is £240,000. Cost as a share of profit = 36,000 ÷ 240,000 × 100 = **15%**. Benefit: 4.8 tonnes less plastic a year. Judgement depends on whether the firm can pass part of the cost on to customers.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Scarcity versus shortage:** permanent and universal versus temporary and in one market.
- **Opportunity cost versus money cost:** a choice can cost nothing in money and still give up an alternative, such as time.
- **Renewable versus non-renewable:** renewable resources are only sustainable if used no faster than they recover.
- **Allocation by price versus by need:** speed and incentives versus fairness and access.

## Exam traps

- Giving all the alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Confusing scarcity with a shortage.
- Writing that "free" services have no opportunity cost.
- Using "sustainable" without saying economic, social or environmental.
- Lists of costs and benefits with no judgement.
- Not using the figures provided in the question.

## Quick self-test

1. Define scarcity.
2. Explain why wants are described as unlimited.
3. State the three questions of the economic problem.
4. Give the difference between scarcity and a shortage.
5. A student can work a shift for £45, go to a match, or revise. She ranks the match first and the shift second. State her opportunity cost of going to the match.
6. Explain why a free museum still has an opportunity cost.
7. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of allocating a service by price.
8. Name one non-renewable and one renewable resource.
9. A firm could use a field for a car park earning £8,000 a year or solar panels earning £11,000 a year. It chooses the solar panels. State the opportunity cost.
10. Give one social and one environmental cost of building houses on farmland.
11. State what an "evaluate" answer must include.

### Answers

1. Resources are limited compared with the wants they could satisfy.
2. When some wants are satisfied, new ones appear, especially as incomes rise.
3. What to produce, how to produce it and for whom to produce it.
4. Scarcity is permanent and applies to all resources; a shortage is a temporary lack of one product in a market.
5. The shift, worth £45.
6. The land, building and tax revenue used for it could have been used for something else.
7. Advantage: responds quickly to what people want (or gives firms an incentive to supply). Disadvantage: people on low incomes may go without.
8. Non-renewable: oil, coal, natural gas or metal ores. Renewable: forests, fish stocks or wind.
9. The car park, worth £8,000 a year.
10. Social: more traffic or pressure on schools and doctors. Environmental: loss of green space or wildlife habitat.
11. Both sides (or alternatives) weighed and a supported judgement.
