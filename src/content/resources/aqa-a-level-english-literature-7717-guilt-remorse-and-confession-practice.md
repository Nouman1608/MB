## Section A: the element in the set texts

**1.** Explain the difference between guilt and remorse, with an example of a character who shows one
without the other. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why Claudius's confession in the prayer scene of *Hamlet* does not lead to forgiveness.
**[2]**

**3.** Explain how the dramatic monologue form turns a speaker's words into a confession, with
reference to one Browning poem. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how Coleridge presents confession as incomplete in *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*.
**[3]**

**5.** Explain how the final section of *Atonement* changes the reader's understanding of Briony's
desire for forgiveness. **[3]**

## Section B: an unseen extract and essay skills

**Unseen extract (original prose monologue).** A retired schoolmaster speaks to a young curate who
has come to visit him.

> You'll want the window open. Everyone does, at first; the room smells of medicine. No, sit there,
> where the light is. I like to see a face when I talk. You've heard the story, I suppose, about the
> boy who drowned at the weir, the year before I retired. They said I should have been with them,
> that I let the older boys take the little ones down alone. Well, I was marking. Forty essays on
> the causes of the war, and not one of them could spell "Versailles". I mention it only so that you
> understand how much there was to do. His mother sends a card every Christmas. No message. Just her
> name. I keep them in the drawer there, eleven of them. You needn't look. I know how many there
> are.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents the speaker's guilt in the extract. **[8]**

**7.** Explain the significance of the curate as listener. **[3]**

**8.** Identify two features of the extract that suggest the speaker is unreliable, and explain the
effect of one. **[3]**

**9.** Explain one connection between the extract and a set text you have studied in the way guilt is
presented. **[3]**

**10.** A student writes: "Sikes feels guilty after he kills Nancy." Explain two ways to develop this
into an analytical point. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer for the question: "Crime writing is less interested in punishment than
in the criminal's conscience." List four elements of your plan, naming two texts not used in your
Section B answer. **[4]**

**12.** Explain how an alternative interpretation (AO5) of the ending of *Brighton Rock* could be used
in an essay on forgiveness. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Guilt is responsibility for the act; remorse is sorrow for it [1]. Example of one without the
other [2]: for instance, the Duke in "My Last Duchess" is guilty of ordering his wife's death [1] but
shows no remorse, speaking of it as a matter of taste [1]; or Briony feels deep remorse for a false
accusation the law never punished.

**2.** He names his crime but cannot repent while he keeps the crown, his ambition and his queen [1],
so, in the words of the scene's closing couplet, his words rise without his thoughts and the prayer
fails [1].

**3.** The form gives one speaker addressing a silent listener [1], so the reader sees more than the
speaker intends [1]; for example, the Duke's account of commands after which "all smiles stopped
together" reveals a murder he presents as a matter of taste [1].

**4.** The Mariner's release begins when he blesses the water-snakes and the albatross falls [1]; he is
shriven by the Hermit [1], but an agony returns and compels him to retell the tale, so confession
must be repeated and forgiveness is never final [1].

**5.** It reveals that Briony is the author of the narrative [1] and that the lovers' reunion was
invented [1], so her desire for forgiveness is expressed through fiction, which she herself doubts can
achieve atonement [1].

**6.** Any eight, [1] each:

- The opening concern with the window and the smell of medicine delays the subject, as if he avoids it.
- "I like to see a face" suggests a need to watch his listener's judgement.
- "You've heard the story, I suppose" distances him, as if the event belongs to gossip.
- "They said I should have been with them" presents the accusation as others' words, not his own.
- "Well, I was marking" is a defensive justification, introduced with a hesitating "Well".
- The detail of forty essays and the misspelling of "Versailles" diverts into irritation, a
  displacement of guilt.
- "I mention it only so that you understand" shows he is aware of making an excuse.
- The mother's card with "No message. Just her name" is a silent accusation; the short sentences
  mark its weight.
- Keeping the cards in a drawer suggests guilt kept hidden but preserved.
- "I know how many there are" ends on a precise number that reveals he counts them, so guilt is
  constant.

**7.** The curate is a figure of the church, so the monologue becomes a kind of confession [1]; the
speaker controls the conversation (where he sits, what he looks at) [1], which suggests he seeks
absolution without fully asking for it [1].

**8.** Two features [1 each]: the diversion into the essays; reporting the accusation as "They said";
the claim "I mention it only so…"; the instruction "You needn't look". Effect of one [1]: for
example, "They said" lets him avoid admitting the fault in his own voice, so the reader suspects the
accusation is true.

**9.** A connection identified [1], supported by reference to a set text [1], with an explanation of the
shared method or effect [1]: for example, like the Duke in "My Last Duchess", the speaker controls a
silent listener and reveals more than he intends; or, like Sikes haunted by Nancy's eyes, he is
haunted by an image (the cards) that keeps the victim present.

**10.** Two developments, [2] each for a developed point or [1] for a partial one: analyse how Dickens
externalises guilt through the image of Nancy's eyes; analyse the narrative pace and setting of
Sikes's flight; consider whether this is remorse or terror, using AQA's distinction; link the guilt
to the novel's moral purpose and the restoration of order through his death.

**11.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis on "less interested … than"; two named texts different from the
Section B text; a paragraph comparing how conscience is shown in both; a paragraph on how punishment
is shown in both; context for each text (for example, religious belief, legal punishment); an
alternative interpretation; a comparative judgement.

**12.** Identify a reading of the ending [1]: for example, the priest's words about the strangeness of
God's mercy suggest forgiveness may reach even Pinkie. Identify a contrasting reading [1]: Rose's walk
towards the recorded message suggests that only horror awaits, so mercy is withheld. Use both to
debate whether the novel offers forgiveness [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using guilt, remorse and confession as interchangeable terms.
- Treating the unseen extract as a comprehension exercise without an argument.
- Ignoring the listener in a confession.
- In Section C, reusing the Section B text or writing about each text separately.

For full practice, write a timed Section A answer of about an hour on the unseen extract above.
