## Reactivity 2.1.1 Writing balanced equations

Atoms are neither made nor destroyed, so each element must appear the same number of times on both sides. The coefficients give the ratio of particles, and so of moles, that react and form.

**Method**

1. Write correct formulas for every reactant and product. Never change a formula to make it balance.
2. Balance elements that appear in only one substance on each side first. Leave hydrogen and oxygen, or any free element, until last.
3. Treat a polyatomic ion such as NO₃⁻ or SO₄²⁻ as one unit if it passes through unchanged.
4. Use the smallest whole-number coefficients.
5. Add state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. The guide asks you to include them.

**Worked example 1.** Solid zinc carbonate is added to nitric acid. Aqueous zinc nitrate, water and carbon dioxide form.

```
skeleton:  ZnCO₃ + HNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂
nitrate:   two NO₃ units on the right, so 2HNO₃ on the left
check:     Zn 1 = 1; C 1 = 1; H 2 = 2; O 3 + 6 = 6 + 1 + 2 = 9
answer:    ZnCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
```

**Worked example 2.** Liquid heptane, C₇H₁₆, burns completely in oxygen. The products are cooled to room temperature.

Carbon first: 7CO₂. Hydrogen next: 16 H atoms give 8H₂O. Oxygen on the right is 14 + 8 = 22 atoms, which is 11O₂. Water is a liquid at room temperature, so:

C₇H₁₆(l) + 11O₂(g) → 7CO₂(g) + 8H₂O(l)

State symbols show which products count towards a gas volume and which substance is a precipitate.

## Reactivity 2.1.2 Using the mole ratio

Every amount calculation follows the same route:

1. Convert what you know into moles (n = m/M for a mass, n = cV for a solution, n = V/Vm for a gas).
2. Multiply by the mole ratio from the equation: wanted coefficient over known coefficient.
3. Convert the moles of the wanted substance into the unit asked for.

### Reacting masses

**Worked example 3.** Tungsten metal for lamp filaments can be made by heating tungsten(VI) oxide in hydrogen:

WO₃(s) + 3H₂(g) → W(s) + 3H₂O(g)

Calculate the mass of tungsten that 2.50 kg of WO₃ can produce, and the volume of hydrogen at STP that reacts.

```
M(WO₃) = 183.84 + 3(16.00) = 231.84 g mol⁻¹
n(WO₃) = 2500 g ÷ 231.84 = 10.78 mol
ratio WO₃ : W = 1 : 1, so n(W) = 10.78 mol
m(W) = 10.78 × 183.84 = 1982 g = 1.98 kg
n(H₂) = 3 × 10.78 = 32.35 mol
V(H₂) = 32.35 × 22.7 = 734 dm³
```

### Gas volumes

Avogadro's law (Structure 1.4) lets you treat gas volume as a measure of amount.

- **Mass to gas volume.** Find moles, use the ratio, then multiply by the molar volume. Heating 3.00 g of potassium chlorate, 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g), gives n(KClO₃) = 3.00 ÷ 122.55 = 0.02448 mol, so n(O₂) = 0.02448 × 3/2 = 0.03672 mol and V(O₂) = 0.834 dm³ at STP.
- **Gas volume to gas volume.** If every substance you compare is a gas at the same temperature and pressure, the volume ratio equals the mole ratio. No molar volume is needed. For 2C₂H₂(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), 30 cm³ of ethyne needs 30 × 5/2 = 75 cm³ of oxygen and makes 30 × 4/2 = 60 cm³ of carbon dioxide.

### Concentrations in solution

In solution, n = cV links amount to concentration, with V in dm³. Square brackets, as in [HNO₃], mean concentration in mol dm⁻³.

**Worked example 4.** A 25.00 cm³ portion of barium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 18.40 cm³ of nitric acid, [HNO₃] = 0.150 mol dm⁻³.

Ba(OH)₂(aq) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

(a) Find [Ba(OH)₂]. (b) Find the concentration of barium nitrate in the mixture at the end point.

```
(a) n(HNO₃) = 0.150 × 0.01840 = 0.002760 mol
    ratio Ba(OH)₂ : HNO₃ = 1 : 2, so n(Ba(OH)₂) = 0.001380 mol
    [Ba(OH)₂] = 0.001380 ÷ 0.02500 = 0.0552 mol dm⁻³
(b) n(Ba(NO₃)₂) = 0.001380 mol
    total volume = 25.00 + 18.40 = 43.40 cm³ = 0.04340 dm³
    [Ba(NO₃)₂] = 0.001380 ÷ 0.04340 = 0.0318 mol dm⁻³
```

A product's concentration uses the total volume after mixing.

## Reactivity 2.1.3 Limiting reactant and yields

The **limiting reactant** is used up first, and it alone sets the maximum amount of product. Any other reactant is **in excess**: some of it is left over.

**Method.** Convert each reactant to moles. Then take one reactant and work out how much of the other it would need. If less is available than needed, that other reactant is limiting. A quick alternative: divide each amount by its coefficient; the smallest result marks the limiting reactant.

**Worked example 5.** 1.35 g of aluminium powder is mixed with 10.0 g of iodine and a drop of water starts the reaction.

2Al(s) + 3I₂(s) → 2AlI₃(s)

```
n(Al) = 1.35 ÷ 26.98 = 0.05004 mol
n(I₂) = 10.0 ÷ 253.80 = 0.03940 mol
Al needed by all the I₂ = 0.03940 × 2/3 = 0.02627 mol
0.02627 mol < 0.05004 mol available, so I₂ is limiting and Al is in excess
n(AlI₃) = 0.02627 mol (ratio I₂ : AlI₃ = 3 : 2)
M(AlI₃) = 26.98 + 3(126.90) = 407.68 g mol⁻¹
maximum mass of AlI₃ = 0.02627 × 407.68 = 10.7 g
Al left over = (0.05004 − 0.02627) × 26.98 = 0.641 g
```

The **theoretical yield** is the amount of product calculated from the limiting reactant, as above: 10.7 g. The **experimental yield** is what you actually collect and weigh.

Why the experimental yield is often lower:

- the reaction is incomplete or reaches equilibrium
- side reactions make other products
- product is lost during filtering, transfer or recrystallisation

Why it can appear higher:

- the product is still wet, or contains impurities such as excess reactant

## Reactivity 2.1.4 Percentage yield

percentage yield = (experimental yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100

Both yields must be in the same unit: grams, or moles, but not one of each.

**Worked example 6.** Ammonium sulfate fertiliser forms when ammonia is passed into sulfuric acid:

2NH₃(g) + H₂SO₄(aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq)

A student uses 4.90 g of H₂SO₄ with excess ammonia, crystallises the product and collects 5.94 g of dry (NH₄)₂SO₄. Calculate the percentage yield.

```
M(H₂SO₄) = 98.09 g mol⁻¹; n = 4.90 ÷ 98.09 = 0.04995 mol
ratio H₂SO₄ : (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 1 : 1
M((NH₄)₂SO₄) = 2(14.01) + 8(1.01) + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 132.17 g mol⁻¹
theoretical yield = 0.04995 × 132.17 = 6.602 g
percentage yield = 5.94 ÷ 6.602 × 100 = 90.0 %
```

Ammonia is in excess, so the calculation starts from the sulfuric acid. Always settle which reactant is limiting before calculating a yield.

## Reactivity 2.1.5 Atom economy

**Atom economy** tells you what fraction of the mass of all the reactants could, at best, end up in the product you want. It is a measure of efficiency in green chemistry. The guide notes that the equation is given in the data booklet:

atom economy = (molar mass of desired product ÷ total molar mass of all reactants) × 100

Use the coefficients from the balanced equation when you total the reactant masses.

**Worked example 7.** Compare two routes to chloroethane, C₂H₅Cl (M = 64.52 g mol⁻¹).

```
Route 1 (addition):     C₂H₄ + HCl → C₂H₅Cl
  reactants = 28.06 + 36.46 = 64.52 g mol⁻¹
  atom economy = 64.52 ÷ 64.52 × 100 = 100 %
Route 2 (substitution): C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl
  reactants = 30.08 + 70.90 = 100.98 g mol⁻¹
  atom economy = 64.52 ÷ 100.98 × 100 = 63.9 %
```

An addition reaction with one product always has an atom economy of 100 %, because every reactant atom ends up in that product.

**Atom economy and waste.** The relationship is inverse. In route 2, 36.1 % of the reactant mass leaves as hydrogen chloride. For every tonne of chloroethane made that way, about 0.565 t of HCl also forms. Lower atom economy means more by-product to separate, sell or dispose of, and more raw material per tonne of product. A by-product that can be sold reduces the waste in practice, but it does not change the calculated atom economy.

**Atom economy is not yield.** A route can have 100 % atom economy and a poor percentage yield, or the reverse. Atom economy comes from the equation alone; percentage yield comes from what happens in the flask. A fair judgement of a process also weighs energy use, rate, safety and raw material cost.

## Common errors

- Changing a subscript, such as writing H₂O₂ for H₂O, to force an equation to balance.
- Using the mass ratio instead of the mole ratio from the equation.
- Inverting the mole ratio: always wanted coefficient over known coefficient.
- Using cm³ in n = cV without dividing by 1000.
- Working out a yield from the reactant in excess.
- Leaving out the coefficients when totalling reactant masses for atom economy.
- Rounding intermediate values, so the final answer drifts in the third figure.
