Condensed notes for the section 3.3 row **Remedies**: "compensatory damages for physical injury to people, damage to property and economic loss", "the principle of mitigation of loss" and "Injunctions". For explanation and worked examples, use the [remedies in tort study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-remedies-tort/).

## The aim of compensatory damages

- Put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in **if the tort had not been committed**: *Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co* (1880).
- Tort damages look **back** to the pre-tort position; contract damages look **forward** to performance.
- Usually a **lump sum**, once and for all.

## Personal injury

| Head | Type | Proof |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings lost before trial | Pecuniary | Special damages |
| Medical and care costs to trial | Pecuniary | Special damages |
| Future loss of earnings and care | Pecuniary | General damages (estimated) |
| Pain and suffering | Non-pecuniary | General damages |
| Loss of amenity | Non-pecuniary | General damages |

- **Loss of amenity:** loss of enjoyment of life, such as sport or hobbies.
- **Future loss:** annual loss multiplied by a figure for the years it will last, adjusted for investment of the lump sum.
- **Consistency:** guideline awards; *Heil v Rankin* (2000) increased the highest non-pecuniary awards.
- **Provisional damages:** s32A Senior Courts Act 1981, return to court on a specified serious deterioration.
- **Periodical payments:** Damages Act 1996, future losses paid over time.

## Property damage

| Situation | Measure |
|---|---|
| Repairable at reasonable cost | Cost of repair |
| Destroyed or uneconomic to repair | Market value of replacement / diminution in value |
| Loss of use | Consequential losses, such as hire of a replacement |
| Impecunious claimant | Higher credit hire costs recoverable: *Lagden v O'Connor* (2003) |

## Economic loss

- Consequential economic loss: recoverable (*Spartan Steel*, 1973: damaged melt and its profit).
- Negligent misstatement: loss caused by reliance on the advice.

## Mitigation

- Claimant must take **reasonable steps** to limit the loss.
- Cannot recover the part of the loss reasonable steps would have avoided.
- Can recover the reasonable cost of mitigating.
- *Darbishire v Warran* (1963): repair cost above market value; only market value recoverable.
- **Burden** on the defendant: *Geest plc v Lansiquot* (2002).
- Standard is not demanding: the claimant need only act reasonably.

## Injunctions

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Prohibitory | Stop doing something |
| Mandatory | Do something |
| Interim | Before trial; balance of convenience (*American Cyanamid*, 1975) |
| Final | After trial |
| Quia timet | Prevent a threatened wrong |

**Discretionary (equitable):** may be refused for delay, unfairness, adequacy of damages or oppression.

| Case | Outcome |
|---|---|
| *Kennaway v Thompson* (1981) | Limited injunction on powerboat events |
| *Miller v Jackson* (1977) | Refused; damages instead |
| *Dennis v Ministry of Defence* (2003) | Military jet noise; damages instead in the public interest |

**Damages in lieu:** s50 Senior Courts Act 1981 (originally Lord Cairns' Act 1858).
- *Shelfer* (1895) working rule: injury small; estimable in money; adequately compensated by small payment; injunction oppressive.
- *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014): not to be applied mechanically; wide discretion; public benefit and planning permission may be considered.

## Interaction with defences

- Contributory negligence reduces the total by the claimant's share.
- Consent and the nuisance or *Rylands* defences remove damages entirely.

## Evaluation points

- **Lump sums** give finality but rest on guesses about the future; periodical payments and provisional damages reduce the risk of under- or over-compensation.
- **Non-pecuniary loss** cannot truly be measured in money; guideline awards give consistency rather than accuracy.
- **Mitigation** is fair to defendants but applied gently because the defendant created the problem.
- **Injunctions** protect property rights, but damages in lieu after *Coventry* let important activities continue at a price.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Compensatory damages | Money to restore the claimant to the pre-tort position |
| Pecuniary loss | Financial loss, such as earnings |
| Non-pecuniary loss | Non-financial loss, such as pain and loss of amenity |
| Special damages | Precisely calculable losses up to trial |
| General damages | Losses assessed by the court, including future losses |
| Mitigation | Duty to take reasonable steps to limit loss |
| Prohibitory injunction | Order to stop an activity |
| Damages in lieu | Damages awarded instead of an injunction |

## Exam traps

- Do not use contract's expectation measure in tort.
- Failure to mitigate removes only the avoidable loss.
- An injunction is never automatic.
- *Coventry* softened *Shelfer*, not abolished it.
- Remember that the defendant must prove failure to mitigate.

## Self-test

1. What is the aim of compensatory damages in tort? *To put the claimant in the position they would have been in had the tort not occurred.*
2. Which case is the classic statement of this aim? *Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880).*
3. Distinguish special and general damages. *Special: calculable losses to trial; general: assessed by the court, including pain, suffering and future loss.*
4. What is loss of amenity? *Loss of the ability to enjoy life as before.*
5. What do provisional damages allow? *A return to court if a specified serious deterioration develops.*
6. What is the usual measure for repairable property damage? *The reasonable cost of repair.*
7. What did *Lagden v O'Connor* decide? *An impecunious claimant can recover higher credit hire costs.*
8. Why was recovery limited in *Darbishire v Warran*? *A reasonable person would have bought a replacement rather than pay more than market value for repairs.*
9. Who must prove failure to mitigate? *The defendant (Geest v Lansiquot).*
10. What is a quia timet injunction? *One that prevents a threatened wrong before it happens.*
11. Name the four *Shelfer* conditions. *Small injury; estimable in money; adequately compensated by a small payment; injunction oppressive.*
12. What did *Coventry v Lawrence* say about *Shelfer*? *It should not be applied mechanically; the court has a wide discretion.*
