## Short-answer questions

Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 question papers are not divided into lettered sections, so the questions below are grouped only by length.

**1.** Which task is part of accounting rather than book-keeping?

A: Posting the sales journal total to the sales account

B: Balancing the cash book at the month end

C: Interpreting the year's results for the owner

D: Entering a purchase invoice in the purchases journal

**[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is **not** a purpose of measuring profit?

A: Deciding how much the owner can withdraw

B: Calculating the tax that may be due

C: Recording credit sales in the sales journal

D: Comparing performance with last year

**[1]**

**3.** Define **(a)** book-keeping and **(b)** accounting.

**(a)** Book-keeping. **[1]**

**(b)** Accounting. **[1]**

**4.** State whether each task is book-keeping or accounting.

**(a)** Writing up the petty cash book. **[1]**

**(b)** Preparing a statement of financial position. **[1]**

**(c)** Posting a customer's payment to her account in the sales ledger. **[1]**

**(d)** Explaining to the owner why profit fell this year. **[1]**

**5.** A business has revenue of $92,000, cost of sales of $41,000 and other expenses of $36,700. Calculate its profit for the year. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a business that has made a loss should still measure it accurately. **[2]**

## Longer questions

**7.** Nkem owns a hair salon. Her capital at the start of the year was $40,000. During the year the salon's revenue was $65,000 and its expenses were $52,400. Nkem took drawings of $15,000.

**(a)** Calculate the profit for the year. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate Nkem's capital at the end of the year. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what these figures tell Nkem, and advise her. **[3]**

**8.** Asif and Bela are partners in a bakery and plan to apply for a bank loan. Explain **three** purposes of measuring their business's profit. **[6]**

**9.** Lakeside Pottery reports these results.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 120,000 | 135,000 | 150,000 |
| Profit for the year | 18,000 | 18,900 | 19,500 |

**(a)** Calculate profit as a percentage of revenue for each year. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how these figures help the owner to monitor the progress of the business. **[3]**

**10.** Wen runs a delivery business and is thinking of buying a second van for $16,000. Her records show a bank balance of $6,500, profit last year of $21,000 and drawings of $19,000. She already repays a loan of $400 a month. Explain how this accounting information helps Wen decide whether to buy the van, and advise her. **[6]**

**11.** The book-keeper of a shop forgot to record an electricity bill of $1,800 for the year.

**(a)** State the effect on the profit for the year. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how this could lead the owner to make a poor decision. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain the link between book-keeping and accounting shown by this example. **[2]**

**12.** The owner of a small gift shop says: "I do not need accounts. I can see how well I am doing from the amount of cash in the bank." Discuss this view. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. Interpreting results uses the records; the others are recording tasks. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Recording credit sales is a book-keeping task, not a purpose of measuring profit. **[1]**

**3. (a)** The detailed, day-to-day recording of a business's financial transactions [1].

**(b)** Using the records to prepare, analyse, interpret and communicate financial information so that users can make decisions [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Book-keeping [1].

**(b)** Accounting [1].

**(c)** Book-keeping [1].

**(d)** Accounting [1]. **[4]**

**5.** 92,000 − 41,000 − 36,700 [1] = **$14,300** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A loss shows the owner that expenses exceed revenue, so action is needed [1], such as raising prices or cutting costs; knowing its size helps the owner judge how serious the problem is and whether the business can continue [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 65,000 − 52,400 = **$12,600** [1].

**(b)** 40,000 + 12,600 − 15,000 [1] = **$37,600** [1].

**(c)** Nkem's drawings of $15,000 were $2,400 more than the profit [1], so her capital fell by $2,400 and the salon has fewer resources [1]. She should reduce her drawings to no more than the profit, or find ways to increase profit [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Any three purposes, each with one mark for the purpose and one for applying it to the partners.

- To support the loan application [1]: the bank will want evidence that profit is large enough to pay interest and repay the loan [1].
- To share profit between the partners [1]: their agreement sets how profit is divided, so it must be measured accurately [1].
- To decide how much each partner can withdraw [1]: drawings above their share of profit would reduce their capital [1].
- Also creditable: to judge performance; to compare with earlier years; to calculate tax; to plan expansion. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Year 1: 18,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **15.00%** [1]. Year 2: 18,900 ÷ 135,000 × 100 = **14.00%** [1]. Year 3: 19,500 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **13.00%** [1].

**(b)** Revenue and profit have risen each year, which suggests growth [1]. However, the profit kept from each $100 of revenue has fallen from $15 to $13 [1], so expenses are growing faster than sales; comparing the years shows the owner that costs need investigating [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The bank balance of $6,500 is far below the $16,000 price, so Wen cannot pay cash without borrowing [1]. Profit of $21,000 shows the business is successful [1], but drawings of $19,000 took almost all of it, leaving only $2,000 to keep in the business [1]. She already pays $4,800 a year on the existing loan [1]. Advice: she should not buy the van now [1] unless she reduces her drawings or can show that the extra deliveries would raise profit enough to cover new loan repayments [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Profit is overstated by **$1,800** [1].

**(b)** The owner believes the business made more profit than it did [1], so might take too much in drawings or decide to expand when the business cannot afford it [1].

**(c)** Accounting depends on the records produced by book-keeping [1]; if the book-keeping is incomplete, the statements and the decisions based on them will be wrong [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the view: the bank balance does show whether the business can pay its immediate bills [1], and a small business may find it a quick check [1].

Against: cash is not the same as profit; for example, money from a loan increases the bank balance but is not profit [1]. Without accounts the owner cannot measure profit, so cannot judge performance, set drawings or calculate tax [1]. The owner also cannot monitor progress by comparing with earlier years, or support a loan application [1].

Conclusion: the view is wrong; the bank balance is useful but incomplete, and the owner needs accounting records to measure profit and make decisions [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing accounting as recording transactions.
- Giving the same purpose twice in different words.
- Forgetting that drawings reduce capital.
- Saying a business is doing well because revenue rose, without checking profit.
- Treating cash in the bank as profit.
