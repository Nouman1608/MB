## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the time value of money, and why it matters when a business appraises an investment. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **two** reasons why the accounting profit from a project is different from the net cash flow it generates. **[4]**

**3.** Corrimore Joinery is considering a machine costing 60,000, paid at the start. It has a 4-year life, no residual value and is depreciated on the straight line. Forecast profits after depreciation are 8,000, 11,000, 14,000 and 9,000 in years 1 to 4.

**(a)** Calculate the net cash flow for each of years 0 to 4. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the payback period in years, and to the nearest month. **[3]**

**4.** Ullesby Laundry plans to buy a pressing line for 84,000 with a 6-year life and no residual value. Net cash inflows are 22,000, 26,000, 30,000, 28,000, 24,000 and 20,000 in years 1 to 6. The target accounting rate of return is 25%.

**(a)** Calculate the ARR, using average profit divided by average investment, to one decimal place. **[5]**
**(b)** State the decision on ARR, and give one limitation of ARR. **[2]**

**5.** Gillbeck Cafe is considering new ovens costing 50,000. Net cash inflows are 18,000, 20,000, 22,000 and 12,000 in years 1 to 4. The cost of capital is 8%. Discount factors at 8% are: year 1 0.9259, year 2 0.8573, year 3 0.7938, year 4 0.7350.

**(a)** Calculate the net present value (NPV). Round each present value to the nearest dollar. **[4]**
**(b)** Advise whether the project should go ahead on this evidence, with a reason. **[2]**

**6.** Orrington Plastics is considering a mould costing 90,000. Net cash inflows are 35,000, 40,000, 38,000 and 20,000 in years 1 to 4. The cost of capital is 14%. Discount factors:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16% | 0.8621 | 0.7432 | 0.6407 | 0.5523 |
| 20% | 0.8333 | 0.6944 | 0.5787 | 0.4823 |

**(a)** Calculate the NPV at 16% and at 20%. Round each present value to the nearest dollar. **[4]**
**(b)** Estimate the internal rate of return (IRR) to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** State the decision, with a reason. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why many accountants regard NPV as a better guide to an investment decision than payback. **[6]**

**8.** Rushbourne Quarry is considering a crusher costing 150,000. At a cost of capital of 10% the NPV is 22,000 and payback is 3.5 years, against a target of 4 years. The crusher would make 6 of the 40 employees redundant, cut lorry journeys through the nearby village by a third and reduce dust, but add evening noise.

**(a)** Identify **two** non-financial factors in the information. **[2]**
**(b)** Recommend whether the quarry should buy the crusher, using the financial and non-financial information. **[6]**

**9.** Lindisford Textiles must choose between two machines. Each costs 70,000, and only one can be bought. The cost of capital is 9%. Discount factors at 9%: year 1 0.9174, year 2 0.8417, year 3 0.7722, year 4 0.7084.

| Year | Machine A inflow | Machine B inflow |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30,000 | 10,000 |
| 2 | 35,000 | 25,000 |
| 3 | 25,000 | 40,000 |
| 4 | 10,000 | 45,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the payback period for each machine in years, to two decimal places. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the NPV of each machine. Round each present value to the nearest dollar. **[4]**
**(c)** Recommend which machine to buy, with reasons. **[4]**

**10.** Sablemere Marine can buy a hull press for 96,000, paid at the start. Its life is 4 years, with no residual value and straight line depreciation. Forecast profits are 9,000, 14,000, 17,000 and 12,000 in years 1 to 4. These are after depreciation and after a charge of 4,000 a year for a share of existing head office costs. The cost of capital is 10%. Discount factors at 10%: year 1 0.9091, year 2 0.8264, year 3 0.7513, year 4 0.6830.

**(a)** Calculate the net cash inflow for each of years 1 to 4. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the NPV, rounding each present value to the nearest dollar. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the head office charge is added back. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Cash received sooner is worth more than the same amount received later. [1] This is because cash now can be invested or used to cut borrowing, and later cash is less certain. [1] Future flows are therefore discounted to present values before they are compared with the outlay. [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to earn more when they link the idea to discounting.

**2.** Any two reasons, each explained. Depreciation is deducted in arriving at profit [1] but no cash leaves the business, so cash flow is higher. [1] The purchase of the asset is a large outflow at the start [1] but reaches profit only gradually through depreciation. [1] (Other valid reasons: apportioned costs that continue anyway; credit given to customers.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason tends to earn more when it says whether cash is higher or lower.

**3. (a)** Depreciation is 60,000 / 4 = 15,000 a year. [1] Net cash flows are profit plus depreciation: year 1 23,000, year 2 26,000, year 3 29,000, year 4 24,000. [1] Year 0 is an outflow of (60,000). [1]
**(b)** Cumulative inflow is 23,000, 49,000 and 78,000. [1] After year 2, 11,000 is still needed out of 29,000 in year 3. [1] Payback = 2 + 11,000 / 29,000 = **2.38 years**, which is about **2 years 5 months**. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show cumulative cash flow by year before calculating the fraction.

**4. (a)** Total inflows are 150,000. [1] Total profit = 150,000 − 84,000 = 66,000. [1] Average profit = 66,000 / 6 = 11,000. [1] Average investment = 84,000 / 2 = 42,000. [1] ARR = 11,000 / 42,000 × 100 = **26.2%**. [1]
**(b)** 26.2% is above the 25% target, so accept on ARR. [1] It uses profit rather than cash flow, so it ignores when the profits arise. [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to earn more when each stage (total profit, average profit, average investment) is shown separately.

**5. (a)** Year 1: 18,000 × 0.9259 = 16,666. [1] Year 2: 20,000 × 0.8573 = 17,146 and year 3: 22,000 × 0.7938 = 17,464. [1] Year 4: 12,000 × 0.7350 = 8,820. [1] Total present value 60,096 less 50,000 gives NPV of **10,096**. [1]
**(b)** The NPV is positive, so the project earns more than 8% and should go ahead. [1] The result depends on the forecasts, which should be checked. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The sign of the NPV only becomes a decision when it is tied to the cost of capital.

**6. (a)** At 16%: 30,174 + 29,728 + 24,347 + 11,046 = 95,295. [1] NPV = **5,295**. [1] At 20%: 29,166 + 27,776 + 21,991 + 9,646 = 88,579. [1] NPV = **(1,421)**. [1]
**(b)** IRR = 16 + [5,295 / (5,295 + 1,421)] × (20 − 16). [1] IRR = **19.2%** (approximately). [1]
**(c)** The IRR of 19.2% is higher than the 14% cost of capital, so accept. [1] The figure is an estimate from interpolation. [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to read better when the interpolation formula is written out with the two NPVs and the rate gap.

**7.** Three points, each developed. NPV discounts cash flows, so it recognises the time value of money, [1] whereas payback treats a dollar in year 4 as equal to a dollar in year 1. [1] NPV uses every cash flow over the life of the project, [1] whereas payback ignores flows after the payback date, so a high-earning project can look worse than it is. [1] NPV gives the dollar value added, [1] whereas payback only measures speed of recovery. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation answers tend to score more when each comparison is two-sided and explains what is lost by using payback.

**8. (a)** Any two: redundancy of 6 employees; fewer lorry journeys through the village; reduced dust; more noise in the evening. [1] [1]
**(b)** Financial: the NPV is positive at 22,000, [1] and payback of 3.5 years is within the 4-year target. [1] Non-financial: the redundancies hurt morale and local employment, [1] while reduced dust and fewer lorry journeys improve the quarry's standing with the village. [1] Noise complaints could restrict opening hours and reduce the cash inflows. [1] Judgement: buy the crusher, provided the quarry manages the noise and treats the affected employees fairly. [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Recommendations usually earn more when they weigh the non-financial factors against the figures and reach a clear decision.

**9. (a)** Machine A: cumulative inflow is 30,000 and 65,000, so 5,000 is needed in year 3. [1] Payback = 2 + 5,000 / 25,000 = **2.20 years**. [1] Machine B: cumulative inflow is 10,000, 35,000 and 75,000, so 35,000 is needed in year 3. [1] Payback = 2 + 35,000 / 40,000 = **2.88 years**. [1]
**(b)** Machine A: 27,522 + 29,460 + 19,305 + 7,084 = 83,371. [1] NPV = **13,371**. [1] Machine B: 9,174 + 21,042 + 30,888 + 31,878 = 92,982. [1] NPV = **22,982**. [1]
**(c)** A has the shorter payback, so it returns cash faster, [1] but B has the higher NPV by 9,611, so it adds more value in present terms. [1] Recommend B, because NPV recognises the time value of money and measures the value added. [1] A could be preferred if liquidity is tight. [1] **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* When techniques disagree, answers tend to earn more when they say which technique is given the most weight and why.

**10. (a)** Depreciation = 96,000 / 4 = 24,000. [1] Add back depreciation and the 4,000 head office charge. [1] Net cash inflows are year 1 37,000, year 2 42,000, [1] year 3 45,000 and year 4 40,000. [1]
**(b)** Present values: 33,637, 34,709, 33,808 and 27,320. [2] Total 129,474. [1] NPV = 129,474 − 96,000 = **33,474**. [1]
**(c)** The head office costs would be incurred whether or not the press is bought, [1] so they are not a cash flow caused by the project and must not reduce its cash inflows. [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion from profit to cash for each year, because the later discounting depends on those figures.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not adding depreciation back when turning profit into net cash flow.
- Discounting the year 0 outlay instead of leaving it undiscounted.
- Using profit instead of cash flow in payback, NPV or IRR.
- Stopping at a number with no decision.
- Interpolating the IRR from two NPVs of the same sign.
- Writing a one-sided recommendation with no non-financial factor.
