Subtopic 2.3 Biogeochemical cycles is part of Topic 2 Ecology in IB Diploma Programme Environmental
Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The IB's public subject brief names Topic 2 but
not its subtopics, so this title follows the course's provisional checklist; confirm it in your
school's copy of the ESS guide.

The [ecosystems, energy and biogeochemical cycles study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-ecosystems-energy-biogeochemical-cycles/)
tabulates the storages and flows of the carbon cycle and the processes of the nitrogen cycle. This
guide goes deeper: fast and slow parts of the carbon cycle, how the ocean takes up carbon, why peat
and permafrost matter, how to calculate whether an ecosystem is a sink or a source, how to work a
nitrogen budget for a farm, and how human impacts on both cycles can be managed.

## What 2.3 asks you to do

- Describe biogeochemical cycles as systems of storages and flows, with residence times.
- Explain the processes that move carbon and nitrogen between storages.
- Distinguish carbon sinks from sources, and calculate net flows.
- Explain how human activities alter the cycles and evaluate ways of managing them.

## Fast and slow carbon

Carbon moves on two very different timescales:

- **Fast cycle (years to centuries):** photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition and
  exchange between air and surface ocean.
- **Slow cycle (thousands to millions of years):** formation of fossil fuels and limestone,
  weathering of rocks, volcanic outgassing and sediment burial.

Burning fossil fuels and making cement take carbon out of the slow cycle and put it into the fast
cycle within decades. That mismatch, not the size of the flow alone, is what makes human impact so
significant.

### Residence time in cycles

Residence time = storage ÷ flow. Carbon in a leaf may stay for a year; in a tree trunk for a
century; in the deep ocean for many centuries; in limestone for millions of years. A storage with a
long residence time, once disturbed, takes a long time to rebuild.

## How the ocean takes up carbon

- **Solubility pump:** CO₂ dissolves in surface water, more readily in cold water. Cold, dense water
  sinks at high latitudes and carries dissolved carbon into the deep ocean.
- **Biological pump:** phytoplankton fix carbon by photosynthesis. Some of this organic matter sinks
  as dead cells and faecal pellets, transferring carbon to deep water and sediments.
- **Carbonate formation:** corals, molluscs and some plankton build shells of calcium carbonate,
  which can accumulate as sediment and eventually limestone.

Warming weakens the solubility pump, because warmer water holds less dissolved gas. Extra dissolved
CO₂ also forms carbonic acid:

`CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻`

The extra hydrogen ions lower pH and react with carbonate ions, leaving less carbonate for shells and
skeletons. This is ocean acidification.

## Peat and permafrost

- **Peatlands** form where waterlogging keeps soils low in oxygen, so decomposition is very slow and
  dead plant matter accumulates over thousands of years. Draining peat for farming or forestry lets
  oxygen in; decomposition speeds up and the peat becomes a carbon source.
- **Permafrost** soils have been frozen for long periods, locking up organic carbon. Thawing allows
  decomposition, releasing CO₂ in drier conditions and methane in waterlogged ones, a positive
  feedback on warming.

## Is an ecosystem a sink or a source?

An ecosystem is a **sink** if it takes up more carbon than it releases, and a **source** if it
releases more.

**Worked example 1 (fictional data, g C m⁻² yr⁻¹).** A temperate forest has:

| Flow | Value |
|---|---|
| Gross photosynthesis (GPP) | 2,200 |
| Plant respiration | 1,200 |
| Decomposer respiration from soil and litter | 750 |

```
NPP = 2200 − 1200 = 1000 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹
Net ecosystem uptake = 1000 − 750 = 250 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹   → a sink
Per hectare: 250 × 10000 = 2 500 000 g = 2.5 t C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
As CO₂: 2.5 × 44/12 = 9.17 t CO₂ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (3 s.f.)
```

If the forest is cleared and the wood burned, photosynthesis stops while decomposition and combustion
continue, so the same land becomes a source. A mature, undisturbed forest may be close to balance,
because decomposition catches up with NPP.

## The nitrogen cycle in practice

### Conditions matter

- **Nitrification** needs oxygen, so it happens in well-aerated soil.
- **Denitrification** happens in waterlogged, anaerobic soil; it returns nitrogen to the air,
  partly as nitrous oxide (N₂O), a greenhouse gas that also contributes to stratospheric ozone loss.
- **Fixation** by *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules is a mutualism: the plant supplies sugars, the
  bacteria supply ammonium.
- **Leaching** is greatest from bare soil after heavy rain, especially when nitrate has just been
  applied.

### Worked example 2: a farm nitrogen budget (fictional data, kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹)

| Inputs | | Outputs | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Synthetic fertiliser | 180 | Removed in harvested crop | 140 |
| Fixation by clover | 40 | Leaching to drains | 45 |
| Deposition from the air | 15 | Denitrification | 20 |
| | | Ammonia lost to air | 15 |

```
Total inputs  = 180 + 40 + 15 = 235
Total outputs = 140 + 45 + 20 + 15 = 220
Change in soil store = 235 − 220 = +15 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
Share of inputs leached = 45 / 235 × 100 = 19.1 % (3 s.f.)
Share removed in crop (use efficiency) = 140 / 235 × 100 = 59.6 % (3 s.f.)
```

About 40% of the nitrogen added does not leave in the crop. Some builds up in the soil; the rest
escapes to water or air, where it can cause eutrophication, add a greenhouse gas or contribute to
fine-particle air pollution.

## Managing human impacts

| Cycle | Problem | Management options | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon | Fossil fuel combustion | Renewable energy, efficiency, carbon pricing | Cost of transition; existing infrastructure |
| Carbon | Deforestation and peat drainage | Protection, reforestation, rewetting peat | Competing land demand; slow regrowth |
| Carbon | Emissions from industry | Carbon capture and storage | Expensive; storage must be secure for centuries |
| Nitrogen | Fertiliser leaching | Precision application, split doses, cover crops | Needs equipment, data and training |
| Nitrogen | Run-off into rivers | Buffer strips of vegetation beside watercourses | Takes land out of production |
| Nitrogen | Dependence on synthetic N | Legumes in rotations | Lower yields in some systems |

The planetary boundaries framework treats nitrogen and phosphorus flows together as "biogeochemical
flows", a reminder that farm-level surpluses add up to a global problem.

## Common mistakes

- Treating carbon in fossil fuels as part of the fast cycle.
- Saying the ocean "absorbs CO₂" without naming a pump or the acidification consequence.
- Confusing nitrification (needs oxygen) with denitrification (no oxygen).
- Forgetting to convert carbon to CO₂ (× 44/12) when a question asks for CO₂.
- Describing a sink without comparing uptake and release.

## Exam technique

When describing a cycle, name the storage, the flow and the process together ("carbon moves from the
atmosphere to producers by photosynthesis"). "Explain" a human impact by linking it to a specific
flow that speeds up or slows down. "Evaluate" a management strategy with a benefit, a limitation and
a judgement about scale.
