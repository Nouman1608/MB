This guide goes deeper into **subtopic 3.3 Industry** of Cambridge O
Level Geography (2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The [Economic
Development study
guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/) lists the
location factors; this page explains how each one works, how they
differ between types of industry, and how to write a case study of an
industrial zone. Then use the [industry revision
notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-industry-revision-notes/)
and the [industry practice
questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-industry-practice/).

## What 3.3 covers

- An **industrial system** has inputs, processes and outputs, and the
  outputs include **waste** as well as products.
- The **types of industry**: manufacturing, processing, assembly and
  high technology.
- The factors influencing the **distribution and location** of
  factories and industrial zones: land, labour, raw materials, fuel and
  power, transport, markets and political factors.
- **Case study**: a factory or industrial zone.

## The industrial system

| Part | Examples |
|---|---|
| Inputs | Raw materials or components, energy, labour, capital, land, research and design |
| Processes | Refining, smelting, cutting, mixing, assembling, testing, packaging |
| Outputs | Finished products, by-products, waste (heat, gases, waste water, scrap), profit |
| Feedback | Profit reinvested in new machinery, training or expansion |

Waste is part of the system, not an afterthought. It links 3.3 to 3.7
(environmental risks): untreated waste water, air emissions and solid
waste are where industrial pollution comes from.

## Four types of industry

- **Processing**: turning a raw material into a more useful product,
  such as refining crude oil, smelting metal ore, milling grain or
  refining sugar cane.
- **Manufacturing**: making goods from materials, such as steel
  products, textiles and furniture. It is the broad category; the other
  three are kinds of manufacturing with particular features.
- **Assembly**: putting together components made elsewhere, such as
  cars, televisions and mobile phones. Assembly plants depend on
  reliable supply chains and good transport.
- **High technology**: products based on advanced research, such as
  microchips, computers, software, pharmaceuticals and aerospace
  equipment. These need highly skilled workers and close links with
  universities and research centres.

## How each location factor works

- **Land**: factories need flat, well-drained land that is cheap
  enough, with room to expand. Out-of-town **greenfield** sites
  offer space; **brownfield** sites reuse old industrial land in
  cities.
- **Labour**: some industries need large numbers of low-cost workers
  (clothing, electronics assembly); others need highly skilled ones
  (high technology). Labour costs are one reason many factories have
  moved to lower-income countries.
- **Raw materials**: industries using heavy, bulky raw materials that
  lose weight during processing (iron and steel, sugar refining) tend
  to locate near the source, to cut transport costs.
- **Fuel and power**: in the past, heavy industry clustered on
  coalfields. Today, electricity supplied through a national grid lets
  most industry locate almost anywhere; such industry is called
  **footloose**. Very energy-hungry industries, such as aluminium
  smelting, still seek cheap power, often hydroelectricity.
- **Transport**: access to ports, motorways, railways and airports
  for moving inputs and products. Industries importing raw materials
  often locate at ports (break-of-bulk points), and high-value,
  lightweight goods can travel by air.
- **Markets**: industries whose products gain weight or bulk, are
  fragile or perishable, or need contact with customers (bakeries,
  soft-drink bottling, newspaper printing) locate close to their
  market.
- **Political factors**: governments attract industry with grants,
  tax breaks, cheap land and planning permission, often in **export
  processing zones** or **special economic zones**. Trade barriers can
  persuade a foreign company to build a factory inside a market rather
  than export to it.

**Agglomeration** matters too: firms cluster where suppliers, skilled
workers and related businesses already exist, and the cluster then
attracts more firms.

## Worked example 1: choosing a site

An illustrative company plans a sugar refinery and has three possible
sites.

| Site | Distance from cane fields | Distance from port | Land cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 km | 120 km | Low |
| B | 90 km | 10 km | Medium |
| C | 150 km | 150 km (inland city) | High |

**Best choice: site A.** Sugar cane is bulky, heavy and perishable,
and it loses most of its weight when refined, so transport costs are
lowest if the refinery is close to the fields; cane must also be
crushed quickly after cutting. The 120 km journey to the port is for
the much lighter refined sugar. Land is also cheapest at A. Site C is
poorest: far from both raw material and port, with expensive land.

This structure (identify the key factor for this type of industry,
apply it to the data, reject the alternatives) works for any
decision-making question.

## Worked example 2: an industrial zone case study — Silicon Valley, California

**Location.** The southern part of the San Francisco Bay Area, in
California, USA, including Palo Alto, Mountain View, Cupertino, Santa
Clara and San Jose. The name comes from the silicon chips
(semiconductors) first developed and made there.

**Type of industry.** High technology: microchips, computers,
software and internet services. Examples include Intel
(headquartered in Santa Clara), Apple (Cupertino) and Google
(Mountain View). Hewlett-Packard was founded in a Palo Alto garage in
1939.

**Location factors.**

- *Labour and research*: Stanford University in Palo Alto supplies
  graduates and research; the area attracts skilled workers from
  around the world.
- *Capital*: many venture-capital firms fund new start-up companies.
- *Agglomeration*: suppliers, specialist services and rival firms
  cluster together, so ideas and staff move easily between companies.
- *Transport*: international airports at San Francisco and San Jose
  and a motorway network; products are light and high-value.
- *Environment*: a pleasant climate that helps attract workers.

**Problems** to include for balance: some of the highest housing costs
in the USA, traffic congestion and inequality between highly paid
technology workers and lower-paid service workers.

*Alternative example*: Bengaluru, India, a major centre of
information-technology services (the headquarters of Infosys and Wipro,
and the Electronic City industrial area), with research institutions
such as the Indian Institute of Science and a large supply of
English-speaking graduates.

## Common mistakes

- Missing **waste** from a list of industrial outputs.
- Confusing processing with assembly.
- Writing "good transport" without saying *what* is transported, by
  which route, and why it matters for that industry.
- Treating all industries as if they need the same factors: a steel
  works and a software company have opposite priorities.
- An industrial-zone case study with no named companies or places.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the system: inputs, processes, outputs (including
  waste) with examples from your factory or zone.
- **Explain** a location: name the factor, then explain *why it
  matters for this industry*.
- **Suggest** for unfamiliar data: use the figures given and decide
  which factor is most important for the product.
- In the 7-mark case-study question, cover several factors with
  named, place-specific detail.
