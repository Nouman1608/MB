This guide goes deeper into section 3.5.2 Preparation of salts (statements a to c) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The course is untiered. The specification asks you to **suggest methods to make a named soluble salt** and to **name the substances needed to make a named insoluble salt**, so this guide focuses on planning: choosing reagents, ordering the steps and justifying each one. The three routes are introduced in the [acids, bases and preparing salts guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-acids-bases-and-preparing-salts/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.5.2 a | Soluble salts from acids with metals (not all are suitable), insoluble bases (added until no more reacts, excess filtered off) or alkalis (indicator shows complete reaction) |
| 3.5.2 b | Salt solutions can be crystallised to give solid salts |
| 3.5.2 c | Insoluble salts by mixing solutions of ions so a precipitate forms; precipitation removes unwanted ions when treating drinking water or effluent |

## Step 1: is the salt soluble or insoluble?

Everything follows from this answer. Use these solubility facts:

- All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble.
- All nitrates are soluble.
- Most chlorides are soluble; silver chloride and lead(II) chloride are not.
- Most sulfates are soluble; barium sulfate and lead(II) sulfate are not, and calcium sulfate only slightly.
- Most carbonates are insoluble, apart from sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates.

**Soluble salt** → react an acid with something, then crystallise.
**Insoluble salt** → mix two solutions, then filter, wash and dry.

## Step 2 for a soluble salt: which starting material?

| If the metal... | Use | How you know the acid is used up |
|---|---|---|
| is moderately reactive (magnesium, zinc, iron) | the metal itself, in excess | fizzing stops and metal remains |
| is too reactive (sodium, potassium, lithium) or too unreactive (copper, silver) | its insoluble oxide or hydroxide, in excess, **unless** that compound is soluble | solid remains undissolved |
| has a soluble hydroxide (sodium, potassium, lithium), or the salt is an ammonium salt | the alkali (or ammonia solution) with an indicator | indicator changes colour |

An insoluble **carbonate** can replace the insoluble base: it reacts with acid to give the salt, water and carbon dioxide, and the end of the reaction shows as fizzing stopping with solid left over.

## Why each step matters

| Step | Reason examiners want |
|---|---|
| Warm the acid | Speeds up the reaction |
| Add the solid **in excess** | Makes sure **all the acid** reacts, so no acid is left in the salt |
| Filter | Removes the unreacted excess solid; the salt is in the **filtrate** |
| Heat to evaporate **some** water | Makes the solution saturated so crystals can form |
| Leave to cool | The salt is less soluble when cold, so it crystallises |
| Filter off crystals and dry them | Separates the crystals from the remaining solution |
| Do **not** heat to dryness | Many salts decompose or lose water of crystallisation |
| Repeat the titration **without indicator** | Stops the indicator contaminating the salt |

## Worked example 1: a full plan for zinc sulfate crystals

*Zinc oxide is an insoluble base. Describe how to make pure, dry crystals of zinc sulfate from zinc oxide.* **[6]**

**Model answer**

1. Pour about 50 cm³ of dilute **sulfuric acid** into a beaker and warm it gently.
2. Add zinc oxide a spatula at a time, stirring, until **some solid remains** undissolved: the acid has all reacted.
3. **Filter** to remove the excess zinc oxide; collect the colourless filtrate of zinc sulfate solution.
4. Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals form on a glass rod dipped in and removed (the solution is saturated).
5. Leave the solution to **cool**; crystals form.
6. Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers.

ZnO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

**Why it scores:** each step is a separate marking point: the correct acid, excess, filtering, concentrating, cooling, and drying.

### Linking to quantities

How much salt could this make? If 4.05 g of zinc oxide reacts (Mᵣ ZnO = 81), that is 4.05 ÷ 81 = 0.0500 mol. The equation ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.0500 mol of zinc sulfate forms. Mᵣ ZnSO₄ = 65 + 32 + 64 = 161, giving a maximum of 0.0500 × 161 = **8.05 g** of anhydrous zinc sulfate. Real crystals contain water of crystallisation and some salt stays in solution, so the measured mass will differ.

## Worked example 2: potassium nitrate by the indicator method

*Suggest a method to make potassium nitrate. Explain your choice of reagents.*

- Potassium is far too reactive to add to an acid, and potassium hydroxide is soluble, so there is no excess to filter off. Use **potassium hydroxide solution** and **nitric acid**.
- Pipette 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide solution into a conical flask, add a few drops of indicator, and run in nitric acid from a burette until the indicator just changes colour. Note the volume.
- Repeat with the same volumes but **no indicator**.
- Crystallise the solution as in worked example 1.

KOH(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)

## Worked example 3: naming reagents for insoluble salts

For each insoluble salt, one solution supplies the positive ion and another the negative ion. Choosing a **nitrate** for the metal ion and a **sodium or potassium** compound for the negative ion guarantees both are soluble.

| Insoluble salt | Solution 1 | Solution 2 | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zinc carbonate | zinc sulfate | sodium carbonate | ZnSO₄(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → ZnCO₃(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq) |
| Silver iodide | silver nitrate | potassium iodide | AgNO₃(aq) + KI(aq) → AgI(s) + KNO₃(aq) |
| Lead(II) sulfate | lead(II) nitrate | sodium sulfate | Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq) |

Then: **filter** to collect the precipitate as the **residue**, **wash** it with distilled water to remove the soluble salt left on it, and **dry** it in a warm oven.

## Precipitation in water treatment

Precipitation turns an unwanted dissolved ion into an insoluble solid that can be settled out or filtered off.

- **Drinking water:** hard water contains dissolved calcium ions. Adding carbonate ions (for example as sodium carbonate) precipitates calcium carbonate.
- **Effluent:** dissolved metal ions from industry can be precipitated as insoluble hydroxides by adding an alkali; dissolved phosphate can be removed as an insoluble phosphate.

The skill being tested is the same as for preparing an insoluble salt, used in reverse: here the precipitate is the **waste** and the cleaned water is the **product**.

## Evaluating a flawed method

Exam questions often show a student's method with errors. Spot these:

- Using a metal that is too reactive (sodium) or unreactive (copper) with dilute acid.
- Not adding the solid in excess, so acid remains in the salt.
- Keeping the residue instead of the filtrate for a soluble salt, or the reverse for an insoluble salt.
- Heating to dryness instead of crystallising.
- Washing a precipitate with tap water, which contains dissolved ions.
- Forgetting to repeat a titration without indicator.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a solid as a reagent for an insoluble salt. Both reagents must be **solutions** of soluble compounds.
- Choosing "copper and sulfuric acid" for copper(II) sulfate.
- Writing "filter the crystals out of the evaporating basin" before the solution has cooled.
- Leaving out the reason for excess: the mark is for "so that all the acid reacts".

## Exam technique: command words

- **Suggest a method:** name the acid, the other reactant and the route; one sentence per step.
- **Describe how to make pure, dry crystals:** expect five or six marks, one per step in the plan.
- **Name the substances needed:** two named solutions, such as "barium nitrate solution and sodium sulfate solution", not ions or elements.
- **Explain why:** give the purpose of the step, not a description of it.
