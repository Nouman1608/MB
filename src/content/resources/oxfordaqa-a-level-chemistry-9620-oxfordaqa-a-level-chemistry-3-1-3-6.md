This guide covers **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. The specification defines **electronegativity as the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond**. Where two bonded elements have different electronegativities, the electron distribution is unsymmetrical, giving a **polar covalent bond**, which **may** give the molecule a **permanent dipole**. You must be able to:

- use partial charges to show that a bond is polar;
- explain why some molecules with polar bonds do not have a permanent dipole.

The [shapes, polarity and intermolecular forces overview](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-shapes-polarity-and-intermolecular-forces/) introduces the idea. Here you will learn to compare bond polarities from data, decide polarity for unfamiliar molecules from their shape, and write the explanation examiners expect. Afterwards use the [bond polarity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6-revision-notes/) and the [bond polarity practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6-practice/).

## Electronegativity

Electronegativity is measured on the Pauling scale, which has no units. Questions supply the values you need. Typical values:

| Element | H | C | N | O | F | Cl | Br | I | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 2.1 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 3.0 | 2.8 | 2.5 | 2.5 |

**Patterns.** Electronegativity increases across a period: the nuclear charge increases while the shielding stays similar and the atomic radius falls, so the bonding pair is attracted more strongly. It decreases down a group: the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and more shielded. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

## Polar and non-polar bonds

- **Same element** (Cl–Cl, H–H): the pair is shared equally. The bond is **non-polar**.
- **Different electronegativities**: the more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair towards itself, gaining a small negative charge, **δ−**. The other atom is left **δ+**. The bond is **polar**.
- The **larger the difference**, the more polar the bond. A very large difference gives ionic bonding: the pair is effectively transferred.

Partial charges are drawn on the atoms of the bond:

```
δ+   δ−          δ−  δ+  δ−
H —— Cl          O == C == O
```

A dipole can also be shown with an arrow along the bond pointing towards the δ− atom.

### Worked example 1: ranking bond polarity

Rank C–F, C–Cl, C–Br and C–I in order of polarity and mark the partial charges on C–Cl.

```
differences:  C–F 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5
              C–Cl 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5
              C–Br 2.8 − 2.5 = 0.3
              C–I  2.5 − 2.5 = 0.0
order: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I (almost non-polar)
```

In C–Cl the chlorine is more electronegative, so carbon is δ+ and chlorine is δ−.

### Worked example 2: the direction can reverse

In N–H, nitrogen (3.0) is more electronegative than hydrogen (2.1): **N is δ−**. In N–F, fluorine (4.0) is more electronegative than nitrogen: **N is δ+**. The same atom can carry either partial charge, depending on its partner. Always compare the two atoms in the bond.

## From polar bonds to a permanent dipole

A molecule has a **permanent dipole** when the electron distribution over the **whole molecule** is unsymmetrical. Two conditions decide this:

1. The molecule must contain **polar bonds**.
2. The bond dipoles must **not cancel**. They cancel when the shape is symmetrical and all the outer atoms are the same.

Each bond dipole has a direction. In a symmetrical shape, equal dipoles pulling in opposite or evenly spaced directions add up to zero, just as equal forces in opposite directions give no overall force.

| Shape (all outer atoms identical) | Example | Permanent dipole? |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | CO₂, XeF₂ | No |
| Trigonal planar | BF₃ | No |
| Tetrahedral | CCl₄ | No |
| Trigonal bipyramidal | PCl₅ | No |
| Octahedral | SF₆ | No |
| Square planar | XeF₄ | No |
| Bent | H₂O | Yes |
| Pyramidal | NH₃, NF₃ | Yes |
| See-saw | SF₄ | Yes |
| T-shaped | ClF₃ | Yes |
| Square pyramidal | BrF₅ | Yes |

Lone pairs do not automatically make a molecule polar. In XeF₄ the two lone pairs sit opposite each other and the four Xe–F dipoles cancel in pairs. Shape decides, and shape comes from [electron-pair repulsion](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-5/).

### When the outer atoms differ

Replacing one outer atom breaks the symmetry even if the shape barely changes:

- CCl₄ is non-polar, but **CHCl₃** (trichloromethane) is polar: the C–H dipole is much smaller than each C–Cl dipole, so the four do not cancel.
- CO₂ is non-polar, but **OCS** (carbonyl sulfide), also linear, is polar: the C=O dipole (difference 1.0) is larger than the C=S dipole (difference 0.0 on these values), so there is a net dipole towards oxygen.

### Worked example 3: an annotated explanation

**Question.** Boron trifluoride, BF₃, contains polar bonds but has no permanent dipole. Nitrogen trifluoride, NF₃, does have a permanent dipole. Explain this difference.

**Annotated answer.**

- Each B–F and N–F bond is polar because fluorine is more electronegative than boron or nitrogen [establishes polar bonds].
- BF₃ has three bonding pairs and no lone pairs, so it is trigonal planar [shape from electron pairs].
- The three equal B–F dipoles are spaced at 120° in one plane, so they cancel [symmetry argument].
- NF₃ has three bonding pairs and one lone pair, so it is pyramidal [shape].
- The three N–F dipoles all point down and away from the lone pair, so they do not cancel: NF₃ has a permanent dipole [conclusion linked to shape].

## Evidence for a permanent dipole

A thin stream of a polar liquid, such as water or trichloromethane, bends towards a charged rod whichever charge the rod carries: the molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod. A stream of a non-polar liquid, such as tetrachloromethane or hexane, is not noticeably deflected.

## Common mistakes

- Defining electronegativity as "attracting electrons" without "the pair of electrons in a covalent bond".
- Putting δ+ and δ− on the wrong atoms, or on a molecule as a whole without saying which atom.
- Concluding a molecule is polar simply because it has polar bonds.
- Concluding a molecule is non-polar simply because its shape "looks" symmetrical, while ignoring different outer atoms.
- Saying lone pairs always make a molecule polar.
- Writing that dipoles "cancel" without giving the shape that makes them cancel.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the specification wording exactly.
- **Show** that a bond is polar: write δ+ and δ− on the correct atoms; one mark usually needs both.
- **Explain** why a molecule has no permanent dipole: (1) the bonds are polar, (2) name the shape, (3) the dipoles are equal and symmetrically arranged, so they cancel.
- **Suggest** polarity for an unfamiliar molecule: work out the shape first, then check whether all outer atoms are the same.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is electronegativity?**
Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond. Fluorine has the highest value; values increase across a period and decrease down a group.

**Why is tetrachloromethane non-polar when its bonds are polar?**
CCl₄ is tetrahedral and all four outer atoms are chlorine. The four equal C–Cl dipoles are arranged symmetrically, so they cancel and the molecule has no permanent dipole.

**Can a linear molecule be polar?**
Yes, if its two ends are different. OCS is linear, but the C=O and C=S dipoles are unequal, so they do not cancel and the molecule has a permanent dipole.
