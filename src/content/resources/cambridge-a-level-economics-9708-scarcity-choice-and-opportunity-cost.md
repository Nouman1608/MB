This guide covers sub-topic **1.1 Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content, examined in Papers 1 and 2 and assumed knowledge for the A Level papers. The syllabus lists four points: the fundamental economic problem of scarcity (1.1.1); the need to make choices at all levels, by individuals, firms and governments (1.1.2); the nature and definition of opportunity cost, arising from choices (1.1.3); and the basic questions of resource allocation, what, how and for whom to produce (1.1.4). ## 1.1.1 The fundamental economic problem

**Scarcity** is the situation in which human wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them. Wants are unlimited: as incomes rise, people want better housing, more travel and new products. The resources that produce goods and services (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are limited at any moment in time.

Three distinctions sharpen an answer.

- **Scarcity is not the same as a shortage.** A shortage is a market situation in which quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price, and it can disappear if the price rises. Scarcity never disappears: even when a market clears, the resources used have alternative uses.
- **Scarcity affects rich and poor.** A wealthy household still faces limited time. A wealthy country still cannot provide every hospital, road and school its citizens would like.
- **Needs and wants.** Needs are things required for survival, such as food, water and shelter; wants are everything else people desire. Scarcity applies to both, but where needs are unmet the consequences of scarcity are more serious.

## 1.1.2 Choices at all levels

Because resources are scarce, every agent must choose. The choice is rarely all-or-nothing; it is usually about **how much more or less** of something. That is what the key concept of the margin means: decisions are made by comparing the extra benefit of one more unit with its extra cost.

| Level | Scarce resource | A marginal choice |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Income and time | Whether to work one more overtime shift this week |
| Firm | Capital, labour and budget | Whether to use a delivery van for one more route |
| Government | Tax revenue and borrowing capacity | Whether to add one more ward to a hospital project |

**Worked example 1: an individual at the margin.** A nurse is offered overtime at 18 currency units (cu) an hour. She values her first hour of free time on a Saturday at 10 cu, her second at 15 cu, her third at 22 cu and her fourth at 30 cu, because each hour given up eats further into rest and family time.

| Hour of overtime | Extra pay | Value of free time given up | Worth working? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 18 | 10 | Yes |
| 2nd | 18 | 15 | Yes |
| 3rd | 18 | 22 | No |

She works two extra hours. The opportunity cost of those two hours is the free time she values at 10 + 15 = 25 cu. The total question "should I work overtime?" is answered by looking at each extra hour.

## 1.1.3 Opportunity cost

**Opportunity cost** is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. It arises from choice: if resources were not scarce there would be nothing to give up.

Three features are tested.

1. **Only the next best alternative counts**, not every option rejected.
2. **It need not involve money.** It can be measured in goods given up, time or anything the decision maker values. Money is simply a convenient common measure.
3. **It is forward-looking.** Costs already paid and impossible to recover (often called sunk costs) are not opportunity costs, because choosing differently now cannot get them back.

**Worked example 2: a firm.** A fishing company owns one boat. On a given day it can fish, earning revenue of 2,400 cu after fuel, or take tourists on a sightseeing trip, earning 2,000 cu after fuel, or keep the boat in harbour for repairs, which it values at 900 cu in avoided future breakdown costs. If it fishes, the opportunity cost is the tourist trip (2,000 cu), the next best option, not 2,000 + 900. The company bought the boat years ago for 150,000 cu; that past payment is not part of today's opportunity cost.

**Worked example 3: a government, and the key concept of time.** A ministry has 60 million cu. It can build a vaccination centre network (60 million) or resurface 300 km of road (60 million, at 0.2 million per km). Choosing the vaccination network means the opportunity cost is 300 km of road. But time matters:

- The road would cut journey times from next year.
- The vaccination network would reduce illness, and so lost working days, over many years.

A choice that looks costly now can raise future output. Choosing capital goods and investment over present consumption gives up consumption today for more output later. Many exam questions on opportunity cost reward answers that recognise this present-versus-future trade-off.

## 1.1.4 What, how and for whom

Scarcity forces every economy to answer three basic questions.

| Question | What is being decided | Example in an invented dry region |
|---|---|---|
| What to produce? | Which goods and services, and how much of each | Should scarce water go to rice, which needs a lot, or to vegetables, which need less? |
| How to produce? | Which combination of factors and methods | Flood irrigation, which is labour-intensive and cheap, or drip irrigation, which is capital-intensive and uses less water? |
| For whom to produce? | How output is shared among people | Should water go to whoever pays most, or be rationed equally per household? |

**Worked example 4: how to produce.** A farm can grow 10 tonnes of vegetables using 50 days of labour and a basic pump, or using 20 days of labour and drip-irrigation equipment hired at 600 cu. Labour costs 40 cu a day.

- Labour-intensive method: 50 × 40 = 2,000 cu.
- Capital-intensive method: 20 × 40 + 600 = 1,400 cu.

The capital-intensive method is cheaper, so it uses fewer resources for the same output. If wages fell to 20 cu a day, the costs would be 1,000 cu and 1,000 cu, and the choice would be finely balanced. The answer to "how" depends on the relative prices of factors.

The three questions are answered differently in market, planned and mixed economies, which is the subject of 1.4.

## Common mistakes

- Defining opportunity cost without the words "next best".
- Adding up all the rejected options.
- Including money already spent and unrecoverable.
- Confusing scarcity with a shortage, or saying scarcity affects only poor countries.
- Treating choices as all-or-nothing when the question is about a little more or less.
- Listing what, how and for whom without explaining what each one decides.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** give the precise meaning in one sentence: "Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made."
- **Explain:** connect scarcity to choice to opportunity cost, in that order, using the agent in the question.
- **Analyse:** use the margin: compare the extra benefit and extra cost of one more unit.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate:** in Paper 2 essays, judge whether an opportunity cost is worth bearing, considering time (present versus future benefits) and who bears the cost.
