## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *progressive taxation* **[2]**
- (ii) *push factor* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of welfare state provision. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways NGOs try to reduce global inequalities. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways transnational companies may affect inequality between societies.
**[6]**

**(e)** Discuss New Right and Marxist criticisms of the welfare state. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that globalisation has increased inequalities between societies. **[14]**

### Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** feminist criticisms of equality legislation. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** economic consequences of migration for sending countries. **[4]**

**(i)** Discuss how far universal basic income could reduce inequality. **[8]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways colonialism may still affect inequalities between societies today.
**[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** A tax system in which people with higher incomes pay a higher percentage of their
income in tax than people with lower incomes (2). "Rich people pay more tax" alone: 1.

**(a)(ii) [2]** A condition in a person's home country that encourages them to leave, such as
poverty, unemployment, conflict or persecution (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 each: free or universal education; free healthcare; unemployment benefits;
pensions; low-cost housing; universal basic income.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (identify + explain):
- **Reducing poverty** — food aid, cash transfers and clean water projects meet basic needs.
- **Medical assistance** — organisations such as Médecins Sans Frontières provide emergency care.
- **Promoting business** — microcredit loans (Grameen Bank) help poor people, especially women,
  start small businesses.
- **Improving education** — building schools, training teachers, supporting girls' attendance.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- Create jobs and investment in poorer countries, raising some incomes.
- Pay low wages and exploit weak labour laws, so profits flow to richer countries.
- Extract natural resources and cause pollution in poorer areas.
- Transfer technology and skills.
- Avoid tax, reducing revenue for public services.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear and developed explanation of both, with a comparison; 4–6 some
development; 1–3 basic.
- **New Right:** welfare is too generous; it reduces work incentives, encourages dependency
  culture and family breakdown, creating an underclass (Murray, 1984); taxes on wealth creators
  discourage enterprise.
- **Marxist:** welfare does not change the capitalist system; it keeps workers healthy and
  productive, prevents unrest and hides exploitation; wealth inequality remains.
- **Comparison:** the New Right wants less welfare; Marxists want deeper change to the economic
  system.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated, with conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Marxist: global capitalism exploits cheap labour and resources in poorer societies.
- Colonialism's legacy shapes unequal trade.
- TNCs move profits to richer countries; pollution and resource extraction in poorer ones.
- Climate change hits poorer countries hardest.
- Brain drain removes skilled workers.
- Feminist: women in poorer societies face double exploitation in low-paid factory work and unpaid
  care.

*Against the view:*
- Investment, trade and technology have helped some countries grow rapidly.
- Remittances from migrants raise incomes in sending countries.
- NGOs and global agreements spread healthcare and education.
- Some inequality comes from internal factors such as corruption or conflict, not globalisation.

*Conclusion:* globalisation creates opportunities but distributes them unequally; credit a supported
judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each: the gender pay gap persists despite equal pay laws; laws do
not change the unequal division of unpaid domestic labour; women are concentrated in low-paid
part-time work; laws can be weakly enforced; laws treat individual cases rather than patriarchy as a
system.

**(h) [4]** Two consequences, 2 marks each: remittances raise family incomes and spending; loss of
skilled workers (brain drain) harms health and education services; reduced unemployment at home;
returning migrants bring savings and skills.

**(i) [8]** Levels as for (e).
- *Could reduce inequality:* gives everyone a guaranteed income floor, so no one falls into absolute
  poverty; avoids the stigma and complexity of means-tested benefits; supports unpaid carers, many
  of whom are women; gives security in a labour market changed by automation.
- *Limits:* if paid at the same rate to everyone it does not by itself narrow the gap between rich
  and poor unless funded by progressive taxation; it is very expensive; New Right critics argue it
  may reduce the incentive to work; trials such as Finland's (2017–2018) were small and short, so
  evidence is limited.
- Credit a judgement that depends on how it is funded and set.

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: economies shaped around exporting raw materials remain dependent
on low-value exports; borders and institutions inherited from colonial rule may contribute to
conflict and weak government; wealth and infrastructure built for extraction rather than local
development; continuing cultural and economic influence of former colonial powers through trade and
debt.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I reach the top level in (f)?** Use several developed arguments on both sides, link each
back to inequality *between societies*, and finish with a judgement.

**Should I use examples from my own country?** Yes — relevant local or regional examples are credited
as application.

**Are (g) and (h) exam-style?** They follow the "explain" style used in parts (c) and (d), with
shorter tariffs for extra practice.
