## D4.1.1 Natural selection as the mechanism of change

Natural selection is the process by which individuals with heritable traits that suit their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those traits become more common in later generations. It acts every generation and over billions of years, which is how today's biodiversity arose.

Before Darwin, many people accepted that species change, but the mechanism was unclear. Lamarck proposed that traits acquired in life are passed on. Darwin's mechanism explained the same observations and replaced that view. The guide calls this a **paradigm shift**: a fundamental change in the accepted framework of a field of science.

## D4.1.2 Where variation comes from

- **Mutation** is a change in the base sequence of DNA. It produces **new alleles**. Only mutations in cells that form gametes can be passed to offspring.
- **Sexual reproduction** produces **new combinations** of existing alleles. Crossing over and independent assortment in meiosis, then random fertilisation, mean that no two offspring from the same parents (apart from identical twins) carry the same combination.


## D4.1.3 and D4.1.4 Why selection happens

Many species produce far more offspring than can survive. A fish may release thousands of eggs, yet a stable population only needs two survivors per pair. The population cannot keep growing because the environment has a **carrying capacity**, the largest population it can sustain. Limiting resources include food, water, light, nesting sites and space. Individuals compete for them, and those with more suitable traits are more likely to get them.

**Worked example.** A fish lays 2000 eggs in her life and the population stays stable, so on average 2 of her offspring replace the parents. Percentage reaching adulthood = 2 ÷ 2000 × 100 = **0.1%**. The other 99.9% die, and survival is not random: it depends partly on traits.

**Abiotic factors** are non-living selection pressures: temperature, rainfall, salinity, pH, fire and light. Extremes such as a severe frost or a drought are **density-independent**: they affect survival whatever the population size. Competition for food is usually **density-dependent**, because it gets worse as numbers rise.

## D4.1.5 Fitness

Individuals of one species differ in how well they are adapted. Competition between members of the same species (**intraspecific competition**) is a major source of selection because they need the same resources.

**Fitness** here means how well a genotype does at surviving and reproducing: its survival value together with its reproductive potential. A long-lived animal that never breeds has a fitness of zero in evolutionary terms. What counts is the number of offspring that survive to reproduce.

## D4.1.6 Heritability

A trait changes a population only if it is **heritable**, meaning determined by alleles passed through gametes. Traits gained during life, such as the muscle of a weightlifter, a scar, or a tan, come from the environment. They are not written into the base sequence of the genes, so they are not inherited. This is the key flaw in Lamarck's idea.

**Reasoning check.** A cold snap kills more slow lizards than fast ones. Allele frequencies shift in the next generation only if speed has a genetic basis. If speed came only from diet, the survivors' offspring would show no change.

## D4.1.7 Sexual selection

In many animals one sex, usually the female, chooses mates, and males compete for access. Traits that help a male win a mate are favoured even if they cost survival. Physical or behavioural traits may signal overall fitness: bright colour, a long tail or an elaborate dance show that the male is healthy enough to afford them.

**Birds of paradise.** Males of many species have extravagant plumage and perform long courtship displays. Females choose among them. Males with the most striking features mate more, so alleles for those features spread over generations. Survival costs, such as being more visible to predators, limit how far it goes.

## D4.1.8 Modelling selection: Endler's guppies

John Endler studied guppies in streams in Trinidad and in experimental populations. Male guppies carry bright colour spots, and females prefer bright males. Predation pushes the other way, because bright males are easier to see. Sexual selection and natural selection therefore act in opposite directions, and the balance depends on the predators.

Endler controlled the selection pressure by keeping populations in pools with different levels of predators, or none, then compared the colour patterns after several generations. Where predators were absent or weak, males became more colourful. Where dangerous predators were present, males became duller.

**Interpreting data.** The figures below are invented to show the pattern, not Endler's results. The mean number of spots per male was 6.0 at the start in each tank.

| Tank | Mean spots after several generations |
|---|---|
| No predators | 8.1 |
| Strong predation | 4.2 |

No predators: (8.1 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **+35%**. Strong predation: (4.2 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **−30%**. With predators removed, female choice pushes colour up. With strong predators, natural selection against conspicuous males dominates. Only predator level changed, so predation is the likely cause.

## D4.1.9 and D4.1.10 Gene pools and isolated populations (HL only)

A **gene pool** is all the genes, with all their different alleles, in a population. The **allele frequency** is the proportion of copies of a gene that are one particular allele.

**Worked example.** A diploid population of 50 plants has 30 BB, 15 Bb and 5 bb. Total alleles = 50 × 2 = 100. Copies of B = 30 × 2 + 15 = 75, so frequency of B = 75 ÷ 100 = **0.75**. Copies of b = 15 + 5 × 2 = 25, so frequency of b = **0.25**. Check: 0.75 + 0.25 = 1.

Geographically isolated populations often differ in allele frequency. There is little or no gene flow between them, each meets different conditions, and mutations arise independently.

**Using databases (skills).** Public databases such as the ALFRED allele frequency database or the 1000 Genomes browser let you search a gene and compare populations. Record the population, sample size and allele. Use at least one human example: the allele for lactase persistence is common in some populations with a history of dairy farming and rare in many others. Treat small samples with caution.

## D4.1.11 Selection changes allele frequency (HL only)

**Neo-Darwinism** combines Darwin's theory of natural selection with genetics: evolution is a change in allele frequency in the gene pool, driven by differences in survival and reproduction between individuals with different heritable traits.

## D4.1.12 Three types of selection (HL only)

| Type | Favoured | Effect on the distribution | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Directional | One extreme | Mean shifts toward it | Insecticide resistance in mosquitoes |
| Stabilizing | Intermediate | Extremes lost, spread narrows | Human birth mass |
| Disruptive | Both extremes | Splits towards two peaks | Bill size in seedcracker finches |

All three change allele frequencies. Even stabilizing selection removes alleles that produce extremes.

## D4.1.13 Hardy-Weinberg calculations (HL only)

For a gene with two alleles, let p be the frequency of the dominant allele and q the frequency of the recessive allele.

- p + q = 1
- p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p² is the frequency of homozygous dominant, 2pq of heterozygous and q² of homozygous recessive.

**Method.** Start from whichever value you can find. The recessive phenotype is usually the only one that reveals a genotype, so start with q².

**Worked example 1.** In a beetle population, 16% are green (recessive, aa). q² = 0.16, so q = √0.16 = **0.4** and p = 1 − 0.4 = **0.6**.
Homozygous dominant p² = 0.36, heterozygous 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = **0.48**, homozygous recessive q² = 0.16. Check: 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.16 = 1. In 500 beetles: 180 AA, 240 Aa, 80 aa.

**Worked example 2.** A recessive condition affects 1.44% of a population. q² = 0.0144, so q = 0.12 and p = 0.88. Carriers = 2pq = 2 × 0.88 × 0.12 = **0.2112**, about 21%. Most recessive alleles sit in carriers.

**Worked example 3 (from genotype counts).** In 400 snails there are 150 AA, 180 Aa, 70 aa. Alleles = 800. A = (2 × 150 + 180) ÷ 800 = 0.60, so q = 0.40. Expected under Hardy-Weinberg: AA 144, Aa 192, aa 64.

## D4.1.14 Hardy-Weinberg conditions (HL only)

Allele and genotype frequencies stay constant from one generation to the next only if:

1. the population is large;
2. mating is random;
3. there is no mutation;
4. there is no migration (gene flow) in or out;
5. there is no selection: all genotypes survive and reproduce equally.

Real populations rarely meet all five, so the equation is a baseline. If genotype counts do not fit it, at least one condition fails. In example 3, heterozygotes are fewer than expected (180 against 192), which suggests non-random mating or lower survival of heterozygotes. A small difference could also be chance.

**Worked example 4: one generation of selection.** Use the beetles: AA 0.36, Aa 0.48, aa 0.16. Suppose only half of the aa beetles survive to breed. Relative numbers are 0.36, 0.48 and 0.08, total 0.92.
New q = (0.48 ÷ 2 + 0.08) ÷ 0.92 = 0.32 ÷ 0.92 = **0.348** (3 s.f.), down from 0.4. Selection against the recessive homozygote has lowered the frequency of its allele.

## D4.1.15 Artificial selection (HL only)

In **artificial selection** humans choose which individuals breed, picking those with desirable traits in crops and domesticated animals. Repeating this over generations shifts the population, as in higher-yielding maize and dairy cattle that give more milk. The cost can be reduced variation.

Unintended results are different. When antibiotics kill most of a bacterial population, resistant bacteria survive and multiply. Nobody chose them for breeding, so this is **natural selection**, with the antibiotic acting as the selection pressure.

## Common errors

- Writing that individuals evolve. Populations evolve; individuals do not change their alleles.
- Treating acquired characteristics as heritable.
- Using p² for the recessive phenotype. The recessive homozygote is q².
- Forgetting to square-root q² before finding p.
- Calling antibiotic resistance artificial selection.
