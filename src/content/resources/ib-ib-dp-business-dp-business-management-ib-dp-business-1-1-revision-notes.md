These notes condense section 1.1 (What is a business?) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. The guide lists the nature of business; the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors; entrepreneurship; and the challenges and opportunities for starting up a business. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [1.1 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1/); for exam-style questions, use the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1-practice/). Sections 1.1 and 1.2 are also summarised together in the [what is a business and types of business entities revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-what-is-a-business-and-types-of-business-entities-revision-notes/). Definitions are standard textbook forms; examples are invented, in dollars.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Business | An organization that combines human, physical and financial resources to provide goods or services |
| Inputs | The resources a business acquires: people, materials, premises, machinery, money |
| Outputs | The goods or services it sells |
| Business functions | Human resources, finance and accounts, marketing, operations |
| Value added | Selling price minus the cost of inputs bought from other firms |
| Chain of production | The stages a product passes through, often across several sectors |
| Sectoral change | A shift over time in the share of output and jobs in each sector |
| Entrepreneur | A person who spots an opportunity, organizes resources and bears the risk of a venture |
| Social entrepreneur | An entrepreneur whose main aim is a social or environmental purpose |
| Start-up capital | The money needed before trading begins |

## The business as a system

**Inputs → process → outputs**, managed by four functions:

- **Human resources**: right people, right skills, motivated.
- **Finance and accounts**: money raised, spent and recorded.
- **Marketing**: customer needs identified and met; products promoted.
- **Operations**: inputs turned into outputs efficiently and to standard.

A change in one function affects the others. Extra demand from marketing needs operations capacity, staff and working capital.

## Value added

```
Value added = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs
```

- Value added is not profit: wages, rent and interest are still to be paid from it.
- Services can add more value than manufacturing if customers pay for convenience, expertise or brand.
- Along a chain, the value added at each stage plus all bought-in inputs from outside the chain equals the final price.

*Reminder.* A café buys coffee beans, milk and cups for 0.90 per drink and sells the drink for 3.50: value added 2.60 per drink.

## The four sectors

| Sector | Activity | Classify by asking |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing natural resources | Is something taken from nature? |
| Secondary | Manufacturing, processing, construction | Are materials changed into products? |
| Tertiary | Services to customers | Is the customer buying a service? |
| Quaternary | Knowledge, research, information | Is the output knowledge or data? |

Classify each activity, not the whole firm.

## Sectoral change

- Economies often shift from primary toward secondary, then tertiary, and some toward quaternary.
- Causes: technology, rising incomes, global competition, changing consumer tastes.
- Effects on firms: changing demand, new skills needed, pressure to relocate or automate, new markets in services and knowledge.

## Entrepreneurship

- The fourth factor of production, organizing land, labour and capital.
- Four elements: spotting an opportunity, bearing risk, organizing resources, innovating.
- Reward: profit (or social impact for a social entrepreneur).
- Motives: independence, income, a social purpose, filling a gap.

## Start-up challenges and opportunities

**Method for a case:**

1. Find the **funding gap**: start-up costs minus finance already arranged.
2. Find the **early cash shortfall**: monthly costs minus monthly contribution in the first months.
3. Find when the business **recovers** that shortfall.
4. Weigh the numbers against the opportunity (demand, low competition, flexibility).
5. Judge, and state what the judgement depends on, usually a forecast.

*Reminder.* Costs of 3,000 a month and contribution of 2,200 a month for four months give a shortfall of 4 × 800 = 3,200 before the business is self-supporting.

## Exam traps

- Calling value added "profit".
- Classifying a firm in one sector when it carries out activities in several.
- Saying services "add no value".
- Defining an entrepreneur without mentioning risk.
- Listing challenges with no data or detail from the case.
- Ignoring early cash flow when judging a start-up.
- A "discuss" or "evaluate" answer with no final judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three types of resource a business combines.
2. Name the four business functions.
3. A furniture maker buys wood and fittings for 140 per table and sells each table for 390. Find the value added.
4. Explain why value added is not the same as profit.
5. A smartphone app studio that sells its data analysis to retailers is in which sector?
6. A dairy farm makes cheese and sells it at a farmers' market. Classify each activity.
7. Give two causes of a shift from the secondary to the tertiary sector.
8. State the four elements of entrepreneurship.
9. How does a social entrepreneur's reward differ from a commercial entrepreneur's?
10. A start-up needs 50,000; the owner has 20,000 and a loan of 22,000. Find the funding gap.
11. Monthly costs are 4,000 and monthly contribution is 2,500 for the first five months. Find the total early cash shortfall.
12. Give one opportunity of starting a business that can be linked to sectoral change.

### Answers

1. Human, physical and financial resources.
2. Human resources, finance and accounts, marketing, operations.
3. 390 − 140 = **250** per table.
4. Value added must still pay wages, rent, interest and other costs that are not bought-in inputs; profit is what remains after those.
5. **Quaternary**: it sells knowledge and information.
6. Dairy farming: primary. Cheese-making: secondary. Selling at the market: tertiary.
7. Any two: rising incomes raising demand for services; manufacturing moving to lower-cost countries; automation reducing factory jobs.
8. Spotting an opportunity, bearing risk, organizing resources, innovating.
9. The social entrepreneur's main reward is the social or environmental impact achieved; any surplus is mainly reinvested in that purpose.
10. 50,000 − 42,000 = **8,000**.
11. 5 × (4,000 − 2,500) = **7,500**.
12. For example: growth in the quaternary sector creates demand for data, research or software services that a new firm could supply.
