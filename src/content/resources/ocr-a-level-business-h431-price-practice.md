## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by price skimming. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** risk to a business of using penetration pricing. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a positive cross elasticity of demand. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Finch Furniture makes a chair. The variable cost is £9 per chair, fixed costs are £90,000 a year and normal output is 15,000 chairs.

**(a)** Calculate the full cost per chair. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the price using a 50% mark-up on full cost. **[1]**

**(c)** A hotel offers to buy 3,000 extra chairs at £13 each. Finch has spare capacity. Calculate the extra contribution from the order. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate whether Finch should accept the hotel's offer. **[6]**

**5.** Finch cuts the price of a desk from £40 to £36. Monthly sales rise from 2,000 to 2,500.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the revenue before and after the price cut. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what the result shows about demand and about the decision to cut the price. **[3]**

**6.** Finch's premium sofa and budget sofa respond differently when consumers' incomes rise by 5%. Demand for the premium sofa rises by 9% and demand for the budget sofa falls by 2%. A rival raises the price of its desks by 6% and Finch's desk sales rise by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the income elasticity of demand for each sofa and state what each shows. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the cross elasticity of demand for Finch's desk with respect to the rival's price and interpret it. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate the usefulness of elasticity calculations to Finch Furniture. **[9]**

**7.** Zinc Drones is about to launch a new camera drone that is faster than any rival's. It has high development costs, and a larger rival is expected to launch a similar product within a year. Recommend and justify a pricing strategy for Zinc Drones. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Setting a high price when a product is launched [1], then lowering it over time, to earn high revenue from customers who will pay more at first [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The low price gives low profit per unit in the early stage [1], so if the expected rise in sales does not happen the business may make a loss [1], and it may be difficult to raise the price later because customers have come to expect the low price [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The demand for one product rises when the price of another product rises [1], which shows the two goods are substitutes [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Fixed cost per chair = 90,000 ÷ 15,000 = £6 [1]; full cost = 9 + 6 = **£15** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 15 × 1.50 = **£22.50** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Contribution per chair = 13 − 9 = £4 [1]; 3,000 × 4 = **£12,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** For: the price of £13 is above the variable cost of £9, so each chair adds £4 to profit [1]; the fixed costs are covered by normal sales, so the £12,000 is extra profit [1]; the spare capacity would otherwise be unused [1].
Against: £13 is below the full cost of £15 and well below the usual price of £22.50 [1]; regular customers who hear about the discount may demand lower prices [1].
Judgement: Finch should accept the order if it has spare capacity and can keep the price confidential or the order is for a different market, since the extra profit is certain and the risk of damage is small [1]. **[6]**

**5. (a)** % change in price = (36 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = −10% [1]. % change in quantity = (2,500 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = +25% [1]. PED = 25 ÷ −10 = **−2.5** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Before: 40 × 2,000 = **£80,000** [1]. After: 36 × 2,500 = **£90,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** A PED of −2.5 means demand is elastic (the number is greater than 1) [1], so the quantity rises by a larger percentage than the price falls [1], and the price cut increases revenue by £10,000, so it was a good decision for revenue, although total profit also depends on the extra cost of making 500 more desks [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Premium: 9 ÷ 5 = **+1.8** [1], a normal good and a luxury, because demand grows faster than income [1]. Budget: −2 ÷ 5 = **−0.4** [1], an inferior good, as demand falls when incomes rise [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** XED = 3 ÷ 6 = **+0.5** [1]; the desks are substitutes, but weak ones, as the effect on Finch's sales is small [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Useful: PED shows whether a price cut or rise will increase revenue, as in the desk example [1]; YED shows which products to expand in a boom (premium sofa) and which to support in a recession (budget sofa) [1]; XED helps Finch to predict the effect of a rival's price change and to decide whether to respond [1]; together they help forecasting and planning stock [1].
Limits: calculations use past data and assume that other factors, such as fashion or advertising, stay the same [1]; one result may not apply to a bigger price change [1]; results differ between customer groups and over time, so averages may mislead [1].
Judgement: the calculations are useful guides to decisions and should be updated regularly with fresh data, but they are estimates and should be combined with market research and testing [2]. **[9]**

**7.** Skimming: the drone is faster than any rival, which gives a USP, and customers who want the best will pay a high price [1]; high development costs can be recovered quickly [1]; the high price signals quality [1]; the price can be lowered later when the rival arrives [1].
Penetration: a low price would win share quickly, but a small firm with high costs would earn little profit and may not recover costs, and it may start a price war with a larger rival that has lower costs [1].
Recommendation: skim at launch, with a plan to reduce the price in steps when the rival's product launches, to keep the customers who value speed [1]. Sell accessories or software at a lower price to build loyalty [1].
Risks: the window is only a year, so skimming must recover costs quickly; if demand is price elastic, volume may be too low [1]. Judgement: skimming is best, but Zinc should test the price with early customers and be ready to cut it [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the quantity by the price instead of the percentage changes.
- Ignoring the sign when interpreting YED or XED.
- Refusing a special order only because the price is below full cost.
- Recommending a strategy without linking it to the situation.
