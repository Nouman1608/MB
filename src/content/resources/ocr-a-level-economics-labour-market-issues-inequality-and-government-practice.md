## Questions

**1.** Country C has 6.0m people under 16, 24.0m aged 16 to 64 and 8.0m aged 65 or over. Its labour force is 19.2m. A typical out-of-work household receives £7,200 a year in benefits; the same worker would take home £12,000.

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the participation rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the replacement ratio and say what it suggests about incentives. **[2]**

**2.** In a market for cleaners in a town, labour demand is Ld = 240 − 10w and labour supply is Ls = −60 + 20w (thousands of hours; w in £ per hour).

**(a)** Find the wage and number of hours at which the market clears. **[2]**
**(b)** The government sets a legal wage floor of £12. Calculate the resulting unemployment. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that the total wage bill rises, and explain what this implies about labour demand. **[2]**

**3.** Using the market in question 2, a maximum wage of £8 is imposed.

**(a)** Calculate the shortage of labour. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two likely consequences. **[2]**
**(c)** Distinguish a voluntary living wage from a legal minimum wage. **[1]**

**4.** Evaluate whether retraining schemes or relocation grants would be more effective in reducing structural unemployment in a region where factories have closed. **[6]**

**5.** A firm agrees a productivity deal: hourly pay rises 3% and output per hour rises 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in labour cost per unit of output. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason a firm may prefer productivity bargaining to a simple pay rise. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how collective bargaining could harm workers outside the union. **[2]**

**6.** In a fruit-picking market, labour demand is Ld = 300 − 15w and native labour supply is Ls = −30 + 15w (thousands; w in £ per hour). Migrants then add 30 workers at every wage.

**(a)** Calculate the original equilibrium. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(c)** State the number of native workers employed after migration. **[1]**
**(d)** Evaluate one effect of migration other than on wages. **[2]**

**7.** Mean hourly pay is £18.00 for Group M and £16.20 for Group F. Differences in hours and experience explain £1.20 of the gap.

**(a)** Calculate the gap as a percentage of Group M's pay. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the unexplained part as a share of the gap. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the unexplained part is not conclusive proof of discrimination. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one economic cost of discrimination. **[1]**

**8.** Country D has 10.0m people under 16, 40.0m aged 16 to 64 and 12.0m aged 65 or over. In 25 years these are projected at 9.0m, 38.0m and 17.0m.

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio now and in 25 years. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate workers (16 to 64) per person aged 65 or over, now and later. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate raising the age at which the state pension is paid. **[4]**

**9.** A job pays £9,600 a year net. Out-of-work benefits are £8,400. Travel and childcare cost £700 a year when working.

**(a)** Calculate the replacement ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net gain from working before and after work costs. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest one policy to improve work incentives and one drawback. **[2]**

**10.** Country E has recorded GDP of 650 (illustrative units). Informal activity is estimated at 8% of recorded GDP and the average tax rate is 28%.

**(a)** Calculate unrecorded output and the maximum tax lost. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this is an upper limit. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one benefit and one cost of informal labour markets. **[2]**

**11.**
**(a)** Distinguish income inequality from wealth inequality. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether education and training policy or changes to the tax and benefit system would do more to reduce income inequality. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Dependents = 6.0 + 8.0 = 14.0m. [1] 14.0/24.0 × 100 = **58.3%**. [1]
**(b)** 19.2/24.0 × 100 = **80%**. [1]
**(c)** 7,200/12,000 × 100 = **60%**. [1] Benefits are well below pay, so the incentive to work is stronger. [1]
*Examiner insight:* choose the working-age group as the denominator and say so. Using the whole population is a common slip that changes every answer.

**2. (a)** 240 − 10w = −60 + 20w gives 300 = 30w. [1] **w = £10**, **L = 140**. [1]
**(b)** At £12: demand = 120 and supply = 180. [1] Unemployment = **60**. [1]
**(c)** Bill before = 10 × 140 = 1,400; after = 12 × 120 = 1,440. [1] The bill rises because the fall in employment (14.3%) is smaller than the wage rise (20%), so demand is inelastic in this range. [1]
*Examiner insight:* put the controlled wage into both equations, and take employment from the demand side. Candidates who read employment from supply overstate it.

**3. (a)** Demand at £8 = 160; supply = 100. [1] Shortage = **60**. [1]
**(b)** Employers cannot fill 60 hours, so output or service suffers. [1] Workers may be paid in cash or leave for better pay elsewhere. [1]
**(c)** A living wage is a rate employers choose to pay, based on living costs; a legal minimum is enforced by law. [1]
*Examiner insight:* a ceiling produces a shortage. Calling it unemployment mixes up the two controls.

**4.** Retraining raises occupational mobility, so unemployed workers can fit vacancies. [1] But it is slow, costly and fails if the new skills do not match local demand. [1] Relocation grants raise geographical mobility, so workers move to jobs. [1] But housing costs, family ties and loss of local population limit take-up. [1] Judgement: retraining tends to be better where the region keeps some demand for labour, relocation where no jobs will return. The mix depends on age, skills and the time available. [2]
*Examiner insight:* answers that weigh the two policies against the region's circumstances read as evaluation. A list of pros and cons for each, without a conclusion, tends to stay descriptive.

**5. (a)** 1.03/1.05 − 1 [1] = **−1.9%**. [1]
**(b)** Pay rises are tied to output gains, so unit costs do not rise. [1]
**(c)** A union wage above equilibrium cuts the quantity of labour hired. [1] Those excluded must find work in lower-paid, non-union jobs, increasing their supply and lowering wages there. [1]
*Examiner insight:* divide the growth factors rather than subtracting the percentages. 3% minus 5% gives a different figure.

**6. (a)** 300 − 15w = −30 + 15w gives w = **£11**, L = **135**. [1][1]
**(b)** Supply becomes L = 15w. [1] 300 − 15w = 15w gives **w = £10** and **L = 150**. [1]
**(c)** Natives: −30 + 150 = **120**. [1]
**(d)** Migrants may fill shortages and pay tax, increasing output. [1] But extra demand for housing and services may raise costs in the short run. [1]
*Examiner insight:* name the effect, give its direction, and say who gains or loses. A claim that migration "helps the economy" without a mechanism carries little.

**7. (a)** 1.80/18.00 × 100 = **10%**. [1]
**(b)** 0.60/1.80 = **33.3%**. [1]
**(c)** The unexplained part may reflect other differences that are not measured, such as job type or qualifications. [1]
**(d)** Talented workers are under-used, so output is lower than it could be. [1]
*Examiner insight:* the gap is a starting point. Careful answers separate the explained part from the unexplained part before drawing any conclusion.

**8. (a)** Now 22.0/40.0 = **55.0%**. [1] Later 26.0/38.0 = **68.4%**. [1]
**(b)** 40/12 = **3.3**; 38/17 = **2.2**. [1]
**(c)** A later pension age cuts state spending and keeps more people in the labour force. [1] It raises tax receipts and eases skills shortages. [1] But people in poor health or physical jobs may be unable to work, so it can worsen inequality, and older workers may find few vacancies. [1] Judgement: effectiveness depends on health, job availability and how quickly the change is phased in. [1]
*Examiner insight:* tie the policy to the ratio you calculated. Linking a lower number of workers per pensioner to the policy gives the answer direction.

**9. (a)** 8,400/9,600 × 100 [1] = **87.5%**. [1]
**(b)** Gain = 9,600 − 8,400 = **£1,200**. [1] After work costs = 1,200 − 700 = **£500**. [1]
**(c)** Lower the rate at which benefits are withdrawn as earnings rise, so more of extra pay is kept. [1] Drawback: it costs more and extends support up the income range. [1]
*Examiner insight:* include work costs when assessing incentives, because the net gain is the figure a worker compares.

**10. (a)** 650 × 0.08 = **52**. [1] 52 × 0.28 = **14.56**. [1]
**(b)** Much informal work would not exist if it had to be declared and taxed, so not all of it would be taxed. [1]
**(c)** Benefit: income and flexibility for low-income households. [1] Cost: no legal protection or pension, and lost tax. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "upper limit" needs a reason. Saying the figure is only an estimate does not explain why it is a maximum.

**11. (a)** Income inequality is the uneven spread of income flows over a period. [1] Wealth inequality is the uneven spread of asset holdings at a point in time. [1]
**(b)** Education and training raise human capital and wages for lower earners. [1] But the effect is slow, depends on the quality of provision and may not reach those already out of work. [1] Tax and benefit changes act quickly: progressive taxes and transfers move income from high to low earners. [1] But high marginal rates and benefit withdrawal can reduce incentives and employment. [1] Judgement: tax and benefits give faster results, training gives longer-term and more lasting change. [1] A combination avoids relying on one lever, and the best mix depends on whether inequality arises from low skills or from low participation. [1]
*Examiner insight:* decide which lever works more quickly and which lasts longer, then give a reason for your choice. Without a chosen answer the response reads as a list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by total population instead of the working-age group.
- Finding unemployment from the supply curve alone.
- Subtracting growth rates instead of dividing the factors.
- Confusing a shortage with unemployment.
- Naming a policy without saying how it changes mobility or incentives.
- Treating every pay gap as discrimination.
- Describing an effect of migration with no direction or mechanism.
- Ending evaluation without a conclusion.
