## 6.2.1 and 6.2.2 Genes and the code

A **gene** is a sequence of nucleotides, forming part of a DNA molecule, that codes for a polypeptide. The order of bases in the gene determines the order of amino acids.

Key properties of the genetic code:

- **Triplet:** three bases code for one amino acid. On mRNA, a triplet is a **codon**.
- **Start and stop:** AUG on mRNA starts translation (and codes for methionine); UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons and code for no amino acid.
- **Universal:** the same triplets code for the same amino acids in almost all organisms. This is why a human coding sequence, without introns, inserted into bacteria can be translated into the human polypeptide.
- **Degenerate:** most amino acids have more than one codon. Twenty amino acids are coded by 64 possible triplets, so some triplets are "spare".
- **Non-overlapping:** each base belongs to only one codon, and codons are read one after another from a fixed start point.

The codons used on this page (all mRNA):

| Codon | Amino acid | Codon | Amino acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| AUG | Met (start) | AAA, AAG | Lys |
| GCU | Ala | CAC | His |
| GAU | Asp | CUU, CUG, UUG | Leu |
| UGG | Trp | GGA | Gly |
| AGC | Ser | AAC | Asn |
| GAA | Glu | ACU, ACA | Thr |
| UGA, UAA, UAG | Stop | | |

## 6.2.3 and 6.2.4 Transcription

1. In the nucleus, the section of DNA containing the gene unwinds, and hydrogen bonds between its two strands break.
2. **RNA polymerase** moves along the **template (transcribed) strand**. Free RNA nucleotides pair with its exposed bases: A on DNA with U on RNA, T with A, C with G, G with C.
3. RNA polymerase joins the RNA nucleotides by phosphodiester bonds, building the RNA 5′ to 3′.
4. At the end of the gene, the RNA is released and the DNA strands rejoin.

Because the RNA is complementary to the template strand, its sequence is the **same as the non-transcribed strand**, except that U replaces T. This gives a quick check in calculations.

## 6.2.5 Splicing in eukaryotes

The first RNA made, the **primary transcript**, contains **introns** (non-coding) and **exons** (coding). Introns are removed and exons are joined to form **mRNA**, which then leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.

**Worked example 1: from primary transcript to polypeptide length.** A primary transcript has 2 601 nucleotides and contains three introns of 300, 450 and 150 nucleotides. Assume the exons contain only the coding sequence, from start codon to stop codon.

```
Introns removed: 300 + 450 + 150 = 900
mRNA length: 2 601 - 900 = 1 701 nucleotides
Codons: 1 701 / 3 = 567
Amino acids: 567 - 1 (stop codon) = 566
```

The gene in DNA is longer than the mRNA, so you cannot calculate polypeptide length from gene length in a eukaryote without knowing the introns.

## 6.2.3 Translation

1. mRNA attaches to a **ribosome** in the cytoplasm.
2. A **tRNA** carrying methionine has the anticodon UAC, which pairs with the start codon AUG by complementary base pairing.
3. A second tRNA, carrying a specific amino acid, pairs its **anticodon** with the next **codon**.
4. A **peptide bond** forms between the two amino acids.
5. The ribosome moves along one codon. The first tRNA, now without its amino acid, leaves to collect another.
6. This repeats until a **stop codon** is reached. No tRNA pairs with it, and the polypeptide is released.

### Worked example 2: from the non-transcribed strand

Non-transcribed strand: 5′ – ATG GCT TGG AAA CAC TGA – 3′

| | Codon 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Template strand (3′→5′) | TAC | CGA | ACC | TTT | GTG | ACT |
| mRNA (5′→3′) | AUG | GCU | UGG | AAA | CAC | UGA |
| tRNA anticodon | UAC | CGA | ACC | UUU | GUG | none |
| Amino acid | Met | Ala | Trp | Lys | His | Stop |

The polypeptide is **Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–His**: five amino acids. Notice that each anticodon has the same letters as the template triplet, with U in place of T.

## 6.2.6 and 6.2.7 Gene mutations

A **gene mutation** is a change in the sequence of base pairs in a DNA molecule that may result in an altered polypeptide. Applying each type to the mRNA above (AUG GCU UGG AAA CAC UGA) shows the range of effects.

| Mutation (shown on mRNA) | New reading | Polypeptide | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | AUG GCU UGG AAA CAC UGA | Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–His | Normal |
| Substitution: AAA → AAG | AUG GCU UGG AAG CAC UGA | Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–His | None: new codon codes for the same amino acid (degenerate code) |
| Substitution: GCU → GAU | AUG GAU UGG AAA CAC UGA | Met–Asp–Trp–Lys–His | One amino acid changed |
| Substitution: UGG → UGA | AUG GCU UGA | Met–Ala | Early stop codon: shortened polypeptide |
| Deletion of the first G of GCU | AUG CUU GGA AAC ACU GA... | Met–Leu–Gly–Asn–Thr... | Frame shift: every codon after the deletion changes; original stop lost |
| Insertion of A after AUG | AUG AGC UUG GAA ACA CUG A... | Met–Ser–Leu–Glu–Thr–Leu... | Frame shift: every codon after the insertion changes |

A substitution changes at most one codon. A deletion or insertion of a single base pair shifts the **reading frame**, so it usually changes many amino acids. If three base pairs are deleted or inserted, one amino acid is lost or gained and the frame is restored.

**Why does a changed amino acid matter?** A different amino acid may have a different R group, which can change the bonds that hold the tertiary structure. An enzyme's active site may change shape, so it may no longer bind its substrate. A change in a part of the protein not involved in its function may have little effect.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Explain why the deletion of one base pair near the start of a gene usually has a greater effect on the polypeptide than a substitution. **[4]**

> A substitution changes only one triplet, so at most one amino acid is changed, and because the code is degenerate it may not change any. *(Substitution, with degeneracy.)*
>
> A deletion causes a frame shift, so every triplet after the deletion is read differently. *(Frame shift named.)*
>
> Many amino acids after the deletion are changed, and a stop codon may appear early, giving a shorter polypeptide. *(Consequences.)*
>
> The primary structure is greatly altered, so the tertiary structure and function of the protein are likely to be lost. *(Links to protein function.)*

## Common mistakes

- Calling a triplet on DNA a codon; codons are on mRNA, anticodons on tRNA.
- Writing T in an mRNA sequence.
- Saying the mRNA is identical to the template strand; it is complementary to it and matches the non-transcribed strand.
- Counting the stop codon as an amino acid.
- Placing transcription in the cytoplasm or translation in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell.
- Saying every mutation changes the polypeptide.
