Subtopic 6.3 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 has eleven learning outcomes. They start with the statement that "some chemical reactions are reversible as shown by the symbol ⇌", build up to predicting "how the position of equilibrium is affected by" temperature, pressure, concentration and a catalyst "using information provided", and end with two industrial processes whose conditions you must explain "in terms of rate of reaction and position of equilibrium… including safety considerations and economics".

This guide practises the prediction skill on unfamiliar reactions, then applies it to the Haber and Contact processes. ## Reversible reactions: the named examples

A reversible reaction can go in either direction, depending on the conditions. Outcome 2 names two hydrated compounds:

```
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)  ⇌  CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)
blue (hydrated)     white (anhydrous)

CoCl₂·6H₂O(s)  ⇌  CoCl₂(s) + 6H₂O(l)
pink (hydrated)     blue (anhydrous)
```

- **Heating** the hydrated compound drives the forward reaction: water of crystallisation is lost and the colour changes.
- **Adding water** to the anhydrous compound drives the reverse reaction, and the original colour returns.

The forward direction (dehydration) is endothermic; the reverse (hydration) is exothermic, so the white powder warms up noticeably when water is added. These reverse reactions are the chemical tests for water in subtopic 10.1: white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns blue, and blue anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride turns pink.

## Equilibrium

A reversible reaction in a **closed system** (nothing can enter or leave) reaches **equilibrium** when:

- the rate of the forward reaction **equals** the rate of the reverse reaction
- the concentrations of reactants and products are **no longer changing**

Both reactions are still happening at equilibrium; they simply cancel each other out. The concentrations are constant, but they are **not necessarily equal**. A closed system is essential: if a product gas could escape, the reverse reaction could not keep pace and equilibrium would never be reached.

"Position of equilibrium" describes the balance of reactants and products. **Shifting to the right** means more products; **shifting to the left** means more reactants.

## Predicting the effect of a change

When a condition is changed, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that **opposes the change**.

| Change | Shift | Information you need |
|---|---|---|
| Increase temperature | Towards the **endothermic** direction | Sign of ΔH for the forward reaction |
| Decrease temperature | Towards the **exothermic** direction | Sign of ΔH |
| Increase pressure | Towards the side with **fewer gas molecules** | Number of gas molecules on each side |
| Decrease pressure | Towards the side with **more gas molecules** | Number of gas molecules on each side |
| Increase concentration of a reactant (or remove a product) | To the **right** | Which substances are reactants and products |
| Add a catalyst | **No shift** | None: equilibrium is reached faster, but the position is the same |

If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, pressure has **no effect** on the position.

### Worked example 1: a coloured gas mixture

N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g); the forward reaction is endothermic. N₂O₄ is colourless; NO₂ is brown.

- **Raise the temperature:** equilibrium shifts in the endothermic direction, to the right. More NO₂ forms and the mixture becomes **darker brown**.
- **Increase the pressure:** the left has 1 gas molecule, the right has 2. Equilibrium shifts to the side with fewer molecules, the left, so the proportion of brown NO₂ **decreases**.
- **Add a catalyst:** no change in colour once equilibrium is reached again; equilibrium is simply reached sooner.

### Worked example 2: equal numbers of gas molecules

H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)

There are 2 gas molecules on each side, so changing the pressure **does not change** the position of equilibrium. It does change the rate, because the gases are more concentrated, so equilibrium is reached faster.

### Worked example 3: changing concentration in solution

2CrO₄²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
yellow                     orange

- **Add acid** (more H⁺, a reactant): equilibrium shifts to the right and the solution turns **orange**.
- **Add alkali**: OH⁻ ions remove H⁺ ions by neutralisation, lowering their concentration, so equilibrium shifts to the left and the solution turns **yellow**.

You do not need to know this reaction; the point is that the question gives you all the information needed.

## The Haber process

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| Equation | N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) |
| Sources | Nitrogen from **air**; hydrogen from **methane** |
| Conditions | **450 °C**, **20 000 kPa / 200 atm**, **iron catalyst** |
| Forward reaction | Exothermic; 4 gas molecules → 2 gas molecules |

**Temperature, 450 °C.** The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature would shift equilibrium to the right and give a higher yield. But at a low temperature the rate is too slow to be economic. 450 °C is a **compromise** between a reasonable yield and a fast enough rate.

**Pressure, 200 atm.** There are fewer gas molecules on the right, so a high pressure shifts equilibrium to the right, increasing the yield; it also increases the rate. Even higher pressures would give more ammonia, but the equipment needed is **expensive** to build and run, and very high pressures increase the **risk** of leaks and explosions. 200 atm balances yield against cost and safety.

**Iron catalyst.** Increases the rate so equilibrium is reached faster; it has **no effect on the yield**.

## The Contact process

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| Equation | 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) |
| Sources | Sulfur dioxide from **burning sulfur** or **roasting sulfide ores**; oxygen from **air** |
| Conditions | **450 °C**, **200 kPa / 2 atm**, **vanadium(V) oxide catalyst** |
| Forward reaction | Exothermic; 3 gas molecules → 2 gas molecules |

**Temperature, 450 °C.** The same compromise as in the Haber process: lower temperatures favour the exothermic forward reaction but make the rate too slow.

**Pressure, 2 atm.** High pressure would favour the right-hand side (fewer gas molecules), but the position of equilibrium already lies well to the right at low pressure, so a high conversion is achieved without it. The small extra yield would not justify the cost and danger of high-pressure equipment. This is the key contrast with the Haber process.

**Vanadium(V) oxide catalyst.** Increases the rate; no effect on the position of equilibrium.

## Common mistakes

- **"At equilibrium the reaction stops."** Both reactions continue at equal rates.
- **"At equilibrium the concentrations are equal."** They are constant, not equal.
- **"A catalyst increases the yield."** It only speeds up the approach to equilibrium.
- **Explaining 450 °C only in terms of yield.** Rate must be mentioned, because rate is the reason the temperature is not lower.
- **Counting all molecules for pressure**, including solids or liquids. Count gas molecules only.
- **Mixing up the two pressures:** 200 atm for Haber, 2 atm for Contact.
- **Forgetting the sources** of hydrogen (methane) and sulfur dioxide (sulfur or sulfide ores).

## Command words

- **State:** an equation, a source or a condition, recalled exactly.
- **Predict:** say which way the equilibrium shifts and what you would observe.
- **Explain:** give the reason, using ΔH for temperature or the number of gas molecules for pressure.
- **Explain** (industrial conditions): cover rate, yield (position of equilibrium), cost and safety for each condition.
