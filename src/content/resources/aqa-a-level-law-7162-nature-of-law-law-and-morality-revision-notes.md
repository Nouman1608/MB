Condensed notes on **Nature of law: law and morality** in AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1. The specification's three strands are the distinction (with moral diversity in a pluralist society), the relationship and its importance, and the legal enforcement of moral values. For full explanation and an annotated paragraph, see the [Law and Morality study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-nature-of-law-law-and-morality/).

## Strand 1: the distinction

| | Law | Morality |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Parliament, courts | Religion, culture, conscience |
| Certainty | Precise, written or reported | Unwritten, disputed |
| Enforcement | State sanctions | Disapproval, guilt |
| Change | Immediate, deliberate | Gradual, uneven |
| Scope | Everyone in the jurisdiction | Those who accept it |

**Pluralist society:** many faiths and outlooks, no single moral code. Any law on a contested issue (abortion, assisted dying, drugs) enforces one view against others.

**Parliament's responses to moral diversity:**

- Conditional permission: Abortion Act 1967.
- Prohibition with prosecutorial discretion: assisting suicide under section 2 of the Suicide Act 1961 needs the consent of the Director of Public Prosecutions to prosecute.

## Strand 2: the relationship

**Three overlaps:** law and morality coincide (murder, theft); law with no moral content (driving on the left); morality without law (lying, breaking a non-contractual promise, failing to rescue a stranger).

**Theories:**

- **Natural law (Aquinas):** an unjust law is not true law; morality is part of the test of validity.
- **Positivism (Austin, Hart):** law is valid if made by the recognised process; whether it is moral is a separate question.
- **Fuller, *The Morality of Law* (1964):** law's "inner morality": rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, possible to obey, stable, and applied as announced.
- **Hart–Fuller debate:** over post-war German informer cases. Fuller: Nazi rules lacked the qualities of law. Hart: more honest to call them law, but wicked law that should not be obeyed.

**Why it matters:**

- Legitimacy: moral law is more readily obeyed.
- Judges use moral ideas: Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932).
- Law can lead morality as well as follow it.
- Hard cases put pressure on judges and Parliament to change the law.

## Strand 3: legal enforcement of moral values

| Idea | Holder | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| Harm principle | Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) | Power over a person against their will is justified only to prevent harm to others |
| Legal moralism | Devlin (1959) | Shared morality holds society together; society may protect it. Test: "intolerance, indignation and disgust" |
| Legal paternalism | Accepted in part by Hart | Law may protect people from harming themselves |

**Cases where courts enforced morality:**

- *Shaw v DPP* (1962): conspiracy to corrupt public morals.
- *Knuller v DPP* (1972): conviction for conspiracy to corrupt public morals over contact advertisements upheld.
- *R v Gibson* (1990): earrings made from freeze-dried foetuses; outraging public decency.
- *R v Brown* (1993): consent no defence to sadomasochistic injury in private.

**Cases showing restraint or difficulty:**

- *R v Wilson* (1996): consensual branding between spouses; not liable. Compare with *Brown*.
- *Re A (Conjoined Twins)* (2000): separation authorised to save one twin; judges resolved a moral dilemma.
- *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* (2014): Supreme Court left assisted suicide primarily to Parliament.

**Parliament changing course:** Sexual Offences Act 1967; Suicide Act 1961 (suicide no longer a crime, assisting still is); Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Morality | Beliefs about right and wrong that guide conduct |
| Pluralist society | A society containing many different moral and cultural outlooks |
| Natural law | Theory that valid law must conform to a higher moral order |
| Legal positivism | Theory that law's validity depends on its source, not its morality |
| Inner morality of law | Fuller's procedural qualities a legal system must have |
| Harm principle | Mill's limit: law may restrict liberty only to prevent harm to others |
| Legal moralism | Enforcing morality for its own sake |
| Legal paternalism | Restricting liberty to protect people from themselves |

## Exam traps

- Confusing paternalism with moralism.
- Using *Brown* without *Wilson*: the comparison carries the evaluation.
- Saying Hart denied any overlap between law and morality; he denied a necessary connection.
- Forgetting pluralism when asked about the distinction.
- Reciting theories with no case or statute attached.

## Self-test

1. Give three differences between law and morality.
2. What is a pluralist society, and why does it complicate the enforcement of morality?
3. Give an example of a law with no moral content.
4. Give an example of immoral conduct that is lawful.
5. What did Aquinas say about an unjust law?
6. What is the positivist view of the relationship?
7. Name three of Fuller's requirements of the inner morality of law.
8. State Mill's harm principle.
9. What test did Devlin propose for conduct the law may punish?
10. How does *R v Wilson* differ from *R v Brown*?
11. Why is *Nicklinson* relevant to the enforcement of morality?
12. How did the Suicide Act 1961 change the law's approach to a moral issue?

**Answers:** 1. Any three: source (state versus religion or conscience), certainty, enforcement (sanctions versus disapproval), speed of change, scope. 2. A society of many moral outlooks; there is no shared code, so enforcing one view imposes it on others. 3. Driving on the left, or company registration rules. 4. Lying to a friend, breaking a non-contractual promise, or failing to rescue a stranger. 5. That it is not true law but a corruption of law. 6. Law is valid because of how it was made; its morality is a separate question. 7. Any three: general, public, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, possible to obey, stable, applied as announced. 8. Power can rightly be exercised over a person against their will only to prevent harm to others. 9. Whether the reasonable person would regard it with intolerance, indignation and disgust. 10. In *Wilson* consensual branding between spouses led to no liability, while in *Brown* consensual sadomasochistic injury was criminal; critics see the distinction as a moral judgement about the relationship. 11. The Supreme Court treated the moral question of assisted suicide as primarily for Parliament, showing judicial restraint. 12. It abolished the crime of suicide but kept assisting suicide as an offence, withdrawing moral enforcement against the individual while protecting the vulnerable.
