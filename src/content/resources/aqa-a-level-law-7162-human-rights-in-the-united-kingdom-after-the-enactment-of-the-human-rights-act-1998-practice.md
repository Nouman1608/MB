## Section A

**1.** Which section of the Human Rights Act 1998 allows a court to make a declaration of incompatibility? A: s2. B: s3. C: s4. D: s6. **[1]**

**2.** Which Article of the Convention was omitted from Schedule 1 to the Human Rights Act 1998? A: Article 5. B: Article 8. C: Article 13. D: Article 14. **[1]**

**3.** State the effect of a declaration of incompatibility under section 4(6). **[2]**

**4.** State the duty imposed by section 19 of the Human Rights Act 1998. **[2]**

**5.** Identify two consequences of the Human Rights Act 1998 for the devolved legislatures of Scotland and Northern Ireland. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 has been used by the courts, referring to two cases. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the limits on the courts' power to interpret legislation under section 3. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the Human Rights Act 1998 preserves parliamentary sovereignty while protecting Convention rights. **[5]**

**9.** Explain what is meant by saying the Human Rights Act 1998 is entrenched in the devolution settlement, referring to one case. **[4]**

**10.** *A new Act of Parliament provides that "a person convicted of any offence shall lose the right to apply for social housing for ten years". Kim, who was convicted of a minor offence, argues that this breaches Article 8 and Article 14.* Suggest what a court can do under the Human Rights Act 1998, and what would happen if the same provision appeared in an Act of the Scottish Parliament. **[6]**

**11.** Explain two criticisms that the Human Rights Act 1998 gives too much power to judges and two criticisms that it gives too little protection to rights. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether the Human Rights Act 1998 has struck an appropriate balance between the protection of rights and the sovereignty of Parliament. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: s4. **[1]**

**2.** C: Article 13. **[1]**

**3.** The declaration **does not affect the validity, continuing operation or enforcement** of the provision [1] and is **not binding on the parties** to the case [1].

**4.** The minister in charge of a Bill must, before second reading, make a **statement that the Bill is compatible** with Convention rights [1], or state that they **cannot make that statement but the government wishes to proceed** [1].

**5.** Any two of: devolved legislation incompatible with Convention rights is **outside competence and not law** [1]; devolved **ministers have no power to act** incompatibly; the devolved legislatures **cannot modify the HRA** [1].

**6.** Section 3 requires legislation to be read compatibly **so far as it is possible** [1]. In **R v A (No 2) (2001)** a restriction on sexual history evidence was read subject to an implied provision protecting the **Article 6 right to a fair trial** [1]. In **Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza (2004)** "as his or her wife or husband" in the Rent Act 1977 was read to include **same-sex partners** [1], with Lord Nicholls saying the reading must **"go with the grain"** of the legislation [1].

**7.** The interpretation must be **compatible with the underlying thrust** of the legislation ("go with the grain", Ghaidan) [1]. Courts cannot use s3 to **depart from a fundamental feature** of an Act [1], as in **Re S and Re W (2002)**, where creating a scheme for supervising care plans would be amendment, not interpretation [1].

**8.** Courts **cannot strike down** Acts of Parliament [1]. Section 3 requires compatible interpretation, but s3(2) states that **validity is unaffected** [1]. Where compatibility is impossible, s4 allows only a **declaration**, which under s4(6) leaves the law in force [1]. **Parliament decides** whether to change the law, by Act or **remedial order under s10** [1]. Parliament is **not a public authority** under s6, so it cannot act unlawfully by passing incompatible legislation [1].

**9.** At Westminster the HRA is an ordinary Act, but in the devolved systems it is **higher law** [1]. Under the **Scotland Act 1998 s29(2)(d)** and the **Northern Ireland Act 1998 s6(2)(c)**, devolved legislation incompatible with Convention rights is **outside competence** [1], and the devolved legislatures **cannot modify the HRA** [1]. In **Christian Institute v Lord Advocate (2016)** the "named person" scheme was held outside competence for incompatibility with Article 8 (or Salvesen v Riddell, 2013) [1].

**10.** The court must first try to read the provision **compatibly under s3** [1]; but the words "any offence" and "ten years" are clear, so a compatible reading may go **against the grain** [1]. If so, the High Court or above may make a **declaration of incompatibility under s4** [1]; the law stays in force and Kim's case is decided under it, leaving Parliament to respond [1]. If the provision were in an **Act of the Scottish Parliament**, it would be **outside competence** under s29(2)(d) of the Scotland Act 1998 if incompatible [1], so the court could hold it **not law** [1].

**11.** Too much power to judges, any two: **s3 lets judges rewrite statutes** (R v A; Ghaidan) [1]; Article 8 claims in **deportation** cases frustrated immigration control; **Strasbourg influence** through s2 undermines national sovereignty; extension of Article 2 to **soldiers abroad** (Smith v MoD, 2013) [1]. Too little protection, any two: the Act is **not entrenched** and can be repealed by simple majority [1]; **declarations do not bind**; **no economic and social rights**; **gaps in s6** for private providers [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The **dialogue model** keeps the final word with Parliament: no strike-down, s4(6) [1].
- In practice governments have **usually responded** to declarations, as after **Bellinger** and **Anderson**, so rights are protected effectively [1].
- **Section 3** is powerful: R v A and Ghaidan show courts reading words in, which critics see as **judicial legislation** [1].
- But **Re S and Re W** shows judges respect the limits of interpretation [1].
- **Section 19** makes Parliament consider rights when legislating, though it may still proceed [1].
- **Proportionality review** gives courts a stronger role over the executive, as in A v SSHD (2004) [1].
- The Act is **not entrenched** at Westminster, so protection depends on political will, though **devolution** makes repeal more complex [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that the balance is broadly appropriate, preserving sovereignty in law while creating strong political pressure to protect rights [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a declaration of incompatibility overturns an Act.
- Describing s3 without its limits.
- Forgetting the devolution contrast in questions about entrenchment.
- Giving criticisms from only one perspective.
- Not referring to specific sections and cases.
