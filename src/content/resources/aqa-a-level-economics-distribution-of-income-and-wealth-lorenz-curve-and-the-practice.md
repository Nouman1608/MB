## Questions

**1.** Define income and wealth, and state one way they differ. **[2]**

**2.** A household has a house worth 320,000 with a mortgage of 210,000, savings of 15,000, pension rights of 55,000 and a car worth 8,000. Its annual income is 36,000. Calculate its net wealth, then divide by annual income to give the ratio. **[3]**

**3.** Country E's household income by fifth, poorest to richest, is shown as a percentage share.

| Fifth | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before tax and benefits | 5 | 8 | 13 | 21 | 53 |
| After tax and benefits | 6 | 10 | 16 | 23 | 45 |

The Gini coefficient is 0.44 before and 0.36 after.

**(a)** Calculate the share of the poorest 60% of households before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times larger the richest fifth's share is than the poorest fifth's, before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Interpret the change in the Gini coefficient. **[2]**

**4.** Country A's income Gini coefficient falls from 0.52 to 0.47.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this does not prove that poor households are better off. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between equality and equity in the distribution of income, using an example. **[4]**

**6.** A household inherits 150,000 and invests it at 4% a year for 15 years.

**(a)** Calculate its value after 15 years. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why inheritance and compounding help to explain why wealth is distributed more unequally than income. **[3]**

**7.** The government moves 2,000 from high-income households with a marginal propensity to consume of 0.25 to low-income households with 0.85.

**(a)** Calculate the net change in consumption. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one possible cost of making the distribution more equal. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a monopsony employer can add to income inequality. **[4]**

**9.** Five households value a vaccination at 90, 80, 66, 55 and 30. It costs 50 to provide, and each vaccination gives an external benefit of 15. The fourth household can afford only 40.

**(a)** State how many vaccinations are bought with and without the budget constraint. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the welfare loss caused by the constraint. **[2]**
**(c)** State what this shows about inequality and market failure. **[1]**

**10.** Two politicians study the same Lorenz curve. One wants a much more equal distribution; the other wants little change. Explain how value judgements can account for this. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a government should aim for a substantially more equal distribution of income. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Income is money coming in during a period, a flow [1]. Wealth is what a household owns at one moment, less what it owes, a stock [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the flow-versus-stock contrast is the clearest difference, so state it rather than saying only that wealth is "bigger".

**2.** Assets = 320,000 + 15,000 + 55,000 + 8,000 = 398,000 [1]. Net wealth = 398,000 - 210,000 = **188,000** [1]. 188,000/36,000 = **5.2** times income [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* subtract debt and show the assets total, so your working is visible if a figure slips.

**3. (a)** Before: 5 + 8 + 13 = **26%** [1]. After: 6 + 10 + 16 = **32%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 53/5 = **10.6** times [1]. After: 45/6 = **7.5** times [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A fall in the Gini coefficient means a more equal distribution [1]. The Lorenz curve has moved towards the line of equality, so taxes and benefits reduced inequality [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* use the figures and name the direction, since "it changed" says little. Do not calculate the Gini, as the specification asks you to interpret it.

**4. (a)** Fall = 0.52 - 0.47 = 0.05 [1]. 0.05/0.52 = **9.6%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The Gini measures relative shares, not absolute income [1]. Incomes at the bottom could have fallen while the top fell further, or the whole economy could have shrunk [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* separate relative from absolute measures.

**5.** Equality means everyone receives the same share [1]. Equity means a distribution judged fair and just [1]. Equity is a value judgement, so people disagree about it [1]. Example: paying more to a household with young children is unequal but may be seen as equitable, while equal shares ignore need [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the example earns more credit when it shows the two ideas pulling apart.

**6. (a)** 150,000 x 1.04^15 [1] = **270,142** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Inheritance gives some households a start without saving from income [1]. Returns compound, so a larger starting sum grows by more in absolute terms [1]. Earnings are received and largely spent each period, whereas assets are carried forward, so wealth gaps can persist across generations [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* connect each factor to why it makes wealth, not just income, more unequal.

**7. (a)** High-income fall: 0.25 x 2,000 = 500 [1]. Low-income rise: 0.85 x 2,000 = 1,700 [1]. Net = 1,700 - 500 = **+1,200** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Higher marginal tax rates may weaken incentives to work, save or invest [1], so output and future income may be lower than otherwise [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* give the cost with a chain of reasoning, and say whether it is likely to be large.

**8.** A monopsony is the sole or dominant buyer of labour [1]. It can pay below the competitive wage because workers have few alternative employers [1]. Profit rises and goes to owners [1], while workers on low pay get a smaller share, so the gap widens [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* explain why the wage is held down, then show who gains and loses.

**9. (a)** Without: values of at least 50 buy, so four buy [1]. With: the fourth cannot pay, so three buy [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The fourth vaccination has a social benefit of 55 + 15 = 70 [1]. Loss = 70 - 50 = **20** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Unequal incomes reduce ability to pay, so inequality can cause under-consumption of a merit good [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* name the link between ability to pay and the loss.

**10.** The data are the same, so the difference lies in values [1]. The first politician weights equality or need heavily [1] and so supports higher taxes on top incomes and more spending on benefits [1]. The second weights reward for effort and incentives [1] and so regards the current inequality as fair, and prefers low tax rates [1]. Measurement can show the size of inequality but not which distribution is equitable, so the positive evidence does not decide the dispute [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* keep positive statements (what the data show) apart from normative ones (what should be done).

**11.** Benefits of more equality:
- Poorer households spend more of extra income, so consumption rises [1].
- Less waste of talent, as more people can fund education and training [1].
- Lower inequality helps cohesion and may reduce market failure from under-consumption of merit goods [1].
- Excessive inequality may harm growth [1].

Costs and counterpoints:
- Higher marginal tax rates can weaken incentives to work, save and invest [1].
- High earners may leave or avoid tax, which reduces revenue [1].
- Some inequality rewards effort and risk-taking, supporting enterprise [1].
- High savers fund investment [1].

Evaluation:
- Depends on how equality is achieved: education may widen opportunity at less cost to incentives than high tax rates [1].
- Depends on the starting level: reducing excessive inequality has larger gains than cutting a modest gap [1].
- Depends on time period, because training pays back slowly [1].
- Judgement with a reason: aim for a more equal distribution where inequality is excessive, using tools that protect incentives, accepting that the aim rests on value judgements [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* credit goes to answers that reach a supported conclusion, rather than listing pros and cons, and that state the value judgement involved.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating wealth as a flow, or using the two terms as if identical.
- Reading a higher Gini as more equality.
- Trying to calculate the Gini when asked to interpret.
- Treating equality and equity as the same.
- Giving the Lorenz axes without saying households are ranked poorest first.
- Claiming a lower Gini proves the poor are better off in absolute terms.
- Forgetting to subtract debt when finding net wealth.
- Describing costs and benefits of redistribution with no judgement.
