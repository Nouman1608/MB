## What 5.4 covers

The syllabus lists:

1. direct and indirect costs
2. direct material, direct labour, prime cost and factory overheads
3. how to make adjustments for work in progress
4. how to calculate factory cost of production
5. how to prepare manufacturing accounts, statements of profit or loss and
   statements of financial position
6. how to make adjustments to financial statements as detailed in 5.1 (sole traders)

## Direct or indirect?

A **direct cost** can be traced to each unit made: the wood in a table, the wages of
the worker who assembles it. An **indirect cost** is incurred for the factory as a
whole and cannot be traced to one unit: the supervisor's wages, factory rent,
machine depreciation.

A test that works in most questions: *if the business made one more unit, would
this cost rise by an identifiable amount for that unit?* If yes, it is direct.

| Cost | Classification | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials used | Direct material | Prime cost |
| Carriage on raw materials | Part of direct material | Prime cost |
| Wages of machine operators | Direct labour | Prime cost |
| Factory supervisor's wages | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Factory cleaners' wages | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Depreciation of factory machinery | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Factory power, rent, insurance | Indirect | Factory overheads |
| Office salaries | Not a factory cost | Statement of profit or loss |
| Carriage on sales (carriage outwards) | Not a factory cost | Statement of profit or loss |
| Depreciation of delivery vehicles | Not a factory cost | Statement of profit or loss |

## Splitting shared costs

Many businesses pay one bill for a building that holds both the factory and the
office. The question gives a basis, for example floor area or a fraction. Only the
**factory** share goes in the manufacturing account; the office share stays in the
statement of profit or loss. Apply any accrual or prepayment **before** splitting.

## A manufacturer that also buys finished goods

Some manufacturers buy in finished goods to sell alongside their own products,
for example a furniture maker that resells imported chairs. The bought-in goods
were not made in the factory, so they never enter the manufacturing account.
Their cost is added in cost of sales, after factory cost of production and before
closing finished goods are deducted. Carriage paid on those purchases is added
with them. Putting them in the manufacturing account would overstate factory cost
of production and make the factory look less efficient than it is.

## How work in progress changes the result

Opening WIP is added because it was started last year and finished this year;
closing WIP is deducted because its costs were incurred this year but the goods
are not finished. If Thornbury's closing WIP had been valued at 7,050 instead of
6,050:

- factory cost of production would fall by 1,000 to 151,200
- cost of sales would fall by 1,000
- gross profit and profit for the year would rise by 1,000
- current assets would rise by 1,000

An error in WIP therefore moves profit exactly as an inventory error does.

## Common mistakes

- Using purchases of raw materials instead of raw materials consumed.
- Putting carriage inwards on raw materials in the statement of profit or loss.
- Including the supervisor's wages in direct labour.
- Putting the whole of a shared cost in the factory.
- Splitting a cost before adjusting it for an accrual or prepayment.
- Adjusting finished goods inventory in the manufacturing account; it belongs in
  cost of sales.
- Listing only one inventory figure in the statement of financial position.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** the manufacturing account with labelled subtotals: cost of raw
  materials consumed, prime cost, factory overheads, factory cost of production.
- **Calculate** prime cost or factory cost of production with the formula written
  first, then the figures.
- **Explain** a classification by referring to whether the cost can be traced to
  a unit of output.
