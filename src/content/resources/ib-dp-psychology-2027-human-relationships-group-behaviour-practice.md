> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the group behaviour strand of the human relationships context (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. Section A questions follow the short-answer pattern, Section B questions apply knowledge to an unseen scenario, and Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response. Other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [group behaviour study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by conformity, and give one example. **[4]**

**2.** Describe two of the acculturation strategies in Berry's model. **[4]**

**3.** Explain one compliance technique, with an example. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how one cultural dimension helps to understand a difference in group behaviour between two cultures. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Mina joined a large secondary school after moving from another country. She speaks her first language at home but wants to make friends at school. In her first term she copies how other students dress and speak. In class discussions she nods along with the majority even when she privately disagrees. By the second term she has joined the school choir and still celebrates family festivals at home.

**(a)** Apply an acculturation strategy to Mina's situation. **[3]**
**(b)** Apply social learning theory or conformity to explain one behaviour in the scenario. **[3]**

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> A school has two houses, Red and Blue, that compete for a trophy each year. This year pupils in each house started posting negative comments about the other house. The head of year decides to run one joint project where mixed teams of Red and Blue pupils must organise a charity concert together.

**(a)** Explain why the head of year's plan could be supported by social identity theory. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest one reason the plan might not reduce the hostility. **[3]**

**7.** A charity wants more young people to volunteer at a food bank. Outline how the foot-in-the-door technique could be used, and state one ethical consideration. **[4]**

**8.** Evaluate one study or theory that explains conformity. **[6]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate the contribution of research on group behaviour to our understanding of how behaviour can be changed. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Conformity is a change in behaviour or belief to match a group **[1]**, because of real or imagined group pressure **[1]**. Example, any relevant one, such as a student laughing at a joke they do not find funny because everyone else is laughing **[1]**, with an explanation, for example that this is normative influence to avoid being rejected **[1]**.

**2.** Two strategies, each described with its two questions (keep own culture? contact with the new culture?). Integration: keeping the culture of origin while taking part in the new one **[1]**; example or effect, such as lower acculturative stress **[1]**. Assimilation: giving up the original culture and adopting the new one **[1]**; example or effect, such as loss of family ties **[1]**. Equally creditable: separation (keeping own culture, avoiding the new one) and marginalisation (losing both). Award one mark for the description and one for the example or consequence of each, up to **[4]**.

**3.** Any one technique, for example foot-in-the-door: a small request is made first and agreed to **[1]**, then a larger request is made **[1]**. People who agree to the first request see themselves as helpful, so they are more likely to agree again (self-perception) **[1]**. Example, such as signing a pledge before being asked to join a weekly litter patrol **[1]**.

**4.** Any one dimension, for example individualism and collectivism **[1]**. Collectivist cultures value group goals above personal goals, individualist cultures the reverse **[1]**. Link to a group behaviour, for example more conformity or stronger in-group loyalty in collectivist cultures **[1]**, and a developed point, such as meta-analytic evidence that Asch-type conformity is higher in collectivist samples, or a caution that averages do not describe every individual **[1]**.

**5.** (a) Mina is using integration **[1]**: she keeps her home culture (first language, festivals) **[1]** and takes part in the new culture (choir, school friends) **[1]**. Credit a justified alternative, such as assimilation in the early weeks if the answer explains that she was copying others and not mentioning her own culture.

(b) Either explanation can earn full marks. Social learning: Mina pays attention to students she admires, remembers how they dress and speak and reproduces it **[1]**, motivated by the acceptance she sees models receive **[1]**, which explains the copying of dress and speech **[1]**. Conformity: she nods along with the majority to avoid rejection, which is normative influence **[1]**, with a second point that her private view differs from her public behaviour (compliance without acceptance) **[1]** and a link to the class discussion detail **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Social identity theory proposes that people favour their own group after categorising themselves **[1]**. Mixed teams change the categories, so pupils see each other as members of the same new group **[1]**, and a shared goal (the concert) gives a common identity that encourages cooperation, as in Sherif's superordinate goals **[1]**.

(b) Any one: pupils may still see themselves as Red or Blue within the team and compete over leadership **[1]**; one project may be too short to change attitudes that formed over a year **[1]**; if the project fails or some pupils do more work, the conflict could grow **[1]**. Award up to **[3]** for a developed reason linked to the scenario.

**7.** Ask for a very small first commitment, such as signing up to receive a newsletter or giving one hour at a stall **[1]**. After agreement, ask for a larger commitment, such as a weekly shift **[1]**. Explanation: agreement changes self-image to "someone who helps" **[1]**. Ethical consideration, any one: the technique relies on a person not fully recognising how the first agreement influences them, so consent and respect for autonomy are issues **[1]**.

**8.** Any one study or theory, for example Asch's line judgement experiments. Description of the finding: a majority giving wrong answers led many participants to conform on some trials **[1]**, and an ally reduced it **[1]**. Evaluation, up to four marks for any combination of: strength is a controlled experiment, so cause and effect is clear; limitation is an artificial task with low stakes and low ecological validity; sample was mainly American male students, so cultural and gender bias limit generalisation; later work such as Bond and Smith found cultural variation; deception raises ethical issues, requiring debriefing. Award **[1]** per developed evaluation point, up to **[6]** in total.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for critical analysis lacking development; lower bands for description or little linking. Indicative content (the examiner credits any relevant content):
- Social identity theory: change identities and goals, for instance shared goals reduce intergroup hostility, but long-term change outside camp or school projects is less well evidenced.
- Social learning: respected role models change norms in groups; evidence is strongest in controlled studies, and the link to long-term change is weaker.
- Compliance techniques: small commitments increase later participation; the techniques work partly outside awareness, raising responsibility issues.
- Acculturation: integration is linked to better mental health, so supporting integration (language classes, welcoming schools) is a route to positive change.
- Conformity and cultural dimensions: culture shapes how much groups conform, so a strategy must fit the culture, which links to perspective and bias.
- Causality: experiments provide cause and effect, but real groups have many interacting variables; field and correlational evidence limit causal claims.
- Responsibility: the same knowledge can manipulate groups, and research on minority groups can stigmatise.
- A conclusion that weighs the strength of the evidence and the conditions under which change lasts.
Award marks by markband, not by counting points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2:** naming the strategies without describing them.
- **Question 5:** describing a theory without applying it to details in the scenario.
- **Question 8:** describing the study and giving no evaluation.
- **Question 9:** a list of studies with only one concept and no conclusion.
