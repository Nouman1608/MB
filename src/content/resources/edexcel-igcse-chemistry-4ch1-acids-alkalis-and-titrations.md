It covers points 2.28 to 2.33C. Points 2.28 to 2.32 have no C reference and can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Point 2.33C, the titration method, has a C reference and is examined on **Paper 2 only**.

## Single indicators (2.28)

| Indicator | In acid | In alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Litmus | Red | Blue |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless | Pink |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |

A single indicator tells you only whether a solution is acidic or alkaline, not how strongly. To use one, add a few drops to a sample of the solution (or dip litmus paper into it) and note the colour.

**Worked example 1.** A solution turns phenolphthalein colourless and methyl orange yellow. What can you conclude?

```
phenolphthalein colourless -> not alkaline enough to turn it pink
methyl orange yellow       -> not acidic enough to turn it red
conclusion: neither single indicator can classify it;
            measure the pH with universal indicator instead
```

Questions like this test whether you know that "colourless in phenolphthalein" does not prove a solution is acidic, and that a single indicator cannot give a pH value.

## The pH scale and universal indicator (2.29, 2.30)

The specification fixes the band names, so use its exact wording.

| pH | Classification |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Strongly acidic |
| 4–6 | Weakly acidic |
| 7 | Neutral |
| 8–10 | Weakly alkaline |
| 11–14 | Strongly alkaline |

**Using universal indicator.** Add a few drops of universal indicator solution to a small sample of the aqueous solution, or put a drop of the solution on universal indicator paper with a glass rod. Compare the colour with a pH colour chart and read off the nearest pH. The result is **approximate**, because the colours change gradually and are judged by eye. Universal indicator is a **mixture** of indicators, which is why it shows many colours, from red at low pH through green at pH 7 to purple at high pH.

**Worked example 2.** Universal indicator gives these results. Classify each solution.

| Solution | Colour | Approximate pH | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lemon juice | Orange | 3 | Strongly acidic |
| Rainwater | Yellow | 6 | Weakly acidic |
| Sea water | Blue-green | 8 | Weakly alkaline |
| Oven cleaner | Purple | 14 | Strongly alkaline |

The band boundaries matter: pH 3 is "strongly acidic", not "weakly acidic", on this specification.

## Hydrogen ions, hydroxide ions and neutralisation (2.31, 2.32)

In aqueous solution an **acid is a source of hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq)**, and an **alkali is a source of hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq)**. For example, hydrochloric acid supplies H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions; sodium hydroxide solution supplies Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions.

An alkali **neutralises** an acid because its hydroxide ions react with the hydrogen ions to form water:

```
H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)
```

The remaining ions form a salt. Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide give sodium chloride and water.

### Following pH during neutralisation

**Worked example 3.** Dilute hydrochloric acid is added a little at a time to sodium hydroxide solution containing universal indicator. Describe how the colour and pH change.

```
start: purple, pH about 13-14   (many OH- ions)
acid added: OH- ions are removed by H+ ions -> pH falls -> blue
exactly neutralised: green, pH 7 (no excess H+ or OH-)
excess acid: H+ ions now in excess -> yellow, orange, then red; pH falls to about 1
```

If the alkali is added to the acid instead, the sequence runs the other way: red to green to purple.

## Carrying out a titration (2.33C) — Paper 2 only

A titration finds the exact volume of one solution that reacts with a fixed volume of another. For an acid-alkali titration you need a pipette with a pipette filler, a burette, a conical flask, a white tile and a single indicator.

### Method with reasons

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Rinse the pipette with a little of the alkali, and the burette with a little of the acid | Any water left inside would dilute the solutions |
| Use the pipette and filler to transfer 25.0 cm³ of alkali to a conical flask | A pipette measures a fixed volume accurately |
| Add a few drops of phenolphthalein or methyl orange | A single indicator gives a sharp colour change; universal indicator changes gradually |
| Fill the burette with acid, removing the funnel and any air bubble in the tip; read the starting volume at eye level, from the bottom of the meniscus | A bubble or funnel left in would make readings wrong |
| Stand the flask on a white tile and add acid while swirling | The tile shows the colour change clearly; swirling mixes the solutions |
| Near the end point, add acid drop by drop | So the end point is not overshot |
| Stop when one drop causes a permanent colour change; read the final volume | The titre is final reading minus initial reading |
| Do a rough titration, then repeat until two or more titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm³) | Repeats show the result is reliable; the rough titre is not used in the mean |

The conical flask can be rinsed with distilled water: extra water does not change the amount of alkali pipetted into it.

**End-point colours** when acid is added from the burette to alkali in the flask: phenolphthalein turns from pink to colourless; methyl orange turns from yellow to orange.

### Reading and processing results

**Worked example 4.** Calculate each titre and the mean titre.

| | Rough | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Final reading / cm³ | 24.80 | 48.65 | 24.25 | 48.30 |
| Initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 24.80 | 0.10 | 24.25 |
| Titre / cm³ | 24.80 | 23.85 | 24.15 | 24.05 |

```
concordant titres (within 0.10 cm3): 24.15 and 24.05
23.85 is 0.20 below 24.05, so it is not used
mean titre = (24.15 + 24.05) / 2 = 24.10 cm3
```

Burette readings are recorded to 2 decimal places, ending in 0 or 5.

Concentration calculations from a titre use point 1.34C (also Paper 2 only); see the [chemical formulae, equations and calculations study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-chemical-formulae-equations-and-calculations/).

## Common mistakes

- Giving methyl orange as "orange in alkali": it is yellow in alkali and red in acid.
- Saying phenolphthalein is "clear" in acid: use "colourless"; clear means transparent.
- Calling pH 5 "strongly acidic" or pH 9 "strongly alkaline".
- Writing that alkalis contain H⁺ ions, or that neutralisation "cancels the acid out" without the ionic equation.
- Using universal indicator in a titration.
- Including the rough titre, or a non-concordant titre, in the mean.
- Rinsing the burette with water just before filling it.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the use of an indicator: say how it is added and what colour is seen in each condition.
- **Describe** a titration: give steps in order, name the apparatus with volumes, and say how the end point is recognised.
- **Explain** a step: give the reason linked to accuracy or reliability.
- **Calculate** a mean titre: show which titres are concordant before averaging.
