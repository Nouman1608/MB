## Structure and high melting points

A metal consists of a **giant structure** of atoms (more exactly, positive ions) arranged in a regular pattern.
The positive ions are held together by **strong metallic bonding**: the electrostatic attraction between the
positive ions and a "sea" of **delocalised electrons** that have come from the outer shells of the metal atoms.

To melt a metal, this attraction must be overcome in many places throughout the structure. There are many strong
bonds, so a large amount of energy is needed. Most metals therefore have **high melting points and boiling
points**.

| Metal | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium | 660 | 2519 |
| Copper | 1085 | 2562 |
| Iron | 1538 | 2862 |
| Sodium | 98 | 883 |
| Mercury | -39 | 357 |

Most metals are solid at room temperature. The Group 1 metals melt at low temperatures compared with most other
metals, and mercury is a liquid at room temperature. These are exceptions to "most metals". The general pattern is
still high melting points. Always write "most metals".

## Why pure metals can be bent and shaped

In a pure metal, all the atoms are the **same size**. They are packed together in regular **layers**. When a
force is applied, one layer can **slide over another**. The delocalised electrons are not fixed to any one ion,
and the metallic bonding remains in place after the layers have moved. So the metal changes shape without
breaking. This is why metals can be bent, hammered into sheets and drawn into wires.

```
Before:        After a force is applied:
 o o o o        o o o o
 o o o o   ->     o o o o     (the layer has slid; the structure is still intact)
 o o o o        o o o o
```

The same property means a pure metal can also be **too soft** for many uses. Pure iron, for example, bends
easily, and pure copper, gold and aluminium are also soft. For building and engineering we need harder materials.

## Alloys

An **alloy** is a **mixture of a metal with one or more other elements**, usually other metals. Alloys are made
because they have more useful properties than the pure metal. In particular, they are **harder**.

| Alloy | Made from | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Brass | Copper and zinc | Harder than pure copper |
| Bronze | Copper and tin | Harder than pure copper |
| Steel | Iron and a small amount of carbon | Harder than pure iron |
| Solder | Tin and lead (or other metals) | Low melting point |

(Steel contains carbon, which is a non-metal. Alloys do not have to be mixtures of two metals.)

## Why alloys are harder: distortion of the layers

An alloy contains atoms of **different sizes**. The extra atoms are either larger or smaller than the atoms of the
main metal. They **distort the regular layers**, so the layers can no longer slide over each other easily.

```
Pure metal:          Alloy:
 o o o o              o o o o
 o o o o              o o O o      (O is a bigger atom)
 o o o o              o o o o

Layers are regular    The bigger atom disrupts the pattern,
so they slide         so layers resist sliding
```

Because the layers resist sliding, a greater force is needed to change the shape. The alloy is **harder** and less
malleable.

### A model answer

*Explain why an alloy is harder than a pure metal.*

1. In a pure metal the atoms are all the same size and are arranged in regular layers.
2. The layers can slide over each other when a force is applied, so the metal is soft.
3. In an alloy there are atoms of different sizes.
4. These distort the layers, so the layers cannot slide so easily.
5. So the alloy is harder.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: explaining a high melting point

*Explain why iron has a high melting point.*

```
Iron has a giant metallic structure.
There is strong metallic bonding between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.
Many strong bonds must be overcome.
A large amount of energy is needed, so the melting point is high.
```

### Worked example 2: explaining malleability

*Explain why copper can be hammered into shape.*

```
In pure copper the atoms are all the same size and are arranged in regular layers.
When hammered, the layers slide over each other.
The metallic bonding still holds the structure together, so it changes shape without breaking.
```

### Worked example 3: calculating the mass of one metal in an alloy

*Brass is 70% copper and 30% zinc by mass. A brass ornament has a mass of 250 g. Calculate the mass of
copper in it.*

```
Mass of copper = 70 / 100 x 250
               = 0.70 x 250
               = 175 g
Mass of zinc = 250 - 175 = 75 g   (check: 30 / 100 x 250 = 75 g)
```

### Worked example 4: choosing a material

*A builder needs a material for a bridge. Explain why steel is used instead of pure iron.*

Pure iron is too soft. Steel is an alloy of iron with a small amount of carbon. The carbon atoms are a different
size from the iron atoms, which distorts the layers so that they cannot slide easily. This makes steel harder
and stronger.

### Worked example 5: interpreting data

*A pure metal has a hardness value of 35 and an alloy of that metal has a value of 80 on the same scale. What
does this tell you?*

The alloy is harder than the pure metal. This fits the idea that different-sized atoms in the alloy distort the
layers and make sliding more difficult.

### Worked example 6: a six-mark explanation

*Explain why pure metals are malleable but alloys are harder, and why metals have high melting points.*

Indicative content:

- Metals have giant structures of atoms with strong metallic bonding.
- Many strong bonds must be overcome to melt a metal, so melting points are high.
- In a pure metal the atoms are the same size and arranged in layers.
- The layers can slide over each other, so the metal can be bent and shaped.
- In an alloy there are atoms of different sizes, which distort the layers.
- The layers cannot slide easily, so the alloy is harder.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that alloys are harder "because they are stronger" or "because they have more bonds". The reason is
  the **distorted layers**.
- Saying that atoms in a pure metal are "different sizes". In a pure metal they are the same size.
- Leaving out "layers slide" when explaining why metals bend.
- Saying that metals are held together by ionic bonds or by shared pairs of electrons. The bonding is
  **metallic**.
- Writing that all metals have high melting points. Say "most metals".
- Forgetting that the alloy is a **mixture**, not a compound, so no new chemical bonds form between the metals.

## Exam technique

- **Explain a high melting point:** giant structure, strong metallic bonding, many bonds, large energy.
- **Explain why a pure metal bends:** same-sized atoms in layers that slide.
- **Explain why an alloy is harder:** different-sized atoms, distorted layers, harder to slide.
- **Calculate:** write the fraction or percentage first, then multiply.
