These notes condense the four specified historical concepts of IB DP History (first assessment 2028) for quick recall, for both SL and HL. For the fuller explanation see the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-concepts/) and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-concepts-practice/).

## The four concepts at a glance

| Concept | Definition (short) | The historian's question |
|---|---|---|
| Cause and consequence | The interplay of multiple reasons for, and results of, events | Why did it happen, and what resulted? |
| Continuity and change | Societies becoming different and keeping similarity across time and place | What changed and what stayed the same? |
| Perspectives | Diverse points of view from and about the past | Who has a view on these events? |
| Significance | Value judgements about people and events | Which stories should be told? |

Cause and consequence, and continuity and change, are interrelated pairs. All four must be taught and should be returned to throughout the course. They are not an isolated unit.

## Cause and consequence

**Three conceptual understandings.**

- Events have **multiple, interrelated, short- and long-term** causes and consequences.
- Causes and consequences come from the interplay of **historical actors** (people who take action) and **conditions** (the circumstances in which they act).
- Nothing was **inevitable**. Causes and consequences can be **probable, improbable, intentional or accidental**.

**Quick tools.**

- Sort factors into actors and conditions, and short-term and long-term.
- Rank causes and say why the top one matters most.
- Show links between causes (one made another possible).
- Separate intended consequences from unintended ones.

**Example line.** "The 1789 French Revolution grew from long-term financial strain and Enlightenment ideas (conditions), but the calling of the Estates-General and the decisions of the Third Estate (actors) shaped when and how it began."

## Continuity and change

**Three conceptual understandings.**

- Continuity and change happen **at the same time** and are interwoven.
- They can be **rapid and transformative** in the short term and follow **slower patterns and trends** in the long term.
- People experience them as **positive, negative or both**.

**Quick tools.**

- Always give one change and one continuity for the same period.
- Say who experienced it: ruling elite, women, peasants, minorities.
- Judge the pace: sudden, gradual, uneven.
- Use words like "transformative", "superficial" and "turning point" only with evidence.

**Example line.** "After 1868 the Meiji government quickly abolished the domains (rapid change), but the emperor's role as source of legitimacy and many rural customs continued."

## Perspectives

**Three conceptual understandings.**

- There are always **diverse** perspectives, from participants and observers at the time and from people looking back later.
- Perspectives are **not all equally valid**. Test the claims being made.
- Perspectives can **corroborate** each other or **contradict** each other, leading to ambiguity that historians try to resolve.

**Quick tools.**

- Name the perspective precisely (who, where, when, in what role).
- Say what that position allowed the person to see and what it hid.
- Compare two perspectives: similarity first, then difference, then which is better supported.
- Do not stop at "biased". Say what the source reveals.

**Example line.** "A colonial official's report and a nationalist newspaper both describe the same protest. They agree on the date and place, which corroborates the facts, but disagree about cause, so the historian must weigh each claim against other evidence."

## Significance

**Three conceptual understandings.**

- Significance is **constructed**: it comes from judgements about who and what is included in or excluded from narratives.
- Judgements reflect the **evidence** available and the **values** of the historian and their society.
- Significance can come from **power, importance and impact**, and also from what something **reveals**, even when the people involved were marginalised.

**Quick tools.**

- State criteria: scale, depth, duration, what it reveals.
- Compare cases against the same criteria.
- Ask whose story is missing and why.
- Remember that judgements change over time.

**Example line.** "The Women's Political Council in Montgomery had little formal power, but it is significant for what it reveals about how the boycott was organised."

## Command terms for concept questions

| Term | Meaning in the guide | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Analyse | Break down to bring out essential elements or structure | Separate the parts of the concept and relate each to your example |
| Explain | Give a detailed account including reasons or causes | Give reasons, not only a description |
| Examine | Consider an argument or concept so as to uncover assumptions and interrelationships | Look at how factors link and what is assumed |
| To what extent | Consider the merits or otherwise of an argument or concept | Argue a position and support it with evidence |
| Discuss | Offer a balanced review including a range of arguments | Weigh several views and reach a conclusion |

## Where the concepts are assessed

- **Paper 2, section A** (6 marks): choose one of two questions. You analyse a concept, illustrated by a relevant, specific example from your thematic study. At the top of the markband the analysis is clear and accurate and the example effectively supports it.
- **Paper 2, section B**: concepts appear, where relevant, in the wording of essay questions. Part (a) is 4 marks, part (b) is 15 marks and needs examples from at least two regions.
- **Historical investigation, criterion A** (6 marks): you must explain how your inquiry question is conceptual and how the concept informs your inquiry.

## Worked example: matching a question to a concept

Question: "Explain how a historical actor and the conditions around them together caused an event you have studied."

1. Concept: cause and consequence (actors and conditions).
2. Pick one example from your thematic study, for instance the 1917 Russian revolutions in an authoritarian rule study. Conditions: wartime defeats, food shortages, an unpopular regime. Actors: the Petrograd workers and soldiers, the Provisional Government, Lenin and the Bolsheviks.
3. Show the interplay: the conditions created a crisis, but the Bolsheviks' decisions in October shaped the outcome.
4. Add a qualification: the outcome was not inevitable, since the Provisional Government might have chosen differently on the war.
5. Finish with a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the concept and then narrating the example.
- Using a vague example with no names or dates.
- Listing causes without ranking them or showing their links.
- Treating change as total, or continuity as "nothing happened".
- Using "biased" as a full evaluation.
- Mixing up consequence (what followed) and significance (why it matters).

## Self-test

1. Name the four specified concepts.
2. Which two concepts are interrelated pairs?
3. What are the two types of factors in cause and consequence?
4. Give one reason why events and their consequences are never inevitable.
5. In continuity and change, what does "interwoven" mean?
6. Give a reason why perspectives are not all equally valid.
7. Name two ways that significance can be constructed.
8. Which paper and section assesses the concepts directly, and for how many marks?

**Answers.**

1. Cause and consequence, continuity and change, perspectives, significance.
2. Cause and consequence; continuity and change.
3. Historical actors and conditions.
4. Different choices were possible, and consequences could be accidental or improbable.
5. Both happen at once and cannot be separated cleanly.
6. Claims differ in how well they are supported by evidence.
7. By deciding what to include or exclude in a narrative, and by the evidence and values of the historian.
8. Paper 2, section A, for 6 marks.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I define the concept in my answer?** Briefly, in one sentence, then move quickly to analysis.

**Can one example serve several concepts?** Yes. Prepare each example against all four.

**Is significance only about big events?** No. The guide says marginalised people and events can be significant for what they reveal.
