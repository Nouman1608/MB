## Questions

**1.** Define inflation. **[2]**

**2.** The table shows the weights and price indices for Country C (illustrative). Calculate the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) for the current year. Show your working. **[4]**

| Category | Weight | Index |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 35 | 120 |
| Housing | 30 | 110 |
| Transport | 15 | 95 |
| Leisure | 10 | 130 |
| Other | 10 | 100 |

**3.** The CPI in Country D (illustrative) rises from 118.4 to 122.9. Calculate the rate of inflation, to 1 decimal place. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between demand-pull inflation and cost-push inflation, giving one possible cause of each. **[4]**

**5.** A teacher in Country E (illustrative) receives a nominal pay rise of 3.5% in a year when inflation is 5.0%. Calculate the approximate change in her real wage and state whether she is better or worse off. **[3]**

**6.** A bank lends 30,000 to a small firm for one year at a fixed interest of 3%. During the year the price level rises by 6% (illustrative figures).
**(a)** Calculate the total repaid. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the value of the repayment in prices at the start of the year. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain who gains and who loses from this loan, with a figure. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why deflation caused by falling demand can make the fall in demand worse. **[5]**

**8.** A bakery (illustrative) has monthly costs of 40,000. Energy is 20% of this. The price of energy rises by 15% and nothing else changes. Calculate the rise in energy cost, the new total cost and the percentage rise in total cost. **[4]**

**9.** Country F (illustrative) has demand-pull inflation. Explain one fiscal policy and one monetary policy the government could use to reduce it. **[6]**

**10.** Say whether each event is more likely to cause demand-side or supply-side deflation, and explain the effect on output in part **(c)**.
**(a)** A new technology halves the cost of producing solar panels. **[1]**
**(b)** Households lose confidence and cut spending sharply. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what happens to output in each case. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate whether raising interest rates is the best way to control cost-push inflation. **[8]**

**12.** The table shows the CPI in Country G (illustrative) over four years. The base year is year 0.

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CPI | 100.0 | 104.0 | 106.1 | 104.0 | 100.9 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of change of the CPI in years 2, 3 and 4, to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the year in which the rate of inflation fell but prices still rose, and the years of deflation. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two policies the government could use if the deflation is demand-side, and state one limit of one of them. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A continuing [1] increase in prices overall, measured by the average price level [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "prices go up" lacks the ideas of a general level and of a sustained movement. Name both.

**2.** Weight x index for each category: 35 x 120 = 4,200; 30 x 110 = 3,300; 15 x 95 = 1,425; 10 x 130 = 1,300; 10 x 100 = 1,000 [1]. Sum = 11,225 [1]. Divide by the total weight of 100 [1]. CPI = **112.25** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication and the division by the total of the weights. A bare final figure gives the marker nothing to credit if it is wrong, and averaging the indices without weights is the usual slip.

**3.** Change = 122.9 - 118.4 = 4.5 [1]. 4.5 / 118.4 [1] x 100 = **3.8%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the old CPI, not the new one. Give the rounding the question asks for.

**4.** Demand-pull inflation is a rise in the price level caused by total demand growing faster than supply [1]. One cause: a cut in income tax that raises consumer spending (or higher government spending) [1]. Cost-push inflation is a rise in the price level caused by rising costs of production passed on by firms [1]. One cause: higher energy prices or wage rises above productivity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each type needs its own cause. A cause such as "higher wages" can belong to either side, so say whether it raises demand or costs.

**5.** Approximate real wage change = 3.5 - 5.0 [1] = **-1.5%** [1]. She is worse off [1] because prices rose faster than her pay. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative answer needs its sign. The conclusion "worse off" must follow from the figure, not from a guess.

**6.** **(a)** 30,000 x 1.03 = **30,900** [1].
**(b)** 30,900 / 1.06 [1] = about **29,151** [1].
**(c)** The bank loses: it lent 30,000 and the repayment has a real value of about 29,151, a loss of about 849 [1]. The firm gains, since it repays in money that buys less [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert to real terms before judging a gain or loss. Comparing 30,900 with 30,000 hides the effect of inflation.

**7.** Falling demand makes firms cut prices [1]. Households expect lower prices later, so they delay buying [1]. Demand falls again [1], so firms cut output and jobs [1], and falling incomes reduce spending further (a downward spiral) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain of linked steps earns more than a list of consequences. Link each step to the next with "so" or "because".

**8.** Energy cost = 40,000 x 20% = 8,000; rise = 8,000 x 15% = **1,200** [1]. New total cost = 40,000 + 1,200 = **41,200** [1]. Percentage rise = 1,200 / 40,000 x 100 [1] = **3%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The percentage rise in total cost is smaller than 15% because energy is only part of the cost. Do not apply 15% to the whole 40,000.

**9.** Fiscal: raise income tax (or cut government spending) [1]. This lowers disposable income, so consumers spend less [1], so total demand falls and price pressure eases [1]. Monetary: raise the interest rate [1]. Loans cost more and saving pays more, so households and firms spend and invest less [1], so total demand falls and price pressure eases [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a policy earns little without the chain to demand and prices. Say what happens to spending, then to the price level.

**10.** **(a)** Supply-side deflation [1].
**(b)** Demand-side deflation [1].
**(c)** In (a), costs fall and supply rises, so output rises as prices fall [1]. In (b), spending falls, so firms cut output and may cut jobs as they cut prices [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The link between the type of deflation and output is the main distinction. State the direction of output for each case.

**11.** Raising interest rates makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so spending and demand fall [1], and this can ease price pressure [1]. For cost-push inflation, the problem is rising costs such as energy or wages, which lower demand does not remove [1]. Output and jobs may fall as prices still rise [1]. A stronger currency from higher rates could reduce import costs [1]. Supply-side policy such as better infrastructure or training lowers costs, but acts slowly [1]. Judgement: higher interest rates are weak against pure cost-push inflation because they treat demand, not the cost source [1]; they work better if wage growth is fuelled by strong demand, or alongside supply-side measures [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when you weigh the policy against the cause and reach a conclusion with a reason. Repeating the mechanism does not.

**12.** **(a)** Year 2: (106.1 - 104.0) / 104.0 x 100 = **2.0%** [1]. Year 3: (104.0 - 106.1) / 106.1 x 100 = **-2.0%** [1]. Year 4: (100.9 - 104.0) / 104.0 x 100 = **-3.0%** [1].
**(b)** The rate fell from 4.0% to 2.0% in year 2 while prices still rose [1]. Years 3 and 4 show deflation [1].
**(c)** Cut the interest rate to make borrowing cheaper and so raise spending [1]. Cut taxes or raise government spending to raise demand [1]. Limit: households and firms may not borrow or spend even at low rates if confidence is low (or higher government spending raises borrowing) [1]. Reasoning that links the policy to the demand-side cause [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Each year's rate uses the previous year's CPI as the base. Match the policy to the demand-side cause named in the question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "general" or "sustained" from definitions.
- Using the new CPI instead of the old CPI as the denominator.
- Averaging category indices without weights.
- Dropping the minus sign for deflation or a real wage fall.
- Treating falling inflation as deflation.
- Comparing money values for a loan without allowing for the price change.
- Listing policies with no chain to demand, costs and prices.
- Evaluating with no judgement or without matching policy to cause.
