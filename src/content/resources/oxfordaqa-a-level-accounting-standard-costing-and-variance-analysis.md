## The purpose of standard costing

A **standard cost** is the planned cost of making one unit, fixed before production starts. It is set by
deciding how much material and how much labour time one unit should need under efficient working, and
what each kilogram or hour should cost. The figures are set out on a **standard cost card**.

**Variance analysis** is the process of comparing what actually happened with those standards. Each
difference is measured in dollars, broken down into a price part and a quantity part, labelled and
then explained.

- A **favourable (F)** variance makes profit higher than the standard predicted.
- An **adverse (A)** variance makes profit lower.

The purpose is control. Standards give each manager a target. Variance reports show where results
drifted from plan, so attention goes to the large or persistent differences rather than to every line
of the accounts. This approach is called **management by exception**.

### Advantages

- **Responsibility is clear.** Each variance points to a person: buying prices to the purchasing
  manager, usage and efficiency to production supervisors, pay rates to whoever agrees wages, and sales
  price and volume to the sales team.
- **Managers save time.** Small variances can be ignored; only significant ones are investigated.
- **Better budgets and prices.** Standard unit costs feed directly into budgets and quotations.
- **Targets can motivate** when staff help to set them and see them as fair.
- **Simpler records.** Inventory can be held at standard cost.
- **Waste is exposed.** Setting standards forces a close look at how each unit is made.

### Disadvantages

- **Expensive to run.** Setting standards, collecting actual data and investigating variances all take
  staff time.
- **Standards go stale.** If supplier prices or methods change and the card is not updated, the
  variances mislead.
- **Limited fit.** Standards work for repeated, identical output. A firm doing one-off jobs, or a service
  with very varied tasks, struggles to set them.
- **Behaviour can suffer.** Over-tight standards produce a stream of adverse results that demoralise
  staff, and managers may chase their own favourable variance at the expense of another department.
- **Information arrives late.** Variances are known after the period ends.
- **Narrow focus.** Quality, delivery times and customer satisfaction are not measured.

## Calculating the six variances

### Worked example 1: Ollerby Rugs

Ollerby Rugs makes one hand-tufted rug.

| Standard cost card, per rug | Dollars |
|---|---|
| Wool: 6 kg at 9.00 per kg | 54.00 |
| Labour: 2.5 hours at 16.00 per hour | 40.00 |
| Standard variable cost | 94.00 |
| Standard selling price | 160.00 |
| Standard contribution | 66.00 |

**Budget for the quarter:** 1,200 rugs; fixed overheads 52,000.
Budgeted profit = (1,200 × 66) − 52,000 = 79,200 − 52,000 = **27,200**.

**Actual for the quarter:** 1,150 rugs made and sold at 166 each (revenue 190,900). Wool used 7,050 kg,
costing 61,335. Labour worked and paid 2,760 hours, costing 45,816. Fixed overheads 52,000, exactly
as budgeted.

Step 1: actual unit prices. Wool 61,335 ÷ 7,050 = 8.70 per kg. Labour 45,816 ÷ 2,760 = 16.60 per hour.

Step 2: flex the standard to **actual output** (1,150 rugs). Standard wool = 1,150 × 6 = 6,900 kg.
Standard hours = 1,150 × 2.5 = 2,875 hours.

**Materials variances**

```
Materials price  = (standard price − actual price) × actual quantity
                 = (9.00 − 8.70) × 7,050 = 2,115 F
Materials usage  = (standard quantity − actual quantity) × standard price
                 = (6,900 − 7,050) × 9.00 = 1,350 A
Total materials  = 1,150 × 54 − 61,335 = 62,100 − 61,335 = 765 F
```

**Labour variances**

```
Labour rate        = (standard rate − actual rate) × actual hours
                   = (16.00 − 16.60) × 2,760 = 1,656 A
Labour efficiency  = (standard hours − actual hours) × standard rate
                   = (2,875 − 2,760) × 16.00 = 1,840 F
Total labour       = 1,150 × 40 − 45,816 = 46,000 − 45,816 = 184 F
```

**Sales variances**

```
Sales price   = (actual price − standard price) × actual units sold
              = (166 − 160) × 1,150 = 6,900 F
Sales volume  = (actual units sold − budgeted units) × standard contribution
              = (1,150 − 1,200) × 66 = 3,300 A
```

The volume variance is valued at standard contribution because each rug not sold also saves 94 of
standard variable cost; only 66 of profit is lost. Since fixed overheads sit on their own line, this
makes the profit reconciliation balance. Where a question supplies a standard profit figure per
unit and asks for it, use that instead.

### Interpreting Ollerby's variances

- **Wool price 2,115 F:** wool cost 30 cents a kilogram less. Perhaps a new supplier, a bulk discount,
  or a lower grade of yarn.
- **Wool usage 1,350 A:** 150 kg more than the standard allowed. Possible causes are breakage of weaker
  yarn, careless cutting or a faulty tufting gun.
- **Labour rate 1,656 A:** 60 cents an hour above standard. Overtime premiums, a pay rise not yet built
  into the card, or more experienced tufters on a higher grade.
- **Labour efficiency 1,840 F:** 115 hours saved. Experienced staff, better tools or simpler designs.
- **Sales price 6,900 F, sales volume 3,300 A:** a 6 price rise cost 50 sales, but net sales
  variances are 3,600 F, so the rise was worth it this quarter.

A good answer gives a likely cause **and** says who is answerable, then judges whether the variance
matters.

## The interrelationship between variances

Variances link in two ways.

**1. They add up.** Price plus usage equals the total materials variance. Rate plus efficiency equals
the total labour variance. Ollerby's wool total of 765 F hides a 2,115 F price variance and a 1,350 A
usage variance. A small total can conceal two large variances pulling in opposite directions, which
is why the split is made. The arithmetic also lets you work backwards from given variances.

**2. One cause can create several variances.**

| Decision or event | Variances it tends to produce |
|---|---|
| Buying cheaper, poorer material | Materials price F; materials usage A; labour efficiency A, as poor material is slower to work |
| Buying premium material | Materials price A; usage F; efficiency F |
| Using less experienced, lower-paid staff | Labour rate F; efficiency A; materials usage A from mistakes |
| Using skilled, higher-paid staff | Labour rate A; efficiency F; usage F |
| Raising the selling price | Sales price F; sales volume A if customers buy less |
| Cutting the selling price | Sales price A; sales volume F if demand responds |
| Poor-quality output reaching customers | Sales volume A as orders are lost |

At Ollerby, cheaper wool (price F) fits the extra wool used (usage A). The higher pay rate (A) fits the
hours saved (F). So a favourable variance is not automatically good news, and blaming the production
supervisor for usage may be unfair if the buyer chose the yarn. Look at the whole pattern and at the
net effect on profit.

### Worked example 2: working backwards at Cheriton Paints

Cheriton Paints' standard is 3 litres of pigment base per tin at 4.50 per litre. It made 800 tins.
Its variances were usage 270 A and price 492 F. Find the actual litres used and actual cost.

```
Standard quantity       = 800 × 3 = 2,400 litres
Usage 270 A             → extra litres = 270 ÷ 4.50 = 60
Actual quantity         = 2,400 + 60 = 2,460 litres
Price 492 F             → saving per litre = 492 ÷ 2,460 = 0.20
Actual price            = 4.50 − 0.20 = 4.30 per litre
Actual cost             = 2,460 × 4.30 = 10,578
Check: total = 2,400 × 4.50 − 10,578 = 10,800 − 10,578 = 222 F = 492 F − 270 A
```

## Reconciliation statements

### Budgeted and actual cost

Ollerby's original budget for materials and labour was 1,200 × 94 = 112,800. Actual cost for those items
was 61,335 + 45,816 = 107,151. Most of the 5,649 gap is simply that fewer rugs were made, so first flex
the budget to the actual output, then bring in the cost variances.

| Ollerby Rugs: reconciliation of budgeted and actual cost | Dollars | Dollars |
|---|---|---|
| Budgeted cost of materials and labour (1,200 × 94) | | 112,800 |
| Less: reduction because output was 50 rugs lower (50 × 94) | | (4,700) |
| Standard cost of actual output (1,150 × 94) | | 108,100 |
| Materials price variance | (2,115) F | |
| Materials usage variance | 1,350 A | |
| Labour rate variance | 1,656 A | |
| Labour efficiency variance | (1,840) F | |
| Net cost variances | | (949) F |
| **Actual cost** | | **107,151** |

In a cost reconciliation, adverse variances are **added** (they raise cost) and favourable ones are
**subtracted**.

### Budgeted and actual profit

Actual profit = 190,900 − 61,335 − 45,816 − 52,000 = **31,749**.

| Ollerby Rugs: reconciliation of budgeted and actual profit | F | A | Dollars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budgeted profit | | | 27,200 |
| Sales volume variance | | 3,300 | |
| Sales price variance | 6,900 | | |
| Materials price variance | 2,115 | | |
| Materials usage variance | | 1,350 | |
| Labour rate variance | | 1,656 | |
| Labour efficiency variance | 1,840 | | |
| Totals | 10,855 | 6,306 | 4,549 F |
| **Actual profit** | | | **31,749** |

For profit it works the other way round: favourable variances **increase** the figure and adverse
ones **reduce** it. Always calculate actual profit independently and check that the
statement arrives at it. If fixed overheads had differed from budget, the difference would go in as one
extra line.

**Commentary:** profit beat budget by 4,549. The price rise did most of the work. The production
variances nearly cancel, but the wool usage figure needs a look: if the cheaper yarn is causing waste,
the buyer and the production supervisor should agree a grade together.

## Common errors

- Flexing to **budgeted** output instead of actual output when finding standard quantity or hours.
- Putting the actual price into the usage formula, or the actual rate into the efficiency formula;
  quantity differences are always priced at **standard**.
- Pricing lost or extra sales at the full selling price.
- Using budgeted units in the sales price variance instead of actual units sold.
- Forgetting to mark variances F or A, or labelling a saving as adverse.
- Treating a favourable variance as proof of good management without checking its knock-on effects.
- In a cost reconciliation, starting from the original budget and skipping the volume adjustment.
