These questions cover sections 3.5.9 to 3.5.11 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, version 4.4: collisions of electrons with atoms, the photoelectric effect and wave-particle duality. This is shared AS and A-level content. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, neutron mass 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Questions

**1.** (a) Convert 2.7 eV to joules. **[1]** (b) Convert 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J to electronvolts. **[1]** (c) Calculate the kinetic energy in joules gained by an electron accelerated from rest through 2500 V. **[1]**

**2.** Explain how a fluorescent tube produces visible light. **[4]**

**3.** An atom Z has energy levels of -8.5 eV, -3.6 eV and -1.9 eV.

**(a)** State its ionisation energy in eV. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the longest wavelength it can emit by transitions between these levels. **[3]**
**(c)** An electron of kinetic energy 5.5 eV collides with an atom in its ground state. State and explain what can happen. **[2]**

**4.** An X-ray tube operates at 80 kV.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum photon energy in joules. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the minimum wavelength produced. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the spectrum contains sharp peaks whose positions depend on the target metal. **[2]**

**5.** A laser emits light of wavelength 633 nm with power 1.5 mW. Calculate the number of photons emitted per second. **[3]**

**6.** The threshold wavelength of a metal surface is 520 nm.

**(a)** Calculate its work function in joules and in eV. **[2]**
**(b)** Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on the surface. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in joules. **[2]**
**(c)** State the stopping potential. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the maximum speed of the photoelectrons. **[1]**

**7.** Violet light above the threshold frequency releases photoelectrons from a clean metal plate.

**(a)** The light intensity is increased to four times its value. State and explain the effect on the photocurrent and on the maximum kinetic energy. **[2]**
**(b)** Red light of any intensity releases no electrons. Explain this using the photon model. **[2]**

**8.** A student measures the stopping potential V(s) for a metal at different frequencies f.

| f / 10¹⁴ Hz | 7.0 | 8.0 | 9.0 | 10.0 | 11.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V(s) / V | 0.24 | 0.65 | 1.06 | 1.47 | 1.88 |

A best-fit line passes close to (7.0, 0.24) and (11.0, 1.88).

**(a)** Use hf = ϕ + Ek(max) to show that the gradient of V(s) against f is h/e. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the gradient. **[2]**
**(c)** Hence find a value for h. **[1]**
**(d)** Find the threshold frequency. **[2]**
**(e)** Find the work function in eV. **[1]**

**9.** An electron is accelerated from rest through 400 V.

**(a)** Calculate its de Broglie wavelength. **[3]**
**(b)** The p.d. is raised to 1600 V. State the new wavelength and explain. **[1]**

**10.** A beam of electrons passes through a thin layer of graphite and produces rings on a screen.

**(a)** State what the rings suggest about electrons. **[1]**
**(b)** The accelerating p.d. is increased. Describe and explain the change in the rings. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why a moving tennis ball shows no such effect. **[1]**

**11.** An electron and a photon each have a wavelength of 0.20 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the energy of the photon in keV. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the kinetic energy of the electron in eV. **[3]**
**(c)** Comment on the difference. **[1]**
**(d)** A neutron has kinetic energy 0.025 eV. Calculate its de Broglie wavelength. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how ideas about the nature of light and of electrons changed over time. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 2.7 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **4.3 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1]
**(b)** 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.5 eV** [1]
**(c)** Ek = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2500 = **4.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ J** [1]
*Examiner insight:* conversions are quick marks that are easy to lose by multiplying when you should divide; check that the number is smaller in joules (a power of ten near -19) than in eV.

**2.** Electrons are accelerated by the p.d. across the tube [1]. They collide with mercury atoms and excite them (some collisions ionise, keeping electrons available) [1]. Excited atoms de-excite, emitting photons mainly in the ultraviolet [1]. The phosphor coating absorbs the ultraviolet and re-emits visible light [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that stop at "excited atoms emit light" usually miss the phosphor step, which carries its own point.

**3. (a)** **8.5 eV** [1]
**(b)** Longest wavelength is the smallest gap: 3.6 to 1.9 gives 1.7 eV = 2.72 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(2.72 × 10⁻¹⁹) [1] = **7.31 × 10⁻⁷ m (731 nm)** [1]
**(c)** The 1 to 2 gap is 4.9 eV, so the atom can be excited to the second level [1]. The 1 to 3 gap is 6.6 eV, more than 5.5 eV, so that is impossible. The electron leaves with 0.6 eV [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* in part (c), quote the gaps you compared and state the kinetic energy left over; a bare "it excites the atom" is incomplete.

**4. (a)** E = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 80 × 10³ = **1.28 × 10⁻¹⁴ J** [1]
**(b)** λ = hc/E = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/1.28 × 10⁻¹⁴ [1] = **1.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1]
**(c)** Incoming electrons eject inner-shell electrons from target atoms [1]. Outer electrons drop into the vacancies, emitting photons of fixed energy equal to the level difference, which depends on the metal [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* the minimum wavelength is linked to the full energy of one electron; show that link before substituting.

**5.** Photon energy = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/633 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.14 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Photons per second = P/E = 1.5 × 10⁻³/3.14 × 10⁻¹⁹ [1] = **4.8 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* convert nanometres to metres and milliwatts to watts at the start; a slip of a factor of a thousand is easy to make.

**6. (a)** ϕ = hc/λ₀ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/520 × 10⁻⁹ = **3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1] = 3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.39 eV** [1]
**(b)** Photon energy at 400 nm = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Ek(max) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ - 3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.15 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1]
**(c)** V(s) = 1.15 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **0.72 V** [1]
**(d)** v = √(2Ek/m) = √(2 × 1.15 × 10⁻¹⁹/9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = **5.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* to avoid rounding drift in a chain of steps, carry an extra digit and round only the final answer.

**7. (a)** The photocurrent increases (four times) because four times as many photons arrive per second, so more electrons are released per second [1]. Ek(max) is unchanged because each photon has the same energy, depending on frequency only [1].
**(b)** Each electron absorbs one photon only [1]. A red photon has hf less than the work function, so no electron can escape, however many photons arrive [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "one photon to one electron" is the key idea, and answers about energy building up over time describe the wave model instead.

**8. (a)** hf = ϕ + eV(s), so V(s) = (h/e)f - ϕ/e [1]. This has the form y = mx + c, so the gradient is h/e [1].
**(b)** Gradient = (1.88 - 0.24)/((11.0 - 7.0) × 10¹⁴) [1] = **4.10 × 10⁻¹⁵ V s** [1]
**(c)** h = gradient × e = 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **6.56 × 10⁻³⁴ J s** [1]
**(d)** At V(s) = 0: f₀ = 7.0 × 10¹⁴ - 0.24/4.10 × 10⁻¹⁵ [1] = **6.4 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]
**(e)** ϕ = hf₀/e = 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 6.42 × 10¹⁴ = **2.6 eV** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* take the gradient from points far apart on the best-fit line, not from table values that sit off the line, and show the multiplication by e.

**9. (a)** Ek = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 400 = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁷ J [1]. p = √(2mEk) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 1.08 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹ [1]. λ = h/p = **6.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1]
**(b)** Four times the p.d. gives twice the momentum, so the wavelength halves to **3.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the route from p.d. to wavelength needs an energy step and a momentum step; skipping either is the usual cause of a wrong answer.

**10. (a)** Electrons have wave properties, because diffraction is a wave effect [1].
**(b)** Higher p.d. gives greater speed and momentum [1]. So λ = h/p decreases [1]. A smaller wavelength diffracts through smaller angles, so the rings shrink towards the centre [1].
**(c)** Its wavelength is far too small compared with any gap, so no diffraction is observable [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* chain the reasoning in order: momentum, wavelength, angle. Saying the rings "change" without direction gains little.

**11. (a)** E = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/0.20 × 10⁻⁹ = 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁶ J [1] = **6.2 keV** [1]
**(b)** p = h/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴/0.20 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.3 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹ [1]. Ek = p²/2m = 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1] = **38 eV** [1]
**(c)** For the same wavelength, the photon has much more energy than the electron, because photon energy is proportional to 1/λ while electron energy is proportional to 1/λ² [1].
**(d)** Ek = 0.025 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.0 × 10⁻²¹ J, p = √(2 × 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ × 4.0 × 10⁻²¹) = 3.66 × 10⁻²⁴ [1], λ = **1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* a comment usually earns credit when it uses numbers from parts (a) and (b), not a general statement about duality.

**12.** Light was explained as a wave (interference and diffraction) [1]. The photoelectric effect needed photons, so light has a particle nature too [1]. Electrons first seemed to be particles, but de Broglie's wavelength and electron diffraction showed wave behaviour [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name the evidence that forced each change, not just the change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Converting between eV and J in the wrong direction.
- Leaving wavelengths in nm inside hc/λ.
- Writing that an electron "absorbs" a photon of any energy; excitation needs a matching gap.
- Saying more intense light gives faster electrons.
- Not turning a stopping potential in volts into energy with e.
- Forgetting to multiply the V(s) gradient by e.
- Using kinetic energy where momentum is required in λ = h/mv.
- Describing a diffraction change without linking it to momentum and wavelength.
- Omitting the phosphor in the fluorescent tube.
