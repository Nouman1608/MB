Condensed recall for **1.2 Discharge relationships within drainage basins** (hydrographs and regimes). For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Discharge | Volume of water passing a point per unit time (cumecs, cubic metres per second) |
| Storm hydrograph | Graph of discharge against time after a rainfall event |
| Rising limb | Discharge increasing as water reaches the channel |
| Peak discharge | Highest discharge in the event |
| Falling limb (recession limb) | Discharge decreasing after the peak |
| Lag time | Time between peak rainfall and peak discharge |
| Baseflow | Groundwater contribution that sustains flow between storms |
| Stormflow | The extra flow from the rainfall event |
| River regime | Pattern of discharge through the year |
| Bankfull discharge | The discharge at which the channel is full; above it, the river floods |
| Antecedent moisture | How wet the basin is before the rain begins |
| Drainage density | Total length of channels divided by basin area |

## Calculating discharge

Discharge = cross-sectional area x mean velocity.

**Worked example:** a channel is 8 m wide with a mean depth of 1.5 m, so its cross-sectional area is 8 x 1.5 = 12 m². If the mean velocity is 1.25 m/s, discharge = 12 x 1.25 = **15 cumecs**. If a storm doubles the depth and raises velocity to 2 m/s, discharge = (8 x 3) x 2 = **48 cumecs**, more than three times higher, because area and velocity both rise.

## Reading a storm hydrograph

1. Rain falls; some is intercepted or infiltrates, so the river responds after a delay.
2. Overland flow and throughflow arrive: the **rising limb**.
3. Peak flow, then the **falling limb** as slower flows (throughflow, then baseflow) dominate.

**Describing a hydrograph from data:** quote the time and value of peak rainfall and peak discharge, calculate the lag time, state the rise from baseflow to peak, and compare the steepness of the two limbs. For example: "Peak rainfall was 12 mm at 10:00; discharge peaked at 45 cumecs at 16:00, giving a lag time of 6 hours. The rising limb is steeper than the falling limb."

**Why the falling limb is gentler:** once overland flow stops, the channel is still fed by throughflow and then groundwater, which drain slowly. Discharge often stays above the old baseflow for some time, especially in basins with permeable rock.

## Flashy versus subdued

| Factor | Flashy (short lag, high peak) | Subdued (long lag, low peak) |
|---|---|---|
| Rock | Impermeable | Permeable |
| Slope | Steep | Gentle |
| Land use | Urban, bare, deforested | Woodland, grass |
| Soil | Saturated, frozen or baked | Dry, deep, porous |
| Basin shape | Circular, many tributaries | Elongated |
| Drainage density | High | Low |
| Rainfall | Intense | Light, steady |

**Explaining two factors in full:**

- **Basin shape:** in a circular basin, most points are a similar distance from the gauging station, so water from all parts arrives together and the peak is high. In an elongated basin, water from distant parts arrives later, spreading the flow over time.
- **Antecedent moisture:** rain on a basin already saturated by earlier rain cannot infiltrate, so saturation-excess overland flow is widespread and the response is fast. The same storm on dry ground gives a much smaller peak.

**Other factors:** snow stores water and releases it on melting, giving a delayed but sometimes large peak; storms that move down a basin towards its outlet can produce a higher peak than storms moving upstream; a large basin usually has a longer lag time than a small one.

## Regimes

A regime graph shows mean monthly discharge. It reflects climate (rainfall seasonality, snowmelt), rock type and vegetation. Rivers fed by snowmelt peak in spring or summer; rivers in seasonal-rainfall climates peak in the wet season.

| Regime type | Cause of peak | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Simple, snowmelt or glacial | Melting in late spring and summer | Alpine rivers such as the upper Rhône peak in summer |
| Simple, seasonal rainfall | Wet season or monsoon | The Nile's flood came from summer rain in the Ethiopian Highlands |
| Complex | Several sources, such as rain and snowmelt from different tributaries | Large rivers crossing several climate zones |

Evaporation also matters: in hot summers, high evapotranspiration lowers discharge even if rainfall is moderate, and dams and abstraction can flatten a regime.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain how geology affects the shape of a storm hydrograph."*

> Permeable rock such as chalk lets water percolate into groundwater storage (**process**). This water reaches the river slowly as baseflow (**pathway**). So the lag time is long and the peak is low (**effect on hydrograph**). On impermeable clay, more overland flow gives a short lag and high peak (**contrast**).

## Exam traps

- Lag time is **peak rainfall to peak discharge**, not the length of the storm.
- Always link a factor to its effect: "impermeable rock reduces infiltration, so more overland flow, so a shorter lag time".
- Use units from the graph in descriptions, and read the scale of both axes.
- A hydrograph shows one event; a regime shows the whole year.
- Discharge depends on both area and velocity; do not treat a deeper river as automatically faster.
- A regime shows monthly means, so it hides individual floods.

## Self-test

1. Define lag time.
2. What do the units cumecs measure?
3. Why does urbanisation steepen the rising limb?
4. Name three basin features that produce a subdued hydrograph.
5. What sustains river flow between storms?
6. What is a river regime?
7. A channel has a cross-sectional area of 20 m² and a mean velocity of 0.8 m/s. What is its discharge?
8. Why does a circular basin give a higher peak than an elongated one?
9. How do antecedent conditions affect a hydrograph?
10. Why is the falling limb usually less steep than the rising limb?
11. What causes the summer peak on a glacial regime?

**Answers:** 1. Time between peak rainfall and peak discharge. 2. Discharge in cubic metres per second. 3. Impermeable surfaces and drains send water quickly to the channel as overland flow. 4. Permeable rock, gentle slopes, woodland, elongated shape, low drainage density. 5. Baseflow from groundwater. 6. The pattern of a river's discharge through the year. 7. 16 cumecs. 8. Water from all parts arrives at the outlet at about the same time. 9. Wet ground gives more overland flow, a shorter lag and a higher peak; dry ground absorbs more. 10. Slower throughflow and baseflow keep feeding the river after overland flow stops. 11. Melting of snow and glacier ice in warm months.
