These revision notes cover exam technique for **Paper 1 – Poetry and
Prose**, Cambridge IGCSE English Literature 0475, 2026 series. Where
Marlbridge's existing Paper 1 study guide covers the paper's format
and assessment objectives, these notes focus on the practical side:
how to structure an answer, use quotation, and respond to the
paper's command words under real exam timing. Paper 1 is compulsory
for every 0475 candidate, worth 50 marks across two questions in 1
hour 30 minutes, and marked against the same four assessment
objectives (AO1–AO4) whichever set text a candidate chooses to
answer on.

Because both questions on Paper 1 -- one from Section A Poetry, one
from Section B Prose -- are marked to the same four assessment
objectives with the same 25-mark weighting, exam technique here
matters as much as text knowledge: two candidates who know their set
texts equally well can score very differently based on how they
structure and time their answers.

## Reading the question before the text

The printed passage or poem is given fresh in the exam, so the first
move should always be reading the question itself, then re-reading
the extract with that question actively in mind -- annotating for
exactly what it asks about (a character, a relationship, a
technique, a feeling) rather than annotating everything the extract
contains. Cambridge's own guidance describes two broad question
styles: a direct personal response ("What do you think about…?",
"What are your feelings about…?") and an implied one ("Explore the
ways in which…"), and recognising which style is in front of you
changes how explicitly AO4's personal response needs to appear in
the answer.

## The command word "Explore"

Explore is the one command word Cambridge formally defines for this
syllabus: "write in detail about particular aspects." In practice
this means an Explore question wants sustained, detailed engagement
with a small number of well-chosen moments in the text, not a broad
summary of the whole passage. Two or three closely analysed
quotations, each unpacked for language, structure and effect, will
consistently outscore six quotations that are each only briefly
mentioned.

## Structuring an answer around all four AOs

Rather than writing one paragraph "about the content" and a
separate one "about the language", aim to fold knowledge of the text
(AO1), understanding of meaning and context (AO2), analysis of the
writer's methods (AO3) and personal response (AO4) together within
each point. A strong paragraph typically states a point about the
text, supports it with a short embedded quotation, analyses the
specific language or structural choice in that quotation, and closes
by connecting it to the writer's likely intention or the reader's
effect -- which naturally satisfies AO4 "by implication" without
needing a separate, bolted-on opinion sentence.

## Quotation and timing

Quotation should be short, embedded and exact. Long quoted passages
eat into answer time without adding analytical value; a tightly
chosen phrase or single line, woven into your own sentence, leaves
more time for analysis. Since the text is printed on the question
paper, there is no excuse for misquoting it -- copy exactly, including
punctuation, since examiners do notice small inaccuracies when a
candidate is claiming to analyse specific word choices. With two
25-mark answers in 90 minutes, spending significantly longer on the
first, more confident answer routinely costs marks on the second; a
rough internal split of 5 minutes reading and planning and 35–38
minutes writing per answer leaves a small buffer without sacrificing
either response.

## Exam traps

- Narrating the plot of the extract instead of analysing it -- retelling what happens earns little beyond baseline AO1 marks unless it is tied to language, structure or effect.
- Treating AO4 as a single "I think…" sentence tacked onto the end of a paragraph, rather than letting personal response run through the analysis itself.
- Choosing quotations for length rather than precision -- a long quotation is harder to analyse in full and wastes time copying it out.
- Spending equal or more time on the second, weaker answer's re-reading than the first, leaving no time buffer for checking either response.
- Answering only the literal, stated part of an implied question ("Explore the ways in which…") without addressing what the exploration is actually building toward -- meaning, effect or the writer's intention.
- Misquoting the printed text, even in small ways, when analysing a specific word or phrase choice.

## Self-test

1. What does the command word "Explore" mean on this syllabus, and what does that imply about how many quotations to use?
2. Name the four assessment objectives tested on Paper 1.
3. Why should a strong paragraph combine all four AOs rather than treat them as separate sections?
4. What is the recommended rough time split for a single 25-mark answer?
5. Give two of the most common ways candidates lose marks on Paper 1.

Because AO4 is often assessed "by implication" through the quality of analysis itself, and separating the AOs into isolated sections tends to produce a plot summary followed by a bolted-on opinion rather than integrated analysis. 4.
