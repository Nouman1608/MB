## 8.1.1 Elasticity

Elasticity of demand shows how far buyers react to a change in something else. You compare two percentage changes. The syllabus names three forms.

| Form | Responds to | Formula (standard form) |
|---|---|---|
| Price elasticity of demand (PED) | Price of the product | quantity response (%) ÷ price change (%) |
| Income elasticity of demand (YED) | Consumer income | quantity response (%) ÷ income change (%) |
| Promotional elasticity of demand (PromED) | Spending on promotion | quantity response (%) ÷ promotional spending change (%) |

The syllabus does not print these formulas, so they are given here in their standard form. The percentage change is (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Keep the sign on the answer, because it carries meaning.

### Price elasticity of demand

**Worked example.** Aldergrove Oat Drinks raises the price of its one-litre carton from £2.00 to £2.20. Weekly sales fall from 50 000 to 46 000 cartons.

1. Price change = £0.20 on a base of £2.00, so the percentage rise is +10%.
2. % change in quantity = (46 000 − 50 000) ÷ 50 000 × 100 = −8%.
3. PED = −8 ÷ +10 = **−0.8**.

PED is almost always negative, because a higher price lowers quantity demanded. Some sources quote only the size. Interpret by size:

- Size above 1: **price elastic**. The percentage move in quantity is larger than the percentage move in price.
- Size below 1: **price inelastic**. The percentage move in quantity is smaller than the percentage move in price.
- Size exactly 1: unitary.

Here demand is inelastic. Check the effect on sales revenue: before = £2.00 × 50 000 = £100 000; after = £2.20 × 46 000 = £101 200. Revenue rises by £1 200, which is the usual result when demand is inelastic and the price goes up. If demand were elastic, a price rise would cut revenue and a price cut would raise it.

### Income elasticity of demand

**Worked example.** Zennick Cycles sells two lines. When average household income rises 5%, demand for its premium electric bikes rises 9% and demand for its budget bikes falls 3%.

- YED for premium bikes = +9 ÷ +5 = **+1.8**.
- YED for budget bikes = −3 ÷ +5 = **−0.6**.

A positive YED means a **normal good**. A value above 1 means demand grows faster than income, so sales of this product are very sensitive to the economy (often called a luxury). A positive value below 1 means demand rises more slowly than income. A negative YED means an **inferior good**: demand falls as income rises.

### Promotional elasticity of demand

**Worked example.** Ossian Pet Foods raises its promotional spending on a premium dog food from £40 000 to £50 000 a year. Sales rise from 8 000 to 8 800 units.

1. % change in promotional spending = 10 000 ÷ 40 000 × 100 = +25%.
2. % change in quantity = 800 ÷ 8 000 × 100 = +10%.
3. PromED = +10 ÷ +25 = **+0.4**.

A positive value means the promotion works, and a value below 1 means each extra pound spent brings a less than proportionate sales response. Whether this is worth it depends on money. The selling price is £15 and the variable cost is £9, so each unit adds £6 of contribution. The extra 800 units add £4 800 of contribution, against £10 000 of extra spending. In the short run the campaign loses money. A longer view (brand loyalty, repeat purchase) could change the judgement.

### Using elasticity in decisions

- **Pricing.** Inelastic demand supports a price rise to lift revenue. Elastic demand supports a price cut, or a non-price approach such as differentiation.
- **Product range.** A high positive YED suggests investing in premium lines during growth. A negative YED suggests budget lines hold up in a downturn, so a mixed range spreads risk.
- **Promotion.** A low PromED suggests the budget may be better spent elsewhere, or on a different medium.

### Limitations of elasticity

Elasticity gives a number, but the number is only as good as its inputs.

- It is calculated from **past data**, and markets change (new competitors, new tastes).
- It assumes **nothing else changed**. In practice weather, rival prices, advertising and fashion move at the same time, so the change in quantity may not come from the price alone.
- The value may differ for different sizes of change, so a result for a 10% rise may not hold for a 30% rise.
- A small survey or short period gives an unreliable estimate.
- **Time matters.** Customers often respond slowly, so a short-run figure can differ from a long-run one.

## 8.1.2 Product development

**Product development** is the process of turning an idea into a product ready for sale. The syllabus asks for the process, the sources of ideas and the importance of R&D. It does not fix the number of stages, so the sequence below is a typical one.

1. **Idea generation.** Collect many ideas.
2. **Screening.** Remove ideas that do not fit the firm's aims, skills, resources or likely demand.
3. **Concept and business analysis.** Estimate costs, price, demand and profit for the surviving ideas.
4. **Development.** Design and build prototypes, using R&D.
5. **Testing.** Use customer trials or test marketing in a limited area.
6. **Launch.** Introduce the product, supported by the marketing mix.

Stopping a weak idea is cheapest early.

### Sources of new ideas

- **Customers**, through surveys, reviews, complaints and social media.
- **Employees**, particularly sales staff, who hear what buyers ask for.
- **Competitors**, whose products can be studied and improved.
- **Suppliers and distributors**, who may know about new materials or trends.
- **R&D staff**, who test new technology and materials.
- **Market research**, which shows gaps and changing tastes.

**Worked example.** Muirfield Audio makes wireless speakers. Its sales team reports that buyers keep asking for a speaker that survives a fall into water. A supplier offers a new sealant. These two sources, customers (through staff) and a supplier, make the idea worth screening.

### Research and development

**Research and development (R&D)** is the work a business does to create new products and improve existing products and processes.

Why it matters:

- It can produce **new products** that open new markets or extend the product life cycle of an ageing one.
- A product that is protected, for example by a patent, can give a competitive advantage for a time.
- It can lower unit costs through better processes.

The limits are just as important. R&D is expensive, results are uncertain, and the payback may take years. A small firm may not be able to afford it. A firm that spends heavily but launches nothing has paid for no return.

## 8.1.3 Sales forecasting

A **sales forecast** is a firm's prediction of what it will sell in a coming period. Firms need it to plan production, buy materials and stock, schedule staff, set budgets and cash flow, and decide marketing spending. Too high a forecast leaves unsold stock; too low leaves empty shelves and lost customers.

### Four period centred moving average

Time series analysis breaks a series of past sales into a **trend** (the underlying direction) and **seasonal variation** (regular differences by time of year). With quarterly data you use a four period moving average. Because four is an even number, the average falls halfway between two quarters, so you centre it.

Method:

1. Find each **four-quarter moving total** (add four consecutive quarters, then drop the oldest and add the next).
2. Add each pair of consecutive totals to get an **eight-quarter total**.
3. Divide by 8. This is the **centred moving average (CMA)**, the trend for the middle quarter.
4. **Seasonal variation** = actual sales − trend.
5. Average the seasonal variations for each quarter across the years.
6. **Forecast** = projected trend + average seasonal variation for that quarter.

**Worked example.** Veradale Chocolates records sales (thousand bars):

| Year | Quarter 1 | Quarter 2 | Quarter 3 | Quarter 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 62 | 48 | 51 | 90 |
| 2 | 66 | 52 | 55 | 96 |
| 3 | 71 | 55 | 58 | 101 |

Four-quarter totals: 251, 255, 259, 263, 269, 274, 277, 280, 285.

For Year 1 Quarter 3: (251 + 255) ÷ 8 = 506 ÷ 8 = **63.25**. For Year 1 Quarter 4: (255 + 259) ÷ 8 = 64.25. Continuing gives:

| Quarter | Actual | CMA (trend) | Seasonal variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Y1 Q3 | 51 | 63.25 | −12.25 |
| Y1 Q4 | 90 | 64.25 | +25.75 |
| Y2 Q1 | 66 | 65.25 | +0.75 |
| Y2 Q2 | 52 | 66.50 | −14.50 |
| Y2 Q3 | 55 | 67.875 | −12.875 |
| Y2 Q4 | 96 | 68.875 | +27.125 |
| Y3 Q1 | 71 | 69.625 | +1.375 |
| Y3 Q2 | 55 | 70.625 | −15.625 |

Average seasonal variations (to 2 decimal places): Quarter 1 +1.06; Quarter 2 −15.06; Quarter 3 −12.56; Quarter 4 +26.44.

The trend rises from 63.25 to 70.625 over seven quarters, which is (70.625 − 63.25) ÷ 7 = about 1.05 a quarter. To forecast Year 4 Quarter 1 (three quarters after the last CMA):

- Trend = 70.625 + 3 × 1.05 = 73.775.
- Forecast = 73.775 + 1.06 = **about 74.8 thousand bars**.

For Year 4 Quarter 4: trend = 70.625 + 6 × 1.05 = 76.925; forecast = 76.925 + 26.44 = **about 103.4 thousand bars**.

The first two and last two quarters have no CMA. Show each total so a slip is easy to find. A forecast made many periods beyond the data carries more risk of error.

### Qualitative sales forecasting

Qualitative forecasting uses opinion and judgement instead of past figures. It is useful when there is no sales history, such as a new product, or when conditions have changed sharply.

- **Expert opinion** (sometimes a panel asked repeatedly until views converge).
- **Sales force opinion**, from staff who know their customers.
- **Consumer panels, surveys and test marketing**, which ask potential buyers about intentions.
- **Market research** into trends and competitors.

Strengths: it can include new information and works without history. Weaknesses: it is subjective, opinions may be biased, buyers' stated intentions often differ from what they buy, and it can be slow and costly. Many firms use both approaches.

### Impact of forecasting on decisions

A forecast shapes production plans, stock levels, hiring of seasonal workers, finance (an expected peak raises working capital needs), and marketing timing. A forecast of strong growth may justify investment; a forecast of decline may lead to cost cutting or a new product. Because forecasts can be wrong, firms add flexibility such as temporary staff.

## Common errors

- Reversing the seasonal variation: it is actual minus trend.
- Dividing a pair of four-quarter totals by 4 instead of adding them and dividing by 8.
- Quoting an elasticity number without saying whether it is elastic or inelastic and what that means for the firm.
- Ignoring the sign of YED. A negative value means an inferior good.
- Describing R&D benefits with no cost or risk, so the answer is one-sided.
