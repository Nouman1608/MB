## Item 1: sources of market failure

- Market failure: the market gives too much or too little of a good compared with the socially optimal output.
- Socially optimal output: where marginal social benefit (MSB) equals marginal social cost (MSC).
- Sources named by the specification: externalities; the free-rider problem and non-provision of public goods; imperfect market information; moral hazard; speculation and market bubbles.
- On this page you need the first two in depth. The other three are overview only.

## Item 2: key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Private benefit | Gain to the consumer or producer directly involved |
| External benefit | Gain to a third party who does not pay |
| Social benefit | Private benefit + external benefit |
| Private cost | Cost to the consumer or producer directly involved |
| External cost | Cost to a third party who is not compensated |
| Social cost | Private cost + external cost |
| Externality | A cost or benefit to third parties that is not reflected in the market price |

Formulas: MSB = MPB + MEB. MSC = MPC + MEC.

## The four types

| Type | Test | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| External cost of production | Harm arises when the good is made | Smoke from a brickworks |
| External cost of consumption | Harm arises when the good is used | Exhaust fumes from driving |
| External benefit of production | Gain arises when the good is made | A hillside orchard that supports local wildlife |
| External benefit of consumption | Gain arises when the good is used | Training that makes a worker safer for colleagues |

## Diagram recipes

**Negative production externality (output too high).**

1. Demand = MPB = MSB, downward sloping.
2. Supply = MPC, upward sloping.
3. MSC above MPC. Vertical gap = MEC.
4. Qm: MPB meets MPC. Qs: MSB meets MSC. Qs is less than Qm.
5. Welfare loss: the triangle between MSC and MSB from Qs to Qm.

**Positive consumption externality (output too low).**

1. Supply = MC = MSC, upward sloping.
2. MPB (demand) downward sloping. MSB above MPB. Vertical gap = MEB.
3. Qm: MPB meets MC. Qs: MSB meets MC. Qs is more than Qm.
4. Welfare gain from correcting: the triangle between MSB and MC from Qm to Qs.

Label each axis (quantity; price or cost and benefit), each curve, both quantities and the shaded area. A sketch that omits one of these loses clarity.

## Worked reminder: congestion (illustrative)

A busy road in Country D. Let n be the number of trips per hour. Demand: MPB = 50 - 0.1n. Average private cost per trip = 20 + 0.1n. Each extra driver slows others, so MSC = 20 + 0.2n.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Market trips | 50 - 0.1n = 20 + 0.1n, so 0.2n = 30 | n = 150 |
| Private cost at 150 | 20 + 15 | 35 |
| External cost per trip at 150 | 0.1 x 150 | 15 |
| Social optimum | 50 - 0.1n = 20 + 0.2n, so 0.3n = 30 | n = 100 |
| MSC and MSB at 100 | 20 + 20 and 50 - 10 | both 40 |
| At 150: MSC and MSB | 20 + 30 and 50 - 15 | 50 and 35 |
| Welfare loss | 0.5 x 50 x (50 - 35) | **375** |

The reasoning chain: each driver weighs only own cost, so trips rise to 150; the extra 50 trips each cost society more than they are worth; resources are misallocated; the loss is 375.

## Worked reminder: positive consumption externality (illustrative)

Country E offers first-aid courses. Per course: MPB = 80 - 4Q, MC = 20 + 2Q, and safer workplaces add MEB = 12.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Market | 80 - 4Q = 20 + 2Q, so 6Q = 60 | Q = 10, price 40 |
| MSB | 80 + 12 - 4Q | 92 - 4Q |
| Social optimum | 92 - 4Q = 20 + 2Q, so 6Q = 72 | Q = 12, MC = 44 |
| At Q = 10: MSB and MC | 92 - 40 and 20 + 20 | 52 and 40 |
| Welfare gain | 0.5 x (12 - 10) x (52 - 40) | **12** |

Chain: buyers count only their own gain, so they buy 10 courses; courses 11 and 12 would add more to society than they cost; the market underprovides; correcting this would add 12 to welfare.

## Evaluation points for externality answers

- Size: a small external cost or benefit means a small welfare area, so the case for action is weaker.
- Measurement: external costs such as damage to health or the climate are hard to value, so MEC and MEB are estimates.
- Who is affected: the same activity may help some third parties and harm others.
- Time: some external costs, such as environmental damage, appear years later.
- Responses: the market may correct some problems itself, for example if neighbours bargain, but this is harder when many people are involved.

## Contexts at a glance

| Context | Effect | Direction of market error |
|---|---|---|
| Transport | Congestion, emissions, noise (negative consumption) | Too many journeys |
| Health | Fewer infections when others are treated (positive consumption) | Too little uptake |
| Education | Wider gains in skills and civic life (positive consumption) | Too little bought |
| Environment | Pollution, habitat loss (negative production) | Too much output |
| Financial | Bank failure spreads loss of confidence (negative) | Too much risk taken |

## Item 3: public goods and the free rider

| Property | Private good | Public good |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Rival | Non-rival |
| Excludability | Excludable | Non-excludable |
| Example | A restaurant meal | A flood-warning siren |

- Non-rival: one more user does not reduce others' use.
- Non-excludable: non-payers cannot be kept out.
- Free rider: someone who benefits without paying.
- Chain: non-excludable, so people will not volunteer to pay; revenue cannot cover cost; private firms do not provide; non-provision of a good that may have positive net social benefit.

Method for classifying a good:

1. Ask: does my use reduce yours? If no, it is non-rival.
2. Ask: can non-payers be stopped? If no, it is non-excludable.
3. Both no: public good. Both yes (rival and excludable): private good. Otherwise say it is a mixed case and explain.

## Must-know distinctions

- External cost versus total cost: external cost is only the part borne by third parties.
- Welfare loss versus total external cost: the loss is the triangle, not the whole cost of the externality.
- Production versus consumption externality: who creates the effect, and when.
- Public good versus a good provided by the state: provision does not decide the category.
- Non-rival versus non-excludable: two separate tests.

## Quick self-test

1. Define an external cost.
2. A good has MPC = 5 + Q and MEC = 3 per unit. Find MSC when Q = 6.
3. At 10 units, MPB = 42 and MSB = 50. State the external benefit per unit, and the total if it is constant over 10 units.
4. A welfare loss triangle has a base of 4 units and a height of 6 currency units. Find its area.
5. Five households have maximum willingness to pay of 12, 9, 7, 6 and 4. A shared security gate costs 30. Is it worth providing? Could any one household fund it?
6. MPB = 60 - 2Q and MEB = 5. Find MSB at Q = 8.
7. State whether a quarry's dust is a production or consumption externality.
8. Name the two features of a public good.
9. Why does the market fail to provide a flood siren?
10. In which direction does Qm differ from Qs for a positive consumption externality?

### Answers

1. A cost imposed on third parties by a transaction, not paid by the buyer or seller and not reflected in the price.
2. MSC = 5 + 6 + 3 = **14**.
3. 50 - 42 = **8** per unit; 8 x 10 = **80** in total.
4. 0.5 x 4 x 6 = **12** currency units.
5. Total willingness to pay = 12 + 9 + 7 + 6 + 4 = 38, which exceeds 30, so net benefit = **8**. The highest single willingness to pay is 12, below 30, so **no single household** would fund it.
6. MSB = 60 - 16 + 5 = **49**.
7. A **production** externality (an external cost), because it arises while the good is made.
8. Non-rival and non-excludable.
9. It is non-excludable, so households have an incentive to free ride, and a firm cannot collect enough revenue to cover the cost.
10. Qm is **less than** Qs; the market produces or consumes too little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not saying who the third party is. Name them.
- Leaving out one of the labels on a diagram: curves, Qm, Qs or the shaded area.
- Placing Qs on the wrong side of Qm.
- Treating the welfare area as a rectangle or as the whole external cost.
- Using the words "negative externality" without stating whether it affects cost or benefit.
- Describing a public good as "free" or "provided by the government" instead of using rivalry and excludability.
- Describing the free-rider problem without the link to non-provision.
- Giving a list of contexts with no chain of reasoning.
- Making a one-sided evaluation that never weighs how hard external costs and benefits are to measure.
