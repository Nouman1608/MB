These notes condense section 1.2 (Types of business entities) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL: the private and public sectors; sole traders, partnerships, privately held and publicly held companies; for-profit social enterprises (private sector companies, public sector companies, cooperatives); and the NGO as a non-profit social enterprise. Worked examples are in the [1.2 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-2/), and exam-style questions in the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-2-practice/). The full feature table is in the [what is a business and types of business entities revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-what-is-a-business-and-types-of-business-entities-revision-notes/). Definitions are standard textbook forms; legal details vary between countries.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Private sector | Organizations owned by individuals or private groups |
| Public sector | Organizations owned and controlled by government |
| Unlimited liability | Owners are personally responsible for all business debts |
| Limited liability | Owners can lose only the money they invested |
| Separate legal entity | The company exists in law apart from its owners, so it owns assets and owes debts itself |
| Shareholder | An owner of part of a company |
| Dilution | A fall in existing owners' share of control when new shares are issued |
| Takeover | Gaining control of a company, usually by buying enough of its shares |
| Social enterprise | An organization that trades to meet a social or environmental aim |
| Cooperative | An organization owned and run by its members, often with one vote each |
| NGO | An organization independent of government that does not distribute profit to owners |
| Mission drift | A social enterprise moving away from its purpose, often under financial pressure |

## The four business forms in one line each

- **Sole trader**: one owner, full control, all profit, unlimited liability, limited finance.
- **Partnership**: two or more owners, shared skills and capital, shared decisions, unlimited liability in general, risk of disputes.
- **Privately held company**: limited liability, separate legal entity, continuity, shares not sold to the public, moderate disclosure.
- **Publicly held company**: limited liability, shares traded publicly, very large finance possible, dilution, takeover risk, high disclosure, short-term pressure.

## Liability: the money test

Debts minus the money raised from selling business assets = the shortfall.

- Unlimited liability: the owner must cover the shortfall personally.
- Limited liability: the owner loses the investment; creditors bear the shortfall.

*Reminder.* Debts 50,000; assets sell for 18,000. Shortfall 32,000. A sole trader owes it personally; a company shareholder who invested 10,000 loses only that 10,000.

## Social enterprises

| Group in the guide | Type | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| For-profit | Private sector company | Profit made and partly used for a social purpose |
| For-profit | Public sector company | Government-owned, commercial, public interest |
| For-profit | Cooperative | Member-owned (workers, customers or producers), democratic |
| Non-profit | NGO | Independent, donation and grant funded, surplus kept for the mission |

**Typical tensions**

- Company with a social purpose: investors' returns against the mission.
- Public sector company: political aims against efficiency.
- Cooperative: democracy against speed and access to finance.
- NGO: dependence on funding and difficulty proving impact.

## Choosing a form: criteria

1. Size of possible debts (liability).
2. Finance needed and who can provide it.
3. How much control the founders want to keep.
4. Need for privacy.
5. Need for continuity.
6. Purpose: profit or mission.

Compare two realistic forms, decide, and state the condition that would change the decision.

## Exam traps

- Limited liability described as "no risk".
- Public sector confused with publicly held company.
- Cooperatives and public sector companies called non-profit; the guide groups them as for-profit social enterprises.
- Listing all four forms when the question asks about one choice.
- A recommendation with no condition attached.
- Ignoring the founder's objectives (control, mission) when recommending a form.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish between the public sector and a publicly held company.
2. A sole trader's business closes owing 62,000. Its assets sell for 27,000. How much must the owner pay personally?
3. In question 2, how much would a shareholder who invested 15,000 in a privately held company lose instead?
4. Explain what "separate legal entity" means for a company.
5. Give one reason a publicly held company is at risk of takeover.
6. Give one drawback of a partnership compared with a privately held company.
7. Name the three for-profit social enterprises in the guide.
8. What is the usual voting rule in a cooperative, and one effect of it?
9. Give two sources of funding for an NGO.
10. Define mission drift.
11. Give one reason a government may keep a service in the public sector.
12. Two founders want to keep full control and need little finance, but face large possible debts. Which form fits, and why?

### Answers

1. The public sector is owned by government; a publicly held company is privately owned by shareholders whose shares are traded publicly.
2. 62,000 − 27,000 = **35,000**.
3. **15,000**, the amount invested.
4. The company owns its assets and owes its debts in its own name, separately from its owners, and continues if owners change.
5. Its shares can be bought by anyone on the stock exchange, so a rival can build up a controlling stake.
6. For example: unlimited liability in general, or the partnership may end if a partner leaves.
7. Private sector companies, public sector companies, cooperatives.
8. One member, one vote: power is shared equally, but decisions can be slow and outside investors cannot buy control.
9. Any two: donations, grants, fundraising events, sales of goods or services.
10. When a social enterprise drifts away from its social purpose, usually under pressure to earn revenue.
11. For example: the service is essential (water), or private owners might stop serving unprofitable areas.
12. A **privately held company**: it gives limited liability against the large debts while shares stay with the founders, keeping control.
