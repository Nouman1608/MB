**Transport** is the movement of goods (and people) from one place to another. In commerce it links producers, wholesalers, retailers and consumers, so nothing can be sold across a distance without it. Section 4.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus asks you to know the **functions** of transport in the supply chain, the **types and methods** of transport, their **advantages and disadvantages**, how to **choose** a method in a given situation, and the **features and drawbacks of containerisation**, including **transshipment**. This guide takes each statement in turn, with a worked cost comparison and an annotated answer.

## What are the three functions of transport in the supply chain?

The syllabus names three functions. Learn them as **time, place and quantity**.

| Function | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Goods arrive when they are needed, not too early or too late | Fresh flowers flown in the day before a festival |
| Place | Goods are moved from where they are made to where they are wanted | Tea picked in the hills and delivered to a city supermarket |
| Quantity | Goods are moved in the amounts the buyer needs | A truck delivers 500 cartons to a wholesaler; a van delivers 10 to a shop |

Always tie your answer to a **supply chain** example. A bare statement such as "transport moves goods" earns little credit. A better answer says that transport adds value by **placing** the goods where customers can buy them, **when** they want them.

## What types and methods of transport does the syllabus list?

| Type | Methods named in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| Road | Delivery vans, trucks, couriers |
| Rail | Freight trains |
| Air | Freight planes |
| Sea | Ferry, tanker, container ship, cargo liner |
| Pipeline | Pipes for liquids and gases |

A few notes help you avoid muddle:

- A **courier** is a company that collects and delivers parcels, usually quickly and often door to door.
- A **tanker** carries liquids such as oil or chemicals in bulk. A **container ship** carries standard metal boxes. A **cargo liner** is a ship that sails a fixed route on a timetable and carries mixed cargo. A **ferry** carries vehicles and passengers over short sea crossings.
- A **pipeline** moves oil, gas, water or other liquids and gases directly from source to destination.

## What are the advantages and disadvantages of each method?

You do not need long paragraphs, but you do need accurate points. Compare each method on cost, speed, flexibility, capacity and risk.

### Road

- **Advantages:** door to door service, so little handling; flexible routes and times; suits short and medium distances; good for small loads.
- **Disadvantages:** limited load size; slower than air over long distances; traffic jams and bad weather cause delay; fuel, driver and road costs are high per tonne; causes pollution and congestion.

### Rail

- **Advantages:** carries heavy, bulky loads cheaply over long distances; fixed timetables; fewer traffic delays; lower pollution per tonne than road.
- **Disadvantages:** only runs where there is track and stations, so goods usually need road transport at each end; not flexible; delays if a line is closed.

### Air

- **Advantages:** fastest method over long distances; suits urgent, high-value and perishable goods; secure and less chance of damage.
- **Disadvantages:** the most expensive per tonne; limited size and weight of loads; airports are often far from the buyer, so road transport is still needed; fuel use is high; weather can cancel flights.

### Sea

- **Advantages:** very large loads at low cost per tonne; good for heavy, bulky goods and for international trade; container ships carry thousands of containers.
- **Disadvantages:** slow; ports needed at each end; delays from storms and port queues; risk of damage or loss at sea; goods usually need road or rail transport inland.

### Pipeline

- **Advantages:** cheap to run once built; continuous, reliable flow; low risk of theft; little labour needed.
- **Disadvantages:** only suits liquids and gases; very costly to build; fixed route; a leak or fault can be hard to find and can harm the environment.

## How do you choose a method of transport?

The syllabus lists six factors: **nature of goods, urgency, cost, security, distance and reliability of the transporter**. In an exam, you are given a situation and must decide which factors matter most there.

| Factor | Question to ask | Pushes towards |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of goods | Are they perishable, fragile, heavy, dangerous or liquid? | Air or refrigerated road for perishables; tanker or pipeline for liquids; sea or rail for heavy goods |
| Urgency | How soon must they arrive? | Air or road when urgent; sea or rail when time is not critical |
| Cost | How much can the business spend? Is the product worth the cost? | Sea, rail or pipeline for low cost per tonne; air only when the goods are valuable enough |
| Security | Are the goods valuable or easily stolen? | Air, or sealed containers with tracking |
| Distance | Short hop or across an ocean? | Road for short distances; sea or air for overseas |
| Reliability of transporter | Does the carrier deliver on time and without damage? | A trusted courier or shipping line, even at a higher price |

The best answers **rank** the factors for the case. For example, fresh fish sent to another continent depends first on nature of goods and urgency, so cost becomes less important.

## What is containerisation?

**Containerisation** is the use of standard-size metal boxes (containers) to carry goods. The goods are packed once at the factory, sealed, and then moved unopened by lorry, train and ship until they reach the buyer.

### Features of containerisation

- Containers are **standard sizes**, so they stack on ships and fit onto trucks and trains.
- They are **sealed**, which protects goods from weather and theft.
- Special **cranes and machinery** lift containers quickly between ships, trains and lorries.
- Goods are handled as **one unit**, not piece by piece.
- Some containers are fitted for special loads, such as refrigerated containers for food.

### What is transshipment?

**Transshipment** means moving a container from one ship, train or truck to another during its journey. For example, a container travels by truck to a port, is lifted onto a large ship to a hub port, is transferred to a smaller ship for the final leg, and then goes onto a truck. The goods stay inside the container throughout; only the container moves.

### Advantages and disadvantages of containerisation

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Goods are protected from damage, weather and theft | Special cranes, ports, lorries and trains cost a great deal |
| Loading and unloading are fast, so ships spend less time in port | Empty containers must be returned, which costs money |
| Lower handling costs and less labour needed | Small businesses may not fill a whole container |
| Easy to move between road, rail and sea | Ports need space, and heavy use causes congestion |
| Lower insurance costs because of lower risk | Containers can be misused for smuggling, so checks are needed |

## Worked example: choosing between road and sea

*A furniture maker in Country X must send 24 tonnes of tables to a buyer in Country Y. Option 1 is road and ferry, at $95 per tonne, taking 3 days. Option 2 is container ship, at $60 per tonne plus a one-off port handling fee of $210, taking 14 days. The buyer needs the tables in 3 weeks.* (invented figures)

1. Road and ferry: 24 x 95 = **$2,280**.
2. Container ship: 24 x 60 = 1,440; add 210 = **$1,650**.
3. Saving from container ship: 2,280 - 1,650 = **$630**.
4. Time check: 14 days is inside the 3 weeks (21 days), so the sea option meets the deadline.

**Judgement:** the container ship is $630 cheaper and arrives in time, so it is the better choice. If the buyer needed the tables in 5 days, urgency would outweigh cost and the road option would be the only one possible.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain two factors that a florist should consider when choosing a method of transport to deliver roses to shops 600 km away.* **[4]**

> The **nature of the goods** [1] is important because roses are perishable and will wilt if they travel slowly in a warm vehicle, so the florist needs a fast, refrigerated method [1]. **Cost** [1] also matters because roses have a low value per box, so the florist cannot afford air freight for every delivery and may choose refrigerated road transport, which is cheaper and goes door to door [1].

Why it scores: each factor is named, then linked to the florist's own goods, not to transport in general.

## What are the common mistakes in 4.1?

- Giving advantages and disadvantages without saying **which method** they belong to.
- Saying "air is best because it is fast" with no reference to the goods, cost or distance in the question.
- Forgetting that rail, air and sea usually need **road transport** at each end.
- Treating transshipment as the same thing as containerisation. Transshipment is the **change of vehicle**; containerisation is the **use of standard boxes**.
- Mixing up the three functions of transport (time, place, quantity) with the six choice factors.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **identify** a method: one or two words.
- **Describe** a feature of containerisation: say what it is and one detail.
- **Explain** a factor: name it, then link it to the goods or situation.
- **Assess** or **justify** a choice: weigh at least two factors, rank them for the case and give a decision.
- For calculations, show each step and label the units.
