Section B of 4ET1 Component 2 asks for one 30-mark essay on your literary heritage text (AO1 10, AO2
10, AO4 10), chosen from two questions, in an open-book exam. Section A below drills the skills that
earn AO4; Section B gives one essay question for each set text on the Issue 3 list. Answer only the
question on your own text, in about 45 minutes, with an unmarked copy.

## Section A: Context skills

**1.** State how the 30 marks for a Section B essay are divided between the assessment objectives,
and name the objective that is not assessed in Section A. **[2]**

**2.** A student writes: "Jane Austen lived from 1775 to 1817. In *Pride and Prejudice* Mrs Bennet is
desperate for her daughters to marry." Explain why this earns little AO4 credit, then rewrite it as
one sentence of integrated context. **[3]**

**3.** For each sentence, name the kind of context it uses (writer's situation, historical setting,
social attitudes, literary context or reception). **[3]**

- (a) "Because the story first appeared in weekly instalments, Dickens ends many chapters on a
  question that pulls the reader forward."
- (b) "A modern audience may hear Shylock's demand for revenge as the result of years of public
  insult."
- (c) "In a society where a daughter was expected to accept her father's choice, Juliet's refusal of
  Paris is a serious act of defiance."

**4.** You have 45 minutes for Section B. Set out a time plan that allows for choosing a question,
planning, locating passages, writing and checking. **[2]**

**5.** You are offered two questions on your text. Give two criteria you would use to choose
between them in under two minutes. **[2]**

## Section B: Essay questions (30 marks each)

Answer the question on your own set text. You should refer to the context of the text in your answer.

**6.** *Romeo and Juliet*: Explore how Shakespeare presents honour and violence. **[30]**

**7.** *Macbeth*: Explore the ways in which Shakespeare presents the supernatural. **[30]**

**8.** *The Merchant of Venice*: Explore how Shakespeare presents Portia. **[30]**

**9.** *Pride and Prejudice*: Explore how Austen presents the pressures on women to marry. **[30]**

**10.** *Great Expectations*: Explore how Dickens presents Pip's relationship with Joe. **[30]**

**11.** *The Scarlet Letter*: Explore the ways in which Hawthorne presents Pearl. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** AO1 10, AO2 10, AO4 10 [1]; AO4 (context) is assessed only in Section B [1].

**2.** The date of Austen's life is a fact placed beside the point without explaining anything about
Mrs Bennet [1]. Rewrite, for example: "Mrs Bennet's constant matchmaking looks comic, but because
Longbourn is entailed to a male cousin, her daughters face losing their home when Mr Bennet dies, so
Austen grounds the comedy in a real financial fear" [1 for a specific context, 1 for linking it to a
specific moment or method].

**3.** (a) Literary context, the novel's original form of publication [1]; (b) reception by a modern
audience [1]; (c) social and cultural attitudes [1].

**4.** For example: about 2 minutes choosing, 5 planning, 3 locating passages in the unmarked text,
32 writing and 3 checking for context in every paragraph [1 for a complete sequence, 1 for realistic
times adding up to about 45].

**5.** Any two [1 each]: whether I can name three moments from different parts of the text; whether
I can attach at least two different contexts; whether the wording lets me analyse methods rather than
retell events; whether the focus matches material I have prepared in depth.

### Level guidance for Questions 6–11

Mark each essay out of 30 on a best-fit basis. Lower answers retell events and mention context
separately. Middle answers show sound knowledge, clearly relevant references and some explanation of
methods, with context that is relevant but sometimes general. Top answers sustain an argument across
the whole text, analyse methods precisely and **integrate** context so that it explains specific
choices. The tables give indicative content; credit any valid alternative.

### Question 6: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | The opening brawl begun by servants; the Prince's threat of death for further fighting; Tybalt's insistence on fighting Romeo; Mercutio's death and Romeo's revenge on Tybalt; the cost to both families at the end |
| AO2 (10) | Bawdy, boastful prose in the opening scene that makes violence seem casual; Mercutio's dying curse on both houses as a turning point that shifts the play from comedy to tragedy; Romeo's sense that love has made him less of a man; the Prologue's sonnet announcing the deaths in advance |
| AO4 (10) | An Elizabethan idea of masculine honour that demanded a public answer to insult; private feuds and street violence as a real concern for civic order, voiced by the Prince; a modern audience may see the honour code itself as the tragedy's cause |

### Question 7: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | The witches' prophecies; the dagger Macbeth sees before the murder; Banquo's ghost at the feast; the apparitions; Lady Macbeth's call to spirits; unnatural events after Duncan's death |
| AO2 (10) | The witches' chanting rhythm and rhyme, set apart from human speech; paradox and riddles that equivocate; the ghost visible to Macbeth but not his guests, creating dramatic tension; the apparitions' prophecies structurally leading to false security and then defeat |
| AO4 (10) | Jacobean belief that witchcraft was real and dangerous; James I's own interest in the subject; regicide as a crime against God's order, reflected in disordered nature; a modern audience may read the supernatural as an image of Macbeth's mind |

### Question 8: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Portia bound by her dead father's casket test; her sharp comments on her suitors; her disguise as a young lawyer; the mercy speech and her defeat of Shylock through the letter of the bond; the ring test of Bassanio |
| AO2 (10) | Contrast between Belmont and Venice as settings; Portia's witty prose with Nerissa versus her formal verse in court; dramatic irony while she is disguised; structure in which she resolves both the trial and the comic ending |
| AO4 (10) | Women barred from the law and expected to obey fathers and husbands, so her power depends on disguise; boy actors playing women on the Elizabethan stage, adding layers to the disguise; modern readings that question whether her mercy extends to Shylock |

### Question 9: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Mrs Bennet's anxiety; Elizabeth's refusal of Mr Collins and of Darcy's first proposal; Charlotte's acceptance of Collins; Lydia's elopement and the danger to her sisters' reputation; the marriages at the end |
| AO2 (10) | The ironic opening sentence about single men with fortunes; free indirect discourse that lets the reader share Elizabeth's judgements and errors; contrasting marriages placed side by side as structure; comedy that exposes serious pressures |
| AO4 (10) | Property passing to male heirs, as with the Longbourn entail; few respectable ways for gentry women to earn a living; reputation as a family asset in Regency society; Austen's position as a woman writer commenting on a system she knew well |

### Question 10: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Joe's kindness to the young Pip at the forge; Pip's growing shame after Satis House; Joe's awkward visit to London; Joe nursing Pip through illness and paying his debts; Pip's recognition of Joe's worth |
| AO2 (10) | First-person narration by an older Pip who judges his younger self, creating regret; Joe's dialect and simple speech set against Pip's acquired polish; the forge as a symbol of honest work; structure that returns Pip to Joe after his expectations collapse |
| AO4 (10) | Victorian ideas of the "gentleman" tied to money and manners rather than character; class barriers between labouring and educated people; Dickens's own experience of social change, used only where it explains Pip's shame; publication in weekly parts building suspense |

### Question 11: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Pearl's wild behaviour and fascination with the letter; the attempt by the town's leaders to remove her from Hester; the brook scene where she insists the letter is worn again; her kiss for Dimmesdale after his public confession; her later life abroad |
| AO2 (10) | Pearl as a living symbol of the letter, often described in imagery of nature, light and the elfin; her refusal to accept anything concealed, which pushes the plot towards truth; the scaffold scenes as a structural frame in which she plays a part each time |
| AO4 (10) | Puritan belief that sin left visible marks, so the community reads Pearl as a sign; a strict seventeenth-century society where church and law overlapped; Hawthorne writing in 1850 with more sympathy for natural feeling than his setting allows; modern readings of Pearl as an outsider child |

**Model AO4 sentence (Question 10).** "When Joe calls Pip 'sir' in London, Dickens shows how quickly
money has built a wall between them; in a society that measured a gentleman by manners and income,
Joe's discomfort is not foolishness but an honest response to a class system that Pip has accepted
too readily."
