## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | Resources used to produce goods and services |
| Land | All natural resources, including seas, minerals and forests |
| Labour | Human physical and mental effort used in production |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services |
| Enterprise | Risk-taking and organisation of the other factors |
| Physical capital | Machines, buildings, vehicles, infrastructure |
| Human capital | Skills, knowledge, experience and health of workers |
| Depreciation | The fall in value of physical capital through wear and age |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products |
| Division of labour | Splitting production into separate tasks done by different workers |
| Entrepreneur | A person who organises factors and bears the risk of production |
| Profit | The residual reward to enterprise after all other factors are paid |

## Classification checks

- **Money is not capital.** It buys capital goods; it produces nothing itself.
- **Use decides the factor.** A van delivering goods is capital; the same van used for family trips is a consumer good.
- **Land is all natural resources**, not just ground: a fishing ground and a copper deposit are both land.
- **A salaried manager** organises but may not bear risk; shareholders bear it in a large company.

## Human versus physical capital

| | Physical | Human |
|---|---|---|
| Built by | Investment in capital goods | Education, training, experience, health |
| Separable from the owner? | Yes, can be sold | No, stays with the worker |
| Loses value by | Wear and obsolescence | Outdated skills, ill health |

They are often complements: equipment needs skilled operators.

**Quick example.** A welder spends 2,000 cu on a certificate that raises pay by 500 cu a year. Over 6 years she gains 3,000 cu, or 1,000 cu more than the cost, provided the skill stays in demand.

## Rewards

| Factor | Reward | Contracted in advance? |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Rent | Usually |
| Labour | Wages | Usually |
| Capital | Interest | Usually |
| Enterprise | Profit | No: it is the residual and may be negative |

**Quick example.** Revenue 500,000 cu; wages 220,000; rent 45,000; interest 25,000; other firms' supplies 150,000. Profit = **60,000 cu**. A 20% fall in revenue (to 400,000) with costs unchanged gives a **loss of 40,000 cu**.

## Division of labour and specialisation

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Faster work through repetition | Boredom and lower motivation |
| No time lost switching tasks | Narrow skills, harder to change jobs |
| Workers matched to tasks they do best | One absence can halt the process |
| Makes specialised machinery worthwhile | Standardised output reduces variety |
| Lower cost per unit | Dependence on exchange with others |

**Productivity check.** Output per worker = total output ÷ number of workers. If 5 workers raise output from 40 to 60 units a day after dividing tasks, output per worker rises from 8 to 12, a 50% rise.

Specialisation also occurs between firms, regions and countries; each relies on exchange and money.

## The entrepreneur

- **Organises:** decides what and how to produce, combines factors efficiently.
- **Bears risk:** commits resources before revenue is known; other factors are paid regardless.
- **Contemporary role:** introduces new products and methods, creates jobs and competition, responds quickly to changing wants.
- **Debate:** in large companies, organisation (managers) and risk (shareholders) can be separated.

## Exam traps

- Money or shares described as capital.
- Profit given as the reward to capital.
- Land described only as ground.
- Advantages of specialisation listed without disadvantages.
- The entrepreneur defined without risk and organisation.
- Human capital treated as owned by the firm.

## Self-test

1. Define capital as a factor of production.
2. Is a fishing ground land or capital? Why?
3. Why is money not a factor of production?
4. Give one difference between human and physical capital.
5. State the reward to each factor.
6. Why is profit described as a residual reward?
7. Revenue is 260,000 cu; wages 120,000; rent 30,000; interest 15,000; supplies 70,000. Calculate profit.
8. Define division of labour.
9. Six workers make 48 units a day. After dividing tasks they make 66. Calculate the percentage rise in output per worker.
10. Give two disadvantages of the division of labour.
11. State the two functions of the entrepreneur named in the syllabus.
12. Why might the entrepreneur's functions be split in a large company?

### Answers

1. Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machinery.
2. Land: it is a natural resource.
3. It produces nothing itself; it is used to buy capital goods and other factors.
4. Any one of: human capital is skills in people while physical capital is equipment; human capital cannot be sold separately from the worker.
5. Land: rent; labour: wages; capital: interest; enterprise: profit.
6. It is what remains after the other factors are paid, so it is uncertain and can be negative.
7. 260,000 − 120,000 − 30,000 − 15,000 − 70,000 = **25,000 cu**.
8. Splitting a production process into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
9. Output per worker rises from 8 to 11, a rise of **37.5%**.
10. Any two of: boredom; narrow skills; interdependence; less variety.
11. Bearing risk and organising the other factors of production.
12. Salaried managers organise production while shareholders bear the risk.
