This guide covers **3.1.8 Moments and levers** (statements a to d) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The section is marked P, so it is assessed in the full Physics award. ## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | The turning effect of a force is its moment: M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance to the pivot |
| b | If an object is not turning, total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment about any pivot; calculate a force or its distance for a balanced object |
| c | If the line of action of the weight lies outside the base, there is a resultant moment and the object topples; examples include vehicles and simple balancing toys |
| d | Simple levers can be used as force multipliers |

## The moment of a force

```
M = F x d     M in N m, F in N, d in m
```

The distance d is the **perpendicular** distance from the pivot to the **line of action** of the force: the shortest distance, measured at right angles to the force. When the force acts at right angles to a bar, d is simply the distance along the bar. When the force acts at an angle, the perpendicular distance is shorter, so the moment is smaller. That is why you push a door at right angles to it, far from the hinges.

Moments have a sense of turning: **clockwise** or **anticlockwise**. Decide the sense of each moment before adding them.

## Balancing several forces

For an object that is not turning, about any pivot:

```
sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments
```

**Worked example 1: three children on a seesaw.** A seesaw is pivoted at its centre and its own weight acts at the pivot. Child A (300 N) sits 2.0 m left of the pivot. Child B (200 N) sits 0.50 m left. Child C (400 N) sits on the right. Find where C must sit to balance.

```
anticlockwise: 300 x 2.0 + 200 x 0.50 = 600 + 100 = 700 N m
clockwise: 400 x d
400 d = 700, so d = 1.75 m to the right of the pivot
```

Add the moments on each side first; never balance one child against another while ignoring the third.

## When the beam's own weight matters

A **uniform** beam has its weight acting at its midpoint. If the pivot is not at the midpoint, the beam's weight has its own moment and must be included.

**Worked example 2: an off-centre plank.** A uniform plank 4.0 m long weighs 120 N. It rests on a pivot 1.5 m from its left end. A 400 N child sits at the left end. Find the downward force F needed at the right end to keep the plank level.

```
distances from the pivot:
  child: 1.5 m to the left
  plank's weight (at 2.0 m from left end): 0.5 m to the right
  F (at the right end): 2.5 m to the right

anticlockwise: 400 x 1.5 = 600 N m
clockwise: 120 x 0.5 + F x 2.5 = 60 + 2.5 F
60 + 2.5 F = 600, so F = 216 N
```

### The force at the pivot

The plank is not accelerating, so the forces also balance: the pivot pushes up with a force equal to the total downward force.

```
upward force from pivot = 400 + 120 + 216 = 736 N
```

Questions often ask for this as a final part because it uses force balance, not moments.

## Toppling: vehicles and balancing toys

Statement c brings together moments and centre of mass. The weight of an object acts at its centre of mass. When the object tilts, the pivot is the edge of its base.

- Line of action of the weight **inside** the base: the weight's moment about the edge turns the object back. It is stable.
- Line of action **outside** the base: there is a resultant moment turning the object further over. It topples.

**Vehicles.** A tall, loaded lorry on a sloping road or a tight bend tilts; if its centre of mass is high, the line of action of its weight passes outside the wheels at a small tilt, and the lorry rolls over. Low loads and wide wheelbases keep the line inside the base.

**Balancing toys.** A balancing toy has its centre of mass **below** its point of support, often because of heavy weights on curved arms. When it tilts, its centre of mass moves to one side but stays low, so the weight's moment always turns it back towards upright and the toy rocks instead of falling.

**Worked example 3: deciding whether a cabinet tips.** A cabinet weighs 500 N and is 0.60 m wide, with its centre of mass at its middle. A horizontal force of 120 N acts on it 1.2 m above the floor. Will it start to tip about its front edge?

```
moment of the push about the front edge = 120 x 1.2 = 144 N m
moment of the weight about the same edge = 500 x 0.30 = 150 N m
```

The weight's moment (150 N m) is larger, so the cabinet does not tip yet; a push slightly bigger than 125 N (150 ÷ 1.2) at that height would start to tip it.

## Levers as force multipliers

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a pivot. In equilibrium the moment of the effort equals the moment of the load:

```
effort x distance of effort from pivot = load x distance of load from pivot
```

If the effort is applied further from the pivot than the load, the effort can be smaller than the load. The lever **multiplies** the force.

**Worked example 4: a bottle opener.** The cap acts as a load of 90 N, 1.5 cm from the pivot. The hand pushes 12 cm from the pivot.

```
effort x 12 = 90 x 1.5 = 135 N cm
effort = 135 / 12 = 11.25 N, about 11 N
```

Both distances are in centimetres, so they can be used without converting to metres: the unit cancels when you divide. Give a moment as a final answer in N m.

### What is given up

A lever does not give something for nothing. The effort end moves through a larger distance than the load end. In example 4 the hand moves 8 times as far as the cap edge (12 ÷ 1.5), while the force is 8 times smaller.

Everyday force multipliers include a crowbar, a wheelbarrow, a bottle opener, a claw hammer pulling a nail, a spanner and scissors cutting near the hinge.

## Common mistakes

- Using the distance along a bar when the force is not at right angles to it.
- Ignoring the weight of a non-uniform or off-centre beam.
- Measuring distances from the end of the beam instead of from the pivot.
- Forgetting to add all the moments on one side.
- Giving the unit as N or N/m instead of N m.
- Saying a balancing toy works because it is "light".

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate**: state the pivot, list each force with its perpendicular distance and sense, then equate total clockwise and anticlockwise moments.
- **Explain** why an object topples: line of action of the weight outside the base, resultant moment.
- **Explain** how a lever acts as a force multiplier: effort further from the pivot than the load, so a smaller effort gives the same moment.
- **Show that**: every line of working, with units.
