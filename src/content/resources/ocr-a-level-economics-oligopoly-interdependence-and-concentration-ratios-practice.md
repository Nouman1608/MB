## Questions

**1.** A market's five largest employers have 640, 410, 350, 220 and 180 workers. Smaller firms employ 200 in total.

**(a)** Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio by employment. **[2]**
**(b)** State one limitation of using a concentration ratio. **[1]**

**2.** A firm's demand has a kink at £5 and 600 units a week. Above it, P = 8 − 0.005Q. Below it, P = 11 − 0.01Q. Its marginal cost is MC = 0.4 + 0.0015Q.

**(a)** Calculate marginal revenue just above and just below 600 units, and the price elasticity of demand on each side. **[3]**
**(b)** Costs then rise by £0.50 per unit. Explain what happens to price. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why interdependence leads oligopolists to rely on non-price competition. **[4]**

**4.** Two firms choose Advertise or Not. Annual profit in £m (firm X first, firm Y second): both advertise 6, 6; X advertises and Y does not 14, 3; X does not and Y advertises 3, 14; neither advertises 10, 10.

**(a)** Identify the dominant strategy and the Nash equilibrium. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the firms may not reach the outcome with neither advertising. **[3]**

**5.** Three firms supply a market with demand P = 68 − Q (Q in thousands, P in £). Each has constant marginal cost of £14 and no fixed cost.

**(a)** If they form a cartel acting as one monopolist and share output equally, find price, total output and each firm's profit (in £ thousand). **[4]**
**(b)** One firm now produces 12 while the others keep to 9 each. Find the new price and each firm's profit. **[2]**

**6.** A firm sells 90,000 units at £12 with variable cost £7 a unit.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by non-price competition. **[2]**
**(b)** An advertising campaign costs £150,000. Calculate the extra units needed to cover the campaign. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two effects of product differentiation on an oligopolist. **[4]**

**8.** Demand is P = 76 − Q and marginal cost is £16 (no fixed cost). Calculate price and output under perfect competition and under monopoly, and the deadweight loss of monopoly. **[6]**

**9.** In long-run monopolistic competition a firm has TC = 64 + 8q + 0.25q² and demand AR = 24 − 0.75q.

**(a)** Show that the firm earns normal profit at q = 8. **[3]**
**(b)** The lowest average cost is at q = 16. Explain what this shows about efficiency. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of oligopoly. **[10]**

**11.** Evaluate, using examples, the extent to which the theory of the firm explains the behaviour and performance of firms. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Total workers = 640 + 410 + 350 + 220 + 180 + 200 = 2,000 [1]. CR3 = (640 + 410 + 350) ÷ 2,000 = **70%** [1]
**(b)** Any one: depends on how the market is defined; ignores foreign competition; hides the split among leaders; says nothing about contestability [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that give the ratio with its n and the unit (a percentage) read as complete, and a limitation should be specific to concentration ratios rather than a generic comment about statistics.

**2. (a)** Upper MR = 8 − 0.01 × 600 = £2; lower MR = 11 − 0.02 × 600 = −£1 [1]. Elasticity above = 5 ÷ (0.005 × 600) = 1.67 [1]; below = 5 ÷ (0.01 × 600) = 0.83 [1]
**(b)** MC at 600 rises from 1.3 to 1.8 [1]. Both lie in the gap between −1 and 2, so MC still cuts the broken MR at 600 and price stays at **£5** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the two MC values against the gap earns more credit than the statement "price is rigid", because the explanation is the comparison.

**3.** Each firm's outcome depends on rivals' reactions [1]. A price cut is likely to be matched, so revenue falls for all [1]. A price rise may not be followed, so the firm loses customers [1]. Branding, quality or service is harder to copy, so it can win loyalty without a price war [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain from interdependence to the reaction of rivals to the choice of tool is stronger than a list of non-price methods.

**4. (a)** If Y advertises, X gets 6 against 3 by not advertising; if Y does not, X gets 14 against 10 [1]. So advertising is dominant for X, and by symmetry for Y [1]. The Nash equilibrium is both advertising, 6, 6 [1]
**(b)** Neither advertising gives 10, 10, better for both than 6, 6 [1]. But each can gain by advertising (14 against 10) [1], and each fears the other will do so, so both end up advertising (a prisoner's dilemma) [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the matrix in the explanation. A statement that the firms "compete" without the 14 against 10 comparison does not show the reasoning.

**5. (a)** MR = 68 − 2Q = 14, so Q = 27 [1]. P = 68 − 27 = £41 [1]. Profit = (41 − 14) × 27 = 729 [1]. Each earns 729 ÷ 3 = **243** [1]
**(b)** Output = 12 + 9 + 9 = 30, so P = **£38** [1]. The cheat earns (38 − 14) × 12 = 288; each other firm (38 − 14) × 9 = 216 [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Finishing with a comparison (288 against 243, but 216 for the others) shows why cartels are unstable and is what the numbers are for.

**6. (a)** Competition on factors other than price [1], such as branding, quality or service [1]
**(b)** Contribution per unit = 12 − 7 = £5 [1]. Extra units = 150,000 ÷ 5 [1] = **30,000** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing the fixed campaign cost by the unit contribution, not by the price, is the step that distinguishes a correct break-even.

**7.** Demand becomes less price elastic [1], because buyers see fewer close substitutes, which gives some pricing power [1]. It builds brand loyalty, which acts as a barrier to entry [1], so supernormal profit is easier to keep [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each effect needs its reason. "It makes demand inelastic" alone describes the result, not why.

**8.** Perfect competition: P = MC, so 76 − Q = 16 [1], Q = 60, P = **£16** [1]. Monopoly: MR = 76 − 2Q = 16, so Q = **30** [1] and P = 76 − 30 = **£46** [1]. Deadweight loss = ½ × (46 − 16) × (60 − 30) [1] = **450** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The triangle base is the lost output (60 − 30) and its height is the price gap over MC. Mixing the two up is a common slip.

**9. (a)** AC = 64 ÷ 8 + 8 + 0.25 × 8 = 8 + 8 + 2 = £18 [1]. AR = 24 − 0.75 × 8 = £18 [1]. Price equals average cost, so profit is normal [1]
**(b)** Lowest AC = 64 ÷ 16 + 8 + 4 = £16 [1]. The firm produces 8, below 16, so it is not productively efficient and has excess capacity [1]. Also P (18) > MC (8 + 0.5 × 8 = 12), so it is not allocatively efficient [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the position of long-run equilibrium to the lowest point of average cost, with figures, earns more than naming the type of efficiency.

**10.** Advantages: economies of scale can lower costs [1]; supernormal profit may fund research and innovation [1]; non-price competition may raise quality and choice [1]; price stability helps planning [1]. Disadvantages: price above MC, so allocative inefficiency (diagram: price at the kink above the MC curve) [1]; collusion can raise price and cut output [1]; barriers to entry protect profit and advertising adds cost [1]. Evaluation: the balance depends on the strength of rivalry and the threat of entry [1], on whether profits are really reinvested [1], and the time period; a justified overall judgement [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Advice that tends to lift an evaluation answer: weigh the two lists against each other with a stated condition and finish with a judgement, rather than listing both sides.

**11.** Supports: the theory gives benchmarks such as P = MC [1]; predicts that high profit attracts entry [1]; kinked demand and game theory explain price rigidity and price wars [1]; a small bakery in a town fits monopolistic competition [1]. Challenges: firms often aim for growth, sales revenue or satisficing rather than maximum profit [1]; the principal-agent problem means managers may follow other aims [1]; firms rarely know MC and MR exactly [1]; real markets sit between the four types [1]; a threat of entry can discipline a seemingly dominant firm [1]. Evaluation: the theory is a benchmark rather than a description [1]; it works best where firms are profit-driven and the market stable, and less well with complex objectives [1]; a clear conclusion supported by an example [1] **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that names a specific type of firm and one assumption it breaks is more convincing than general doubt about "realism".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a concentration ratio without n or without the unit.
- Mixing up which side of the kink is elastic.
- Calling an outcome a Nash equilibrium without checking each player's best reply.
- Forgetting to divide the cartel profit between members.
- Dividing campaign cost by price rather than contribution per unit.
- Using the wrong base for a deadweight loss triangle.
- Drawing a diagram with unlabelled axes, curves or equilibrium.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages without weighing them.
