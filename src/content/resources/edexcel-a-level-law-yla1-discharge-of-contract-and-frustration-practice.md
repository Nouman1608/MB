## Section A

**1.** State the general rule on discharge by performance, using **one** decided case. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the doctrine of substantial performance, using **two** decided cases. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between bilateral and unilateral discharge by agreement. **[3]**

**4.** Identify **three** situations in which a contract will **not** be frustrated even though performance has become harder. **[3]**

**5.** State what section 1(3) of the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 provides. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Chen agrees to tile Mira's bathroom for £2,400, payable on completion. He tiles the whole room but several tiles are cracked; replacing them would cost £150. Mira refuses to pay anything.

Advise Chen. **[4]**

**7.** In March, Hollis Ltd agrees to supply 500 chairs to a hotel on 1 September. In May, Hollis writes to say it will not deliver.

Explain the options open to the hotel, using decided cases. **[6]**

**8.** Omar charters a ship for two years. During the first year the ship is out of service for five weeks because the crew are inexperienced. Omar wants to end the charter.

Explain whether Omar may treat the contract as ended. **[4]**

**9.** Ayesha, a famous violinist, contracts to perform at a festival. A month before, she breaks her wrist and cannot play for six months. The organisers had paid her a £5,000 advance; she had spent £600 on travel arrangements.

**(a)** Explain whether the contract is frustrated. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the 1943 Act deals with the advance and her expenses. **[3]**

**10.** A shipping company has two vessels and three contracts. One vessel sinks, and the company chooses to use the other for two of the contracts, cancelling the third.

Explain whether the third contract is frustrated. **[3]**

**11.** Explain, using decided cases, the difference between *Krell v Henry* and *Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton*. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the doctrine of frustration and the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 deal fairly with unforeseen events. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Performance must be **complete and exact** to discharge the contract [1]: in *Cutter v Powell* (1795) a sailor who died before the end of the voyage earned nothing under a lump-sum contract [1].

**2.** Where a party has **substantially performed**, they may recover the contract price **less the cost of remedying defects** [1]. In *Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952] decorating with minor defects was substantial performance, so the price was payable less repair costs [1]. The doctrine does not apply where defects are **serious** [1]: in *Bolton v Mahadeva* [1972] a heating system that gave off fumes and heated poorly was not substantially performed, so nothing was payable [1].

**3.** In **bilateral** discharge both parties still have obligations, so each gives up rights, which is **consideration on both sides** [1]. In **unilateral** discharge one party has already performed [1], so a release by that party needs **new consideration** (accord and satisfaction) or a **deed** [1].

**4.** Any three [1] each: performance is merely **more expensive or difficult** (*Davis Contractors*; *Tsakiroglou*); the purpose is only **partly** defeated (*Herne Bay*); the event is **self-induced** (*Maritime National Fish*; *Super Servant Two*); the event was **foreseen** or provided for by a **force majeure** clause.

**5.** Where a party has obtained a **valuable benefit** other than money before the frustrating event [1], the court may order them to pay a **just sum** for it (*BP Exploration v Hunt*) [1].

**6.** Strictly, payment is due only on **complete** performance (*Cutter v Powell*) [1]. But Chen has **substantially performed**: the job is done and the defects are minor compared with the price [1], as in *Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952] [1]. He can recover **£2,400 less £150**, that is £2,250 [1].

**7.** Hollis has committed an **anticipatory breach** [1]. The hotel may **accept the breach and sue immediately** for damages (*Hochster v De La Tour* (1853)) [1], and should then mitigate by buying chairs elsewhere [1]. Alternatively it may **affirm** the contract and wait until 1 September [1], but it then takes the risk of later events, as in *Avery v Bowden* (1855), where the outbreak of war frustrated the contract and the right to sue was lost [1]. If it keeps the contract alive it may in some cases perform and claim the price only where it has a **legitimate interest** (*White and Carter v McGregor* [1962]) [1].

**8.** The term that the ship is seaworthy and properly crewed is likely an **innominate term** [1]. Under *Hong Kong Fir Shipping* [1962] the innocent party may end the contract only if the breach deprives them of **substantially the whole benefit** of the contract [1]. Five weeks out of two years is a small part of the charter [1], so Omar can claim **damages** but probably cannot treat the contract as ended [1].

**9. (a)** The contract is **personal**: only Ayesha can perform it [1]. Her injury, which is **no one's fault** and occurred **after formation** [1], makes performance impossible on the date, as in *Robinson v Davison* (1871), so the contract is **frustrated** [1].
**(b)** Under **s 1(2)** the £5,000 advance is **recoverable** by the organisers [1]. The court **may** allow Ayesha to keep up to £600 for expenses incurred in performing [1], if it considers this **just**; it is not bound to, as *Gamerco v ICM* [1995] shows [1].

**10.** Frustration cannot be **self-induced** [1]. The company **chose** which contract to cancel, so the failure to perform the third contract results from its own decision rather than from the sinking alone [1]. As in *The Super Servant Two* [1990], the third contract is **not frustrated** and the company is liable for breach [1].

**11.** In *Krell v Henry* [1903] rooms were hired solely to watch the coronation procession [1]; when it was cancelled the **whole purpose** of the contract was defeated, so it was frustrated [1]. In *Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton* [1903] a boat was hired to watch the naval review **and** cruise round the fleet [1]; when the review was cancelled the cruise was still possible, so the purpose was **only partly** defeated and the contract was not frustrated [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: the test (*Taylor v Caldwell*; *Davis Contractors*); categories; limits (self-induced, more expensive, foreseen); the 1943 Act, s 1(2) and s 1(3), with *Gamerco* and *BP v Hunt*.
- **Fairness**: frustration releases parties from obligations they could not perform through no fault of their own; the Act prevents a party keeping advance payments (reversing the harshness before *Fibrosa*); the court's discretion over expenses lets loss be shared.
- **Criticisms**: the doctrine is narrow, so parties bear the risk of much hardship (*Tsakiroglou*); the line between frustration and mere difficulty is uncertain (*Krell* versus *Herne Bay*); the expenses discretion makes results unpredictable; the Act does not apply to some contracts, such as many insurance contracts.
- **Evaluation**: reach a conclusion, for example that the combination works fairly because businesses can draft *force majeure* clauses, while the law supplies a fallback for truly unforeseen events.

Top-level answers apply cases accurately and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a contract frustrated because it became more expensive.
- Treating every breach as allowing the contract to be ended.
- Forgetting the risks of waiting after an anticipatory breach.
- Ignoring self-induced frustration.
- Saying the payee always keeps expenses under the 1943 Act.
