## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Describe and explain global patterns and trends in the consumption of water, food (land) and energy.
- Relate those trends to population change, poverty reduction and the growth of a new global middle class.
- Use measures such as the ecological footprint to compare consumption between places and over time.
- Interpret data on consumption accurately, using totals, per-capita figures and rates of change.

## Splitting growth into its causes

Total consumption of any resource is the product of how many people there are and how much each person uses. A widely used way of expressing human impact is the **IPAT identity**, proposed by Paul Ehrlich and John Holdren in the early 1970s:

**Impact = Population x Affluence x Technology**

Here affluence is consumption per person, and technology is the resource or environmental cost of each unit of consumption. The identity is not a theory of cause, but it is a useful way to ask *which* factor is driving a trend.

### Worked example: decomposing meat consumption

Figures are invented for an emerging economy, Country M.

```
                     Year 1          Year 2 (40 years later)
Population           900 million     1,400 million
Meat per person      15 kg a year    60 kg a year

Total meat           900 million x 15 = 13,500 million kg = 13.5 million tonnes
                     1,400 million x 60 = 84,000 million kg = 84 million tonnes

Growth in total      84 / 13.5 = 6.2 times
  from population    1,400 / 900 = 1.56 times
  from affluence     60 / 15 = 4.0 times
  check              1.56 x 4.0 = 6.2
```

Population grew by about half, but consumption per person quadrupled, so rising affluence explains most of the sixfold growth. The interpretation to write: in emerging economies, the new middle class's change in diet matters more for resource demand than population growth, whereas in a low-income country with rapid population growth and little change in diet, the balance would be the other way round.

## Poverty reduction and the middle class: why definitions matter

The size of the "new global middle class" depends on how it is defined. Researchers usually set a band of daily spending per person, adjusted for purchasing power, so the group includes households that have escaped poverty but are not rich. Move the lower threshold up or down and hundreds of millions of people enter or leave the category. When you use the idea, say that it is a defined band, explain that its growth reflects falling extreme poverty in East and South Asia above all, and link it to consumption: it is the households crossing that threshold that buy their first refrigerator, motorbike or regular meat.

## How consumption changes inside households

The **energy ladder** describes how households move to cleaner, more convenient fuels as incomes rise:

1. Collected biomass: firewood, crop waste and dung, burned on open fires.
2. Purchased biomass and charcoal.
3. Kerosene and bottled gas.
4. Electricity and piped gas.

Each step usually reduces indoor air pollution and the time spent gathering fuel, often a burden carried by women and children, but raises demand for commercial energy. In practice many households **stack** fuels, using electricity for lights and phones while still cooking with charcoal, because supply is unreliable or cooking traditions favour it. Fuel stacking is a good example of why trends in national data can hide slow change at household scale.

Similar ladders exist for water (from carrying water from a river, to a shared standpipe, to piped water in the home) and diet (from staple grains towards meat, dairy and processed food). As people climb each ladder, consumption per person rises.

## Water: withdrawal and consumption are not the same

A careful answer distinguishes two measures:

- **Withdrawal** is water taken from rivers, lakes or aquifers.
- **Consumption** (consumptive use) is the part that is not returned to the local system, because it evaporates, is transpired by crops, or is built into products.

A thermal power station may withdraw large volumes for cooling but return most of them, warmer, to the river. Irrigation withdraws large volumes and consumes a high share, because crops transpire it. This is why agriculture dominates consumption of fresh water even more strongly than it dominates withdrawal, and why switching irrigation methods matters so much in dry regions.

## Land and food: more output from the same area

Global food production has risen faster than the area of cropland, because yields per hectare have increased through improved seeds, fertiliser, irrigation and mechanisation. Rising demand can therefore be met in two ways: **extensification**, clearing new land, as in parts of the Amazon and South-east Asia, or **intensification**, raising yields on existing land. Each has different consequences, which link this sub-topic forward to 10.2.

## Rates of change and doubling time

Consumption data are often given as annual growth rates. A quick way to see what a rate means over time is the **rule of 70**: the doubling time in years is roughly 70 divided by the percentage growth rate.

- Growth of 2 per cent a year: doubling in about 70 / 2 = 35 years.
- Growth of 7 per cent a year: doubling in about 70 / 7 = 10 years.

The rule shows why apparently small differences in growth rate produce very different futures. A country whose electricity demand grows at 7 per cent a year needs to double its generating capacity within a decade.

## Annotated model description

*Describe the trend in per-capita energy use shown for Country K.* **(sample answer using invented data: 0.4 toe per person in 1990, 0.5 in 2000, 1.1 in 2010, 1.6 in 2020)**

"Per-capita energy use in Country K rose throughout the period, from 0.4 to 1.6 tonnes of oil equivalent per person, a fourfold increase **[overall trend with start and end values and units]**. Growth was slow in the 1990s, rising by only 0.1 toe, but accelerated sharply after 2000, with the largest increase, 0.6 toe, between 2000 and 2010 **[rates of change compared between periods]**. Growth slowed slightly between 2010 and 2020, rising by 0.5 toe **[change in rate noted]**."

Notice that a description stops at the pattern. If the command term were explain, you would go on to give reasons, such as industrialisation, urbanisation and rising household incomes.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming population growth explains rising consumption, without checking per-capita change.
- Using withdrawal and consumption of water as if they were the same.
- Calling the energy ladder a smooth, one-way process, ignoring fuel stacking.
- Treating a global trend as if it applies to every country or every household.
- Describing a graph as "going up" without values, units, dates or changes in rate.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** trends with values, units and periods; compare rates, not only totals.
- **Explain** trends by naming the driver and the mechanism, for example rising incomes changing diets.
- **Distinguish** needs both terms defined and the difference stated, for example withdrawal against consumption.
- **Compare** two countries' consumption using the same measure for each, and say which measure you are using.
- In calculations, show the working, carry units through and round sensibly.
