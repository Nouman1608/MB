These questions practise key question 4.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *How is family life
changing?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for each Paper 2
question; Set B gives extra practice on demographic change, alternatives to marriage and the roles of
children and grandparents.

---

## Set A — Changing family life

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *ageing population* **[2]**
- (ii) *boomerang children* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** alternatives to marriage. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why divorce rates have risen in many societies. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the role of grandparents has changed. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the impact of industrialisation and urbanisation on family structures. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that conjugal roles have become equal. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** effects of having children in later life on family life. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** reasons why cohabitation has increased. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways the internet may affect marriage and relationships. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** reasons why children are now seen as an economic cost. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways cultural expectations may slow changes in marriage patterns. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss the view that families have become more child-centred. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** A population in which the proportion of older people is rising (1), caused by falling
birth rates and rising life expectancy (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Adult children who return to live in their parents' home after a period of
independence (1), often because of housing costs, debt or unemployment (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: cohabitation; singlehood; lone parenthood; civil partnership.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Legislation** has made divorce cheaper, quicker or easier to obtain, so unhappy couples can end
  marriages legally.
- **Changing attitudes**: less stigma, so divorced people are not excluded or shamed.
- **Secularisation**: religion has less influence, weakening the belief that marriage is for life.
- **Female empowerment**: women's education and earnings mean they do not depend on a husband.
- **Higher expectations of marriage** (Fletcher): couples end marriages that fail to provide love and
  fulfilment.
- **Longer life expectancy**: marriages last longer, giving more time for breakdown.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Childcare**: grandparents look after children so that both parents can work in dual worker
  families.
- **Economic support**: help with housing deposits, school fees or living costs.
- **Wisdom and advice**: guidance on parenting and family decisions, though this may be valued less
  where the young are more educated or digitally skilled.
- **Dependency**: longer lives mean more grandparents need care, often from the pivot generation.
- **Longer grandparenthood**: beanpole families mean grandparents and great-grandparents play a role
  for longer.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- **Parsons:** industrial work needs mobility; the isolated nuclear family fits; extended kin lose
  importance.
- **Young and Willmott:** the pre-industrial family as a unit of production; extended kin support in
  early industrial cities; the move from Bethnal Green to Greenleigh weakened kin contact and produced
  home-centred families.
- **Urbanisation:** migration to cities splits families; smaller urban households keep rural ties.
- **Evaluation:** historians found nuclear households before industrialisation and extended
  households during it (Laslett; Anderson); modified extended families show kinship adapts rather than
  disappears; change varies between societies.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Young and Willmott (1973): the symmetrical family, with similar roles and shared leisure.
- Dual worker families: women's earnings give them more power in decisions.
- Changing attitudes to fatherhood; more men involved in childcare.
- Laws on equal pay and parental leave in some countries support sharing.

*Against the view:*
- Oakley (1974): women still do most housework; men "help".
- Dual burden and triple shift (Duncombe and Marsden): women add emotion work.
- Edgell (1980): men take the important decisions.
- Cultural expectations keep segregated roles strong in many communities.
- Lone-parent families have no partner to share with, so the claim does not apply.

*Conclusion:* roles are more joint than in the past but rarely equal; change depends on class,
culture and whether both partners work. Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Two effects, 2 marks each: smaller families because there is less time to have several
children; parents are often more financially secure, giving more resources per child; a wider gap
between generations; grandparents may be older and less able to help, or need care themselves at the
same time as young grandchildren.

**(h) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: less stigma attached to living together; secularisation weakens
religious objections; cohabitation as a trial before marriage; the cost of weddings; fear of divorce
after seeing parents divorce; greater independence of women.

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: dating apps and websites widen partner choice beyond the local
community; people can meet partners from other cultures or countries; social media can create
jealousy or contact with former partners; online contact may make relationships feel easier to end
and replace.

**(j) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: child labour laws stop children earning; compulsory schooling
and longer education keep them dependent for longer; spending on clothes, technology and activities
has risen; parents may pay for higher education or housing.

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: expectations of arranged marriage keep marriage rates high;
stigma around divorce discourages couples from separating; family honour or reputation may keep empty
shell marriages together; religious communities may oppose cohabitation.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *For:* smaller families give more time and money per child; spending and leisure revolve around
  children; children have more say in decisions; laws protect children's rights; Ariès on childhood as
  a protected modern stage.
- *Against:* children in poverty, child labour or abusive homes are not at the centre; adult control
  over children's time and space remains strong; child-centredness varies by class and culture.
- *Judgement:* many families are more child-centred than in the past, but not all children share this
  experience.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need statistics on divorce?** No. Explaining trends and their causes is what matters; avoid
figures you are not sure of.

**Can I use examples from my own country?** Yes. Applying changes to a society you know shows the
cross-cultural awareness the topic rewards.

**What is the best way to start (f)?** State the view, say which perspectives support or challenge it,
then work through each side before your judgement.
