This guide covers subtopic **28.1, General physical and chemical properties of the first row
of transition elements, titanium to copper**, from Topic 28, Chemistry of transition elements,
of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content.
The broader
[Transition Elements: Properties, Complexes and Redox Chemistry guide](/resources/a-transition-elements-properties-complexes-and-redox-chemistry/)
covers the whole of the early part of Topic 28; this page goes deeper into the six learning
outcomes of 28.1: applying the definition to awkward cases, describing the two d orbitals you
must sketch, and building the explanations for variable oxidation states, catalysis and complex
formation step by step.

## What the syllabus asks for

The learning outcomes for 28.1 are:

1. define a transition element as **a d-block element which forms one or more stable ions with
   incomplete d orbitals**;
2. sketch the shape of a **3d<sub>xy</sub>** orbital and a **3d<sub>z²</sub>** orbital;
3. understand that transition elements **have variable oxidation states**, **behave as
   catalysts**, **form complex ions** and **form coloured compounds**;
4. explain variable oxidation states in terms of the **similarity in energy of the 3d and 4s
   sub-shells**;
5. explain catalytic behaviour in terms of **more than one stable oxidation state**, and **vacant
   d orbitals that are energetically accessible and can form dative bonds with ligands**;
6. explain complex ion formation in terms of **vacant d orbitals that are energetically
   accessible**.

Why the colour arises is the subject of 28.3; here you only need to know that coloured compounds
are a characteristic property.

## Electron configurations: the foundation

Every explanation in this subtopic starts from the electron configuration. From titanium to
copper the 3d sub-shell fills, with two exceptions:

| Element | Configuration (after [Ar]) |
|---|---|
| Ti | 3d² 4s² |
| V | 3d³ 4s² |
| Cr | **3d⁵ 4s¹** |
| Mn | 3d⁵ 4s² |
| Fe | 3d⁶ 4s² |
| Co | 3d⁷ 4s² |
| Ni | 3d⁸ 4s² |
| Cu | **3d¹⁰ 4s¹** |

When these atoms form positive ions, the **4s electrons are lost first**, then 3d electrons.
For example, Fe → Fe²⁺ gives [Ar]3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ gives [Ar]3d⁵; Cr³⁺ is [Ar]3d³; Cu²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁹.

## Applying the definition

The definition has two tests: the element must be in the **d block**, and it must form at least
one **stable ion with an incomplete d sub-shell** (one to nine d electrons).

**Worked example 1.** Explain why copper is a transition element but zinc is not, and why
scandium is not usually counted either.

- **Copper:** Cu²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁹, an incomplete d sub-shell. Cu⁺ is [Ar]3d¹⁰, which is complete,
  but the definition needs only **one** stable ion with incomplete d orbitals, so copper
  qualifies.
- **Zinc:** its only ion, Zn²⁺, is [Ar]3d¹⁰. The d sub-shell is full, so zinc is a d-block
  element but not a transition element.
- **Scandium:** its only ion, Sc³⁺, is [Ar], with **no** d electrons, so its d sub-shell is
  empty rather than incomplete.

A complete answer gives the configuration of the relevant ion and links it to the definition.

## Sketching 3d<sub>xy</sub> and 3d<sub>z²</sub>

You must be able to draw these two orbitals on a set of x, y and z axes.

- **3d<sub>xy</sub>:** four lobes, all lying **in the xy plane**, pointing **between** the x and
  y axes (at 45° to them). Opposite lobes are the same size, and the nucleus is at the centre.
- **3d<sub>z²</sub>:** two lobes pointing **along the z axis**, one above and one below the
  nucleus, plus a **ring (doughnut shape) around the middle** in the xy plane.

When sketching, label the axes, keep the lobes symmetrical about the nucleus, and make clear
whether the lobes lie on or between the axes. Placing the 3d<sub>xy</sub> lobes along the axes
is the most common error; that describes a different d orbital.

## The four characteristic properties

| Property | Example |
|---|---|
| Variable oxidation states | manganese from +2 in Mn²⁺ to +7 in MnO₄⁻ |
| Catalysts | iron in the Haber process; Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ in the I⁻/S₂O₈²⁻ reaction |
| Complex ions | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [CoCl₄]²⁻ |
| Coloured compounds | blue copper(II) sulfate solution; purple manganate(VII) |

Compare a Group 2 metal such as calcium: it forms only Ca²⁺, is not used as a catalyst in this
way, forms few complexes, and its compounds are white.

## Explaining variable oxidation states

The key idea is that the **3d and 4s sub-shells are similar in energy**. Building the
explanation:

1. Electrons in the 4s and 3d sub-shells have similar energies.
2. So the energy needed to remove each successive electron rises **gradually** as 4s and then
   3d electrons are removed; there is no very large jump until all of them have gone.
3. Different numbers of electrons can therefore be lost (or used in bonding) for similar energy
   costs, and several oxidation states are stable.

Common oxidation states, with the highest one for each element shown in the last column:

| Element | Common oxidation states | Highest |
|---|---|---|
| Ti | +2, +3, +4 | +4 |
| V | +2, +3, +4, +5 | +5 |
| Cr | +2, +3, +6 | +6 |
| Mn | +2, +4, +6, +7 | +7 |
| Fe | +2, +3 | +3 (commonly) |
| Co | +2, +3 | +3 |
| Ni | +2 | +2 (commonly) |
| Cu | +1, +2 | +2 |

From titanium to manganese the highest oxidation state equals the total number of 3d and 4s
electrons (Ti, four; Mn, seven). For calcium, by contrast, the third ionisation energy is very
much larger than the second because it removes an electron from an inner, lower-energy shell,
so only +2 forms.

**Worked example 2.** Deduce the oxidation state of the transition element in VO₂⁺, CrO₄²⁻
and MnO₄²⁻.

- VO₂⁺: V + 2(−2) = +1, so V = **+5**
- CrO₄²⁻: Cr + 4(−2) = −2, so Cr = **+6**
- MnO₄²⁻: Mn + 4(−2) = −2, so Mn = **+6**

High oxidation states usually occur in oxo-anions or oxides, because oxygen is electronegative
enough to stabilise them.

## Explaining catalytic behaviour

The syllabus gives two linked reasons; a full answer uses both.

- **More than one stable oxidation state.** The metal or its ion can accept electrons from one
  species and pass them to another, changing oxidation state and then changing back. Iron does
  this in the I⁻/S₂O₈²⁻ reaction: Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺ by S₂O₈²⁻, then reduced back to Fe²⁺ by
  I⁻.
- **Vacant d orbitals that are energetically accessible.** These can accept lone pairs and form
  **dative bonds** with ligands or reactant molecules, holding reactants on a surface or in a
  complex so that bonds within them are weakened. Iron adsorbing nitrogen and hydrogen in the
  Haber process is an example.

## Explaining complex ion formation

A ligand donates a lone pair of electrons to the central metal ion, forming a dative covalent
bond. This is possible because the transition metal ion has **vacant d orbitals of suitable
(accessible) energy** that can accept those electron pairs. The small size and relatively high
charge of many transition metal ions also attract lone pairs strongly, but the syllabus
explanation centres on accessible vacant orbitals.

## Common mistakes

- Removing 3d electrons before 4s when writing ion configurations.
- Forgetting the chromium and copper exceptions (3d⁵ 4s¹ and 3d¹⁰ 4s¹).
- Defining a transition element as "an element with an incomplete d sub-shell" without
  mentioning **ions**.
- Drawing the 3d<sub>xy</sub> lobes on the axes, or drawing 3d<sub>z²</sub> without its ring.
- Explaining variable oxidation states by "they have d electrons" without the energy argument.
- Giving only one of the two reasons for catalysis.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the syllabus wording, including "d-block", "stable ions" and "incomplete d
  orbitals".
- **Sketch**: label axes and show orientation clearly.
- **Explain** variable oxidation states: name the 3d and 4s sub-shells and say their energies are
  similar, then link to successive ionisation energies.
- **Explain** catalysis or complex formation: refer to vacant, energetically accessible d
  orbitals and dative bonds; for catalysis add changes of oxidation state.
- **Deduce** oxidation states: write the algebra.
