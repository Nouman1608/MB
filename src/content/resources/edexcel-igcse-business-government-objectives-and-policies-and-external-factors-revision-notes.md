## 1.6.1 Government spending

**Public services** are provided by the government and paid for from public funds (for example health, education, policing).

**Taxation** raises the money. In general terms:

| Tax falls on | Effect on a business |
|---|---|
| Business profits | Less profit kept for growth or owners |
| Workers' incomes | Less to spend, so weaker demand |
| Spending | Higher customer price, so possibly lower sales |

**Constraints on public spending**

1. Tax revenue is limited.
2. Borrowing must be repaid with interest.
3. Opportunity cost: spending on one thing means not spending on another.
4. A weak economy lowers tax receipts.

**Reminder calculation.** Sales tax: price × rate = tax; price + tax = customer price. At 12% on 85, the tax is 10.20 and the customer pays 95.20.

## 1.6.2 How governments affect business activity

| Policy | What it is | Likely effect on a business |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure | Roads, rail, ports, power, communications | Lower transport costs, reliability, access to markets and workers |
| Legislation | Laws on consumers, employees, environment, competition | Compliance costs, product changes, penalty risk, consumer trust |
| Trading bloc | Group of countries trading on easier terms | Cheaper access to a bigger market, but more competition |
| Tariff | Tax on imports | Dearer imports, protected home producers, revenue for government |

**Method in steps: working out a tariff**

1. Tariff = import price × tariff rate.
2. New cost = import price + tariff.
3. Compare with the home-produced price.
4. Decide who gains (home producers, government) and who loses (importers, customers).

Reminder: 40 with a 15% tariff becomes 46.00, so a home product at 44 is now cheaper.

## 1.6.3 Interest rates

**Businesses.** Higher rates mean higher interest on loans and overdrafts, lower profit, and fewer projects worth the borrowing cost. Lower rates reverse this.

**Consumers.** Higher rates make credit dearer and saving more attractive, so spending on credit-financed goods falls. Lower rates raise spending.

| Rates rise | Rates fall |
|---|---|
| Interest cost up | Interest cost down |
| Investment delayed | Investment more attractive |
| Consumer spending down | Consumer spending up |
| Biggest hit: cars, furniture, houses | Biggest boost: same big-ticket goods |

**Reminder calculation.** Interest = loan × rate. 120,000 at 5% is 6,000; at 8% is 9,600. That is a rise of 3 percentage points but a 60% rise in the bill.

## 1.7.1 External factors

| Factor | Examples | Effect on decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Social | Ageing population, changing tastes, ethical attitudes | Product range, advertising, staffing |
| Technological | Automation, online selling, new software | Costs, sales channels, retraining |
| Environmental | Pollution, waste, resource use, climate concern | Packaging, energy use, reputation |
| Political | Government policy, elections, stability | Planning, tax, trade, laws |

An external factor is something the firm cannot control but must respond to. Always link the factor to a specific decision: what to sell, where, at what price, how to produce.

## Method in steps: answering an analyse or evaluate question

1. Identify the policy or factor in the stem (for example a rate rise, a new tariff, a social trend).
2. Name the first effect on the business: costs, price, demand or the way it operates.
3. Expand: say what that does to profit, cash flow, market share or customers.
4. Use the numbers in the case. Work out the change before commenting on it.
5. For evaluate, weigh the other side and say which effect is likely to be larger for this business and why. Give a supported conclusion.

## Worked reminders

**Tax on profit.** A business makes a profit of 200,000 and the tax rate on profit is 20%. Tax = 40,000; profit after tax = 160,000. The business can use the 160,000 to reinvest or to pay owners.

**Consumer borrowing.** A shopper borrows 2,400 on a store card. At 6% the interest is 144 a year; at 9% it is 216. The difference of 72 is a small amount for one person, but it is repeated across many customers, so a retailer who sells on credit sees demand change.

**Online sales share.** A shop has sales of 640,000, of which 18% are online (115,200). If online sales grow by 25% they reach 144,000, which is 22.5% of the original total. A technological shift can change the sales mix quickly.

**Wage costs and social change.** A rise of 8% in an hourly wage of 5.50 gives 5.94. Social factors such as changing expectations of pay and conditions feed through to the cost of labour in the same way.

## Linking the two sections

Government policy and external factors overlap. Environmental concern leads customers to demand greener products (social and environmental), and the government may respond with legislation (political). Technology can create an infrastructure need, such as better communications networks. When a question gives one factor, ask what others it triggers.

| Starting point | Possible chain |
|---|---|
| Rates rise | Loan interest up, consumer credit costly, sales fall, profit falls |
| Tariff on imports | Imported inputs dearer, price rises or margin falls, home rivals gain |
| New environmental law | Compliance cost, product change, reputation gain |
| Better roads | Lower delivery cost, wider customer reach, new competitors arrive |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Tariff versus tax on spending:** a tariff applies to imports; a tax on spending applies to purchases generally.
- **Percentage points versus percentage change:** from 5% to 8% is 3 points; the interest bill rises 60%.
- **Public service versus infrastructure:** public services are provided to people; infrastructure is the physical network businesses use.
- **Legislation versus policy:** a law must be obeyed; a policy is a plan of action that may use laws, taxes or spending.
- **Internal versus external:** the firm controls its own prices and staff; it does not control social, technological, environmental or political factors.

## Command words

The specification defines these as follows. Outline: give two linked points about a business concept or issue, placed in context. Explain: give a statement of fact with two further expansion points, with no context. Analyse: write an extended answer with expansion and exploration, placed in context. Justify: use the information provided to recommend one of two options. Evaluate: use knowledge of the content to reach a supported conclusion about a business situation.

## Quick self-test

1. State one way a government pays for public services.
2. Give two constraints on public spending.
3. Define infrastructure.
4. A 25% tariff is placed on an import costing 52. Calculate the new cost.
5. Name one benefit and one drawback of joining a trading bloc for a business.
6. A business has a loan of 150,000. The rate rises from 6% to 9%. Calculate the extra yearly interest.
7. Explain why higher interest rates may reduce sales of cars.
8. Name the four external factors in 1.7.1.
9. Sales fall from 9,600 to 8,160. Calculate the percentage change.
10. Give one way a technological change could affect a small business.

### Answers

1. Taxation (it may also borrow).
2. Any two of: limited tax revenue, borrowing must be repaid, opportunity cost, weak economy lowering tax receipts.
3. The basic physical networks such as roads, rail, ports, power and communications that businesses rely on.
4. 52 × 25% = 13.00, so the cost is 65.00.
5. Benefit: cheaper access to a larger market. Drawback: more competition from other members.
6. 150,000 × 9% = 13,500; 150,000 × 6% = 9,000; extra = 4,500.
7. Cars are often bought on credit; dearer borrowing makes monthly payments higher, so fewer people buy.
8. Social, technological, environmental, political.
9. (8,160 − 9,600) ÷ 9,600 × 100 = −15%.
10. For example, online selling opens a wider market without a shop; or the cost of new equipment strains its cash.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a policy without naming its effect on the business in the question.
- Treating a tariff as a tax on exports.
- Giving only one side of a bloc, tariff or interest rate change.
- Saying "interest rates go up so profit goes down" without explaining that the loan interest increases.
- Mixing up percentage points and percentage change.
- Listing the four external factors but not linking any to a decision.
- Ignoring the context: a small local business and a large multinational feel the same policy differently.
- In evaluate questions, stopping at analysis and not giving a supported conclusion.
