**This guide covers the prose option on Paper 4 Unseen.** The syllabus states that "One question is
based on a passage of literary prose, such as an extract from a novel or short story", and that both
questions "require candidates to write a critical commentary on a poem or prose extract printed in
the question paper and to demonstrate an appreciation of the text." The
[Paper 4 Unseen study guide](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-4-unseen/) sets out the format
and the choice between options; this page teaches how to read an unfamiliar prose passage and build
a commentary on it, with an original extract. For the poetry option see
[the unseen poetry critical commentary](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-unseen-poetry-commentary/).

## How unseen prose differs from unseen poetry

A prose passage is longer and contains more incident than most poems. That changes the danger: with
poetry, candidates sometimes over-read single words; with prose, they retell the story. The skill is
selection. You cannot analyse every sentence of a page of fiction in 55 minutes of writing, so you
choose the moments that best answer the question and analyse them closely.

You also have to work out context from the passage alone. An extract from a novel begins in the
middle of a story. You do not know what came before, and you should not invent it. Infer what you can
from the words and say what remains uncertain.

## Orientation: the first reading

In the planning time (the syllabus advises "around 20 minutes reading the questions and planning"),
start by establishing:

- **Narrative voice:** first person ("I") or third person; if third, whether the narrator stays close
  to one character's thoughts or ranges more widely.
- **Who and where:** the characters present, the setting, the time.
- **What happens:** in two sentences at most.
- **The question's focus:** underline its key words ("tension", "the relationship", "the writer's
  presentation of the place").
- **Any introductory note:** some passages come with a sentence of context; use it, and do not
  contradict it.

## Tracking shifts

Prose extracts are chosen because something changes within them: a mood darkens, a character learns
something, a calm scene is interrupted. Divide the passage into three or four sections and give each a
heading that describes what is happening to the focus of the question. This gives your commentary its
structure and stops it becoming a list of devices.

## Methods to analyse in prose

- **Narrative perspective:** whose view we share, and what that hides.
- **Characterisation:** speech, actions, gestures, what others notice, what is withheld.
- **Dialogue:** what is said and not said; who controls the conversation.
- **Setting and atmosphere:** how place and weather reflect feeling.
- **Sentence structure and pace:** long sentences that pile up detail, short ones that shock, lists,
  questions.
- **Imagery:** similes, metaphors and patterns of related words.
- **Paragraphing:** a one-line paragraph for impact; a sudden break in time.
- **Openings and endings** of the passage, which often frame the change.

## Worked example: an original passage

The passage below was written for this guide. It is not from any published novel.

> *(original passage)*
>
> The letter came on the first warm day of the year, when the whole street had its windows open and
> the smell of cut grass drifted into every kitchen. Hana's mother read it standing up, by the sink,
> with the tap still running.
>
> "Who's it from?" Hana asked.
>
> Her mother did not answer. She folded the letter along its creases, then folded it again, smaller,
> as if a thing could be made to mean less by taking up less room. Outside, someone's radio was
> playing a song about summer. The tap ran on.
>
> "Mum?"
>
> "Turn that off, would you?" her mother said, and Hana understood that she meant the tap, although
> she was looking at the window, at the radio, at the whole bright street that did not know.
>
> Hana turned off the tap. In the silence, the song went on outside, cheerful and stupid, and her
> mother put the small square of paper in her apron pocket and kept her hand there, pressing it flat.

**Question:** How does the writer create a sense of shock in this passage?

**Sections and headings:**
1. *Paragraph 1: an ordinary warm day.* The detail of open windows and "cut grass" establishes
   normality, so the letter arrives into a world not ready for it.
2. *Paragraphs 2–3: the silent reaction.* The mother "did not answer"; folding the letter "smaller"
   shows her trying to contain its meaning.
3. *Paragraphs 4–5: displaced feeling.* "Turn that off" is about the tap, but her gaze at the street
   suggests she wants to switch off the whole cheerful world.
4. *Paragraph 6: silence and the pocket.* The tap stops, but the song continues; her hand "pressing
   it flat" ends the passage on suppressed emotion.

**Key analysis points:**
- The contents of the letter are **never revealed**, so the reader experiences the shock through the
  mother's reaction, as Hana does.
- The **running tap** is a repeated detail ("with the tap still running", "The tap ran on") that
  measures the mother's stillness: she is too shocked to perform a simple action.
- The **simile-like comment** "as if a thing could be made to mean less by taking up less room" is
  the narrator's insight into a desperate, almost childlike hope.
- The **contrast** between the "cheerful and stupid" song and the silent kitchen sharpens the shock;
  the adjectives are Hana's, showing her sudden resentment of the bright world.
- **Free indirect style:** "the whole bright street that did not know" blends narration with Hana's
  understanding, so the reader shares her realisation.

**Model paragraph:**

> The writer creates shock largely through what the mother does not do. She reads the letter "with the
> tap still running", and when Hana asks who it is from, she "did not answer". The detail of the tap is
> returned to in a short sentence, "The tap ran on.", which makes time seem to carry on without her:
> she is too stunned to reach out and turn it off. Her careful folding of the letter "smaller, as if a
> thing could be made to mean less by taking up less room" is, for me, the most painful moment,
> because the narrator lets us see a grown woman trying, like a child, to shrink bad news by hiding it.
> We never learn what the letter says, and that silence makes the shock more powerful than any
> explanation could.

*Why it works:* it selects details that serve "shock"; it analyses repetition, sentence length and
narrative comment; it comments on withholding; and it ends with a personal judgement grounded in the
text.

## Structuring the commentary

1. **Introduction:** narrative situation in one or two sentences, your answer to the question, and
   how the passage develops.
2. **Body:** three or four paragraphs following the sections you identified, each analysing two or
   three short quotations.
3. **Ending:** a paragraph on the final lines of the passage, which often carry the strongest effect.
4. **Conclusion:** a judgement on the question's key word.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling.** Narrating the events in order without analysis of method.
- **Inventing context.** Deciding what the letter says or what happened before the passage.
- **Ignoring the narrator.** Writing about characters as real people rather than as the writer's
  creations, presented through a particular viewpoint.
- **Analysing only dialogue.** Description, pace and paragraphing matter too.
- **Stopping before the end** of the passage.
- **Quoting long sentences.** Quote the few words you will analyse.

## Command words and question focuses

"Explore" means "write in detail about particular aspects". A prose question usually names an effect
("tension", "shock", "humour") or a focus ("the relationship", "the setting"). Use it as your filter
during planning: annotate only what helps you answer it.
