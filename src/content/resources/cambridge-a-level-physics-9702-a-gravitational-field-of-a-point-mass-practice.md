## Section A

**1.** Derive, from Newton's law of gravitation and the definition of gravitational field strength, the expression g = GM/r² for the field strength at distance r from a point mass M. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why the gravitational field strength may be taken as constant inside a building 100 m tall, and estimate the fractional change in g between its ground floor and its roof. **[3]**

**3.** Describe the shape of a graph of g against r for points outside a uniform spherical planet of radius R, starting from r = R. **[2]**

**4.** A satellite is at a point where the Earth's gravitational field strength is 2.45 N kg⁻¹. Without using G, calculate its height above the Earth's surface. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why g = GM/r² can be used for a satellite orbiting the Earth even though the Earth is not a point. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A planet has mass 3.0 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 4.0 × 10⁶ m. Treat it as a uniform sphere.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational field strength at its surface. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the field strength at a height of 1.0 × 10⁶ m above its surface. **[2]**

**(c)** State the height above the surface at which the field strength is one quarter of the surface value. **[1]**

**7.** A planet has 64 times the mass of its moon. Their centres are 4.5 × 10⁸ m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the distance from the planet's centre of the point on the line between them where the resultant gravitational field is zero. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the value of G is not needed in part (a). **[1]**

**(c)** A spacecraft with its engines off is momentarily at this point. State the resultant gravitational force on it. **[1]**

**8.** A probe measures the gravitational field strength of an unexplored planet as 5.0 N kg⁻¹ at a distance of 8.0 × 10⁶ m from the planet's centre.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the planet. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the field strength at 1.6 × 10⁷ m from the centre. **[1]**

**(c)** The planet's radius is 5.0 × 10⁶ m. Calculate the field strength at its surface. **[2]**

**9.** A geostationary satellite orbits at a radius of 4.22 × 10⁷ m.

**(a)** Calculate the Earth's gravitational field strength at this radius. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the centripetal acceleration of the satellite, using its period of 24 hours. **[2]**

**(c)** Comment on your answers to (a) and (b). **[1]**

**10.** A uniform spherical planet has density ρ and radius R.

**(a)** Show that the gravitational field strength at its surface is g₀ = (4/3)πGρR. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate g₀ for a planet with ρ = 5500 kg m⁻³ and R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m. **[1]**

**11.** The Earth (mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg) and the Moon (mass 7.35 × 10²² kg) are 3.84 × 10⁸ m apart. Calculate the resultant gravitational field strength at the midpoint of the line joining their centres, and state its direction. **[3]**

**12.** A student says: "At twice the distance from the centre of the Earth, the gravitational field strength is half as big." Correct the statement and explain the correct relationship. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Newton's law: the force on a small test mass m at distance r is F = GMm/r² [1]. Field strength is force per unit mass, g = F/m [1]. So g = (GMm/r²) ÷ m = GM/r², the test mass cancelling [1].

**2.** The height of the building is very small compared with the Earth's radius, so the distance from the centre, and hence g = GM/r², hardly changes [1]. Fractional change ≈ 2h/R = 2 × 100 ÷ 6.37 × 10⁶ [1] = 3 × 10⁻⁵ (about 0.003%) [1].

**3.** g starts at its surface value and falls as r increases, steeply at first and then more gradually [1]; it is an inverse square curve (g ∝ 1/r²) that approaches zero without reaching it [1].

**4.** 9.81 ÷ 2.45 = 4.0, so r² is 4 times R² and r = 2R [1]; height = r − R = R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m [1].

**5.** The Earth is approximately a uniform sphere [1], and for a point outside a uniform sphere its mass acts as if concentrated at its centre, so r is measured from the centre [1].

**6. (a)** g = GM/R² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.0 × 10²⁴ ÷ (4.0 × 10⁶)² [1] = 12.5 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** r = 5.0 × 10⁶ m [1]; g = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.0 × 10²⁴ ÷ (5.0 × 10⁶)² = 8.0 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** One quarter at r = 2R, so at a height equal to R: 4.0 × 10⁶ m [1].

**7. (a)** GM_p/x² = GM_m/(d − x)² [1]; x/(d − x) = √64 = 8 [1]; x = 8d/9 = 8 × 4.5 × 10⁸ ÷ 9 = 4.0 × 10⁸ m [1].

**(b)** G appears on both sides of the equation and cancels [1].

**(c)** Zero [1].

**8. (a)** M = gr²/G = 5.0 × (8.0 × 10⁶)² ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ [1] = 4.8 × 10²⁴ kg [1].

**(b)** Distance doubled, so g = 5.0 ÷ 4 = 1.25 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** g₀ = g(r/R)² = 5.0 × (8.0 ÷ 5.0)² [1] = 12.8 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**9. (a)** g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ (4.22 × 10⁷)² [1] = 0.224 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** ω = 2π ÷ 86 400 = 7.27 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹ [1]; a = rω² = 4.22 × 10⁷ × (7.27 × 10⁻⁵)² = 0.223 m s⁻² [1].

**(c)** They are equal (within rounding), because gravity is the only force on the satellite and provides its centripetal acceleration [1].

**10. (a)** M = ρ × (4/3)πR³ [1]; g₀ = GM/R² = G(4/3)πR³ρ ÷ R² = (4/3)πGρR [1].

**(b)** g₀ = (4/3)π × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5500 × 6.4 × 10⁶ = 9.8 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**11.** r = 1.92 × 10⁸ m from each body. Earth's field: 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ (1.92 × 10⁸)² = 1.08 × 10⁻² N kg⁻¹ [1]. Moon's field: 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²² ÷ (1.92 × 10⁸)² = 1.33 × 10⁻⁴ N kg⁻¹ [1]. They act in opposite directions, so the resultant is 1.07 × 10⁻² N kg⁻¹, towards the Earth [1].

**12.** The field strength is one quarter as big, not half [1], because g = GM/r² is an inverse square law: doubling r divides g by 2² = 4 [1].

---

## Where marks are usually lost

- Skipping the definition g = F/m in a derivation.
- Measuring r from the surface instead of the centre.
- Using direct proportion instead of inverse square when distances change.
- Forgetting to take the square root when locating a zero-field point.
- Adding opposing fields instead of subtracting them, or not stating the direction of the resultant.
- Explaining constant g near the ground without comparing the height with the Earth's radius.
