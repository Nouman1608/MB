## Questions

**1.** Explain two social or economic reasons for the emergence of the African American civil rights movement. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "We did not come to Birmingham to cause trouble. We came because the courts and the city had left us no other way. A right delayed is a right denied, and our children cannot wait for the comfortable to feel ready." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase in the style of a 1963 civil rights statement.)

**(a)** Identify two reasons given in the extract for direct action. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the extract could be used to understand the methods of the civil rights movement, and one limitation of using it alone. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between the approach of Martin Luther King Jr. and that of Malcolm X. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the role of women in the civil rights movement. **[6]**

**5.** Explain the importance of the Civil Rights Act (1964) and the Voting Rights Act (1965). **[6]**

**6.** Examine the methods used by one social movement in the Americas, other than the African American civil rights movement, to create change. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the extent of change achieved by one social movement in the Americas other than the African American civil rights movement. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did radical activism help the African American civil rights movement? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "The civil rights movement achieved legal change but little economic change." To what extent do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Social: Jim Crow laws and customs enforced segregation in schools, transport and public places, backed by violence such as lynching [1], and incidents such as the murder of Emmett Till (1955) outraged people and showed the cost of the system [1]. Economic: many Black southerners were poor sharecroppers or low-paid workers [1], and mechanisation of cotton farming pushed people to northern cities, where Black communities had more political freedom and organisation [1]. Social and economic: wartime experience, with the "Double V" campaign, and a rising Black middle class raised expectations [1], while continued inequality created frustration [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** The courts and city authorities had offered no remedy [1]; delay in granting rights is itself a denial of rights, especially for children [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It shows that direct action was presented as a last resort after other routes had failed, consistent with the use of boycotts, sit-ins and marches [1], and shows how moral argument was used to appeal to white moderates [1]. Limitation: it is a statement of purpose written to persuade, not an account of what happened in Birmingham [1]; a paraphrase presents one side, and does not show the experiences of participants or the violence they faced [1]. **[4]**

**3.** King advocated non-violent direct action and integration, appealing to American ideals and Christian moral argument [1]. Malcolm X, in his Nation of Islam period, called for Black separation, pride and self-defence, and criticised integration [1]. Over time, Malcolm X moved towards a wider, more political approach, and King turned to economic issues and opposition to the Vietnam War, so the contrast is not fixed [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Ella Baker helped to found SNCC and believed in local, group-centred leadership rather than a single leader [2]. Fannie Lou Hamer spoke for the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party in 1964 and exposed violence against voters [1]. Rosa Parks's arrest prompted the Montgomery boycott, and local women organised much of it [1]. Angela Davis became a prominent radical voice [1]. Women often did the daily organising but received less recognition, and they sometimes faced sexism within organisations [1]. **[6]**

**5.** The Civil Rights Act (1964) banned discrimination in public places and employment and set up the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission [2]. It ended legal segregation in hotels, restaurants and other facilities and gave federal power to withhold funds from segregated schools [1]. The Voting Rights Act (1965) ended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to registration offices in the South [2]. Black registration rose sharply, for example in Mississippi from under ten per cent to more than half by the late 1960s [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Choose the Chicano movement and the UFW. Non-violent: Chávez and Huerta organised strikes, a boycott of grapes, the 1966 march to Sacramento and fasts [2]. Linking to the Delano strike of 1965 [1]. More militant or cultural: Tijerina's land-grant campaign and the 1967 courthouse raid, school walkouts, the Brown Berets, the Chicano Moratorium and La Raza Unida [3]. Role of key leaders and women such as Huerta [1]. Judgement on which methods were most effective [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Legal: California's Agricultural Labor Relations Act (1975) gave farm workers bargaining rights, and bilingual education laws and MALDEF supported rights [2]. Political: more Latino officials were elected [1]. Social and cultural: Chicano studies, murals, pride in heritage [2]. Economic: UFW contracts improved some conditions, but many farm workers stayed poor and the union weakened in the 1980s [2]. Judgement: significant but partial change [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Help: Malcolm X and Black Power raised pride and shifted the debate, making moderates seem more acceptable to officials [2]; the Panthers' community programmes met local needs [1]. Harm: violent rhetoric and urban riots alarmed some white voters and led to a conservative backlash [2]; COINTELPRO's attacks diverted energy [1]. Judgement: radicals contributed to cultural change and bargaining power, but the main legal gains came earlier through non-violent action [2]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Legal change (about 4 marks): *Brown*, the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Voting Rights Act (1965), the Fair Housing Act (1968), rising Black registration. Economic change (about 4 marks): a Black middle class grew, but poverty, unemployment and wealth gaps remained, segregation in housing continued, King's turn to the Poor People's Campaign. Perspectives (about 3 marks): views that stress legal victories and the "long civil rights movement" including economic goals; critics who see limited change; Black Power perspectives on economic self-determination. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that legal change was significant and economic change was partial, partly because the movement's legal successes did not address structural inequality. **[15]**
