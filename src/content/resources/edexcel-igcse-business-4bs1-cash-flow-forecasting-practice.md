These questions cover section 3.2 Cash flow forecasting (points 3.2.1 and 3.2.2) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [cash flow forecasting study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-cash-flow-forecasting/) and the [cash flow forecasting revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-cash-flow-forecasting-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Figures in brackets are negative. Show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define net cash flow. **[2]**

**2.** State two cash outflows a café would expect each month. **[2]**

**3.** A business has an opening balance of 3,400. Its cash inflows for the month are 8,250 and its cash outflows are 9,100. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the net cash flow. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the closing balance. **[1]**

**4.** Complete the table by calculating the eight missing values. **[8]**

| | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 6,200 | ? | 7,800 |
| Total outflows | ? | 7,300 | 6,900 |
| Net cash flow | (400) | 1,100 | ? |
| Opening balance | 1,500 | ? | ? |
| Closing balance | ? | ? | ? |

**5.** Explain why a profitable business can still fail. **[3]**

**6.** Outline one way a business could improve its cash flow by changing the credit terms it gives to customers. **[2]**

**7.** Penrose Furniture sells on credit. Customers pay 40% of the price in the month of sale and 60% the following month. Sales are 20,000 in September, 25,000 in October and 30,000 in November. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the cash received from customers in October. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cash received from customers in November. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Sorrel Bikes hires out bicycles to tourists. Its forecast for the first four months of the year is below.

| | January | February | March | April |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 2,000 | 2,500 | 6,000 | 9,000 |
| Total outflows | 3,400 | 3,600 | 4,000 | 4,500 |
| Net cash flow | (1,400) | (1,100) | 2,000 | 4,500 |
| Opening balance | 1,000 | (400) | (1,500) | 500 |
| Closing balance | (400) | (1,500) | 500 | 5,000 |

**(a)** Identify the month with the lowest closing balance. **[1]**
**(b)** State the minimum overdraft limit Sorrel Bikes would need to cover the forecast. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse one reason why the forecast for Sorrel Bikes may turn out to be inaccurate. **[4]**

**9.** In one month Garnet Studio, a photography business, makes sales of 14,000. Customers pay 9,000 in the month and the rest later. Its costs for the month of 10,500 are all paid in the month. It also buys a new camera for 3,000 in cash; the camera will be used for several years, so its cost is not counted against this month's profit.

**(a)** Calculate Garnet Studio's profit for the month. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its net cash flow for the month. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the profit and the net cash flow are different. **[3]**

**10.** Using the forecast in question 8, Sorrel Bikes is considering two ways to deal with its negative balances.

- **Option A:** an overdraft. The bank charges interest of 2% a month on the closing overdrawn balance.
- **Option B:** ask the bicycle supplier to delay a payment of 1,800, due in February, until April.

**(a)** Calculate the total interest Sorrel Bikes would pay under Option A for January and February. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify which option Sorrel Bikes should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Larkspur Foods plc is a large food manufacturer with rising sales. It is building a new warehouse, paid for in instalments from Month 2. Its forecast closing balances are:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closing balance | 250,000 | (150,000) | (420,000) | (610,000) | (380,000) | (90,000) |

**(a)** Calculate the net cash flow in Month 5. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Larkspur Foods should finance the shortfall with a bank overdraft or a bank loan. **[9]**

**12.** Anika is about to open a new gift shop and has written her first cash-flow forecast.

**(a)** Give two cash inflows, other than sales, that could appear in her forecast. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of a cash-flow forecast for a new business. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between total cash inflows and total cash outflows [1] over a period, such as a month [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: wages, rent, payment for food and drink supplies, electricity or gas, insurance, loan repayments [1 each]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** 8,250 − 9,100 = **(850)** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 3,400 − 850 = **2,550** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** January outflows: 6,200 + 400 = **6,600** [1]. January closing: 1,500 − 400 = **1,100** [1]. February opening: **1,100** [1]. February inflows: 7,300 + 1,100 = **8,400** [1]. February closing: 1,100 + 1,100 = **2,200** [1]. March net: 7,800 − 6,900 = **900** [1]. March opening: **2,200** [1]. March closing: 2,200 + 900 = **3,100** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For January, outflows = inflows − net cash flow = 6,200 − (−400). Subtracting a negative adds it. Write the rule before you substitute.

**5.** Profit can be made on sales that customers have not yet paid for [1], so the business may not have cash to pay suppliers, overheads or wages when they are due [1]. If it cannot pay its debts on time it becomes insolvent and may be forced to close, whatever its profit [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Shorten the time customers are given to pay, for example from 60 to 30 days [1], so cash from sales arrives sooner and is available to pay bills [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Offering a small discount for early payment is also valid, as long as you say it brings cash in sooner.

**7. (a)** 0.4 × 25,000 + 0.6 × 20,000 [1] = 10,000 + 12,000 = **22,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 0.4 × 30,000 + 0.6 × 25,000 [1] = 12,000 + 15,000 = **27,000** [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** February [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 1,500 [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** For example: inflows depend on tourist numbers [1], which could be lower than expected if the weather is poor in March and April [1]. Then inflows would fall short of 6,000 and 9,000 [1], so the business could still be overdrawn in March and need a larger or longer overdraft than planned [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Other valid causes include repair costs for bikes, a rival opening, or a change in interest or rent. Whichever you choose, follow it through to the effect on the closing balances.

**9. (a)** 14,000 − 10,500 = **3,500** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 9,000 − 10,500 − 3,000 [1] = **(4,500)** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 5,000 of the sales have not been paid yet, so they count in profit but not in cash [1]. The camera cost 3,000 in cash this month [1], but because it will be used for years its cost is not counted against this month's profit, so cash is lower than profit by the camera's cost as well [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* 5,000 + 3,000 = 8,000, which is exactly the gap between 3,500 and (4,500). Checking this shows you have the explanation right.

**10. (a)** January: 400 × 0.02 = 8 [1]; February: 1,500 × 0.02 = 30; total **38** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For Option A: the overdraft covers every negative balance, including January's (400), and costs only 38 in interest [1]. Against Option A: the bank can ask for repayment at short notice, and a small business may find it hard to get if it has no trading record [1]. For Option B: February's outflows fall by 1,800, so February closes at 300 instead of (1,500) and no interest is paid [1]. Against Option B: January's (400) is not solved, so a small overdraft is still needed [1], and the supplier may refuse or be less willing to give credit in future [1]. Judgement: for example, Option A, because the interest is very small, it covers both months, and it avoids the risk of upsetting a key supplier just before the busy season [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** −380,000 − (−610,000) = **230,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For an overdraft: it is flexible, so Larkspur pays interest only on the amount overdrawn each month [1], and the shortfall is shrinking by Month 6 as rising sales bring cash in [1]. Against an overdraft: the shortfall reaches 610,000 and lasts at least five months [1], which is large and long for an overdraft, and the bank could ask for repayment at short notice [1]. For a loan: the warehouse is a long-term asset, so long-term finance matches the need [1], and fixed repayments are easier to plan [1]. Against a loan: Larkspur would pay interest on the full amount even after cash recovers [1], and the balance is forecast to be close to positive by Month 6, so a loan may be larger than needed [1]. Judgement: for example, a loan for the warehouse cost, because it is a long-term asset and the shortfall is large, perhaps with a small overdraft to cover any remaining short gaps [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The key evaluation point is matching the finance to the need. A long-term asset paid for in instalments is not the same as a short seasonal gap.

**12. (a)** Any two of: owner's capital or personal savings put in; a bank loan received; money from selling an asset; crowdfunding received [1 each]. **[2]**
**(b)** A new business has no past figures to base its estimates on [1], so forecast sales may be much too high or too low [1], which means the forecast may not warn of a cash shortage, or may lead Anika to borrow more than she needs [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing sales as receipts when the question says customers pay later.
- Losing the minus sign when working with negative net cash flows.
- Forgetting that the opening balance is the previous month's closing balance.
- Giving a general list of ways to improve cash flow instead of using the months and figures in the case.
- Recommending finance without matching it to how long the shortage lasts.
