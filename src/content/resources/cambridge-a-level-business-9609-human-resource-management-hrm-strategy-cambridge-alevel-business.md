This guide goes deeper into **section 7.4 Human resource management (HRM) strategy** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, examined through case studies and essays.

The [leadership and HRM strategy guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-leadership-and-human-resource-management-hrm-strategy/) defines each contract type and performance measure. This page concentrates on *strategy*: why a business chooses one approach over another, how to compare options with figures, and how to judge whether a change will work. Then use the [HRM strategy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-human-resource-management-hrm-strategy-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [HRM strategy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-human-resource-management-hrm-strategy-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

All businesses are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

Section 7.4.1 Approaches to HRM lists six points:

- the difference between "hard" and "soft" HRM
- flexible working contracts: temporary contracts or flexible contracts including zero hours contracts, part-time, full-time, annualised hours, flexi-time, home working, shift working, job sharing, compressed working hours, the gig economy
- the measurement, causes and consequences of poor employee performance
- strategies for improving employee performance
- Management by Objectives (MBO): implementation and usefulness
- the changing role of Information Technology (IT) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) in HRM

## What makes HRM *strategic*

At AS Level, HRM was about tasks: recruiting, training, dismissing. At A Level, HRM strategy asks how the management of people supports the business's overall objectives. A business competing on low price needs low labour cost per unit; one competing on expert service needs skilled, loyal staff. The HR approach should follow from the competitive strategy, not the other way round.

## Hard and soft HRM as strategic choices

| | Hard HRM | Soft HRM |
|---|---|---|
| View of staff | A cost, like any other resource | An asset whose value can grow |
| Planning horizon | Short term; adjust numbers to demand | Long term; develop skills and loyalty |
| Typical tools | Flexible contracts, tight targets, close monitoring | Training, consultation, career paths, empowerment |
| Fits | Cost-focused strategy; low-skill, easily replaced work | Quality- or innovation-focused strategy; scarce skills |
| Link to theory | McGregor's Theory X assumptions; Taylor | Theory Y assumptions; Mayo, Herzberg |

Many businesses run both. A common pattern is a **core** of permanent, well-trained staff treated in a soft way, surrounded by **peripheral** workers on temporary, zero hours or gig arrangements treated in a hard way. This keeps expertise while making labour cost flexible. The risk is a two-tier workforce in which peripheral staff feel little loyalty and customers notice the difference in service.

## Flexible working contracts: making the decision

Rather than learning eleven lists of pros and cons, judge any contract against four questions:

1. **Demand pattern.** Is demand steady, seasonal, or unpredictable hour by hour?
2. **Skill and training needs.** Can a new worker be productive quickly, or does the job take months to learn?
3. **Quality and customer contact.** Will customers notice if staff change often?
4. **Employee needs.** Will the arrangement attract or drive away the people the business wants?

**Applying the questions.** A supermarket with predictable evening peaks can use part-time and shift working. A software firm employing scarce developers may offer home working and flexi-time to attract them, accepting harder supervision. A delivery platform with demand changing by the hour may use gig workers but must accept less control over service.

**Worked example: covering a seasonal peak at Brockley Toys.** Brockley needs 6 extra shop staff for 10 weeks before a festival, at 35 hours a week each, which is 6 × 10 × 35 = 2,100 hours.

```
Option 1: agency temporary staff at $19 an hour
          2,100 x 19 = $39,900

Option 2: Brockley's own temporary contracts at $14 an hour
          Wages:        2,100 x 14         = $29,400
          Recruitment:  6 x $400           =  $2,400
          Training:     6 x 15 hours x $14 =  $1,260
          Total                            = $33,060

Saving from Option 2: 39,900 - 33,060 = $6,840
```

The figures favour direct temporary contracts. The judgement must add what the figures leave out: the agency replaces absent workers at once and handles recruitment at short notice, while Brockley's own temporary staff could be offered permanent posts if they perform well. If the peak date is close, the agency's speed may be worth the extra cost.

## Poor employee performance: measure, cause, consequence

**Measuring.** Choose measures that fit the work:

- Output per worker or per hour (production).
- Sales per employee (retail).
- Wastage or reject rate (manufacturing).
- Absenteeism and labour turnover (all businesses).
- Customer complaints per 1,000 transactions (services).
- Appraisal results against agreed objectives.

**Worked example: two branches of Ravensworth Hardware.** Figures are for one year.

| | North branch | South branch |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $1,560,000 | $1,320,000 |
| Average employees | 24 | 16 |
| Sales per employee | 1,560,000 ÷ 24 = $65,000 | 1,320,000 ÷ 16 = $82,500 |
| Leavers | 9 | 2 |
| Labour turnover | 9 ÷ 24 × 100 = 37.5% | 2 ÷ 16 × 100 = 12.5% |

North's sales per employee are $17,500 lower, and its turnover is three times South's. A likely chain: many new staff, still learning the products, serve customers less effectively. The figures identify *where* the problem is; the cause still needs investigation through exit interviews, appraisals or staff surveys.

**Causes** fall into four groups: the person (skills, health, personal circumstances), the job (boring, unclear, badly designed), the management (weak leadership, no feedback, poor selection) and the resources (equipment, systems, working conditions).

**Consequences** run in a chain: lower output or service quality → higher unit costs and lost customers → lower profit; and, inside the business, colleagues covering for poor performers lose morale, so absence and turnover rise further.

## Strategies for improving performance

Match the strategy to the cause, then judge cost and time.

| Cause | Strategy | Cost and time |
|---|---|---|
| Skills gap | Training, mentoring | Moderate cost; results in weeks to months |
| Low motivation | Financial incentives, job enrichment, recognition | Incentives cost money every year; enrichment needs job redesign |
| Unclear goals | Appraisal, MBO, regular feedback | Mainly management time |
| Poor leadership | Leadership development, changing the manager | Slow, or disruptive |
| Poor resources | New equipment, better systems | High capital cost; quick effect once installed |
| Persistent failure | Disciplinary procedure, redeployment, dismissal | Legal risk; recruitment cost |

## Management by Objectives (MBO)

The idea is associated with Peter Drucker. Objectives flow down from the corporate level to departments and individuals, and each individual objective is **agreed**, not simply imposed. Many businesses check objectives against the SMART test: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

**Implementation pitfalls to evaluate:**

- Objectives set too low so that bonuses are easy to earn.
- Measurable targets crowding out what is hard to measure, such as teamwork or customer care.
- Objectives becoming out of date when the market changes during the year.
- Staff who have no control over results being judged on them.

MBO is most useful where staff are skilled, results can be measured and conditions are fairly stable. It is least useful for routine, tightly specified jobs where there is little to agree.

## The changing role of IT and AI in HRM

| Area | How IT and AI change it | Strategic question |
|---|---|---|
| Recruitment | Online applications; AI screening and ranking | Does it remove human bias or copy past bias? |
| Workforce planning | Data on absence, turnover and skills used to forecast needs | Is the data accurate and complete? |
| Training | Online modules, tracked completion | Does it build real skill or only completion rates? |
| Scheduling | Software matching shifts to forecast demand | Does it suit staff, or make hours unpredictable? |
| Employee queries | Self-service portals and chatbots | Does it free HR staff for complex cases? |
| Monitoring | Tracking of output and activity | Does it build performance or damage trust? |

The overall shift is that routine HR administration is automated, so HR staff spend more time on judgement: handling grievances, developing managers and planning skills. Businesses must still meet data-protection rules and decide which decisions must stay with people.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether Ravensworth Hardware's North branch should introduce MBO to improve performance.*

> "MBO would give North's staff clear, agreed sales objectives, which could raise sales per employee towards South's $82,500. However, the 37.5% labour turnover suggests that the main cause is that staff do not stay long enough to learn the products, and MBO does not address why they leave. Objectives agreed with staff who leave within months will not be met. Training and an investigation of why staff leave should come first; MBO is useful once the team is stable, because it then turns their knowledge into measurable targets."

It uses the figures, tests the strategy against the cause, and gives a sequence rather than a yes or no.

## Common mistakes

- Treating hard HRM as unethical by definition, rather than as a strategic choice with costs.
- Recommending a contract type without reference to the demand pattern or skill level.
- Calculating a performance measure and stopping, without saying what it suggests.
- Describing MBO steps without evaluating its usefulness for this workforce.
- Discussing AI only as a cost saving, ignoring bias and trust.

## Exam technique

**Analyse** questions need a chain from the HR decision to staff behaviour to a business result such as cost, quality or profit. **Evaluate** and **assess** questions need both sides and a judgement that names the condition: "flexible contracts suit this business *if* demand is unpredictable and skills are quick to learn". **Justify** asks you to support one choice with evidence from the case.
