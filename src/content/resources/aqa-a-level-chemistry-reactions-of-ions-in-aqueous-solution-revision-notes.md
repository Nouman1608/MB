## Scope (3.2.6, A-level only)

- 2+ aqua ions: **Fe and Cu only**.
- 3+ aqua ions: **Al and Fe only**.
- Bases: **OH⁻, NH₃ and CO₃²⁻ only**.
- Explain acidity using **charge/size ratio**.
- Amphoteric hydroxide example: **aluminium**.
- **Required practical 11**: simple test-tube reactions to identify transition metal ions in aqueous solution.

## Key definitions

- **Metal-aqua ion**: a metal ion with six water ligands, each bonded by a co-ordinate bond from oxygen, for example [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺.
- **Acidity of an aqua ion**: its tendency to give H⁺ from a water ligand to a solvent water molecule.
- **Amphoteric**: able to react with (dissolve in) both acids and bases.
- **Charge/size ratio**: ionic charge compared with ionic radius; a higher value means a more polarising ion.

## Colours of the aqua ions

| Ion | Colour of solution |
|---|---|
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | pale green |
| [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | blue |
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ | yellow-brown (as usually seen) |
| [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ | colourless |

## Acidity in four steps

```
[M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [M(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺
```

1. M³⁺ has a higher charge and a smaller radius than M²⁺.
2. So it has a higher charge/size ratio and is more polarising.
3. It pulls electron density from the O of the water ligands, weakening the O–H bonds.
4. H⁺ is released more easily: [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ solutions are more acidic (lower pH) than [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ solutions of the same concentration.

## Master observation table

| Ion | NaOH, few drops | NaOH, excess | NH₃, few drops | NH₃, excess | Na₂CO₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fe²⁺ | green ppt | no change | green ppt | no change | green ppt (FeCO₃) |
| Cu²⁺ | pale blue ppt | no change | pale blue ppt | deep blue solution | blue-green ppt (CuCO₃) |
| Fe³⁺ | brown ppt | no change | brown ppt | no change | brown ppt + bubbles |
| Al³⁺ | white ppt | colourless solution | white ppt | no change | white ppt + bubbles |

ppt = precipitate. The iron(II) precipitate darkens to brown on standing in air, as iron(II) is oxidised to iron(III).

Read the table by column for "what's different":

- **Excess NaOH** dissolves only the aluminium precipitate (amphoteric).
- **Excess NH₃** dissolves only the copper precipitate (ligand substitution).
- **Carbonate** gives bubbles only with the 3+ ions.

## Formulas of the products

| Product | Formula |
|---|---|
| iron(II) hydroxide | [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] |
| copper(II) hydroxide | [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] |
| iron(III) hydroxide | [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] |
| aluminium hydroxide | [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] |
| aluminate ion | [Al(H₂O)₂(OH)₄]⁻, also written [Al(OH)₄]⁻ |
| copper–ammonia complex | [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ |
| carbonates (2+ only) | FeCO₃, CuCO₃ |

The neutral hydroxides carry **no charge**. That is why they precipitate.

## Method in steps: writing any equation in this section

1. Start from the hexaaqua ion, [M(H₂O)₆]ⁿ⁺.
2. Decide how many H⁺ the base removes: 2 for M²⁺, 3 for M³⁺ to reach the neutral hydroxide.
3. Pick the H⁺ acceptor:
   - OH⁻ → H₂O
   - NH₃ → NH₄⁺
   - CO₃²⁻ → CO₂ + H₂O (3+ ions only)
4. Write the neutral hydroxide with (6 − n) H₂O ligands and n OH⁻ ligands.
5. Check atoms and charge.

```
OH⁻:  [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [M(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O
NH₃:  [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3NH₃ → [M(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3NH₄⁺
CO₃²⁻ (2+): [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → MCO₃ + 6H₂O
CO₃²⁻ (3+): 2[M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3CO₃²⁻ → 2[M(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3CO₂ + 3H₂O
```

The three extra equations to learn:

```
[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + OH⁻ → [Al(H₂O)₂(OH)₄]⁻ + H₂O
[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺
[Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2H₂O + 2OH⁻
```

## Must-know distinctions

- **NH₃ as a base vs NH₃ as a ligand.** A few drops: base (precipitate). Excess with Cu²⁺: ligand (deep blue solution).
- **2+ vs 3+ with carbonate.** 2+: metal carbonate precipitates. 3+: hydroxide precipitates and CO₂ is given off, because the 3+ aqua ion is acidic enough to protonate carbonate.
- **Amphoteric vs ligand substitution.** Al(OH)₃ dissolves in excess NaOH because it loses another H⁺ (acting as an acid). Cu(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NH₃ because NH₃ replaces ligands.
- **Colourless vs clear.** A solution with no colour is "colourless". "Clear" means transparent and can be blue.
- **Why a precipitate forms vs why it redissolves.** A precipitate forms when the complex loses enough H⁺ to become neutral (or, for 2+ ions with carbonate, when MCO₃ forms). It redissolves only by further H⁺ loss (Al) or by ligand substitution (Cu).
- **Fe²⁺ vs Fe³⁺.** Green vs brown hydroxide; no bubbles vs bubbles with carbonate.

## Required practical 11 in brief

1. About 1 cm³ of test solution in a clean test tube; record its colour.
2. Add reagent drop by drop, shaking; record precipitate colour.
3. Continue to excess; record whether the precipitate dissolves.
4. Use a fresh sample for each reagent (NaOH, NH₃, Na₂CO₃).
5. Leave an iron(II) precipitate to stand and look for darkening.

Hazards: NaOH(aq) is corrosive; NH₃(aq) is an irritant. Wear eye protection and work in a ventilated room.

The specification also suggests (as an opportunity, not required content) that you identify the positive and negative ions met elsewhere in the course. The Group 2 cation tests and sulfate test are in [Group 2 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-group-2-the-alkaline-earth-metals-revision-notes/).

## Small worked reminders

**Moles of base.** 0.0200 mol of [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ needs 3 × 0.0200 = 0.0600 mol NH₃ to precipitate all of it as [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃].

**Gas from carbonate.** 0.0100 mol of [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ with excess carbonate: Al³⁺ : CO₂ = 2 : 3, so n(CO₂) = 0.0100 × 3/2 = 0.0150 mol.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the formula and colour of the copper(II) aqua ion.
2. Which is more acidic: [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ or [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺?
3. Give the two features of the 3+ ion that give it a higher charge/size ratio.
4. Write the formula of the precipitate formed when a few drops of NaOH(aq) are added to Fe³⁺(aq).
5. What do you see when excess NaOH(aq) is added to Al³⁺(aq)?
6. What do you see when excess NH₃(aq) is added to Cu²⁺(aq)?
7. Write the equation for [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ with sodium carbonate solution.
8. Why does Fe³⁺(aq) fizz with sodium carbonate solution but Fe²⁺(aq) does not?
9. Define amphoteric and name the example in this section.
10. Write the equation for aluminium hydroxide dissolving in acid.
11. A green precipitate forms with NaOH(aq) and turns brown at the surface on standing. Which ion is present?
12. How many moles of OH⁻ react with 0.0100 mol of [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ to form the precipitate?

### Answers

1. [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, blue.
2. [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺.
3. Higher charge (3+) and smaller ionic radius.
4. [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃].
5. A white precipitate forms, then dissolves in excess to give a colourless solution.
6. A pale blue precipitate forms first, then dissolves to give a deep blue solution.
7. [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → FeCO₃ + 6H₂O.
8. [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is acidic enough to give H⁺ to carbonate ions, forming CO₂; [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is too weakly acidic, so FeCO₃ precipitates instead.
9. Reacts with both acids and bases; aluminium hydroxide.
10. [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺.
11. Fe²⁺ (iron(II)).
12. 2 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "Fe(OH)₃ precipitate" when the question asks for the formula of the complex: give [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃].
- Leaving a charge on a neutral hydroxide, or leaving the charge off [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺.
- Stating that FeCO₃-type carbonates form for Fe³⁺ or Al³⁺.
- Missing "effervescence" as a separate observation for 3+ ions with carbonate.
- Giving only the final observation with excess reagent and missing the precipitate that forms first.
- Explaining acidity with charge alone; you need both charge and size, then the effect on the O–H bond.
- Saying ammonia dissolves aluminium hydroxide, or NaOH dissolves copper hydroxide.
- Writing "clear" instead of "colourless".
- Forgetting NH₄⁺ as the product when ammonia acts as a base.
