This guide covers **Core Content Option B: International Relations since 1919**, for Cambridge IGCSE
World History 0470, 2024–2026 series. All candidates study Core content in either Option A (The
Nineteenth Century, 1848–1914) or Option B, each built around six key questions, plus at least one of
five named Depth studies.

## Where this fits in 0470

Option B is the alternative Core content route through 0470 to Option A — a school teaches one or the
other, not both — and it traces international relations across the twentieth century, from the
peacemaking that followed the First World War through to the collapse of Soviet control over Eastern
Europe. Assessment is via Paper 1 (Structured Questions) and Paper 2 (Document Questions), which every
candidate takes, plus either Component 3 Coursework or Paper 4.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE WORLD HISTORY 0470 — CORE CONTENT OPTION B: INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS SINCE 1919**

1. Was the Treaty of Versailles fair? — the roles of Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George; the terms of
   the Treaty; its social, economic and political impact on Germany to the end of 1923; contemporary
   opinions about the Treaty
2. To what extent was the League of Nations a success? — its structure, aims and membership;
   peacekeeping in the 1920s (Vilna, the Aaland Islands, Corfu, Bulgaria); its humanitarian work
   (refugees, health, working conditions, slavery); the Manchurian and Abyssinian crises of the 1930s
3. How far was Hitler's foreign policy to blame for the outbreak of war in Europe in 1939? — Hitler's
   foreign policy aims, rearmament, the Saar, remilitarisation of the Rhineland, the Rome–Berlin Axis and
   Anti-Comintern Pact, the Spanish Civil War, Anschluss, the Czechoslovak crisis and Munich Agreement,
   the Polish crisis
4. Who was to blame for the Cold War? — the 1945 summit conferences and the breakdown of the
   US–Soviet alliance, Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe to 1948, the occupation of Germany and the
   Berlin Blockade, NATO and the Warsaw Pact
5. How effectively did the United States contain the spread of communism? — case studies of Korea
   (1950–53), Cuba (1959–62) and Vietnam (1955–75)
6. How secure was the USSR's control over Eastern Europe, 1948–c.1989? — resistance in Hungary (1956)
   and Czechoslovakia (1968), the Berlin Wall, Solidarity in Poland, Gorbachev and the collapse of Soviet
   control

Each key question is explored through **focus points** — the specific issues candidates need to
understand — and **specified content**, which sets out the historical detail expected.

## Versailles and the League of Nations (questions 1–2)

The peace settlement after the First World War was shaped by the competing aims of the "Big Three":
US President Wilson favoured a settlement based on his Fourteen Points and self-determination; French
Premier Clemenceau wanted Germany permanently weakened to protect French security; British Prime
Minister Lloyd George sought a settlement firm enough to satisfy British public opinion but not so
harsh it would destabilise Europe. The resulting Treaty's terms and their impact on Germany through
1923 — economic, social and political — are central to judging whether the settlement was fair or
storing up future problems.

The **League of Nations**, created partly to enforce the new settlement, is assessed on its structural
weaknesses (limited membership, no standing army) against its actual record. Of the four named
1920s disputes, the **Aaland Islands** dispute (Sweden/Finland, 1921) and the **Bulgaria** incident
(1925) are conventionally classed as successes — the League's recommendation was accepted and enforced.
**Vilna** (Poland/Lithuania, 1920) and **Corfu** (Italy/Greece, 1923) are conventionally classed as
failures: at Vilna, Poland kept the city regardless of the League, with the Conference of Ambassadors
recognising Polish sovereignty three years later; at Corfu, the matter passed from the League to the
Conference of Ambassadors, and the victim state (Greece) ended up paying an indemnity to the aggressor
(Italy). Its humanitarian work on refugees and health is a separate, genuine strand of success, and its
more visible failures in the 1930s, when the Manchurian crisis (Japan) and Abyssinian crisis (Italy)
exposed how the Depression-era international climate made collective security far harder to enforce.

## Hitler's foreign policy and the outbreak of war (question 3)

This question asks candidates to weigh Hitler's foreign policy against other contributing causes.
Specified content runs chronologically: rearmament in breach of Versailles, the Saar plebiscite,
remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the Rome–Berlin Axis and Anti-Comintern Pact aligning Germany
with Italy and Japan, involvement (with Italy) in the Spanish Civil War, the Anschluss with Austria
(1938), the Sudeten crisis and Munich Agreement, and finally the Polish crisis that triggered British
and French declarations of war in September 1939. Examiners expect candidates to be able to discuss
**appeasement** as a British and French policy choice, not simply narrate Hitler's actions in isolation.

## The Cold War and containment (questions 4–5)

The origins of the Cold War are traced from the 1945 wartime summit conferences, through the breakdown
of US–Soviet cooperation, Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe by 1948, and the flashpoint of the Berlin
Blockade, which hardened the division of Europe into NATO and Warsaw Pact blocs. US containment policy
is then examined through three specific case studies rather than in the abstract: the Korean War
(1950–53) following North Korea's invasion of the South, the Cuban Revolution and Missile Crisis
(1959–62), and American involvement in Vietnam (1955–75), covering the reasons for US involvement,
the tactics and strategy used, and the reasons for eventual withdrawal.

## Soviet control of Eastern Europe (question 6)

The final key question examines how Soviet power over its Eastern European satellite states was
challenged and eventually collapsed: uprisings in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968) and the
Soviet response to each, the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961, the rise of the Solidarity movement in
Poland as a sign of weakening Soviet influence, and finally Gorbachev's personal role in the collapse of
Soviet control over Eastern Europe by the late 1980s.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating Versailles as simply "harsh" or "fair"** without weighing the competing aims of Wilson,
  Clemenceau and Lloyd George — the syllabus explicitly frames this as a judgement question.
- **Listing League of Nations crises without linking cause and consequence** — the Manchurian and
  Abyssinian crises matter because they exposed the League's structural weaknesses in practice.
- **Discussing containment in Korea, Cuba and Vietnam as three unconnected events**, rather than as case
  studies of a single, evolving US policy of containment.
- **Assuming the Cold War's origins are only about the USSR or only about the US** — the specified
  content expects candidates to weigh both sides' actions from 1945 to the Berlin Blockade.

## How to approach it

Because Option B is examined through six broad key questions rather than narrow factual recall, build
your revision around the **focus-point questions** the syllabus attaches to each key question — they are
the actual form exam questions tend to take. For each key question, practise writing a short paragraph
that reaches a judgement (not just a description) using two or three pieces of specified content as
evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the six Option B key questions?**
Versailles, the League of Nations, Hitler's foreign policy, who was to blame for the Cold War, US containment of communism, and the security of Soviet control over Eastern Europe, 1948–c.1989.

**How is IGCSE History 0470 Paper 1 structured?**
Paper 1 (2 hours, 60 marks, 40%) has Section A on the Core Content (eight questions, answer two) and Section B on the Depth Studies (two on each, answer one). Each question is a three-part structured essay worth 4, 6 and 10 marks.

**Do schools teach both Core Content options?**
Schools normally teach one option, A or B, alongside at least one Depth Study.
