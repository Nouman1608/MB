This guide covers topic 3.3 **Supply side policy** in Implementing policy, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to explain, with the aid of a diagram, six groups of measures: privatisation, deregulation and subsidies; competition policy; investment in infrastructure, education, training, research and development; reforms of the tax and benefit system; improved labour market flexibility; and immigration control. You must then evaluate the effectiveness of using supply side policy measures to achieve the government's macroeconomic objectives. All figures in the worked examples are invented.

Use it with the [supply side policy revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-supply-side-policy-revision-notes/) and the [supply side policy practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-supply-side-policy-practice/). The curves these measures shift are explained in [aggregate supply](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as/), and the A Level treatment is in [supply-side policy](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-supply-side-policy/).

## 1. The aim of supply side policy

**Supply side policies** are measures intended to increase the productive capacity of the economy and the efficiency of markets. On a diagram they shift **LRAS to the right**, and many also lower costs, shifting SRAS right.

The appeal is that, unlike a rise in AD, a rightward shift of LRAS can raise output **and** reduce inflationary pressure at the same time. Over time it can also lower structural unemployment and improve international competitiveness, helping the current account.

Measures are often grouped into two types.

- **Market-based** (free-market) policies reduce the role of the state and sharpen incentives: privatisation, deregulation, tax cuts, benefit reform and labour market flexibility.
- **Interventionist** policies use the state to fix market failures: spending on infrastructure, education, training and research, and subsidies.

## 2. Privatisation, deregulation and subsidies

**Privatisation** is the transfer of state-owned firms to private ownership. The UK privatised many industries in the 1980s, including British Telecom in 1984 and British Gas in 1986. The argument is that the profit motive, pressure from shareholders and the threat of takeover push firms to cut costs and innovate. Against this, a privatised natural monopoly may simply become a private monopoly, needing regulation.

**Deregulation** removes rules that restrict entry or add to costs, such as licensing barriers. Lower barriers allow new firms to enter, increasing competition and pushing prices down. Rules that protect safety, workers or the environment exist for reasons, so removing them can create external costs.

**Subsidies** to producers lower costs or reward activities with wider benefits. A subsidy for investment in new equipment or for research encourages spending that raises productivity. Subsidies have an opportunity cost and can keep inefficient firms alive.

## 3. Competition policy

**Competition policy** aims to make markets work better by preventing the abuse of market power. It covers investigating mergers that would reduce competition, breaking up cartels that fix prices, and stopping dominant firms from excluding rivals. In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority carries out this work.

More competition gives firms an incentive to lower costs and improve quality, raising productive and allocative efficiency across the economy. The supply-side effect is gradual and hard to measure, but it can be important in sectors such as banking, energy and telecoms.

## 4. Investment in infrastructure, education, training and R&D

- **Infrastructure** (transport, energy networks, digital connections) lowers firms' costs and connects workers to jobs.
- **Education** raises the quality of the future labour force.
- **Training** updates the skills of existing workers and helps the unemployed move into new industries, reducing structural unemployment.
- **Research and development** produces new technology and products.

**Worked example 1: training and unit costs.** A firm's workers produce 30 units an hour. After a training scheme they produce 33, a 10% rise in productivity. Wages rise by 4%. Unit labour costs change by 1.04 / 1.10 - 1 = **-5.5%**. Lower unit costs shift SRAS right, and higher productivity raises capacity, shifting LRAS right.

These measures also raise AD in the short run because they are government spending. Evaluation should note that the demand effect comes first and the supply effect much later.

## 5. Reforms of the tax and benefit system

**Tax reform** can increase incentives. Lower marginal rates of income tax raise the reward for extra hours or effort; lower corporation tax raises the return on investment and may attract firms from abroad.

**Benefit reform** aims to make work pay. If a person moving into work loses most of their benefits as their earnings rise, the gain from working is small.

**Worked example 2: the gain from taking a job.** An unemployed person receives benefits of 120 a week. A job pays 300 a week, on which tax and contributions are 45.

| | Before reform | After reform |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings | 300 | 300 |
| Tax and contributions | -45 | -45 |
| Benefits withdrawn | -120 | -60 |
| Net gain from working | **135** | **195** |
| Share of earnings lost | 55% | 35% |

Before the reform, 165 of the 300 earned is lost to tax and withdrawn benefits. A slower withdrawal of benefits raises the gain to 195, increasing the incentive to work and the size of the labour force.

The trade-off is equity: lower benefits or lower top tax rates can widen inequality, and some people cannot work whatever the incentives.

## 6. Improved labour market flexibility

A **flexible labour market** is one in which wages, hours and jobs adjust quickly to changes in demand. Measures include reducing the costs of hiring and dismissing workers, reforming trade union law, encouraging part-time and flexible contracts, and helping workers move between regions, for example through housing policy.

Greater flexibility can reduce unemployment and make the economy adjust faster after shocks. But it can also mean less job security, irregular hours and weaker bargaining power for workers, and firms may invest less in training workers they do not expect to keep.

## 7. Immigration control

The specification names **immigration control** as a supply side measure, because migration changes the size and skills of the labour force.

- **Looser controls**, or schemes targeted at shortage occupations, raise the labour supply and can fill skills gaps, shifting LRAS right. Migrants of working age may also raise tax revenue.
- **Tighter controls** reduce the growth of the labour force. Supporters argue they protect wages and jobs for low-paid workers and reduce pressure on housing and public services; the cost may be persistent skills shortages and slower growth in potential output.

In an answer, explain the effect on labour supply and LRAS, and evaluate with effects on wages, public finances and specific sectors, rather than making a general claim.

## 8. Evaluating supply side policies

| Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Raise capacity, so growth need not bring inflation | Long time lags: education and infrastructure take years |
| Reduce structural unemployment | Little help against cyclical unemployment caused by weak AD |
| Improve competitiveness and the current account over time | Costly for the government, raising borrowing |
| Can raise productivity, the key to long-run living standards | Uncertain effects: tax cuts may not raise effort much |
| Market-based measures can cut costs without extra spending | Equity costs: insecurity, inequality |
| | Risk of government failure: subsidies to the wrong projects |

The effectiveness of a measure depends on the problem it targets. Training helps when unemployment is structural; it does little in a recession caused by a fall in demand. Policies also work better together: infrastructure is more valuable when there are skilled workers to use it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all supply side policies as tax cuts.
- Forgetting that interventionist measures raise AD before they raise LRAS.
- Claiming supply side policy solves every unemployment problem.
- Leaving out the time lag in evaluation.
- Shifting AD instead of LRAS on the diagram.
- Writing about immigration as a political issue instead of an effect on labour supply.

## Exam technique

- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw LRAS1 and LRAS2 with AD, show the new equilibrium with higher output and a lower price level, and link the shift to the named measure.
- Build a chain: measure, effect on incentives or factor quantity and quality, effect on productivity or labour supply, LRAS shift, effect on the objective.
- **Evaluate** with time lags, cost, the type of unemployment, equity and uncertainty, and conclude on which objective the policy serves best.
