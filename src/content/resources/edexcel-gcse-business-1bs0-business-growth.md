This guide covers content item 2.1.1, Business growth, in Topic 2.1 (Growing the business) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It opens Theme 2, Building a business, which is examined in Paper 2. The broader [guide to business growth and changing aims](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-business-growth-and-changing-aims/) defines each method of growth and source of finance. This page concentrates on choosing: which growth method suits which business, and which source of finance suits which growth plan. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 2.1.1 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| Methods of business growth and their impact | Internal (organic) growth: new products (innovation, research and development), new markets (through changing the marketing mix or taking advantage of technology and/or expanding overseas). External (inorganic) growth: merger, takeover |
| The types of business ownership for growing businesses | Public limited company (plc) |
| Sources of finance for growing and established businesses | Internal sources: retained profit, selling assets. External sources: loan capital, share capital, including stock market flotation (public limited companies) |

Note the words "and their impact". Questions rarely ask only for a definition; they ask what a method of growth does to the business, its costs, its staff and its owners.

## Organic growth: growing from within

Organic growth means the business expands using its own resources, by selling more.

**New products.** *Innovation* is turning a new idea into a product or process customers will buy. *Research and development (R&D)* is the investigation and testing needed to create or improve products. R&D costs money up front, takes time and may fail, but a successful new product can give a business something rivals do not have.

**New markets.** The specification names three routes:

- **Changing the marketing mix** to appeal to a new group, for example a children's version of a product, a new price point or promotion aimed at a different age group.
- **Taking advantage of technology**, for example selling online to customers outside the local area.
- **Expanding overseas**, selling to customers in other countries.

**Impact of organic growth:** it is usually slower and lower-risk than buying another business, the owners keep control, and the culture of the business is preserved. But it is limited by how fast the business can raise finance and win customers, and rivals may grow faster.

## External growth: merger and takeover

External (inorganic) growth means growing by joining with, or buying, another business.

- **Merger:** two businesses agree to join together to form one business.
- **Takeover:** one business buys enough of another to gain control of it.

**Impact of external growth:**

| Possible benefits | Possible drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Rapid increase in size, customers and market share | Expensive; may need large borrowing or new shares |
| Gains the other firm's products, staff, skills or locations | Different ways of working can clash, lowering motivation |
| Can remove a competitor | Job losses where roles are duplicated, which can upset staff and the community |
| May allow lower costs by buying in larger quantities | Managers may find a larger business harder to control |

## Choosing a growth method

Ask what the business wants and what it has.

| If the business... | Organic growth is more suitable | External growth is more suitable |
|---|---|---|
| Needs to grow quickly | No | Yes |
| Has limited finance | Yes | No |
| Wants to keep the owners in control | Yes | No |
| Lacks a skill or product another firm has | No | Yes |
| Is cautious about risk | Yes | No |

## The public limited company (plc)

A growing business may convert to a plc so that it can sell shares to the public on the stock market.

**Impact for the business:** much larger sums can be raised; the business becomes better known, which can help it borrow and sell; shareholders have limited liability.

**Drawbacks:** the original owners usually lose some control as shares spread among many shareholders; anyone can buy shares, so the business can be taken over; more information about the business must be made public; and setting up a flotation is expensive.

## Sources of finance for growth

| Source | Internal/external | Best suited to | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retained profit | Internal | Steady organic growth by a profitable business | Limited to profit already made; owners receive less |
| Selling assets | Internal | A business with equipment or property it no longer needs | The asset is gone; may be sold cheaply in a hurry |
| Loan capital | External | A clear project with predictable returns | Interest and repayments must be paid even if sales fall |
| Share capital | External | Large projects; businesses that already have debt | Ownership and profit are shared |
| Stock market flotation | External (plc) | Very large growth plans | Cost, loss of control, takeover risk |

### Worked example: funding a new branch

Oakhurst Opticians Ltd needs £150,000 to open a second branch. It has three options.

**Option A: a bank loan of £150,000** with a total repayment of £180,000 over five years.

1. Interest = £180,000 − £150,000 = £30,000
2. Interest (%) = £30,000 ÷ £150,000 × 100 = 20% over the whole loan
3. Average yearly repayment = £180,000 ÷ 5 = £36,000

**Option B: sell new shares worth £150,000** to an investor. The two founders currently own all 100,000 shares; the investor would receive 50,000 new shares.

4. Total shares = 150,000, so the founders' share falls to 100,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 66.7%

**Option C: retained profit.** Oakhurst retains £40,000 a year, so it would take £150,000 ÷ £40,000 = 3.75 years to save enough.

**Weighing up:** the loan keeps control with the founders, but £36,000 a year must be paid even if the new branch is slow to attract patients. Share capital has no repayments, but the founders give up a third of the business and its future profit. Retained profit costs nothing in interest or control but delays growth by nearly four years, during which a rival might open nearby. A good recommendation links the choice to the founders' priorities: control (loan), low risk (shares) or patience (retained profit).

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse style):** Analyse the impact on Fenwright Tools of growing by taking over a rival toolmaker. [6]

> Taking over the rival would quickly increase Fenwright's market share, because it gains the rival's customers *(benefit, in context)*. It could also buy steel in larger quantities for both factories, which could lower its cost per tool *(chain)*. However, the takeover would be expensive and may need a large loan, so interest payments would reduce profit *(drawback)*. The two workforces may have different working methods, and if staff in the rival's factory fear job losses, their motivation could fall, lowering productivity *(second chain)*.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any increase in sales "external growth". External growth means joining with or buying another business.
- Treating plc as a start-up option.
- Forgetting that retained profit is only available if the business has made a profit.
- Saying share capital must be repaid.
- Recommending a source of finance without considering control, cost and risk.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** organic growth, merger, takeover, plc, retained profit.
- **Calculate:** interest on loans (%); shares and ownership percentages; time to save.
- **Analyse:** impact of a growth method on the named business.
- **Justify:** recommend one of two growth methods or sources of finance using the data, and state what the recommendation depends on.
