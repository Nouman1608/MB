## Section A: Short-answer and calculation questions

**1.** State what is meant by the secondary sector. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "share capital (equity)". **[2]**

**3.** Kelso Foods plc has 45 million shares in issue, trading at 320p. It pays total dividends of £3.6 million.

**(a)** Calculate the dividend per share. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the dividend yield. **[2]**

**4.** After a poor set of results, Kelso Foods' share price falls to 256p. The dividend per share is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in Kelso's market capitalisation. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the new dividend yield, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** difference between the objectives of a private sector business and a public sector organisation. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** disadvantage of a partnership compared with a sole trader. **[3]**

## Section B: Extended-response questions

**7.** Ama and Kofi each run their own dental practice as sole traders in the same town. Both want to buy expensive new equipment, and both are working long hours. They are considering forming a partnership. Analyse **two** advantages to Ama and Kofi of forming a partnership. **[6]**

**8.** Mirza Spices Ltd is owned by three generations of one family. Its products are sold in local shops, and it now plans to supply a national supermarket chain. The family disagrees about whether to borrow, and the founder, aged 72, has not said who will run the business after her. Assess whether being a family business will help or hinder Mirza Spices as it expands. **[9]**

**9.** Explain **two** disadvantages of being a small business for an independent bakery competing with supermarkets. **[4]**

**10.** Lumen Tech plc's share price has risen by 50% in a year. The company plans to issue new shares to fund a new research centre. Analyse the significance of the rise in share price for Lumen Tech. **[6]**

**11.** A national supermarket chain plans to open a large store on the edge of a small market town. Analyse the impact of this decision on **two** external stakeholders. **[6]**

**12.** Juno plans to open a café that trains and employs young people who have left school without qualifications. She needs £60,000 to start. She is deciding whether to set up as a social enterprise or as a private limited company aiming to maximise profit. Evaluate which form of business Juno should choose. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The part of the economy that turns raw materials or components into goods [1], such as manufacturing, processing and construction [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The money invested in a company by its shareholders in return for shares [1], which does not have to be repaid and carries no fixed interest [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** £3,600,000 ÷ 45,000,000 [1] = **8p** [1].

**(b)** 8 ÷ 320 × 100 [1] = **2.5%** [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** Fall in price = 64p; 45,000,000 × £0.64 [1] = **£28.8 million** fall (from £144 million to £115.2 million) [1].

**(b)** 8 ÷ 256 × 100 = 3.125 [1], so **3.1%** to one decimal place [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The yield has risen only because the price has fallen. Interpreting this, not just calculating it, earns credit in longer questions.

**5.** A private sector business usually aims to make a profit for its owners [1], whereas a public sector organisation, owned or controlled by government, usually aims to provide a service such as healthcare or education [1], so it may provide services free or below cost and be judged on quality and value for money rather than profit [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Partners usually have unlimited liability [1], and each can be responsible for debts run up by the other partners [1], so one partner's poor decision could put the others' personal possessions at risk (also credit: profits shared; disagreements slow decisions) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Advantage 1: more capital [1]. By combining their funds, Ama and Kofi can afford the expensive equipment that neither could easily buy alone [1], and could share it across one practice instead of buying two sets, lowering costs per patient [1]. Advantage 2: shared workload and skills [1]. They could cover for each other, reducing their long hours [1], and if each has different specialist skills they could offer patients a wider range of treatments, attracting more customers; however, they would need a partnership agreement to settle how profits and decisions are shared [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Help: family owners often take a long-term view [1], so they may be willing to accept lower profits while the supermarket contract builds up [1]. Shared values and commitment could protect the quality and recipes that made its spices successful [1]. Hinder: the family's disagreement about borrowing could delay decisions [1], and the supermarket contract may need quick investment in production capacity [1]. With no succession plan, there is uncertainty over who will lead the expansion [1], which may worry the supermarket and lenders [1]. Judgement: being a family business is likely to hinder Mirza Spices unless the family settles its disagreement and names a successor [1]; if it does, its long-term commitment could become a strength in a demanding contract [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Disadvantage 1: higher unit costs [1]. The bakery buys flour and other ingredients in small quantities, so it cannot get the bulk discounts supermarkets receive, making it hard to match their prices [1]. Disadvantage 2: difficulty raising finance [1]. Banks may see a small bakery as risky, so it may struggle to borrow for new ovens or a second shop (also credit: dependence on the owner; limited specialist skills) [1]. **[4]**

**10.** A higher share price means each new share can be sold for more [1], so Lumen will need to issue fewer shares to raise the money for the research centre [1], reducing the dilution of existing shareholders' ownership and control [1]. The rise also increases shareholders' wealth and confidence in the directors [1], making it more likely investors will support the new share issue [1]. However, the rise does not itself provide cash, and if the price falls before the issue, the funds raised could be lower than planned [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Local shops (community and competitors): the large store may take customers from shops in the town centre [1], because it can offer lower prices and a wider range [1], which could reduce their sales and lead some to close, changing the character of the town centre [1]. Local residents (community): they gain more choice and possibly lower prices [1], and some will find jobs in the store [1], but people living near the edge of town may suffer more traffic and noise (also credit: government, extra tax and planning issues; suppliers, new contracts for local firms) [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Social enterprise, for: its aim of training young people may attract grants and social investors [1], helping Juno raise the £60,000 [1]. Customers may choose the café because of its purpose, giving it a competitive advantage [1]. Social enterprise, against: investors seeking high returns are unlikely to invest [1], and training staff who lack experience will raise costs, so surplus may be small [1]. Private limited company, for: limited liability protects Juno's personal possessions [1], and aiming for profit may attract conventional investors [1]. Against: a profit-maximising objective may conflict with employing and training unqualified young people [1], who may be less productive at first [1]. Judgement: Juno should set up as a social enterprise [1], because her main purpose is social and this form is most likely to attract funding that accepts lower returns [1]; she can still choose a limited company as its legal structure to gain limited liability, as long as most surplus is reinvested in the social aim [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Recognising that a social enterprise can still have limited liability, if set up as a company, shows strong understanding.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a plc in the public sector.
- Forgetting that partners usually have unlimited liability.
- Mixing pounds and pence in the dividend yield.
- Giving a higher yield as good news without asking why the price fell.
- Describing a family business without applying its strengths and weaknesses to the case.
- Naming stakeholders without analysing the impact on them.
