## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Name the part of an HIV particle that comes from a host cell, and the molecules embedded in it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why HIV infects helper T-cells but not red blood cells. **[2]**

**3.** Which sequence of events in HIV replication is correct?

A: binding → integration → reverse transcription → transcription

B: binding → reverse transcription → integration → transcription

C: reverse transcription → binding → integration → transcription

D: binding → transcription → reverse transcription → integration **[1]**

**4.** State the role of each of these enzymes in HIV replication: reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease. **[3]**

**5.** Suggest why an HIV particle carries its own reverse transcriptase and integrase rather than relying on the host cell. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why people with AIDS often become ill with infections that rarely affect healthy people. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** Drug C binds to the glycoproteins on the HIV envelope and changes their shape. Explain how drug C reduces the number of helper T-cells that become infected. **[3]**

**8.** Drug D binds to reverse transcriptase away from its active site and changes the shape of the active site. Explain how drug D prevents new HIV particles being made. **[3]**

**9.** HIV-infected cells were treated with one of three drugs, P, Q or R. A tick shows the substance was detected.

| Detected in treated cells | Drug P | Drug Q | Drug R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viral RNA in the cytoplasm | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Viral DNA | | ✓ | ✓ |
| Viral DNA inside host chromosomes | | | ✓ |
| Long, uncut viral proteins | | | ✓ |
| Infectious virus particles released | | | |

**(a)** Identify the enzyme inhibited by each drug. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why no infectious particles are released from cells treated with drug R. **[2]**

**10.** The table shows illustrative data for a patient who took HIV drugs for 18 months and then stopped.

| Time | Viral load (copies per mm³) | Helper T-cell count (cells per mm³) |
|---|---|---|
| Start of treatment | 90 000 | 240 |
| 18 months | 300 | 480 |
| 4 months after stopping | 60 000 | 350 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in helper T-cell count during treatment. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the factor by which viral load fell during treatment. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the change in helper T-cell count during treatment. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why viral load rose after treatment stopped. **[2]**

**11.** HIV mutates frequently. Patients are usually given two or more drugs that act on different viral enzymes. Suggest why. **[3]**

**12.** Compare the contributions of the virus and the host cell to the production of new HIV particles. **[4]**

**13.** Suggest why a person can be infected with HIV for several years before showing symptoms. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The envelope, from the host cell plasma membrane [1]; glycoproteins [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The glycoproteins are coded for by viral genes, but the membrane they sit in is the host's.

**2.** HIV glycoproteins bind to CD4 receptors [1]; helper T-cells have CD4 receptors on their surface, but red blood cells do not [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Binding depends on a specific receptor. Answers about red blood cells lacking a nucleus miss the point at the binding stage.

**3.** **B** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* DNA must be made before it can be integrated, and integrated before it is transcribed.

**4.** Reverse transcriptase makes (double-strand) DNA using viral RNA as a template [1]. Integrase inserts the viral DNA into a host chromosome [1]. Protease cuts long viral protein chains into separate, working proteins [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Be precise about direction: RNA to DNA, not DNA to RNA.

**5.** Uninfected host cells do not have the enzymes needed to make DNA from the viral RNA genome or to insert it into a chromosome [1], so without its own enzymes the virus's genes could not be integrated or expressed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the enzymes to steps the host cannot perform for the virus.

**6.** HIV destroys helper T-cells, so their number falls [1]. Helper T-cells initiate the body's response to infections [1], so the immune response to other pathogens is weak and these pathogens can cause disease [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* HIV is not the direct cause of these illnesses. Explain the chain from helper T-cells to a weakened response.

**7.** The glycoproteins can no longer bind to (are no longer complementary to) CD4 receptors [1]. The virus cannot attach to helper T-cells, so its contents are not released into them [1]. Fewer cells are infected, so fewer helper T-cells are destroyed [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The shape change is the key. Mention complementary shapes, as you would for an enzyme and substrate.

**8.** The substrate (viral RNA or nucleotides) can no longer bind to the active site, so reverse transcriptase cannot make DNA from viral RNA [1]. With no viral DNA, nothing can be integrated into the host chromosome [1], so no viral RNA or proteins are produced and no new particles are made [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow the consequences forward to new particles. Stopping at "no DNA is made" earns one mark.

**9. (a)** P: reverse transcriptase [1]. Q: integrase [1]. R: protease [1].

**(b)** Long viral protein chains are not cut into working proteins [1], so the proteins needed for infectious particles are not available; any particles that form cannot infect new cells [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Work along the table. The first substance missing tells you which step is blocked.

**10. (a)** (480 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = **100%** [1].

**(b)** 90 000 ÷ 300 = **300 times** [1].

**(c)** The drugs reduce HIV replication, so fewer new particles are made [1]; fewer helper T-cells are infected and destroyed, so their number rises as new cells are produced [1].

**(d)** Viral DNA had been integrated into host chromosomes and was not removed by the drugs [1]; when treatment stopped, this DNA was transcribed again and new virus particles were made [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use integration to explain the rebound. "The virus came back" earns nothing.

**11.** A mutation may change a viral enzyme so that one drug no longer works (the virus becomes resistant) [1]. A virus with a mutation giving resistance to one drug is still inhibited by a drug acting on a different enzyme [1], so it is far less likely that a virus resistant to all the drugs at once will appear and multiply [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The argument depends on the drugs having different targets. Say so explicitly.

**12.** The virus provides: the genetic information (RNA), the enzymes reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease, and glycoproteins for binding [1]. The host cell provides: ribosomes for translation, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP [1]; its enzymes transcribe the integrated viral DNA [1]; and its plasma membrane forms the envelope of new particles [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparisons need both sides. The host supplies almost all the raw materials and machinery; the virus supplies information and three specialised enzymes.

**13.** Integrated viral DNA may not be transcribed for a long time, so few new particles are made [1]. Helper T-cell numbers fall only gradually, so the immune response stays strong enough to control other infections for years [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification's word "may" is the clue: integrated DNA is not always expressed straight away.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "HIV attacks white blood cells" instead of naming helper T-cells and CD4.
- Reversing the roles of reverse transcriptase and integrase.
- Stopping a drug explanation before reaching the effect on new particles.
- Explaining AIDS deaths as caused directly by HIV.
- Leaving out percentage or factor calculations' working.
- Comparing virus and host with points on one side only.
