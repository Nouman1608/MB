## Section A

**1.** State which section of Paper 2 is based on a set text, and
which is based on an unseen passage. **[2]**

**2.** Name the three steps of a repeatable method for approaching the
unseen passage in Section B. **[3]**

**3.** State one reason memorised quotations are less useful for
Section B than for Section A. **[1]**

## Section B

**4.** A candidate writes, of an unseen extract: "The writer uses long
sentences." Explain why this observation alone is unlikely to score
well, and what should be added to strengthen it. **[3]**

**5.** A candidate preparing for Section A memorises ten quotations
and a fixed set of arguments about their set text, intending to use
them regardless of the specific question asked.

**(a)** Explain why this approach is risky. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a more resilient way to prepare for Section A. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why Section B "carries no benefit from memorised
quotations or pre-prepared arguments," and what this implies about how
candidates should practise for it. **[3]**

**7.** A candidate revises Section A and Section B separately, each in
isolation, without ever practising the full paper under timed
conditions.

**(a)** Explain the risk this creates once the candidate sits the real
exam. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a better approach to timed practice for this paper. **[2]**

**8.** An unseen extract describes a crowded train station using long,
breathless, unpunctuated sentences. One candidate writes that this
"shows the busy atmosphere." Another candidate writes that the
unbroken syntax "mimics the narrator's own sensory overload amid the
crowd, denying the reader any pause."

**(a)** Explain the key difference between these two responses. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the second response would score more highly. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why Section B weights analysis of language, form and
structure especially heavily compared with Section A. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why tracking a set text's themes and characters
chronologically across the whole novel is recommended over revising
only a handful of memorable scenes. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Section A is based on a set text; Section B is based on an
unseen prose passage [2].

**2.** A first read for overall sense and tone; a second read
annotating specific language and structural choices and their
effects; and planning a response built around a small number of
well-developed points [3].

**3.** Because there is no set text to draw prepared material from in
Section B — every extract is genuinely unfamiliar, so a memorised
quotation or argument cannot be adapted to it in the way it can for a
studied Section A text [1].

**4.** This observation alone is unlikely to score well because it
names a technique without explaining what effect it has on the
reader's impression of character, event or mood in this specific
passage [2]. It should be strengthened by specifying, for example,
what the long sentences do to pace, tone, or the reader's sense of the
character's state of mind, grounded in the actual content of the
extract [1].

**5. (a)** This approach is risky because a question can approach the
same set text from many different angles across different exam
series, and forcing pre-learned quotations and arguments onto a
question they do not actually fit produces an answer that feels
prepared rather than responsive to what was actually asked [2].
**(b)** A more resilient approach is knowing the text's overall
structure, key turning points, and how each major character or theme
develops well enough to select and adapt relevant textual detail to
whatever the specific question asks [2].

**6.** Because Section B presents a genuinely new, unseen passage with
no way to know in advance what it will contain, no quotation or
argument prepared beforehand can apply to it [2]. This implies that
the only reliable preparation is repeated timed practice analysing
fresh, previously unseen extracts, building a fast and consistent
close-reading method rather than accumulating content to recall [1].

**7. (a)** The risk is that the candidate cannot judge realistically
how long each section actually needs under real exam pressure, and may
run out of time on whichever section they find less comfortable, at
the expense of the other [2].
**(b)** Rehearsing both sections together under full exam timing, not
just revising each separately, so that pacing reflects the demands of
the real paper [2].

**8. (a)** The first response names the technique (long, unpunctuated
sentences) and asserts an effect (busy atmosphere) without explaining
how the technique produces that effect. The second response specifies
the mechanism: the unbroken syntax recreates the narrator's sensory
overload by denying the reader any pause, mirroring the disorientation
of the scene at the level of the sentence itself [2].
**(b)** The second response would score more highly because it
demonstrates the technique-then-effect analysis the paper rewards,
explaining precisely how a specific technical choice shapes the
reader's experience, rather than simply asserting that an effect
exists [2].

**9.** Section B weights language, form and structure heavily because,
unlike Section A, there is no wider text or established context to
draw on beyond the extract itself, so analysis has to be grounded
entirely in what is present on the page [2].

**10.** Because strong Section A answers draw on the text as a whole,
demonstrating how a theme or character develops across the entire
novel; revising only a handful of memorable scenes in isolation tends
to produce answers that treat the text as a series of disconnected
moments, rather than showing the kind of whole-text understanding that
distinguishes higher-scoring responses [3].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Because Section A and Section B genuinely test different skills —
depth of textual knowledge built up over the whole course versus a
fast, reliable close-reading method applied to unfamiliar material —
the most effective preparation treats them as two separate disciplines
requiring separate practice, brought together only in full timed
mock papers. Across both sections, the habit that separates strong
answers from weak ones is the same: never leave a named technique
unexplained. After identifying any device — a sentence structure, a
narrative choice, a specific word — always ask "what does this
actually do to the reader's impression here," and answer that question
explicitly rather than assuming the device speaks for itself.
