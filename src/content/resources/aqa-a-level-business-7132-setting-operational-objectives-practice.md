These questions cover section 3.4.1 Setting operational objectives of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [operational objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives/) and the [operational objectives revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A product sells for £25 and uses £9 of bought-in materials. What is its added value? **[1]**

A: £9

B: £16

C: £25

D: £34

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an environmental operational objective? **[1]**

A: Cut the time taken to deliver orders from three days to one

B: Reduce the defect rate to under 1%

C: Reduce the amount of packaging waste sent to landfill by 30%

D: Increase the number of products offered

**3.** Define the term "flexibility" in operations. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** risk to a business of setting a strict cost-reduction objective. **[3]**

**5.** Pelham Pottery sells a mug for £14. The bought-in clay, glaze and packaging cost £5.60 per mug.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per mug in pounds and as a percentage of the selling price. **[2]**

**(b)** A new design allows Pelham to sell the mug at £16 with no change in bought-in costs. Calculate the new added value as a percentage of the selling price. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** way, other than raising the price, in which Pelham could increase added value. **[2]**

**6.** A bottling firm spends £150,000 a year on plastic. Currently 10% of this is wasted. It sets an objective to cut waste to 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving in the cost of wasted plastic. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason, other than cost, why the firm may set this objective. **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by an "order winner". **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** ways in which a strong quality objective could benefit a restaurant chain. **[6]**

**9.** Skyline Air is a low-cost airline. It is considering giving priority to a speed-of-response objective (fast turnaround of aircraft) over its cost objective. Assess whether it should do so. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how operational objectives need to be linked to marketing decisions in a business that promises next-day delivery. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** possible conflicts between operational objectives. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether environmental objectives should be as important as cost objectives for a manufacturer. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. £25 − £9 = £16. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Cutting waste to landfill reduces harm to the environment; the other options concern speed, quality and product range. **[1]**

**3.** The ability of the business to change its output quickly [1], for example the volume produced, the mix of products or the specification of a product [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A strict cost objective may lead managers to cut corners [1], for example by buying cheaper materials or reducing staff training [1], which could harm quality, increase returns and complaints, and damage the brand, so that the lost sales outweigh the savings [1]. Credit also: lower staff morale; reduced flexibility. **[3]**

**5. (a)** £14 − £5.60 = **£8.40** [1]; 8.40 ÷ 14 × 100 = **60%** [1].

**(b)** Added value = £16 − £5.60 = £10.40 [1]; 10.40 ÷ 16 × 100 = **65%** [1].

**(c)** Reduce the cost of bought-in inputs, for example by negotiating cheaper clay or reducing wasted materials [1], which widens the gap between the selling price and the input cost [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** Current waste = 10% × £150,000 = £15,000 [1]. New waste = 6% × £150,000 = £9,000, so the saving is **£6,000** a year [1].

**(b)** Customers and investors increasingly expect businesses to cut waste, so a good record protects the brand's reputation [1]. Alternatively, the firm may face laws or taxes on plastic use [1]. **[4]**

**7.** A feature of the product or service that makes customers choose it over rivals [1], for example the lowest price for a budget airline or the best quality for a luxury hotel [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: customer loyalty and reputation [1]. Consistent food and service lead to satisfied customers who return and recommend the chain [1], which cuts the cost of attracting new customers [1]. Benefit 2: ability to charge higher prices [1]. Customers who see high quality will pay more [1], which raises profit margins and added value per meal [1]. Credit also: fewer complaints and refunds; less waste from errors. **[6]**

**9.** For: faster turnaround means aircraft spend less time on the ground and more time earning revenue [1], which can lower the cost per passenger-seat and so support the cost objective [1]. Punctual flights improve customer satisfaction and reduce compensation payments [1]. Against: speed may require more ground staff and equipment, raising costs [1]. Rushed turnarounds may risk safety checks, quality and delays when things go wrong [1]. A low-cost airline's customers choose it mainly for price, so cost is the order winner and speed is a qualifier [1]. If fares rise because of higher costs, customers may switch [1]. Judgement: Skyline should not put speed above cost but should aim for turnaround speed that reduces cost per seat [1], because the two objectives support each other up to a point beyond which extra spending on speed brings little benefit [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Marketing's promise of next-day delivery becomes an operational speed-of-response objective [1]. Operations needs enough stock and capacity to meet demand across the range [1], and suitable transport and order-processing systems [1]. If operations cannot deliver, customers will be disappointed and the brand's reputation will be damaged [1]. Marketing needs to know the cost of next-day delivery before setting prices or promotions [1], and the two functions should agree the cut-off times and areas covered [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Conflict 1: cost against quality [1]. Using better materials or more thorough inspection improves quality but increases unit costs [1]. Conflict 2: cost against flexibility [1]. Producing small, varied batches to meet different orders is more expensive per unit than long runs of a standard product, so economies of scale are lost [1]. Accept speed against quality, or cost against environmental objectives, with explanation. **[4]**

**12.** For: environmental objectives can reduce costs through less waste and energy use, so they support cost objectives [1]. Customers and investors increasingly care about sustainability, so a green image can win sales [1]. Laws and taxes on pollution and waste make non-compliance costly [1]. Failing to act can harm reputation and cause long-term loss [1]. Environmental aims can also motivate staff and help to attract recruits [1]. Against: environmental improvements can need expensive equipment, raising costs in the short run [1]. If the manufacturer competes mainly on price, customers may not pay more for greener products [1]. Cost cuts are easier to measure and may be more urgent for survival [1]. Some firms cannot afford to lead on environmental issues [1]. Factors: the industry's regulation, the firm's customers, and its financial position [1]. Judgement: the two should be treated as close in importance in the long run, as many environmental measures reduce costs [1], but a business in financial difficulty may need to prioritise cost in the short term while meeting legal minimums [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say which objective is the order winner for the business in the case and test the argument against that.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing added value with profit.
- Writing objectives with no measure.
- Describing the six objectives without choosing those that fit the business.
- Ignoring conflicts between objectives.
- Using "quality" to mean expensive rather than meeting customers' expectations.
- Forgetting that environmental measures can reduce costs.
