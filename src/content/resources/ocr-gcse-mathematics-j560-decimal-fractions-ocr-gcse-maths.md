Most of 2.02 is content for **both tiers**. One statement, converting a recurring decimal to an exact fraction and back, is **Higher tier only** and is labelled where it appears.

## Fractions to terminating decimals through powers of 10

When the denominator divides into 10, 100 or 1 000, scale the fraction up instead of dividing.

```
3/25  = 12/100   = 0.12      (× 4)
7/40  = 175/1000 = 0.175     (× 25)
9/50  = 18/100   = 0.18      (× 2)
```

This works because a fraction in its simplest form gives a terminating decimal exactly when its denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5. Denominators such as 3, 6, 7, 9, 11 and 12 always produce a recurring decimal.

**Decimals back to fractions.** Read the last decimal place as the denominator, then simplify: 0.175 is 175 thousandths, so 175/1 000 = 7/40.

## Division that recurs

Short division gives the decimal for any fraction. When a remainder repeats, the digits repeat from that point.

**Worked example 1.** Convert 5/6 and 4/7 to decimals.

```
5/6:  50 ÷ 6 = 8 r 2;  20 ÷ 6 = 3 r 2;  20 ÷ 6 = 3 r 2 ...
      5/6 = 0.8333...   (3 recurring)

4/7:  remainders 5, 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, then 5 again
      4/7 = 0.571428571428...   (the block 571428 recurs)
```

A dot is written over a single recurring digit; for a block, dots go over its first and last digits. In words, 4/7 is "0.571428 recurring". Dividing by 7 can produce at most six different non-zero remainders, so the block has at most six digits.

## Recurring decimals to fractions (Higher tier only)

Call the decimal x. Multiply by a power of 10 so that the recurring parts line up, then subtract.

**Worked example 2 (Higher tier only).** The specification's example: write 0.41 recurring (0.414141...) as a fraction.

```
x     = 0.414141...
100x  = 41.414141...
99x   = 41
x     = 41/99
```

**Worked example 3 (Higher tier only).** Write 0.1222... (0.12 with only the 2 recurring) as a fraction.

```
10x   = 1.2222...
100x  = 12.2222...
90x   = 11
x     = 11/90
```

The non-repeating digit 1 means you need two multiples of x whose decimal parts match exactly: 10x and 100x.

**Worked example 4 (Higher tier only).** Write 1.27 recurring (1.272727...) and 0.234 recurring (0.234234...) as fractions.

```
1.2727...: the whole number 1 stays; 0.2727... = 27/99 = 3/11
           so 1.2727... = 1 3/11 = 14/11

0.234234...: 1000x − x = 234, so x = 234/999 = 26/111
```

"Vice versa" in the specification means the reverse too: 7/11 by division is 0.6363..., that is 0.63 recurring.

## Place value in decimals and using a known fact

Each place to the right of the point is worth one tenth of the place before. Multiplying by 10 moves every digit one place left; dividing by 10, or multiplying by 0.1, moves every digit one place right.

**Worked example 5.** Given that 36 × 47 = 1 692, write down 3.6 × 0.47, 1 692 ÷ 4.7 and 0.1692 ÷ 0.36.

```
3.6 × 0.47:    36 ÷ 10 and 47 ÷ 100, so 1 692 ÷ 1 000 = 1.692
1 692 ÷ 4.7:   1 692 ÷ 47 = 36, and dividing by a number 10 times smaller gives 10 times more: 360
0.1692 ÷ 0.36: 1 692 ÷ 36 = 47; 0.1692 is 1 692 ÷ 10 000 and 0.36 is 36 ÷ 100,
               so 47 ÷ 100 = 0.47
```

Check each with an estimate: 3.6 × 0.47 ≈ 4 × 0.5 = 2, which is close to 1.692.

## Adding, subtracting and multiplying

**Negative decimals.** The integer sign rules apply.

```
−2.75 + 4.1 = 4.10 − 2.75 = 1.35
0.6 − 3.25  = −(3.25 − 0.60) = −2.65
```

When the larger size is negative, find the difference of the sizes and give it the negative sign.

**Multiplying.** Multiply as whole numbers, then count the decimal places in the question.

```
0.07 × 0.3:   7 × 3 = 21, three decimal places → 0.021
3.14 × 2.6:   314 × 26 = 8 164, three decimal places → 8.164
−1.25 × 0.8:  125 × 8 = 1 000, three decimal places → 1.000, negative → −1
```

The last line shows why you should place the point before removing zeros: 1.000 is 1.

## Dividing decimals

**By a whole number.** Keep the point in line.

```
−9.36 ÷ 12:   936 ÷ 12 = 78, so 9.36 ÷ 12 = 0.78; different signs → −0.78
£8.34 for 6 pens:  8.34 ÷ 6 = 1.39, so £1.39 each
```

**By a decimal.** Multiply both numbers by the same power of 10 to make the divisor whole. The fraction 2.94/0.07 becomes 294/7, an equivalent fraction.

```
2.94 ÷ 0.07     = 294 ÷ 7 = 42           (both × 100)
0.0156 ÷ 0.012  = 15.6 ÷ 12 = 1.3        (both × 1 000)
```

Choose the power of 10 by the divisor's decimal places, not the dividend's. 0.012 has three decimal places, so multiply by 1 000; the dividend can stay a decimal.

## Common mistakes

- Writing 3/25 as 0.325 or 0.03: the digits of the fraction are not the digits of the decimal.
- Stopping a recurring decimal too early and treating 5/6 as 0.83.
- (Higher tier only) Subtracting 10x − x for a two-digit recurring block, which leaves a decimal behind.
- Lining up the last digits instead of the decimal points when adding or subtracting.
- Counting decimal places in the answer instead of the question when multiplying.
- Scaling only the divisor when dividing by a decimal.
- Losing the negative sign in 0.6 − 3.25.

## Exam technique

- **"Without a calculator"** or a Paper 2 or Paper 5 question: write the column method or the scaled division, so method marks are available.
- **"Show that"** a recurring decimal equals a fraction (Higher tier only): write x, the multiplied line, the subtraction and the simplification.
- **"Write down"** after a given fact: little working is expected, but a one-line estimate guards against a misplaced point.
- **Money answers**: two decimal places.
