These notes condense section **3.3.10.1 Bonding** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Bonding in benzene study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-benzene-bonding/), and exam-style questions are in the [Bonding in benzene practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-benzene-bonding-practice/). ## What you must be able to do

- Describe the bonding in a benzene ring: **planar**, bond length **between single and double**.
- Explain that **delocalisation of p electrons** makes benzene more stable than the theoretical **cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene**.
- Use **enthalpy of hydrogenation** data as thermochemical evidence.
- Explain why **substitution** is preferred to **addition**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Delocalised electrons | Electrons shared over more than two atoms |
| Planar | All the atoms lie in one plane |
| Enthalpy of hydrogenation | Enthalpy change when one mole of an unsaturated compound reacts with hydrogen |
| Delocalisation energy | The extra stability of benzene compared with the localised model |
| Aromatic | Containing a benzene ring or a similar delocalised ring |
| Kekulé structure | Ring with alternating single and double bonds (a model that does not fit the evidence) |

## Structure of benzene

- Formula **C₆H₆**; six carbons in a regular hexagon.
- **Planar**; bond angles **120°**.
- Each carbon makes **3 sigma bonds** (2 C–C, 1 C–H).
- Each carbon's fourth electron is in a **p orbital** at right angles to the ring.
- Sideways overlap forms a **ring of delocalised electron density above and below the plane**.
- **Six** electrons are delocalised.
- All six C–C bonds are **equal** in length.

## Bond lengths

| Bond | Approximate length / nm |
|---|---|
| C–C | 0.154 |
| C=C | 0.134 |
| C–C in benzene | 0.140 |

Benzene's bonds are intermediate. A Kekulé ring would have **alternating long and short** bonds, which is not observed.

## Enthalpy of hydrogenation evidence

| Compound | ΔH hydrogenation / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| Cyclohexene | −120 |
| Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene (predicted) | 3 × −120 = −360 |
| Benzene (measured) | −208 |

- All form **cyclohexane**, so the difference lies in the **starting energy**.
- −208 is **less exothermic** than −360 by **152 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Benzene is **152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable** than the hypothetical triene.
- This extra stability comes from **delocalisation**.

Method for the calculation:

1. Multiply the cyclohexene value by 3.
2. Subtract it from the measured benzene value.
3. State the result as the extra stability, in kJ mol⁻¹, and what it shows.

## Substitution rather than addition

| | Addition | Substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on the ring | Delocalised system destroyed | Delocalised system kept |
| Energy | Loses 152 kJ mol⁻¹ of extra stability | Stability kept |
| Result | Not favoured | Favoured |

Benzene's ring has lower electron density than an isolated C=C, so it does not react with bromine water or with weak electrophiles.

## Benzene, cyclohexene and cyclohexane compared

| | Cyclohexane | Cyclohexene | Benzene |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C bonding | Single | One localised C=C | Delocalised |
| Shape | Not planar | Not planar | Planar |
| Bromine water | No reaction | Decolourises | No reaction |
| Typical reaction | Radical substitution | Electrophilic addition | Electrophilic substitution |

## Writing a full answer on stability

A model answer for "Explain why benzene is more stable than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene" has four steps.

1. Benzene has delocalised pi electrons spread over all six carbons.
2. Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene would have three localised double bonds.
3. Hydrogenation data: predicted −360 kJ mol⁻¹, measured −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
4. Benzene releases 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less energy, so it starts at a lower energy and is more stable.

Include the numbers and the word "delocalised" to secure every mark.

## Quick calculation checklist

- Always write the predicted value as a multiple of the cyclohexene value.
- Show the subtraction with signs: −208 − (−360) = +152 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Say what the result means: benzene is more stable by this amount.

Example: if cyclohexene had ΔH = −118 kJ mol⁻¹, the predicted value is 3 × (−118) = −354 kJ mol⁻¹. With a measured value of −205 kJ mol⁻¹, benzene would be more stable by 149 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Exam traps

- Alternating double and single bonds drawn as the true structure.
- Saying benzene "flips" or "resonates" between two forms.
- Explaining stability as "stronger bonds" rather than delocalisation lowering the energy.
- Predicted value not shown as three times cyclohexene.
- Sign errors in the difference.
- Forgetting that the same product forms, so the difference comes from the energy of benzene.
- Stating that benzene cannot undergo addition at all.
- Bond lengths given with no comparison to C–C and C=C.

## Self-test

1. State the molecular formula and shape of benzene.
2. What is the bond angle in benzene?
3. How many electrons are delocalised in the benzene ring?
4. Where does the delocalised electron density lie relative to the plane?
5. Compare the C–C bond length in benzene with C–C and C=C.
6. What is the predicted enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene?
7. The measured value for benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹. How much more stable is benzene than predicted?
8. Why does the same product from both compounds matter in the argument?
9. Why is substitution preferred to addition?
10. Why does benzene not decolourise bromine water?
11. What does the circle in the hexagon represent?
12. How many sigma bonds are in benzene?

### Answers

1. C₆H₆; planar hexagonal ring.
2. 120°.
3. Six.
4. Above and below the plane of the ring.
5. It is between the C–C (0.154 nm) and C=C (0.134 nm) lengths, and all six bonds are equal.
6. −360 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 × −120).
7. By 152 kJ mol⁻¹.
8. Both give cyclohexane, so the difference in ΔH must come from different starting energies.
9. Addition destroys the delocalised ring and its extra stability; substitution keeps it.
10. The electron density in the ring is spread out and lower than at a localised C=C, and addition would destroy the delocalisation.
11. The delocalised pi electrons shared by all six carbons.
12. Twelve (six C–C and six C–H).
