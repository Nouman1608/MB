This guide covers the full syllabus structure of **IB Diploma Programme
Language A: Literature**, first assessment 2021, for both Standard Level
(SL) and Higher Level (HL) students. It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-subject-guide/)
and the
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by setting out what the course actually studies --
three syllabus components built entirely around literary texts.

Total teaching hours are 150 at SL and 240 at HL, split evenly across
three components (50 hours each at SL, 80 hours each at HL), and a
Higher Level essay and individual oral round out the assessment
alongside the two written examination papers -- the same overall
structure as
[Language A: Language and Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/),
but applied to a different body of material, as explained below.

## Readers, writers and texts (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Introduces close literary analysis: how a writer's choices of language,
form and literary craft shape meaning, and how readers actively
construct interpretations of literary works rather than simply
receiving them. Because this course studies literary works exclusively,
this foundational component goes deeper into literary technique
specifically -- narrative voice, poetic form, dramatic structure -- than
the equivalent component in a course that must also cover non-literary
text types.

## Time and space (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Examines how literary works relate to the context in which they were
written and received -- historical period, culture, and place -- and how
the same literary work can be read differently by readers in different
times and places. Works are drawn from a genuinely international range
of literary traditions, so this component often introduces students to
works originally written in languages other than the one they are
studying in, read in translation.

## Intertextuality: connecting texts (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Explores relationships between literary works: how one work of
literature echoes, responds to or subverts another, and how meaning is
shaped by reading works of literature in dialogue with each other. Since
the whole course is built from literary works, this component can draw
comparisons across a wide span of literary history and form -- a modern
novel read alongside a much older play, for instance -- in a way a mixed
literary/non-literary course has less room to do.

## Why "literature" alone, rather than "language and literature"

This course exists alongside
[Language A: Language and Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/)
as a genuine alternative, not a harder or more advanced version of the
same course. Students choose Literature when their interest and
strength lie specifically in sustained literary study across a wide
range of authors, periods and traditions, rather than in comparing how
meaning is made across both literary and non-literary communication.
Both courses develop closely related skills of close reading and
critical analysis, and share an identical hour allocation and syllabus
component structure, but Literature commits the entire allocation to
literary works exclusively.

## The defining feature: an exclusively literary reading list

What distinguishes this course from
[Language A: Language and Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/)
is that all study across all three components above is of **literary
works only** -- novels, poetry, drama and other literary forms -- with
no non-literary text-type component. Paper 1's guided analysis is set on
unseen **literary** passages, and the individual oral specifically
requires one work originally written in the language studied and one
work studied in translation, reflecting this course's emphasis on
reading widely across literary traditions and languages rather than
across text types.

## How to approach it

Because every component of this course works from literary texts, build
your revision around the specific works your class has studied rather
than around general text-type categories -- unlike
[Language A: Language and Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/),
there is no separate non-literary strand to prepare in parallel. For the
individual oral, remember it requires pairing a work originally written
in your studied language with a work studied in translation: start
planning this pairing well before the assessment, since finding a
genuinely productive connection between the two works around a common
global issue takes real revision time, not something to improvise on
the day. For Paper 2's comparative essay, practise writing timed
comparisons between two of your studied literary works well before the
exam, since comparative analysis under time pressure is a distinct
skill from single-text close reading.

Because the works studied span different times, places and (through
translation) different original languages, keep a structured set of
notes for each work covering its context of production, its major
formal and stylistic features, and the specific critical arguments you
can make about it -- rather than relying on general plot recall, which
Paper 1's unseen literary passage will not test at all and Paper 2's
comparative essay only partly rewards. Since Paper 1 requires guided
analysis of an unseen literary passage, deliberately practise with
unfamiliar poems, prose extracts and drama excerpts beyond your set
texts throughout the course, not only in the weeks immediately before
the exam -- the analytical skill being tested is transferable close
reading, not memorised knowledge of any one specific work.

Choosing between the two courses is usually made in consultation with a
student's language A teacher, based on where a student's genuine
interest and reading strength lie, rather than on any perceived
difficulty difference between them -- both are assessed to the same
standard and carry equal recognition.
