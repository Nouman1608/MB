These questions cover **section 3.2.4.2 Exchange rates** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: how exchange rates are determined by demand and supply, and the effects of changes on consumers and producers. There are no tiers. All rates, prices and firms are invented. For explanations, see the [exchange rates study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-exchange-rates/) and the [exchange rates revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-exchange-rates-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following would increase the demand for pounds? **[1]**

A: UK firms building factories in India

B: More US tourists visiting London

C: UK residents buying more French wine

D: UK savers moving money into US banks

**2.** The exchange rate changes from £1 = ¥180 to £1 = ¥200. Which **one** of the following is correct? **[1]**

A: The pound has depreciated

B: UK exports to Japan have become cheaper in yen

C: Japanese goods have become cheaper in pounds

D: The yen has appreciated against the pound

**3.** State what is meant by a depreciation of the pound. **[1]**

**4.** The exchange rate falls from £1 = $1.40 to £1 = $1.26.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the pound. **[2]**

**(b)** A UK bicycle is priced at £350. Calculate its price in dollars before and after the change. **[2]**

**(c)** A US laptop is priced at $882. Calculate its price in pounds before and after the change. **[2]**

**5.** The exchange rate is £1 = €1.25. State how many pounds one euro is worth. **[1]**

**6.** A UK firm buys components costing €36,000 a month. The exchange rate falls from £1 = €1.20 to £1 = €1.125. Calculate the change in the monthly cost of the components in pounds. **[2]**

**7.** For each event, state which curve in the market for pounds shifts and in which direction. **[4]**

- Foreign banks open new offices in the UK.
- UK households buy more imported electronics.
- Fewer overseas students choose to study at UK universities.
- UK pension funds buy more shares in foreign companies.

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how UK inflation that is higher than inflation abroad could cause the pound to depreciate. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how an appreciation of the pound could affect the UK tourism industry. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how an appreciation of the pound could help reduce UK inflation. **[3]**

**11.** A UK clothing retailer buys all its stock from Asian factories, priced in dollars, and sells only in the UK. The pound depreciates by 15%.

**(a)** Explain how the depreciation is likely to affect the retailer's costs and profit. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** action the retailer could take in response. **[2]**

**12.** "A strong pound is good for the UK economy." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Tourists need pounds to pay for their visit; the other options involve UK residents selling pounds. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Each pound buys more yen, so Japanese goods cost fewer pounds. **[1]**

**3.** A fall in the value of the pound, so each pound buys less foreign currency [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** (1.26 − 1.40) ÷ 1.40 × 100 [1] = **−10%**: the pound fell by 10% [1].

**(b)** Before: 350 × 1.40 = **$490** [1]. After: 350 × 1.26 = **$441** [1].

**(c)** Before: 882 ÷ 1.40 = **£630** [1]. After: 882 ÷ 1.26 = **£700** [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Pounds to dollars: multiply. Dollars to pounds: divide.

**5.** 1 ÷ 1.25 = **£0.80** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Before: 36,000 ÷ 1.20 = £30,000. After: 36,000 ÷ 1.125 = £32,000 [1]. The cost rises by **£2,000** a month [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Foreign banks: demand for pounds shifts right [1]. Imported electronics: supply of pounds shifts right [1]. Fewer overseas students: demand for pounds shifts left [1]. Foreign shares: supply of pounds shifts right [1]. **[4]**

**8.** UK goods become relatively more expensive than foreign goods [1]. Foreign buyers buy fewer UK exports, so demand for pounds falls [1]. UK residents switch to cheaper imports, so they sell more pounds and the supply of pounds rises [1]. With lower demand and higher supply, the pound depreciates [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Each foreign currency buys fewer pounds, so a UK holiday becomes more expensive for overseas visitors [1]. Fewer foreign tourists come, so hotels, restaurants and attractions lose revenue [1]. UK residents also find holidays abroad cheaper, so some switch away from UK holidays, reducing demand further [1]. **[3]**

**10.** A stronger pound makes imported goods cheaper in pounds [1]. Imported materials and energy also cost UK firms less, so their costs fall [1]. Lower import prices and lower costs reduce the general price level's rate of increase [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Each pound buys fewer dollars, so the stock costs about 15% more in pounds [1]. If the retailer keeps its prices unchanged, its profit margin on each item falls [1]; if it raises prices, customers may buy less, so revenue may fall [1].

**(b)** Any one developed: switch to suppliers in countries whose currencies have also fallen, or to UK suppliers [1], to reduce the cost of stock [1]; or raise prices on items where customers are less sensitive to price [1], protecting the margin with a smaller fall in sales [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *For a strong pound:*

- Imports are cheaper, so UK consumers can buy more and their living standards rise [1].
- Firms that import materials face lower costs [1].
- Lower import prices help keep inflation down [1].

*Against:*

- UK exports are dearer abroad, so exporters lose sales, which can cost jobs [1].
- UK firms competing with imports face cheaper foreign rivals [1].
- The current account deficit may widen as exports fall and imports rise [1].
- Fewer foreign tourists visit, harming the tourism industry [1].

*Judgement:* a strong pound is good for consumers and for controlling inflation but harmful for exporters and jobs in exporting regions [1]; whether it is good overall depends on how far it is above what UK firms can compete with, and on whether inflation or unemployment is the bigger problem at the time [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Name the groups who gain and lose before reaching a judgement on "the economy".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting demand for pounds when UK residents change behaviour, which shifts supply.
- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting into pounds.
- Calling a rise in yen per pound a depreciation of the pound.
- Giving effects on one group only in a question about the economy.
- Forgetting that many firms both import and export.
