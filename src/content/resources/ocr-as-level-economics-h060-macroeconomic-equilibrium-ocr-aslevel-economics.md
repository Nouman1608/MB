This guide covers **Macroeconomic equilibrium** in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). Within topic 1.4 The interaction of aggregate demand and supply, the specification asks you to explain "equilibrium in the macroeconomy" and to evaluate the "effects of changes in aggregate demand and aggregate supply on macroeconomic indicators". This page explains what equilibrium is, how the economy moves towards it, how short-run and long-run equilibrium differ, and how changes in equilibrium show up in growth, unemployment, inflation and the current account. ## 1. What macroeconomic equilibrium means

**Macroeconomic equilibrium** is the price level and level of real output at which aggregate demand equals aggregate supply. At that point the total output firms plan to produce is exactly the total that households, firms, the government and foreigners plan to buy, so there is no pressure for output or the price level to change.

On a diagram it is where the AD curve crosses the AS curve, labelled P1 and Y1 on the axes.

## 2. How the economy reaches equilibrium

Equilibrium matters because the economy tends to move towards it. The table gives invented schedules for Country D.

| Price level | AD (real output demanded, bn) | SRAS (real output supplied, bn) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | 660 | 500 | Excess demand 160 |
| 90 | 630 | 550 | Excess demand 80 |
| 100 | 600 | 600 | Equilibrium |
| 110 | 570 | 650 | Excess supply 80 |
| 120 | 540 | 700 | Excess supply 160 |

- **Above equilibrium** (P = 110): firms supply 650 but only 570 is bought. Unsold stocks build up, so firms cut prices and output. The price level falls and the economy moves down along both curves towards P = 100.
- **Below equilibrium** (P = 90): planned spending is 630 but firms supply only 550. Stocks run down and order books lengthen, so firms raise prices and output until the gap closes at P = 100.

Unplanned changes in stocks are the signal. This links the AD/AS model to the circular flow: when injections exceed withdrawals, spending exceeds output and stocks fall.

## 3. Short-run and long-run equilibrium

### Short-run equilibrium

**Short-run equilibrium** is where AD meets SRAS. It can occur at any level of output: below full capacity, at it, or temporarily above it when firms run overtime and workers delay retirement.

### Long-run equilibrium

**Long-run equilibrium** is where AD, SRAS and LRAS all meet. In the neo-classical model this happens only at potential output, the full-capacity level.

**Worked example 1: a fall in AD.** In Country D, potential output is 600, so the starting point in the table is a long-run equilibrium. Business confidence collapses and AD falls by 80 at every price level. The new AD schedule gives 580 at P = 80, 550 at P = 90 and 520 at P = 100.

- The new short-run equilibrium is at **P = 90, Y = 550** (AD = SRAS = 550).
- Output is 50 below potential, a shortfall of about 8.3%, so unemployment rises.
- The price level is 10% lower than before.

What happens next depends on which view of the economy you take.

- **Neo-classical view.** With unemployment high, money wages fall. Lower costs shift SRAS to the right until the economy returns to potential output of 600, at an even lower price level. Equilibrium below full capacity is temporary.
- **Keynesian view.** Wages are sticky downwards and falling prices may make households delay spending. The economy can stay at 550 for a long time. This is an equilibrium with unemployment, so government action to raise AD may be needed.

The disagreement is about **how fast** and **whether** the economy corrects itself. It explains why some economists favour active demand management and others prefer to focus on supply-side policies.

## 4. Effects of changes in AD and AS on macroeconomic indicators

The specification asks you to evaluate effects on indicators, so always translate the P and Y result into the four objectives.

| Change | Economic growth | Unemployment | Inflation | Current account |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD right | Rises in the short run | Falls | Rises (more so near capacity) | Usually worsens, as higher incomes raise imports |
| AD left | Falls | Rises | Falls | Usually improves, as imports fall |
| SRAS right | Rises | Falls | Falls | May improve, as competitiveness rises |
| SRAS left | Falls | Rises | Rises | May worsen, as competitiveness falls |
| LRAS right | Raises sustainable growth | Can fall in the long run | Lower pressure at any level of AD | May improve if competitiveness rises |

**Worked example 2: reading the indicators.** Country E is at P = 100 and Y = 800, with unemployment of 5.0%. A cut in interest rates shifts AD right and the new equilibrium is P = 103, Y = 824.

- **Growth:** real output rises by 24 / 800 = **3.0%**.
- **Inflation:** the price level rises by **3.0%**.
- **Unemployment:** likely to fall, since firms need more workers to produce 3% more output.
- **Current account:** likely to worsen, because higher incomes raise imports and higher prices make exports less competitive.

The same 3% demand-led growth gives a very different mix of indicators to 3% supply-led growth, which would come with *falling* inflation.

## 5. Evaluating the effects

The effect of any change on the indicators depends on five things. Use them to turn description into evaluation.

1. **The starting point.** With spare capacity, a rise in AD mainly raises output and jobs. Near full capacity, it mainly raises inflation.
2. **The size of the shift.** Small changes in AD may be absorbed by stocks and overtime; large ones show up in prices and employment.
3. **The time period.** Demand shifts work quickly; supply-side changes in capacity take years. In the long run, on the neo-classical view, AD affects only the price level.
4. **Other shifts at the same time.** A rise in AD may coincide with a cost shock, changing the outcome entirely.
5. **Responses of policymakers and expectations.** If the central bank raises interest rates when AD rises, the effect on output is smaller. If people expect higher inflation, wages may rise and shift SRAS left.

**Worked example 3: conflicting indicators.** After an energy price shock, Country F's inflation rises from 2% to 6% and its real output falls by 1.5%. The government could raise AD to restore output, but that would push inflation higher still. It could cut AD to reduce inflation, but that would deepen the fall in output and raise unemployment. Equilibrium analysis shows why a supply shock forces a choice between objectives that a demand shock does not.

## Common mistakes

- Defining equilibrium as "where supply equals demand" without saying *aggregate*, or without naming the price level and real output.
- Assuming equilibrium means full employment. Short-run equilibrium can be far below capacity.
- Forgetting the adjustment mechanism: unplanned stock changes drive firms to change output and prices.
- Giving the effect on P and Y but not on the indicators the question asks about.
- Claiming a rise in AD always reduces unemployment and raises inflation by fixed amounts, ignoring the starting point.
- Mixing up short-run equilibrium (AD = SRAS) with long-run equilibrium (AD = SRAS = LRAS).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** equilibrium in two sentences: definition, then why the economy moves towards it.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** show P1, Y1 and P2, Y2, and an LRAS line if you discuss full capacity. Mark the gap between actual and potential output.
- **Evaluate** the effects on indicators with the five factors in section 5, then conclude which indicator is most affected and why.
- In data questions, calculate the percentage changes in P and Y before discussing them, and quote the figures.
