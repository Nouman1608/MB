These questions cover section **3.5.10 Photoelectric effect** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: the photon model, E = hf = hc/λ, threshold frequency, work function, stopping potential and hf = ϕ + Ek(max). Marks are shown for each part. All data are invented for practice. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

Revise first with the [photoelectric effect study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-photoelectric-effect-oxfordaqa/) and the [photoelectric effect revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-photoelectric-effect-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the work function of a metal. **[1]**

**2.** What is the energy of a photon of wavelength 400 nm?

A: 2.7 × 10⁻²⁸ J

B: 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

C: 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

D: 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ J **[1]**

**3.** A metal has a work function of 3.7 eV. Calculate its threshold frequency. **[2]**

**4.** Photons of energy 5.2 eV fall on the metal in Question 3. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in eV, and state the stopping potential. **[2]**

**5.** A graph of Ek(max) against frequency is drawn for a metal. The gradient of the line is

A: the work function

B: the threshold frequency

C: the Planck constant

D: the charge of the electron **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the photoelectrons emitted from a metal surface have a range of kinetic energies. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** In an investigation, the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons from a metal is found for three frequencies.

| f / 10¹⁴ Hz | 6.0 | 8.0 | 10.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ek(max) / 10⁻¹⁹ J | 0.98 | 2.30 | 3.63 |

**(a)** Use the first and last results to find a value for the Planck constant. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the work function of the metal, in J and in eV. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the threshold frequency. **[1]**

**8.** An ultraviolet lamp delivers 3.0 mW of radiation of wavelength 254 nm to a metal surface.

**(a)** Calculate the number of photons arriving each second. **[3]**

**(b)** On average, one photon in every 500 releases an electron. Calculate the photocurrent if every emitted electron is collected. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, using the photon model, why there is a threshold frequency for photoelectric emission. **[4]**

**10.** Light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal surface.

**(a)** The intensity is doubled at the same frequency. State and explain the effect on Ek(max) and on the number of photoelectrons emitted per second. **[2]**

**(b)** The frequency is increased while the number of photons per second stays the same. State and explain the effect on Ek(max) and on the number of photoelectrons emitted per second. **[2]**

**11.** Violet light of wavelength 420 nm falls on a metal of work function 2.1 eV.

**(a)** Calculate the photon energy in eV. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in joules. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the maximum speed of the photoelectrons. **[2]**

**(d)** State the stopping potential. **[1]**

**12.** Describe three observations of the photoelectric effect that the wave model of light cannot explain. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum energy needed by an electron to escape from the surface of the metal [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. E = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/400 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**3.** ϕ = 3.7 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.92 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; f₀ = ϕ/h = 5.92 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = **8.9 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]

**4.** Ek(max) = 5.2 − 3.7 = **1.5 eV** [1]; stopping potential = **1.5 V** [1]

**5.** **C** [1]

**6.** The work function is the energy needed by electrons at the surface; electrons from deeper inside lose energy in collisions before they escape [1], so only some electrons leave with Ek(max) = hf − ϕ and the rest have less [1].

**7.**

**(a)** Gradient = (3.63 − 0.98) × 10⁻¹⁹/(10.0 − 6.0) × 10¹⁴ [1] = **6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J s** [1]

**(b)** ϕ = hf − Ek(max) = 6.625 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.0 × 10¹⁴ − 0.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1] = **1.9 eV** [1]

**(c)** f₀ = ϕ/h = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ = **4.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]

**8.**

**(a)** E = hc/λ [1] = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/254 × 10⁻⁹ = 7.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; N = P/E = 3.0 × 10⁻³/7.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.8 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Electrons per second = 3.83 × 10¹⁵/500 = 7.66 × 10¹² s⁻¹ [1]; I = ne = 7.66 × 10¹² × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.2 × 10⁻⁶ A** [1]

**9.** Light consists of photons of energy hf, and each electron absorbs the energy of one photon only [1]. An electron needs at least the work function ϕ to escape [1]. If hf is less than ϕ, no photon can give an electron enough energy, and the electron cannot accumulate energy from several photons, so there is no emission however intense the light [1]. So emission needs hf ≥ ϕ, giving a threshold frequency f₀ = ϕ/h [1].

**10.**

**(a)** Ek(max) is **unchanged**, because each photon still has the same energy hf [1]; the number of photoelectrons per second **doubles**, because twice as many photons arrive each second [1].

**(b)** Ek(max) **increases**, because each photon has more energy and Ek(max) = hf − ϕ [1]; the number per second is **unchanged**, because the photon rate is unchanged and each photon releases at most one electron [1].

**11.**

**(a)** E = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/420 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.74 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1] = **2.96 eV** [1]

**(b)** Ek(max) = 2.96 − 2.1 = 0.86 eV [1] = 0.86 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1]

**(c)** v = √(2Ek/m) = √(2 × 1.38 × 10⁻¹⁹/9.11 × 10⁻³¹) [1] = **5.5 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹** [1]

**(d)** **0.86 V** [1]

**12.** Below a threshold frequency, no electrons are emitted however intense the light [1]; above it, emission starts immediately, with no delay even for very weak light [1]; the maximum kinetic energy depends on the frequency and not on the intensity [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Linking intensity with the energy of photoelectrons (Questions 10 and 12).
- Saying electrons gather energy from several photons (Question 9).
- Mixing eV and J in hf = ϕ + Ek(max) (Questions 4, 7 and 11).
- Forgetting that each photon releases at most one electron (Questions 8 and 10).
- Using the kinetic energy in eV directly in ½mv² (Question 11).
- Treating Ek(max) as the energy of every photoelectron (Question 6).
