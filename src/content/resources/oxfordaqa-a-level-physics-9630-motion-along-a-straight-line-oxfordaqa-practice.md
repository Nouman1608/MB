These questions cover **3.2.3 Motion along a straight line** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, including Required practical 1. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Experimental data are invented for practice. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [motion along a straight line study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-motion-along-a-straight-line-oxfordaqa/) or the [motion along a straight line revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-motion-along-a-straight-line-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) first. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻² and ignore air resistance unless told otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between the average velocity and the instantaneous velocity of an object. **[2]**

**2.** What does the gradient of a velocity–time graph represent?

A: displacement

B: velocity

C: acceleration

D: change in velocity

**[1]**

**3.** A sprinter accelerates uniformly from rest to 9.0 m s⁻¹ in 3.0 s. Calculate the distance covered in this time. **[2]**

**4.** A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge and hits the water 1.8 s later. Calculate the height of the bridge above the water and the speed of the stone on impact. **[2]**

**5.** A ball is thrown vertically upwards. What is its acceleration at the highest point?

A: zero

B: 9.81 m s⁻² upwards

C: 9.81 m s⁻² downwards

D: it depends on the mass of the ball

**[1]**

**6.** A car travelling at 108 km h⁻¹ brakes with a uniform deceleration of 6.0 m s⁻². Calculate the time it takes to stop. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why the distance travelled found from a velocity–time graph can be larger than the displacement found from the same graph. **[2]**

### Section B

**8.** A lift starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 1.2 m s⁻² for 2.5 s. It then moves at constant speed for 6.0 s before decelerating uniformly to rest in 2.0 s.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum speed of the lift. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total distance travelled. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the deceleration. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the average velocity for the whole journey. **[1]**

**9.** A ball is thrown vertically downwards at 4.0 m s⁻¹ from the top of a cliff 25 m above the sea.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of the ball when it reaches the sea. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the time it takes to reach the sea. **[2]**

**(c)** A second ball is thrown vertically upwards at 4.0 m s⁻¹ from the same point. Explain why it reaches the sea at the same speed as the first ball. **[2]**

**10.** A ball is dropped from rest onto a hard floor. It bounces twice, rising less high each time. Take upwards as positive.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the velocity–time graph from release until the top of the second bounce. **[3]**

**(b)** State what the gradient of the straight sections represents and give its value. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how the graph shows that energy is lost at each bounce. **[1]**

**11.** A lorry travelling at a constant 15 m s⁻¹ passes a police car parked at the roadside. Exactly 2.0 s later the police car sets off from rest with a uniform acceleration of 3.0 m s⁻².

**(a)** Calculate how far ahead of the police car the lorry is when the police car starts. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the time the police car takes, after starting, to catch the lorry. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the speed of the police car at that moment. **[1]**

**12.** In Required practical 1, a student releases a steel ball from an electromagnet and times its fall to a trapdoor. She obtains these results:

| Height s / m | Time t / s |
|---|---|
| 0.400 | 0.286 |
| 0.600 | 0.350 |
| 0.800 | 0.404 |
| 1.000 | 0.452 |

**(a)** Calculate t² for the first and last rows, and explain why a graph of s against t² should be a straight line through the origin. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the first and last rows to estimate the gradient of the graph, and hence calculate g. **[2]**

**(c)** The electromagnet releases the ball a short time after the timer starts. State the type of error this causes and its effect on the value of g. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Average velocity is the total displacement divided by the total time [1]; instantaneous velocity is the velocity at a particular moment, the gradient of the tangent to the displacement–time graph at that time [1] **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1] **[1]**

**3.** s = ½(u + v)t = ½(0 + 9.0) × 3.0 [1] = **13.5 m** [1] **[2]**

**4.** s = ½ × 9.81 × 1.8² = **15.9 m** [1]; v = 9.81 × 1.8 = **17.7 m s⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

**5.** **C** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The velocity is zero at the top, but the acceleration is not. If it were, the ball would stay there.

**6.** 108 km h⁻¹ = 108 ÷ 3.6 = 30 m s⁻¹ [1]; t = 30 ÷ 6.0 = **5.0 s** [1] **[2]**

**7.** Areas below the time axis represent displacement in the negative direction and are subtracted to give the displacement [1]; for distance, all areas are added as positive values, so if the object reverses, distance exceeds displacement [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**8.**

**(a)** v = 1.2 × 2.5 = **3.0 m s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Accelerating: ½ × 3.0 × 2.5 = 3.75 m [1]; steady: 3.0 × 6.0 = 18 m; decelerating: ½ × 3.0 × 2.0 = 3.0 m [1]; total = **24.8 m** (24.75 m) [1]

**(c)** 3.0 ÷ 2.0 = **1.5 m s⁻²** [1]

**(d)** 24.75 ÷ 10.5 = **2.4 m s⁻¹** in the direction of travel [1] **[6]**

**9.**

**(a)** Downwards positive: v² = 4.0² + 2 × 9.81 × 25 = 506.5 [1]; v = **22.5 m s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** t = (v − u) ÷ a = (22.5 − 4.0) ÷ 9.81 [1] = **1.89 s** [1]

**(c)** The second ball rises, stops, and falls back past the cliff top moving downwards at 4.0 m s⁻¹ [1]; from there its motion is identical to the first ball's, so it reaches the sea at 22.5 m s⁻¹ [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* With upwards positive, the second ball has u = +4.0 m s⁻¹ and s = −25 m; v² = 16 + 2(−9.81)(−25) gives the same 506.5. The direction of the throw does not appear in v².

**10.**

**(a)** A straight line from zero sloping down to a large negative velocity at the first impact [1]; a near-vertical jump to a smaller positive velocity, then a straight line sloping down (parallel to the first) through zero at the top of the bounce and on to a negative value at the second impact [1]; a second jump to a still smaller positive velocity and a parallel line falling to zero at the top of the second bounce [1]

**(b)** Acceleration of free fall, **−9.81 m s⁻²** [1]

**(c)** Each rebound velocity is smaller in size than the impact velocity just before it, so the ball's kinetic energy falls at each bounce [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Lines of different gradient lose a mark. Between bounces the only force is weight, so every flight section has the same gradient.

**11.**

**(a)** 15 × 2.0 = **30 m** [1]

**(b)** Police car: s = ½ × 3.0 × t² = 1.5t²; lorry: s = 30 + 15t [1]; 1.5t² − 15t − 30 = 0, so t² − 10t − 20 = 0 [1]; t = (10 + √180) ÷ 2 = **11.7 s** [1]

**(c)** v = 3.0 × 11.7 = **35 m s⁻¹** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Measure both displacements from the same point and the same instant. Forgetting the 30 m head start gives t = 10 s.

**12.**

**(a)** 0.286² = 0.0818 s²; 0.452² = 0.204 s² [1]; from rest s = ½gt², so s is proportional to t², giving a straight line through the origin with gradient g/2 [1]

**(b)** Gradient = (1.000 − 0.400) ÷ (0.2043 − 0.0818) = **4.90 m s⁻²** [1]; g = 2 × 4.90 = **9.80 m s⁻²** [1]

**(c)** Systematic error [1]; every time is too long, so the calculated value of g is too small [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Human error" earns nothing in (c). Name the error type and say which way it pushes the result.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking s as positive for an object that finishes below its starting point.
- Treating zero velocity as zero acceleration.
- Sloping graph sections for the bouncing ball drawn with different gradients.
- Forgetting a head start or delay in catch-up problems.
- Using a gradient of g instead of g/2 for s against t².
