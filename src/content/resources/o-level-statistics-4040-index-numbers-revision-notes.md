These revision notes condense **index numbers**: Topic 8 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics 4040 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, and the matching content in Topic 10 of Cambridge IGCSE Statistics 0479. The 4040 text lists price relatives, weighted aggregate index numbers, and their use and limitations. The 0479 learning outcomes spell the same content out in more detail, so these notes follow them. For full explanations, start with the [index numbers study guide](/resources/o-level-statistics-4040-index-numbers/). ## What you must be able to do

| Outcome | One-line summary |
|---|---|
| Calculate a price relative | Price now divided by base-year price, times 100 |
| Understand what a price relative means | 100 means no change; the gap from 100 is the percentage change |
| Calculate a weighted aggregate index number | Sum of (weight x price relative) divided by sum of weights |
| Find weights when they are not given | Base-year price x base-year quantity (base-year expenditure) |
| Interpret and use an index number | Average weighted price change since the base year |
| Know the limitations | Weights age as consumption changes; the index is an average of a fixed basket |

## 1. Key terms

- **Base year**: the year used for comparison. Every price relative for the base year is 100.
- **Given year** (current year): the year you are measuring.
- **Price relative**: the price of one item in the given year, as a number where the base-year price is 100.
- **Weight**: a number showing how much importance an item has in the index.
- **Weighted aggregate index number**: one figure that summarises the price change of several items, using weights.

## 2. Formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Price relative | (price in given year / price in base year) x 100 |
| Base-year price from a relative | (given-year price / price relative) x 100 |
| Given-year price from a relative | (price relative / 100) x base-year price |
| Weight from base-year expenditure | base-year price x base-year quantity |
| Weighted aggregate index | Sum of (weight x price relative) / sum of weights |
| Percentage change in the index | (new index - old index) / old index x 100 |

The weighted aggregate formula is a weighted mean. If you can calculate a mean from a frequency table, you can calculate this: the weights play the part of frequencies and the price relatives play the part of the values.

## 3. Method in steps

**Price relative**
1. Identify the base year and the given year.
2. Divide the given-year price by the base-year price.
3. Multiply by 100.
4. Check the direction. A price that rose must give a figure above 100.

**Weighted aggregate index with weights given**
1. Write each price relative next to its weight.
2. Multiply each weight by its price relative and add the products.
3. Add the weights.
4. Divide the first total by the second.

**Weighted aggregate index with weights to be calculated**
1. Work out each price relative.
2. Work out each weight as base-year price x base-year quantity.
3. Carry on as above.

## 4. Worked reminder (weights calculated)

A small farm tracks four inputs. The base year is 2022. Prices are in rupees.

| Input | Price 2022 | Price 2025 | Quantity bought in 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed (kg) | 90 | 117 | 50 |
| Fertiliser (bag) | 1 800 | 2 070 | 10 |
| Diesel (litre) | 150 | 177 | 80 |
| Pesticide (bottle) | 600 | 720 | 5 |

**Price relatives (2022 = 100):**
- Seed: 117 / 90 x 100 = 130
- Fertiliser: 2 070 / 1 800 x 100 = 115
- Diesel: 177 / 150 x 100 = 118
- Pesticide: 720 / 600 x 100 = 120

**Weights (base-year expenditure):**
- Seed: 90 x 50 = 4 500
- Fertiliser: 1 800 x 10 = 18 000
- Diesel: 150 x 80 = 12 000
- Pesticide: 600 x 5 = 3 000
- Sum of weights = 37 500

**Index:**
(4 500 x 130 + 18 000 x 115 + 12 000 x 118 + 3 000 x 120) / 37 500
= (585 000 + 2 070 000 + 1 416 000 + 360 000) / 37 500
= 4 431 000 / 37 500
= **118.16**, so **118.2** to 1 decimal place.

**Meaning:** on average, weighted by 2022 spending, the farm's input costs in 2025 were about 18.2% higher than in 2022. Seed has the largest price relative, but its weight is small, so fertiliser and diesel pull the index more.

**Quick check:** the plain average of the four relatives is 120.75. That is higher than 118.16 because the heaviest-weighted items (fertiliser and diesel) have lower relatives than seed and pesticide. A weighted answer that lands between the lowest and highest relatives (115 and 130) is plausible. One outside that range is wrong.

## 5. Reading price relatives and indices

- **Above 100**: prices have risen. A relative of 108 means 8% higher than in the base year.
- **Below 100**: prices have fallen. A relative of 85 means 15% lower.
- **Exactly 100**: no change.
- An index describes the **average** change. It does not mean every item changed by that amount.
- Comparing two later years: use the percentage change formula, not the difference in index points. Index points of 108 and 118.8 differ by 10.8 points, but the rise between them is 10.8 / 108 x 100 = 10%.

## 6. Limitations (always link them to the data)

1. **Weights go out of date.** They come from base-year spending. If people change what they buy, the weights no longer match real spending. This is the effect of changes in consumption that the 0479 outcomes name.
2. **Substitution.** When an item becomes dear, buyers often switch to a cheaper one. A fixed-weight index keeps counting the old quantity, so it can overstate the true rise in costs.
3. **Limited basket.** New items, and items left out, are not in the index.
4. **An average hides differences.** A household or business with a different spending pattern may face a much bigger or smaller change than the index shows.

A good limitation answer names a specific item. Compare "the weights may be out of date" with "the farm now buys less diesel, so diesel's weight of 12 000 is too high".

## 7. Must-know distinctions

| Often confused | Difference |
|---|---|
| Price relative and index number | A price relative is for one item. A weighted aggregate index combines several items. |
| Weight and quantity | A weight is usually base-year expenditure (price x quantity), not the quantity alone. |
| Dividing by the number of items and dividing by the sum of weights | A weighted average divides by the sum of weights. |
| 118.2 and 118.2% rise | An index of 118.2 means a rise of 18.2%. |
| Index points and percentage points | The percentage change between two later years needs a division by the earlier index. |

## Quick self-test

1. The price of a kilogram of rice was Rs 160 in the base year and is Rs 184 now. Find the price relative.
2. A fabric cost Rs 480 per metre in the base year and costs Rs 408 now. Find the price relative and say what it shows.
3. The price relative for a school bag is 125 and it now costs Rs 750. Find its base-year price.
4. What does a price relative of 108 tell you?
5. A transport index uses three items. Their weights are 40, 35 and 25 and their price relatives are 112, 105 and 130. Calculate the weighted aggregate index to 1 decimal place.
6. A tuition centre records three items. Base-year prices are Rs 20, Rs 45 and Rs 10 per unit, and base-year quantities are 30, 12 and 60. Current prices are Rs 23, Rs 54 and Rs 11. Calculate the weighted aggregate index to 1 decimal place.
7. Interpret your answer to question 6 in a sentence.
8. An index was 108 last year and is 118.8 this year. Find the percentage increase.
9. In question 6, a student takes the plain average of the three price relatives and gets 115. Explain why this is not the weighted index.
10. Give one limitation of the index in question 6 if the centre now buys far fewer units of the second item.

### Answers

1. 184 / 160 x 100 = **115**.
2. 408 / 480 x 100 = **85**. The price has fallen by 15% since the base year.
3. 750 / 125 x 100 = **Rs 600**.
4. The price is 8% higher than in the base year.
5. (40 x 112 + 35 x 105 + 25 x 130) / 100 = (4 480 + 3 675 + 3 250) / 100 = 11 405 / 100 = 114.05, so **114.1** to 1 decimal place.
6. Relatives: 23 / 20 x 100 = 115; 54 / 45 x 100 = 120; 11 / 10 x 100 = 110. Weights: 20 x 30 = 600; 45 x 12 = 540; 10 x 60 = 600; sum 1 740. Index = (600 x 115 + 540 x 120 + 600 x 110) / 1 740 = (69 000 + 64 800 + 66 000) / 1 740 = 199 800 / 1 740 = 114.83, so **114.8**.
7. On average, weighted by base-year spending, the centre's costs are about 14.8% higher than in the base year.
8. (118.8 - 108) / 108 x 100 = **10%**.
9. A plain average gives every item the same importance. The weights are 600, 540 and 600, so the second item counts for less than the other two, and the weighted index (114.8) is slightly below 115.
10. The weight of the second item (540) is now too large, so the index puts too much importance on a price the centre hardly pays.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the division the wrong way up, so a price rise gives a relative below 100.
- Using plain quantities as weights when the question points to base-year expenditure.
- Dividing the weighted total by the number of items rather than by the sum of the weights.
- Reading an index of 118.2 as a 118.2% rise rather than 18.2%.
- Taking the difference in index points as the percentage change between two later years.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100, and giving 1.15 where 115 is needed.
- Giving limitations in general terms with no link to the items in the question.
- Rounding too early: keep the full weighted total until the final division.
