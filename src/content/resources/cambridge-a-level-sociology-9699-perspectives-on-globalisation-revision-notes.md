These notes condense section 7.1, *Perspectives on globalisation*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 4 Section A. Syllabus strands: key definitions and issues (globalisation, glocalisation, global culture, problems with defining globalisation); cultural, political and economic dimensions; and Marxist, feminist, postmodernist, globalist, sceptic and transformationalist perspectives on who benefits.

Full explanations: [perspectives on globalisation study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-on-globalisation/).

## Core argument

Globalisation is real but uneven: its dimensions move at different speeds, and its benefits depend on who can move capital, skills or themselves across borders. Essays should set the globalist–sceptic–transformationalist debate against conflict and postmodern perspectives, then judge.

## Definitions

| Concept | Definition | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Globalisation | Constraints of geography on social and cultural arrangements recede; people know they are receding and act accordingly | Waters (1995) |
| Intensification of worldwide social relations | Distant events shape local ones | Giddens (1999) |
| Time-space compression | Faster flows shrink distance | Harvey (1989) |
| Glocalisation | Global products and ideas adapted locally | Robertson (1992) |
| Global culture | Shared goods, media and ideas circulating worldwide; uneven and disjunctive | Appadurai |

## Held et al. (1999): four features

- **Extensity** — how far networks stretch.
- **Intensity** — how dense connections are.
- **Velocity** — how fast flows move.
- **Impact** — how far distant events affect local life.

Use them to show today's globalisation differs in intensity and velocity from earlier empires.

## Problems with defining globalisation

1. Is it new, or a difference of degree?
2. One process or several moving at different speeds?
3. Is it truly global, given unequal access?
4. Is the word ideological — presenting political choices such as deregulation as unstoppable necessity?

## Appadurai's five scapes

| Scape | Flow |
|---|---|
| Ethnoscapes | People: migrants, tourists, refugees, students |
| Mediascapes | Media images and stories |
| Technoscapes | Technology |
| Financescapes | Capital |
| Ideoscapes | Political ideas such as democracy and rights |

Flows are **disjunctive**, so global culture is not uniform.

## Dimensions: unevenness

- **Economic:** capital moves freely; labour much less so.
- **Political:** international bodies grow, but states keep borders, citizenship and armies.
- **Cultural:** products spread fast; meanings are reinterpreted locally.

## Six perspectives on who benefits

| Perspective | Key idea | Names | Who benefits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Globalist | Globalisation is real and transformative; trade spreads growth | Ohmae; Bhagwati, *In Defense of Globalization* (2004) | Consumers, export sectors, integrating countries |
| Sceptic | Overstated; trade concentrated in a Triad (Europe, North America, Japan); pre-1914 economy similarly open in some respects | Hirst and Thompson | National policies still decide |
| Transformationalist | Real, uneven, open-ended; states change role | Held et al. (1999) | Varies by country, class, region |
| Marxist | Capitalist expansion led by a transnational capitalist class | Sklair (2001): corporate, state, technical, consumerist fractions; Harvey (2005) | Economic elites |
| Feminist | Global feminisation of flexible, low-paid labour; care chains | Standing (1989); Hochschild; Enloe | Employers, richer consumers; women gain income on unequal terms |
| Postmodernist | Freedom for the mobile, insecurity for the immobile; fragmented identity | Bauman (1998): tourists and vagabonds | Those able to choose mobility |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Extensity / intensity / velocity / impact | Held et al.'s four features of globalisation |
| Disjuncture | Global flows moving unevenly and in different directions |
| Transnational capitalist class | Elite whose interests are global rather than national (Sklair) |
| Neo-liberalism | Policy favouring free markets, deregulation and privatisation |
| Global feminisation of labour | Growth of women's flexible, low-paid work (Standing) |
| Tourists and vagabonds | Bauman's contrast between the freely mobile and the stuck or forced to move |
| Triad | Hirst and Thompson's concentration of trade in three regions |
| Hyperglobalist | Another name for globalist |

## Worked essay plan

*"Globalisation benefits everyone." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define globalisation (Waters) and note the problem of definition.
2. For: globalist and neo-liberal arguments (Ohmae, Bhagwati); cheaper goods; export jobs.
3. Against: Marxist transnational capitalist class (Sklair, Harvey); feminist flexible labour (Standing); Bauman's vagabonds.
4. Middle ground: transformationalists — benefit depends on how each state responds; sceptics — national policy decides.
5. Judgement: gains are real but concentrated among those able to move capital, skills or themselves.

## Exam traps

- Listing six perspectives without comparing them or reaching a judgement.
- Treating "globalist" as always positive; the defining claim is that globalisation is real and powerful.
- Assuming global culture means uniformity — Appadurai's disjuncture says otherwise.
- Ignoring dimensions: a claim about economic benefit may not hold for cultural change.
- Using examples from one society only; Paper 4 rewards contrasting evidence.

## Self-test

1. Give the three parts of Waters's definition.
2. Name Held et al.'s four features.
3. Why might "globalisation" be described as ideological?
4. Name Appadurai's five scapes.
5. What does "disjunctive" mean for global culture?
6. What is the Triad, and whose concept is it?
7. Name Sklair's four fractions.
8. What did Standing describe?
9. Explain Bauman's tourists and vagabonds.
10. What did Bhagwati argue?
11. How does a transformationalist answer "who benefits"?
12. Give one reason labour benefits less than capital from economic globalisation.

### Answers

1. Geographical constraints recede; people become aware of this; people act accordingly.
2. Extensity, intensity, velocity and impact.
3. It can present political choices such as deregulation as unstoppable economic necessity.
4. Ethnoscapes, mediascapes, technoscapes, financescapes and ideoscapes.
5. Flows move unevenly and in different directions, so no single uniform culture results.
6. The concentration of trade and investment in Europe, North America and Japan; Hirst and Thompson.
7. Corporate, state, technical and consumerist.
8. The global feminisation of labour: growth of women's flexible, often insecure, low-paid work.
9. Tourists move freely by choice; vagabonds are stuck in place or forced to move, so globalisation brings freedom for some and insecurity for others.
10. Trade has helped reduce poverty, and critics wrongly blame globalisation for problems with other causes.
11. It depends on the country, class and region and on how each society responds.
12. Capital crosses borders freely, while workers face border controls, so firms can relocate but workers cannot follow.
