This guide covers section **2.3.2 Financial planning** in Unit 2 (Managing business activities) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business, Issue 1. It is about looking ahead: estimating sales and costs, finding the output needed to cover costs, predicting cash and setting budgets. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Managing Business Activities study guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-business-managing-business-activities/). Condensed notes are in the [Financial Planning revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-financial-planning-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Financial Planning practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-financial-planning-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Sales, revenue and costs:** calculating sales volume and sales revenue; fixed, variable, total and average costs; ways of improving sales volumes and revenues.
2. **Sales forecasting:** purpose; factors affecting forecasts (consumer trends, economic variables, actions of competitors); difficulties.
3. **Break-even:** contribution (selling price − variable cost per unit); the break-even point (total fixed costs + total variable costs = total revenue); using contribution to find it; margin of safety; interpreting break-even charts; limitations.
4. **Cash flow:** constructing and interpreting simple cash-flow forecasts; their use and limitations.
5. **Budgets:** purposes; budgets based on historical figures and zero-based budgets; variance analysis; difficulties of budgeting.

## Sales, revenue and costs

```
Sales revenue  = selling price x sales volume
Sales volume   = sales revenue / selling price
Total costs    = fixed costs + variable costs
Variable costs = variable cost per unit x output
Average cost   = total costs / output
```

**Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run (rent, salaries, insurance); **variable costs** rise directly with output (materials, packaging, piece-rate wages).

**Worked example 1.** Pennant Print has fixed costs of $18,000 a month and a variable cost of $2.50 a unit.

| Output | Fixed costs | Variable costs | Total costs | Average cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6,000 | $18,000 | $15,000 | $33,000 | $5.50 |
| 9,000 | $18,000 | $22,500 | $40,500 | $4.50 |

Average cost falls as output rises because the same fixed costs are spread over more units. This is why higher sales volumes can improve profit per unit, not just total revenue.

**Ways of improving sales volumes and revenues** include promotion, better distribution, improving the product, entering new markets and changing price. A price change only raises revenue in the right direction for the product's price elasticity: a cut helps if demand is elastic, a rise helps if it is inelastic.

## Sales forecasting

A **sales forecast** predicts future sales volume or revenue. Its **purposes** are to plan production and inventory, plan staffing, prepare cash-flow forecasts and budgets, and support applications for finance.

The specification names three groups of **factors** that affect forecasts. **Consumer trends**, such as a shift towards healthier food, change what people buy over time. **Economic variables**, such as incomes, inflation, interest rates and exchange rates, change customers' ability to spend. **Actions of competitors**, such as a new rival or a price cut, change a firm's share of what is bought.

**Difficulties:** new businesses and new products have no sales history; tastes can change quickly; economic conditions are hard to predict; rivals' actions are unknown in advance; and an over-optimistic owner may bias the figures. A wrong forecast feeds errors into every plan based on it.

## Break-even analysis

```
Contribution per unit = selling price - variable cost per unit
Break-even output     = total fixed costs / contribution per unit
Margin of safety      = actual (or forecast) sales - break-even output
At break-even:  total fixed costs + total variable costs = total revenue
```

**Worked example 2.** Pennant sells each unit at $8.50, with variable cost $2.50 and fixed costs $18,000 a month. It expects to sell 4,200 units.

```
Contribution     = 8.50 - 2.50 = $6.00
Break-even       = 18,000 / 6.00 = 3,000 units
Check: 18,000 + (2.50 x 3,000) = 25,500 = 8.50 x 3,000
Margin of safety = 4,200 - 3,000 = 1,200 units
```

If ink costs push the variable cost up to $3.50, contribution falls to $5.00, break-even rises to 3,600 units and the margin of safety halves to 600 units. A small change in unit cost has a large effect on how safe the business is.

**Interpreting a break-even chart.** The fixed cost line is horizontal. The total cost line starts at the fixed cost level and rises. The total revenue line starts at zero and rises more steeply. Break-even is where total revenue crosses total cost; the vertical gap between the lines to the right of that point is profit, and to the left is loss. The horizontal distance between break-even output and actual output is the margin of safety. A price rise makes the revenue line steeper, so break-even moves left; a rise in fixed costs shifts the total cost line up, so break-even moves right.

**Limitations:** it assumes all output is sold; it assumes price and variable cost per unit stay constant, ignoring bulk discounts or price cuts; it treats fixed costs as fixed at every output; it is hard to use for a business selling many products; and it is only as reliable as the sales forecast behind it.

## Cash-flow forecasts

A cash-flow forecast predicts cash **inflows** and **outflows** each month. Net cash flow = inflows − outflows; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow; and each month's closing balance becomes the next month's opening balance.

**Worked example 3.** Holloway Tiles sells half its goods for cash and half on one month's credit. Sales were $10,000 in December, so $5,000 is owed at the start of January.

| | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | $12,000 | $15,000 | $18,000 |
| Cash from this month's sales | $6,000 | $7,500 | $9,000 |
| Cash from last month's credit sales | $5,000 | $6,000 | $7,500 |
| **Total inflows** | **$11,000** | **$13,500** | **$16,500** |
| Total outflows | $12,500 | $14,000 | $15,000 |
| Net cash flow | −$1,500 | −$500 | +$1,500 |
| Opening balance | $4,000 | $2,500 | $2,000 |
| **Closing balance** | **$2,500** | **$2,000** | **$3,500** |

Sales rise every month, yet cash falls in January and February because half of each month's sales are not received until the next month. The forecast lets Holloway see this in advance and arrange an overdraft or shorten credit terms.

**Uses:** spotting shortfalls in time to arrange finance, supporting a loan application and setting targets. **Limitations:** it depends on sales and cost forecasts that may be wrong; customers may pay late; and unexpected costs, such as repairs, are not included.

## Budgets and variance analysis

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period. Budgets set targets, control spending, allocate resources and allow managers' performance to be measured.

A budget **based on historical figures** takes last year's figures and adjusts them; it is quick but repeats past inefficiencies. A **zero-based** budget starts from nothing and requires every item to be justified; it removes waste but takes much more time.

A **variance** is the difference between budgeted and actual figures. A **favourable** variance raises profit (revenue above budget or costs below); an **adverse** variance lowers it.

**Worked example 4.** Holloway's quarterly budget against actual results:

| | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $60,000 | $56,000 | $4,000 adverse |
| Materials | $22,000 | $20,500 | $1,500 favourable |
| Labour | $18,000 | $19,200 | $1,200 adverse |
| Overheads | $9,000 | $9,000 | none |
| **Profit** | **$11,000** | **$7,300** | **$3,700 adverse** |

The materials variance looks good, but it may simply reflect lower sales volume rather than better buying. Labour is over budget despite lower sales, which is the variance to investigate first.

**Difficulties of budgeting:** forecasts behind the budget may be inaccurate; targets set too high demotivate, while targets set too low invite slack; unexpected events make budgets out of date; and setting them takes managers' time.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of contribution.
- Rounding break-even output down: 3,272.7 units means 3,273 must be sold.
- Treating sales revenue as cash received when customers buy on credit.
- Forgetting to carry the closing balance forward as the next opening balance.
- Calling a cost variance favourable because the number is positive: decide by its effect on profit.

## Exam technique

**Calculate** questions (4 marks) reward a stated formula, substitution and answer with units, so show each line. **Explain** answers on forecasting should name one factor and trace its effect on the named business. In **discuss** and **assess** questions on break-even, cash flow or budgets, the evaluation lies in the limitations: question the forecast, the assumptions and how quickly conditions change.
