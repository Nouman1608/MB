This set practises key question 3.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What attempts have been
made to reduce social inequalities?* Parts (a) to (f) follow the six-part shape the syllabus describes
for Paper 1 Question 3; parts (g) to (k) give extra practice on individual measures and criticisms.

---

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *redistribution of wealth* **[2]**
- (ii) *dependency culture* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of equal opportunities laws or measures. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways free education may improve people's life chances. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** criticisms of the work of NGOs. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the view that the welfare state has created an underclass. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that legislation is the most effective way to reduce social inequality.
**[14]**

### Extra practice (3.2.1 to 3.2.4)

**(g)** Explain **two** limitations of a minimum wage as a way of reducing inequality. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain the culture of poverty theory. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways free healthcare may reduce inequalities in life chances. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways NGOs promote business in poorer communities. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain why a sales tax on everyday goods is described as regressive, using an example.
**[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** Moving income or wealth from richer to poorer groups (1), usually by taxing higher
earners and paying benefits or providing services to those on low incomes (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The idea that people come to rely on state benefits rather than supporting themselves
(1), losing motivation to work and passing this attitude on to their children (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: equal pay laws (for example the UK Equal Pay Act 1970); laws against
discrimination by race, sex, disability or age (for example the UK Equality Act 2010); reservations or
quotas in education and jobs (for example in India); affirmative action programmes.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (identify + explain):

- **Qualifications** — free schooling allows children from poor families to gain qualifications that
  lead to better-paid work, supporting upward mobility.
- **Equal access** — removing fees means ability rather than income decides who stays in education.
- **Health and well-being** — schools may provide meals and health checks, improving children's
  health.
- **Breaking cycles of poverty** — educated parents tend to earn more and support their own children's
  schooling, so benefits pass to the next generation.
- **Skills for participation** — literacy helps people claim rights, use services and take part in
  democracy.

**(d) [6]** Three criticisms, 2 marks each:

- **Dependency** — communities may come to rely on outside help rather than developing their own
  solutions.
- **Accountability to donors** — NGOs answer to funders in richer countries, so projects may reflect
  donors' priorities rather than local needs.
- **Imposing Western values** — programmes may disregard local culture and knowledge.
- **Scale** — NGOs are too small to change structural causes of inequality such as trade rules or
  land ownership.
- **Letting governments off the hook** — NGO provision may allow governments to avoid responsibility
  for services.
- **Lack of coordination** — many NGOs working separately can duplicate or leave gaps.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed discussion of both sides with evidence; 4–6 some
explanation; 1–3 basic points.

- **For (New Right):** Murray argued that generous benefits reward not working and lone parenthood,
  creating an underclass outside mainstream values; Marsland argued universal benefits encourage
  dependency; dependency culture may be passed to children.
- **Linked idea:** Lewis's culture of poverty suggests values of fatalism keep groups poor.
- **Against:** many people claiming benefits are in work, sick, disabled, retired or caring; most
  unemployed people want work, and unemployment rises when jobs disappear; people move in and out of
  poverty rather than forming a fixed group; Marxists argue the problem is capitalism and low wages,
  not welfare; the underclass label stigmatises the poor (labelling).
- **Judgement:** credit a reasoned conclusion, for example that the evidence for a permanent,
  welfare-created underclass is weak.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For:* laws apply to everyone and are enforceable; progressive tax and benefits redistribute on a
  large scale; minimum wage raises the lowest pay; equality laws such as the Equal Pay Act 1970 and
  Equality Act 2010 make discrimination unlawful; reservations in India have opened education and
  government jobs to Scheduled Castes; conditional cash transfers such as *Progresa* link support to
  education and health.
- *Against:* laws are only as effective as their enforcement; the gender pay gap persists despite
  equal pay laws (feminist criticism); laws cannot reach unpaid care or informal discrimination; tax
  avoidance by the wealthy limits redistribution; welfare services (education, healthcare) may do more
  for life chances; NGOs reach people governments do not; Marxists argue no legislation within
  capitalism removes class inequality; New Right argue some measures create dependency.
- *Conclusion:* legislation is a necessary foundation, but works best combined with welfare provision
  and enforcement. Credit a reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each:

- **No help for those without work** — unemployed, retired and sick people gain nothing (1 + 1).
- **Possible job losses** — some employers may cut jobs or hours to keep costs down (1 + 1).
- **Low level** — the minimum may still be too low to escape poverty if living costs are high (1 + 1).
- **Informal work** — workers in informal or illegal employment are not protected (1 + 1).

**(h) [4]** Oscar Lewis studied poor families in Mexico and Puerto Rico (1). He argued that poor
communities develop distinctive values such as fatalism, living for the present and distrust of
institutions (1). These values are passed on to children through socialisation (1), so poverty
continues across generations even if economic conditions improve; critics say the values are a
response to poverty rather than its cause (1).

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Treatment regardless of income** — poorer people can see doctors and receive treatment without
  paying, so illness does not push them further into poverty (1 + 1).
- **Prevention** — free vaccinations and check-ups reduce illness among low-income groups, narrowing
  gaps in life expectancy (1 + 1).
- **Ability to work and learn** — healthier adults can keep working and healthier children attend
  school more regularly (1 + 1).

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Microfinance** — small loans allow people without access to banks, often women, to start or grow
  small businesses (1 + 1).
- **Training** — NGOs teach skills such as bookkeeping, farming methods or marketing that raise
  income (1 + 1).
- **Cooperatives and market access** — helping producers join together to sell at fairer prices
  (1 + 1).

**(k) [4]** A regressive tax takes a larger **percentage** of poorer people's income (1). Poorer
households spend most or all of their income on everyday goods, while richer households save more
(1). For example, if both pay the same tax on food, the tax is a much bigger share of a low income
than of a high income (1), so the tax widens rather than narrows inequality (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the same example in (c) and (f)?** Yes, but develop it differently: in (c) explain how
education helps; in (f) compare it with legislation.

**Should I name Murray in (e)?** It strengthens the answer, because the underclass argument is most
closely associated with him.
