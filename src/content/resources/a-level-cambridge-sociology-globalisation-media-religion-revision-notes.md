## Format at a glance

Paper 4, **A-Level only** (not AS). Three named sections; candidates answer on **2 of the 3**.

- **4.1 Globalisation** — causes and consequences for societies, culture and identity.
- **4.2 Media** — role/influence of media, including relationship to identity, culture, social control.
- **4.3 Religion** — role of religion in society; sociological explanations for belief, organisation and change.

## Strategy for "2 of 3"

Study all three to a working level early, then narrow to deep revision on two closer to the exam — but keep the third fallback-ready, since an unexpectedly hard question on a "safe" section can make the third valuable.

## 4.1 Globalisation — go beyond "the world is more connected"

Discuss specific consequences for identity and culture — e.g. tension between global cultural influences and the persistence/revival of local and national identities — supported by **named sociological perspectives**, not general observation alone.

## 4.2 Media — contrast competing explanations, don't assert one

Contrast views that see media as **directly shaping** audience attitudes/behaviour against views that see audiences as **actively selecting and interpreting** content. Apply whichever perspective a question foregrounds to a **specific, named example**. Common weakness: asserting media "affects behaviour" without engaging the actual debate.

## 4.3 Religion — weigh competing explanations, don't assume one

Weigh: does religion primarily maintain social cohesion, or can it be a source of social change/conflict? Treat **secularisation** (whether religion's social influence has declined) as a genuinely debated question with evidence and counter-evidence — not an assumed fact.

## The thread that runs through all three: link back to methods

For any claim about globalisation, media or religion, be ready to suggest **how a sociologist could actually research that claim**, and evaluate the method's strengths/limitations. This connects Paper 4 back to Paper 1's methods content and is what "joined-up sociological thinking" looks like at the top of the mark range.

## Worked example: social media and youth identity

Weak: describes social media's features, asserts it "has a big influence." Strong: selects a specific perspective on media influence → applies it directly to identity formation (e.g. curated self-presentation online reshaping ideas of a stable, singular identity) → briefly names a research method that could investigate the claim and one limitation of it for something private/platform-dependent. The method link, even brief, signals joined-up understanding.


## Worked example: applying the same method-linking habit to religion

A typical question might ask candidates to discuss whether religious organisation is declining in contemporary society. A weak answer lists types of religious organisation (church, sect, denomination, cult/new religious movement) and asserts that traditional organisation is "in decline." A stronger answer names a specific claim about organisational change (for example, a shift toward smaller, more loosely structured religious groups rather than large traditional institutions), evaluates at least one sociological explanation for why this shift might be occurring (such as increasing individual choice in a more pluralistic society), and then briefly considers how this claim could be researched — for instance, noting that survey data on formal membership captures institutional affiliation but may miss more informal or private forms of religious belief and practice, which a qualitative or ethnographic method might capture instead. This mirrors the media worked example above: a theoretical claim, applied to a specific pattern, then linked to a research method with a stated limitation.

## A quick comparison across the three sections

| Section | Central debate to weigh | Common weak-answer trap |
|---|---|---|
| Globalisation | Cultural homogenisation vs persistence/revival of local identity | Treating globalisation as purely economic |
| Media | Direct media effects vs active audience interpretation | Asserting influence without naming a specific theory |
| Religion | Social cohesion vs source of change/conflict; secularisation debate | Treating secularisation as an assumed fact |

Learning this table alongside the fuller content notes gives a fast way to check, in the exam, that an answer is engaging with the actual debate a question is testing, rather than describing the topic in general terms.

## Exam traps

- Discussing globalisation as purely economic, ignoring its cultural/identity consequences — specifically what this paper examines.
- Presenting one media-influence theory as simply true, rather than one of several competing explanations to be weighed.
- Treating secularisation as settled fact instead of a debated question with evidence on more than one side.
- Zero reference to research methods, when Paper 4 questions frequently reward connecting a substantive claim to how it could be studied.
- Relying only on textbook cases from decades past — a specific, plausible contemporary example makes an argument feel genuinely engaged.

## Self-test

1. How many of Paper 4's three sections must candidates answer on?
2. Name the three sections.
3. State the two contrasting views of media influence that should be weighed against each other.
4. Why should secularisation be treated as a debated question rather than a fact?
5. What is the "thread" that should run through answers on all three sections?
6. Give one weakness of only using textbook examples from decades past.

**Answers:** 1. 2 of the 3. 2. Globalisation; Media; Religion. 3. Media as directly shaping audience attitudes/behaviour, versus audiences actively selecting and interpreting media content. 4. Because there is genuine evidence and counter-evidence on both sides of whether religion's social influence has declined, rather than settled consensus. 5. Linking a substantive claim back to research methods — how a sociologist could actually investigate it, and the method's strengths/limitations. 6. It can make an argument feel disconnected from genuinely engaging with rapidly changing, contemporary social life, which all three sections deal with.
