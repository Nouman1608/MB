## 4.8.1.1 The solar system and how stars form

### What belongs to the solar system

- **One star**: the Sun.
- **Eight planets** orbiting the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
- **Dwarf planets** orbiting the Sun, such as Pluto.
- **Natural satellites**: the moons that orbit planets.

The whole solar system is a small part of the **Milky Way galaxy**, which contains a vast number of other stars.
A useful order of size for "put these in order" questions: moon < planet < star (Sun) < solar system < galaxy.

### From nebula to star

The Sun formed from a **nebula**, a cloud of dust and gas, **pulled together by gravitational attraction**. Explain
why the centre heats up rather than just saying it does:

1. Gravity pulls the particles towards the centre, so they speed up as they fall in.
2. As more material collects, the centre becomes denser and the particles collide more often and faster, so the
   temperature rises. The hot, dense ball is a **protostar**.
3. When the core is hot enough and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei join to form helium: **nuclear fusion** begins.
4. Fusion releases energy, and the star starts to shine.

The specification's wording is that dust and gas drawn together by gravity **causes fusion reactions**. Make sure
gravity is the first link in the chain.

### Why a star is stable: an equilibrium of two effects

Once fusion starts, two effects act on the star together:

- **Gravitational collapse**: gravity pulls all the material inwards.
- **Expansion due to fusion energy**: energy released in the core heats the gas, pushing material outwards.

When these balance, the star neither shrinks nor grows: it is in **equilibrium**. This stable stage is the **main
sequence**, which lasts for most of a star's life. Questions often ask what would happen if the balance changed:

- If fusion released **less** energy, gravity would win and the star would contract.
- If fusion released **more** energy, the outward effect would win and the star would expand.

That idea explains the later stages: they begin when the balance changes.

## 4.8.1.2 The life cycle of a star

The **size (mass) of the star determines its life cycle**. Both paths start: nebula → protostar → main sequence.

### Explaining each stage, not just naming it

| Stage | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Main sequence | Hydrogen fuses to helium in the core; fusion energy balances gravity |
| Red giant (star like the Sun) | Hydrogen in the core runs low, the balance changes; the star expands and its surface cools, so it looks red |
| White dwarf | Outer layers drift away; the small, hot, dense core remains |
| Black dwarf | The white dwarf has cooled until it no longer emits significant energy |
| Red super giant (massive star) | As for a red giant, but the star is far larger |
| Supernova | The massive star collapses and then explodes |
| Neutron star | Very dense core left after a supernova |
| Black hole | Left by the most massive stars; gravity so strong that not even light can escape |

A star about the size of the Sun **never** becomes a supernova, neutron star or black hole.

### How stars make the elements

**Fusion processes in stars produce all of the naturally occurring elements.** Build the explanation in order:

1. Main sequence: hydrogen nuclei fuse to make **helium**.
2. In later stages, helium and then heavier nuclei fuse to make **heavier elements**, up to **iron**.
3. **Elements heavier than iron are produced in a supernova.**
4. The **supernova explosion distributes the elements throughout the universe**.
5. That scattered material can collapse into new nebulae, stars and planets.

So the heavy elements on Earth today, such as gold or uranium, must have been made in a supernova before the solar
system formed.

## 4.8.1.3 Orbits and satellites

### Gravity provides the force

**Gravity provides the force that allows planets and satellites, natural and artificial, to maintain their
circular orbits.** Without it, an orbiting object would carry on in a straight line at a tangent to its orbit.

Similarities and distinctions examiners look for:

- **Similar**: planets, moons and artificial satellites all move in (near-)circular orbits held by gravity; none
  produces its own light.
- **Different**: planets orbit the Sun; moons orbit planets; artificial satellites are made and launched by people,
  usually into orbit around the Earth, for uses such as communications and weather monitoring; planets are much
  larger than artificial satellites; some planets have many moons and some have none.

### Higher tier only: constant speed, changing velocity

1. In a circular orbit the object moves at **constant speed**.
2. Its **direction is always changing**, and velocity is speed **in a given direction**, so its **velocity changes**.
3. A changing velocity is an **acceleration**.
4. The acceleration is produced by a resultant force: **gravity**, acting **towards the centre** of the orbit.
5. Gravity acts at right angles to the direction of motion, so it changes the direction but not the speed.

### Higher tier only: speed and radius

For a **stable orbit**, there is only one speed for each radius. **If the speed changes, the radius must change.**
A smaller orbit means a stronger gravitational pull, so the object must move **faster** to stay in orbit. A larger
orbit goes with a **slower** speed.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 (speed of the Moon).** The Moon's orbit has a radius of about 3.84 × 10⁸ m, and one orbit takes
about 27.3 days. Estimate its orbital speed.

```
time for one orbit = 27.3 × 24 × 3600 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s
distance = 2 π r = 2 × π × 3.84 × 10⁸ = 2.41 × 10⁹ m
v = distance / time = 2.41 × 10⁹ / 2.36 × 10⁶ = 1020 m/s (3 s.f.)
```

Convert days to seconds first; that is where most errors occur.

**Worked example 2 (Higher tier only, invented data).** Satellite A orbits at a radius of 7.0 × 10⁶ m and satellite B
at 4.2 × 10⁷ m. Which moves faster, and why?

> A moves faster. A is in the smaller orbit, where the Earth's gravitational pull is stronger, so it must travel
> faster to stay in a stable orbit rather than fall towards the Earth.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the Milky Way inside the solar system, or calling the solar system a galaxy.
- Saying the nebula "explodes" to form a star. Gravity pulls it together.
- Naming gravity or fusion alone for stability. Name both and say they **balance**.
- Saying a red giant is hotter. Its surface is cooler; that is why it is red.
- Saying all elements are made in supernovae. Up to iron is made by fusion in stars.
- (Higher tier only) Saying the speed changes in a circular orbit. The velocity changes.
- (Higher tier only) Saying a faster satellite moves to a higher orbit to stay stable.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a life cycle by giving the stages in order with the correct names; **explain** it by adding what causes
each change. **Compare** planets, moons and artificial satellites by writing each point about all three. For an
equilibrium question, use the phrase "gravitational collapse balances expansion due to fusion energy". In
**calculate** questions about orbits, the distance is the circumference 2πr, and the time must be in seconds.
