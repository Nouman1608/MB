## What resource allocation means

**Resource allocation** is the way scarce resources (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are divided between competing uses. Every economy must decide what to produce, how and for whom. An allocation can be judged by whether it gives people the goods they value most from the resources available, and by how fairly the output is shared.

## The price mechanism

In a market, the **price mechanism** allocates resources through changes in prices driven by demand and supply. Prices perform three functions.

### Signalling

A price change carries information. When the price of a good rises, it signals to producers that consumers want more of it relative to what is available, and to consumers that it has become scarcer.

*Example (invented).* Demand for lithium for car batteries rises. Its price doubles. Mining firms see the signal and explore new deposits; battery makers see it and research alternatives. No authority tells them to do this.

### Rationing

When a good is scarce, a higher price limits the quantity demanded to the quantity available. Those most willing and able to pay buy it; others go without or switch to substitutes. In a market economy, price, rather than queues or official permits, decides who gets the good.

### Incentive

A higher price raises the reward for producing, so firms are encouraged to expand output and new firms are encouraged to enter. A falling price does the reverse, encouraging resources to move to more valued uses. The profit motive turns price signals into action.

The three functions work together. A rise in demand raises the price (signal), which reduces the quantity some buyers want (rationing) and raises the quantity firms supply (incentive), until a new equilibrium is reached.

## Worked example: a ticket price held below equilibrium

A concert venue has 4,000 seats. Demand for tickets is Qd = 9,000 - 100P, where P is the price in pounds.

- **Market-clearing price:** set Qd equal to 4,000: 9,000 - 100P = 4,000, so P = **£50**. Every seat is sold to a buyer willing to pay at least £50.
- **Price capped at £30:** Qd = 9,000 - 3,000 = 6,000 tickets demanded for 4,000 seats, a **shortage of 2,000**.

With price no longer rationing the tickets, something else must: queues, a ballot, "first come, first served" online, or tickets going to friends of the organisers. Some buyers who obtain tickets at £30 may resell them at a higher price, a secondary market that brings the price back toward £50. The cap is lower for those who get tickets, but it does not create more seats, and the incentive to put on more concerts is weaker.

This example shows why economists say prices allocate scarce goods: when the price cannot do it, another allocation method appears, and it has its own costs, such as time spent queuing.

## Incentives

An **incentive** is a reward or penalty that changes the pay-off of a choice and so changes behaviour. Prices are one incentive; others include taxes, subsidies, fines, bonuses and recognition.

*Example (invented).* A city adds a 20p refundable deposit to every plastic bottle. The share of bottles returned for recycling rises from 45% to 85% within a year. A small financial reward changed millions of decisions.

**How effective are incentives?** It depends on:

- **Size of the reward or penalty** relative to the cost of changing behaviour: a 2p deposit might have changed little.
- **Information:** people must know about the incentive and understand it.
- **Alternatives:** a fuel tax changes driving less where there is no public transport.
- **Unintended effects:** a bonus for the number of patients seen may reduce the time spent with each one.
- **Habits and motivation:** people may respond to fairness, social pressure or habit as much as to money, and a financial reward can sometimes weaken a sense of duty.

## Allocation in different economic systems

| | Market economy | Planned economy | Mixed economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides | Households and firms | A central authority | Both |
| Main mechanism | Prices and profit | Plans, targets and state-set prices | Prices for most goods; state provision for some |
| Ownership | Mostly private | Mostly state | Both |

### Evaluating the market allocation

- **Advantages:** responds quickly to changing wants; uses information spread across millions of people; incentives encourage efficiency and innovation; wide choice.
- **Disadvantages:** under-provides goods that people cannot be excluded from; ignores external costs and benefits; allocates by ability to pay; can be unstable.

### Evaluating the planned allocation

- **Advantages:** can direct resources to national priorities and basic needs; can reduce inequality; can take external effects into account directly.
- **Disadvantages:** planners lack the information to match output to wants, so shortages and surpluses appear; weak incentives for efficiency and innovation; little consumer choice; queues replace prices as the rationing method, as in the ticket example.

### Evaluating the mixed allocation

Most economies rely on markets for most goods and use government for public goods, merit goods, redistribution and regulation. The mixed economy aims to combine the strengths of both, but the right balance is contested: too little intervention leaves market failures uncorrected; too much risks government failure.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how the rationing function of price allocates a scarce good. [3]*

> When a good is scarce relative to demand, its price rises. The higher price reduces quantity demanded, as some buyers are not willing or able to pay it and switch to substitutes. Price rises until the quantity demanded equals the quantity available, so the good goes to those willing and able to pay the most.

*Why it works:* it starts from scarcity, gives the mechanism (quantity demanded contracts), and ends with who receives the good.

## Common mistakes

- Running the three functions together without saying what each does.
- Saying a higher price "rations" by stopping all buyers; it reduces quantity demanded to what is available.
- Assuming a price cap makes a good more available; it creates a shortage.
- Describing incentives without evaluating their effectiveness.
- Treating planned economies as having no rationing; queues, permits and waiting lists ration instead.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items often ask which function of price is shown in a short scenario. Look for the word that matters: information (signalling), limiting demand (rationing) or reward (incentive).
- **Section B:** calculate the shortage or surplus if the data allow, then explain how the good is allocated.
- **Section C:** an essay comparing systems needs a judgement about which allocates better and in what circumstances, for example consumer goods compared with health care.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is rationing by price fair?**
That is a normative question. It allocates by willingness and ability to pay, which some regard as efficient and others as unfair to those on low incomes.

**Do incentives only work through money?**
No. Recognition, social approval and convenience can change behaviour too, although Version 3.0 asks you to evaluate the effectiveness of incentives on the behaviour of economic agents and resource allocation.
