This guide covers the "Maps in association with photographs" part of section 3.3.1.1, Cartographic skills, in the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The specification lists: "be able to compare maps"; "sketch maps: draw, label, understand and interpret"; "photographs: use and interpret ground, aerial and satellite photographs"; "describe human and physical landscapes (landforms, natural vegetation, land-use and settlement) and geographical phenomena from photographs"; "draw sketches from photographs"; and "label and annotate diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs". Geographical skills "will be assessed in all three written exams". For all the skills in this unit, start with the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

## Types of photograph

| Type | View | Best for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | Taken at ground level, looking across | Detail of landforms, buildings and people; what a place looks like to someone there | Shows only what is in line of sight; foreground hides background |
| Oblique aerial | From the air, at an angle | Shape of landforms and settlement with a sense of height | Scale changes from front to back |
| Vertical aerial | From the air, looking straight down | Layout, like a map; measuring and matching to maps | Height is hard to judge |
| Satellite | From space, usually vertical | Large areas; change over time, such as urban growth or forest loss | Less detail; colours may be processed |

## Reading a photograph systematically

A photograph contains far more than you can mention. A system makes sure you find the features the question needs.

1. **Read the caption and question first.** Note the place, the date if given, and whether the question is about physical or human features.
2. **Divide the photograph.** Use foreground, middle ground and background, or imagine a grid of thirds (left, centre, right). Refer to features by position: "in the left foreground".
3. **Work through the specification's four categories.**
   - **Landforms:** relief (flat, gently sloping, steep), valleys, cliffs, beaches, dunes.
   - **Natural vegetation:** type and density, such as dense forest, scattered shrubs, grassland, bare ground.
   - **Land use:** farming, housing, industry, transport, recreation.
   - **Settlement:** size, density, building types, pattern (clustered or spread along a road).
4. **Look for clues to scale.** People, vehicles, trees and buildings give a sense of size.
5. **Quote what you see.** "A wide, flat valley floor with a river bending across it" is evidence; "a nice river" is not.

**Describe or explain?** If the command is describe, say what is there and where. If it is explain or suggest, give reasons, for example why settlement is on the flat valley floor (flat land, fertile soil, near water).

## Matching a photograph to a map

Exam questions often pair a photograph with a map extract.

- **Find the direction of view.** Identify two features visible in the photograph, such as a church and a bridge, locate them on the map, and work out which way the camera was pointing. Give it as a compass direction: "The photograph was taken looking north-east."
- **Locate features with grid references.** Use a four-figure reference for an area and a six-figure reference for a point.
- **Use each source for what it does best.** The map gives height, distance, names and the wider area; the photograph shows land use, vegetation and the appearance of the land.
- **Note differences.** If the photograph shows new housing that is not on the map, the map may be older than the photograph.

## Comparing maps

Comparing maps usually means comparing the same place at two dates, or two maps that show different information about the same area.

- **Change over time:** identify what has appeared, disappeared or grown, quote grid references and give approximate measurements, such as how far a settlement has spread.
- **Different information:** look for links, for example between a relief map and a population map, as the atlas skills in the specification require.
- **Structure:** similarity, then difference, then a short explanation if the question asks for one.

## Drawing a field sketch from a photograph

A field sketch is a simple drawing that picks out the important features of a view. It is not an artistic copy.

1. **Draw a frame** the same shape as the photograph.
2. **Add the horizon** and the main lines of the landscape: skyline, slopes, coastline, river, roads.
3. **Add only the features relevant to the question**, using simple outlines.
4. **Label** each feature.
5. **Annotate** to explain, where the question asks.
6. **Add a title**, the place and the **direction of view**.

Neatness matters only in that the sketch must be clear. A few clean lines with good annotations beat a detailed drawing with no labels.

## Sketch maps

A sketch map is a simplified map of an area, drawn from a map, from memory or in the field. It shows the layout of features as if seen from above. The specification asks you to "draw, label, understand and interpret" them.

- Include a **title**, a **north arrow**, an approximate **scale** or distance, and a **key** if symbols are used.
- Show only what is relevant, for example the route of a transect and the location of sampling sites in a fieldwork enquiry.
- When **interpreting** a sketch map in an exam, treat it like any other map: describe the layout and patterns, and remember it is simplified, so measurements are approximate.

**Field sketch or sketch map?** A field sketch shows a view from one point, looking across the land. A sketch map shows a layout from above.

## Labels and annotations

The specification asks you to "label and annotate diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs".

| Label | Annotation |
|---|---|
| Names a feature | Explains or describes it |
| "Beach" | "Wide sandy beach, built up by constructive waves in a sheltered bay" |
| "Housing" | "Terraced housing close to the old town centre, high density" |

A good annotation often includes a reason or a process, which links the skill to your knowledge of the topic.

## Worked example

A ground photograph (described in words here) shows a river valley. In the foreground is a narrow, fast-flowing river with large boulders. In the middle ground, steep, wooded slopes rise on both sides. In the background is a bare, rounded hilltop. There are no buildings.

**Question:** Describe the physical landscape shown in the photograph. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "In the foreground there is a narrow river with large boulders in its channel, suggesting it can carry heavy material when in flood. The valley sides in the middle ground are steep and covered in dense woodland. In the background is a bare, rounded hilltop with no trees. The valley is narrow and V-shaped, typical of an upland river."

The answer works from foreground to background, uses the categories of landform and vegetation, and stays with description; the one short inference about floods is linked to visible evidence.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the whole photograph** instead of what the question asks about.
- **No location words**: features mentioned without saying where they are in the photograph.
- **Explaining when asked to describe**, or the reverse.
- **Field sketches with no labels** or no title and direction of view.
- **Labels when annotations were asked for.**
- **Ignoring the map** when a question pairs a photograph with a map extract.
- **Forgetting limitations**: a photograph shows one moment, one season and one framing chosen by the photographer.
