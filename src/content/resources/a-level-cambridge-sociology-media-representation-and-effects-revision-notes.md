These notes condense heading 10 *Media representation and effects* of Cambridge International
A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4. The core debate: do media representations and messages shape
behaviour directly, or do active audiences interpret and resist them? Representation answers need
named evidence for each group; effects answers need at least three models set against one another.

Full explanations: [media representation and effects study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-representation-and-effects/).

## Syllabus coverage

9699 (2024–2026) Paper 4 Section B, heading 10: **10.1** Media representations of class, gender,
ethnicity and age groups; **10.2** Different models of media effects; **10.3** The impact of the
media on behaviour.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Representation** | How the media portray a group, event or idea |
| **Stereotype** | Simplified, generalised image of a group |
| **Symbolic annihilation** | Absence, trivialisation or condemnation of women in media (Tuchman, 1978) |
| **Male gaze** | Media framing women as objects for male viewers (Mulvey, 1975) |
| **Moral panic** | Exaggerated public concern about a group seen as a threat (Cohen, 1972) |
| **Folk devil** | The group blamed in a moral panic |
| **Deviance amplification** | Reaction to deviance increases it (Wilkins, 1964; Young, 1971) |
| **Popular culture** | Culture consumed by the majority |
| **Opinion leader** | Person who interprets media for others (two-step flow) |
| **Cultivation** | Long-term shaping of perceptions by repeated exposure (Gerbner) |
| **Desensitisation** | Reduced emotional response after repeated exposure to violence |

## 10.1 Representations — evidence bank

- **Gender:** Tuchman (1978); Mulvey (1975); Ferguson, *Forever Feminine* (1983); Wolf, *The Beauty
  Myth* (1990); McRobbie on *Jackie*; Gauntlett (2002) — more diverse images today.
- **Ethnicity:** Said, *Orientalism* (1978); van Dijk, *Racism and the Press* (1991); Hall et al.,
  *Policing the Crisis* (1978) — "mugging" racialised.
- **Class:** Jones, *Chavs* (2011) — demonisation of working-class people.
- **Age:** youth as folk devils (Cohen, 1972); older people under-represented.
- **Popular culture:** Frankfurt School "culture industry" critique vs postmodern blurring of high
  and popular culture (Strinati, 1995).

## 10.2 Effects models compared

| Model | Audience | Key evidence | Main criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypodermic syringe | Passive | Bandura (1961, 1963) | Artificial, short-term, ignores context |
| Two-step flow | Influenced via opinion leaders | Katz and Lazarsfeld (1955) | Social media blur leaders and followers |
| Uses and gratifications | Active, selective | Blumler and McQuail (1968) | Media may create the "needs" |
| Cultural effects | Slowly shaped | Gerbner cultivation; GUMG | Hard to measure long-term change |
| Normative | Influenced via norms, especially where other bonds weak | Imitation among the weakly socialised | Hard to isolate media influence |

Also: Hall's **encoding/decoding** (1973) — dominant, negotiated, oppositional readings; Morley
(1980) *Nationwide* audience study.

## 10.3 Behaviour

| Claim | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Violent media → violence | Bandura; Newson Report (1994); desensitisation | Charlton's St Helena study (TV from 1995); Gauntlett (1998); Buckingham (1993) |
| Media → crime | Deviance amplification; moral panics; copycat offending | Other causes (poverty, peers) far stronger |
| Media → fear of crime | Gerbner: heavy viewers overestimate risk | Audiences judge local risk from experience |
| Positive effects | Health campaigns, charity appeals, positive role models | Effects may be short-lived |
| Sensationalism and stereotyping | Prejudice, self-fulfilling prophecy | Audiences can resist and challenge stereotypes |

## Worked essay plan

*Practice question:* "The media has little effect on human behaviour." Evaluate this view. [35]

1. Introduce the effects debate and the range of models.
2. Against the view: hypodermic syringe evidence (Bandura); moral panics and deviance amplification
   (Cohen, Young); cultivation (Gerbner).
3. For the view: uses and gratifications; two-step flow; encoding/decoding; Charlton on St Helena;
   Gauntlett's critique of effects research.
4. Middle ground: cultural and normative models — long-term, indirect influence.
5. Judgement: the media rarely cause behaviour directly but shape norms, fears and agendas over time.

## Model evaluation paragraph

*On Bandura as evidence for direct effects:* "Bandura's Bobo doll experiments show that children can
imitate aggression they have just watched, which supports the hypodermic syringe model. But the
laboratory setting was artificial, the doll was designed to be hit, and effects were measured
minutes after viewing, so the study tells us little about long-term behaviour in everyday life.
Charlton's St Helena research, where television's arrival was not followed by more anti-social
behaviour, and Gauntlett's critique of effects research both suggest that family, peers and
community matter more than screen content. Direct effects may exist, but they appear small,
short-term and conditional."

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Describing Bandura without evaluating the laboratory method.
- Writing "the media brainwashes people" with no model or evidence.
- Confusing the **two-step flow** (people interpret for others) with **uses and gratifications**
  (individuals choose media for needs).
- Representation answers that cover only gender; the syllabus names class, ethnicity and age too.
- Forgetting the **positive impact** bullet in 10.3.

## Quick self-test

1. Who coined "symbolic annihilation"?
2. What did Cohen study in 1964 seaside towns?
3. Name the three readings in Hall's encoding/decoding model.
4. Give one criticism of Bandura's research.
5. What did Charlton's St Helena research suggest?

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best evidence against direct effects?** Charlton's St Helena research, Morley's
audience study and Gauntlett's critique together show audiences interpreting media actively.

**Do moral panics count as a media effect?** Yes — they show the media shaping public reaction and
the behaviour of agencies of control, even if not the behaviour of individual viewers.

**How many models should an essay use?** At least three, contrasted, with evidence for each.
