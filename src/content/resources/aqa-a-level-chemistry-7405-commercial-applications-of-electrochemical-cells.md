## How a cell produces a current

Every cell has two electrodes that undergo different redox reactions. Oxidation releases electrons at one electrode. Those electrons travel through the external circuit to the other electrode, where reduction uses them. Ions move through the electrolyte to complete the circuit, because electrons cannot pass through it.

The electrode with the **more negative** electrode potential is the **negative electrode**. Oxidation happens there. The electrode with the **more positive** electrode potential is the **positive electrode**, and reduction happens there. In a working cell the electrons flow from the negative electrode to the positive electrode through the wire.

The EMF is the cell voltage under standard conditions with no current flowing:

```
EMF = E(positive electrode) – E(negative electrode)
```

The EMF is always positive for a cell that works as written. If your answer is negative, you have the electrodes the wrong way round.

## The lithium cell

Lithium is a very reactive metal, and its half-equation Li⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Li has an electrode potential of –3.04 V, one of the most negative values known. This makes it an excellent negative electrode, because it releases electrons very readily and gives a high EMF. It is also very light, so a lithium cell stores a lot of energy for its mass.

The specification gives the simplified reactions in a lithium cell:

```
Positive electrode:  Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode:  Li → Li⁺ + e⁻
```

Adding them (the electrons cancel) gives the overall reaction on discharge:

```
Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻      (often written LiCoO₂)
```

At the negative electrode lithium is oxidised (oxidation state 0 to +1). At the positive electrode, cobalt in CoO₂ is reduced from +4 to +3. Lithium ions move through the electrolyte from the negative to the positive electrode as the cell discharges. Water must be excluded, because lithium reacts with it, so these cells use a non-aqueous electrolyte.

A lithium cell can be built as a non-rechargeable cell or as a rechargeable one. When it is recharged, an external supply forces the electrons in the opposite direction and the reactions run backwards, as the next sections explain.

## Non-rechargeable, rechargeable and fuel cells

| Type | How it works | Example | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-rechargeable (irreversible) | Reactions go one way until a reactant is used up | Zinc–carbon, alkaline and button cells | Reactions cannot be reversed in practice, so the cell is thrown away |
| Rechargeable | Reactions are reversible; a current from outside drives them backwards | Lithium-ion, nickel–cadmium | Can be used many times |
| Fuel cell | Fuel and oxidant are supplied continuously from outside | Hydrogen–oxygen | Never needs electrical recharging while fuel is supplied |

In a non-rechargeable cell the reactants are sealed inside. When the limiting reactant is used up, the voltage falls and the cell is finished. In a rechargeable cell the products stay in contact with the electrodes. During charging the cell acts like an electrolysis cell: the external supply makes the positive electrode the site of oxidation and the negative electrode the site of reduction, reversing the discharge reactions.

## Using electrode data to deduce reactions

The specification expects you to be given electrode data and work out what happens. Use this method every time.

1. Write down the two half-equations as reductions, with their electrode potentials.
2. The **more negative** one is the negative electrode. Reverse its half-equation, because oxidation happens there.
3. The **more positive** one stays as a reduction at the positive electrode.
4. Balance the electrons and add the half-equations to give the overall equation.
5. Calculate EMF = E(positive) – E(negative).

### Worked example 1: a non-rechargeable silver oxide button cell

Data (alkaline electrolyte):

```
Ag₂O + H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Ag + 2OH⁻       E = +0.34 V
Zn(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn + 2OH⁻           E = –1.25 V
```

- The zinc half-equation is more negative, so zinc is the negative electrode and is oxidised: Zn + 2OH⁻ → Zn(OH)₂ + 2e⁻.
- The silver half-equation is the positive electrode: Ag₂O + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2Ag + 2OH⁻.
- Both involve 2 electrons, so they add directly. The OH⁻ ions cancel on both sides: 2 on the left of the zinc step and 2 on the right of the silver step.
- Overall: Zn + Ag₂O + H₂O → Zn(OH)₂ + 2Ag.
- EMF = (+0.34) – (–1.25) = **+1.59 V**.

The reaction cannot easily be reversed in this design, so it is non-rechargeable.

### Worked example 2: a rechargeable nickel–cadmium cell

Data (alkaline electrolyte):

```
NiO(OH) + H₂O + e⁻ ⇌ Ni(OH)₂ + OH⁻    E = +0.49 V
Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd + 2OH⁻             E = –0.81 V
```

**On discharge.** Cadmium is the negative electrode: Cd + 2OH⁻ → Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻. The nickel half-equation is the positive electrode and must be doubled to use 2 electrons: 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2Ni(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻. Adding them:

```
Cd + 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + 2Ni(OH)₂
```

The hydroxide ions cancel. EMF = (+0.49) – (–0.81) = **+1.30 V**.

**On charging.** An external supply pushes electrons the other way. Every discharge reaction reverses: Cd(OH)₂ is reduced back to Cd at the negative electrode, and Ni(OH)₂ is oxidised back to NiO(OH) at the positive electrode. The cell is rechargeable because both the solid products stay on the electrodes and the reactions are reversible.

## The alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

In a fuel cell, hydrogen is fed to one electrode and oxygen (usually from air) to the other. Both electrodes are porous and contain a catalyst (often platinum) so the gases react at the electrode surface. The electrolyte is aqueous potassium hydroxide, so the cell is called alkaline.

```
Negative electrode (fuel):    H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻
Positive electrode (oxidant): ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻
Overall:                      H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O
```

Multiplying the positive half-equation by 2 gives O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻, and doubling the negative half-equation to match gives 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻. The overall reaction is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Both versions are acceptable if they balance.

Electrode data: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻, E = +0.40 V; 2H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ + 2OH⁻, E = –0.83 V. So the EMF = (+0.40) – (–0.83) = **+1.23 V**.

How it generates a current: hydrogen is oxidised at the negative electrode and releases electrons. Those electrons pass through the external circuit (and do work, for example driving a motor) to the positive electrode, where they reduce oxygen. Hydroxide ions made at the positive electrode move through the electrolyte to the negative electrode, where they are used up, completing the circuit. Water is the only product.

Because fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously, the electrode materials are not consumed, so the cell never needs recharging with electricity. It stops only when the supply of fuel stops.

## Benefits and risks to society

The specification asks you to weigh these. Always say what the benefit is and who gets it, then the risk.

| Cell | Benefits | Risks and drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Non-rechargeable | Cheap, small, ready to use, long shelf life | Waste; metals such as zinc, silver or mercury can pollute if not recycled; resources used for one use |
| Rechargeable | Reused many times, less waste, cost-effective over time | Cadmium is toxic; lithium cells can overheat and catch fire if damaged; mining metals such as cobalt has social and environmental costs; capacity falls with age |
| Hydrogen fuel cell | Only water is produced at the point of use; efficient; quiet; no recharging delay | Hydrogen is flammable and hard to store (high pressure or low temperature); most hydrogen is made from fossil fuels, using energy and releasing CO₂; platinum catalyst is expensive; fuel infrastructure is limited |

A good evaluation answer separates **use** from **manufacture**. A hydrogen fuel cell car emits only water, but if the hydrogen was made from natural gas the overall process is not carbon neutral. If the hydrogen was made by electrolysis using renewable electricity, the overall benefit is much greater.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the electrodes: the negative electrode has the **more negative** E value and is where **oxidation** occurs.
- Forgetting to reverse the half-equation at the negative electrode.
- Adding electrode potentials instead of subtracting.
- Multiplying an electrode potential when you multiply a half-equation. The E value never changes with the number of electrons.
- Leaving electrons in the overall equation.
- Saying a fuel cell "stores" energy; it converts the energy of fuel supplied from outside.
- Claiming a hydrogen fuel cell has no environmental impact without mentioning how the hydrogen is made.
- Writing "Li⁺ is oxidised"; it is lithium atoms that are oxidised, and the ion is the product.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Deduce the reaction**: show which half-equation is reversed and give the overall equation with electrons cancelled.
- **Calculate the EMF**: show E(positive) – E(negative) with signs, then the answer with a sign and unit.
- **Explain how a current is generated**: say where electrons are released, where they go and what ions do in the electrolyte.
- **Evaluate or discuss**: give at least one benefit and one risk, each linked to the cell in the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can a fuel cell run for longer than a normal battery of the same size?**
Its reactants are stored outside the cell and supplied continuously, so the electrodes do not run out of material. The cell lasts as long as fuel and oxygen keep arriving.

**Why must the electrodes in a fuel cell be porous?**
A large surface area, with catalyst on it, lets gas, electrolyte and electrode meet so the reaction is fast.
