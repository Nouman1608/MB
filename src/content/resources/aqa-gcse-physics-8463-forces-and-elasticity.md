## Forces that change shape

To stretch, bend or compress a **stationary** object, **more than one force** must act on it. A single
force on its own would give the object a resultant force, so it would accelerate in the direction of that
force rather than stay still and change shape. With two or more forces acting, the object stays where it is
and the forces deform it.

| Change of shape | Forces involved (example) |
|---|---|
| Stretching a spring hung from a clamp | Weight of the masses pulling down; the clamp pulling up at the top |
| Stretching a resistance band | Your two hands pulling in opposite directions |
| Bending a ruler across two books | Your push down in the middle; the books pushing up at each end |
| Compressing a car's suspension spring | The car's weight pushing down; the axle pushing up |
| Squashing a sponge | Your fingers pushing in from both sides |

## Elastic and inelastic deformation

- **Elastic deformation**: the object returns to its original length and shape when the forces are
  removed. A spring with a small load is elastically deformed.
- **Inelastic deformation**: the object does **not** return to its original length and shape when the
  forces are removed. A spring that has been overstretched stays longer; modelling clay stays squashed.

You can test for inelastic deformation by removing the load and measuring the length again. If it has
not returned to its original length, the deformation was inelastic.

## F = k e

The **extension** of an elastic object, such as a spring, is **directly proportional** to the force
applied, **provided that the limit of proportionality is not exceeded**.

```
force = spring constant × extension
F = k e      F in N,  k in N/m,  e in m          (recall this equation)
```

- **Extension** = stretched length − original (unstretched) length. It is not the total length.
- The same relationship applies to **compression**, where e is how much shorter the object has become.
- The **spring constant**, k, measures stiffness: a stiffer spring has a larger k and needs more force for
  each metre of extension.

**Worked example 1.** A spring's natural length is 12.0 cm. With a 3.0 N load its length is 19.5 cm.
Find the spring constant, and predict the extension with a 2.0 N load.

```
e = 19.5 − 12.0 = 7.5 cm = 0.075 m
k = F / e = 3.0 / 0.075 = 40 N/m
for 2.0 N:  e = F / k = 2.0 / 40 = 0.050 m = 5.0 cm
```

The prediction is valid only if 2.0 N is below the limit of proportionality, which it must be if 3.0 N
still gave a proportional extension.

## Linear and non-linear relationships

| Linear | Non-linear |
|---|---|
| Force–extension graph is a **straight line through the origin** | Graph **curves** |
| Equal increases in force give equal increases in extension | Equal increases in force give bigger (or smaller) increases in extension |
| Below the limit of proportionality | Beyond the limit of proportionality, or for materials such as rubber bands |

The **limit of proportionality** is the point where the graph stops being a straight line.

### Reading the gradient

- If **force is on the y-axis** and extension on the x-axis, the gradient of the straight section **is** k.
- If **extension is on the y-axis**, the gradient is 1/k, so k = 1 / gradient.

Check which way round the axes are before you use the gradient.

## Elastic potential energy

A force that stretches or compresses a spring does **work**, and **elastic potential energy** is stored in
the spring. Provided the spring is **not inelastically deformed**, the work done on the spring equals the
elastic potential energy stored.

```
elastic potential energy = 0.5 × spring constant × (extension)²
Eₑ = ½ k e²          (on the equation sheet; use up to the limit of proportionality)
```

**Worked example 2.** A spring with k = 250 N/m is stretched by 8.0 cm. Find the energy stored.

```
e = 8.0 cm = 0.080 m
Eₑ = 0.5 × 250 × 0.080² = 0.5 × 250 × 0.0064 = 0.80 J
```

Check with the graph: the force at this extension is F = k e = 250 × 0.080 = 20 N. The area under a straight
force–extension line is a triangle, ½ × 20 × 0.080 = 0.80 J. Both methods agree because the work done equals
the energy stored.

Note the square: doubling the extension makes the stored energy **four** times as large.

## Required practical 6

**Aim:** investigate the relationship between force and extension for a spring.

**Method**

1. Clamp a spring to a stand, with a metre rule clamped vertically beside it. Fix the stand to the bench
   (with a G-clamp or a heavy weight) so it cannot topple.
2. Attach a pointer (for example a horizontal splint) to the bottom of the spring so the reading is taken at
   the same point each time.
3. Record the unstretched length (or the pointer reading with no load).
4. Hang a mass hanger and add masses one at a time, for example 100 g (weight about 1 N) each time. After
   each addition, let the spring stop moving, then record the pointer reading with your eye level with it.
5. Calculate each extension: reading − original reading.
6. Plot force (weight of the masses) against extension and draw a line of best fit.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | Force (weight of the masses) |
| Dependent | Extension |
| Control | The same spring; the same point of measurement |

**Reducing errors:** reading at eye level avoids parallax error; the pointer makes readings consistent;
removing the masses at the end and rechecking the length shows whether the spring was inelastically
deformed. **Safety:** wear eye protection in case the spring snaps or flies off, and keep feet clear of the
masses.

### Interpreting results (worked example 3)

| Force (N) | 0 | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extension (mm) | 0 | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 | 63 | 85 |

```
each 0.50 N adds 12 mm up to 2.00 N  →  proportional (linear) up to 2.00 N
from 2.00 N to 2.50 N the extension rises by 15 mm, then by 22 mm  →  non-linear
limit of proportionality: between 2.00 N and 2.50 N
k = 2.00 / 0.048 = 41.7 N/m ≈ 42 N/m   (use a point on the straight section)
```

Use only the linear section to calculate k, and convert millimetres to metres first.

## Common mistakes

- Using the total length instead of the extension.
- Forgetting to convert cm or mm to m before using F = k e or Eₑ = ½ k e².
- Squaring k, or forgetting to square e, in Eₑ = ½ k e².
- Using Eₑ = ½ k e² beyond the limit of proportionality.
- Calling any curved graph "inelastic". A curve shows a **non-linear** relationship; inelastic deformation is
  shown by the object not returning to its original length.
- Saying one force can bend a stationary ruler.
- Using the gradient as k when extension is on the y-axis.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** why more than one force is needed: use the idea of resultant force and acceleration.
- **Describe** an investigation: give apparatus, steps in order, the measurements, how to calculate the
  extension, and a safety point.
- **Determine** the limit of proportionality: quote the data or the graph point where proportionality ends.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, convert units, substitute and give the unit (N/m or J).
