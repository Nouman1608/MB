## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actual growth | Percentage change in real GDP over a period |
| Potential growth | Rate of increase in productive capacity (LRAS or PPF shifts right) |
| Export-led growth | Growth driven mainly by rising exports |
| FDI | Foreign direct investment: a foreign firm builds or buys productive capacity at home |
| Net migration | Immigration minus emigration |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input, such as output per worker or per hour |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |

## Formulas

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | (new real GDP - old real GDP) / old real GDP x 100 |
| AD | C + I + G + (X - M) |
| Contribution of a component | change in component / old GDP x 100 (percentage points) |
| GDP | employment x output per worker |
| Growth (approx.) | employment growth + productivity growth |
| Net investment | gross investment - depreciation |
| Real GDP per head | real GDP / population |
| Extra imports | change in income x marginal propensity to import |
| Extra tax | tax share of GDP x change in GDP |

## Method in steps: growth and contribution questions

1. Write the formula.
2. Substitute with units and the correct base year.
3. Give the answer to one decimal place unless told otherwise, with "%" or "percentage points".
4. Say what it means in context, for example "faster than potential growth".

## Actual versus potential growth

- Actual growth: movement of AD along or against a given AS. Potential growth: shift of LRAS or the PPF.
- Actual growth above potential growth is sustainable only while there is spare capacity.
- Illustration: actual 4.5% and potential 3.0% means spare capacity is being used up.

## Causes: one-line chains

- **AD components:** C rises (confidence, lower interest rates), I rises (expected profit), G rises (policy), net exports rise (competitiveness, foreign incomes) so AD shifts right, so real GDP rises.
- **Export-led:** bigger market, scale economies, earnings pay for imported capital, competition raises efficiency. Risk: exposure to foreign demand and exchange rates.
- **Investment and FDI:** capital stock rises, so capacity and productivity rise; FDI also brings technology and skills. Risk: profit outflows and crowding out.
- **Innovation:** new products and methods, so more output per input. Needs research spending, skills and incentives; arrives with a lag.
- **Labour force and net migration:** more workers, so capacity rises. GDP per head rises only if the extra workers are productive and capital keeps pace.
- **Competition:** pressure to cut waste and innovate. Too little competition allows complacency; very intense competition may squeeze the profits needed for investment.
- **Productivity:** sustained growth in GDP per head depends on it, since the labour force cannot grow forever.

## Benefits

| Benefit | Chain | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Living standards | More output per person | Must beat population growth; distribution matters |
| Unemployment | More output needs more labour | Structural unemployment remains |
| Profits | Higher sales, spread fixed costs | Rising wage costs can offset |
| Investment | Profits and confidence | Depends on expectations and interest rates |
| Tax revenue | Larger tax base | Depends on tax system and evasion |
| Public services | Extra revenue funds spending | Government may choose other priorities |

## Costs

| Cost | Chain | Offset |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | Resources for investment cannot be consumed now | Future consumption is higher |
| Environment | Resource use, pollution, negative externalities | Cleaner technology, regulation |
| Trade deficit | Higher income pulls in imports | Strong exports or lower import share |
| Inequality | Gains uneven across groups | Progressive tax, benefits, skills |
| Inflation | AD outpaces potential output | Faster growth of capacity |

## Must-know distinctions

- Actual growth versus potential growth.
- Real GDP growth versus growth in GDP per head.
- Domestic investment versus FDI.
- Growth in the labour force versus growth in productivity.
- Percentage change versus percentage points.

## Evaluation toolkit

Use these questions to turn a list into a judgement.

- **Source:** is growth driven by consumption and borrowing, or by investment and exports? Investment-led growth also raises capacity, so it is more likely to last.
- **Speed:** growth faster than potential growth tends to bring inflation and a wider trade deficit; growth at or below the trend is safer but slower.
- **Distribution:** who gains? Average income can rise while the lowest incomes barely move (for example +2% for the bottom group and +10% for the top group).
- **Time:** costs such as pollution may arrive later than the benefits, and benefits such as higher investment build up over years.
- **Context:** a country with high unemployment and idle capacity gains more from extra AD than one already at full capacity.
- **Policy response:** taxes, regulation, training and exchange-rate policy can reduce a cost or spread a benefit.

A good conclusion picks a side and says what it depends on: "Growth is most likely to raise living standards when it is investment-led, faster than population growth and shared across income groups."

## Worked chains to copy

**Cause: net migration.** Net inflow of working-age people, so the labour force rises (an 80,000 inflow into 10 million is 0.8%), so potential output rises if they are employed, so LRAS shifts right. Limit: capital must grow too, or output per worker falls.

**Benefit: tax revenue.** Real GDP rises, so incomes and spending rise, so revenue from income tax and sales taxes rises, so the government can spend more or borrow less. Limit: depends on tax rates and compliance.

**Cost: trade deficit.** Incomes rise, so households buy more imports (marginal propensity to import of 0.35 on a 36 rise in income gives 12.6 more imports), so the deficit widens unless exports rise by as much. Limit: a deficit financed by capital inflows may be sustainable for a while.

**Cost: inflation.** AD grows faster than capacity, so firms compete for scarce labour and materials, so wages and costs rise, so prices rise. Limit: if growth comes from higher capacity, inflation need not follow.

## Exam technique

- Define the key term in the first sentence, then develop one point per paragraph.
- Use the data in the item: quote a figure and say what it shows.
- In calculations, show the formula, the substitution and the unit.
- Draw a labelled diagram when asked, and refer to it. For actual growth, shift AD along a given AS curve. For potential growth, shift LRAS right.
- Keep evaluation separate from analysis, and end with a reasoned conclusion.

## Quick self-test

1. Real GDP rises from 1,500 to 1,560. Calculate the growth rate.
2. State which of AD or LRAS shifts for potential growth.
3. Investment rises by 15 when GDP was 1,500. Calculate its contribution to growth.
4. A labour force grows 1.5% and output per worker grows 2.5%. Estimate GDP growth.
5. Define net migration.
6. Tax is 25% of GDP and GDP rises by 60. Calculate the extra tax.
7. Give two reasons why FDI may raise potential growth.
8. Gross investment is 90 and depreciation is 70. Calculate net investment.
9. GDP rises 6% and population 2%. Calculate approximate growth in GDP per head.
10. Give one reason why growth may widen the trade deficit.

### Answers

1. 60/1,500 = 4.0%.
2. LRAS (or the PPF).
3. 15/1,500 = 1.0 percentage point.
4. About 4.0% (1.5 + 2.5).
5. Immigration minus emigration.
6. 0.25 x 60 = 15.
7. It adds capital; it can transfer technology and skills (also export links).
8. 90 - 70 = 20.
9. 1.06/1.02 - 1 = 3.9% (approximately 4%).
10. Higher incomes raise spending on imports.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong base year when calculating growth.
- Writing "GDP" and not "real GDP".
- Giving a list of causes with no chain of reasoning.
- Confusing a shift of AD with a shift of LRAS.
- Citing FDI as a benefit without any limit such as profit outflows.
- Concluding "growth is good" without weighing costs.
- Forgetting that the same growth rate has different consequences depending on its source.
- Missing the context in a data question, such as the country's stage of development.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does growth from higher AD shift the AD curve or LRAS?**
Actual growth from a rise in consumption, investment, government spending or net exports is a shift of AD. Potential growth from more or better factors of production is a shift of LRAS; confusing the two is a common error.

**What are the opportunity costs of economic growth?**
Resources used for investment or training to raise future output cannot be used for current consumption, so faster growth can mean lower living standards now.

**Why does productivity matter most for long-run growth?**
A country cannot keep adding workers, but productivity can keep rising. Real GDP growth is roughly the growth of employment plus the growth of output per worker.

**Does economic growth always cause inflation or inequality?**
No. Both depend on the source and speed of growth. Inflation builds if AD rises faster than potential output, and inequality rises only if the gains go mainly to higher earners.
