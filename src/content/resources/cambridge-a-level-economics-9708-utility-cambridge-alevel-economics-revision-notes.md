## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Utility | Satisfaction gained from consuming a good or service |
| Total utility (TU) | Satisfaction from all units consumed in a period |
| Marginal utility (MU) | Change in TU from consuming one more unit |
| Diminishing marginal utility | Each extra unit consumed in a period adds less satisfaction than the one before |
| Equi-marginal principle | Utility is maximised when MU per unit of money is equal across goods and income is fully spent |
| Rational consumer | One who aims to maximise utility from a given income using full information |
| Marginal utility of money | The satisfaction from one more unit of money, assumed constant |

## Formulae

```
MU                = change in TU / change in quantity
TU of n units     = sum of MU of units 1 to n
Equi-marginal     MU(X) / P(X) = MU(Y) / P(Y) = ... and income fully spent
```

## Reading a TU and MU table

- MU positive → TU rising.
- MU = 0 → TU at its maximum.
- MU negative → TU falling.
- MU falling with each unit → diminishing marginal utility.

Quick example: TU = 25, 45, 60, 70, 75. MU = 25, 20, 15, 10, 5.

## Finding the best bundle from a table

1. Divide each unit's MU by its price.
2. Buy units in order of the highest ratio.
3. Stop when income is spent.
4. Check: the ratios of the last units bought are equal (or as close as the table allows).
5. Add up TU and, if asked, compare with another affordable bundle.

## Why equal ratios maximise utility

If the last unit of money on X gives more utils than the last on Y, switching money from Y to X raises TU. Diminishing MU means the ratio for X falls and that for Y rises as you switch, until they are equal. Then no switch helps.

## Deriving demand

```
Price of X falls
-> MU(X)/P(X) rises above MU(Y)/P(Y)
-> consumer buys more X
-> MU(X) diminishes until the ratios are equal again
-> lower price, larger quantity: a point further down the demand curve
```

Repeat at several prices and plot price against quantity.

## Assumptions and limitations

| Assumption | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Utility can be measured | Utils are invented and cannot be observed |
| Perfect information | Consumers often do not know how much they will enjoy a good |
| Consistent preferences | Tastes change with fashion, advertising and peers |
| Deliberate calculation | Habit and impulse drive many purchases |
| Goods independent | Complements and substitutes affect each other's MU |
| MU of money constant | Large price changes alter the value of money to the consumer |
| Diminishing MU applies | Addictive goods may show rising desire for more |

**Evaluation line:** the assumptions are unrealistic, but the main prediction, that quantity demanded rises when price falls, is widely observed.

## Exam traps

- TU falls only when MU is negative, not whenever MU falls.
- Compare MU per unit of money, not MU, when prices differ.
- A bundle that satisfies equal ratios but does not use the whole income is not the answer.
- State the time period for diminishing MU.
- Give the switching argument, not just the formula.
- Link each limitation to the prediction it weakens.

## Self-test

1. Define marginal utility.
2. TU from 1, 2 and 3 units is 40, 70 and 90. Calculate MU of the third unit.
3. MU of four units is 18, 14, 10 and 6. Calculate TU of four units.
4. At what point is TU at its maximum?
5. State the equi-marginal principle.
6. The last unit of X gives 30 utils at a price of 5; the last unit of Y gives 24 utils at a price of 3. Should the consumer buy more X or more Y?
7. Explain why the consumer in question 6 gains by switching.
8. What happens to the MU per unit of money of every unit of X when its price halves?
9. Why does a fall in price lead a utility-maximising consumer to buy more?
10. Name two assumptions of rational behaviour.
11. Give one reason why the marginal utility of money may not be constant.
12. Give one way in which habit weakens marginal utility theory.

**Answers:** 1. The change in total utility from consuming one more unit of a good. 2. 90 − 70 = 20 utils. 3. 18 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 48 utils. 4. Where MU is zero. 5. Utility is maximised when MU per unit of money is equal for all goods and income is fully spent. 6. More Y: 24 ÷ 3 = 8 utils per unit of money against 30 ÷ 5 = 6 for X. 7. Moving one unit of money from X to Y loses about 6 utils and gains about 8, so TU rises. 8. It doubles. 9. The MU per unit of money of X rises above that of other goods, so buying more X raises TU until diminishing MU restores equal ratios. 10. Any two of: aims to maximise utility, full information, consistent preferences, utility measurable, MU of money constant. 11. A large price change alters real income, so each unit of money is worth more or less to the consumer. 12. Consumers buy out of routine without comparing MU per unit of money, so they may not respond to price changes as the theory predicts.
