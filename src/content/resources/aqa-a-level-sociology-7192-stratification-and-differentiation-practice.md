These questions cover the Stratification and Differentiation topic (section 4.2.8) of the AQA AS and A-level Sociology (7191, 7192) specification, Version 1.2 (14 October 2021). The topic is assessed in Paper 2: Topics in Sociology, Section B, where you answer on "one from option 2: 4.2.5, 4.2.6, 4.2.7 or 4.2.8". All towns, firms, people and data below are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "life chances". **[2]**

**2.** Outline two differences between Marxist and Weberian views of social class. **[4]**

**3.** Outline two problems of using occupation to measure social class. **[4]**

**4.** Read the fictional data below.

> **Lornbury mobility survey (fictional).** A survey of 1,000 adults in the fictional town of Lornbury compared each person's class with their parent's class.

| Class of origin (parent) | Now service class | Now intermediate class | Now working class | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Service | 120 | 50 | 30 | 200 |
| Intermediate | 105 | 120 | 75 | 300 |
| Working | 100 | 175 | 225 | 500 |
| Total | 325 | 345 | 330 | 1,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of all respondents who were upwardly mobile. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare the chances of reaching the service class for people of service-class and working-class origin. **[2]**
**(c)** Using the table, explain how absolute mobility can be high while relative mobility remains unequal. **[4]**

**5.** Amira is a hospital pharmacist. Her partner, Tom, works part-time on a supermarket checkout. Explain how the conventional approach and the individual approach would classify this household, and identify one problem with each approach. **[6]**

**6.** Read the fictional data below.

> **Calderway Logistics (fictional firm).** The firm employs 200 men and 200 women.

| Grade | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Senior managers | 18 | 6 |
| Supervisors | 40 | 30 |
| Warehouse and office staff | 142 | 164 |

Applying material from the data, analyse two sociological explanations of gender inequality in paid work. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why some sociologists argue that class identities have weakened in the UK. **[6]**

**8.** Distinguish between the medical model and the social model of disability, using an example. **[4]**

**9.** Outline the New Right view of the underclass and give one criticism of it. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate functionalist explanations of social stratification. **[10]**

**11.** To what extent has globalisation changed structures of inequality in the UK? **[16]**

**12.** Assess the view that the UK is a meritocratic society, using evidence from studies of social mobility. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Life chances are a person's chances of gaining things valued in a society [1], such as good health, education, income or a long life, a term from Weber [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns the second mark only with a concrete example of what life chances include.

**2.** Marx defines class by ownership or non-ownership of the means of production [1], whereas Weber defines it by market situation, such as skills and qualifications [1]. Marx sees two main classes heading towards polarisation [1], whereas Weber sees many classes and separates class from status and party [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each difference needs both sides stated; describing Marx alone and then Weber alone without a contrast word loses marks.

**3.** Occupation misses people who do not work, such as the very wealthy who live on investments [1], so the upper class is hidden from the scale [1]. Occupation-based scales ignore cultural and social resources [1], which Savage's Great British Class Survey added through cultural and social capital [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" needs each problem developed with a reason or example, not just a two-word label.

**4. (a)** Upwardly mobile = 105 + 100 + 175 = 380 [1]. 380 out of 1,000 = 38% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 60% of those of service-class origin (120 of 200) reached the service class [1], compared with 20% of those of working-class origin (100 of 500), so they were three times as likely [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Absolute mobility is high because over half the sample (535 of 1,000) changed class [1]. The service class grew from 200 origins to 325 destinations, creating room at the top [1]. But a service-class child still had three times the working-class child's chance of a service-class job [1]. So structural change raised movement without equalising chances, as the Oxford Mobility Study found [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Data answers must quote figures from the table; a general definition of relative mobility gains little on its own.

**5.** The conventional approach classifies the household by one main earner [1], here Amira's professional job, so the whole household counts as professional or managerial [1]. One problem: it ignores Tom's own job and the inequality within the household, as Acker and Stanworth argued [1]. The individual approach classifies each adult by their own job [1], so Amira is professional and Tom is in a routine occupation [1]. One problem: a couple who share income and housing are placed in two classes, which Goldthorpe argued misrepresents shared life chances [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Application marks need Amira and Tom's actual jobs; a scenario question answered in general terms stays in the lowest marks.

**6.** Walby's idea of patriarchy in paid work explains the pattern as structural [1]. Women are 25% of senior managers (6 of 24) even though they are half the workforce [1], which suggests vertical segregation and barriers to promotion [1]. Hakim's preference theory offers a different explanation [1]. More women than men are in the lowest grade (164 compared with 142), which Hakim might link to adaptive women choosing roles that fit with family life [1]. Critics reply that such "choices" are shaped by childcare costs and employer practices, so the data alone cannot settle the debate [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each explanation should be tied to a specific figure from the data, and analysis means saying what the figure shows.

**7.** Deindustrialisation has reduced the manual jobs and workplace communities that supported working-class identity [1]. With fewer large factories and weaker trade unions, people are less likely to see themselves as part of a collective class [1]. Pakulski and Waters argue that class is dissolving in advanced societies [1], so many inequalities once read as class are better seen as differences of status [1]. Beck's idea of individualisation adds that people increasingly have to construct their own lives as individuals [1], although critics reply that class still shapes life chances even when people do not identify with it [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a developed chain of explanation; two bare claims followed by names gain only knowledge marks.

**8.** The medical model sees disability as caused by a person's impairment, which needs treatment [1]. The social model, set out by Oliver, sees people as disabled by barriers in society [1]. For example, a wheelchair user is unable to reach a first-floor office because there is no lift, not because of their legs [1]. So under the social model the solution is to remove the barrier, while the medical model looks to the individual [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must show the difference between the two models, not just describe a disability.

**9.** The New Right, notably Charles Murray, argued that an underclass exists below the working class [1]. He linked it to welfare dependency, which he said rewarded worklessness [1], and to distinct values such as weak commitment to work and family [1]. Criticism: research suggests poor people largely share mainstream values but lack opportunities such as secure work, so the cause is structural [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The criticism needs a reason; writing "this has been criticised" scores nothing.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points.

*Knowledge.* Davis and Moore (1945) argued that stratification is functional and universal [1]. Some positions are functionally more important and need scarce talent and long training [1]. Higher rewards motivate the most able to fill them [1].

*Application.* In the UK, long medical training is followed by high pay, which seems to fit the theory [1].

*Analysis and evaluation.* Tumin (1953) argued that functional importance is hard to measure: a refuse collector may be as vital as a banker [1]. Stratification limits talent, because children from poor families have fewer chances to train [1]. Inherited wealth is a reward without any role, which suggests power not function sets rewards [1]. Marxists see stratification as exploitation that benefits the owning class [1]. Feminists note the theory ignores unpaid work, mostly done by women [1]. *Judgement.* Functionalism explains why some unequal rewards are accepted, but it struggles to explain inherited advantage or the low pay of vital jobs [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation should come from named alternative views or evidence, not just "it is a good theory" or "it is outdated".

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points.

*Introduction.* Define globalisation as growing links between societies in trade, culture and politics [1]. Define structures of inequality as lasting patterns of class, gender, ethnic and age inequality [1].

*Changes.* Sklair argues a transnational capitalist class has emerged [1], with corporate, state, technical and consumerist fractions [1]. Capital can move production abroad, weakening workers' bargaining power in the UK [1]. Deindustrialisation has reduced manual work and grown service jobs [1]. Standing (2011) identifies a growing precariat in insecure work [1]. Savage's survey found an elite and a precariat at the two ends of a seven-class model [1]. Global migration has changed the ethnic make-up of the workforce, and Modood's survey showed large differences in position between minority groups [1]. Women's employment has grown in a service economy, changing gender patterns [1].

*Continuity and evaluation.* The Marmot Review (2010) still found a social gradient in health, suggesting class inequality continues [1]. Goldthorpe found relative mobility stayed unequal over time [1]. Critics of Sklair argue national interests still divide business elites [1]. Pakulski and Waters claim class is dissolving, but critics reply that class still shapes life chances [1]. Globalisation may make inequality more global rather than replace class [1].

*Judgement.* One possible conclusion, which must follow from the evidence: globalisation has changed the shape of the class structure more than its existence [1]. **[16]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" requires a weighed judgement; listing changes without asking what has stayed the same keeps an answer out of the top level.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points.

*Knowledge.* A meritocracy rewards ability and effort, so positions are achieved, not ascribed [1]. Absolute mobility is total movement; relative mobility compares chances between origins [1].

*For the view.* The Oxford Mobility Study found large absolute upward mobility as professional jobs grew [1]. Saunders (1996) argued ability and effort explain much of who rises [1]. Wider access to education has opened routes into professional work [1].

*Against the view.* Glass (1954) found the strongest self-recruitment at the top of the class structure [1]. Goldthorpe found relative chances stayed unequal despite more upward movement [1]. Blanden, Gregg and Machin (2005) found income mobility fell between the 1958 and 1970 cohorts [1]. Breen and Goldthorpe (1999) re-analysed Saunders' data and found class origins still matter [1]. Children from less advantaged origins needed substantially more merit to reach the same destinations [1].

*Evaluation.* Studies that compared only men with their fathers missed women's mobility, so older evidence is incomplete [1]. *Judgement.* A possible line: the UK has more absolute mobility than in the past but is not fully meritocratic [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers keep absolute and relative mobility separate; blurring them leads to a judgement the evidence does not support.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not quoting figures from data or names from a scenario when the question asks you to apply them.
- Confusing absolute and relative mobility, or treating more movement as proof of meritocracy.
- Describing Marx and then Weber with no direct comparison.
- Writing "Weber had three classes" instead of class, status and party.
- Defining terms with no example, especially "life chances".
- Discussing globalisation without the transnational capitalist class or any link to UK inequality.
- Giving a criticism with no reason ("this is outdated").
- Essays that list studies but never weigh them, so analysis and evaluation marks are missed.
