These notes condense section **9.5 Scatter diagrams** from Topic 9 Statistics of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. You must draw and interpret scatter diagrams, understand positive, negative and zero correlation, and "draw by eye, interpret and use a straight line of best fit". C9.5 and E9.5 are identical, so every line here is for Core and Extended candidates.

Full explanations and worked examples are in the [scatter diagrams study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-scatter-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-maths/). For marked questions, go to the [scatter diagrams practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-scatter-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scatter diagram | A graph of paired data, one variable on each axis, points not joined |
| Bivariate data | Data in pairs, such as (height, mass) for each person |
| Correlation | The relationship between two variables shown by the points |
| Positive correlation | As one variable increases, the other tends to increase |
| Negative correlation | As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease |
| Zero correlation | No linear relationship |
| Line of best fit | A single ruled straight line showing the trend of the points |
| Interpolation | Estimating inside the range of the data |
| Extrapolation | Estimating outside the range of the data |
| Outlier | A point that does not follow the pattern of the others |

## Drawing: checklist

- Even scales on both axes, using most of the grid (axes need not start at 0).
- Both axes labelled, with units.
- Each pair plotted as a **small cross (×)**, as the syllabus notes suggest.
- Count the crosses: one for every pair in the table.
- Do **not** join the points.

## Correlation at a glance

```
Positive:  points rise from left to right    /
Negative:  points fall from left to right    \
Zero:      no pattern                        scattered
```

- Points close to a line: **strong**. Widely spread about a line: **weak**.
- Describe in context with "tends to": "Taller people **tend to** have longer arm spans."

**Small reminders.**

| Pair of variables | Correlation |
|---|---|
| Distance travelled and fuel used | Positive |
| Speed of a runner and time to finish a race | Negative |
| Shoe size and mark in a maths test | Zero |
| Hours of sunshine and umbrellas sold | Negative |

## Line of best fit: the syllabus rules

The syllabus notes say the line:

1. "should be a single ruled line drawn by inspection";
2. "should extend across the full data set";
3. "does not need to coincide exactly with any of the points but there should be a roughly even distribution of points either side of the line over its entire length".

So: one ruler line; from the first point's region to the last point's; balanced crosses above and below, all the way along. Not through the origin unless the data put it there. Not joined dot to dot. Not a curve. Ignore an obvious outlier when placing it. Draw no line for zero correlation.

## Using the line

```
To estimate y from x:  up from x to the LINE, across to the y-axis.
To estimate x from y:  across from y to the LINE, down to the x-axis.
Show the reading lines on the diagram.
```

**Small reminder.** A line of best fit passes through (10, 78) and (24, 34). For x = 17, halfway between 10 and 24, the reading is halfway between 78 and 34: **56**.

## How reliable is an estimate?

| Situation | Reliability |
|---|---|
| Inside the data range, strong correlation | Reliable |
| Inside the data range, weak correlation | Less reliable: points far from the line |
| Outside the data range (extrapolation) | Unreliable: the pattern may not continue |
| Extrapolation gives an impossible value (negative mass, score over 100) | Clearly invalid |

## Outliers

- A cross far from the main pattern.
- Do not let it pull the line of best fit.
- Give a reason that fits the context: a measuring or recording error, or an unusual individual.

## Correlation is not cause

Two variables can be correlated because a third variable affects both. Hot weather increases both cold-drink sales and swimming-pool visits; drinks do not cause swims.

## Exam traps

- Joining the crosses, or drawing a curve.
- A line drawn from corner to corner of the grid, far beyond the data.
- Forcing the line through (0, 0) or through the first and last crosses.
- "Positive" with no context when the question asks what the diagram shows.
- Saying every value of y increases with x; correlation describes a tendency.
- An estimate read from the line that does not match your own line.
- Drawing a line of best fit for zero correlation.
- Claiming one variable causes the other.
- Plotting with large dots so the exact position cannot be seen.

## Quick self-test

1. What type of correlation would you expect between the age of a car and its value?
2. What type of correlation would you expect between the length of a person's name and their height?
3. Write a sentence interpreting a positive correlation between hours of practice and number of goals scored.
4. Give the three syllabus rules for a line of best fit.
5. A student draws a line of best fit through the origin, although the data are all between x = 20 and x = 60. What is wrong?
6. What is the difference between interpolation and extrapolation?
7. Which is more reliable, and why?
8. A line of best fit passes through (2, 40) and (12, 90). Estimate y when x = 8.
9. Using the line in question 8, estimate x when y = 55.
10. A scatter diagram of daily rainfall and ice-cream sales shows one point with heavy rain and high sales. What is this point called, and should the line of best fit pass near it?
11. The number of fire engines sent to a fire and the cost of the damage show positive correlation. Do the fire engines cause the damage? Explain.
12. Should you draw a line of best fit when there is zero correlation?

### Answers

1. **Negative.**
2. **Zero** (no correlation).
3. Players who practise for longer **tend to** score more goals.
4. A **single ruled** line drawn by inspection; it **extends across the full data set**; points are **roughly evenly distributed either side** along its whole length.
5. The line has been forced through the origin; it should follow the data and **extend across the full data set only** (from about x = 20 to x = 60).
6. **Interpolation** is inside the range of the data; **extrapolation** is outside it.
7. **Interpolation**: we have data there, but we do not know whether the pattern continues outside the data.
8. Gradient = 50 ÷ 10 = 5; y = 40 + 5 × 6 = **70**.
9. 55 − 40 = 15; 15 ÷ 5 = 3; x = 2 + 3 = **5**.
10. An **outlier**; **no**, the line should follow the main pattern, not the outlier.
11. **No**: bigger fires need more fire engines **and** cause more damage; the size of the fire explains the link.
12. **No.**
