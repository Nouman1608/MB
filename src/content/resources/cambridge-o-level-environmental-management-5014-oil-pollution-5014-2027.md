Sub-topic 3.5 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 deals with oil in marine and coastal ecosystems. It has four statements: the causes of oil pollution (3.5.1), its impacts (3.5.2), strategies to **prevent** spills (3.5.3) and strategies to **minimise the impacts** of spills once they happen (3.5.4). The last two lists are separate in the syllabus, and questions often test whether you can tell them apart.

## Causes of oil pollution

The syllabus names five causes. They range from sudden disasters to slow, routine leaks.

| Cause | How oil escapes |
|---|---|
| Off-shore and on-shore oil extraction | Blow-outs, leaks from wells and platforms, spills when oil is loaded; on land, leaks from wells near rivers and coasts that drain to the sea |
| Pipelines | Corrosion, damage by ships' anchors or storms, poor joints and theft from lines |
| Shipping | Tankers and other vessels running aground or colliding; fuel leaks from any ship |
| Cleaning of tanks at sea | Washing out oily residues from empty tanks and releasing the waste water |
| Refineries | Leaks, spills and waste water from coastal plants that process crude oil |

Large tanker accidents attract attention, but routine releases such as tank cleaning and small leaks add oil to the sea every day. This is a useful point in an explanation of why prevention through regulation matters.

## What happens to spilled oil

Oil is less dense than water, so most of it floats and spreads into a thin layer called a **slick**. Wind and currents move it. Some of the lighter parts evaporate, some mixes with water to form a thick emulsion, some breaks into droplets, and some eventually sinks to the seabed or is washed ashore as sticky lumps. This explains why oil harms organisms at the surface, in the water and on the seabed and shore.

## Impacts on marine and coastal ecosystems

The syllabus lists seven groups. You do not need specific species; you do need the reason each is harmed.

- **Birds**: oil mats feathers, so they lose waterproofing and insulation; birds become cold, lose buoyancy and may drown. Birds that preen swallow oil and are poisoned.
- **Marine mammals**: oil coats fur, reducing insulation in furred mammals; animals that surface to breathe inhale toxic fumes; oil is swallowed while feeding or grooming.
- **Fish**: oil can coat gills, so oxygen uptake falls; eggs and young fish at the surface are killed; toxic compounds build up in tissues, so fish may be unsafe to eat and cannot be sold.
- **Crustaceans**: crabs, shrimps and other crustaceans on the shore and seabed are smothered by oil that sinks or washes up, and absorb toxic compounds. Contamination ruins local shellfish catches.
- **Seaweeds**: a coating of oil blocks light, so photosynthesis falls; seaweeds on the shore are smothered at low tide. Animals that shelter or feed in seaweed lose their habitat.
- **Coral reefs**: oil and its droplets smother corals and block light, and toxic compounds damage coral tissue, so corals may die. Because reefs support high biodiversity, the whole reef food web is affected.
- **Beaches**: sand and rocks are coated with oil, organisms living in the sand die, tourism declines and clean-up is costly and slow.

A strong answer links an impact on organisms to a wider effect, for example oiled seaweed → less food and shelter → fewer crustaceans and fish → less food for seabirds.

## Preventing oil spills

**Prevention** stops oil entering the sea in the first place.

- **MARPOL**, the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships, sets international rules for ships, including strict limits on releasing oily waste and rules on how tanks are cleaned and waste is handed over at ports. It reduces routine pollution as well as accidents.
- **Double-hulled oil tankers** have two layers of hull with a space between them. If the outer hull is holed in a collision or grounding, the inner hull still holds the oil.
- **Risk assessments** identify hazards before they cause harm, such as storm routes, corroding pipes or busy shipping lanes, and plan how to reduce them and respond if they occur.
- **Regular maintenance** of platforms, pipelines, tankers and refineries finds corrosion, worn valves and weak joints before they fail.

## Minimising the impacts of oil spills

These strategies reduce the damage once oil is escaping, or reduce the chance of a ship causing a spill on a dangerous route.

- **Improved navigation systems for ships** help crews avoid rocks, shallow water and other ships, reducing groundings and collisions on busy routes.
- **Booms and sorbents**: booms are floating barriers that contain a slick or steer it away from sensitive coasts; sorbents are materials that soak up oil so it can be collected.
- **Detergent sprays** break oil into small droplets that mix into the water, so less oil reaches the shore and bacteria can break the droplets down more quickly.
- **Skimmers** remove oil from the water surface and pump it into storage tanks.
- **Controlled burning** sets fire to thick, fresh oil at sea, contained within fire-resistant booms, removing large amounts quickly.

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Booms | Protect sensitive coasts and hold oil for collection | Fail in strong winds, waves and currents |
| Sorbents | Simple; useful on beaches and in small spills | Oily sorbents become waste that must be disposed of safely |
| Detergent sprays | Treat large areas quickly from ships or aircraft | Detergents can be toxic; the oil is still in the water, affecting fish and coral |
| Skimmers | Recover oil, which can sometimes be reused | Slow; work poorly in rough seas or on thin oil |
| Controlled burning | Removes large volumes fast, far from the coast | Smoke pollutes the air; needs calm weather and thick oil; residue may sink |

## Worked example 1: what happens to a spill

*Figures invented for practice.* A tanker loses 2,000 tonnes of oil. Over the following week, 20% evaporates, skimmers recover 15% and controlled burning removes 25%.

1. Total removed or lost = 20 + 15 + 25 = 60%.
2. Remaining in the sea or on shore = 100 − 60 = 40%.
3. Mass remaining = 2,000 × 0.40 = 800 tonnes.

Interpretation: even with three responses, 800 tonnes remain, which is why prevention is described as the best strategy.

## Worked example 2: how much boom?

*Figures invented for practice.* A roughly circular slick is 1.2 km across. To encircle it, the length of boom needed is the circumference: π × 1.2 = 3.8 km (to one decimal place). If the slick doubles in width, the boom needed doubles too, to about 7.5 km, so fast deployment matters.

## Common mistakes

- Putting skimmers or booms under prevention. They minimise impacts after oil is spilled.
- Writing "oil kills birds" with no reason. Explain insulation, buoyancy or poisoning.
- Treating detergent sprays as removing oil. They disperse it into the water.
- Forgetting routine causes such as tank cleaning and refineries.
- Giving MARPOL as a clean-up method rather than international regulation.

## Exam technique

**Describe** causes by naming the activity and how oil escapes. For impacts, give the effect and the reason for each organism group, and link to a wider food-web effect where marks allow. For strategies, read the stem carefully: "prevent" and "minimise impacts" need different lists. In a "discuss" question, match strategies to conditions, such as booms in calm sheltered water and controlled burning far offshore.
