These questions cover section 3.7.4 political and legal change of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-political-legal-change/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-political-legal-change-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of the impact of competition law? **[1]**

A: A government raises the minimum wage

B: A competition authority blocks a merger that would leave one firm with most of the market

C: A central bank raises interest rates

D: A tariff is placed on imported steel

**2.** Which **one** of the following is the role of a regulator? **[1]**

A: To set the company's prices to maximise profit

B: To appoint the directors of private companies

C: To oversee a market and enforce rules to protect consumers

D: To collect corporation tax

**3.** Define the term "tariff". **[2]**

**4.** Brandling Imports buys 40,000 units a year from overseas at £35 each. The government puts a 12% tariff on these imports. Calculate the extra annual cost and the extra cost per unit. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which stricter environmental legislation could be an opportunity for a business. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Pellam Foods makes ready meals and employs 120 staff. New law requires employers to give all staff predictable hours and to pay when shifts are cancelled at short notice. Analyse **two** effects of this law on Pellam Foods' functional decisions. **[6]**

**7.** The government announces that an expected high-speed rail link to a northern city will not be built. A logistics company had planned to open a large depot beside the route. Explain how this decision might affect the company's strategy. **[4]**

**8.** Calder Water is a regulated water company. The regulator announces a cap on the prices it can charge for the next five years, which is lower than the company asked for. Assess the likely effect on the company's strategy. **[9]**

**9.** A rise in the minimum wage of 5% affects a care business and its competitors equally. Explain why the business may be able to pass much of the cost on to customers. **[4]**

**10.** A government introduces grants and tax relief for new businesses starting in a deprived area. Analyse the opportunities and threats this creates for an established cafe in the area. **[6]**

**11.** The UK government signs a trade agreement that removes tariffs on cars between the UK and a large overseas market. A UK car maker has a factory in the UK and sells 60% of its output to the UK. Discuss the effect on the car maker. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate the extent to which changes in the political and legal environment are more of a threat than an opportunity for a large manufacturing business. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Competition authorities can block mergers that would reduce competition. The other options are labour law, monetary policy and trade policy. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Regulators oversee markets with rules on prices, quality and service. **[1]**

**3.** A tax [1] on imported goods, which raises their price [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Cost before tariff = 40,000 × £35 = £1,400,000 [1]; tariff = 12% × £1,400,000 = **£168,000** a year [1]; per unit = £168,000 ÷ 40,000 = **£4.20** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Stricter rules mean customers and firms must change what they use [1]. A business that already makes cleaner products, or can develop them quickly, will see demand rise [1] and may gain market share from rivals who are slower to comply, or charge a premium for being compliant [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Effect 1, human resources: the company must change contracts and rotas to give predictable hours [1], and may need more staff or cross-trained workers to cover cancelled shifts [1], which raises recruitment, training and administration costs [1]. Effect 2, finance: payments for cancelled shifts and extra staff increase labour costs [1], so the budget must be revised [1] and prices or margins reviewed to protect profit [1]. (Credit operations instead of either function: production schedules planned further ahead and demand forecasting improved.) **[6]**

**7.** The depot was planned to use the rail link to cut delivery times and costs [1], so cancellation weakens the case for the site [1]. The company may need to find another location with road or air links, delaying expansion [1], or reduce its plans if the costs are higher [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Likely effects: lower revenue and profit than planned [1], so less money for investment in pipes and treatment [1]. The company may cut costs, for example by reducing staff or maintenance [1], with risk of poorer service and regulatory penalties [1]. It may need to borrow more, raising gearing [1]. Strategically, it may look for efficiency through technology, or other activities where prices are not capped [1]. Counter: the regulator may allow price rises if the company shows it needs to invest [1], and a monopoly supplier of an essential service has stable demand [1]. Judgement: a price cap restricts profit but the company has few competitors, so the main risk is to long-term investment, not demand [1]. **[9]**

**9.** If every rival faces the same rise in costs, none can undercut the others on price [1], so customers cannot avoid higher prices by switching [1]. Demand for care is also relatively inelastic because it is a necessity [1], and many fees are paid by local authorities who may adjust their rates [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Threats: new businesses competing for customers [1], perhaps with lower prices because of grants and tax relief [1]. Opportunities: more new businesses bring more people to the area [1], which could increase footfall and demand [1]. The cafe may also qualify for some support if it expands [1], or supply new businesses with catering [1]. **[6]**

**11.** For: removing tariffs makes UK cars cheaper in the overseas market [1], which could raise exports and revenue [1], and may allow a strategy of building market share there [1]. Against: overseas rivals can also sell cars into the UK without tariffs [1], increasing competition in the 60% of sales that is domestic [1]. Imports of parts may become cheaper, lowering costs [1]. Judgement: the effect depends on how competitive the car maker's prices and quality are against rivals [1], and on the exchange rate [1]. If it is already efficient, the agreement is likely to be an opportunity [1]. **[9]**

**12.** Threats: tougher environmental and labour laws raise costs [1]; competition law can block mergers, limiting growth [1]; tariffs and sanctions can disrupt supply and markets [1]; regulation and political uncertainty make long-term planning harder [1]. Opportunities: grants for green investment and research [1]; trade deals open export markets [1]; better infrastructure cuts costs [1]; strong regulation can set a level playing field and favour efficient firms [1]. Factors: the industry (heavy industry faces more environmental regulation) [1], size of the firm and its ability to lobby and adapt [1], and whether rivals face the same rules [1]. Judgement: change is more a threat if it brings costs the firm cannot pass on, and more of an opportunity if the firm prepares early and rivals are slower [1]. Large manufacturers have resources to turn compliance into an advantage. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Good answers tie each law or policy to a decision the business must make, and judge the net effect with a reason.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a law without explaining its effect on costs, revenue or decisions.
- Treating all political change as a threat.
- Forgetting to say whether rivals are affected equally.
- Mixing up the roles of government and regulators.
- Ignoring whether a change is proposed or already law.
