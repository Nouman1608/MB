## Families, marriage and kinship (3.1.1.1)

- **Nuclear**: two generations. **Vertically extended**: three or more generations. **Horizontally extended**: same-generation relatives. **Single parent**.
- **Alternatives**: communes, living alone, foster care, children's homes.
- **Therborn**: different family systems in different world regions; patriarchy declined unevenly; no universal family.
- **Patriarchal** (male authority) v **matriarchal** (female authority).
- **Marriage**: arranged, love, monogamy.

## Perspectives (3.1.1.2)

| Perspective | Sociologist | Key idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | Parsons | Primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities; nuclear family fits industrial society | Laslett: pre-industrial households were mostly nuclear; ignores abuse |
| Marxism | Engels | Monogamy arose to pass on private property; families as agents of social control | Ignores benefits; overlooks variety |
| Feminisms | Oakley | Families shape gender inequality; women do most housework | May understate change |

## Social change (3.1.1.3)

- **Birth rate down**: contraception, women's education and work, cost of children.
- **Death rate and infant and child mortality down**: nutrition, clean water, sanitation, vaccination, healthcare.
- **Ageing populations**: longer life plus fewer births (for example, Japan).
- **Migration**: internal (rural to urban) and international.
- **Industrialisation and urbanisation**: Parsons' fit thesis; Young and Willmott found extended networks in East London, then the **symmetrical family**.
- **Diversity**: pivot or sandwich generation, reconstituted or blended, living apart together, child-free, beanpole.
- **Stacey**: postmodern, negotiated, divorce-extended families. **Smart**: personal life, chosen relationships.
- **Policy**: China's one-child policy (1980), two-child (2016), three-child (2021).

## Gender, age and family life (3.1.1.4)

- **Instrumental** (male breadwinner) v **expressive** (female carer) roles: Parsons.
- **Triple shift**: paid work + domestic work + emotional work.
- **Power**: men often control major decisions.
- **Violence**: Dobash and Dobash; WHO estimate about 1 in 3 women worldwide.
- **Children**: childhood socially constructed (Ariès); **child-centredness**.
- **Boomerang** families; grandparents' roles; status of older people varies.

## Perspectives in more depth

**Functionalism.** Parsons argued that, once industrial society took over many earlier family tasks (such as education and production), the family kept two "irreducible" functions: **primary socialisation**, teaching children society's norms and values, and the **stabilisation of adult personalities**, giving adults emotional security away from the pressures of work. He argued the **isolated nuclear family** suited industry because it could move to where jobs were, and because status in the workplace is achieved rather than inherited. Another functionalist, **Murdock**, listed four functions: sexual, reproductive, economic and educational. **Evaluate**: functionalism presents a harmonious picture and pays little attention to conflict, inequality or abuse within families.

**Marxism.** Engels linked the monogamous family to private property and inheritance. Later Marxists add that families keep capitalism going by **reproducing the labour force** (raising the next generation of workers), acting as **units of consumption** that buy goods, and teaching children to accept hierarchy. **Evaluate**: Marxism sees family life almost entirely through economics and can overlook the love, support and choice many people value.

**Feminisms.** **Liberal feminists** see gradual progress towards equality through changes in law, education and attitudes. **Radical feminists** see the family as a central site of **patriarchy**, pointing to domestic labour and violence. Oakley's work on housework is the key named study: women carried most of the burden even when men "helped". **Evaluate**: feminists may understate how much roles have changed for some couples, and women's experiences vary by class, ethnicity and society.

## Marriage and kinship detail

- **Arranged marriage**: families play a major part in choosing the partner, and both partners consent. It is not the same as **forced marriage**, where one or both do not consent.
- **Love marriage**: partners choose each other on the basis of romantic attachment, the dominant ideal in many Western societies.
- **Monogamy**: one spouse at a time. **Serial monogamy**: a sequence of marriages over a lifetime, after divorce or death.
- **Kinship networks** can provide childcare, money, housing and care for older relatives, which is why extended families remain strong where welfare provision is limited.

## Linking change to family types

| Change | Effect on families |
|---|---|
| Falling birth rate | Smaller families; more child-centred family life |
| Longer life expectancy | Beanpole families; pivot or sandwich generation; more grandparent care |
| Rural-to-urban migration | Extended households may split, but kinship ties are kept through visits and money sent home |
| International migration | Transnational families; children sometimes cared for by grandparents |
| Changing laws and policy | Easier divorce supports reconstituted and single-parent families; China's policies shaped family size |
| Women's education and paid work | Later marriage and childbirth; more dual-earner couples; debates about the triple shift |

## Model paragraph: are roles now equal?

*Topic: have gender roles in families become equal?*

> Young and Willmott argued that families have become more symmetrical, with husbands and wives sharing paid work, housework and leisure more than in the past. This suggests roles have moved away from Parsons' instrumental and expressive division. However, Oakley found that women still did most housework and that men's contribution was limited to "helping". The idea of the triple shift suggests many women now add paid work to domestic and emotional work rather than swapping one for the other. Studies of decision making also suggest men often control the biggest financial choices. Overall, roles may be more equal than before, but inequality in domestic labour and power remains in many families.

Why it works: a claim with a sociologist, a counter-claim with a named sociologist, a further concept, and a judgement.

## Command words

- **Identify**: a brief point, no development.
- **Describe**: the features, with detail.
- **Explain**: a reason developed with "because" and "so", or a linked consequence.
- **Discuss how far**: both sides, with sociologists and examples from a society you know, and a reasoned conclusion.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kinship | Relationships based on blood, marriage or adoption |
| Monogamy | Marriage to one person at a time |
| Beanpole family | Many generations, few members in each |
| Pivot generation | Adults caring for children and ageing parents at once |
| Living apart together | A committed couple who live in separate homes |
| Child-centredness | Family life organised around children's needs |
| Triple shift | Women's paid, domestic and emotional work |
| Symmetrical family | Husbands and wives share roles more equally (Young and Willmott) |
| Reconstituted family | A family including children from a previous relationship |
| Serial monogamy | A sequence of marriages, one at a time |
| Primary socialisation | Learning the basic norms and values of society in early childhood |
| Patriarchy | A system of male dominance |
| Ageing population | A rising average age, from longer lives and fewer births |

## Exam traps

- Mixing up vertically and horizontally extended families.
- A perspective with no named sociologist, or a name with no argument.
- A demographic change with no explanation of why it happened.
- Treating one society's family pattern as universal: Therborn shows it is not.
- No example from your own society when the question asks for one.
- Calling an arranged marriage a forced marriage: consent is the difference.
- Giving Parsons' functions without the reason they matter for industrial society.
- Assuming all change points one way: extended families stay strong in many societies, and migration can keep kinship ties alive at a distance.
- Equating "men help more" with equal roles; check who has responsibility and power.

## Self-test

1. What is a vertically extended family?
2. Name Parsons' two essential functions of the family.
3. Why did Engels link monogamy to private property?
4. What did Oakley's research on housework find?
5. Give two reasons for falling birth rates.
6. What is a boomerang family?
7. How did China's population policy change after 2015?
8. What did Young and Willmott mean by the symmetrical family?
9. Give one criticism of Parsons' view of the family.
10. What is the difference between an arranged and a forced marriage?
11. How can a falling birth rate and longer life expectancy produce beanpole families?
12. What did Smart mean by "personal life"?

**Answers:** 1. A family of three or more generations, such as grandparents, parents and children. 2. Primary socialisation and the stabilisation of adult personalities. 3. Men needed certainty about legitimate heirs to pass on property. 4. Women did most housework and men's help was limited. 5. Any two of contraception, women's education and paid work, the cost of raising children, lower infant mortality. 6. One where adult children return to live with their parents. 7. A two-child policy replaced the one-child policy in 2016, followed by a three-child policy in 2021. 8. A family in which husbands and wives share paid work, housework and leisure more equally. 9. Laslett found most pre-industrial English households were already nuclear; functionalism also ignores conflict and abuse. 10. In an arranged marriage both partners consent; in a forced marriage at least one does not. 11. Fewer children in each generation but more generations alive at once gives a long, thin family. 12. That people decide for themselves who counts as family, including chosen relationships such as friends or former partners.
