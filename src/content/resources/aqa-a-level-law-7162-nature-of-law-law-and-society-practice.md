## Section A

**1.** State **two** roles that law plays in society. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by an "enforceable right". **[2]**

**3.** Identify the level of fault required for a strict liability offence, and name one case on strict liability. **[2]**

**4.** State the effect of contributory negligence on a claimant's damages. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain how Roscoe Pound's idea of social engineering relates to the balancing of competing interests. **[4]**

**6.** Using **one** case, explain how the courts balance public and private interests in the tort of nuisance. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 balances the interests of the state and the individual. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why fault is important in criminal law, referring to the difference between murder and manslaughter. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the meaning of fault in the tort of negligence, using *Nettleship v Weston* (1971). **[4]**

**10.** Carla, a learner driver, crashes into a wall and injures her passenger, Dev, who was not wearing a seatbelt. Explain how fault would affect both Carla's liability and the damages Dev receives. **[5]**

**11.** Explain **two** examples of liability without fault in civil law, and suggest one justification for imposing such liability. **[5]**

**12.** "The law is only effective in society if people can actually enforce their rights." Discuss. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: maintaining order and social control [1]; protecting people and property; regulating relationships such as employment and family life; providing a peaceful means of resolving disputes [1].

**2.** A right that the law recognises and that the holder can uphold by going to a court or tribunal [1], which can grant a remedy if the right is infringed, such as damages or an injunction [1].

**3.** No fault (mens rea) is required as to at least one element of the offence [1]; for example *Alphacell v Woodward* (1972) or *Harrow LBC v Shah* (1999) [1].

**4.** Damages are reduced to reflect the claimant's share of responsibility for the harm [1].

**5.** Pound saw law as a tool of social engineering whose task is to balance competing interests [1], which he grouped as individual, public and social interests [1]. Law should satisfy as many of these interests as possible with the least friction or waste [1], which is what courts and Parliament do when, for example, they weigh a neighbour's enjoyment of land against the public benefit of an activity [1].

**6.** In *Kennaway v Thompson* (1981) the claimant suffered noise from motorboat racing on a lake near her home [1]. The club and spectators had an interest in continuing the races, which was a wider public interest in recreation [1], while the claimant had a private interest in enjoying her property [1]. The Court of Appeal balanced these by granting an injunction that limited the number and timing of race meetings rather than banning racing altogether [1]. (Also accept *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014): public benefit and planning permission can be relevant to whether damages replace an injunction.)

**7.** The state has an interest in detecting and preventing crime, so the Act gives police powers to stop and search, arrest and detain suspects [1] [1]. The individual has an interest in liberty and fair treatment, so the Act limits those powers [1], for example by requiring reasonable grounds for suspicion, setting time limits on detention without charge and guaranteeing the right to legal advice [1].

**8.** Fault means blameworthiness and is expressed in criminal law through mens rea [1]. It links punishment to moral responsibility, so the more blameworthy defendant receives the more serious label and sentence [1]. A killing with intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm is murder [1], whereas a killing without that intention, for example through gross negligence, is manslaughter, showing that the level of fault determines the offence even when the harm is the same [1].

**9.** Fault in negligence means falling below the standard of care expected of the reasonable person in the defendant's position [1]. The test is objective, so it does not depend on the defendant's own ability or effort [1]. In *Nettleship v Weston* (1971) a learner driver who injured her instructor was held to the standard of a reasonably competent driver [1], so she was at fault even though she was doing her best, which shows that fault in tort can differ from moral blame [1].

**10.** Carla's liability: as a learner she is still judged by the standard of a reasonably competent driver, following *Nettleship v Weston* [1]. Crashing into a wall suggests she fell below that standard, so she is at fault and liable to Dev in negligence [1]. Dev's damages: Dev's failure to wear a seatbelt is fault on his own part [1], so under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 his damages would be reduced [1], as in *Froom v Butcher* (1976), to reflect his share of responsibility for his injuries [1].

**11.** Vicarious liability: an employer is liable for a tort committed by an employee in the course of employment, although the employer is not personally at fault [1] [1]. Product liability: under the Consumer Protection Act 1987 a producer is liable for damage caused by a defective product without proof of negligence (also accept *Rylands v Fletcher*, 1868) [1] [1]. Justification: the party best able to bear or insure against the loss, and to prevent it, should pay, which ensures the victim is compensated [1].

**12.** For the statement: law performs its roles of protecting people and resolving disputes only if rights can be used [1]; *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* (2017) showed that tribunal fees caused claims to fall sharply, so employment rights were weakened in practice [1]; the cost of advice and representation, and delay, deter people from enforcing consumer or negligence rights [1]. Against: law also works through deterrence and voluntary compliance, so most people obey rules without any case being brought [1]; the existence of a right shapes behaviour, as employers follow equality and dismissal law to avoid claims [1]; and the courts can protect access, as the Supreme Court did in *UNISON* [1]. A developed point on alternatives such as tribunals or the small claims track lowering barriers [1]. A reasoned conclusion, such as that enforceability is essential for some roles of law but not for its role as a guide to conduct [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a case name with no explanation of which interests it balanced.
- Treating fault in negligence as the defendant's personal blame rather than an objective standard.
- In scenario questions, discussing liability but forgetting the effect of the claimant's own fault on damages.
- In "discuss" questions, arguing one side only.
