Every person, organisation and data set below is fictional unless a named published study is referred to.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by internalisation as a type of conformity. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the type of conformity in each case.

> A: Sami laughs at his new colleagues' jokes in the office but tells his partner he finds them offensive.
> B: After a month in a vegetarian household, Grace stops eating meat and keeps to this after moving out.

**[2]**

**3.** Explain why conformity increased when Asch made the line-judgement task more difficult. **[3]**

**4.** A fictional replication of Asch's procedure varied the number of confederates.

| Number of confederates | 1 | 2 | 3 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conformity on critical trials (%) | 4 | 12 | 30 | 33 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern shown. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference in conformity between three and six confederates. **[1]**
**(c)** Use normative social influence to explain why conformity rises as the majority grows. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by moral strain and binding factors in the agentic state explanation of obedience. **[3]**

**6.** Bilal's manager tells staff to delete customer complaints from the records. Bilal refuses, saying, "What happens to me depends on my own choices, not on the boss." Identify the explanation of resistance shown and explain your answer. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In a fictional obedience study, participants were told by an experimenter to give unfairly harsh scores to another person. There were three conditions, with 40 different participants in each.

| Condition | Participants who obeyed fully |
|---|---|
| Experimenter in laboratory coat, university building | 24 |
| Experimenter in laboratory coat, community centre | 17 |
| Experimenter in casual clothes, community centre | 10 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who obeyed fully in each condition. **[3]**
**(b)** Use legitimacy of authority to explain these results. **[3]**
**(c)** Identify the experimental design and explain **one** limitation of it for this study. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how proximity affected obedience in Milgram's research. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how locus of control may account for resistance to social influence, and give **one** limitation of this explanation. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate Asch's research into conformity. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss Milgram's research into situational variables affecting obedience. **[12]**

**12.** At a youth camp, most teenagers obey a senior leader's order to swim in a cold lake at dawn. When two friends refuse together, several others also refuse. Discuss explanations of resistance to social influence. Refer to the camp in your answer. **[8]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** Internalisation is genuine acceptance of the group's view as correct [1], so the change is private as well as public and continues even when the group is absent [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A: compliance [1]. B: internalisation [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the clue in B is that the change continues after Grace leaves the group.

**3.** When the comparison lines were more similar, the correct answer became less obvious [1]. Participants were less sure of their own judgement, so they looked to the majority for information [1]. Informational social influence therefore increased conformity [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Conformity rises steeply from 4% with one confederate to 30% with three [1], then levels off, reaching only 33% with six [1].
**(b)** 33 - 30 = 3 percentage points [1].
**(c)** A larger majority increases the fear of rejection or ridicule for standing out [1], so participants publicly go along to be accepted, though beyond about three the extra pressure adds little [1]. **[5]**

**5.** Moral strain is the distress a person feels when ordered to do something they believe is wrong [1]. Binding factors are aspects of the situation that keep them obeying despite this strain [1], for example not wanting to break an agreement or appear rude, or reducing responsibility by blaming the victim [1]. **[3]**

**6.** An internal locus of control [1], because Bilal believes outcomes depend on his own actions, so he relies on his own judgement rather than the manager's authority [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 24 / 40 = 60% [1]; 17 / 40 = 42.5% [1]; 10 / 40 = 25% [1].
**(b)** The university setting gave the experimenter the greatest legitimacy, so obedience was highest at 60% [1]. Moving to a community centre reduced the institution's prestige, and obedience fell to 42.5% [1]. Removing the laboratory coat removed a visible symbol of authority, so obedience fell further to 25% [1].
**(c)** Independent groups [1]. Participant variables, such as differences in personality or attitudes to authority between the groups, could explain the differences rather than the conditions [1], although random allocation would reduce this risk [1]. **[9]**

**8.** When the learner was in the same room as the participant, obedience fell from 65% to 40% [1], and when the participant had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate, it fell to 30% [1]. Being close to the victim makes the harm harder to ignore, increasing moral strain [1]. When the experimenter gave orders by telephone, obedience fell to 20.5%, because distance from the authority made disobedience easier [1]. **[4]**

**9.** People with an internal locus of control believe outcomes result from their own actions [1], so they take responsibility for their behaviour and are more likely to resist pressure from a group or authority [1]. Holland (1967) reported that internals were more likely to resist in a Milgram-type procedure [1]. Limitation: later research has given mixed support, and situational factors may outweigh personality [1]. **[4]**

**10.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Asch found about 37% conformity on critical trials, rising with group size up to three and falling with a dissenter [1]. Strength: high control, with a control group showing the task was unambiguous [1]. Strength: variations isolate group size, unanimity and task difficulty [1]. Limitation: the task was trivial and artificial, so findings may not apply to important decisions [1]. Limitation: the sample was American male students [1]. Bond and Smith (1996) found higher conformity in collectivist cultures, so findings are culture-bound [1]. Ethical issues: deception and stress for participants [1]. Judgement: valuable for identifying variables, but limited in generalisability [1]. **[8]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Baseline: 65% of 40 men went to 450 volts, and all reached 300 volts [1]. Proximity: obedience fell to 40% with the learner in the same room [1] and to 30% with touch proximity [1]. Telephone orders: obedience fell to 20.5% [1]. Location: obedience fell to 47.5% in a run-down Bridgeport office [1]. Ordinary member of the public as authority: about 20% [1]. Strength: systematic variation identifies which situational factors matter [1]. Hofling et al. (1966) supports obedience to legitimate authority in a real hospital [1]. Limitation: some participants may have doubted the shocks were real, though Sheridan and King (1972) found similar obedience with real shocks [1]. Burger (2009) found about 70% continued past 150 volts, suggesting the findings still apply [1]. Ethical issues: deception and severe distress [1]. Judgement: strong evidence that situations shape obedience, though individual differences also matter [1]. **[12]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Social support: the two friends refusing together breaks the unanimity of the group [1]. Others see that resistance is possible and that they will not be alone in facing the leader [1]. Evidence: in Milgram's variation with two disobedient peers, obedience fell to 10% [1]. Asch found that one dissenter cut conformity to about 5.5% [1]. Locus of control: the first two friends may have an internal locus of control, trusting their own judgement [1]. Those who refused only afterwards may be more external, needing support before resisting [1]. Limitation: research on locus of control is mixed [1]. Judgement: social support explains the spread of refusal at the camp more fully than personality alone [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing conformity with obedience in scenario questions.
- Percentages calculated without using the group size given.
- Applying legitimacy of authority without referring to the setting or clothing.
- Naming a design without explaining its limitation for this study.
- Extended answers that describe studies with no evaluation or judgement.
