## Types of organisation (1.1 a)

The specification names the four types and the stakeholder topic but does not list their characteristics or the stakeholder groups. Those details here, including unlimited liability and partnership agreements, are standard accounting knowledge, not specification wording.

| Type | Owned by | Main aim | Money comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public sector | Government (national or local) | Provide a service | Mainly taxes, plus charges |
| Private sector | Individuals or groups | Usually profit | Owners' capital, loans, kept profit |
| Sole trader | One person | Profit for the owner | Owner's savings, kept profit, borrowing |
| Partnership | Two or more partners | Profit shared by the partners | Each partner's capital, kept profit, borrowing |

Sole traders and partnerships sit inside the private sector.

### Sole trader versus partnership

| Point | Sole trader | Partnership |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | One | Two or more |
| Profit | Owner keeps all | Shared in the agreed ratio |
| Liability | Unlimited | Partners are personally responsible for the debts |
| Capital | Smaller pool | Larger pool |
| Decisions | One person, fast | Shared, slower, possible disagreement |
| Skills | One person's | Combined |
| Agreement | Not needed | Normally a partnership agreement |

**Must-know distinction.** Unlimited liability means personal assets are at risk if the business cannot pay its debts. Shared decisions and shared profit are the usual points to set against more capital and more skills.

## Stakeholders (1.1 b)

Stakeholder: a person or group with an interest in the business.

| Stakeholder | Wants to know | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Owner | Is the investment earning a return? | Profit for the year, capital |
| Manager | Are costs controlled? | Profit and expense figures |
| Employee | Is my job safe, can I be paid more? | Profit, general strength |
| Lender (bank) | Can the loan be repaid? | Profit, assets, existing debts |
| Supplier | Will I be paid on time? | Liquid assets against amounts owed |
| Customer | Will the business keep trading? | Overall stability |
| Tax authority | How much tax is due? | Profit |
| Community | Will jobs and local activity continue? | Growth or decline signs |

**Method in three steps.** (1) Name the stakeholder. (2) Name the item in the statements. (3) State the decision it helps with. A list of names alone does not show the connection the specification asks for.

## The six concepts (1.2 a)

| Concept | Rule in one line | Why it matters | Quick example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency | Use the same method period after period | Years can be compared; no profit-shifting | Keep straight-line depreciation each year |
| Prudence | Do not overstate profit or assets; recognise likely losses early | Users are not misled by optimism | Inventory valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value |
| Accruals | Record income and expenses when earned or incurred, not when cash moves | Profit matches the period | Insurance paid 1 Aug: only the months used are this year's expense |
| Materiality | Only items big enough to affect decisions need separate treatment | Saves effort; statements stay clear | Stapler is an expense; machine is a non-current asset |
| Money measurement | Record only what can be measured in money | Figures are objective and addable | Staff skills and reputation are left out |
| Business entity | The business is separate from its owner | Owner's private affairs stay out | Personal spending is drawings |

### Worked reminders

**Accruals.** Rent of 3,600 is paid on 1 August for 12 months and the year ends on 31 December. Monthly rent = 3,600 ÷ 12 = 300. Used this year: 5 months, 5 × 300 = **1,500**. Prepayment: 7 months, 7 × 300 = **2,100**.

**Prudence.** 60 units cost 40 each and now have a net realisable value of 33 each. At cost: 60 × 40 = 2,400. At net realisable value: 60 × 33 = 1,980. Inventory is shown at **1,980** and profit falls by **420**.

**Business entity.** Opening capital is 20,000. Profit is 15,000. The owner takes cash drawings of 6,000 and goods worth 520. Closing capital = 20,000 + 15,000 − 6,000 − 520 = **28,480**. Profit stays at 15,000.

**Materiality.** A purchase of 9 against revenue of 360,000 is 9 ÷ 360,000 × 100 = 0.0025% of revenue, so it is immaterial. Judge against the size of the business, not in isolation.

**Consistency.** A machine costing 24,000 is depreciated at 10% straight line in year 1, which is 2,400. If the method changes to reducing balance at 25% in year 2, the charge is 25% × 21,600 = 5,400, which is 3,000 more than straight line. Profit falls only because the method changed.

### Must-know distinctions

- Accruals is about **when** items are recorded. Prudence is about **how cautious** the figure is.
- Materiality is about **size**. Money measurement is about **whether the item can be given a money value at all**.
- Business entity separates **owner from business**. A sole trader is still legally the same person as the business, yet the records treat them as separate.
- Consistency allows a change of method only with a good reason. It does not forbid ever changing.

### Method for longer answers

- **Explain**: point, then because/so. For example, "owned by the government, so decisions follow public policy".
- **Apply a concept**: name it, state the rule in a few words, then use a figure from the question.
- **Evaluate**: use the numbers, give one point for and one against, then reach a supported decision.

### Quick comparisons with figures

**Liability.** A sole trader's business owes 60,000 and its assets would raise 45,000. The shortfall is 60,000 − 45,000 = **15,000**, which can fall on the owner's personal assets.

**Pooled capital.** Two partners put in 25,000 and 15,000. Capital is 25,000 + 15,000 = **40,000**. If profit of 18,000 is shared equally, each partner receives 18,000 ÷ 2 = **9,000**, whereas a sole trader would keep all 18,000.

**Stakeholder link.** A bank sees profit of 24,000 and a loan repayment of 6,000 a year. 24,000 ÷ 6,000 = **4 times**, so repayment looks affordable.

## Quick self-test

1. State two characteristics of a public sector organisation.
2. Name the type of organisation owned by two or more people who share the profit.
3. Give one disadvantage of unlimited liability.
4. Which stakeholder would look at profit to decide whether a loan can be repaid?
5. Name the concept that leaves out the value of a good reputation.
6. Insurance of 1,200 is paid on 1 October for 12 months. The year ends on 31 December. Calculate the expense for the year.
7. Name the concept applied when inventory is valued at net realisable value because it is below cost.
8. The owner pays a private holiday from the business bank account. State the concept and how it is recorded.
9. A business depreciates a vehicle (cost 15,000) at 20% on cost, then switches method. Which concept is broken?
10. Calculate closing capital: opening 30,000, profit 12,500, drawings 9,200.
11. Explain why a stapler bought by a large business is not recorded as a non-current asset.
12. A business holds 20 units that cost 30 each, with a net realisable value of 26 each. State the value used for the inventory and the effect on profit.

### Answers

1. Owned by the government; aims to provide a service rather than profit; funded mainly through taxes (any two).
2. A partnership.
3. The owner's personal assets, such as a home, can be used to pay business debts.
4. The lender, for example a bank.
5. Money measurement.
6. 1,200 ÷ 12 = 100 a month; 3 months = **300**.
7. Prudence.
8. Business entity; it is recorded as drawings, not as a business expense.
9. Consistency.
10. 30,000 + 12,500 − 9,200 = **33,300**.
11. Its cost is tiny compared with the size of the business, so it is immaterial and is charged as an expense instead.
12. Prudence: 20 × 26 = **520** against a cost of 20 × 30 = 600, so inventory is shown at 520 and profit falls by **80**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "limited liability" for a sole trader or ordinary partnership.
- Saying public sector bodies make no money at all, instead of saying profit is not their main aim.
- Naming a stakeholder without linking them to a particular figure.
- Defining a concept but not applying it to the scenario given.
- Mixing up prudence and accruals when a question is about a likely loss.
- Counting the whole payment as the expense when only part of the period has passed.
- Putting drawings through the expenses, which wrongly reduces profit.
- Calling an item material or immaterial without comparing it with the size of the business.
- Giving a one-word answer when the command word is Explain.
- Forgetting that a concept answer needs a figure or fact from the scenario, not only a definition learned by heart.
