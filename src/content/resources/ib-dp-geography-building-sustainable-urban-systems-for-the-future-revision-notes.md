Condensed recall for **7.4 Building sustainable urban systems for the future** (Option G: Urban
environments). For the full explanation, read the
[Urban environments study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-urban-environments-study-guide/).

## Definition

A **sustainable city** aims to meet present needs without harming the ability of future
generations to do so, balancing **economic, social and environmental** goals.

The three goals pull against each other. A plan that is environmentally strong but unaffordable
fails the social test; a plan that creates jobs but locks in car dependence fails the
environmental test. Sustainability is therefore a **balance and a direction of travel**, not a
finished state. At the global scale, Sustainable Development Goal 11 (adopted in 2015) commits
countries to make cities "inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable", and the New Urban Agenda
agreed at the Habitat III conference in Quito (2016) set out how national and city governments
should plan for this.

## Features of a sustainable city

- Compact, mixed-use development.
- Efficient public transport.
- Renewable energy and resource efficiency.
- Circular approaches to waste.
- Green and blue infrastructure.
- Inclusive governance.
- Resilience to climate and other hazards.

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Compact, mixed-use development | Shorter journeys, less land taken from the rural fringe, cheaper per-person services |
| Efficient public transport | Cuts congestion, emissions and household transport costs |
| Renewable energy and efficiency | Reduces the city's carbon footprint and exposure to fuel price shocks |
| Circular waste systems | Turns waste into a resource and reduces landfill and pollution |
| Green and blue infrastructure | Parks, street trees, wetlands and rivers cool the city, absorb floodwater and support well-being |
| Inclusive governance | Plans fit local needs and gain the support needed to last |
| Resilience | The city keeps functioning when shocks arrive |

**Green infrastructure** is the network of vegetated spaces (parks, green roofs, street trees);
**blue infrastructure** is the water network (rivers, canals, ponds, wetlands) managed for flood
control and amenity.

## Ideas to compare

| Idea | Core point |
|---|---|
| New urbanism | Walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods |
| Transit-oriented development | Dense development clustered around public transport |
| Eco-cities | Cities designed around environmental goals |
| Smart-city technologies | Data and digital systems to improve efficiency |

Be critical: technology can improve efficiency but raises concerns such as privacy and who has
access to it.

| Idea | Evaluation point |
|---|---|
| New urbanism | Attractive streets, but new developments can become expensive enclaves |
| Transit-oriented development | Works best when land-use zoning and transport are planned together over decades |
| Eco-cities | Often new-build showcases; hard to copy in existing, unequal cities |
| Smart cities | Real-time data can manage traffic and energy, but raises surveillance concerns and can exclude people without digital access |

## Case studies to know

- **Curitiba, Brazil.** From the 1970s, under mayor Jaime Lerner, the city built a bus rapid
  transit network on dedicated lanes, later adding raised tube stations where passengers pay
  before boarding. Zoning concentrated dense development along the main bus axes, an early form
  of transit-oriented development. A "green exchange" scheme let residents trade recyclable waste
  for food or bus tickets. Limits: the wider metropolitan region has grown beyond the planned
  axes, car ownership has risen and buses can be overcrowded.
- **Medellín, Colombia.** Cable-car lines (Metrocable, first opened in 2004) and outdoor
  escalators in Comuna 13 link steep, low-income hillside neighbourhoods to the metro, alongside
  "library parks" and other public buildings. This approach, often called **social urbanism**,
  shows that sustainability includes access and safety, not only emissions.
- **Masdar City, Abu Dhabi.** Announced in 2006 as a showcase low-carbon city with renewable
  energy and car-free design. It has been built far more slowly and on a smaller scale than first
  promised, and critics describe it as an exclusive enclave in a country with very high per-capita
  energy use.
- **Songdo, South Korea.** A planned smart city on reclaimed land near Incheon, with sensors,
  extensive green space and a pneumatic waste-collection system. It took years to attract
  residents and is often cited as an example of top-down, technology-led planning.
- **Rotterdam, Netherlands.** Climate adaptation through green roofs and "water squares" such
  as Benthemplein, public spaces that double as stormwater storage during heavy rain.

## Stakeholders and participation

- Stakeholders: national and city governments, planners, developers, businesses, community groups
  and residents.
- **Participation matters:** top-down plans that ignore residents often fail.
- International goals recognise the importance of inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable
  cities.

| Stakeholder | Typical interest |
|---|---|
| National government | Growth, national targets, infrastructure funding |
| City government | Services, tax base, liveability, re-election |
| Developers | Profitable land and housing |
| Businesses | Accessible workforce and customers |
| Community groups and residents | Affordable homes, safety, green space, a voice in decisions |
| NGOs | Environmental protection, rights of informal residents |

Participation can range from simple consultation to **participatory budgeting**, in which residents
vote on how part of a city budget is spent; Porto Alegre in Brazil is the best-known early example.

## Resilience and trade-offs

**Urban resilience** is how a city copes with shocks such as extreme heat, floods or economic change.
Strong answers weigh trade-offs, for example dense development that cuts emissions but raises
housing costs or reduces green space.

Other trade-offs to use:

- **Green gentrification:** new parks or riverside improvements raise property values and can
  push out the lower-income residents they were meant to help.
- **Cost versus speed:** large rail systems cut emissions for decades but take years and huge
  budgets; bus rapid transit is cheaper and quicker but carries fewer passengers.
- **Retrofitting versus new-build:** most people will live in cities that already exist, so
  improving existing buildings and transport often matters more than flagship new cities.

Resilience planning also considers future scenarios: hotter summers, sea-level rise for coastal
cities, ageing populations and changes in the economy.

## Model paragraph (evaluate)

*"Curitiba shows that cheap, bus-based transit linked to zoning can shape a city's growth for
decades: dense corridors grew along the bus axes rather than as unplanned sprawl. However, its
success depended on unusually long political continuity, and growth in the wider metropolitan
area has outrun the original plan. Masdar, by contrast, had large funding but little existing
population, so it shows technology more than lived sustainability. Overall, Curitiba is the more
transferable model because it works within an existing city and budget."*

The paragraph names two cities, gives a strength and a limit, compares them and reaches a judgement.

## Exam traps

- Presenting smart or green technology as a complete solution.
- Describing a sustainable city with no named example or no evaluation.
- Ignoring who is excluded from a plan.
- Treating a flagship new-build city as typical of how most urban residents live.
- Covering only the environmental pillar and forgetting economic and social sustainability.
