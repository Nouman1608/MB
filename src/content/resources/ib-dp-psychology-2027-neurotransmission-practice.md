> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on neurotransmission in the biological approach (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [neurotransmission study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-neurotransmission/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-neurotransmission-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Describe the process of neurotransmission. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish between an excitatory and an inhibitory neurotransmitter effect. **[3]**

**3.** Explain how one drug or treatment changes neurotransmission. **[4]**

**4.** Distinguish between a hormone and a neurotransmitter. **[3]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Nadia has taken an SSRI for six weeks for depression. For the first fortnight she felt no change and had headaches. She now reports a steadier mood. Her cousin says, "So depression just means low serotonin."

**(a)** Explain how an SSRI affects neurotransmission. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the cousin's claim may be incorrect. **[3]**

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> A researcher in a laboratory studies rats. Rats in one group can press a lever that gives a mild electrical pulse to a reward-related brain region. They press the lever hundreds of times and ignore food. A control group has a lever that gives no stimulation, and those rats press it rarely.

**(a)** Identify the neurotransmitter most likely to be involved. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what the results suggest about reward and neurotransmission. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one ethical consideration and one limitation of generalising to humans. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how knowledge of neurotransmission has helped psychologists to improve the health of people with a named condition. **[4]**

**8.** Evaluate the use of animal research in the study of neurotransmission. **[6]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate the biological approach's explanation of human behaviour in terms of the contribution of neurotransmission. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** An electrical impulse (action potential) reaches the end of the presynaptic neuron **[1]**. Vesicles release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft **[1]**. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron and makes it more or less likely to fire **[1]**. The signal ends when the neurotransmitter is taken back (reuptake) or broken down by enzymes **[1]**.

**2.** An excitatory effect makes the postsynaptic neuron more likely to fire **[1]**, as with glutamate **[1]**. An inhibitory effect makes it less likely to fire **[1]**, as with GABA (credit a correct example).

**3.** Any one. For example SSRIs: they block the reuptake of serotonin by the presynaptic neuron **[1]**, so more serotonin remains in the synaptic cleft **[1]**, and serotonin receptors on the postsynaptic neuron are stimulated more **[1]**; used to treat depression and anxiety, though mood effects take weeks **[1]**. Equally creditable: L-DOPA (precursor of dopamine), benzodiazepines enhancing GABA, nicotine as an agonist, cocaine blocking dopamine reuptake.

**4.** A neurotransmitter is released by a neuron into a synaptic cleft and acts on a neighbouring cell across a tiny gap **[1]**. A hormone is released by a gland into the bloodstream and travels to target cells around the body **[1]**. Neurotransmitters act quickly and locally; hormones act more slowly and for longer **[1]**.

**5.** (a) An SSRI blocks serotonin reuptake **[1]**, so serotonin stays longer in the synaptic cleft **[1]**, increasing stimulation of receptors on the postsynaptic neuron **[1]**.

(b) Any one reason, up to **[3]**. The drug raises serotonin quickly but mood improved only after weeks, so the process is more complex than a simple shortage **[1]**. A drug working does not prove the original cause (reverse inference) **[1]**. Large reviews have not found consistent evidence that low serotonin causes depression, and other factors such as stress, thoughts and relationships are involved **[1]**. Credit placebo effects with development.

**6.** (a) Dopamine **[1]**.

(b) Rats chose stimulation over food, which suggests the stimulated region is part of a reward system **[1]**. The reward system is associated with dopamine, so activating it is reinforcing **[1]**. The control group's low pressing suggests the behaviour is caused by stimulation and not by the lever **[1]**.

(c) Ethical: animal welfare, such as the reduce, refine, replace principles and minimising harm and distress **[1]**. Limitation: rat brains differ from human brains, and human reward and addiction involve thoughts, learning and social factors, so results may not generalise **[1]**. Award the third mark for a developed point on either, such as the justification that the research benefit has to outweigh harm, or that human behaviour is more complex **[1]**.

**7.** Name a condition **[1]**, for example Parkinson's disease. Knowledge of the cause, a loss of dopamine neurons **[1]**, led to a treatment, L-DOPA, which the brain converts to dopamine **[1]**, reducing movement symptoms **[1]**. Credit a limitation as an extension, such as effects declining over time. Equally creditable: SSRIs for depression.

**8.** Description (up to 2): animals used to study the effects of drugs and brain stimulation on behaviour **[1]**; examples such as lever-pressing for brain stimulation **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4): strengths include control and experiments that would be unethical in humans **[1]**; the brains and neurotransmitter systems of mammals share basic features **[1]**; limitations include ethical concerns about animal welfare **[1]**, differences in human cognition and culture limiting generalisation **[1]**, and artificial lab conditions **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Neurotransmission gives a clear mechanism: release, binding, reuptake. It explains how drugs and some disorders act **[2]**.
- Applications: SSRIs, L-DOPA, understanding of addiction and reward show practical value for health **[2]**.
- Causality: drug effects do not prove cause (reverse inference); correlational evidence in humans; the serotonin story is more complex than a simple deficit **[2]**.
- Measurement: indirect measures in humans and the reliance on animal models **[2]**.
- Perspective: biological reductionism leaves out thoughts, culture and relationships; the diathesis-stress model links biology with environment **[2]**.
- Bias and responsibility: publication bias and placebo effects in drug trials, animal welfare, and informed consent for patients **[2]**.
- Change: treatments change behaviour for many people, but effects can be delayed or fade **[2]**.
- A conclusion on how far neurotransmission explains behaviour, for example that it is essential for understanding mechanisms and treatments but insufficient alone **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** forgetting reuptake or breakdown, or saying the neurotransmitter travels along the neuron.
- **Question 4:** describing only one of the messengers.
- **Question 5(b):** repeating the claim without explaining why it may be wrong.
- **Question 9:** only describing the synapse without evaluation or using only one concept.
