This guide covers **Theme 1 Population and Settlement**, for Cambridge
IGCSE Geography 0460, 2025–2026 series. 0460 (A*-G grading) and 0976
(9-1 grading, UK-only) are parallel variants of the same
qualification that cannot be taken together in the same series; the
0976 syllabus itself states that textbooks endorsed for 0460 remain
suitable for it, and Cambridge publishes one shared Learner Guide
covering both codes together, which is consistent with -- though not
an explicit published confirmation of -- content-identical subject
matter. This guide verifies against 0460 directly. All candidates
take Paper 1 and Paper 2, plus either Component 3 Coursework or Paper
4 (Alternative to Coursework).

## Where this fits in 0460

Population and Settlement is the first of three themes, alongside The
Natural Environment and Economic Development. It is the theme most
directly about people rather than physical processes, and it introduces
case-study-based thinking — every sub-topic here is examined with
reference to named, real-world examples — which is the same approach
the syllabus expects across all three themes.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE GEOGRAPHY 0460 — THEME 1 POPULATION AND SETTLEMENT**

- 1.1 Population Dynamics — the causes of rapid world population growth
and of change in population size, including birth rates, death rates
and migration; contrasting rates of natural population change;
over-population and under-population; and describing and evaluating
population policies
- 1.2 Migration — the causes and consequences of national and
international migration, both voluntary and forced
- 1.3 Population Structure — the causes and consequences of different
and changing population structures, read through population pyramids
- 1.4 Population Density and Distribution — the physical and human
factors influencing where people live and at what density
- 1.5 Settlements (Rural and Urban) and Service Provision — the
factors influencing the sites, growth and functions of settlements,
and the pattern and provision of services within them
- 1.6 Urban Settlements — land use patterns within urban areas, and the
problems associated with rapid urban growth, particularly in developing
countries
- 1.7 Urbanisation — the causes and consequences of urbanisation, and
strategies for managing its effects

## How to approach it

Every sub-topic in this theme is assessed with reference to specific
case studies — a named city with a squatter settlement problem, a named
international migration flow, a named country's population pyramid —
so revision should be organised case-study first, principle second:
know two or three concrete, detailed examples per sub-topic rather than
only the abstract definitions, since exam questions consistently ask
candidates to "describe" or "explain, using examples" rather than
define in the abstract. Population pyramids are worth particular
attention because they are examined both as a stand-alone skill
(reading age-sex structure from a given pyramid) and as a link to other
sub-topics — a youthful population pyramid connects directly to high
birth rates in 1.1 and to pressure on services in 1.5, so practise
tracing those connections across sub-topics rather than studying each
in isolation.

## The Demographic Transition Model

The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) sits underneath most of 1.1 and
1.3, and it is worth understanding as a story rather than memorising as
five isolated boxes. Stage 1 (high fluctuating) describes remote,
pre-industrial populations with high birth and death rates roughly in
balance, so natural increase stays low. Stage 2 (early expanding) is
where the population explosion happens: the death rate falls sharply
first, thanks to improved medicine, sanitation, clean water and a more
reliable food supply, while the birth rate stays high because
attitudes towards family size, religion and the economic value of
children change far more slowly than public health does — that lag
between a fast-falling death rate and a stubbornly high birth rate is
the entire mechanism behind rapid population growth. Stage 3 (late
expanding) is when the birth rate finally starts to fall too, driven
by wider contraception use, female education and employment, falling
infant mortality (so families no longer need as many births to
guarantee surviving children), urbanisation, and the rising cost of
raising children in an urban, non-agricultural economy. Stages 4 and 5
(low fluctuating and decline) describe countries where both rates are
low and population growth flattens or turns negative, as in Japan and
Germany. When answering, name a real country at each stage rather than
describing the stage in the abstract, and be ready to critique the
model itself: it was built from European historical experience, it
ignores migration entirely, it assumes every country follows the same
path in the same order, and it gives no fixed timescale for how long
each stage lasts.

## Population policies, over-population and under-population

1.1 also requires you to describe and evaluate population policies,
and to show an understanding of over-population and under-population
-- both are frequently tested and neither is a DTM concept, so they
need separate preparation. A country experiencing rapid growth (DTM
stage 2) may adopt an **anti-natalist** policy aimed at lowering the
birth rate, such as China's former one-child policy, which combined
financial incentives and penalties to sharply reduce fertility, but at
the cost of a rapidly ageing population, a shrinking future workforce,
and a skewed gender ratio in places where the policy interacted with a
cultural preference for sons. A country with a low or falling birth
rate (DTM stage 4 or 5) may instead adopt a **pro-natalist** policy
aimed at raising it, such as France's system of child benefits,
extended parental leave and subsidised childcare, evaluated as only
partially successful at reversing long-term fertility decline.

**Over-population** describes a situation where the number of people
in an area exceeds what the available resources, technology and
level of organisation can adequately support, at the current standard
of living -- it is a relationship between population and resources,
not a raw population total, so a sparsely populated area can still be
over-populated if its resources are scarce enough. **Under-population**
is the opposite: an area has more resources than its current
population can fully exploit, so output or development potential per
person could rise with more people. Both are relative to the
available resources and technology at the time, which is why exam
answers should evaluate a specific named country's situation rather
than assert an absolute population figure as "too many" or "too few"
in isolation.

## Reading population pyramids and structure

Population pyramids translate 1.3's structure content into a visual
skill that is examined on its own and as a link to other sub-topics. A
pyramid with a wide base that narrows rapidly at every age band
signals a high birth rate *and* a high death rate — a youthful, but
short-lived, **stage 1** population. Stage 2 is defined by the death
rate falling while the birth rate stays high, so its pyramid keeps
the wide base but narrows far less sharply going up, since more
people now survive into and through adulthood — this is the shape
associated with the rapid population growth the stage is named for. A
pyramid with straight, near-vertical sides and a narrow base signals
low birth and death rates — an ageing, stage 4 or 5 population. The dependency
ratio, calculated as (population under 15 plus population over 64)
divided by the working-age population (15–64), then multiplied by 100,
quantifies how much of the pyramid's shape translates into economic
pressure. An ageing population raises pension and healthcare costs,
shrinks the workforce and increases the tax burden on those still
working, and countries respond with a higher retirement age,
encouraging inward migration, or pro-natalist policies that incentivise
larger families. A youthful population instead strains schools and
health services now and risks high youth unemployment once that large
cohort reaches working age, though it also promises a large future
workforce if that potential is used well.

## Migration in depth

1.2 expects both the causes and the consequences of migration to be
argued from two sides at once. Push factors driving people to leave —
unemployment, poverty, conflict, natural disaster, persecution, and
poor access to services — should always be paired with the pull
factors drawing them toward a specific destination, such as available
jobs, higher wages, better services, greater safety, or family already
settled there. Consequences are best organised by country: the
receiving country typically gains a labour-shortage fix and often a
younger, tax-paying workforce plus cultural diversity, but faces added
pressure on housing and services and sometimes social tension; the
source country benefits from remittances sent home, reduced local
unemployment and skills brought back by returning migrants, but risks
brain drain, the loss of working-age people, and family separation.
Mark schemes consistently reward answers that evaluate both sides
rather than treating migration as a one-directional story.

## Settlement site, situation and hierarchy

1.5 and 1.6 distinguish **site** — the actual physical land a
settlement occupies — from **situation** — its position relative to
surrounding features and other settlements. Classic site factors
include a reliable water supply, a dry point above a floodplain,
defensibility, favourable relief and aspect, available building
materials, fuel, and fertile land for early agriculture. Settlement
hierarchy then describes how, as settlement size increases up from
hamlet to village, town and city, the number of settlements at that
size decreases while the range and number of services they offer
increases. Two linked ideas make hierarchy answers precise: **range**
is how far people will realistically travel to use a service, and
**threshold population** is the minimum number of customers needed to
make that service viable. A department store has both a high threshold
and a large range, which is exactly why there are relatively few of
them, each serving a wide catchment; a newsagent has a low threshold
and a small range, so many can exist close together. Urban land-use
within a city typically follows a recognisable pattern too — an
accessible, high-value central business district, an inner city of
older housing and former industry, lower-density suburbs, and a
rural–urban fringe — and 1.6 and 1.7 both connect this pattern to the
problems of rapid urban growth in developing countries, where
rural–urban migration combined with high natural increase among young
migrants produces informal settlements, congestion, pollution and
strain on services, prompting responses such as site-and-service
schemes, self-help housing schemes, and rural development aimed at
reducing the push factors that drive migration to the city in the
first place.

## A note on the syllabus successor

This guide is written against the 2025-2026 series of Cambridge IGCSE
Geography 0460. Only the November 2026 series still sits on that
syllabus. A successor syllabus for the 2027-2029 series has already
been published and restructures the qualification significantly: the
three themes become ten topics (so the "Theme 1" numbering used
throughout this guide will not carry over), the first two papers are
renamed and reweighted from Themes and Skills to Physical and Human
Geography at 36% each, and the alternative-to-coursework paper is
also renamed. This guide has not yet been re-based onto the successor
syllabus.
