**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 3, "The African slave trade (1500 to 1900)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The skill is to evaluate historical arguments: understand the claim, weigh perspectives, reach a judgement. This subject involves the suffering of millions of people. Write about it precisely and respectfully, and remember that enslaved people were historical actors who resisted, not only victims.

The study has six inquiry topics: the expansion of the Atlantic slave trade; the expansion of slavery in East Africa from the late eighteenth century; the impact of slavery in Africa; resistance by enslaved peoples; the economic reasons for the decline of the trade; and the reasons for its abolition.

## 1. Reasons for the expansion of the Atlantic slave trade

Portuguese ships began taking captives from the African coast in the fifteenth century, and the trade grew once European colonies in the Americas needed labour. Estimates by historians suggest that about 12 million people were forced onto ships between 1500 and the 1860s, with the largest numbers in the eighteenth century. Treat figures as estimates and say "about".

- **Growth of maritime commerce.** Better ships, navigation and insurance, and joint-stock companies, allowed regular voyages. Portuguese, Dutch, British, French and others competed for the trade. Ports such as Liverpool, Bristol, Nantes and Lisbon grew rich from it.
- **Development of plantation agriculture.** Sugar, then tobacco, coffee, rice and cotton, needed large, cheap labour forces in Brazil, the Caribbean and North America. Indigenous labour had been reduced by disease and war, and European indentured labour was costly and limited.
- **European demand and African responses.** Europeans mostly stayed at coastal forts such as Elmina and Cape Coast. African rulers and merchants controlled the supply from the interior and chose whether to sell captives, on what terms, and for which goods (cloth, metal, alcohol, cowrie shells and firearms). Some, like Dahomey and Oyo, built power on the trade. Some, like parts of the Kongo kingdom, struggled to control it. Afonso I of Kongo wrote to the king of Portugal in 1526 to complain about its damage.
- **Internal rivalries and warfare.** Wars, raids and judicial punishments produced captives, and the demand for captives encouraged more warfare. Historians debate how far this "guns for slaves" cycle explains the growth of the trade and how far it was driven by European demand.

**Argument to weigh:** "European demand, not African supply, was the main reason for the expansion of the trade." Support: the plantation system created the demand and the purchasing power. Challenge: the supply depended on African political decisions and capacity, and African elites gained from it.

## 2. Reasons for the expansion of slavery in East Africa from the late eighteenth century

Slavery and the trade in people across the Indian Ocean were much older than the Atlantic trade. The guide asks you to explain the late eighteenth-century growth.

- **Existing trade between the Middle East and the Swahili Coast.** Enslaved Africans had long been taken to Arabia, Persia and India as domestic and military labour.
- **Expansion of the Sultanate of Oman.** Omani merchants took over coastal towns and, in the nineteenth century, Sayyid Said of Oman shifted his centre to Zanzibar (in the 1830s and 1840s).
- **Rise of the Zanzibar Sultanate and clove plantations.** Clove farming on Zanzibar and Pemba from the early nineteenth century needed large numbers of enslaved workers, and Arab and Swahili planters held thousands. Inland, traders such as Tippu Tip built caravan networks that brought ivory and people to the coast.
- **Escape from British antislavery control in the Atlantic.** After Britain banned the trade in 1807 and used its navy against slavers, demand and traders shifted. French sugar islands in the Indian Ocean and Brazilian and Cuban buyers drew East African captives.

British pressure gradually followed, with treaties limiting the sultan's trade and the closure of Zanzibar's slave market in 1873. Legal slavery on the islands ended in 1897.

## 3. Impact of slavery in Africa

- **Social impact.** Families were torn apart, and fear of capture shaped settlement patterns. The Atlantic trade took more men than women overall, so in some regions women outnumbered men and slavery within Africa often involved women and children as workers and wives. Within African societies the number of enslaved people grew, and some societies became more militarised.
- **Economic impact.** Imports of cloth, metal goods and guns could undercut local craftspeople, while the sale of people made some merchants and rulers wealthy. Resources went to the trade rather than to other production. Historians disagree about how deeply it harmed long-term economic development, and about how to measure it.
- **Demographic changes.** Population growth in affected areas, especially West Central Africa and the Bight of Benin, was held back. The gender imbalance and loss of young adults reduced labour.
- **Expanding power of African states.** Dahomey, Oyo, Asante and the Niger Delta city-states such as Bonny gained wealth and weapons. Yet this power could be brittle, since it depended on continuing the trade.

**Argument to weigh:** "The slave trade enriched African states." A strong answer separates short-term elite gain from long-term damage to society, and notes who gained and who suffered.

## 4. Resistance to slavery by enslaved peoples

- **Day-to-day resistance.** Work slowdowns, damaging tools, feigned illness, preserving languages, religion and music, and keeping family ties. These actions are hard to see in official records but were widespread.
- **Rebellions.** Revolts on ships (for example the *Amistad* in 1839 and the *Creole* in 1841), and uprisings on plantations such as Tacky's War in Jamaica in 1760 and the Baptist War in 1831. The most successful was the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804), which led to the end of slavery and the creation of a new state. Rebellions led to harsh repression but also pushed debate about abolition.
- **Escape and communities of escaped people.** Maroon communities in Jamaica, Suriname and Brazil (Palmares in the seventeenth century) were durable and sometimes won treaties.
- **Legal and political resistance in Africa.** Some African rulers tried to restrict sales, such as Afonso I in the sixteenth century and Almamy Abdul Kader of Futa Toro in the late eighteenth century, who banned the export of Muslims. Individuals fought capture. Resistance in Africa was limited by the power of slave-trading states and the lack of unity.

## 5. Economic reasons for the decline of the slave trade

- **Industrialisation and new technology.** Steam power and factories in Britain created demand for raw materials and new markets, which could be met by African cash crops.
- **Rise of legitimate commerce in West Africa.** Palm oil from the Niger Delta and groundnuts from Senegambia grew in importance during the nineteenth century, and some African merchants moved into this trade.
- **Need for a labour force in African plantations.** Palm oil, cloves and grain were produced with enslaved labour inside Africa, so slavery did not simply end but changed form.
- **Reduced productivity of slave labour.** Some writers, from Adam Smith onward, argued free labour was more efficient. Historians still debate whether slavery was in economic decline by 1807.

**The Williams debate.** Eric Williams, in *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), argued that the trade was ended for economic reasons because it had become less profitable for Britain. Later historians such as Seymour Drescher argued that the British trade was still profitable in 1807 and that abolition required a moral and political movement, a view often called "econocide" for the idea that Britain damaged its own interests. This is a classic argument to evaluate.

## 6. Reasons for the abolition of the slave trade

- **Ideological factors.** Enlightenment ideas of natural rights, and evangelical and Quaker Christianity that treated slavery as sin.
- **Role of the abolitionist movement.** In Britain, the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade (1787), campaigners such as Thomas Clarkson and William Wilberforce, and the testimony of formerly enslaved writers such as Olaudah Equiano. Petitions, boycotts of sugar and mass publication brought public pressure. In Haiti and elsewhere enslaved people's own actions mattered.
- **Political and legal factors.** The British Slave Trade Act of 1807 banned the trade in British ships, and the 1824 Act strengthened enforcement by treating slave trading as piracy. A navy patrol, the West Africa Squadron, was established. The United States banned the import of enslaved people in 1808. Slavery itself was abolished in the British Empire in 1833 and in Brazil as late as 1888.
- **The expansion of European colonialism.** Antislavery became a reason given for the occupation of African territory in the late nineteenth century, though colonial rule often kept forced labour in other forms.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "Economic factors were the main reason for the abolition of the slave trade." Evaluate this claim.

1. **Understand the claim.** Abolition followed profits and trade changes more than morals.
2. **Perspectives.** Williams (economic decline); Drescher (continuing profit, moral movement); historians who stress the actions of enslaved people.
3. **Evidence.** Industrialisation, legitimate commerce; against: sugar profits in 1807, evangelical petitions, the 1791 Haitian revolution's effect on thinking.
4. **Judgement.** A defensible position: economic change made abolition easier to accept, but without a mass moral movement the law would not have passed in 1807.

## Common mistakes

- Writing only about the Atlantic trade; the guide includes East Africa and the Indian Ocean.
- Presenting Africans only as victims or only as sellers.
- Giving precise numbers as facts. Use "about" and explain they are estimates.
- Treating abolition in 1807 as the end of slavery.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I discuss the Americas?** Yes, as context for demand and resistance, but the focus is Africa and the Africans.

**Do I need to cite historians?** Not for full marks, but using the Williams and Drescher arguments clearly helps you evaluate perspectives.
