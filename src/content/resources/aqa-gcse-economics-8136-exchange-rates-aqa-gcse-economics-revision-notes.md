These notes cover **section 3.2.4.2 Exchange rates** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "how exchange rates are determined" and "the effects of changes in the exchange rate on consumers and producers", where students should understand "how exchange rates are determined through the interaction of demand and supply". They add a curve-by-curve method for shifts and a wider set of conversion calculations to the shorter [international trade and exchange rates revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-international-trade-and-exchange-rates-revision-notes/). Full explanations are in the [exchange rates study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-exchange-rates-aqa-gcse-economics/); test yourself with the [exchange rates practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-exchange-rates-aqa-gcse-economics-practice/). All rates are invented.

## Condensed recall

- An exchange rate is the **price of one currency in terms of another**.
- The pound floats: its rate is set where the **demand for pounds** equals the **supply of pounds**.
- On the diagram: vertical axis = exchange rate (e.g. $ per £); horizontal axis = quantity of pounds.
- **Demand for pounds** comes from foreigners paying in pounds; **supply of pounds** comes from UK residents paying in foreign currency.
- Demand rises (shifts right) → pound **appreciates**. Supply rises (shifts right) → pound **depreciates**.
- A change in the rate affects consumers through import prices, and producers through export prices and the cost of imported materials.
- The size of the effect depends on how responsive buyers are to price and on how long the change lasts.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Foreign exchange market | Where currencies are bought and sold |
| Floating exchange rate | A rate set by demand and supply without a government target |
| Appreciation | A rise in a currency's value: it buys more foreign currency |
| Depreciation | A fall in a currency's value: it buys less foreign currency |
| Equilibrium exchange rate | The rate at which demand for a currency equals its supply |
| Speculation | Buying or selling a currency to profit from an expected change in its value |

## Which curve shifts? A method

1. Ask **who** is changing their behaviour: people abroad, or people in the UK?
2. People abroad wanting more UK goods, services, holidays or assets → **demand** for pounds shifts **right**.
3. People in the UK wanting more foreign goods, services, holidays or assets → **supply** of pounds shifts **right**.
4. Read off the new rate: more demand raises it; more supply lowers it.

| Event | Shift | Result |
|---|---|---|
| UK exports become more popular | Demand right | Appreciation |
| Foreign tourists visit the UK less | Demand left | Depreciation |
| UK firms buy more foreign machinery | Supply right | Depreciation |
| UK interest rates rise relative to abroad | Demand right | Appreciation |
| Traders expect the pound to fall and sell pounds | Supply right | Depreciation |

## Effects on different groups

| Group | Appreciation | Depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| UK consumers | Cheaper imports and holidays abroad | Dearer imports and holidays abroad |
| Exporters | Less competitive abroad | More competitive abroad |
| Firms importing materials | Lower costs | Higher costs |
| Firms competing with imports | More pressure from cheaper rivals | Rivals dearer; may gain sales |
| Foreign tourists coming to the UK | UK dearer, fewer visit | UK cheaper, more visit |
| Inflation | Downward pressure | Upward pressure |

## Worked calculations

**1. Turning a rate round.** £1 = €1.15, so €1 = 1 ÷ 1.15 = **£0.87** (2 d.p.).

**2. Size of a change.** The pound moves from $1.30 to $1.15. Fall = 0.15 ÷ 1.30 × 100 = **11.5%** (1 d.p.).

**3. Price for a foreign buyer.** A £180 UK coat costs a US buyer 180 × 1.30 = **$234** at $1.30, and 180 × 1.15 = **$207** at $1.15.

**4. Cost of an imported input.** A UK café buys coffee beans costing €2,400. At £1 = €1.20 this costs 2,400 ÷ 1.20 = **£2,000**; at £1 = €1.05 it costs 2,400 ÷ 1.05 = **£2,285.71**.

**5. A holiday.** A UK tourist changes £500 into yen at £1 = ¥190 and receives **¥95,000**.

**Rule:** pounds → foreign currency, **multiply**; foreign currency → pounds, **divide**.

## Exam traps

- UK tourists going abroad **supply** pounds; foreign tourists coming here **demand** pounds.
- A rise in the number of dollars per pound is an **appreciation** of the pound (and a depreciation of the dollar).
- A depreciation helps an exporter but raises the costs of any firm that imports materials; many firms do both.
- Export sales rise after a depreciation only if foreign buyers respond to the lower price.
- State the direction of the shift and the curve, not just "the pound changes".

## Self-test

1. Define an exchange rate.
2. Name the two curves in the market for pounds.
3. Give two groups who demand pounds.
4. Give two groups who supply pounds.
5. What happens to the pound if UK residents buy many more imported cars? Explain using a curve.
6. What happens to the pound if foreign investors expect it to rise?
7. £1 = $1.60. How many pounds is $1 worth?
8. A £60 UK book: what does it cost in euros at £1 = €1.10 and at £1 = €1.25?
9. A UK firm imports parts costing $9,000. What do they cost in pounds at £1 = $1.20?
10. The pound rises from €1.10 to €1.21. Find the percentage appreciation.
11. Who gains from an appreciation of the pound?
12. Why might a depreciation not increase a firm's export sales much?

### Answers

1. The price of one currency in terms of another.
2. The demand curve for pounds and the supply curve of pounds.
3. Any two: foreign buyers of UK exports, foreign tourists in the UK, foreign investors, foreign savers, speculators expecting a rise.
4. Any two: UK buyers of imports, UK tourists abroad, UK firms investing abroad, UK savers moving money abroad.
5. UK buyers sell pounds to pay for the cars, so the supply of pounds shifts right and the pound depreciates.
6. They buy pounds now, so demand shifts right and the pound appreciates.
7. 1 ÷ 1.60 = £0.625 (about 63p).
8. 60 × 1.10 = €66; 60 × 1.25 = €75.
9. 9,000 ÷ 1.20 = £7,500.
10. 0.11 ÷ 1.10 × 100 = 10%.
11. UK consumers of imports, UK tourists abroad, and firms that import materials.
12. If foreign buyers are not very responsive to price, or the firm has to raise prices to cover dearer imported parts, sales may change little.
