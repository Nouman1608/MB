This study guide teaches **Social organization**, the fourth of the five prescribed themes in the
content outline of IB Diploma Programme Language B. It follows the IB Language B subject brief for
first assessment 2020, which remains the examined course until the new syllabus is first assessed in
May 2029, so it applies to the 2026, 2027 and 2028 sessions. It covers SL and HL: the brief places
the difference between the levels in the competency you show in receptive, productive and
interactive skills. It is written in English; apply each point to your target language.

Use it with the [Social organization revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-social-organization-revision-notes/)
and the [Social organization practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-social-organization-practice/).
For the whole course, see the [Language B course hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-b/) and the
[printable Language B checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-b/).

## The theme as the brief states it

The brief gives each theme a guiding principle, a set of optional recommended topics and some
possible questions. For Social organization they are:

- **Guiding principle:** "Explore the ways in which groups of people organize themselves, or are
  organized, through common systems or interests."
- **Optional recommended topics:** social relationships; community; social engagement; education;
  the working world; law and order.
- **Possible questions:** "What is the individual's role in the community?" and "What role do rules
  and regulations play in the formation of a society?"

The theme is **prescribed**, but the topics are **optional and recommended**: your teacher chooses which of them to teach.

### Two directions in one sentence

Read the guiding principle slowly. It contains two directions:

- **People organize themselves.** A group chooses to act together because it shares an interest: a
  sports club, a residents' association, a volunteer team, a workers' cooperative.
- **People are organized.** A structure is set from outside or above: a school timetable, a
  company's hierarchy, a national law.

The tension between the two is often the point of a text. The possible questions follow the same
split: the first asks what one person contributes (from below), the second what rules do (from
above). With any unseen text, ask: who made this system, and who has to follow it?

[Identities](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-identities/) asks who you are as a person;
[Sharing the planet](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-sharing-the-planet/) asks what global challenges
communities face. Social organization asks **how groups are structured and what part each person
plays in them**.

## The six recommended topics

| Topic | Vocabulary areas to build | Organize themselves / are organized |
|---|---|---|
| Social relationships | friendship, family roles, neighbours, peer pressure, trust, conflict | Friend groups set their own unwritten rules; families have roles and expectations |
| Community | local area, residents, shared spaces, belonging, local services | Residents run a garden or festival; a council decides bin collections |
| Social engagement | volunteering, campaigns, petitions, youth councils, civic participation | Volunteers start a project; a school requires service hours |
| Education | school types, subjects, exams, uniforms, school rules, student voice | A student council proposes a change; a ministry sets a curriculum |
| The working world | jobs, applications, interviews, contracts, rights at work, part-time work | Staff share decisions in a cooperative; a manager sets a rota |
| Law and order | rules, laws, fines, rights and responsibilities, courts, police, safety | Neighbours agree on quiet hours; parliament passes a law |

Build vocabulary in three layers: **people and institutions** (a volunteer, a committee, an
employer), **actions** (to join, to elect, to enforce) and **words that judge a system** (fair,
strict, flexible, inclusive, outdated). The third layer lets you discuss the possible questions
instead of only describing a school or a job.

## The language of rules

Rules and regulations sit at the centre of the second possible question, so you must read and write
the language of rules precisely. English uses five functions:

| Function | English model | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Obligation | Members **must** attend. / You **are required to** sign in. | It is necessary |
| Absence of obligation | You **do not have to** wear a uniform. | It is not necessary, but you may |
| Permission | Visitors **may** park here. / You **are allowed to** leave early. | It is allowed |
| Prohibition | You **must not** smoke. / Smoking **is not permitted**. | It is not allowed |
| Recommendation | Students **should** book in advance. | It is advised |

The trap is the difference between **absence of obligation** and **prohibition**. "You don't have to
come" and "You must not come" mean very different things, and target languages mark the difference
in ways that do not map directly onto English:

- **German:** *Du musst nicht kommen* means "you don't have to come". "You must not come" needs
  *dürfen*: *Du darfst nicht kommen*.
- **French:** *Il ne faut pas fumer* is a prohibition. "You don't have to" is often *Vous n'êtes pas
  obligé de…* or *Il n'est pas nécessaire de…*.
- **Spanish:** *Está prohibido fumar* is a prohibition. "You don't have to" is often *No hace falta…*
  or *No es necesario…*.

Notices also like impersonal forms, which present a rule as the group's rather than one person's:
English passives ("Food is not permitted"), French *on*, German *man*, Spanish *se* (*Se prohíbe
fumar*). Check with your teacher which forms your language prefers on signs, in school rules and in
contracts.

### Worked example 1: reading a rule text

Original text written for Marlbridge:

> **Study rooms, second floor.** Rooms can be booked for up to two hours. Groups must leave the room
> as they found it. Food is not permitted; water is. Silence is not required, but please keep voices
> low.

Step by step:

1. **Classify each rule.** *Can be booked* = permission. *Must leave* = obligation. *Food is not
   permitted* = prohibition; *water is* = permission. *Silence is not required* = absence of
   obligation. *Please keep voices low* = request.
2. **Note the conditions.** "Up to two hours" limits the permission. Watch for "only", "unless" and
   "except" too.
3. **Ask what the rules reveal.** The rooms are shared, so each rule protects the next group (clean
   room, low voices) while leaving each group free to talk. That is an answer to the second possible
   question: here, rules make a shared space work for people who never meet.
4. **Transfer.** Rewrite the notice in your target language, including one impersonal form.

## Formal register: writing to an institution

Many Social organization tasks involve an institution: a head teacher, a council, an employer, a
committee. Get the register right before anything else.

- **Formal "you".** French *vous*, German *Sie*, Spanish *usted*. Use it for an official or a
  stranger and keep it consistent: switching forms is an accuracy error.
- **Set openings and closings.** French *Madame, Monsieur,* … *Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur,
  l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.* German *Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,* … *Mit
  freundlichen Grüßen*. Spanish *Estimado señor / Estimada señora:* … *Atentamente*. Learn the set
  formulas for your language exactly.
- **State purpose early**, in the first paragraph.
- **Request, do not demand.** Learn your language's polite request forms ("I would be grateful
  if…", "Could you…").

### Worked example 2: planning a Paper 1 answer

The brief states that Paper 1 tests productive skills with **one writing task from a choice of
three**, worth 25% of the final grade (30 marks). Original prompt written for Marlbridge:

> Your town council plans to close the only youth centre on weekday evenings to save money. Write to
> the council to give your view and suggest an alternative.

**Step 1 — context, audience, purpose.** The brief names context, audience, purpose, meaning and
variation as the course's conceptual understandings. Context: a council decision. Audience: council
members who do not know you. Purpose: to persuade politely and propose.

**Step 2 — text type and register.** A formal letter or email: formal "you", set opening and closing,
no slang, clear paragraphs.

**Step 3 — plan.**

1. Opening formula; who you are; why you are writing (the planned closure).
2. What the centre does on weekday evenings: homework club, sport, a safe place to meet.
3. Who would lose out: two or three specific groups, not "everyone".
4. Acknowledge the council's reason (the budget) before disagreeing.
5. Propose an alternative: volunteer-run evenings, shared use with a sports club.
6. A polite request for a reply or a meeting; closing formula.

**SL and HL.** The brief says HL Paper 1 tasks "require more complex language and structures and
demand higher-order thinking skills", with a higher word range. At HL, weigh the council's case
properly and argue for a compromise, using concession ("Although the budget is limited…") and
hypothesis ("If volunteers ran two evenings a week, the centre could…").

The subject brief does not list text types, so your teacher will share the list and conventions from
the full subject guide.

## Reading and listening on Social organization texts

Paper 2 tests receptive skills in separate listening and reading sections, each worth 25% (listening
25 marks, reading 40 marks). Texts on this theme are often notices, reports on a decision or debates.
Practise these habits:

- **Classify modal verbs as you read.** Must, may, must not, do not have to: one word changes the
  answer to a true/false question.
- **Circle the conditions and exceptions.** "Unless", "only if", "except", "up to" limit a rule.
- **Track who decides and who follows.** A report on a new school rule may quote the head teacher,
  a parent and a student. Match each view to the right person.

## The individual oral and Social organization

The oral is internally assessed, worth 25% (30 marks). At **SL** the stimulus is a visual image
clearly relevant to one or more themes: here perhaps a public meeting, a classroom or volunteers at
a clean-up. Describe briefly, then ask who organized what you see and what role each person plays.
Link it to a target-language culture you have studied.

At **HL** the stimulus is an excerpt from one of the two literary works you study, originally written
in the target language. Mark scenes where a character belongs to, rebels against or is shaped by a
group (a family, a school, a workplace, a village) as ready links to this theme.

## Comparing systems without stereotyping

School systems, working hours and how local decisions are made differ between countries and within
them. Compare with specific examples from texts you have studied and hedged language (*in the school
the article describes*), never "in country X everyone must…". If you are unsure of a fact, present it
as what a text says.

## Common errors on this theme

- **Confusing "must not" and "don't have to"**, especially where the target language builds them
  differently from English.
- **Register drift.** Starting a letter to a council with the formal "you" and slipping into the
  informal form, or ending with a friendly sign-off.
- **Describing a system without judging it.** Say what the rules achieve and for whom.
- **Missing exceptions** in reading: answering "true" to a rule statement that the text limits with
  "unless" or "only".
- **Only one direction.** Writing only about rules imposed from above and forgetting how people
  organize themselves, or the reverse.
- **Revising only education.** Revise the working world, law and order, and social engagement as
  seriously.
