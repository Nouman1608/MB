This guide covers section **1.6 Classification of goods and services** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content in Topic 1, so it can appear on Paper 1 (multiple choice) and Paper 2 (data response and essays), and A Level candidates are expected to know it as well.

For a wider view of Topic 1, see the [Production possibility curves and classification of goods guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-production-possibility-curves-and-the-classification/). When you have worked through this page, test yourself with the [classification of goods revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics-revision-notes/) and the [classification of goods practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

| Outcome | Wording in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| 1.6.1 | nature and definition of free goods and private goods (economic goods) |
| 1.6.2 | nature and definition of public goods |
| 1.6.3 | nature and definition of merit goods: under-consumption as a result of imperfect information in the market |
| 1.6.4 | nature and definition of demerit goods: over-consumption as a result of imperfect information in the market |

Notice what the wording leaves out. Externalities, social costs and social benefits belong to Topic 7 (A Level). At AS, merit and demerit goods are defined by **imperfect information**, and the reasons for government action belong to section 3.1. A Topic 1 answer should classify and explain; it does not need a full policy discussion.

## Two separate questions

Most errors in this section come from mixing two different tests.

1. **What are the good's characteristics?** Scarcity, excludability and rivalry sort goods into free, private and public goods. These are facts about the good itself.
2. **Do consumers judge its value correctly?** Imperfect information explains why some goods are under-consumed (merit goods) and others over-consumed (demerit goods). This is about the buyer's knowledge, not the good's physical features.

Because the tests are separate, one good can pass both. A course of vaccinations is scarce, excludable and rival, so it is a private good; it is also a merit good because many people underestimate how much it protects them. Saying "a merit good is a type of public good" mixes the tests and loses marks.

## Free goods and private goods

A **free good** has no opportunity cost. It is so plentiful that one person's use does not mean anything else is given up, and no resources are needed to make it available. Air for breathing in most places, sunlight and sea water at the shore are the usual examples.

A **private good** (also called an **economic good**) is scarce. Making it uses resources that could have produced something else, so it has an opportunity cost, and markets normally ration it with a price. Private goods are:

- **excludable**: the seller can stop anyone who will not pay from consuming it;
- **rival**: when one person consumes a unit, that unit is not available to anyone else.

A good is free or economic in a **particular situation**, not forever. Sand on a desert dune is a free good. The same sand washed, graded, bagged and delivered to a building site is an economic good, because labour, capital and fuel were used. If an exam stimulus describes a good that "has no price", check whether it is truly free (no opportunity cost) or simply provided without charge. A state school place has no price to the family, but it uses teachers and buildings, so it is an economic good paid for through taxes.

## Public goods

A **public good** has two characteristics, and you must give both.

- **Non-excludable:** once it is provided, nobody can be prevented from benefiting, whether or not they pay.
- **Non-rival:** one person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others. An extra user adds no extra cost.

Non-excludability causes the **free-rider problem**. Each person can benefit without paying, so few will volunteer payment. A firm cannot earn enough revenue to cover its costs, and the market fails to supply the good at all, even when its total value to the community is greater than its cost. Non-rivalry adds a second point: since an extra user costs nothing, charging a price that kept people out would waste benefit that could have been enjoyed for free.

### Worked example: a flood barrier

An invented riverside village has 400 households. A flood barrier would cost 45,000 cu (currency units). Each household values the protection at 150 cu.

```
Total value to the village = 400 x 150 = 60,000 cu
Cost of the barrier                    = 45,000 cu
Net benefit to the village             = 15,000 cu

Voluntary scheme: 25% of households pay 150 cu each
Money raised = 100 x 150 = 15,000 cu   (well short of 45,000 cu)

Compulsory tax: 45,000 / 400 = 112.50 cu per household
Net gain per household = 150 - 112.50 = 37.50 cu
```

The barrier is worth building, yet voluntary payment fails because a household that refuses to pay is protected anyway. The barrier is non-excludable (water is held back from every house) and non-rival (protecting one house does not reduce the protection of the next). A tax removes the choice to free ride.

### Goods that fail one test

Many goods meet only one condition, and exam questions often test whether you notice. A road with tolls is excludable. An uncongested road is close to non-rival, but once traffic builds up, each extra car slows everyone else, so it becomes rival. A broadcast with a subscription lock is non-rival but excludable. None of these is a public good under the 9708 definition, because both properties are needed. Saying why a good falls short of the definition is strong analysis.

## Merit goods

A **merit good** is **under-consumed as a result of imperfect information in the market**. Consumers underestimate the benefit they themselves will receive, so they buy less than they would if they were fully informed.

### Worked example: a dental check-up

Amira is offered a dental check-up at a price of 50 cu. She believes it is worth 30 cu to her. With full information about how check-ups catch problems early, she would value it at 70 cu.

```
Decision with her information:  30 cu < 50 cu  -> she does not buy
Decision if fully informed:     70 cu > 50 cu  -> she would buy
Benefit she misses = 70 - 50 = 20 cu
```

The market price is not the problem; her information is. Multiply this decision across many consumers and the market quantity of check-ups is below the quantity that fully informed buyers would choose. That gap is the under-consumption that defines a merit good. Common examples are education, health screening, vaccinations and saving for a pension, where benefits arrive in the future and are hard to judge today.

## Demerit goods

A **demerit good** is **over-consumed as a result of imperfect information in the market**. Consumers underestimate the harm the good does to them, so they buy more than they would if fully informed. Cigarettes, highly sugared drinks and gambling are standard examples.

The reasoning mirrors the merit-good case. Suppose a high-caffeine energy drink sells for 3 cu. A student values a can at 4 cu, but if she knew the full effect on her sleep and health she would value it at only 1 cu. She buys (4 cu > 3 cu), although a fully informed buyer would not (1 cu < 3 cu). Many such choices make market consumption higher than informed consumption.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why national defence is a public good. **[4]**

> National defence is **non-excludable**: once a country is defended, every resident is protected, and it is not possible to leave out those who refuse to pay *(1: first property, applied)*. It is also **non-rival**: one more person living in the country does not reduce the protection given to anyone else *(1: second property, applied)*. Because nobody can be excluded, people have an incentive to **free ride**, enjoying protection without paying *(1: mechanism)*. A private firm therefore could not collect enough revenue to cover the cost, so the free market would not provide defence *(1: consequence)*.

The answer earns marks for applying each property to defence rather than reciting definitions, and for the chain from free riding to non-provision.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a public good as "a good provided by the government". Provision does not define it; the two properties do.
- Giving one property of a public good, or confusing non-rival with non-excludable.
- Defining merit goods as goods that are "good for you" or that "the government provides". The syllabus definition is under-consumption caused by imperfect information.
- Bringing externalities into an AS Topic 1 definition. They are A Level content.
- Calling a good "free" because the user pays no price. A free good has no opportunity cost.
- Assuming merit goods cannot be sold privately. Private schools and private clinics sell merit goods.

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions want the precise syllabus meaning: for a merit good, include both "under-consumed" and "imperfect information".
- **Explain** questions want a chain: property, then mechanism (free riding or misjudged benefit), then the result for the quantity provided or consumed.
- **Identify** or **State** items in Paper 1 may list several goods and ask which is a public good. Check both properties for each option.
- In a Paper 2 data response, quote the evidence in the extract that shows the information problem, such as "many parents were unaware of the vaccine's long-term protection".
- When a question asks you to **Assess** a classification, say which property is in doubt and why, then conclude whether the good fits the definition.
