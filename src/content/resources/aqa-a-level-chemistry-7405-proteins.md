## The peptide link

When two amino acids react, the –COOH of one and the –NH₂ of the other join with the loss of water. The new link, –CO–NH–, is the **peptide link** (an amide link).

```
H₂N–CH₂–COOH + H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH
        → H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–COOH + H₂O
        glycylalanine (a dipeptide)
```

Three amino acids joined give a **tripeptide** with two peptide links, and a chain of n amino acids has n − 1 peptide links. A long chain is a **polypeptide**. A protein is one or more polypeptide chains, folded into a specific shape.

### Drawing a peptide

1. Write the amino acids in order, each as H₂N–CH(R)–COOH, with the free –NH₂ end (the N-terminus) at the left.
2. For each pair, join the –COOH of the left one to the –NH₂ of the right one, deleting –OH and –H.
3. Write –CO–NH– between them.
4. The right-hand end keeps its free –COOH (the C-terminus).

For glycine–alanine–serine:

```
H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH
```

The sequence matters. Alanine–glycine is a different dipeptide from glycine–alanine.

## Hydrolysis of peptides

Hydrolysis is the reverse of formation. Peptide links break when the peptide is heated with aqueous acid (for example hydrochloric acid) for a long time, or by enzymes. Each break adds the elements of water.

```
–CO–NH– + H₂O → –COOH + H₂N–
```

All the peptide links break, so the products are the **constituent amino acids**. In strong acid, the amino groups are protonated, so the products are written as the ions ⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COOH (with Cl⁻ as the counter-ion). For the tripeptide above:

glycine ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH, alanine ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH and serine ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH.

To draw the products, break each –CO–NH– link, put –OH on the carbonyl carbon and H on the nitrogen.

## Levels of protein structure

| Level | What it describes | Interactions that hold it |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The sequence of amino acids in the chain | Covalent peptide links |
| Secondary | Regular folding of the chain: α-helix or β-pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds between the N–H and C=O groups of peptide links |
| Tertiary | The overall three-dimensional folding of the whole chain | Hydrogen bonds between side chains, S–S bonds (disulfide bridges) and other interactions between R groups |

### Secondary structure

- **α-helix**: the chain coils into a spiral. Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide link and the N–H of a link four amino acids further along, holding the coil in shape. The bonds run roughly parallel to the axis of the helix.
- **β-pleated sheet**: sections of chain lie side by side in a zigzag, with hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O groups on neighbouring chains or sections.

In both cases, hydrogen bonding between the backbone peptide links is responsible. The hydrogen bond is a partly charged N–H hydrogen atom attracted to the lone pair on the oxygen of C=O.

### Tertiary structure

The chain with its secondary structure folds again into a compact shape. Two features are named in the specification:

- **Hydrogen bonding** between side chains, for example between –OH and –COOH groups.
- **Sulfur–sulfur (disulfide) bonds**: the amino acid cysteine has an –SH group in its side chain. Two –SH groups in different parts of the chain can be oxidised to form an S–S covalent bond, which acts as a strong cross-link.

Other attractions between side chains, such as ionic attractions and London forces, also contribute but are not named in the specification.

Primary structure is the **order**; secondary is the **local shape** held by backbone hydrogen bonds; tertiary is the **overall shape** held by side-chain interactions including S–S bonds.

## Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)

TLC separates a mixture such as the amino acids from a hydrolysed protein.

- **Stationary phase**: a thin layer of silica or alumina on a plate.
- **Mobile phase**: a solvent that moves up the plate by capillary action.
- Amino acids separate because they differ in how strongly they adsorb to the stationary phase compared with how soluble they are in the solvent.

### Method

1. Draw a baseline in **pencil**, about 1 cm from the bottom edge. Ink would dissolve in the solvent and run.
2. Place small spots of the amino acid solutions on the line, and the unknown mixture alongside.
3. Stand the plate in a tank containing a shallow layer of solvent that stays **below the baseline**. Put the lid on, so that the atmosphere is saturated with solvent vapour.
4. Remove the plate before the solvent reaches the top and mark the **solvent front** at once.
5. Dry the plate. Amino acids are colourless, so locate the spots with a **developing agent**. **Ninhydrin** reacts with amino acids to give purple spots. Alternatively, if the plate contains a fluorescent material, spots can be seen under **ultraviolet light**.
6. Measure the distances and calculate Rf values.

### The Rf value

```
Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front
```

Both distances are measured from the baseline, the spot distance to the centre of the spot. Rf has no units and lies between 0 and 1. Under identical conditions (the same solvent and plate), the same amino acid always has the same Rf value, so the unknown can be identified by comparing Rf values with known amino acids.

## Worked example 1: a tripeptide and its Mᵣ

**Question.** Calculate the relative molecular mass of the tripeptide glycine–alanine–serine. Mᵣ: glycine 75.0, alanine 89.0, serine 105.0.

```
Sum of amino acids = 75.0 + 89.0 + 105.0 = 269.0
Two molecules of water are lost = 2 × 18.0 = 36.0
Mr of tripeptide = 269.0 − 36.0 = 233.0
```

## Worked example 2: hydrolysis products

**Question.** The dipeptide H₂N–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH₂–COOH is hydrolysed. Give the structures of the products.

Break the –CO–NH– link: the carbonyl side gets –OH and the nitrogen side gets –H. Products: H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH (alanine) and H₂N–CH₂–COOH (glycine). In acid solution these are present as ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH and ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH.

## Worked example 3: calculating Rf

A chromatogram has a solvent front 8.0 cm above the baseline. Spot A is at 2.0 cm, spot B at 4.4 cm and spot C at 6.0 cm.

```
Rf(A) = 2.0 ÷ 8.0 = 0.25
Rf(B) = 4.4 ÷ 8.0 = 0.55
Rf(C) = 6.0 ÷ 8.0 = 0.75
```

If the known Rf values under the same conditions are 0.25 for amino acid P, 0.55 for Q and 0.75 for R, then the spots are P, Q and R respectively.

## Worked example 4: counting possible tripeptides

**Question.** How many different tripeptides can be made from one molecule each of glycine, alanine and serine?

Each of the three can be placed first, then one of the remaining two second, then the last one: 3 × 2 × 1 = **6** different sequences, because a different order gives a different peptide.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a peptide link as –NH–CO– in the wrong direction without checking which end is which.
- Forgetting that water is lost, or counting the wrong number of waters (n − 1 for n amino acids).
- Describing the primary structure as a shape. It is the sequence.
- Giving covalent bonds, not hydrogen bonds, as the force in an α-helix.
- Saying S–S bonds are hydrogen bonds. They are covalent bonds between sulfur atoms.
- Using ink for the baseline in TLC.
- Measuring Rf to the edge, not the centre of the spot, or from the bottom of the plate instead of the baseline.
- Writing Rf with units or with a value above 1.

## Exam technique

- **Draw a peptide**: keep the order, show the C=O and N–H in every link and the free ends.
- **Hydrolysis**: add –OH to the C=O and H to the N for every link.
- **Identify structures**: helix or sheet shows hydrogen bonds between backbone links; S–S shows tertiary structure.
- **TLC**: pencil line, solvent below it, developing agent, Rf from centre of spot.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the protein sequence so important?**
The order of amino acids decides which side chains are present and where, and so decides how the chain folds.

**Why are developing agents needed?**
Amino acids are colourless. Ninhydrin gives a coloured product, and ultraviolet light shows spots on a plate containing a fluorescent compound.

**Why do the Rf values need identical conditions?**
They depend on the solvent and the plate. Different conditions give different values for the same compound.
