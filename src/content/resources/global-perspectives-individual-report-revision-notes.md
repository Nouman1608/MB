## What it is, in one line

An internally set, **externally marked**, **1500-2000 word** report (excluding bibliography), worth
**30%** of the qualification, on a global issue of the candidate's own choosing — entirely the
candidate's own independent work.

## Five skills, always the same

Research, analysis, evaluation, reflection, communication — the same five skills Global Perspectives
assesses throughout the qualification, applied here to independent, candidate-led research.

## Build the question, not the topic

A broad topic title ("Climate change") gives no structure. Build a **research question** with a
named place, a named issue, and room for more than one perspective:

> "Should upstream and downstream countries share responsibility equally for managing a shared river's
> water supply?"

This single question already sets up at least two perspectives (an upstream government prioritising
its own agriculture; a downstream government facing shortages) that the report can research, compare
and evaluate before proposing a course of action.

## Structure in the order the criteria expect

1. **Explain the issue and its different perspectives** — local and/or national alongside global.
2. **Analyse causes and consequences** of the conflict or disagreement.
3. **Evaluate possible courses of action**, then propose and justify one.
4. **Reflect and conclude** — your own view, your learning, and others' perspectives, directly
   answering the research question.

Examiners mark **against this structure** — a well-argued essay covering the same ground in a
different order scores lower than a report built in this exact sequence.

## Referencing — start on day one

Keep a running reference list from the **first source read**, not reconstructed at the end.
Incomplete or inconsistent referencing is one of the most common reasons reports lose marks on the
evaluation criterion. No single required style, but it must be **clear and consistently applied**.

## Evaluating sources — not just citing them

For every source: state its **value to the research**, and whether it is **reliable and credible**.
Citing a source without evaluating it misses half of what the evaluation criterion actually rewards.

## Perspectives must explain "why," not just "what"

It is not enough to state that a group holds a view — explain **why** their perspective differs from
another's. Contrasting local farmers and local politicians on the same issue, for instance, means
explaining the different pressures (livelihood vs. budget, short-term vs. long-term) driving each
view.

## Why "knowledge of content is not assessed" changes how you revise

Global Perspectives' own specification states plainly that content knowledge is not what earns marks
in this component — there is no fixed topic list to memorise the way there is for a traditional
subject. This means the highest-value revision activity is not reading more about your chosen issue,
but practising the transferable skills themselves: reading past exemplar reports on entirely different
topics can still be genuinely useful preparation, since the same five criteria — research, analysis,
evaluation, reflection, communication — apply whatever subject matter a candidate chooses, and seeing
what a strong analysis or well-evaluated source looks like in someone else's report transfers directly
to your own.

## The teacher's role versus your own independent work

Teachers guide candidates toward a feasible, manageable topic, help formulate a genuinely researchable
question, support research and referencing skills, and monitor time management across the drafting
process. But the report itself must be produced **independently** — it is marked externally by
Cambridge, not by your own teacher, which is precisely why it carries more weight as evidence of
independent research skill than a component marked in-house might. Treat teacher feedback during
drafting as guidance on structure and scope, not as pre-approval of content, since the final judgement
on quality comes from an external examiner who has not seen your drafts.

## A second worked example

Consider a candidate investigating **urban air pollution**. A weak topic title ("Air pollution") gives
no structure. A strong research question — "Should city governments restrict private car use in
city centres to reduce air pollution, even where public transport remains limited?" — immediately
builds in a specific policy question, a specific place-type (a city centre), and at least two
perspectives to research and compare: a city government weighing public health against economic
disruption to businesses, and commuters who depend on cars because public transport does not yet
reach them. From here, the report can analyse causes (traffic volume, industrial emissions) and
consequences (health costs, economic impact), evaluate possible courses of action (congestion
charging, expanded public transport, phased vehicle bans), and propose one with a realistic
implementation plan — following the same four-part structure regardless of the specific city or
country chosen.

## Exam traps

- Choosing a broad topic title with no built-in perspective or scope.
- Writing a persuasive essay structure instead of following the research → analyse → evaluate →
  reflect order.
- Stating perspectives without explaining why they differ.
- Reconstructing references at the end rather than logging them as sources are used.

## Self-test

1. What is the word count range for the Individual Report (excluding bibliography)?
2. What percentage of the qualification does Component 2 carry?
3. Name the five skills assessed throughout Global Perspectives.
4. In what order should the report's four sections be structured?
5. What must an evaluation of a source state, beyond just citing it?

**Answers:** 1. 1500-2000 words. 2. 30%. 3. Research, analysis, evaluation, reflection,
communication. 4. Explain the issue and perspectives; analyse causes and consequences; evaluate
courses of action and propose one; reflect and conclude. 5. Its value to the research, and whether it
is reliable and credible.
