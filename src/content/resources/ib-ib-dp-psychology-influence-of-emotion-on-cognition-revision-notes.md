These notes cover the cognitive approach topic **"emotion and cognition"**, content point **"the influence of emotion on cognitive processes"** (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2019). The wider approach is in the [cognitive approach revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-cognitive-approach-revision-notes/); the full explanations and an essay plan are in the [emotion and cognition study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-influence-of-emotion-on-cognition/).

## The core argument in five lines

1. Emotional events produce memories that feel vivid and certain (Brown and Kulik, 1977).
2. When accuracy is checked against a record made at the time, those memories change like any other (Neisser and Harsch, 1992).
3. Compared with everyday memories, they lose consistency at a similar rate but keep their vividness and confidence (Talarico and Rubin, 2003).
4. Arousal and the amygdala help explain why they feel strong (Cahill et al., 1994; Sharot et al., 2007).
5. Emotion also narrows attention, so central details are encoded at the expense of the rest (Loftus, Loftus and Messo, 1987).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cognitive process | a mental process such as attention, perception, memory or decision-making |
| Flashbulb memory | a vivid, detailed memory of the circumstances in which a person learned of a surprising, important event |
| "Now Print!" mechanism | Brown and Kulik's proposed special process that records a surprising, consequential scene |
| Consequentiality | how important an event is to the person; Brown and Kulik linked it to rehearsal |
| Canonical categories | place, ongoing activity, informant, own feelings, others' feelings, aftermath |
| Consistency | whether a later account matches an earlier one; the usual measure of accuracy in this research |
| Test-retest design | recording a memory soon after an event and again later, then comparing the two |
| Weapon focus | attention drawn to a weapon, reducing memory for other details such as the face |
| Amygdala | a brain structure involved in emotion that interacts with memory systems |
| Propranolol | a drug that blocks beta-adrenergic receptors, reducing the effect of arousal hormones |

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Design | Finding | Shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brown and Kulik (1977) | retrospective questionnaire on public and personal shocks | vivid memories of hearing news; Black participants reported more for the assassinations of civil rights leaders | personal importance matters; supports a special mechanism |
| Neisser and Harsch (1992) | *Challenger* questionnaire the next morning; retest about two and a half years later | many accounts changed, with high confidence | reconstruction; vividness is not accuracy |
| Talarico and Rubin (2003) | 9/11 memory and an everyday memory recorded on 12 September 2001; retests at 1, 6 or 32 weeks | similar decline in consistency; vividness and belief in accuracy stayed high for 9/11 only | emotion affects confidence more than accuracy |
| Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987) | slides of a customer holding a gun or a cheque; eye movements recorded | more fixations on the gun; poorer identification of the customer | emotion influences attention |
| Cahill et al. (1994) | propranolol or placebo; emotional or neutral slide story; test a week later | memory advantage for the emotional story disappeared with propranolol | arousal hormones support emotional memory |
| Sharot et al. (2007) | fMRI during recall of 9/11, about three years later | greater amygdala activation in people who had been close to the World Trade Center | a neural basis for vivid emotional memory |

## Evaluation points to have ready

- **Retrospective self-report** (Brown and Kulik) cannot measure accuracy.
- **Test-retest designs** (Neisser and Harsch; Talarico and Rubin) give a baseline, which is their main strength.
- **Control condition:** only Talarico and Rubin compared flashbulb with everyday memories.
- **Samples:** mostly students at single American universities; generalisation to other ages and cultures is limited.
- **Natural events cannot be manipulated**, so researchers cannot control how much each participant rehearsed or watched coverage.
- **Alternative explanation:** repeated media coverage and retelling may produce vividness, not a special mechanism.
- **Ethics:** studying memories of real disasters needs sensitivity to distress; laboratory analogues avoid this but lose realism.

## Exam traps

- **"Emotion improves memory."** Say which aspect: detail, accuracy, vividness or confidence.
- **No baseline.** When evaluating, explain why a record made at the time is needed.
- **Approach drift.** In a cognitive-approach essay, biological studies support the argument but should not dominate it.
- **One process only.** If the question allows, weapon focus shows emotion acting on attention.
- **Evaluation in a short answer.** A 9-mark short answer needs description and explanation, not critique.

## Self-test

1. Define flashbulb memory.
2. Which two factors did Brown and Kulik say produce flashbulb memories?
3. What did the "Now Print!" mechanism propose?
4. Why does the timing of Neisser and Harsch's first questionnaire matter?
5. What did Neisser and Harsch find about confidence?
6. What control condition did Talarico and Rubin (2003) use?
7. State the main conclusion of Talarico and Rubin.
8. What is weapon focus, and which study showed it?
9. What did propranolol do in Cahill et al. (1994)?
10. Which group showed greater amygdala activation in Sharot et al. (2007)?
11. Give one alternative explanation for the vividness of flashbulb memories.
12. Give one limitation shared by most flashbulb memory studies.

**Answers**

1. A vivid, detailed and long-lasting memory of the circumstances in which a person learned of a surprising and important event.
2. Surprise and consequentiality (personal importance).
3. That a surprising, consequential event triggers a special neural process that records the scene in detail, strengthened by rehearsal.
4. It was completed the morning after the explosion, so it gives a baseline against which later accounts can be checked for accuracy.
5. Many students were highly confident in later accounts that differed from their original ones.
6. An everyday memory from the same period, recorded and retested in the same way.
7. Flashbulb and everyday memories lost consistency at a similar rate, but only flashbulb memories kept high vividness and belief in accuracy.
8. Attention drawn to a weapon at the expense of other details; Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987).
9. It removed the memory advantage for the emotional version of the story, suggesting arousal hormones are needed for it.
10. People who had been in downtown Manhattan, close to the World Trade Center.
11. Repeated rehearsal, retelling and media coverage, rather than a special recording mechanism.
12. For example: samples of students from one university; no control over rehearsal; events cannot be manipulated.
