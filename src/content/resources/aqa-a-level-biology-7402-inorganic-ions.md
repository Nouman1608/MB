This guide goes deeper into **3.1.8 Inorganic ions** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015, examined at AS and A-level. The sub-topic is unusual: it has only three short statements, and its main job is to make you recognise the role of four ions wherever they appear in the rest of the course.

The [water and inorganic ions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-water-and-inorganic-ions/) introduces each of the four ions with one worked example. This page connects each role to the ion's properties, as the specification asks, gives pH calculations using the logarithm formula the specification suggests, and maps every later place in the course where these ions turn up. Condense it with the [3.1.8 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-inorganic-ions-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.8 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-inorganic-ions-practice/).

## What the specification says

- Inorganic ions occur in solution in the cytoplasm and body fluids of organisms, some in high concentrations and others in very low concentrations.
- Each type of ion has a specific role, depending on its properties.
- Students should be able to recognise the role of ions in: hydrogen ions and pH; iron ions as a component of haemoglobin; sodium ions in the co-transport of glucose and amino acids; and phosphate ions as components of DNA and of ATP.

"Recognise" is the key word. Questions often describe a process from another topic and ask what an ion is doing. You need to spot the ion and state its role.

## Why the role depends on the properties

| Ion | Property | Role that follows |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen ion (H⁺) | Small, positively charged; its concentration sets pH | Interacts with charged and polar groups in proteins, so it affects their shape and function |
| Iron ion (Fe²⁺ in haemoglobin) | Can bind oxygen reversibly when held in the haem group | Lets haemoglobin load oxygen in the lungs and unload it in tissues |
| Sodium ion (Na⁺) | Positively charged; can be kept at different concentrations on each side of a membrane | Its concentration gradient is a store of energy used to drive co-transport |
| Phosphate ion (PO₄³⁻) | Negatively charged; forms bonds with sugars and other phosphates | Links nucleotides in DNA and RNA; forms the phosphate groups of ATP |

Linking role to property is what separates an explanation from a list.

## Hydrogen ions and pH

### pH is a logarithmic scale

The specification suggests calculating pH from hydrogen ion concentration with:

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]

where [H⁺] is in mol dm⁻³. Because the scale is logarithmic, a change of 1 pH unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration, and a lower pH means a higher concentration.

### Worked example 1: pH calculations

1. [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³. pH = −log₁₀(10⁻⁷) = **7**.
2. [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. pH = −log₁₀(3.2 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.49** (to 3 significant figures).
3. Blood at pH 7.4 and stomach contents at pH 2.0 differ by 5.4 units. The stomach has 10⁵·⁴ ≈ **2.5 × 10⁵** times the hydrogen ion concentration of blood.
4. Working backwards: a solution at pH 6.2 has [H⁺] = 10⁻⁶·² ≈ **6.3 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³**.

Example 1 also shows the point of the first specification statement. At pH 7, the hydrogen ion concentration is only 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, one of the "very low concentrations", yet small changes in it have large effects.

### What hydrogen ions do to proteins

Hydrogen ions interact with charged R groups. A change in pH alters the charges on amino acids, disrupting **ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds** that hold the tertiary structure (3.1.4). For an enzyme, this changes the shape of the active site, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form; at extreme pH the enzyme is denatured. This is why each enzyme has an optimum pH.

### Where else hydrogen ions matter

- **Haemoglobin (3.3.4.1):** higher carbon dioxide concentration in respiring tissues lowers the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, so it unloads more oxygen. The usual explanation is that carbon dioxide forms an acid in the blood, raising hydrogen ion concentration.
- **ATP synthesis (3.5, A-level):** in photosynthesis and respiration, hydrogen ions are pumped across membranes; their flow back through ATP synthase drives the condensation of ADP and Pi.

## Iron ions in haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a protein with quaternary structure: four polypeptides, each associated with a **haem group** that contains an **iron ion (Fe²⁺)**. Each iron ion can bind one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule can carry up to four oxygen molecules.

The binding is **reversible**, which is the whole point. Oxygen loads where its partial pressure is high (lungs) and unloads where it is low (respiring tissues). Without iron there is no site for oxygen to bind, so a shortage of iron reduces the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.

### Worked example 2: oxygen carried

Use the data supplied. A sample of blood contains 8.0 × 10¹⁷ haemoglobin molecules, and is 90% saturated with oxygen.

1. Maximum oxygen molecules = 4 × 8.0 × 10¹⁷ = 3.2 × 10¹⁸.
2. At 90% saturation: 0.90 × 3.2 × 10¹⁸ = **2.9 × 10¹⁸ oxygen molecules** (to 2 significant figures).
3. Iron ions present = 4 × 8.0 × 10¹⁷ = 3.2 × 10¹⁸, one per haem group, whether or not oxygen is bound.

## Sodium ions in co-transport

Co-transport moves two substances together through one carrier protein. In the epithelial cells of the ileum (3.3.3), glucose and amino acids are absorbed this way.

1. Sodium ions are actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP. This keeps the sodium ion concentration inside the cell low.
2. A sodium ion concentration gradient now exists from the lumen of the ileum into the cell.
3. Sodium ions diffuse into the cell down this gradient through a co-transporter protein, carrying glucose (or an amino acid) with them, even against the glucose concentration gradient.
4. Glucose then moves from the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.

The sodium ion's role is to provide the **concentration gradient** whose energy moves glucose. ATP is used to maintain the gradient, not to move the glucose directly; this is why co-transport is described as indirect active transport. Sodium ions also generate nerve impulses in 3.6.2 (A-level), again through gradients across membranes.

## Phosphate ions in DNA and ATP

- **DNA and RNA (3.1.5):** a phosphate group is one of the three parts of every nucleotide. Phosphodiester bonds between phosphate and sugar form the backbone. Because phosphate is negatively charged, DNA as a whole carries a negative charge.
- **ATP (3.1.6):** the three phosphate groups are the part of ATP that changes. Hydrolysis removes one as Pi, releasing energy; the Pi can phosphorylate other molecules.
- **Phospholipids (3.1.3):** the hydrophilic head is a phosphate-containing group. This use is not one of the four named roles, but it is the same ion.

## Exam technique

- **Recognise:** name the ion and state its role in that context in one sentence.
- **Explain:** connect the role to a property, such as charge, the ability to bind oxygen reversibly, or forming a gradient.
- **Calculate:** for pH, use −log₁₀[H⁺]; for ratios between two pH values, use 10 raised to the difference.
- Use precise words: "concentration gradient" for sodium, "haem group" for iron, "phosphodiester bond" for phosphate in DNA.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a higher pH means more hydrogen ions. It means fewer.
- Treating a change of one pH unit as a small change. It is tenfold.
- Saying sodium ions "carry glucose using ATP". ATP maintains the sodium gradient; the gradient drives co-transport.
- Writing that iron "is" haemoglobin or that iron carries oxygen on its own. Iron is part of the haem group in the protein.
- Forgetting phosphate in ATP when asked where phosphate ions are found.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which inorganic ions are named in AQA A-level Biology 3.1.8?**
Hydrogen ions (pH), iron ions in haemoglobin, sodium ions in co-transport of glucose and amino acids, and phosphate ions in DNA and ATP.

**How do you calculate pH?**
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol dm⁻³. Each fall of one unit is a tenfold rise in hydrogen ion concentration.

**What is the role of sodium ions in co-transport?**
A sodium ion concentration gradient, kept up by active transport, drives sodium ions into the cell through a co-transporter, carrying glucose or amino acids with them.

**Why do changes in pH affect enzymes?**
Hydrogen ions disrupt ionic and hydrogen bonds in the tertiary structure, changing the shape of the active site.
