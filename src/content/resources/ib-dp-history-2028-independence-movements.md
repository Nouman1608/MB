**HL only.** This is the third study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. Every question on this study will require you to refer to **one country** from the region. This guide uses the **United States**, **Haiti** and **Venezuela / Gran Colombia** (Simón Bolívar) as main examples, with Mexico, Argentina and Brazil for comparison. Choose one country and know it in depth, then use others to widen your argument.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: reasons for the emergence of independence movements; the contribution of key leaders; the reasons for the outcome of revolutionary wars; the establishment of new nation states; post-independence challenges; and the impact of US relations with independent states in Latin America. The concepts of **cause and consequence**, **significance** and **continuity and change** apply throughout. The [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) shows how to use them.

## Key chronology

- 1763: end of the Seven Years' War. Britain gains territory in North America and large debts.
- 1765: Stamp Act. 1773: Boston Tea Party. 1774: Coercive Acts.
- 1775: fighting at Lexington and Concord. 4 July 1776: Declaration of Independence.
- 1781: British surrender at Yorktown. 1783: Treaty of Paris. 1787: US Constitution.
- 1791: slave uprising begins in Saint-Domingue. 1 January 1804: Haitian independence.
- 1808: Napoleon's invasion of Spain, leading to a crisis of authority in Spanish America.
- 1810: juntas in Caracas and elsewhere; Hidalgo's call in Mexico. 1811: Venezuelan declaration.
- 1819: Boyacá. 1821: Carabobo and Mexican independence. 1822: Brazilian independence under Pedro. 1824: Ayacucho.
- 1823: Monroe Doctrine. 1826: Congress of Panama.

## Reasons for the emergence of independence movements

**Actions of the colonial power.** In British North America, the cost of the Seven Years' War led Parliament to raise taxes and tighten control. Colonists objected to taxes passed in a legislature in which they had no representatives. In Spanish America, the Bourbon reforms of the 18th century increased taxation and gave top offices to Spanish-born officials, which angered American-born elites (criollos). In Saint-Domingue, the world's richest sugar colony, planters, free people of colour and enslaved people all had grievances against the French state. Brazil was different: the Portuguese court itself moved to Rio in 1807 to escape Napoleon, and independence came as a split within the ruling house.

**Political and economic factors.** Colonial elites wanted control of trade and taxation. Restrictions on trade with foreign powers, tariffs and monopolies were unpopular. In Spanish America the crisis of 1808, when Napoleon forced the Spanish king to abdicate, left local elites with the question of who ruled in his name.

**Social and religious factors.** Colonial societies were divided by race and legal status. In Spanish America, criollos, mestizos, Indigenous people, free people of African descent and enslaved people all had different interests. The Church was powerful, and some priests (such as Miguel Hidalgo in Mexico) led movements, while the hierarchy often supported the crown.

**Enlightenment ideas.** Writers such as Locke, Montesquieu and Rousseau supplied language about natural rights, consent and representation. Thomas Paine's *Common Sense* (1776) reached a wide audience. The American and French revolutions provided models. Historians debate how far ideas caused revolution rather than justified decisions already driven by interests. A strong answer says that ideas gave a vocabulary and a goal, and that interests and events supplied the trigger.

## Contributions of key leaders

**Intellectual.** Jefferson drafted the Declaration; Paine wrote *Common Sense*; Bolívar's *Jamaica Letter* (1815) analysed Spanish America's situation.

**Political, including declaring independence.** The Continental Congress declared independence in 1776; the Venezuelan congress did so in 1811; Dessalines proclaimed Haitian independence in 1804.

**Military.** Washington held the army together and avoided decisive defeat. Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man, built a disciplined army and became governor of the colony, and Dessalines won the final campaign. Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre led campaigns across northern South America, while José de San Martín crossed the Andes to free Chile and then moved on Peru.

**Mobilising support.** Leaders had to win over groups with different aims: Bolívar's decree of "war to the death" in 1813 and later offers of freedom to enslaved men who served; Toussaint's appeals to former slaves and to free people of colour; Washington's reliance on state militias and Congress. The Haitian leaders faced a society where freedom for the enslaved was the central demand.

## Reasons for the outcome of the revolutionary wars

**Military organisation and strategy.** The Continental Army, with its training in part from European officers, survived by avoiding annihilation and winning at key points, notably Saratoga (1777). In Venezuela, early republics fell, in part because of royalist counter-attack and the 1812 earthquake. Success came from combining *llanero* cavalry from the plains with a campaign across the Andes to Boyacá (1819).

**Foreign support and regional collaboration.** France's alliance with the United States (1778) provided money, supplies and a fleet that was vital at Yorktown. Britain gave loans, volunteers (the British Legions) and trade to Latin American patriots. Colombia, Argentina and Chile cooperated against Spanish power in the Andes.

**Weaknesses of the colonial power.** Britain fought a distant war with long supply lines against a large population. Spain was weakened by the Napoleonic Wars, a civil war and then liberal revolution at home in 1820. France lost an army in Haiti largely to yellow fever and a war it could not sustain.

## Establishment of new nation states

**Constitutions.** The US moved from the Articles of Confederation (1781) to the Constitution (1787), balancing federal and state power. Gran Colombia (1819 to 1830) tried to create a large republic with a strong executive. Mexico passed through an empire under Iturbide (1822 to 1823) to a federal republic in 1824. Brazil became a monarchy under Pedro I.

**Borders and diplomacy.** New states had to decide boundaries from colonial administrative lines, which were often vague, and to win recognition. The United States gained recognition in the Treaty of Paris. Haiti was isolated, as France and others refused recognition until France demanded a large indemnity in 1825.

**National identity.** Leaders built symbols, anthems, flags and histories. In Haiti, the name itself recalled the Indigenous Taíno. In Latin America, identities were often regional or local rather than national, which weakened unity.

**Role of leaders.** Washington accepted limits on power and stepped down. Bolívar sought a strong president and, in the end, came to be seen as a dictator by some rivals. Dessalines took the title of emperor in 1804.

## Post-independence challenges

**Economic.** War debt, damaged infrastructure and disrupted trade were common. Haiti paid the French indemnity for decades. Latin American states borrowed from British banks, and some defaulted in the 1820s.

**External threats.** The US faced Britain again in the War of 1812. Mexico lost Texas (1836) and later large territories to the US. European states sought influence.

**Disputes over leadership.** Gran Colombia broke up in 1830 into Venezuela, Colombia and Ecuador. Many Latin American states saw strongmen (**caudillos**) and civil wars. In the US, the Federalist and Democratic-Republican parties fought bitterly, and disputes over slavery grew.

**Marginalised groups.** Independence did not mean equal rights. Slavery survived in the US, Brazil, Cuba and Spanish America, though many new states passed laws to end it gradually or ended it by the 1850s. Indigenous peoples lost land, as in US removal policy (Indian Removal Act, 1830), or faced new taxes and pressure on communal land in Latin America. People of African descent in the US were excluded from citizenship in many states. In Haiti, former slaves gained freedom, but many were pressed into plantation labour by the state.

## Impact of US relations with independent states

**US support.** Official support was cautious. Popular sympathy was strong, and the US recognised Gran Colombia and others in 1822. The **Monroe Doctrine** (1823) warned European powers not to colonise or interfere in the Americas. In practice, the British navy gave it force, and historians debate whether it was a protection or the start of US claims to dominance.

**Cooperation and integration.** Bolívar called the Congress of Panama (1826) to plan cooperation among the new states. The United States sent delegates who arrived too late, and little came of it. **Trade** grew between the US and Latin America, but Britain remained the greatest trading partner.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Were these revolutions about ideas, or interests? (Enlightenment liberty versus elites protecting wealth.)
- Were they social revolutions or elite-led transfers of power? Haiti is the exception.
- Did leaders shape events, or did conditions? Compare Bolívar's role with the 1808 crisis.
- Was the Monroe Doctrine a shield or a claim? 

Useful perspectives include the "economic" reading of the American Revolution, the "ideological" reading associated with Bernard Bailyn, C. L. R. James on Haiti in *The Black Jacobins*, and John Lynch on Spanish America.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a narrative of battles without answering the question.
- Treating Latin America as a single case.
- Forgetting that the question is about one country, then covering many without depth.
- Ignoring slavery, Indigenous peoples and women.
