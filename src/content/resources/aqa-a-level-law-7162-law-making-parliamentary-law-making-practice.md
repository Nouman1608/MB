## Section A

**1.** State what happens at the first reading of a Bill. **[1]**

**2.** Identify **two** routes by which a backbench MP can introduce a private member's Bill. **[2]**

**3.** State the effect of the Parliament Act 1949. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a hybrid Bill. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Outline the committee and report stages of a Bill in the House of Commons. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how an Act comes into force after it has received Royal Assent. **[3]**

**7.** Distinguish between insider and outsider pressure groups, and give **one** example of a campaign that influenced an Act. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** advantages and **two** disadvantages of political parties and manifestos as an influence on Parliament. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the principle that no Parliament can bind its successors, referring to a decided case. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the decision in *Thoburn v Sunderland City Council* (2002) qualifies the doctrine of implied repeal. **[3]**

**11.** A government Bill fulfilling a manifesto promise is passed by the Commons but rejected by the Lords. Explain how the government could still make it law, and what conventions or Acts are relevant. **[6]**

**12.** "Parliamentary supremacy is now more of a theory than a reality." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The Bill's title is read out and it is formally introduced, with no debate or vote [1].

**2.** Any two: the ballot [1]; the Ten Minute Rule; presentation [1].

**3.** It reduced the period by which the House of Lords can delay a non-money public Bill from two years to one year [1] [1].

**4.** A public Bill, usually introduced by government, that also affects particular private interests [1], for example the High Speed Rail (London - West Midlands) Act 2017, which affected particular landowners along the route [1].

**5.** At committee stage the Bill is examined line by line, usually by a Public Bill Committee of MPs reflecting party strength, which can amend it [1] [1]; major constitutional Bills may instead be considered by a Committee of the whole House [1]. At report stage the amended Bill is considered by the whole House, which may make further amendments [1].

**6.** An Act may come into force on the day it receives Royal Assent [1], on a date stated in the Act itself [1], or on a date set later by a minister through a commencement order, which is a form of statutory instrument [1].

**7.** Insider groups have regular access to ministers and civil servants and are consulted on policy, such as the British Medical Association [1]; outsider groups lack this access and rely on publicity, campaigns and protest [1]. Example: Gina Martin's campaign contributed to the Voyeurism (Offences) Act 2019, or the campaign after a police dog was stabbed contributed to the Animal Welfare (Service Animals) Act 2019 [1] [1].

**8.** Advantages: a manifesto gives the government a democratic mandate from the election [1]; voters know in advance what laws are planned [1]. Disadvantages: a government with a large majority can pass laws with little effective opposition [1]; a manifesto bundles many policies, so voting for a party does not show support for each proposal [1].

**9.** Each Parliament is sovereign, so it cannot pass an Act that a later Parliament is unable to change [1]. If a later Act conflicts with an earlier one, the later Act prevails and impliedly repeals the earlier one to the extent of the inconsistency [1]. In *Ellen Street Estates v Minister of Health* (1934) a provision in an earlier Act claiming that it would override later inconsistent Acts was held ineffective [1]; the later Act prevailed (also accept *Vauxhall Estates v Liverpool Corporation*, 1932) [1].

**10.** Laws LJ said that some statutes are "constitutional statutes", such as the European Communities Act 1972 and the Human Rights Act 1998 [1]. These cannot be impliedly repealed by a later inconsistent Act [1]; they can only be repealed by express words, so the ordinary rule of implied repeal does not apply to them [1].

**11.** Under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 the Lords cannot veto most public Bills [1]. If the Lords reject the Bill, it can be reintroduced in the next session and passed by the Commons, and after a delay of about a year it can receive Royal Assent without the Lords' consent [1] [1], as happened with the Hunting Act 2004 [1]. The Salisbury Convention also means the Lords would not normally block a manifesto Bill at second reading, so rejection would be politically controversial [1]. Before using the Parliament Acts, the government might first try to agree amendments through ping-pong [1].

**12.** For the statement: the Human Rights Act 1998 lets courts make declarations of incompatibility, and Parliament usually responds [1]; devolution has transferred law-making powers that would be politically very hard to reclaim [1]; *Thoburn* (2002) suggests some statutes are protected from implied repeal [1]; while the UK was in the EU, *Factortame* allowed courts to disapply Acts [1]. Against: a declaration does not invalidate an Act, so Parliament keeps the final word [1]; *Miller* (2017) confirmed the Sewel Convention is not legally enforceable [1]; withdrawal from the EU removed the *Factortame* limit [1]; *Pickin* (1974) and *Jackson* (2005) show courts still refuse to question validly passed Acts [1]. Conclusion: legally supremacy remains intact, but political and constitutional constraints make its full use increasingly unrealistic [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming stages without explaining what happens at each.
- Confusing the Parliament Acts with the Salisbury Convention.
- In supremacy essays, treating political limits as legal ones without saying so.
- Giving influences without examples or evaluation.
