Section A concentrates on case study 1, the US civil rights movement. Section B is a one-hour source paper on case study 2, apartheid South Africa, with the Paper 1 tariffs of 3, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks.

**Source A** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a leaflet distributed by ANC volunteers in a township, 1952.

> Volunteers! You will enter the "Europeans Only" entrance of the post office, you will walk the streets after curfew without a pass, and you will be arrested. Go calmly and with dignity. Do not resist the police and do not pay fines: choose prison. When the jails are full, the government will learn that unjust laws cannot be enforced against a whole people.

**Source B** -- *Invented for practice:* description of a cartoon published in a British newspaper, March 1960. A South African policeman, labelled "Law and Order", sits on a large pile of pass books. Smoke rises from his rifle. In the background, a crowd of small figures lies on the ground. The caption reads: "Order restored."

**Source C** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a speech by a National Party minister to Parliament, 1960.

> The disturbances at Sharpeville were the work of agitators who wish to destroy this country. The police faced a mob of thousands and acted to protect their lives. The organisations behind these events take their orders from communists abroad. The government will not allow them to exist, and it will not be moved by the opinions of newspapers in London or New York.

**Source D** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a modern historian's account of the anti-apartheid struggle, published in 2012.

> Sharpeville did not create the case for armed struggle; it ended the case against it. For a decade the ANC had petitioned, defied and boycotted, and each campaign had been answered with new laws and longer sentences. Once the organisation itself was banned, its leaders faced a choice between abandoning the struggle and fighting it by other means. Even then, the decision to begin with sabotage rather than attacks on people showed how reluctant many of them remained.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** methods used in the South to prevent Black citizens from voting before 1965. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the murder of Emmett Till in 1955 attracted national attention. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **two** reasons for the success of the Montgomery Bus Boycott. **[4]**

**4.** Outline the aims and methods of SNCC between 1960 and 1964. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the Birmingham campaign of 1963 was a turning point for federal policy. **[3]**

**6.** Compare the views of Martin Luther King Jr and Malcolm X on the methods the movement should use. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why the Voting Rights Act of 1965 was needed after the Civil Rights Act of 1964. **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** What, according to Source A, were the aims and methods of the volunteers? **[3]**

**9.** What is the message conveyed by Source B? **[2]**

**10.** With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source C for a historian studying the government's response to protest in 1960. **[4]**

**11.** Compare and contrast what Sources A and D reveal about non-violent protest against apartheid. **[6]**

**12.** Using the sources and your own knowledge, examine the reasons why protest against apartheid failed to change government policy between 1948 and 1964. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: literacy tests applied unfairly by white registrars (1); poll taxes (1); intimidation and violence against those who tried to register (1); economic pressure such as loss of jobs or credit (1); white primaries or deliberately obstructive registration procedures (1). **[2]**

**2.** His mother insisted on an open-casket funeral, and photographs of his body were published widely, so the brutality was seen nationally (1). The all-white jury acquitted the men accused, which exposed the failure of Southern justice (1). He was a 14-year-old boy visiting from Chicago, which made the case shocking to Northern audiences and helped mobilise a generation of activists (1). **[3]**

**3.** 2 marks for each developed reason: the Black community was united and organised through churches and the Montgomery Improvement Association, sustaining the boycott for over a year with car pools (2); the bus company depended on Black passengers, so the economic pressure was real (2); the legal case *Browder v. Gayle* ended bus segregation when the Supreme Court upheld the ruling in 1956 (2); King's leadership and non-violence attracted national publicity and sympathy (2). **[4]**

**4.** Aims: to end segregation and to win voting rights through grassroots, student-led action (1). Methods: sit-ins and participation in the Freedom Rides (1); voter registration in the Deep South, including Freedom Summer in Mississippi in 1964 (1). **[3]**

**5.** Television images of police dogs and fire hoses used against young marchers shocked national and international opinion (1). The violence embarrassed the United States during the Cold War and made the federal government's inaction harder to defend (1). In June 1963 Kennedy made a national address on civil rights and sent a bill to Congress that became the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (1). **[3]**

**6.** 1 mark for each valid point of comparison, up to 4: King advocated non-violent direct action, while Malcolm X defended self-defence "by any means necessary" (1); King sought integration within American society, while Malcolm X, as a Nation of Islam minister, called for separation and Black self-reliance (1); King appealed to Christian and American ideals to win white support, while Malcolm X saw white society as the oppressor (1); both condemned segregation and racial violence and sought dignity and rights for Black Americans (1); after leaving the Nation of Islam in 1964 Malcolm X moderated some views and spoke of human rights (1). **[4]**

**7.** Any one developed reason: the 1964 Act did little to stop literacy tests and intimidation that kept Black voters off the rolls in states such as Mississippi and Alabama (1); events at Selma in 1965 showed that voting rights could not be secured without federal registrars and suspension of the tests (1). **[2]**

**8.** 1 mark per point, up to 3: to defy apartheid laws such as segregated entrances and the pass and curfew laws (1); to accept arrest without resisting (1); to refuse to pay fines and choose prison (1); to fill the jails so that the laws could not be enforced (1). **[3]**

**9.** 1 mark per point, up to 2: the police used deadly force on protesters against the pass laws (1); the government's claim to have restored "law and order" is presented as hypocritical (1); the cartoonist condemns the killing, reflecting foreign hostility after Sharpeville (1). **[2]**

**10.** Markbands (summarised from the guide's Question 2 descriptors): **3-4 marks**, explicit discussion of both value and limitations, linked to origin, purpose and content; **1-2 marks**, limited, one-sided, or not linked to the topic.
- *Value:* origin, a government minister speaking in Parliament in 1960; purpose, to defend the police and justify banning the organisations; content shows the official explanation (agitators, communists, self-defence) and the decision to ban and ignore foreign criticism.
- *Limitations:* a political defence of the government, so it denies or minimises police responsibility and does not mention the dead; it gives no protester's view and cannot show how the response was carried out or received. **[4]**

**11.** Markbands: **5-6 marks**, detailed comparison and contrast with explicit links throughout; **3-4 marks**, some similarities and differences, possibly unbalanced; **1-2 marks**, superficial or sources described separately.
- *Comparisons:* both describe deliberate, organised defiance of apartheid laws; both show protesters accepting the cost of arrest or punishment.
- *Contrasts:* A is confident that mass arrests would force change, D shows that each campaign was "answered with new laws and longer sentences"; A is written at the start of the 1950s campaigns, D looks back on their failure; A sees non-violence as a strategy that can win, D as a strategy closed off by the bans; D adds that reluctance to use violence continued, which A cannot address. **[6]**

**12.** Markbands: **7-9 marks**, focused response using the sources and accurate own knowledge, with effective synthesis; **4-6 marks**, mostly focused, uses sources and some own knowledge; **1-3 marks**, limited focus, sources described or own knowledge only.
- *From the sources:* A (non-violent mass defiance), B (lethal force at Sharpeville), C (official refusal to compromise, communism used as justification), D (escalating repression and the bans).
- *Own knowledge, state power:* the Suppression of Communism Act (1950) and banning orders; the Treason Trial (1956-1961); the state of emergency and the Unlawful Organisations Act (1960); the arrest of Mandela (1962) and the Rivonia Trial (1963-1964).
- *Own knowledge, the movement:* the Defiance Campaign (1952); the Freedom Charter (1955); bus boycotts; the ANC-PAC split (1959); MK's sabotage from 1961.
- *Other factors:* the white electorate's support for the National Party; limited international pressure before 1964; South Africa's departure from the Commonwealth (1961) reducing outside leverage.
- *Judgement:* for example, protest failed mainly because the state was willing and able to use overwhelming repression, while divisions within the opposition limited its strength. **[9]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use knowledge of Mandela's later release in Question 12?**
No. The case study ends in 1964, and the question asks about that period only.

**Does Source B count as a visual source?**
Yes. In the exam the cartoon itself would be printed; here a description stands in for it.
