## Hydrocarbons and unsaturation

A **hydrocarbon** contains carbon and hydrogen only. A **saturated** hydrocarbon has only single bonds, so each carbon holds the maximum number of hydrogen atoms. An **unsaturated** hydrocarbon contains at least one C=C double bond (or a triple bond) and so holds fewer hydrogens than the saturated hydrocarbon with the same number of carbons.

| Family | General formula | Example | Bonds between carbons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | Propane, C₃H₈ | Single only |
| Alkene (one C=C) | CₙH₂ₙ | Propene, C₃H₆ | One double bond |
| Cycloalkane | CₙH₂ₙ | Cyclopropane, C₃H₆ | Single only (a ring) |

Alkenes and cycloalkanes share a formula. This matters: the formula alone does not prove a molecule has a C=C bond. A reaction such as decolourising bromine water is needed to distinguish them.

## What the double bond is made of

A C=C double bond contains two bonds of different kinds.

- A **sigma (σ) bond**: formed by head-on overlap of orbitals on the axis between the two carbon nuclei. The electron density lies directly between the nuclei.
- A **pi (π) bond**: formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals on the two carbons. The electron density lies in two regions, **above and below** the plane of the molecule.

Each carbon of the double bond makes three sigma bonds (to the other carbon and to two further atoms) and uses its fourth electron in the pi bond. In ethene, C₂H₄:

```
      H        H
       \      /
        C == C        sigma bonds: 5 (4 C–H and 1 C–C)
       /      \       pi bonds:    1
      H        H
```

### Shape

The three sigma bonds around each carbon repel each other as far as possible, giving a **trigonal planar** arrangement with bond angles of about **120°**. The six atoms of ethene all lie in one plane. The pi electron density sits above and below that plane.

### Restricted rotation

The pi bond only exists while the p orbitals overlap sideways. Twisting one end of the molecule about the C=C axis would pull the p orbitals out of line and break the pi bond. There is therefore **no free rotation** about a C=C bond. This is the origin of E/Z isomerism: if each carbon of the double bond has two different groups, the groups are fixed on the same side (Z) or on opposite sides (E).

## Why alkenes are reactive

Compare an alkane with an alkene.

| Feature | Alkane | Alkene |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds between carbons | σ bonds only | σ bond plus π bond |
| Bond polarity | C–C and C–H are essentially non-polar | The C=C region is electron rich |
| Typical reaction | Substitution by free radicals (needs UV light) | Addition with electrophiles at room temperature |
| Reactivity | Low | High |

The pi bond makes the difference.

1. The pi electrons lie **above and below** the plane, away from the line between the nuclei, so they are held less strongly and are more exposed than sigma electrons.
2. The double bond is a **region of high electron density**, which attracts species that are short of electrons.
3. An **electrophile** is a species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a covalent bond. Alkenes react with electrophiles, which is the basis of the Addition reactions of alkenes sub-topic.

The pi bond breaks, and each carbon forms a new sigma bond to an atom or group from the attacking reagent. The product is saturated, so the reaction is an **addition**.

## Bond enthalpy and the strength of the double bond

Use mean bond enthalpies (in kJ mol⁻¹): C–C 347, C=C 614, C–H 413, H–H 436.

The C=C bond is stronger than C–C, but it is **not twice as strong**. This tells you that the pi bond is weaker than the sigma bond:

```
pi bond enthalpy ≈ 614 − 347 = 267 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Hence the weaker, more exposed pi bond is the part that breaks in addition reactions, and the stronger C–C sigma bond stays.

## Worked example 1: counting sigma and pi bonds

**Question.** How many sigma and pi bonds are in propene, CH₃–CH=CH₂?

- C–H bonds: 3 in CH₃, 1 in CH and 2 in CH₂, so 6 sigma bonds.
- C–C single bond: 1 sigma bond.
- C=C double bond: 1 sigma and 1 pi bond.

Total: **8 sigma bonds and 1 pi bond**.

## Worked example 2: enthalpy change from bond enthalpies

**Question.** Use mean bond enthalpies to estimate the enthalpy change for C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆.

```
Bonds broken:  1 × C=C + 1 × H–H   = 614 + 436 = 1050 kJ
Bonds formed:  1 × C–C + 2 × C–H   = 347 + 2(413) = 1173 kJ
ΔH = bonds broken − bonds formed   = 1050 − 1173 = −123 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The negative value shows that adding hydrogen across the double bond is exothermic: the product bonds are stronger in total. Note that this is different from the enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene in the aromatic chemistry sub-topics.

## Worked example 3: identifying a hydrocarbon

**Question.** A hydrocarbon has a relative molecular mass of 70.0 and contains 85.7 % carbon by mass. It decolourises bromine water. Suggest a molecular formula and explain the test result.

```
Alkenes CnH2n have empirical formula CH₂ (Mr 14.0).
% carbon in CH₂ = 12.0 ÷ 14.0 × 100 = 85.7 %    (matches)
n = 70.0 ÷ 14.0 = 5
molecular formula = C₅H₁₀
```

The compound decolourises bromine water, so it is unsaturated: it is an alkene (pentene), not a cycloalkane. Without the test, a cycloalkane with the same formula would also fit the data.

## Worked example 4: counting alkene isomers

**Question.** How many alkenes have the molecular formula C₄H₈?

- but-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃
- (E)-but-2-ene
- (Z)-but-2-ene
- 2-methylpropene, (CH₃)₂C=CH₂

That makes **four** alkene isomers. But-2-ene has E/Z isomers because each double-bond carbon carries two different groups (H and CH₃). But-1-ene has two H atoms on one double-bond carbon, and 2-methylpropene has two CH₃ groups on one, so neither has E/Z isomers.

## Linking structure to properties

- Alkenes are **non-polar** overall, so they are insoluble in water but soluble in non-polar solvents. Their boiling points rise with chain length because of increasing London forces.
- They burn in air to form carbon dioxide and water, as alkanes do.
- The reactivity comes from the pi bond, not from polarity of the molecule.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the double bond is "two sigma bonds". It is one sigma and one pi bond.
- Saying that the C=C bond is reactive because it is strong. It is reactive because the pi component is relatively weak and electron rich.
- Drawing bond angles of 109.5° around the double-bond carbons. They are about 120°.
- Saying pi electrons lie "between the nuclei". They lie above and below the plane.
- Describing alkenes as attracted to nucleophiles. The electron-rich double bond attracts electrophiles.
- Assuming CₙH₂ₙ proves a compound is an alkene.
- Defining unsaturated as "has a double bond" and forgetting to mention that the compound has fewer hydrogens than the saturated equivalent.
- Saying there is "no rotation" without giving the reason: the sideways overlap of the p orbitals would be lost.

## Exam technique

- **Explain why alkenes are reactive**: refer to the pi bond, the high electron density and attraction to electrophiles, in that order.
- **Describe the bonding**: name the sigma and pi bonds and say where the electron density lies.
- **Calculate**: write the bond breaking and bond making sums separately.
- **Give a test**: bromine water goes from orange to colourless; name both colours.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the pi bond weaker than the sigma bond?**
The sideways overlap of p orbitals is less effective than the head-on overlap in a sigma bond, and the pi electrons are further from the nuclei.

**Why can alkenes undergo addition but alkanes cannot?**
An alkane has no pi bond and so has no spare electron pair to share with an attacking reagent. An alkene can use the electrons of the pi bond to bond to the electrophile.

**What is the shape around each carbon of ethene?**
Trigonal planar, with bond angles of about 120°.
