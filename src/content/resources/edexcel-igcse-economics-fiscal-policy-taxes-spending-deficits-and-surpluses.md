## 1. What fiscal policy is

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of taxation and government spending to influence the level of economic activity and to help meet its macroeconomic objectives. A good definition has two parts: the tools (taxes and spending) and the purpose (influencing the economy). The official command table says a "What is meant by" question needs two separate parts to the definition.

Fiscal policy is set in the government's budget. Two directions are worth naming:

- **Expansionary** (or reflationary) fiscal policy: higher government spending, lower taxes, or both. It aims to raise total demand.
- **Contractionary** fiscal policy: lower government spending, higher taxes, or both. It aims to reduce total demand.

## 2. Government revenue: direct and indirect taxes

Government revenue is the money the state receives, and most of it comes from taxes.

**Direct taxes** are charged on income or profit, and the person or firm that is taxed pays the government directly. Examples: income tax and corporation tax (tax on company profits).

**Indirect taxes** are charged on spending. A seller collects the tax in the price and passes it to the government, so the buyer bears it. Examples: sales tax or VAT, and excise duties on goods such as fuel or tobacco.

| | Direct taxes | Indirect taxes |
|---|---|---|
| Charged on | Income and profit | Spending |
| Who pays the government | The taxpayer | The seller, who passes it on in the price |
| Examples | Income tax, corporation tax | Sales tax, excise duty |
| Effect of a rise | Lower take-home pay or profit | Higher prices |

### Worked example: income tax and net pay

Country A (illustrative) charges income tax in bands: 0% on the first 10,000 earned, 20% on income from 10,001 to 30,000, and 40% on income above 30,000.

For gross pay of 42,000:

- First 10,000 at 0% = 0
- Next 20,000 at 20% = 4,000
- Remaining 12,000 at 40% = 4,800
- Total tax = 8,800
- Net pay = 42,000 − 8,800 = **33,200**
- Average tax rate = 8,800 ÷ 42,000 × 100 = **20.95%**

For gross pay of 15,000: tax = 5,000 at 20% = 1,000; net pay = **14,000**; average rate = 6.67%. The higher earner pays a larger share of income in tax. A tax with rates that rise with income takes more from the rich, and this is how direct taxes support redistribution.

### Worked example: an indirect tax

Country A charges a 12% sales tax. A coat has a pre-tax price of 250. Tax = 250 × 0.12 = 30, so the shelf price is **280**.

Now compare two households, both buying only taxed goods. A low-income household has an income of 12,000 and spends 9,000, tax included. The tax inside this spending is 9,000 × 12 ÷ 112 = 964.29, which is **8.04%** of its income. A high-income household has an income of 60,000 and spends 20,000. The tax inside is 2,142.86, which is only **3.57%** of income. A flat percentage tax on spending takes a bigger share of a low income, because poorer households spend most of what they earn.

## 3. Government expenditure: the main areas

Government spending (expenditure) pays for public services and transfers. The main areas are:

- **Healthcare**: hospitals, doctors, public health.
- **Education**: schools, colleges, teacher pay.
- **Social protection**: benefit payments such as unemployment and pension payments, which move income from taxpayers to recipients.
- **Defence and public order**: armed forces, police, courts.
- **Infrastructure**: roads, railways, ports, water and power networks.
- **Debt interest**: the cost of servicing past borrowing.

## 4. Fiscal deficits and fiscal surpluses

- **Fiscal deficit** = government spending − government revenue, when spending is the larger. The government borrows to cover it.
- **Fiscal surplus** = government revenue − government spending, when revenue is the larger.

A deficit is a flow in one year. Deficits added together over time build up the national debt, which is a stock.

### Worked example: the Country A budget (illustrative year, currency units in billions)

| Revenue | Amount | Spending | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 310 | Healthcare | 240 |
| Corporation tax | 120 | Education | 160 |
| Sales tax | 280 | Social protection | 300 |
| Excise duties | 90 | Defence | 70 |
| Other revenue | 50 | Infrastructure | 90 |
| | | Debt interest | 60 |
| | | Other | 80 |
| **Total** | **850** | **Total** | **1,000** |

- Deficit = 1,000 − 850 = **150**.
- Nominal gross domestic product (GDP) is 5,000, so the deficit is 150 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **3.0% of GDP**.
- Direct taxes = 310 + 120 = 430, which is 50.6% of revenue. Indirect taxes = 280 + 90 = 370, which is 43.5%.
- Healthcare = 240 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **24.0%** of spending. Social protection is the largest item at 30.0%.
- Debt interest takes 60 ÷ 850 × 100 = **7.1%** of revenue before a single new service is paid for.

Country B (illustrative) collects 420 and spends 390, so it has a **surplus of 30**. With GDP of 2,500, that is 1.2% of GDP.

## 5. The impact of a deficit and of a surplus

**A deficit can help.**

- Borrowing to build roads and schools adds to demand now and to productive capacity later.
- In a downturn, a deficit supports demand and jobs. Spending on benefits rises and tax receipts fall, which cushions households.

**A deficit can harm.**

- Interest on the debt takes up a bigger share of revenue each year, as the Country A example shows. That money cannot go to health or education.
- Large borrowing may push up interest rates. This raises business borrowing costs and can reduce private investment, called crowding out.
- If lenders lose confidence, borrowing becomes costly, and future taxpayers face higher taxes or spending cuts.

**A surplus can help.**

- It can repay debt, which cuts future interest bills.
- It builds a reserve for later emergencies.

**A surplus can harm.**

- It is reached by taxing more, spending less, or both. Either one takes demand out of the economy, which may slow growth and raise unemployment.
- Cuts may hit healthcare, education or infrastructure, which affect long-run output.

A balanced budget is the in-between case, where revenue equals spending.

## 6. Fiscal policy and the macroeconomic objectives

Build each answer as a chain: change in policy, then change in spending, then change in demand and output, then the objective.

**Economic growth.** Higher government spending or lower taxes raise aggregate demand. Firms produce more and GDP grows.

*Worked example.* Country C (illustrative) has GDP of 2,000. It raises spending on infrastructure by 60. The direct effect is 60 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 3.0% of GDP. Suppose construction workers and suppliers then spend part of their new income, so total GDP rises by 90: growth is 90 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **4.5%**.

**Unemployment.** More demand means firms hire more workers. Public works create jobs directly. Higher spending on training can also help.

**Inflation.** Fiscal expansion pushes demand towards the economy's capacity. If output cannot rise fast enough, prices rise. Contractionary policy cuts demand and eases price pressure.

**Current account of the balance of payments.** Higher incomes mean more spending on imports, so the current account may worsen. Cutting demand has the opposite effect.

**Redistribution of income.** Progressive direct taxes, benefits and free public services move income towards low earners.

**Environmental protection.** Indirect taxes on pollution or fuel raise the cost of damaging goods. Spending on public transport or clean energy supports greener production.

### Worked example: a tax cut

Country D (illustrative) has GDP of 60,000 (millions) and cuts income tax so that 800,000 workers each gain 1,500.

- Extra disposable income = 800,000 × 1,500 = 1,200 million.
- Households spend 80%: extra spending = 960 million. They save 240 million.
- A quarter of that spending is on imports: 240 million leaks abroad. Domestic demand rises by 720 million.
- That is 720 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **1.2%** of GDP.

The tax cut helps growth, but only part of it stays in the economy.

## 7. Evaluating fiscal policy

An evaluation answer weighs points and reaches a supported conclusion. Useful points:

- **Size of the effect.** It depends on how much people save or spend on imports, as in the tax-cut example.
- **Time lags.** Spending projects take time to plan and build, and the economy may have moved on.
- **State of the economy.** Expansion works best when resources are idle. When the economy is close to full capacity, it mainly pushes prices up.
- **Cost.** Borrowing to fund expansion raises the deficit and debt.
- **Who is affected.** Tax rises hit some groups harder than others.

## Common errors

- Writing "indirect taxes are paid by firms". The firm collects and pays them over, but the buyer bears them in the price.
- Treating a deficit as the national debt. The deficit is one year's gap. The debt is the total owed.
- Describing only the first step of a chain and not reaching the named objective.
- Calling a surplus always good. It can reduce demand.
