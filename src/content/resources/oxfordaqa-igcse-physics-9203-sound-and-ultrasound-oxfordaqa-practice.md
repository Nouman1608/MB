These questions cover **3.3.3 Sound and ultrasound**, statements a to h, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statements d to h are P content. Take the speed of sound as 340 m/s in air and 1500 m/s in soft tissue and sea water unless a question says otherwise. Learn the content first from the [Sound and ultrasound study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-sound-and-ultrasound-oxfordaqa/) and the [Sound and ultrasound revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-sound-and-ultrasound-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State whether sound waves are transverse or longitudinal, and describe how the particles of the medium move. **[2]**

**2.** State the approximate range of frequencies that humans can hear. **[1]**

**3.** A bat emits a call at 45 kHz. State whether a human can hear it, and give a reason. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the wavelength in air of a 20 Hz sound. **[2]**

**5.** State which property of a sound wave determines:

**(a)** its pitch **[1]**

**(b)** its loudness. **[1]**

**6.** A thunderclap echoes off a cliff and is heard again 2.4 s later by the person who heard the original sound. Calculate the distance to the cliff. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** An electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar. A pump slowly removes the air from the jar.

**(a)** Describe what happens to the sound heard outside the jar. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain this observation. **[2]**

**(c)** State why the bell can still be seen. **[1]**

**8.** A microphone connected to an oscilloscope picks up two notes, X and Y. The settings are the same for both. The time base is 1.0 ms per division.

- Note X: one complete wave covers 4.0 divisions; peaks are 2.0 divisions high.
- Note Y: one complete wave covers 2.0 divisions; peaks are 3.0 divisions high.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of each note. **[3]**

**(b)** Compare the pitch and loudness of the two notes. **[2]**

**9.** A student says: "You can hear people talking in the next room through an open door because sound reflects off the walls."

**(a)** Name the other wave effect that explains this. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why this effect is large for sound passing through a doorway. **[2]**

**10.** An ultrasound scanner sends a pulse into a patient's arm. An oscilloscope shows the transmitted pulse at 0 and two echoes, at 3.0 divisions and 8.0 divisions. The time base is 20 μs per division.

**(a)** Explain why more than one echo is received. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the depth of each boundary below the skin surface. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest why the second echo is smaller than the first. **[1]**

**11.** A survey ship uses ultrasound to measure the depth of the sea bed.

**(a)** Describe how the ship's equipment produces ultrasound. **[1]**

**(b)** At one point the echo returns 1.2 s after the pulse is sent. Calculate the depth of the water. **[2]**

**(c)** Later the echo time falls to 0.30 s. Calculate the new depth and describe what this suggests about the sea bed. **[2]**

**12.** Ultrasound is used in medicine for prenatal scanning and for treating kidney stones.

**(a)** Describe how an ultrasound scan produces an image of a fetus. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why ultrasound is preferred to X-rays for prenatal scanning. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe how ultrasound is used to treat kidney stones, and give **one** advantage over surgery. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Longitudinal [1]; the particles vibrate backwards and forwards parallel to the direction the sound travels [1]. **[2]**

**2.** About 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz [1]. **[1]**

**3.** No [1]; 45 kHz is above the upper limit of human hearing, about 20 kHz, so it is ultrasound [1]. **[2]**

**4.** λ = v ÷ f = 340 ÷ 20 [1] = **17 m** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Frequency [1].

**(b)** Amplitude [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Time to reach the cliff = 2.4 ÷ 2 = 1.2 s [1]; s = v × t = 340 × 1.2 [1] = **408 m**, about 410 m [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** The sound becomes quieter and eventually cannot be heard [1].

**(b)** Sound is a vibration that travels through a medium [1]; as the air is removed there are fewer particles to carry the vibrations, and a vacuum cannot transmit sound [1].

**(c)** Light (an electromagnetic wave) can travel through a vacuum [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** X: T = 4.0 × 1.0 = 4.0 ms = 0.0040 s, f = 1 ÷ 0.0040 = **250 Hz** [1]; Y: T = 2.0 ms = 0.0020 s, f = **500 Hz** [1]; correct method (period from divisions × time base, then f = 1 ÷ T) [1].

**(b)** Y has a higher pitch than X, because its frequency is greater [1]; Y is louder than X, because its amplitude is greater [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Diffraction [1].

**(b)** The wavelength of speech sounds is comparable to the width of a doorway [1], so the sound spreads out considerably after passing through the gap [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** The ultrasound is partially reflected at each boundary between different tissues [1]; the transmitted part travels on and is reflected again at a deeper boundary, giving a later echo [1].

**(b)** First echo: t = 3.0 × 20 = 60 μs = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ s [1]; depth = 1500 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 2 = **0.045 m** (4.5 cm) [1]. Second echo: t = 8.0 × 20 = 160 μs = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ s [1]; depth = 1500 × 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 2 = **0.12 m** (12 cm) [1].

**(c)** Some of the ultrasound energy was reflected at the first boundary (or absorbed by the tissue), so less reaches and returns from the second [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* the divide-by-two step is easy to forget in a multi-step trace question; write "there and back" beside your working.

**11. (a)** An electronic system (an electrical signal at a frequency above 20 kHz) makes a transducer vibrate [1].

**(b)** depth = 1500 × 1.2 ÷ 2 [1] = **900 m** [1].

**(c)** depth = 1500 × 0.30 ÷ 2 = **225 m** [1]; the sea bed rises steeply here, for example an underwater hill or ridge (or there is an object such as a shoal of fish or a wreck) [1]. **[5]**

**12. (a)** Pulses of ultrasound are sent into the body [1]; they are partially reflected at boundaries between different tissues [1]; the times (and strengths) of the echoes are used by a computer to build up an image [1].

**(b)** Ultrasound is non-ionising [1], so it does not damage the cells of the developing fetus, whereas X-rays are ionising and can harm cells [1].

**(c)** High-energy ultrasound is directed at the stone from outside the body [1]; the vibrations break the stone into small pieces that pass out of the body naturally [1]. Advantage: no surgical cut is needed (so less risk of infection and faster recovery) [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to halve the echo time.
- Not converting μs or ms to seconds.
- Linking pitch to amplitude.
- Medical explanations that do not say ultrasound is non-ionising.
