Condensed recall for **8.1 Population and economic development patterns** (Unit 1: Changing
population). For the full explanation, read the
[Population Distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/).

## Why population is unevenly distributed

| Physical factors | Human factors |
|---|---|
| Climate | Economic opportunity |
| Relief (terrain) | Historical patterns of settlement |
| Soil fertility | Political stability |
| Availability of fresh water | Infrastructure |
| Resource access | |

A strong answer applies **named** factors to a **named** place rather than listing categories.

## Global pattern

- Most of the world's people live in the **Northern Hemisphere**, and large clusters are found in
  **East Asia, South Asia and Europe**, with smaller ones in West Africa, the Nile valley and the
  north-eastern USA.
- **Sparse areas** include hot deserts (the Sahara), cold regions (northern Canada, Siberia,
  Antarctica), high mountains (the Himalaya, Andes) and dense rainforest (the Amazon basin).
- **Egypt** is a classic example of physical control: the great majority of its people live along
  the narrow Nile valley and delta, a small fraction of the country's area, because the rest is desert.
- **Australia:** most people live near the coast, especially in the south-east, where rainfall and
  historical settlement concentrated cities; the dry interior is very sparsely populated.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population density | People per unit area, usually per square kilometre |
| Population distribution | The overall pattern of where people are located |
| Carrying capacity | Maximum population an environment can sustainably support given its resources |
| Natural change | Births minus deaths |
| Net migration | Immigration minus emigration |

The same density can hide very different distributions: two areas with 18 people over nine squares
both average 2 per square, but one may be evenly spread and the other clustered.

**Density contrast:** Bangladesh has well over 1,000 people per square kilometre, one of the highest
densities of any large country, supported by fertile delta soils that allow intensive rice farming;
Mongolia has only a few people per square kilometre because of its dry, cold climate and short
growing season.

## Classifying economic development

- The **World Bank** groups countries by gross national income (GNI) per capita into **low-income,
  lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income and high-income** countries; the thresholds are revised
  every year.
- **Emerging economies** are countries growing rapidly and industrialising, such as China, India,
  Brazil and Indonesia. Groupings such as BRICS reflect their rising weight.
- Classifications are **simplifications**: a single average hides inequality within a country, and
  countries move between groups over time.

## Core and periphery at the national scale

**Friedmann's core-periphery model** (1966) describes how development concentrates in a dynamic
**core** region while the **periphery** supplies labour and raw materials and falls behind.
**Myrdal's cumulative causation** (1957) explains why: investment attracts more investment and
migrants (**backwash effects** drain the periphery), until growth eventually spreads outwards
(**spread effects**).

| Country | Core | Periphery |
|---|---|---|
| Brazil | The south-east (São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro) | Much of the north and north-east |
| Italy | The industrial north | The south (the Mezzogiorno) |
| UK | London and the South East | Parts of northern England, Wales and Scotland |

Governments respond with regional policy: new capitals (Brazil moved its capital to Brasília in
1960, partly to develop the interior), infrastructure, tax incentives and investment in lagging regions.

## Megacity growth

A **megacity** has more than 10 million people. Megacities grow through **natural increase**
(young populations with many births) and **migration**: rural-urban, international and forced.
Examples of rapidly growing megacities include **Lagos** (Nigeria), **Dhaka** (Bangladesh), **Delhi**
(India) and **Kinshasa** (DR Congo).

**Case-study outline: Dhaka.** Migrants arrive from rural areas, including people displaced by river
erosion, flooding and cyclones; the garment industry offers jobs, particularly for women. Growth has
outpaced housing, water, sanitation and transport, so many people live in informal settlements, and
traffic congestion is severe. Learn your own figures carefully from a reliable source.

## Demographic transition model

The demographic transition model describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops.

- Both rates start **high**, with death rates falling first.
- Births exceed deaths by the widest gap in the middle stages, so growth is **fastest** then.
- Both rates end **low**, and growth slows.

Each stage has a distinct effect on overall growth rate. The model links population change to
economic development, but it is a generalisation, so note where real countries differ.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Main reasons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | High and fluctuating | Disease, famine, little health care |
| 2 | High | Falling rapidly | Better food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination |
| 3 | Falling | Low and still falling | Contraception, women's education and work, urbanisation, lower infant mortality |
| 4 | Low | Low | High living standards; children costly to raise |
| 5 (sometimes added) | Very low | Low or rising slightly | Ageing population; natural decrease, as in Japan or Italy |

**Limitations:** based on the experience of Europe; many lower-income countries passed through
stage 2 much faster because of imported medicine; it ignores migration; and culture, religion and
government policy can hold birth rates up or push them down regardless of income.

## Exam traps

- Confusing density with distribution.
- Blending natural change and migration into one figure instead of stating them separately.
- Listing physical factors with no named place.
- Treating carrying capacity as fixed; resources and technology change it.
- Treating income groups as fixed labels rather than annually revised thresholds.
- Assuming every country will follow the demographic transition model in sequence.

## Self-test

1. Name three physical and three human factors affecting distribution.
2. Distinguish density from distribution.
3. Define carrying capacity.
4. Why is growth fastest in the middle stages of the demographic transition model?
5. What are the two components of population change?
6. Why is Egypt's population concentrated in a small area?
7. Name the four World Bank income groups.
8. What are backwash effects?
9. Give two ways a megacity grows.
10. Why does the death rate fall in stage 2?
11. Give two limitations of the demographic transition model.

**Answers:** 1. Physical: climate, relief, soil fertility, fresh water, resource access; human: economic
opportunity, historical settlement, political stability, infrastructure. 2. Density is people per unit
area; distribution is the pattern of where they are. 3. The maximum population an environment can
sustainably support. 4. Death rates have fallen while birth rates are still high, so the gap is
widest. 5. Natural change and net migration. 6. Most of the country is desert, so people live along
the Nile valley and delta. 7. Low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income. 8. The draining of
labour and capital from the periphery towards the core. 9. Natural increase and migration. 10. Better
food, clean water, sanitation and vaccination. 11. For example it is based on Europe and ignores
migration.
