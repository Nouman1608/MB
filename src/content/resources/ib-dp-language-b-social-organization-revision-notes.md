## The theme in one box

```
Guiding principle   "Explore the ways in which groups of people organize
                     themselves, or are organized, through common systems
                     or interests."
Optional topics      social relationships · community · social engagement ·
                     education · the working world · law and order
Possible questions   1. What is the individual's role in the community?
                     2. What role do rules and regulations play in the
                        formation of a society?
```

- The **theme** is prescribed. The **topics** are optional and recommended, so your class may have
  studied some or all of them.
- Key phrase: **"organize themselves, or are organized"**. Two directions: from below (people choose
  to act together) and from above (a structure is set for them).
- One-line contrast: Identities = who I am; Experiences = what happened to me; Social organization
  = how groups are run and my part in them.

## Two directions, six topics

| Topic | From below: people organize themselves | From above: people are organized |
|---|---|---|
| Social relationships | A friend group's unwritten rules | Family roles and expectations |
| Community | Residents run a street party | The council sets parking zones |
| Social engagement | Volunteers start a litter pick | A school requires service hours |
| Education | A student council proposes a change | A ministry sets the curriculum |
| The working world | A cooperative shares decisions | A manager writes the rota |
| Law and order | Neighbours agree quiet hours | Parliament passes a law |

## Vocabulary map

| Topic | People and institutions | Actions | Words that judge a system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social relationships | peer, neighbour, sibling, role model | to get on with, to fall out, to trust | supportive, toxic, close-knit |
| Community | resident, council, local shop, association | to belong, to take part, to organize | welcoming, isolated, lively |
| Social engagement | volunteer, campaigner, charity, petition | to volunteer, to campaign, to sign, to vote | committed, apathetic, worthwhile |
| Education | head teacher, pupil, curriculum, timetable | to enrol, to revise, to fail, to repeat a year | demanding, outdated, inclusive |
| The working world | employer, employee, colleague, contract | to apply, to hire, to resign, to promote | well paid, insecure, flexible |
| Law and order | law, fine, court, rights, duty | to obey, to break, to enforce, to ban | fair, strict, unjust, proportionate |

Make the same table in your target language. The last column is what lets you answer a possible
question instead of describing.

## Rule language toolkit

| Function | English | French | German | Spanish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Obligation | must / have to | *il faut*, *devoir* | *müssen* | *tener que*, *hay que* |
| No obligation | don't have to | *ne pas être obligé de* | *nicht müssen* | *no hace falta*, *no es necesario* |
| Permission | may / can / be allowed to | *avoir le droit de*, *pouvoir* | *dürfen* | *poder*, *se permite* |
| Prohibition | must not / not permitted | *il est interdit de*, *il ne faut pas* | *nicht dürfen*, *verboten* | *está prohibido*, *se prohíbe* |
| Recommendation | should | *il vaut mieux*, *devrait* | *sollte* | *debería* |
| Impersonal rule | "Food is not permitted" | *on* | *man* | *se* |

If your target language is not in the table, build the same six rows with your teacher. The row that
catches most learners is **no obligation vs prohibition**: German *Du musst nicht* = you don't have
to; *Du darfst nicht* = you must not.

## Formal register checklist

```
[ ] Formal "you" chosen (vous / Sie / usted or your language's form) and kept throughout
[ ] Set opening formula for your language
[ ] Reason for writing in the first paragraph
[ ] Polite requests ("I would be grateful if…"), not orders
[ ] No slang, no text-speak, no exclamation marks in a row
[ ] Set closing formula for your language
```

Formulas to learn exactly: French *Madame, Monsieur,* … *Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur,
l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.* German *Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,* … *Mit
freundlichen Grüßen*. Spanish *Estimado señor / Estimada señora:* … *Atentamente*.

## Method in steps: reading a rule text

```
1. Identify who wrote the rules and for whom
2. Classify each rule: obligation / no obligation / permission / prohibition
3. Circle limits: only, unless, except, up to, at least
4. Separate the rule itself from people's reactions to it
5. Ask what the rules protect, and whose interests they serve
6. Answer with the text's exact words where a short answer is asked
```

## Method in steps: writing to an institution

```
1. Context, audience, purpose (the brief's conceptual understandings)
2. Opening formula + who you are + why you are writing
3. The situation now, with specific effects on specific people
4. Acknowledge the other side's reason
5. Your proposal, with one hypothetical ("If…, then…")
6. Polite request for action or reply + closing formula
```

## Method in steps: an SL oral image on this theme

```
1. Describe briefly: who, where, what is happening
2. Direction: did these people organize themselves, or were they organized?
3. Role: what is each person's part? (possible question 1)
4. Rules: what rules, visible or unwritten, shape the scene? (possible question 2)
5. Culture: link to a specific example from the target-language culture
6. Judge: your view, with a reason
```

## Must-know distinctions

- **Community vs society.** A community is a group linked by place or shared interest (a
  neighbourhood, a club). A society is the wider organized whole, with its laws and institutions.
- **Social relationships vs social engagement.** Relationships are the links between people (family,
  friends, neighbours). Engagement is taking part in public life (volunteering, campaigning).
- **Rule vs law.** A rule is set by a group or organization (a school, a club). A law is made by the
  state and applies to everyone in its area, with legal penalties.
- **Right vs responsibility.** A right is what you are entitled to; a responsibility is what you owe
  others. Arguments on this theme often weigh the two.
- **Don't have to vs must not.** No obligation against prohibition. Build each in your target
  language from memory.
- **Formal vs informal "you".** Formal for officials and strangers, informal for friends and family.
  Never mix them in one text.
- **SL vs HL oral stimulus.** SL: a visual image clearly relevant to one or more themes. HL: an
  excerpt from one of the two literary works studied.
- **SL vs HL Paper 1.** Same outline; HL tasks demand more complex language and structures,
  higher-order thinking, and have a higher word range.

## Worked reminder: informal to formal

Original text written for Marlbridge. A message to a friend:

> Hey! They're shutting the pool on Sundays. So annoying. Can you tell them it's a rubbish idea?

The same content for the manager of the leisure centre:

> Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing about the planned Sunday closure of the swimming pool. Sunday is
> the only day on which many families can swim together. I would be grateful if you could explain
> the reasons for this decision and consider opening for at least a few hours.

- Greeting and purpose: *Dear Sir or Madam*, *I am writing about*.
- Emotion replaced by a specific effect: *the only day on which many families can swim together*.
- Order replaced by polite request: *I would be grateful if you could*.
- A compromise offered: *at least a few hours*.

## Quick self-test

1. Quote the guiding principle of Social organization.
2. Name the six optional recommended topics.
3. State the two possible questions for this theme in the brief.
4. A text describes neighbours who set up a rota to check on elderly residents. Is this people
   organizing themselves or being organized?
5. Which topic best fits a text about a teenager's first job interview?
6. What is the difference between "You don't have to wear a tie" and "You must not wear a tie"?
7. In German, which verb do you need for "must not": *müssen* or *dürfen*?
8. Rewrite "Don't eat in here" as an impersonal notice in English.
9. Give one difference between a rule and a law.
10. At SL, what kind of stimulus is used for the individual oral?
11. What percentage of the final grade does the internal assessment carry?
12. Name three of the five conceptual understandings the brief lists.

### Answers

1. "Explore the ways in which groups of people organize themselves, or are organized, through
   common systems or interests."
2. Social relationships, community, social engagement, education, the working world, law and order.
3. "What is the individual's role in the community?" and "What role do rules and regulations play in
   the formation of a society?"
4. Organizing themselves: the neighbours chose to act together; no one imposed it.
5. The working world.
6. The first is no obligation (you may wear one if you like); the second is a prohibition.
7. *Dürfen*, negated: *nicht dürfen*. *Nicht müssen* means "don't have to".
8. "Food is not permitted in this room" (or "Eating is not allowed here").
9. A rule is set by a group or organization for its members; a law is made by the state, applies to
   everyone in its area and carries legal penalties.
10. A visual image clearly relevant to one or more of the themes.
11. 25% (the individual oral).
12. Any three of: context, audience, purpose, meaning, variation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading "must not" as "don't have to" (or the reverse) and so answering a true/false question
  wrongly.
- Ignoring a limiting word (*only*, *unless*, *except*, *at least*) that changes what a rule allows.
- Crediting a rule-maker's view to a person who is only reacting to the rule, or the reverse.
- Mixing formal and informal "you" in a letter to an institution, or closing a formal letter with a
  friendly sign-off.
- Using an informal register for a council, head teacher or employer because the topic feels
  familiar.
- Describing school or work systems without judging them, so neither possible question is answered.
- Writing only about rules imposed from above and forgetting how people organize themselves.
- Stating unchecked "facts" about a target-language country's laws or schools instead of using a
  text you have studied.
