This guide goes deeper into section **6.4 Interested parties** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Candidates should understand "how accounting information may be used for decision-making" by owners, managers, employees, banks, investors and lenders, suppliers, customers, governments / tax authorities, club members, and "other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies".

The shared [inter-business comparison, interested parties and limitations guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-inter-business-comparison-interested-parties-and-limita/) introduces each group. This page takes one company's figures and shows each party reaching its own decision from them, because exam questions reward the link between a named user, a specific figure and a decision. Recall the points with the [interested parties revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [interested parties practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties-practice/). All organisations and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## The key idea: a decision, not just an interest

A weak answer says "the bank is interested in the accounts". A strong answer names the decision and the information that helps make it: "the bank uses the acid test ratio and the profit for the year to decide whether to grant an overdraft, because it needs to know that the business can pay interest and repay on time". Every party below is described in that way.

| Party | Typical decision | Most useful information |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Whether to continue, expand or invest more; how much to withdraw | Profit, margins, ROCE, trend over years |
| Managers | How to control costs, set prices, plan and manage working capital | Detailed costs, margins, inventory and receivables days |
| Employees | Whether jobs are secure; whether a pay rise is affordable | Profit and its trend, liquidity |
| Banks | Whether to lend or extend an overdraft, and on what terms | Liquidity ratios, profit, assets available as security, existing loans |
| Investors and lenders | Whether to buy shares or lend; whether returns justify the risk | ROCE, profit, dividends paid, existing debt |
| Suppliers | Whether to give credit and how much | Trade payables days, liquidity ratios |
| Customers | Whether the business will keep supplying goods, spares and after-sales service | Profit trend, liquidity, continuity |
| Governments / tax authorities | How much tax is due; national statistics and policy | Profit for the year, revenue, wages paid |
| Club members | Whether subscriptions should change; whether the committee manages funds well | Surplus or deficit, accumulated fund, income from activities |
| Public and environmental bodies | Effect on local jobs and the environment | Number employed, spending on environmental matters, continuity |

## Worked example: Greystone Bakery Ltd

Greystone Bakery Ltd supplies bread to shops and cafés. Extracts from its ratios:

| Ratio | 2027 | 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Profit margin | 11.20% | 8.60% |
| ROCE | 17.50% | 13.80% |
| Current ratio | 1.90 : 1 | 1.30 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1.10 : 1 | 0.60 : 1 |
| Trade receivables turnover | 26 days | 28 days |
| Trade payables turnover | 34 days | 75 days |

The company's suppliers allow 30 days' credit. Here is how several parties might use these figures.

**A flour supplier deciding whether to increase Greystone's credit limit.** Greystone now takes 75 days to pay against 30 days allowed, and its acid test ratio has fallen to 0.60 : 1. The supplier is likely to refuse a higher limit, ask for payment on delivery or charge for late payment. The payables figure matters most to this party.

**The bank considering a request for a larger overdraft.** Liquidity has fallen sharply, and profit has fallen too, so there is less profit to cover interest. The bank may lend only with security over Greystone's premises or equipment, or at a higher interest rate.

**An investor considering buying shares.** ROCE has fallen from 17.50% to 13.80%. The investor compares this with returns available elsewhere and with the risk shown by the weak liquidity. A fall in both profitability and liquidity makes the shares less attractive unless there is a clear reason, such as new ovens that will raise output next year.

**Employees asking for a 5% pay rise.** Profit has fallen and cash is short, so the directors may argue that the rise is unaffordable. Employees might point out that the company is still profitable. Both sides use the same statements to support their case.

**A café chain that buys all its bread from Greystone.** As a customer, it needs a reliable supplier. Falling liquidity and late payments to flour suppliers warn that deliveries could be disrupted, so the chain may look for a second bakery to reduce its risk.

**The managers.** They can see from the ratios exactly where to act: the profit margin fall needs a look at prices and costs, and the payables days show that suppliers are being used as a source of finance, which risks the supply of flour.

## A club's members

Club members use the income and expenditure account and the statement of financial position. Suppose a swimming club's income and expenditure account shows a deficit of $2,400 for the year, after the charge for pool hire rose by $4,300. Members can decide whether to vote for a higher subscription, ask the committee to negotiate the pool hire, or hold more galas. The accumulated fund tells them whether the club has reserves to absorb a deficit for a year.

## Other interested parties

The syllabus names public and environmental bodies as examples. A town council may use a factory's accounts to judge whether it is likely to stay and keep employing local people. An environmental group may look for spending on waste disposal or cleaner equipment. These users often need information that financial statements show only partly, which links to the [limitations of accounting statements guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements/).

## Conflicting interests

Different parties can want opposite outcomes from the same figures.

- **Shareholders and lenders**: shareholders may want high dividends now; lenders prefer cash to stay in the company so loans can be repaid.
- **Employees and owners**: higher wages raise expenses and reduce the profit left for owners.
- **Owners and tax authorities**: owners prefer lower taxable profit; the tax authority needs the true figure.
- **Club members**: some want low subscriptions; others want better facilities that cost more.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a party is "interested in profit" without naming a decision.
- Using the same answer for every party.
- Confusing suppliers (who give the business credit) with customers (who buy from it).
- Forgetting that managers are inside the business and have far more detailed information than outsiders.
- Missing club members, governments or environmental bodies when a question asks for "other" users.

## Exam technique

Questions on 6.4 may use command words such as **identify**, **state**, **explain** and **suggest**. For **explain**, use a three-part chain: party, decision, information: "A supplier would look at the trade payables turnover to decide whether to give credit, because a long payment period suggests it may be paid late." When a question gives figures, quote the figure that matters to that party. If asked how two parties' interests conflict, name both sides and the figure they would read differently. For the whole topic, see the [analysis and interpretation study guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-analysis-and-interpretation/) and the [O Level Accounting 7707 subject guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-subject-guide/).
