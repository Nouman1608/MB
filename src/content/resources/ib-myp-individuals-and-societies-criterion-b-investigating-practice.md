> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** for revision and practice on Criterion B. They are **not** reproduced IB material and they do not copy the structure or mark tariffs of any on-screen examination. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (achievement levels 1-8), so the `[n]` marks below are a revision aid, not IB marks.

Study first: the [Criterion B study guide](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-b-investigating/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-b-investigating-revision-notes/). Also: [IB MYP Individuals and Societies hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/) and [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/). All places and people are invented.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion B) State two features of a clear and focused research question. **[2]**

**2.** (Criterion B) A student is choosing between three research questions on trade.

- A: "Trade."
- B: "How has the opening of a new port in a named region changed export earnings and jobs in the surrounding towns since 2015?"
- C: "Is trade fair?"

Identify the best question and justify your choice. **[3]**

**3.** (Criterion B) Rewrite the topic "Industrialisation" as a focused research question. Then justify its relevance, using at least one piece of evidence you could use. **[4]**

**4.** (Criterion B) A student's research question is: "How has online shopping affected the high street shops in my town?" Formulate three sub-questions. **[3]**

**5.** (Criterion B) For each sub-question, suggest a suitable research method and give one reason.

**(a)** What do shoppers in the town think about online shopping? **[2]**
**(b)** How has the number of shops on the high street changed over 20 years? **[2]**

**6.** (Criterion B) Here are two questionnaire questions.

- Question X: "Don't you agree that online shopping is ruining town centres?"
- Question Y: "How old are you? (a) young (b) old"

Identify one problem with each question and rewrite each one so that it is suitable. **[4]**

**7.** (Criterion B) A student's action plan says: "I will ask some people and look at the internet. I will finish by Friday." Explain four ways in which this plan falls short of a comprehensive action plan. **[4]**

**8.** (Criterion B) A student's source log has this entry: "Found on a website." Explain why this is not enough and state what should be recorded. **[3]**

**9.** (Criterion B) A student writes: "I surveyed 10 friends at school. 8 said they prefer online shopping, so most people prefer online shopping." Evaluate the process and the result. **[5]**

**10.** (Criterion B) Formulate an action plan to investigate the research question: "To what extent has a new cycle lane scheme changed how pupils travel to school in my town?" Include sub-questions, methods, sources, a sample, a timeline and one ethical consideration. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: it is specific (names a place, time or aspect) [1]; it can be answered with evidence that is available [1]; it is manageable in the time available [1]; it connects to the subject and is relevant [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Question B is the best [1]. It names a place type, a time period and two aspects (export earnings and jobs), so it is focused [1]. It can be investigated with data and sources. Question A is a topic, not a question, and Question C is too broad and a judgment that depends on opinion [1]. **[3]**

**3.** For example: "How did the growth of factories in a named city between 1800 and 1850 change where people lived and worked?" [1] Relevance: it helps explain why cities grew and how living conditions changed, which links to the unit on industrialisation [1]. Evidence: census population figures, a contemporary report or a museum source that shows rapid population growth [1] and a statement of how this evidence shows the issue is real [1]. **[4]**
*Marking note:* Accept any question that names a place, a period and an aspect. Relevance without evidence earns at most one of the two relevance marks.

**4.** Any three, one mark each:
- How many shops on the high street have closed or opened in the last 20 years?
- How do shoppers in the town divide their spending between shops and websites?
- What do shop owners say has happened to their sales and how have they responded?
- Which types of shop have been most affected?
**[3]**

**5. (a)** A questionnaire [1], because it collects the views of many people and the answers can be counted [1]. (Accept interviews with a reason.)
**(b)** Secondary data such as council records, old maps or directories [1], because they give figures from different years and allow comparison [1]. (Accept observation or a count with a reason.)
**[4]**

**6.** Question X is a leading question, because it suggests the answer [1]. Rewrite, for example: "How has online shopping affected your use of the town centre?" [1] Question Y has vague categories, so the answers cannot be compared [1]. Rewrite, for example: "Which age group are you in? Under 18, 18-34, 35-54, 55 or over." [1] **[4]**

**7.** Any four of: no research question or sub-questions are stated [1]; "some people" gives no sample size or selection method [1]; "look at the internet" does not name sources or say how they will be judged [1]; no methods are described [1]; there is a deadline but no timeline of stages [1]; no ethical or safety considerations [1]; no plan for recording or presenting information [1]. **[4]**

**8.** A website is not a source: the reader cannot find or check it [1]. The log should give the author or organisation, title, date, address (URL) and date accessed [1]. It should also say why the source was used, and what information was taken from it [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Process: a sample of 10 friends is very small [1] and all are from one school and age group, so it is not representative [1]. Results: 8 out of 10 is 80% of the sample, but this cannot be generalised to "most people" [1]. The conclusion goes beyond the evidence [1]. Improvement: survey a larger and more varied sample, for example 40 people of different ages in the town centre, and add another method such as interviews [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Award up to eight marks.
- Sub-questions: for example how pupils travelled before and after the scheme; how many pupils use the lanes; what pupils and parents think about safety [1].
- Methods matched to sub-questions, with at least two different types: a questionnaire for pupils and parents, a count of cyclists at the school gate on set mornings, an interview with a teacher or a council officer [2].
- Sources: school travel records, council reports on the scheme, local news, maps of the route [1].
- Sample: a stated number and mix (for example 50 pupils from different year groups) and how they are chosen [1].
- Timeline: stages with times (for example planning in week 1, data collection in weeks 2 to 3, analysis in week 4, write-up in week 5) [1].
- Ethical consideration: permission from the school, anonymous answers, and not photographing children [1].
- A plan for recording and presenting information (log, table, graphs, map) or a review point when the plan may be revised [1].
**[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a topic instead of a question.
- Justifying relevance with an opinion, not evidence.
- Listing methods without matching them to sub-questions.
- Leading or vague questionnaire questions.
- A plan with no timeline, sample or ethics.
- A conclusion that is bigger than the evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there only one correct research question for question 3?**
No. Any question that is focused, can be investigated and is justified with evidence can earn the marks.

**Why are leading questions a problem?**
They push the respondent toward an answer, so the data is biased and the conclusion is less valid.

**How can I improve a plan that has too few details?**
Add sub-questions, a sample, named sources, a timeline and ethical steps, then check that each method answers a sub-question.
