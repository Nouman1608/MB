**Key question 1 of Core Content Option B in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks "Was the Treaty of Versailles
fair?"** The question is about judgement from the start: fair to whom, by what standard, and in whose eyes at the time?
The specification limits the impact on Germany to the end of 1923 and asks for contemporary opinions, so answers must
stay inside 1919–23 rather than jumping to Hitler. The question is the **Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2026**, so
2147 candidates sitting then will meet it on the document paper as well as on Paper 1.

For condensed recall, use the [Treaty of Versailles revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-treaty-of-versailles-revision-notes/);
for exam questions and mark schemes, use the
[Treaty of Versailles practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-treaty-of-versailles-practice/).
The [O Level twentieth century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-twentieth-century-1919-1989/) gives the
overview of Option B.

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. What were the motives and aims of the Big Three at Versailles?
2. Why did the victors not get everything they wanted?
3. What was the impact of the Treaty on Germany up to the end of 1923?
4. Could the Treaty be justified at the time?

Specified content: the roles of Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George in the peacemaking process; the terms of the
Treaty; the social, economic and political impact of the Treaty in Germany to the end of 1923; contemporary opinions
about the Treaty.

## The Big Three: motives and aims

| | Woodrow Wilson (USA) | Georges Clemenceau (France) | David Lloyd George (Britain) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Experience of war | USA joined in 1917; no fighting on its soil | North-east France devastated; about 1.4 million soldiers dead | Heavy losses; German navy and trade seen as threats |
| Main aim | A lasting peace based on the Fourteen Points (January 1918) | Security against another German invasion | A peace that weakened Germany but let it recover as a trading partner |
| Key demands | Self-determination, open diplomacy, disarmament, a League of Nations | Weak German army; Rhineland frontier; heavy reparations | German navy and colonies removed; reparations for pensions; avoid German bitterness |
| Domestic pressure | Republicans in Congress hostile to foreign commitments | French public wanted Germany to pay | The December 1918 "khaki election" promised to make Germany pay |

Lloyd George's position was the most divided. He had campaigned on harsh terms, yet in the **Fontainebleau Memorandum
(March 1919)** he warned that a peace that seemed unjust would leave Germany seeking revenge and might push it towards
Bolshevism.

## Why did the victors not get everything they wanted?

Compromise was unavoidable because the aims conflicted. Four examples show how:

- **The Rhineland.** Clemenceau wanted the Rhineland detached from Germany as a separate state. Wilson and Lloyd George
  refused, as it would create a new grievance. France accepted demilitarisation and fifteen years of Allied occupation,
  plus an Anglo-American promise to defend France. When the US Senate rejected the Treaty, that guarantee collapsed.
- **Self-determination.** Wilson's principle could not be applied evenly. Germans in the Sudetenland and in Austria
  were placed outside Germany, and union between Germany and Austria was forbidden.
- **Poland's frontier.** Lloyd George resisted giving Poland as many German-speaking areas as France wanted. Danzig
  became a free city under the League instead of Polish territory, and Upper Silesia's future was left to a plebiscite
  (held in 1921).
- **Reparations.** France wanted the highest possible sum, Britain a moderate one, and Wilson none beyond actual
  damage. The figure was left to a Reparations Commission, which set it at £6,600 million in 1921.

Wilson gained the League of Nations, written into the Treaty, but at home the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty and
the USA never joined. Clemenceau was criticised in France for being too soft. Each leader went home with less than he
had promised.

## The terms

- **Article 231:** Germany accepted responsibility for causing the loss and damage of the war (the "war guilt"
  clause), which justified reparations.
- **Military:** army limited to 100,000 men, no conscription, no tanks, no military aircraft, no submarines, navy
  limited to six battleships; the Rhineland demilitarised.
- **Territory in Europe:** Alsace-Lorraine to France; Eupen and Malmédy to Belgium; North Schleswig to Denmark after a
  plebiscite; West Prussia and Posen to Poland, creating the Polish Corridor; Danzig a free city; Memel taken; the Saar
  under the League for fifteen years with its coal going to France; Anschluss forbidden.
- **Colonies:** all overseas colonies taken and run by the victors as League mandates.
- **The League:** the Covenant formed the first part of the Treaty, and Germany was not allowed to join at first.

The Treaty was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on 28 June 1919. German delegates had not been allowed to
negotiate, which is why Germans called it a **Diktat**, a dictated peace.

## Impact on Germany to the end of 1923

| Area | Impact |
|---|---|
| Political | The new Weimar Republic was blamed for signing; the "stab in the back" myth claimed the army had been betrayed by the "November criminals"; the Kapp Putsch (March 1920) from the right; assassinations of Matthias Erzberger (1921), who had signed the armistice, and Walther Rathenau (1922); Hitler's Munich Putsch (November 1923) |
| Economic | Loss of coal, iron and farmland; reparations payments; when Germany fell behind, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January 1923; passive resistance and printing money led to hyperinflation |
| Social | Savings of the middle classes wiped out by 1923; resentment among soldiers and nationalists; Germans in lost territories now under foreign rule |

Be careful with cause and effect. Germany's financial problems began with wartime borrowing, so the Treaty made them
worse rather than creating them alone. The government's decision to print money during the Ruhr occupation turned a
crisis into hyperinflation. By late 1923 Gustav Stresemann had ended passive resistance and introduced a new currency,
the Rentenmark.

## Could the Treaty be justified at the time?

**Arguments that it was justified, from the victors' view in 1919:**
- Germany had imposed the far harsher **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** on Russia in March 1918, taking vast lands,
  population and resources.
- France and Belgium had suffered invasion and destruction; reparations paid for real damage.
- Germany was not broken up and remained potentially the strongest state in Europe.
- Many terms were softened by compromise, as the Rhineland and Danzig show.

**Contemporary criticism:**
- German opinion across all parties condemned it as a Diktat and rejected war guilt.
- The British economist **John Maynard Keynes**, who resigned from the British delegation, attacked the reparations in
  *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), arguing they would damage the whole European economy.
- Some in France thought it too lenient; Marshal Foch is reported to have said it was not peace but an armistice for
  twenty years.
- In the USA, the Senate rejected it, partly because of the League's obligations.

A strong answer distinguishes **fair** from **wise**: a settlement can be defended as fair to the victims of the war
while still being judged unwise for the peace of Europe.

## Annotated model answer: a (c) question

*"The Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.*

> Many terms were harsh. Germany lost about 13% of its European territory, all its colonies and its air force, and
> Article 231 forced it to accept blame that most Germans rejected. **[Agree, with evidence.]** The reparations figure
> of £6,600 million set in 1921 was used to explain the economic crisis that led to the Ruhr occupation and
> hyperinflation of 1923. **[Developed impact.]** However, the Treaty could be defended at the time. Germany itself had
> taken huge territories from Russia at Brest-Litovsk in 1918, and northern France had been devastated. Germany also
> kept its industrial heartland and was not divided, as Clemenceau had wanted. **[Counter-argument explained.]**
> Overall the Treaty was harsh enough to create deep resentment, but not harsh enough to stop Germany recovering,
> which made it the worst of both worlds; on the narrow question of harshness compared with Brest-Litovsk, it was not
> excessive. **[Judgement with a clear criterion.]**

## Common mistakes

- Running the impact past 1923. The specification stops at the end of that year.
- Saying the Treaty created the League of Nations as a separate agreement; the Covenant was part of the Treaty.
- Confusing the Treaty of Versailles with the other peace treaties (Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly, Sèvres).
- Treating Lloyd George as simply moderate. He campaigned for harsh terms in 1918.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe**: precise terms with numbers, such as the 100,000-man army.
- **Explain**: why one of the Big Three was dissatisfied, linked to his aims.
- **How far**: define "fair" or "harsh" and judge against it.
- **Paper 2, June 2026**: expect cartoons from British, French and German publications. The national origin of a
  cartoon usually explains its view of the Treaty.
