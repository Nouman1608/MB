Sub-topic 3.6 Plastic pollution is new in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029. It has seven statements. The first five are definitions of types of plastic (3.6.1 to 3.6.5), which are easy marks if the wording is exact and easy to lose if the categories are mixed up. The last two ask you to describe and explain the impacts on marine ecosystems (3.6.6) and strategies for managing them (3.6.7).

## Four ways to classify a plastic

The key to the definitions is that they answer two different questions.

- **What is it made from?** Fossil fuels (a **conventional plastic**) or fully or partly biological raw materials (a **bioplastic**).
- **What happens to it at the end of its life?** Decomposed by microorganisms (**biodegradable**) or only broken down over a long time (**non-biodegradable**).

Because the questions are separate, the categories overlap. A bioplastic can be biodegradable or non-biodegradable. A plastic made from fossil fuels is generally non-biodegradable, but the material it is made from does not on its own decide what happens at the end.

| Type | Syllabus description | Point to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional plastics | Made from fossil fuels; generally non-biodegradable | Most packaging, bottles and bags |
| Bioplastics | Fully or partly made from biological raw materials rather than fossil fuels; can be biodegradable or non-biodegradable | "Bio" describes the source, not the fate |
| Biodegradable plastics | Designed to decompose in water or soil; decomposed by bacteria and fungi into water, biomass and gases (carbon dioxide and methane); rates depend on biotic and abiotic factors | Decomposition can be slow in cold or low-oxygen places |
| Non-biodegradable plastics | Break down over a long period of time | They fragment into smaller pieces rather than being decomposed |

## Biodegradable plastics in more detail

Statement 3.6.3 has three parts.

1. They are **designed** to decompose in water or soil. The design matters: a biodegradable item is made so that microorganisms can use it as food.
2. **Bacteria and fungi** decompose them into **water, biomass and gases**. Carbon dioxide is released where oxygen is present; methane can be released where oxygen is absent, such as deep in a landfill. Methane is a greenhouse gas, which links this sub-topic to climate change in Topic 4.
3. They decompose at **different rates depending on biotic and abiotic factors**. Biotic factors are the living conditions, such as how many and which decomposers are present. Abiotic factors include temperature, moisture, oxygen and light. Warm, moist, well-aerated soil with many microorganisms gives fast decomposition; cold, dark sea water gives slow decomposition. This is why a "biodegradable" bag can still persist for a long time in the ocean.

## Microplastics

The syllabus describes microplastics as plastics that are:

- **less than 5 mm in length**;
- **formed when larger plastics break down**, for example when sunlight makes a bottle brittle and waves grind it into fragments;
- **used in commercial products**, for example tiny beads added to some cosmetics and cleaning products.

Microplastics are hard to see and almost impossible to remove from the sea, and their small size means filter feeders and small animals take them in.

## Impacts on marine ecosystems

### Visual pollution

Plastic litter on beaches and floating at sea spoils the appearance of coasts. Tourists may stay away, so local businesses lose income, and communities pay for beach cleaning.

### Entanglement

Discarded fishing nets, ropes, packing straps and rings trap animals such as turtles, seals, seabirds and fish. Entangled animals may drown, be unable to feed or escape predators, or suffer wounds as they grow and the plastic cuts into them. Lost nets can keep trapping animals for years.

### Risk of being mistaken for food

Floating bags can look like jellyfish to turtles; small fragments can look like eggs or plankton to seabirds and fish. Swallowed plastic fills the stomach, so the animal feels full and starves, or it blocks or damages the gut.

### Bioaccumulation and biomagnification

Small animals eat microplastics, which can carry toxic chemicals. The plastic and chemicals build up in an organism over time (**bioaccumulation**), and as small animals are eaten by larger ones, the amount increases up the food chain (**biomagnification**). Top predators, and people who eat seafood, take in the most.

## Strategies for managing plastic pollution

| Strategy | How it works | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Alternative packaging | Paper, card, glass, metal or reusable containers replace plastic | Alternatives may be heavier, cost more or have their own environmental costs |
| Avoiding single-use plastics | Reusable bags, bottles and cups; refill schemes | Relies on people changing habits |
| Safe disposal | Collection services and managed landfill keep plastic out of rivers and the sea | Many places lack collection; landfill space is limited |
| Recycling | Plastic is collected, sorted and made into new products | Many plastics are hard to sort or are contaminated; quality often falls each time |
| Legislation and enforcement | Bans or charges on items such as bags, bans on microbeads in products | Laws need enforcing; plastic crosses borders from countries without such laws |

Notice that most strategies reduce the plastic entering the sea; none removes the microplastics already there. That is a strong point in a "discuss" or "evaluate" style answer.

## Worked example 1: a bag charge

*Figures invented for practice.* Volunteers counted plastic bags on a 100 m stretch of beach. Before a government charge on plastic bags they found 48 bags; a year after the charge they found 12.

1. Decrease = 48 − 12 = 36 bags.
2. Percentage decrease = 36 ÷ 48 × 100 = 75%.

Interpreting it: the charge appears to work, because legislation changed behaviour quickly. A careful answer would add that one beach and one year are limited evidence, and that other items such as bottles and fishing gear are unaffected.

## Worked example 2: rate of decomposition

*Figures invented for practice.* A 20 g biodegradable bag loses mass at 0.5 g per day in warm, moist soil but at 0.1 g per day in cold sea water.

- Time to decompose in soil = 20 ÷ 0.5 = 40 days.
- Time to decompose in cold sea water = 20 ÷ 0.1 = 200 days.

The bag lasts five times longer in the sea, because low temperature slows the bacteria and fungi. This illustrates statement 3.6.3(c).

## Common mistakes

- Writing that all bioplastics are biodegradable.
- Saying non-biodegradable plastics "never break down". They break down over a long time, into smaller pieces.
- Giving microplastics as "less than 5 cm". The figure is 5 mm.
- Describing entanglement and ingestion as the same impact.
- Recommending "clean the oceans" as a strategy. Use the syllabus strategies and explain them.

## Exam technique

**Describe** definitions using the syllabus wording. **Describe and explain** impacts by naming the impact, the animal group and the result, for example "a turtle swallows a bag mistaken for a jellyfish, so its stomach fills and it starves". For strategies, say how the strategy reduces plastic reaching the sea and give a limitation where a question asks you to evaluate or discuss.
