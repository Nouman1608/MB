Review the [concepts study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-accounting-concepts-and-conventions/) or the [concepts revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-accounting-concepts-and-conventions-revision-notes/) first. All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## Section A

**1.** Identify the accounting concept that justifies each treatment.

**(a)** Electricity used in December but billed in January is included in December's year-end expenses. **[1]**

**(b)** The owner's withdrawal of goods for personal use is recorded as drawings. **[1]**

**(c)** A $15 stapler is charged as an expense. **[1]**

**(d)** The same inventory valuation method is used every year. **[1]**

**2.** Define the going concern concept. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between the accruals concept and the realisation concept. **[2]**

**4.** A business holds two lines of inventory at the year end.

| Line | Cost | Expected selling price | Costs to complete and sell |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4,800 | 5,200 | 600 |
| B | 2,100 | 3,000 | 200 |

Calculate the value of closing inventory. **[3]**

**5.** State two benefits to users of financial statements prepared in line with International Accounting Standards. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Lindale Traders, whose year ends on 31 December, has a draft profit of $52,300. The following have not been dealt with correctly.

1. The owner's family holiday of $2,400 was paid by the business and charged to travel expenses.
2. Insurance of $3,600 for twelve months from 1 October was paid and charged in full as an expense.
3. Goods with a selling price of $4,500 (cost $3,000) were sent on sale or return and recorded as sales. The customer has not accepted them, and they were not included in inventory.
4. Commission receivable of $800, earned during the year, has not been recorded.
5. Closing inventory includes obsolete items at cost of $1,250, which can now be sold for $300.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[6]**

**(b)** Name the concept that applies to each of the five items. **[5]**

**7.** The owner of Marsh Tools has decided to close the business in two months. Its equipment has a carrying value of $18,000 but would sell for $7,500. Its inventory cost $9,000 but would sell for $6,200.

**(a)** Explain why the going concern basis is no longer appropriate. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total reduction in the value of these assets. **[2]**

**8.** In a year of unusually high profit, the owner of a business proposes to change from straight line to reducing balance depreciation, mainly to reduce the tax payable. Evaluate the proposal. **[5]**

**9.** Two businesses each buy a printer for $600. Business P has revenue of $40,000 and profit of $8,000. Business Q has revenue of $5 million. Explain how the materiality concept may lead them to treat the printer differently. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two limitations that the historic cost and money measurement concepts create for users of a statement of financial position. **[4]**

**11.** Trade receivables at the year end are $60,000. Past experience suggests that 2% will not be collected, but the owner proposes an allowance for irrecoverable debts of 15%.

**(a)** Calculate the difference in profit between the two allowances. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the owner's proposal is not an acceptable use of prudence. **[2]**

**12.** On 15 December a customer orders goods priced at $5,000 and pays a deposit of $500. The goods are delivered on 5 January and the balance is paid on 31 January. The year ends on 31 December. Explain how the deposit and the sale should be treated in the financial statements for the year ended 31 December. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Accruals** [1].

**(b)** **Business entity** [1].

**(c)** **Materiality** [1].

**(d)** **Consistency** [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Financial statements are prepared on the assumption that the business will **continue trading for the foreseeable future** [1], so assets are shown at cost less depreciation rather than at what they would fetch on closure [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Accruals matches income and expenses to the **period they relate to**, whenever cash is paid [1]. Realisation decides **when revenue is earned**: when goods or services are provided and payment becomes due [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Line A: NRV = 5,200 − 600 = 4,600, below cost, so **4,600** [1]. Line B: NRV = 2,800, above cost, so **2,100** [1]. Total **$6,700** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two: statements can be **compared** with other businesses and countries [1]; users can **trust** that recognised rules were followed [1]; there is **less scope to manipulate** results [1]; standard **terminology** makes statements easier to understand [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Draft profit | 52,300 |
| Holiday treated as drawings | +2,400 |
| Insurance prepaid: 3,600 × 9/12 | +2,700 |
| Sale or return: −4,500 revenue, +3,000 inventory | −1,500 |
| Commission receivable | +800 |
| Obsolete inventory: 1,250 − 300 | −950 |
| **Corrected profit** | **55,750** |

One mark for each correct adjustment with the correct sign [5]; corrected profit [1].

**(b)** 1 **Business entity** [1]; 2 **Accruals** [1]; 3 **Realisation** [1]; 4 **Accruals** [1]; 5 **Prudence** [1]. **[11]**
*Examiner insight:* Only 3 of the 12 months of insurance belong to this year; the other 9 months are a prepayment, which increases profit.

**7. (a)** The business will not continue for the foreseeable future [1], so its assets should be shown at what they are expected to realise on closure rather than at cost less depreciation [1].

**(b)** Equipment 18,000 − 7,500 = 10,500 and inventory 9,000 − 6,200 = 2,800 [1]. Total reduction **$13,300** [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Up to 5 marks:

- Reducing balance gives a **higher charge in early years**, lowering profit now and raising it later, so total depreciation over the life is broadly unchanged [1].
- A change made to **reduce tax** rather than to give a fairer view breaks the **consistency** concept [1].
- Profits become **harder to compare** with earlier years, which misleads users such as lenders [1].
- A change is acceptable if reducing balance better reflects how the asset is used, for example if it loses value fastest early on, and the effect is disclosed [1].
- **Judgement:** the proposal should not go ahead for the reason given; a change is justified only if it gives a fairer view [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Materiality depends on the size of an item **relative to the business** [1]. For P, $600 is 7.5% of profit, so it is material and should be a non-current asset and depreciated [1]. For Q, $600 is insignificant, so it may simply be expensed [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Historic cost: assets such as land are shown at their **original price**, which may be far from current value [1], so users cannot see what the assets are worth now [1]. Money measurement: items such as **staff skill or reputation** are not recorded [1], so the statement omits resources that affect the business's prospects [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** 15% × 60,000 = 9,000 and 2% × 60,000 = 1,200 [1]. Profit is **$7,800 lower** under the owner's proposal [1].

**(b)** Prudence means **caution, not deliberate understatement** [1]. An allowance far above experience understates profit and receivables, and the excess could be released later to inflate profit, so users would be misled [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The goods were not delivered until 5 January, so **no revenue** is recognised in the year to 31 December (realisation) [1]. The $500 deposit is shown as a **current liability** (other payables), because the business owes the customer goods [1]. The **$5,000** sale is revenue of the next year [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a concept without applying it to the item.
- Confusing accruals with realisation.
- Correcting only one side of a sale or return item.
- Taking NRV as the selling price without deducting costs.
- Treating prudence as permission to understate profit.
- Evaluations without a judgement.
