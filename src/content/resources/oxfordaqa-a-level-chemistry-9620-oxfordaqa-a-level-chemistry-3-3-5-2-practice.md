This set practises **3.3.5.2 Elimination** from section 3.3.5 Alcohols of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and in the synoptic Unit 5. Revise first with the [alcohol elimination study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-5-2/) or the [alcohol elimination revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-5-2-revision-notes/). Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0 and O = 16.0.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which reagent is used to eliminate water from an alcohol?

A: dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide

B: concentrated phosphoric acid

C: acidified potassium dichromate(VI)

D: bromine water **[1]**

**2.** Write an equation for the elimination of water from butan-1-ol. **[1]**

**3.** Name the type of intermediate formed when water leaves the protonated alcohol. **[1]**

**4.** Explain how the mechanism shows that the acid acts as a catalyst. **[1]**

**5.** 2-Methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH, is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid. Name the alkene formed and explain why only one alkene forms. **[2]**

**6.** Which alcohol gives **only one** alkene on acid-catalysed elimination?

A: pentan-2-ol

B: butan-2-ol

C: propan-2-ol

D: 2-methylbutan-2-ol **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Pentan-3-ol, CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃, is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism for the formation of pent-2-ene. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why pent-2-ene forms as two stereoisomers. **[1]**

**8.** Heptan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃, undergoes acid-catalysed elimination. Name all the alkenes formed and explain why there are this many. **[3]**

**9.** 2-Methylpentan-3-ol, CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH(CH₃)₂, is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Give the structures and names of the two structurally different alkenes formed. **[2]**

**(b)** State which of these alkenes shows E/Z isomerism, and explain. **[2]**

**10.** Alcohol E has the formula C₅H₁₂O. E is not oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate(VI). With concentrated phosphoric acid, E gives two structurally different alkenes, neither of which shows E/Z isomerism. Identify E and both alkenes. **[3]**

**11.** A company makes poly(ethene) from ethanol produced from sugar cane.

**(a)** Write equations for the two stages: ethanol to ethene, and ethene to poly(ethene). **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the advantage of this route compared with using ethene from crude oil. **[1]**

**(c)** The overall yield from ethanol to poly(ethene) is 75.0 %. Calculate the mass of ethanol needed to make 2.80 tonnes of poly(ethene). **[3]**

**(d)** A student claims that the poly(ethene) made this way will be biodegradable. Evaluate this claim. **[2]**

**12.** In Required practical 4, cyclohexanol (boiling point about 161 °C) is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid and cyclohexene (boiling point about 83 °C) is distilled off.

**(a)** Explain the difference in boiling points. **[2]**

**(b)** 7.50 g of cyclohexanol (Mr 100.0) gives 4.51 g of cyclohexene (Mr 82.0). Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]

**2.** **CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH → CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ + H₂O** [1]

**3.** Carbocation [1]

**4.** H⁺ used in the first step is released again in the last step, so it is not used up [1]

**5.** **2-Methylpropene** [1]; the three carbons adjacent to the C–OH carbon are all equivalent CH₃ groups, so C=C can form in only one position [1]

**6.** **C** [1]

**7. (a)** Arrow from a lone pair on the O of –OH to H⁺, giving the protonated alcohol CH₃CH₂CH(OH₂⁺)CH₂CH₃ [1]; arrow from the C–O bond to O, so water leaves [1]; carbocation CH₃CH₂C⁺HCH₂CH₃ shown [1]; arrow from a C–H bond on C2 (or C4) to the C–C bond, forming C=C and releasing H⁺ [1]

**(b)** Rotation about C=C is restricted and each double-bond carbon carries two different groups (H with CH₃; H with C₂H₅), so E and Z isomers form [1]

**8.** **Hept-1-ene** [1]; **(E)-hept-2-ene and (Z)-hept-2-ene** [1]; H can be lost from C1 or C3, which are not equivalent, and hept-2-ene has two different groups on each double-bond carbon, giving E/Z isomers [1] **[3]**

**9. (a)** **CH₃CH=CHCH(CH₃)₂, 4-methylpent-2-ene** [1]; **(CH₃)₂C=CHCH₂CH₃, 2-methylpent-2-ene** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Number from the end nearer the double bond: CH₃CH=CHCH(CH₃)₂ is 4-methylpent-2-ene, not 2-methylpent-3-ene.

**(b)** 4-Methylpent-2-ene shows E/Z isomerism [1]; each of its double-bond carbons has two different groups (H and CH₃; H and CH(CH₃)₂), whereas in 2-methylpent-2-ene one carbon carries two identical CH₃ groups [1]

**10.** Not oxidised, so E is tertiary [1]; E is **2-methylbutan-2-ol**, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₃ [1]; alkenes: **2-methylbut-1-ene** and **2-methylbut-2-ene** [1] **[3]**

**11. (a)** **CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O** [1]; **n CH₂=CH₂ → –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ–** [1]

**(b)** The monomer is not derived from crude oil, which is a finite resource; sugar cane is renewable [1]

**(c)** 28.0 t of poly(ethene) needs 46.0 t of ethanol at 100 % yield [1]; so 2.80 t needs 2.80 × 46.0 ÷ 28.0 = 4.60 t at 100 % [1]; at 75.0 % yield: 4.60 ÷ 0.750 = **6.13 tonnes** [1]

**(d)** The claim is wrong [1]; the polymer is chemically identical to poly(ethene) from crude oil, with a saturated, unreactive chain, so it is no more biodegradable [1]

**12. (a)** Cyclohexanol has an O–H group and forms hydrogen bonds between molecules [1]; cyclohexene has only van der Waals forces between molecules, which are weaker and need less energy to overcome [1]

**(b)** n(cyclohexanol) = 7.50 ÷ 100.0 = 0.0750 mol [1]; n(cyclohexene) = 4.51 ÷ 82.0 = 0.0550 mol [1]; yield = 0.0550 ÷ 0.0750 × 100 = **73.3 %** [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- First arrow drawn from H⁺.
- Water leaving before the alcohol is protonated.
- H removed from the C–OH carbon itself.
- Alkene names numbered from the wrong end.
- Bio-sourced polymers described as biodegradable.
