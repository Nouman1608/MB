## Questions

### Section A: short answer

**1.** Define the term **pressure group**. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two methods a pressure group could use to influence a business. **[2]**

**3.** Define the term **boycott**. **[2]**

**4.** State two business decisions that a pressure group might influence. **[2]**

**5.** A pressure group's petition is signed by 2,600 of a supermarket's 20,000 regular customers. Calculate the percentage of regular customers who signed. **[2]**

**6.** Outline how lobbying government can influence business decisions. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Torrin Chemicals makes cleaning products that it sells to hospitals and hotels. A local group, Riverside Friends, says the factory's waste is harming fish in a nearby river. The group has posted videos of the river on social media.

**(a)** Explain two ways Riverside Friends could influence Torrin Chemicals' decisions. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain one reason why Riverside Friends may have limited influence over Torrin Chemicals. **[2]**

**8.** A pressure group launches the same campaign against two businesses that use plastic packaging. Brightling Burgers is a well-known chain of restaurants. Holcombe Packaging sells boxes to other businesses and is not known to the public. Explain which business is more likely to change its decision about packaging. **[4]**

**9.** Madeley Homes plans to build 120 houses on a field used by local people as a park. A local pressure group has collected 3,000 signatures, organised protests outside the town hall and asked the local council to refuse permission. Changing the plan to keep half the field as a park would mean building only 70 houses.

Consider whether Madeley Homes should change its plans. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how a pressure group could benefit a business. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how a social media campaign by a pressure group could affect the sales of a clothing retailer. **[3]**

**12.** Explain two factors that affect how much influence a pressure group has over a business. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** An organised group of people who share a concern [1] and campaign to change the decisions of businesses or government [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: boycott; publicity through the media or social media; petition; protest; lobbying government; direct talks with managers. One mark for each of two different methods [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** When customers refuse to buy a business's products [1] in order to pressure it into changing what it does [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two from: what to produce; how to produce; where to locate; which suppliers to use; how to promote products; what packaging to use. One mark for each of two different decisions [1] [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 2,600 / 20,000 x 100 [1] = **13%** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The group tries to persuade government to pass a new law or change rules [1]; if it succeeds, businesses must change their decisions to obey the law, for example on pollution or advertising [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** The group could spread its videos widely so that the public and the media learn about the pollution [1], damaging Torrin's reputation, so that hospitals and hotels may not want to be linked with it and choose another supplier [1]. The group could lobby the local council or government to enforce stricter rules on waste [1], which would force Torrin to change how it disposes of waste or face fines [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Torrin sells to hospitals and hotels, not to the public [1], so a public boycott would have little direct effect on its sales, and its buyers may care more about price and quality than about the campaign [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Brightling Burgers is more likely to change [1]. It sells directly to the public under a well-known name, so a campaign can quickly reach its customers and damage its reputation [1]. Its customers can easily eat at a rival restaurant, so a boycott could cut its sales [1]. Holcombe is unknown to the public and sells to other businesses, which buy mainly on price and quality, so the campaign has less effect on its sales [1]. **[4]**

**9.** For changing the plans: the group has strong local support (3,000 signatures) and is lobbying the council, which could refuse permission altogether, leaving Madeley with no houses to sell [1]; a compromise could win approval more quickly and protect Madeley's reputation with future buyers in the town [1]. Against changing: building 70 houses instead of 120 means 50 fewer to sell, which greatly reduces Madeley's revenue from the project [1]; the land cost stays the same, so the profit on each house may fall [1]. Judgement: Madeley should offer the compromise [1], because if the council refuses permission it earns nothing from the site, whereas 70 houses with a park nearby may sell faster and at higher prices, making up some of the lost revenue [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The key analysis is that the council's decision, not the protest itself, is what can stop the building. Linking the group's lobbying to the council is what turns a list of methods into an explanation of influence.

**10.** A pressure group may praise a business that improves its practices, for example by listing it as an approved supplier [1]. The business can use this in its promotion [1], which may attract customers who care about the issue and increase sales [1]. Also accept: early warning of an issue customers will soon care about, with explanation. **[3]**

**11.** The campaign could spread quickly online, so many of the retailer's customers learn about the issue, such as poor conditions in a supplier's factory [1]. Some customers may stop buying from the retailer or choose a rival [1], which reduces its sales and revenue, especially among younger customers who use social media most [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Public support [1]: if many people agree with the group, more customers may join a boycott, so the business is under more pressure to change [1]. Whether customers can switch easily [1]: if rivals offer similar products, customers can stop buying without losing anything, so a boycott is more likely to cut sales [1]. Also accept: media coverage, how well known the brand is, cost of the change, or size of the group, with explanation. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a pressure group's methods without saying how they affect the business's decisions.
- Saying a pressure group can force a business to change.
- Assuming every campaign succeeds, without using the case to judge the group's strength.
- Forgetting that lobbying works through government.
- Ending a "consider" answer without a justified decision.
