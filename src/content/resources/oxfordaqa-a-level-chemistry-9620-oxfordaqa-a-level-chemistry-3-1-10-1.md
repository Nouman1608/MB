This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.10.1 Brønsted–Lowry acid–base equilibria in aqueous solution** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification states three things: "an acid is a proton donor", "a base is a proton acceptor" and "acid–base equilibria involve the transfer of protons". It is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3, and it underpins every later point in section 3.1.10.

The [acids and bases study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/) covers the whole of 3.1.10. This page focuses on the skill examiners test most in 3.1.10.1: reading any equation and saying which species is the acid and which is the base, in both directions. Condense it with the [3.1.10.1 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-10-1-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.10.1 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-10-1-practice/).

## What a proton is

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Removing the electron leaves **H⁺, a bare proton**. In Brønsted–Lowry theory, acids and bases are defined by what they do with H⁺:

- an **acid** donates H⁺;
- a **base** accepts H⁺, using a lone pair of electrons to form a new bond to it.

A bare proton is tiny and highly charged, so in water it bonds at once to a lone pair on a water molecule, forming the **oxonium ion, H₃O⁺**:

```
H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺
```

So H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) describe the same species; both forms are used in equations.

## Identifying acids and bases in an equation

**Method:**

1. Compare each species on the left with the species on the right.
2. A species that has **lost one H⁺** (and its charge has fallen by 1) acted as an **acid**.
3. A species that has **gained one H⁺** (and its charge has risen by 1) acted as a **base**.
4. For an equilibrium, repeat for the **reverse** reaction: the species that gives H⁺ back is the acid in that direction.

### Worked example 1: two familiar equilibria

```
HNO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₂⁻(aq)
```

- Forward: HNO₂ → NO₂⁻ (lost H⁺), so **HNO₂ is the acid**; H₂O → H₃O⁺ (gained H⁺), so **H₂O is the base**.
- Reverse: H₃O⁺ gives H⁺ to NO₂⁻, so **H₃O⁺ is the acid** and **NO₂⁻ is the base**.

```
NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
```

- Forward: **H₂O is the acid** (becomes OH⁻), **NH₃ is the base** (becomes NH₄⁺).
- Reverse: **NH₄⁺ is the acid**, **OH⁻ is the base**.

Water acts as a base towards nitrous acid and as an acid towards ammonia. Whether a species is an acid or a base depends on what it reacts with.

## Conjugate pairs

In every acid–base equilibrium, two pairs of species differ by exactly one H⁺. Each pair is called a **conjugate acid–base pair**:

| Equilibrium | Pair 1 (acid / base) | Pair 2 (acid / base) |
|---|---|---|
| HNO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + NO₂⁻ | HNO₂ / NO₂⁻ | H₃O⁺ / H₂O |
| NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ | NH₄⁺ / NH₃ | H₂O / OH⁻ |

Checking that each pair differs by one H⁺, and that each charge differs by one, is a fast way to confirm your identifications.

## Species that can act as either: amphiprotic species

A species with an H⁺ to give **and** a lone pair to accept another H⁺ can act as an acid or a base. Water is the most important example. Others are ions formed when a polyprotic acid has lost some, but not all, of its protons.

### Worked example 2: the hydrogencarbonate ion

With an acid:

```
HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → H₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)       HCO₃⁻ is a base
```

With a base:

```
HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)         HCO₃⁻ is an acid
```

Other examples: HSO₄⁻ (can become H₂SO₄ or SO₄²⁻) and H₂PO₄⁻ (can become H₃PO₄ or HPO₄²⁻).

## Polyprotic acids transfer protons one at a time

Sulfuric acid can donate two protons, but they are transferred in two separate steps:

```
H₂SO₄ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻
HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + SO₄²⁻
```

Each step is a separate acid–base reaction, and HSO₄⁻ is the base in one and the acid in the next. Phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄, transfers up to three protons in three steps. Writing each step separately keeps the charges right.

## Proton transfer without water

The definition does not need water. Two examples from elsewhere in the course:

**Hydrogen chloride and ammonia gases** form a white smoke of ammonium chloride:

```
HCl(g) + NH₃(g) → NH₄⁺Cl⁻(s)
```

HCl donates a proton (acid); NH₃ accepts it with its lone pair (base).

**Forming the nitronium ion** for the nitration of benzene (see the [aromatic chemistry guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-aromatic-chemistry/)):

```
HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻
```

Here sulfuric acid donates protons and **nitric acid accepts one**, so nitric acid is acting as a **base**. Nitric acid is normally thought of as an acid, but against the even stronger proton donor H₂SO₄ it is the proton acceptor. This is the clearest illustration that "acid" and "base" describe a role in a reaction, not a fixed label.

## Neutralisation as proton transfer

Familiar neutralisations are all proton transfers to a base:

| Reaction | Ionic equation | Base accepting H⁺ |
|---|---|---|
| Acid + hydroxide | H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O | OH⁻ |
| Acid + carbonate | 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → H₂O + CO₂ | CO₃²⁻ |
| Acid + ammonia | H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺ | NH₃ |
| Acid + metal oxide | 2H⁺ + O²⁻ → H₂O | O²⁻ (in the solid oxide) |

The carbonate reaction happens through proton transfers to CO₃²⁻ and then HCO₃⁻, giving H₂CO₃, which decomposes to water and carbon dioxide.

## How far the proton transfer goes

The specification describes acid–base **equilibria**. With a **strong** acid such as hydrochloric acid, proton transfer to water is essentially complete, so the equation is written with a single arrow. With a **weak** acid such as ethanoic acid, only a small fraction of molecules transfer a proton, so a reversible arrow is used. This distinction is used in [pH calculations (3.1.10.2)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-10-2/) and later in weak acids and Ka.

## Common mistakes

- Defining an acid as "a substance that releases H⁺ ions" or "contains hydrogen". Use **proton donor**.
- Identifying the acid and base for the forward reaction only when the question asks about the equilibrium.
- Writing the base as "H₂O" when water is clearly losing H⁺ (it is the acid there).
- Getting charges wrong: losing H⁺ lowers the charge by one; gaining it raises the charge by one.
- Treating nitric acid as always an acid.
- Writing a polyprotic acid losing all its protons in one step.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **define**: "proton donor" and "proton acceptor" are the marking words.
- **Identify** acids and bases: name the exact formula, and label both directions if the equation is an equilibrium.
- **Explain** why a species is an acid or a base: say what it gains or loses, for example "HCO₃⁻ donates a proton to OH⁻, forming CO₃²⁻".
- **Write an equation** showing a species acting as an acid (or base): include water or another named partner, with the correct charges and state symbols.
