**Trade (Section 6) and Transport and telecommunications (Section 7) are the two linked sections of
Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448 Paper 2 that deal with how goods, people and information
move.** Trade asks what Pakistan exports and imports, who its partners are, and why its balance of
trade is hard to improve. Transport and telecommunications asks how road, rail, air, sea and
communications networks are organised, what helps or hinders them, and how they support
development. This guide covers both because questions often connect them.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE PAKISTAN STUDIES (0448), 2026 — PAPER 2, SECTION 6: TRADE**

**(a) Major exports and imports**
- The main exports and imports, and recent changes in their types, amounts and value
- The meaning of GNP and GDP and the difference between them
- The effect of changing export and import trends on the balance of trade and the economy

**(b) Pakistan's trading partners**
- Main trading partners, their location, and the goods exchanged with them
- Factors promoting or hindering trade; why a developing country finds it hard to keep or increase its share of world trade
- Trading blocs, trade barriers and currency exchange rates

**SECTION 7: TRANSPORT AND TELECOMMUNICATIONS**

**(a) Internal transport**
- Regional variations in density and pattern of road, rail and air networks, from maps
- Factors helping and hindering location, maintenance and development of roads, railways and airports
- Recent improvements and the feasibility of new developments; comparing road, rail and air for goods and people; evaluating new schemes such as motorways and airports

**(b) International transport**
- Cross-border roads (with the passes they use) and railways in use for at least part of the year
- The ports of Keamari, Qasim and Gwadar, and the cities with international airports
- Factors affecting cross-border routes, seaports, dry ports and airports; the meaning of a dry port, an example, and why they have been developed

**(c) Telecommunications**
- The importance of radio, television, phones, fax, email and the internet to education, industry, services and trade
- Problems of providing telecommunications in some areas; evaluating their role in development

## What does Pakistan export and import?

| Exports | Imports |
|---|---|
| Cotton yarn, cloth, garments, bed linen and towels | Crude oil and petroleum products |
| Rice | Machinery and transport equipment |
| Leather and leather goods | Chemicals, fertilisers and medicines |
| Sports goods and surgical instruments | Edible oil (palm oil) |
| Fruit, vegetables, fish | Iron and steel; tea |

The long-term change has been from exporting raw materials such as raw cotton towards exporting
processed and manufactured goods such as yarn, cloth and garments, which earn more value-added.
Imports are dominated by fuel and machinery, so the import bill rises sharply when world oil
prices rise.

## What are GNP, GDP and the balance of trade?

**GDP** (gross domestic product) is the total value of goods and services produced within Pakistan
in a year. **GNP** (gross national product) is GDP plus income earned by Pakistan's people and firms
abroad, minus income sent out by foreign firms. Remittances from Pakistanis working overseas help
make GNP larger than GDP. The **balance of trade** is the value of exports minus the value of
imports. Pakistan usually has a **deficit** because imports cost more than exports earn. A
persistent deficit means borrowing, pressure on the rupee and less money for development.

## Who are Pakistan's trading partners?

Learn current partners from your course statistics, but the pattern is stable: the **USA** is a
leading market for textiles; **China** is a leading source of machinery and manufactured goods and a
growing partner through the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC); **the UAE and Saudi Arabia**
supply oil; **the UK and EU countries** buy textiles, rice and leather; **Afghanistan** is a land
neighbour that buys food and manufactured goods.

## Why is it hard for Pakistan to increase its share of trade?

- Exports are concentrated in a few products, especially textiles, so falling prices or demand hit earnings hard.
- Competition from other low-cost producers of textiles and rice.
- Trade barriers such as tariffs, quotas and quality standards in richer markets.
- Energy shortages and high costs raise production costs.
- A weak or falling rupee makes imports dearer, though it can make exports cheaper abroad.
- **Promoting factors:** trading blocs and agreements such as SAARC, ECO and the EU's GSP+ scheme, which lowers tariffs on many Pakistani exports.

## How do transport networks vary across Pakistan?

Networks are densest across the Indus Plain of Punjab and Sindh, where flat land, many people,
farms and industry make building roads and railways cheap and worthwhile. They are thinnest in
Balochistan, the mountainous north and the deserts, where steep slopes, landslides, floods, snow,
sand and sparse population raise costs and lower demand.

**Recent improvements** include motorways such as the M-2 Lahore–Islamabad, the Karakoram Highway
upgrade, new airports such as the new Islamabad International Airport, and planned upgrading of the
main Karachi–Peshawar railway line.

| Mode | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Road | Door-to-door; flexible; reaches villages | Congestion; accidents; fuel costs; damaged by floods |
| Rail | Cheap for bulky goods over long distances | Slow; old track and rolling stock; fixed routes |
| Air | Fast over long distances; links remote northern towns | Expensive; small loads; weather delays in the mountains |

## Which international routes and ports must you know?

- **Roads:** to China via the Khunjerab Pass (Karakoram Highway); to Afghanistan via the Khyber Pass (Torkham) and via Chaman near Quetta; to Iran via Taftan; to India at Wagah near Lahore.
- **Railways:** to Iran from Quetta via Taftan; the line to India via Wagah exists, but passenger services have been suspended since 2019, so check your course materials for its current status.
- **Seaports:** Keamari (Karachi Port), Port Qasim and Gwadar.
- **International airports:** including Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, Quetta, Multan.

**Dry ports** are inland terminals where goods are cleared through customs and loaded into
containers, so they travel sealed to and from the seaport by rail or road. Examples include Lahore
and Faisalabad. They save time, reduce congestion at Karachi and help exporters in inland
industrial cities.

## Why do telecommunications matter for development?

Phones, the internet and email allow businesses to trade and bank online, schools to deliver
distance learning, farmers to check prices, and services such as call centres to grow. Problems
include high costs of building masts and cables in mountains and deserts, low population density,
unreliable power and low incomes and literacy in some rural areas.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a dry port?**
An inland customs terminal linked to a seaport by rail or road, where goods
are cleared and containerised. Lahore has one.

**Why does Pakistan have a trade deficit?**
It imports expensive oil, machinery and chemicals while
its exports rely heavily on lower-value textiles and farm products.

**Why are rail and road networks sparse in Balochistan?**
Mountains, plateaux and deserts raise
building costs, and the low, scattered population means low demand.
