Subtopic 6.7 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 has two parts. The first is information systems in medicine: the "characteristics and uses of patient records, pharmacy records". The second is 3D printers, "including printing of prosthetics, tissue engineering, artificial blood vessels, customised medicines". The [ICT Applications study guide](/resources/igcse-ict-applications-systems/) lists these. This guide explains what the records contain, how staff and pharmacists use them, how 3D printing works in medicine, and how to weigh the benefits and risks.

## Information systems in medicine

Hospitals, clinics and pharmacies keep their information in **databases**. Each patient has a **unique patient identification number**, which is the key field that links their records across departments.

### Patient records

**Characteristics:** a patient record typically holds:

- personal details: name, date of birth, address, contact number, next of kin
- the patient ID number
- medical history: past illnesses, operations and conditions
- allergies, for example to a particular medicine
- test results, X-ray and scan images
- current and past medicines and treatments
- appointments and the doctors involved

**Uses:**

- a doctor sees the full history before diagnosing, even when the patient has not been seen before
- in an emergency, staff can quickly check blood group, allergies and current medicines
- test results are added as soon as they are ready and are seen by every authorised department
- appointments and reminders are generated automatically
- data from many records can be analysed to plan services, such as how many beds are needed

### Pharmacy records

**Characteristics:** a pharmacy system stores:

- patient details and patient ID
- prescriptions: medicine, dose, how often, how long, and the prescribing doctor
- a database of medicines: names, doses, side effects and which medicines should not be taken together
- stock levels of each medicine

**Uses:**

- when a prescription is entered, the system **checks** it against the patient's allergies and current medicines, and warns the pharmacist of dangerous interactions or an unusual dose
- labels with dosage instructions are printed automatically
- repeat prescriptions are managed
- stock is reduced as medicines are dispensed, and new stock is ordered when levels fall

### Worked example: a prescription check

A doctor prescribes a new medicine for a patient. At the pharmacy:

1. The pharmacist scans or types the prescription and the patient ID.
2. The system opens the patient's record and finds that she is already taking another medicine.
3. It searches the medicines database and finds that the two should not be taken together.
4. A warning appears on screen. The pharmacist contacts the doctor, who prescribes an alternative.
5. The new prescription is checked, the label is printed, and stock is updated.

Without the computerised check, the pharmacist would have to know or look up every possible interaction by hand.

### Advantages and disadvantages of computerised records

**Advantages**

- Records can be shared instantly between departments, hospitals and pharmacies.
- Faster access in emergencies than finding paper notes.
- No problems reading handwriting, so fewer errors in doses.
- Automatic checks for allergies and interactions.
- Less storage space than paper files, and backups protect against loss.

**Disadvantages**

- Medical data is highly sensitive; hacking or careless access could expose it, so strong security, passwords and access levels are needed.
- Data protection laws must be followed, adding responsibility and cost.
- If the system fails or the network is down, records may be unavailable.
- Staff need training, and the system is expensive to set up and maintain.
- An error typed into a record is copied to every department that uses it.

### Protecting medical records

Because health data is so sensitive, medical systems use several layers of protection:

- **Access levels**: a receptionist can see appointments and contact details, but not test results; a doctor can see the full clinical record; a pharmacist sees prescriptions and allergies.
- **Strong passwords** or smart cards for logging in, with automatic log-out when a computer is left unattended.
- **Audit trails**: the system records who opened each record and when, so improper access can be traced.
- **Encryption** of data stored on servers and sent across networks.
- **Regular backups**, kept in a separate place, so records can be restored after a failure.

These measures answer the disadvantage that shared records are a target: they reduce the risk rather than remove it.

## 3D printers in medicine

A **3D printer** builds a solid object by adding material **layer by layer**, following a 3D design made with computer-aided design (CAD) software. In medicine the design is often created from a **scan** of the patient's body, so the object fits that one person exactly.

### Prosthetics

A **prosthetic** is an artificial body part, such as a hand, arm or leg. The patient's limb is scanned, a model is designed to fit, and the prosthetic is printed. Benefits: a better, more comfortable fit; much cheaper and quicker than traditional methods; easy to reprint as a child grows. Limitations: printed parts may be less strong or durable than some traditionally made prosthetics.

### Tissue engineering

**Tissue engineering** uses 3D printing to build up layers of **living cells** to form tissue. This is sometimes called bioprinting. It could provide tissue for repairs, such as skin for burns patients, and may reduce the need for donors. It is a developing technology, and printing complex organs is not yet routine.

### Artificial blood vessels

3D printers can create tubes with a structure similar to blood vessels, using materials that are compatible with the body. These can be used to replace or bypass damaged vessels, and to supply blood to printed tissue.

### Customised medicines

3D printers can produce tablets designed for one patient, with an exact dose, a particular shape, or several medicines combined in one tablet. Layers can be designed to release medicine at different rates. This helps patients who would otherwise take several tablets or need a dose that is not made as standard.

### Advantages and disadvantages of 3D printing in medicine

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Items are customised to the individual patient | 3D printers and materials can be expensive |
| Faster and often cheaper than traditional manufacture | Printing can be slow for complex items |
| Easy to change the design and reprint | Bioprinting tissue is still being developed and tested |
| Reduces reliance on donors for some tissue | Strict testing is needed before use in patients |
| Less waste, as only the material needed is used | Needs skilled staff for scanning and design |

## Common mistakes

- Listing what is in a patient record without saying how it is used.
- Saying pharmacy records are only for stock control. The key use is checking prescriptions for allergies and interactions.
- Saying 3D printers "print organs" as routine. Tissue engineering is developing.
- Forgetting the scan and CAD design stage before printing.
- Writing "hacking" as a disadvantage without explaining the consequence: sensitive medical data exposed.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the uses of patient or pharmacy records: give the action and who benefits.
- **Explain** an advantage: point plus consequence, such as "allergies are flagged, so a dangerous medicine is not given".
- **Describe** how a 3D-printed prosthetic is made: scan, design in CAD, print layer by layer, fit.
- **Discuss**: give both sides and a short judgement.
