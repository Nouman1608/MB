Condensed for the final weeks. These notes cover content area **2.3.9**, "Trespass to land": "unlawful entry", "intentional and direct interference" and "continuing trespass and remedies". For the full explanation, use the [trespass to land study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-trespass-to-land/); the whole section is in the [individual revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-the-individual-revision-notes/).

## Definition

An **unjustifiable, direct and intentional interference with land in the possession of another**.
- **Actionable per se**: no damage needed.
- Protects **possession**: tenants can sue, even against the landlord.
- "Land" = surface, buildings, subsoil and airspace needed for ordinary use.

## Forms of unlawful entry

| Form | Case |
|---|---|
| Walking or driving onto land | The basic case |
| Placing objects against or on land | *Westripp v Baldock* [1939] (ladder) |
| Overhanging airspace | *Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco* [1957] (sign); *Anchor Brewhouse v Berkley House* [1987] (crane jibs); *Laiqat v Majid* [2005] (extractor fan) |
| Below the surface | *Bocardo v Star Energy* [2010] (oil wells deep below) |
| Not trespass: aircraft at a reasonable height | *Bernstein v Skyviews* [1978]; Civil Aviation Act 1982 |
| Exceeding permission | *The Calgarth* [1927] (staircase and banisters) |
| Staying after permission revoked | *Robson v Hallett* [1967] (reasonable time to leave) |
| Misusing the highway | *Harrison v Duke of Rutland* [1893]; *Hickman v Maisey* [1900]; contrast *DPP v Jones* [1999] |

## Intentional and direct

- **Intention to enter**, not to trespass: mistake is no defence (*Basely v Clarkson* (1681)).
- **Involuntary entry** is not trespass (*Smith v Stone* (1647)).
- Liability for **animals under control** if intended or negligently allowed (*League Against Cruel Sports v Scott* [1986]).
- **Direct** physical interference only; indirect harm (roots, smells, seepage) is **nuisance**, which needs damage.

## Continuing trespass

- Object or structure left on the land.
- **Fresh cause of action each day** (*Holmes v Wilson* (1839)).
- Supports a **mandatory injunction** to remove it.

## Defences

| Defence | Key point |
|---|---|
| Licence | Express or implied; lost if exceeded or revoked |
| Lawful authority | PACE 1984 powers; no authority, no right to enter (*Entick v Carrington* (1765)) |
| Statute | Access to Neighbouring Land Act 1992 |
| Necessity | Serious, imminent danger (*Rigby v Chief Constable of Northamptonshire* [1985]) |

## Remedies

- **Damages**: nominal if no loss; compensatory for harm or use (*Bocardo*: modest sum).
- **Injunction**: prohibitory or mandatory (*Kelsen*; *Anchor Brewhouse*); damages in lieu if oppressive (*Jaggard v Sawyer* [1995]).
- **Self-help**: request to leave, then reasonable force (*Collins v Renison* (1754): excessive force not allowed).
- **Possession order** or peaceful re-entry.
- **Criminal**: aggravated trespass (CJPOA 1994); squatting in a residential building (LASPO 2012).

## Trespass or nuisance?

| | Trespass | Nuisance |
|---|---|---|
| Interference | Direct | Indirect |
| Damage needed? | No | Yes |
| Typical examples | Entry, objects, overhanging structures | Noise, smells, roots, seepage |

## Model evaluation paragraph

*Because trespass is actionable per se, the law gives strong protection to those in possession: a claimant like the one in* Bocardo *can win without proving any harm. This supports privacy and property rights and deters interference, and the remedies are flexible, since damages in lieu (*Jaggard v Sawyer*) avoid oppressive injunctions. But strong protection can restrict others' legitimate activity, which is why the law makes exceptions for aircraft (*Bernstein*), reasonable use of the highway (*DPP v Jones*) and statutory access for repairs.*

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actionable per se | Actionable without proof of damage |
| Possession | Occupation and control of land, which gives the right to sue |
| Licence | Permission to be on land |
| Continuing trespass | A trespass that persists, giving a fresh claim each day |
| Nominal damages | A small sum recognising a legal wrong without loss |
| Mandatory injunction | An order to do something, such as remove an object |
| Prohibitory injunction | An order to stop doing something |
| Damages in lieu | Damages awarded instead of an injunction |
| Self-help | Lawful action by the occupier, such as removing a trespasser with reasonable force |

## Exam traps

- No damage is needed.
- Mistake about ownership or the boundary is no defence.
- Indirect interference is nuisance, not trespass.
- A visitor becomes a trespasser by exceeding or outstaying permission.
- Overflight at a reasonable height is not trespass.

## Self-test

1. Define trespass to land.
2. Who can sue for trespass?
3. What does "actionable per se" mean?
4. Why was there a trespass in *Anchor Brewhouse*?
5. What did *Bocardo v Star Energy* decide?
6. Why was there no trespass in *Bernstein v Skyviews*?
7. What did Scrutton LJ say in *The Calgarth*?
8. What did *Robson v Hallett* decide?
9. Is a mistake about a boundary a defence?
10. What is a continuing trespass, and why does it matter?
11. Give two defences to trespass to land.
12. When will a court award damages instead of an injunction?

**Answers:** 1. An unjustifiable, direct and intentional interference with land in the possession of another. 2. The person in possession, such as a tenant, not only the owner. 3. It can be sued on without proof of damage. 4. Crane jibs swung through the claimant's airspace at a height that affected ordinary use. 5. Drilling wells far beneath the claimant's land was a trespass. 6. The aircraft flew at a reasonable height, above the airspace needed for ordinary use. 7. "When you invite a person into your house to use the staircase, you do not invite him to slide down the banisters." 8. A person whose licence is revoked has a reasonable time to leave before becoming a trespasser. 9. No (*Basely v Clarkson*). 10. A trespass that persists, such as an object left on land; it gives a fresh cause of action every day. 11. Any two: licence; lawful authority; statutory access; necessity. 12. Where an injunction would be oppressive and damages are an adequate remedy (*Jaggard v Sawyer*).
