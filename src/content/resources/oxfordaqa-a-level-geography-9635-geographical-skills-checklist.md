This guide covers **3.5.1 Geographical skills checklist** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. The specification lists five groups of skills -- 3.5.1.1 core, 3.5.1.2 cartographic, 3.5.1.3 graphical, 3.5.1.4 statistical and 3.5.1.5 ICT -- and says they should be developed "in an integrated way and not as a separate theme or topic". Knowledge of the list is needed for **Units 1 to 4** as well as Unit 5, so any of these techniques can appear on any paper.

The [geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/) already defines every technique and works through a positive Spearman's rank and a one-row chi-square test. This guide concentrates on what that overview leaves out: **choosing** the right technique for a data set, constructing maps and graphs to scale, and two further statistical cases -- a negative correlation and a two-by-two chi-square. Condensed notes are in the [skills checklist revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-skills-checklist-revision-notes/) and you can test yourself with the [skills checklist practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-skills-checklist-practice/). All places and figures below are invented.

## Choosing the right technique

Most skills questions are really choice questions: "suggest an appropriate method", "justify your choice", "assess the suitability". Start from the type of data.

| Data | Suitable presentation | Suitable statistic |
|---|---|---|
| Values at named points (river sites, shops) | Located proportional symbols, located bar graphs | Mean, median, dispersion |
| Values for areas (districts, wards) | Choropleth (rates or densities only) | Median and inter-quartile range across areas |
| Continuous surfaces (temperature, height, pollution) | Isoline map | -- |
| Movements between places | Flow lines (volume), desire lines (origin to destination), trip lines (single journeys) | -- |
| Change over time | Line graph (simple, comparative, compound or divergent) | Percentage change, rate of change |
| Two continuous variables | Scatter graph with best fit line | Spearman's rank |
| Counts in categories | Bar graph, pie chart, divided circle | Chi-square |
| Three components that sum to 100% | Triangular graph | -- |
| Values spanning several orders of magnitude | Logarithmic scale | -- |
| Spread of one data set | Dispersion diagram | Range, inter-quartile range, standard deviation |

A justification needs **two parts**: what the technique shows, and why that suits this data. "A scatter graph, because it shows whether two continuous variables are related and reveals anomalies" earns more than "a scatter graph, because it is clear".

## Core skills: annotation, literacy and numeracy

**Annotation** explains; labelling names. On a field sketch, "cliff" is a label; "steep cliff of jointed limestone -- joints exploited by hydraulic action" is an annotation linking feature to process.

**Literacy** in the specification includes "factual text and discursive/creative material" -- for example a planning report, a newspaper article or a resident's account. Ask who wrote it, for whom, and what it leaves out.

**Numeracy** covers number and measurement. The calculations that appear most often are:

- **Percentage change** = (new - old) / old x 100. A town growing from 48,000 to 61,200 people has grown by 13,200 / 48,000 x 100 = **27.5%**.
- **Mean rate of change** = change / time. Over 12 years that is 13,200 / 12 = **1,100 people a year**.
- **Per capita or density** values, so that places of different sizes can be compared fairly.
- **Sensible rounding**: give answers to the precision of the original data.

**Questionnaires and interviews** need careful design. Closed questions and rating scales (for example 1 to 5) produce data that can be counted and tested; open questions produce richer but harder-to-compare answers. Avoid leading wording ("Don't you agree the new road is dangerous?"), pilot the questions, and record where and when each response was collected.

## Cartographic skills: constructing to scale

**Isoline interpolation.** To draw an isoline you estimate where the value falls between two measured points. Point A records 140 and point B records 190; they are 25 mm apart on the map. The 160 isoline lies (160 - 140) / (190 - 140) = 20 / 50 = 0.4 of the distance from A, so **10 mm from A**. Interpolation assumes the value changes evenly between points, which is why isoline maps need dense data.

**Flow lines.** Line width is proportional to the volume moving. With a scale of 1 mm = 500 vehicles a day, a road carrying 3,750 vehicles is drawn **7.5 mm** wide.

**Proportional symbols.** Area, not radius, must be proportional to the value. If 2,500 visitors are shown by a circle of radius 8 mm, then 10,000 visitors (four times as many) needs a radius of 8 x square root of 4 = **16 mm**, and 6,400 visitors needs 8 x square root of (6,400 / 2,500) = 8 x 1.6 = **12.8 mm**.

**Choropleth maps** should show rates or densities. Choose a small number of classes (often four to six), use a single colour that darkens as values rise, and remember that the map implies sudden change at boundaries.

## Graphical skills: reading and judging

**Triangular graphs.** A soil sample with 20% clay, 30% silt and 50% sand is plotted where the three gridlines meet; check that the three values add to 100% before plotting. Points that cluster show samples of similar composition.

**Logarithmic scales.** Each cycle is ten times the one below (1-10, 10-100, 100-1,000). Read values by counting cycles first, then the position within the cycle -- the gridlines inside a cycle are not evenly spaced. On a log scale, a straight rising line means a constant **percentage** rate of growth.

**Best fit lines and residuals.** Draw the line through the general trend so roughly equal numbers of points lie on each side. Points well away from it are **residuals**; explaining them is often where analysis marks are found.

## Statistical skills: interpreting spread and testing relationships

**Comparing spread.** Two sets of seven infiltration times share a mean of 22 seconds. Set A (18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26) has a standard deviation of **2.4 s**; set B (8, 12, 17, 22, 27, 32, 36) has **9.6 s** (using division by n). The same mean hides very different behaviour: set A's ground is uniform, set B's varies greatly, perhaps with compacted paths and loose soil. Always report a measure of spread alongside an average.

**A negative Spearman's rank.** Null hypothesis: there is no relationship between distance from the town centre and the number of recorded crimes. Eight sites, both variables ranked with the highest value as rank 1:

| Site | Distance (km) | Rank | Crimes | Rank | d | d^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.2 | 8 | 42 | 1 | 7 | 49 |
| B | 0.5 | 7 | 38 | 3 | 4 | 16 |
| C | 0.9 | 6 | 40 | 2 | 4 | 16 |
| D | 1.3 | 5 | 29 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| E | 1.8 | 4 | 25 | 6 | -2 | 4 |
| F | 2.4 | 3 | 27 | 5 | -2 | 4 |
| G | 3.0 | 2 | 18 | 7 | -5 | 25 |
| H | 3.7 | 1 | 15 | 8 | -7 | 49 |

Sum of d^2 = 164. rs = 1 - (6 x 164) / (8^3 - 8) = 1 - 984 / 504 = **-0.952**. The sign shows direction (negative), the size shows strength. Compare the size of rs, ignoring the sign, with the critical value supplied: for n = 8 a two-tailed table gives 0.738 at the 0.05 level and 0.881 at the 0.01 level, so the null hypothesis is rejected at the 0.01 level. Crime falls significantly with distance from the centre -- but correlation does not show why; land use, footfall and reporting rates could all be involved.

**A two-by-two chi-square.** Null hypothesis: awareness of flood risk does not differ between residents living near the river and those further away.

| | Aware | Not aware | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Within 200 m of river | 34 | 16 | 50 |
| Over 200 m from river | 21 | 29 | 50 |
| Total | 55 | 45 | 100 |

Expected value = row total x column total / grand total. For "within 200 m, aware": 50 x 55 / 100 = 27.5. The four expected values are 27.5, 22.5, 27.5 and 22.5. Chi-square = sum of (O - E)^2 / E = 1.54 + 1.88 + 1.54 + 1.88 = **6.83**. Degrees of freedom = (2 - 1) x (2 - 1) = 1. Critical values for df = 1 are 3.84 (0.05) and 6.63 (0.01), so the difference is significant at the 0.01 level. The test confirms a difference exists; explaining it requires geographical reasoning, such as direct experience of past floods.

## ICT skills: judging digital sources

- **Remotely sensed data** -- satellite and drone imagery -- covers large or inaccessible areas repeatedly, so change can be tracked over time. Judge it by resolution, date, cloud cover and whether ground truthing has checked what the image shows.
- **Electronic databases** -- census tables, river gauge records -- are large and consistent but were collected for other purposes and may use boundaries that do not match your study area.
- **Crowd sourcing and 'big data'** -- volunteered mapping, social media posts, phone location records -- are huge and near real-time, but are unrepresentative of people who do not contribute and raise privacy questions.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute, give units and round to the data's precision.
- **Justify / suggest:** name the technique and give the reason it suits these data.
- **Interpret a test result:** state the value, compare with the critical value at a named level, accept or reject the null hypothesis, then explain geographically.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a choropleth for raw totals.
- Scaling circle radius, not area, to the value.
- Forgetting that the sign of rs is ignored when comparing with the critical value.
- Calculating chi-square expected values from percentages.
- Interpreting a significant result as proof of cause.
