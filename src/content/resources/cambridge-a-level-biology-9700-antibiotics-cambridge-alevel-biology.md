## What 10.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | Command word | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 10.2.1 | Outline | How penicillin acts on bacteria and why antibiotics do not affect viruses |
| 10.2.2 | Discuss | The consequences of antibiotic resistance and the steps that can be taken to reduce its impact |

An **antibiotic** is a substance that kills bacteria or stops their growth without harming the cells of the person taking it. Penicillin, the first antibiotic to be widely used, is made by a fungus, *Penicillium*.

## How penicillin acts

### The target: a growing cell wall

A bacterial cell wall is made of **peptidoglycan**: long polysaccharide chains linked to each other by short chains of amino acids (peptide **cross-links**). The cross-linked mesh is strong, and it stops the cell bursting. The cytoplasm has a lower water potential than the surroundings, so water tends to enter by osmosis, and only the wall resists the pressure.

For a bacterium to grow, its wall must enlarge:

1. Some enzymes (autolysins) **break bonds** in the existing wall, making small gaps.
2. New peptidoglycan chains are inserted.
3. Other enzymes (**transpeptidases**) form **new cross-links**, sealing the wall.

### What penicillin does

Penicillin **binds to and inhibits the transpeptidases**, so new cross-links cannot form. The breaking enzymes keep working. The wall becomes **weaker** at each growth point. Water continues to enter by osmosis, the wall can no longer resist the pressure, and the cell **bursts** (**osmotic lysis**).

### Consequences to state

- Penicillin affects bacteria **only while they are growing and making new wall**. A dormant bacterium is not killed.
- Human cells have **no cell wall** and no peptidoglycan, so penicillin does not harm them.
- Bacteria whose walls are made differently, or that have enzymes able to destroy penicillin, are not affected.

## Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

Antibiotics work on features found in bacteria: the peptidoglycan wall, bacterial (70S) ribosomes, and enzymes of bacterial metabolism. A virus has:

- no cell wall;
- no ribosomes;
- no metabolism of its own.

A virus is a strand of nucleic acid in a protein coat. It reproduces inside a host cell, using the host's ribosomes and enzymes. There is nothing in the virus for the antibiotic to act on, and attacking the host's own machinery would harm the patient. So antibiotics are useless against viral diseases such as influenza and HIV/AIDS. They may be prescribed during a viral illness only to treat a secondary bacterial infection.

## Antibiotic resistance

### How resistance appears

Resistance begins with a random **mutation** in a bacterium's DNA. For penicillin, a common example is a gene coding for **penicillinase**, an enzyme that breaks down penicillin before it can act. The mutation happens by chance; the antibiotic does **not** cause it.

### How resistance spreads

- **Selection**: when the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria die. Resistant bacteria survive, so they no longer compete with the others, and they reproduce rapidly.
- **Vertical transmission**: resistant bacteria divide by binary fission, passing the allele to all their offspring.
- **Horizontal transmission**: resistance genes are often carried on **plasmids**, which can be copied and passed to other bacteria, including other species, by conjugation.

Because bacteria reproduce very quickly, a resistant population can arise in a short time. Well-known examples include MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) and strains of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* resistant to several drugs.

### Worked example 1: selection in numbers

A patient's infection contains 10⁸ bacteria. Resistant bacteria make up 1 in 10⁶ of them. An antibiotic kills 99.9 % of susceptible bacteria but none of the resistant ones. The numbers are invented for practice.

```
resistant bacteria = 10⁸ ÷ 10⁶ = 100
susceptible bacteria ≈ 10⁸
susceptible survivors = 0.1 % of 10⁸ = 10⁵
proportion resistant afterwards = 100 ÷ (10⁵ + 100) ≈ 0.001 = 0.1 %
proportion resistant before = 1 ÷ 10⁶ = 0.0001 %
increase = 0.1 ÷ 0.0001 = 1000 times
```

One treatment raises the proportion of resistant bacteria a thousandfold. If the survivors reproduce and spread, the next infection starts with far more resistant cells. This is why unnecessary use of antibiotics matters.

## Discussing consequences

"Discuss" asks for consequences and their significance, not a bare list.

| Consequence | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Infections take longer to cure or cannot be cured | More deaths from infections that used to be easily treated |
| Longer stays in hospital | Higher costs; patients act as a source of resistant bacteria for others |
| Newer, more expensive antibiotics needed | Some health systems cannot afford them; some have more side effects |
| Routine surgery, cancer treatment and care of premature babies become riskier | These depend on preventing or treating bacterial infection |
| Multi-drug-resistant TB | Treatment is longer and more difficult, making TB control harder |

## Steps to reduce the impact

Each step works by **reducing selection pressure** or **reducing spread**. Say which.

- Prescribe antibiotics only when they are needed, never for viral infections (less selection).
- Identify the bacterium and test which antibiotic it is sensitive to, then use a narrow-spectrum antibiotic (fewer other bacteria exposed, less selection).
- Patients complete the full course and do not share or keep leftovers (fewer bacteria survive to develop and pass on resistance).
- Rotate the antibiotics used in a hospital (no single drug selects for long).
- Reduce antibiotic use in farm animals, especially to promote growth (less selection; fewer resistant bacteria passed to humans).
- Hand-washing, cleaning and isolating infected patients in hospitals (less spread).
- Fund research into new antibiotics and other treatments.

## Worked example 2: interpreting test data

Discs soaked in three antibiotics are placed on a lawn of bacteria growing on agar. After incubation, a clear zone (no growth) surrounds each disc. The data are invented for practice.

| Antibiotic | Diameter of clear zone / mm |
|---|---|
| P | 24 |
| Q | 12 |
| R | 6 (same as the disc) |

```
area of zone P = π × 12² = 452 mm²
area of zone Q = π × 6²  = 113 mm²
ratio P : Q    = 452 ÷ 113 = 4.0
```

The bacteria are most sensitive to P, less sensitive to Q, and resistant to R (no clear zone beyond the disc). Doubling the diameter gives four times the area. A sound conclusion also adds a limit: zone size depends partly on how quickly each antibiotic diffuses through agar, so a larger zone suggests, but does not prove, a more effective drug.

## Common mistakes

- Saying penicillin "breaks down the wall" or "makes holes". It stops cross-links forming as the wall grows.
- Missing the role of osmosis in lysis.
- Saying antibiotics do not work on viruses "because viruses are too small" or "are not alive", without naming the missing targets.
- Saying the antibiotic causes the mutation, or that bacteria "become immune" or "get used to" the drug.
- Listing steps to reduce resistance without saying how each reduces selection or spread.
- Using the radius and diameter interchangeably in area calculations.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline** (penicillin): wall structure → enzymes inhibited → weakened wall → osmotic lysis; a concise sequence, not an essay.
- **Explain** (viruses): name the structures antibiotics target and state that viruses lack them.
- **Discuss** (resistance): consequence plus significance, then step plus how it works.
- **Calculate**: area = πr² with r = diameter ÷ 2.
