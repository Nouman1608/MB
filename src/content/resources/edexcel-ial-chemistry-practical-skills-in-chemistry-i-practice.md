Every question below targets Unit 3: Practical Skills in Chemistry I from the YCH11 specification (Pearson Edexcel, International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Chemistry, Issue 1, September 2017). It is an International AS (IAS) unit covering the skills from Units 1 and 2, including Core Practicals 1 to 8. The methods are taught in the [study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-chemistry-practical-skills-in-chemistry-i/) and summarised in the [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-chemistry-practical-skills-in-chemistry-i-revision-notes/). Data for this set: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Na = 23.0, S = 32.1, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40.1, Cu = 63.5. Gas constant R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Specific heat capacity of solutions = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

## Questions

**1.** Uncertainties per reading: balance ±0.005 g; burette ±0.05 cm³.

**(a)** 0.240 g of a solid is weighed by difference. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the mass. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest how to reduce this uncertainty with the same balance. **[1]**
**(c)** Burette readings are 0.85 cm³ (start) and 19.15 cm³ (end). Calculate the titre and its percentage uncertainty. **[2]**

**2.** Core Practical 1. 0.350 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid, giving 79.5 cm³ of carbon dioxide, collected over water at 20 °C and 100 kPa.

**(a)** Give a balanced equation, including state symbols, for this reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Use these results to find the molar volume of the gas in dm³ mol⁻¹. **[2]**
**(c)** Use pV = nRT to calculate the expected molar volume at 20 °C and 100 kPa. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest a reason, other than a leak, for the low result. **[1]**

**3.** Core Practical 2. You want ΔH for CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s).

- 4.00 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ dissolves in 50.0 cm³ of water: rise of 7.8 °C.
- 6.24 g of CuSO₄·5H₂O dissolves in 50.0 cm³ of water: fall of 1.3 °C.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for dissolving each solid, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[4]**
**(b)** Use a Hess cycle to calculate ΔH for the hydration. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the hydration cannot be measured directly. **[1]**

**4.** Core Practical 3. 25.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid is titrated with 0.0950 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide using methyl orange. Titres: rough 26.40, then 25.85, 26.10 and 25.95 cm³.

**(a)** Give the colour change seen when the end point is reached. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean titre to use. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the concentration of the acid in mol dm⁻³. **[2]**
**(d)** Convert your answer to g dm⁻³. **[1]**

**5.** Core Practical 4.

**(a)** Describe how to make 250 cm³ of a standard solution from a weighed sample of a solid acid. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the burette is rinsed with the sodium hydroxide solution, not with water. **[1]**

**6.** Core Practical 5. Three isomers of C₄H₉Br are tested with silver nitrate in ethanol at 40 °C. Times for a precipitate: 1-bromobutane 520 s; 2-bromobutane 190 s; 2-bromo-2-methylpropane 15 s.

**(a)** State the colour of the precipitate. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the relative rate, 1/time, for 2-bromo-2-methylpropane. **[1]**
**(c)** State the trend shown, using the terms primary, secondary and tertiary. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why ethanol is used. **[1]**
**(e)** State one variable, other than temperature, that must be controlled. **[1]**

**7.** Core Practical 6. 10.0 cm³ of 2-methylpropan-2-ol (density 0.786 g cm⁻³) is shaken with excess concentrated hydrochloric acid, giving 6.24 g of pure 2-chloro-2-methylpropane (density 0.84 g cm⁻³, boiling temperature 51 °C).

**(a)** Is the product in the top or bottom layer of the funnel? Give a reason. **[1]**
**(b)** The crude product is shaken with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution. Give the purpose of this step and explain why the funnel is vented often. **[2]**
**(c)** State how you know the product is dry. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(e)** State a suitable collection range for the product. **[1]**

**8.** Core Practical 7. A student oxidises propan-1-ol using potassium dichromate(VI) in dilute sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Explain why propanal is distilled off as it forms, into a cooled receiver. **[2]**
**(b)** Give a test, with the result, that shows the distillate contains an aldehyde. **[1]**
**(c)** Give a test, with the result, for propanoic acid. **[1]**
**(d)** State the colour change of the oxidising agent. **[1]**
**(e)** State one hazard in this practical and a matching precaution. **[1]**

**9.** Core Practical 8. Three unknowns are tested.

- Solid W: lilac flame; white precipitate with dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride.
- Solid Y: fizzes with dilute acid, giving a gas that turns limewater milky; warmed with sodium hydroxide, gives a gas that turns damp red litmus blue.
- Liquid Z, C₃H₈O: steamy fumes with phosphorus(V) chloride; turns warm acidified dichromate(VI) orange to green; the product does not affect Benedict's solution.

**(a)** Identify W. Give the ionic equation for the barium chloride test. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify Y. Give the ionic equation for the reaction with sodium hydroxide. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify Z, explaining how each observation supports your answer. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the barium chloride is added after acid. **[1]**

**10.** An unfamiliar practical. A 250 cm³ solution is made from 3.58 g of Na₂CO₃·xH₂O. Each 25.0 cm³ portion needs, on average, 25.00 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid (methyl orange). Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + CO₂ + H₂O.

**(a)** Calculate the value of x. **[5]**
**(b)** Explain why methyl orange is used, not phenolphthalein. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest how to reduce the percentage uncertainty in the titre. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 2 × 0.005 = 0.010 g; 0.010 ÷ 0.240 × 100 = **4.2%** [1].
**(b)** Weigh a larger mass of solid [1].
**(c)** Titre = 19.15 − 0.85 = **18.30 cm³** [1]; 0.10 ÷ 18.30 × 100 = **0.55%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the doubling of the reading uncertainty; a single-reading value of 0.05 gives half the correct percentage.

**2. (a)** CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) [1].
**(b)** n = 0.350 ÷ 100.1 = 3.497 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; 79.5 ÷ 3.497 × 10⁻³ = 22 700 cm³ mol⁻¹ = **22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** V = RT ÷ p = 8.31 × 293 ÷ 100 000 [1] = 0.0243 m³ = **24.3 dm³ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** Some carbon dioxide dissolves in the water it is collected over [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use kelvin and pascals in pV = nRT, then convert m³ to dm³; mixed units cannot give the right value.

**3. (a)** Anhydrous: energy = 50.0 × 4.18 × 7.8 = 1630 J [1]; n = 4.00 ÷ 159.6 = 0.02506 mol; ΔH₁ = −1.630 ÷ 0.02506 = **−65.0 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. Hydrated: energy = 50.0 × 4.18 × 1.3 = 272 J [1]; n = 6.24 ÷ 249.6 = 0.0250 mol; ΔH₂ = **+10.9 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** ΔH = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂ = −65.0 − 10.9 = **−75.9 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Adding exactly five moles of water without dissolving some solid is impossible [1].
*Examiner insight:* A rise gives a negative ΔH and a fall a positive one; write the sign on every value.

**4. (a)** Red to orange [1].
**(b)** 25.85 and 25.95 are concordant; mean = **25.90 cm³** [1].
**(c)** n(NaOH) = 0.0950 × 0.02590 = 2.461 × 10⁻³ mol = n(HCl) [1]; c = 2.461 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0984 mol dm⁻³** [1].
**(d)** 0.0984 × 36.5 = **3.59 g dm⁻³** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show which titres you chose; quote the mean to two decimal places, like the readings.

**5. (a)** Weigh by difference, then dissolve the solid in a beaker of distilled water [1]. Move the solution and all beaker and rod washings into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask [1]. Make up until the meniscus bottom sits on the mark at eye level [1]. Stopper and invert to mix [1].
**(b)** Water would dilute the alkali and change its concentration [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the detail that makes each step work: "rinse the beaker into the flask" is stronger than "transfer it".

**6. (a)** Cream [1].
**(b)** 1 ÷ 15 = **0.067 s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Rate increases from primary to secondary to tertiary [1].
**(d)** Ethanol dissolves both the halogenoalkane and the aqueous reagent [1].
**(e)** Concentration and volume of silver nitrate solution, or amount of each halogenoalkane [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the controlled variable precisely; "the amounts" alone is weak.

**7. (a)** Top: 0.84 g cm⁻³ is below the density of the aqueous layer [1].
**(b)** Neutralises leftover acid [1]; it releases carbon dioxide, so pressure builds up [1].
**(c)** The liquid turns from cloudy to clear [1].
**(d)** Mass of alcohol = 10.0 × 0.786 = 7.86 g; n = 7.86 ÷ 74.0 = 0.1062 mol [1]. Theoretical mass = 0.1062 × 92.5 = 9.83 g [1]. Yield = 6.24 ÷ 9.83 × 100 = **63.5%** [1].
**(e)** About 49 to 53 °C [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert volume to mass with the density before finding moles.

**8. (a)** Removing propanal as it forms prevents further oxidation to propanoic acid [1]; propanal is volatile, so cooling reduces loss by evaporation [1].
**(b)** Warmed Benedict's (or Fehling's) solution: blue, then a red precipitate [1].
**(c)** Add sodium hydrogencarbonate: effervescence of a gas that turns limewater milky [1].
**(d)** Orange to green [1].
**(e)** Dichromate(VI) is toxic: gloves. Or the alcohol is flammable: no naked flame [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say what distilling prevents: further oxidation.

**9. (a)** **Potassium sulfate**, K₂SO₄ [1]; Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) [1].
**(b)** **Ammonium carbonate**, (NH₄)₂CO₃ (ammonium hydrogencarbonate also fits) [1]; NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l) [1].
**(c)** Steamy fumes: an –OH group, so an alcohol [1]. Orange to green: primary or secondary [1]. No aldehyde, so the product is a ketone and Z is secondary: **propan-2-ol** [1].
**(d)** Acid removes carbonate (or similar) ions that would also give a white precipitate [1].
*Examiner insight:* Ionic equations need state symbols and no spectator ions; including K⁺ or Cl⁻ weakens the answer.

**10. (a)** n(HCl) = 0.100 × 0.02500 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. n(Na₂CO₃) in 25.0 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. In 250 cm³: 1.25 × 10⁻² mol [1]. M = 3.58 ÷ 0.0125 = 286 g mol⁻¹ [1]. x = (286 − 106) ÷ 18.0 = **10** [1].
**(b)** Phenolphthalein changes colour at the hydrogencarbonate stage, after only half the acid needed [1].
**(c)** Use more dilute acid, so the titre is larger [1].
*Examiner insight:* Scale from 25.0 cm³ to 250 cm³ before finding M; missing the factor of 10 is an easy slip.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Averaging every titre, including the rough one.
- Halving the percentage uncertainty by forgetting that a titre uses two readings.
- Losing the sign of ΔH, or giving it in J mol⁻¹ when kJ mol⁻¹ is asked for.
- Writing "turns green" with no starting colour.
- Giving "wear goggles" when a specific hazard is named.
- Leaving out the scale-up factor for a 25.0 cm³ portion of a 250 cm³ solution.
