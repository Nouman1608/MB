These questions cover 3.5.3 Criminal and deviant behaviour, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 2: social class, gender, ethnicity and age as factors; public debates over violent crime, sentencing, young offenders, the prison system and media coverage; the four perspectives; and the key ideas of Albert Cohen and Carlen. All people and places in the items are fictional.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which term did Albert Cohen use for the frustration felt by working-class boys unable to gain status legitimately?

A. Anomie
B. Status frustration
C. Relative deprivation
D. Master status

**[1]**

**2.** Which research method did Carlen use in *Women, Crime and Poverty*?

A. A national victim survey
B. In-depth unstructured interviews
C. A self-report questionnaire
D. Content analysis of newspapers

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "chivalry thesis". **[2]**

**4.** Identify two activities valued by the delinquent subculture described by Albert Cohen. **[2]**

**5.** Describe Carlen's "class deal". **[2]**

**6.** Identify and explain one reason why young people are more likely than older people to be recorded as offenders. **[3]**

**7.** Identify and describe one public debate over criminal and deviant behaviour. **[3]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Read the item.

> Dean, 16, lives on an estate in Calderbrook (a fictional town). He left school with no qualifications and has applied for twenty jobs without success. He now spends his evenings with a group of friends who spray graffiti and shoplift. Dean says: "Round here, people respect you if you can get away with it."

Using the item and Albert Cohen's ideas, explain why Dean may have joined a delinquent subculture. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> Sharon, 34, from Eastmere (a fictional city), lost her job when a factory closed. Her partner left, and she struggled to pay rent for herself and two children. She was later convicted of fraud for claiming benefits she was not entitled to.

Using the item and Carlen's ideas, explain why Sharon may have committed crime. **[4]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why some ethnic minority groups are over-represented in official crime statistics. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a Marxist would explain the pattern of social class in official crime statistics. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that working-class people commit more crime than middle-class people. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that women commit less crime than men because they are more closely controlled. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Status frustration [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Anomie is Merton's term and master status is Becker's.

**2.** B. In-depth unstructured interviews [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Learn the method and sample size (39 women); both are useful in evaluation.

**3.** The claim that a male-dominated criminal justice system treats women more leniently than men [1], so women's crimes are less likely to be recorded or punished, making female crime appear lower than it is [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs the consequence for the statistics.

**4.** Any two from: stealing, vandalism, truancy. First activity [1]; second activity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* These three come from the specification's summary of Cohen.

**5.** Respectable working-class women are offered consumer goods [1] in return for their wage labour [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both sides of the exchange are needed for both marks.

**6.** Young people spend more time in public spaces such as streets and parks [1], so they are more visible to the police [1] and more likely to be stopped, arrested and recorded than older people who offend in private [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Peer pressure or having less to lose are also credited if explained through to offending or recording.

**7.** For example, the treatment of young offenders [1]. Some argue young offenders should be punished to deter them and protect the public [1], while others argue they should be rehabilitated, because labelling them as criminals may push them into a deviant career [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe a debate" means both sides of the argument, not just the topic.

**8.** Cohen argued working-class boys share mainstream success goals but fail at school [1]. Dean left school with no qualifications and has failed to find work, so he is stuck at the bottom with no legitimate route to status [1]. This produces status frustration [1]. His group offers an alternative route: "people respect you if you can get away with it" shows prestige from peers for success by the subculture's values, such as shoplifting and graffiti [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dean's quotation is the evidence of prestige within the subculture; use it.

**9.** Carlen used control theory, arguing working-class women conform for the promise of rewards [1]. Sharon's class deal failed when she lost her job, so she lost the consumer goods offered in return for a wage [1]. Her gender deal failed when her partner left, removing the material rewards of a male breadwinner [1]. With both deals failing and children to support, crime became a viable alternative [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name both deals and show each one failing using the item.

**10.** First, some ethnic minority groups are more likely to live in poverty and in deprived areas [1], so deprivation, not ethnicity itself, may explain higher offending [1]. Second, institutional racism and stereotyping may mean some groups are stopped and arrested more often [1]; the Macpherson Report (1999) concluded the Metropolitan Police was institutionally racist, so the statistics may reflect policing rather than offending [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two different explanations are needed: one about offending and one about policing works well.

**11.** Marxists argue laws are made and enforced in the interests of the ruling class [1]. Working-class crimes such as theft are heavily policed and prosecuted [1], while white collar and corporate crimes by the powerful are harder to detect and often dealt with outside the criminal courts, so the working class are over-represented [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer should explain the pattern, not just state that the working class are over-represented.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Official statistics show working-class people over-represented among recorded offenders [1].
- Merton: blocked legitimate means lead some working-class people to innovation [1].
- Albert Cohen: educational failure leads to status frustration and delinquent subcultures [1].
- Carlen: working-class women may turn to crime when the class deal fails [1].
- Material deprivation may push some people towards property crime [1].
- Against: interactionists argue police patrol working-class areas more and label working-class youths [1].
- More policing produces more recorded working-class crime regardless of actual offending [1].
- Marxists argue white collar and corporate crimes are under-recorded and often not prosecuted [1].
- The dark figure means official statistics are an incomplete guide to who commits crime [1].
- Self-report studies suggest offending is more widespread across classes than official figures show [1].
- Criticism of Cohen: working-class youths may reject mainstream goals rather than share them [1].
- Judgement: working-class people are more likely to be recorded as offenders, but whether they commit more crime is uncertain because crimes of the powerful are hidden [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The key distinction between committing crime and being recorded is what moves an answer into the top band.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Men commit far more recorded crime than women [1].
- Heidensohn argues patriarchal societies control women more effectively than men [1].
- At home, housework and childcare leave women little time for crime [1].
- In public, fear of male violence and of losing reputation limits women's behaviour [1].
- At work, male-dominated hierarchies and harassment control women [1].
- Daughters are more closely supervised than sons [1].
- Carlen also uses control theory: when the class and gender deals fail, some women offend [1].
- Against: socialisation may make girls more caring and less risk-taking, a different explanation [1].
- The chivalry thesis suggests women's crime is under-recorded, so the gap may be smaller than it appears [1].
- Evaluation: Heidensohn generalises to all women and her claims are not always backed by strong research evidence [1].
- Carlen's sample was only 39 women, so it may not be representative [1].
- Judgement: control explains much of the gap, but socialisation and recording also contribute, so it is the main but not the only reason [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Use Carlen carefully: she supports control theory by showing what happens when control breaks down.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing Albert Cohen with Stanley Cohen.
- Describing Carlen's deals without showing them failing.
- Treating official statistics as proof of who offends.
- Naming a public debate without the two sides of the argument.
- Not using the item details in Questions 8 and 9.
- Giving one perspective only in a "discuss how far" answer.
