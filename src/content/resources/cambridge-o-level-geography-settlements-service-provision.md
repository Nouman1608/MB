This guide covers **sub-topic 1.5 Settlements (Rural and Urban) and Service Provision** of
Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 (Version 3). The
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/)
introduces site, situation and hierarchy for the whole theme; this page adds settlement patterns,
the full set of site factors, changing functions and the methods used to study service provision.

## What 1.5 asks you to do

You need to be able to:

- explain the **patterns** of settlement: **dispersed**, **nucleated** and **linear**;
- explain the factors that influence the **sites, growth and functions** of settlements;
- give reasons for the **hierarchy** of settlements and services, using **sphere of influence**,
  **threshold population** and **range**;
- use a case study of **settlement and service provision in an area**, which is often your own
  local area or region.

Urban land use and the problems of cities are separate sub-topics: see
[1.6 Urban Settlements](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-urban-settlements/) and
[1.7 Urbanisation](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-urbanisation/). Here the focus is
on why settlements are where they are, why they grow, and what services they can support.

## Key idea 1: settlement patterns

The pattern is the arrangement of buildings and settlements across the landscape, best seen on a
map.

| Pattern | Description | Typical reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **Dispersed** | Isolated farms or houses spread across an area | Large farms; pastoral or plantation farming; plentiful water everywhere; few defence needs |
| **Nucleated** | Buildings clustered tightly round a centre, such as a crossroads, well or market | Shared water source; defence; need for cooperation; scarce flat land; around a church, mosque or market |
| **Linear** | Buildings in a line or ribbon | Along a road, railway, river, canal, coast or narrow valley floor; on raised land above a floodplain |

On a map extract, describe the pattern you see and then **suggest** a reason linked to a feature
on the map, such as "linear along the main road, which gives access to markets".

## Key idea 2: site factors

The **site** is the land a settlement is built on. Early settlements were sited to meet basic
needs.

- **Water supply**: a **wet point** site near a spring, river or well, especially in dry areas.
- **Dry point**: raised land above a floodplain or marsh, safe from flooding.
- **Defence**: a hilltop, an island or a meander loop that is surrounded by water on most sides.
  Durham in England is a classic example: its cathedral and castle stand on a high peninsula
  almost encircled by a meander of the River Wear.
- **Bridging point** or ford: the lowest place where a river could be crossed.
- **Gap**: a natural route through hills.
- **Shelter and aspect**: protection from prevailing winds; slopes facing the sun.
- **Building materials, fuel and fertile land** nearby.

## Key idea 3: growth and functions

Whether a settlement **grows** depends more on its **situation** — its position relative to other
places, routes and resources — than on its site. A settlement at the meeting point of several
routes (a **route centre**), at a natural harbour, or near valuable resources is accessible to many
people and attracts trade and jobs.

The **function** of a settlement is its main economic or social purpose:

| Function | Example of a reason |
|---|---|
| Market town | Central to a farming area; trade in produce |
| Port | Sheltered deep-water harbour; links to inland routes |
| Administrative centre | Chosen as a capital or regional seat of government |
| Industrial or mining town | Near raw materials, energy or a workforce |
| Resort | Beaches, mountains, heritage or climate attractive to tourists |
| Commuter settlement | Within travelling distance of a city's jobs |

Functions **change over time**. Large cities are multi-functional. A small fishing village may
become a tourist resort; a mining town may decline when the mine closes; a farming village near a
city may become a commuter village whose residents work elsewhere. Islamabad shows a planned
administrative function: it was planned and built in the 1960s as Pakistan's new capital.

## Key idea 4: the hierarchy of settlements and services

A **settlement hierarchy** ranks settlements by size and by the number and range of services they
provide: isolated dwelling, hamlet, village, town, city, conurbation. As you go up the hierarchy
there are **fewer settlements**, each **larger**, further apart, with **more and higher-order
services** and a **larger sphere of influence**.

The reasons come from three linked ideas:

- **Threshold population**: the minimum number of people needed to keep a service running.
- **Range**: the maximum distance people are prepared to travel to use it.
- **Sphere of influence**: the area a settlement or service draws its customers from.

**Low-order services** (convenience goods such as bread, a newspaper, a local clinic or primary
school) are used often, are cheap, have a **low threshold** and a **short range**, so they are
found even in villages. **High-order services** (comparison goods such as jewellery, cars,
specialist hospitals and universities) are used rarely, are expensive, have a **high threshold**
and a **long range**, so only large settlements with large spheres of influence can support them.

**Service provision is changing.** In many rural areas village shops, banks and post offices have
closed because higher car ownership lets people shop in larger towns, online shopping and banking
reduce visits, and populations have aged or declined. Responses include mobile services
(libraries, clinics, banking vans), multi-purpose shops combining a post office and café, and
community-run shops. In fast-growing urban areas the opposite problem arises: services cannot be
built fast enough to meet demand.

## Worked example: measuring a sphere of influence

A student surveys 40 shoppers in a market town about where they live. Illustrative results:

| Service | Mean distance travelled (km) | Furthest customer (km) |
|---|---|---|
| Newsagent | 1.5 | 4 |
| Supermarket | 6 | 15 |
| Furniture store | 22 | 55 |

- The **furniture store** has the largest range and sphere of influence, so it is a high-order
  service with a high threshold.
- The **newsagent** has the smallest, so it is low-order.
- On a map, the student would draw **desire lines** from the town to each customer's home, then
  outline the sphere of influence for each service.

Conclusion: the larger the threshold and range of a service, the fewer settlements can support it,
which is why the hierarchy forms. Limitations to mention in an evaluation: a small sample, one day
only, and the time and day of the survey may not be typical.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **site** (the land) with **situation** (the relative position).
- Describing a pattern as "nucleated" without naming what it is clustered around.
- Defining threshold as "the number of people who use a service". It is the **minimum** needed to
  keep it viable.
- Explaining the hierarchy without using threshold and range.
- Case study answers with no named settlements or services.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a pattern or site factor from a map: use the exact term and give map evidence, such
  as a grid reference or a feature name.
- **Describe** a hierarchy from a table: number of settlements, size and services at each level,
  with figures.
- **Explain**: link threshold and range to why a service is found, or missing, in a settlement.
- **Suggest** changes in function or services using clues in the question, such as a new road or
  a closed factory.
- For the case study, name settlements at different levels in one area and the services each has.
