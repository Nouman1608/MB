## Questions

**1.** (a) Define inflation. **[1]**
**(b)** In Country A the inflation rate falls from 4% to 2%. Explain why this is not deflation. **[2]**

**2.** Country B's CPI uses four groups with weights of 35, 25, 20 and 20. The CPI in 2025 was 104.25. In 2026 the group indices (base year 2020 = 100) were 108, 113, 104 and 97, in the same order.
**(a)** Calculate the CPI for 2026. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate of inflation for 2026 to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** The fourth group's index fell from 98 to 97, while the indices of the other three groups all rose. Explain why the CPI still rose. **[1]**

**3.** Country E's CPI falls from 125 to 120. Calculate the rate of change and state what it shows. **[3]**

**4.** State whether each is more likely to cause demand-pull or cost-push inflation.
**(a)** A government cuts income tax and raises its spending when firms are already near full capacity. **[1]**
**(b)** Dock workers across a country win a 9% pay rise with no gain in output per worker. **[1]**
**(c)** The world price of an imported fuel rises sharply. **[1]**

**5.** Explain how a rise in consumer confidence could lead to demand-pull inflation. **[4]**

**6.** A small laundry in Country F washes 4,000 loads a month. Monthly costs are detergent 2,000, wages 9,000, energy 3,000 and rent 2,000. Detergent rises 15%, wages 4% and energy 20%. Rent is unchanged.
**(a)** Calculate the new monthly cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in total cost, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how this is an example of cost-push inflation if many firms respond in the same way. **[2]**

**7.** Explain one cause of demand-side deflation and one cause of supply-side deflation. **[4]**

**8.** A saver in Country G puts 8,000 in an account paying 1.5% a year. Inflation is 3.5%.
**(a)** Calculate the account balance at the end of the year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its value in the prices of a year earlier, to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a borrower with a fixed-rate loan gains in this situation. **[2]**

**9.** A worker's monthly pay rises from 3,200 to 3,296. Over the same year the CPI rises from 120.0 to 124.8.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in pay. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in the CPI. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate real pay in each year (pay ÷ CPI × 100) and state what happened to the worker's buying power. **[2]**

**10.** Explain two consequences of deflation for firms and one for the economy as a whole. **[6]**

**11.** Country H has inflation of 7%. Most of it comes from a rise in the price of imported energy. The central bank is considering a rise in interest rates. Evaluate whether this is the best policy. **[8]**

**12.** Country J has deflation of 2% a year, caused by weak consumer spending and falling export orders. Discuss which policies the government might use and how effective they are likely to be. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A sustained rise in the general level of prices [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Prices are still rising [1], only more slowly [1]. Deflation requires the price level to fall, meaning a negative inflation rate. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit when they say prices still rise and when they distinguish level from rate; just saying "it is lower" is a description of the figures, not an explanation.

**2. (a)** Weight × index: 35 × 108 = 3,780; 25 × 113 = 2,825; 20 × 104 = 2,080; 20 × 97 = 1,940. Total = 10,625 [1]. 10,625 ÷ 100 = **106.25** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (106.25 − 104.25) ÷ 104.25 × 100 [1] = **1.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The other three groups, with a combined weight of 80, all rose, so they outweighed the small fall in a group with weight 20 [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing out the weight-times-index line for each group makes the working visible and lets a small slip be traced. Divide by the sum of the weights, here 100.

**3.** Change = (120 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 [1] = **−4.0%** [1]. A negative rate means prices fell on average: deflation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the old value, not the new one, is the usual point of difference between a correct and an incorrect result. Naming deflation in words completes the answer.

**4. (a)** Demand-pull, because demand rises when the economy cannot produce more [1]. **(b)** Cost-push, because wage costs rise without output rising [1]. **(c)** Cost-push, because input costs rise [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Short classification items are secured by naming the starting point: demand rising, or costs rising. A label with no reason is weaker.

**5.** Higher confidence makes households more willing to spend and borrow [1]. Total demand rises [1]. If firms are near full capacity they cannot raise output quickly, so they raise prices and offer higher wages to attract workers [1]. The general price level rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A good chain has each step leading to the next. Answers that jump from confidence to higher prices miss the link of demand exceeding what the economy can supply.

**6. (a)** Detergent 2,000 × 1.15 = 2,300; wages 9,000 × 1.04 = 9,360; energy 3,000 × 1.20 = 3,600 [1]. New total = 2,300 + 9,360 + 3,600 + 2,000 = **17,260** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (17,260 − 16,000) ÷ 16,000 × 100 = **7.9%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Costs of production have risen [1], so firms raise prices to protect profit, and when many do so the general price level rises [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each rise to its own cost line before adding. Applying one percentage to the total gives a different figure and loses credit.

**7.** Demand-side: high interest rates reduce borrowing and spending [1], so firms with unsold stock cut prices [1]. Supply-side: new technology or higher productivity lowers costs of production [1], so firms can cut prices and still earn profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit tends to follow the description of how each cause reaches prices. A bare list of causes earns less than explained ones, and the two causes must be on opposite sides.

**8. (a)** 8,000 × 1.015 = **8,120** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 8,120 ÷ 1.035 [1] = **7,845** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The borrower repays a fixed sum [1], which is worth less in real terms because prices have risen, so the debt shrinks in real terms [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Real value means the amount deflated by prices. Comparing 8,120 with 8,000 alone shows a gain in money terms, which is not the point of the question.

**9. (a)** (3,296 − 3,200) ÷ 3,200 × 100 = **3.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** (124.8 − 120.0) ÷ 120.0 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Real pay before = 3,200 ÷ 120.0 × 100 = 2,666.7; after = 3,296 ÷ 124.8 × 100 = 2,641.0 [1]. Real pay fell about 1.0%, so buying power fell even though money pay rose [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Pay rising slower than prices is the standard reason for a real fall. A conclusion in words after the numbers shows you understand what the figures mean.

**10.** Firms: falling prices cut revenue while costs such as wages and loan repayments may not fall as fast [1], so profits are squeezed [1]. A second firm consequence: firms delay investment because they expect lower prices [1]. Economy: consumers delay buying, expecting lower prices [1], so demand falls [1], output and employment fall, and a downward spiral may follow [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each consequence needs a reason attached. Naming the group in the question, firms and then the economy, keeps the answer on target.

**11.** Case for: higher rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so spending falls and price pressure eases [1]. A higher rate may raise the exchange rate, which makes imported energy cheaper [1]. Case against: the inflation comes from costs, not excess demand [1], so the rise cuts output and raises unemployment while the energy price still pushes costs up [1]. The effect takes time [1]. Firms with loans face higher costs themselves [1]. Alternative: supply-side policy or reducing dependence on imported energy lowers costs over time, though slowly [1]. Judgement: higher interest rates are a weak main tool for imported cost-push inflation, useful only if a second round of wage and demand pressure appears [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluation depends on the stated cause. Answers that apply the standard remedy without noting the cause is imported cost-push are limited. The final judgement should be supported.

**12.** Monetary: cut interest rates to encourage borrowing and spending [1]. This helps only if households and firms are confident enough to borrow, and there is a limit to how far rates can fall [1]. A fall in the exchange rate would make exports cheaper and revive export orders [1], but it raises import prices [1]. Fiscal: cut taxes or raise government spending [1], accepting a budget deficit [1]. A tax cut may be saved, and a bigger deficit adds to debt [1]. Judgement: since demand is the cause, demand-side policy fits; a mix, with confidence and lags, is likely best [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Match the policy to the cause given. Credit is more likely where each measure is paired with a reason it may be limited in this case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a falling inflation rate deflation.
- Dividing by the new figure in a percentage change.
- Forgetting to divide by the total of the weights.
- Applying one percentage to a total instead of each cost line.
- Not stating the meaning of a negative result in words.
- Writing about "people" instead of consumers, workers, savers, lenders or firms as asked.
- Naming a policy without explaining how it reaches prices.
- Concluding without a judgement based on the cause in the case.
