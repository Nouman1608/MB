## Why chemists combine techniques

Each technique answers a different question:

- **Mass spectrometry** gives the molecular mass and formula.
- **Infrared spectroscopy** shows which bonds, and so which functional groups, are present.
- **NMR spectroscopy** shows the carbon or hydrogen environments, what each is next to, and (for ¹H)
  how many H atoms are in each.

Together they confirm the structure of a new compound. In problems you must make all the data agree.

## How NMR gives information

In a strong magnetic field, ¹H and ¹³C nuclei absorb radio-frequency radiation. The frequency depends
on the atoms around each nucleus, its **environment**, and atoms in the same environment give one
signal. So NMR shows the **position** of each ¹H or ¹³C atom in the molecule.

### Equivalent atoms

Atoms are **equivalent** (in the same environment) when the molecule's symmetry makes them
interchangeable, like the three H atoms of a CH₃ group. Look for symmetry before you look at spectra.

## Chemical shift and the δ scale

A spectrum plots absorption against **chemical shift, δ**, measured in parts per million (ppm). The
scale runs from right (δ = 0) to left (larger δ). Chemical shift depends on the molecular environment.
A nucleus close to an electronegative atom such as O, or close to a C=O group, appears at a larger δ.

The specification expects you to use shift data from the Chemistry Data Booklet. The ranges below are
**approximate values written for this page**, not copied from the AQA booklet. Always work from the
AQA booklet itself.

| ¹H environment (approximate) | δ / ppm | ¹³C environment (approximate) | δ / ppm |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₃, CH₂, CH in an alkyl chain | 0.7–1.7 | C–C in an alkyl chain | 5–50 |
| H on C next to C=O | 2.0–2.7 | C–O (alcohol, ether, ester) | 50–90 |
| H on C next to O (alcohol, ether, ester) | 3.3–4.3 | C=C (alkene or arene) | 100–160 |
| H on C=C (alkene) | 4.5–6.0 | C=O (acid or ester) | 160–185 |
| Aldehyde H, –CHO | 9.0–10.0 | C=O (aldehyde or ketone) | 190–220 |
| Carboxylic acid H, –COOH | 10.0–12.0 | | |

### TMS as the standard

Chemical shifts are measured relative to **tetramethylsilane (TMS)**, Si(CH₃)₄, whose signal is set at
δ = 0. You need to explain why it is suitable:

- All 12 H atoms are equivalent (and all 4 C atoms are equivalent), so TMS gives **one strong, single
  peak**.
- Its peak lies at a **lower δ than almost all organic compounds**, so it does not overlap the sample's
  peaks.
- It is **inert**, so it does not react with the sample.
- It is **volatile**, so it is easily removed from the sample afterwards.

### Solvents for ¹H NMR

Samples are dissolved in a solvent. An ordinary solvent such as CHCl₃ contains ¹H atoms and would give a
large peak that swamps the sample. So ¹H NMR uses a **deuterated solvent**, such as CDCl₃, in which ¹H is
replaced by deuterium (²H), which does not give a signal in a ¹H spectrum. **CCl₄** also works because it
contains no hydrogen at all.

## ¹³C NMR

In a ¹³C spectrum you meet at this level:

- **number of peaks = number of carbon environments**;
- the **δ value** of each peak shows the type of carbon;
- each environment gives a single line, with no splitting.

This is why ¹³C NMR gives **simpler spectra than ¹H NMR**. There is no splitting to interpret, and the
δ range is much wider (about 0–220 ppm, against about 0–12 ppm for ¹H), so peaks rarely overlap.

### Worked example 1: three isomers of C₅H₁₂

Predict the number of peaks in the ¹³C spectrum of each isomer.

```
pentane              CH3–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH3
2-methylbutane       CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3
2,2-dimethylpropane  C(CH3)4
```

- **Pentane** is symmetrical about the middle carbon. The two end CH₃ carbons match, the two CH₂ carbons
  next to them match, and the middle CH₂ is unique: **3 peaks**.
- **2-methylbutane**: the two CH₃ groups on C2 are equivalent. The CH, the CH₂ and the end CH₃ are all
  different: **4 peaks**.
- **2,2-dimethylpropane**: four equivalent CH₃ carbons and one central carbon: **2 peaks**.

¹³C NMR alone separates them.

### Worked example 2: a carbonyl compound, C₄H₈O

Compound A has ¹³C peaks at δ 205, 41 and 16. Suggest a structure.

1. Three peaks, but four carbons, so two carbons must be equivalent.
2. δ 205 is in the 190–220 range: an aldehyde or ketone C=O.
3. δ 41 and 16 are both alkyl carbons.
4. The structure with a C=O and two equivalent carbons is **2-methylpropanal, (CH₃)₂CHCHO**.
   Butanal and butanone would each give four peaks.

Butanal and butanone both give four ¹³C peaks, one above 190. ¹H NMR separates them: only the aldehyde
has a peak at δ 9–10.

## ¹H NMR

A ¹H spectrum gives three pieces of information:

1. **Number of peaks (groups of peaks)** = number of hydrogen environments.
2. **Chemical shift** of each peak = what that hydrogen is near.
3. **Integration**: the relative area under each peak = the relative number of H atoms in that
   environment.

### Integration

An integration trace has step heights proportional to peak areas. Divide each height by the smallest to
get the simplest whole-number ratio, then scale it to the total number of H in the molecular formula.

### Worked example 3: integration and splitting together

Compound B, C₅H₁₀O, has two ¹H peaks: δ 2.4 (quartet) and δ 1.1 (triplet). The integration trace
heights are 32 mm and 48 mm. Deduce the structure.

1. Ratio = 32 : 48 = 1 : 1.5 = **2 : 3**.
2. The formula has 10 H, and 2 + 3 = 5, so multiply by 2: **4 H at δ 2.4 and 6 H at δ 1.1**.
3. A quartet next to a triplet is the pattern of an **ethyl group**, CH₃CH₂–. Having 4 H and 6 H means two
   equivalent ethyl groups.
4. δ 2.4 is in the range for H on a carbon next to C=O.
5. Two equivalent ethyl groups on a C=O: **pentan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃**.

Note that the ratio was 2 : 3, not 4 : 6. Integration only gives the **relative** numbers until you use
the formula.

## Spin–spin splitting: the n+1 rule

The signal for a set of protons is split by **non-equivalent protons on adjacent carbon atoms**.

> A signal is split into **n + 1** lines, where n is the number of H atoms on the adjacent carbon atoms.

| H on adjacent carbons (n) | Lines (n + 1) | Name |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | singlet |
| 1 | 2 | doublet |
| 2 | 3 | triplet |
| 3 | 4 | quartet |

The specification limits this to doublets, triplets and quartets in aliphatic compounds. Three rules:

- Equivalent protons do not split each other. 1,2-dichloroethane, ClCH₂CH₂Cl, gives a **singlet**.
- Splitting tells you about the **neighbours**, not about the group itself. A CH₂ that appears as a
  **triplet** is next to a CH₂. In an ethyl group the CH₂ is the quartet (next to CH₃) and the CH₃ is
  the triplet (next to CH₂).
- In the spectra you meet, an O–H proton normally appears as a singlet.

Useful patterns:

| Pattern | What it means |
|---|---|
| Quartet (2H) + triplet (3H) | CH₃CH₂– (ethyl group) |
| Singlet (3H) | CH₃ attached to a carbon or oxygen with no H, e.g. CH₃CO– or CH₃O– |
| Singlet (9H) | (CH₃)₃C– |
| Doublet (3H) + quartet (1H) | CH₃CH< with no other H neighbours |

### Worked example 4: two esters, C₄H₈O₂

Ethyl ethanoate (CH₃COOCH₂CH₃) and methyl propanoate (CH₃CH₂COOCH₃) both show a singlet (3H), a quartet
(2H) and a triplet (3H). How does ¹H NMR tell them apart?

- In **ethyl ethanoate** the CH₂ is bonded to O, so its quartet is at about **δ 4.1**. The singlet CH₃ is
  next to C=O, at about **δ 2.0**.
- In **methyl propanoate** the CH₃ is bonded to O, so the singlet is at about **δ 3.7**. The quartet CH₂ is
  next to C=O, at about **δ 2.3**.

The splitting patterns match, so the **chemical shift** decides which group is bonded to O.

## Putting the techniques together

### Worked example 5: identifying compound C

Compound C gives these results:

- Mass spectrum: molecular ion at m/z = 72.
- Infrared: strong absorption at 1715 cm⁻¹; no broad absorption for O–H.
- ¹³C NMR: four peaks, at δ 209, 37, 29 and 8.
- ¹H NMR: δ 2.4 (quartet, 2H), δ 2.1 (singlet, 3H), δ 1.1 (triplet, 3H).

Deduce the structure.

1. Mr = 72 fits C₄H₈O (4 × 12.0 + 8 × 1.0 + 16.0 = 72.0).
2. The IR absorption at 1715 cm⁻¹ is C=O. With no O–H, the compound is an aldehyde or ketone.
3. ¹³C: four peaks, so four different carbons. δ 209 is an aldehyde or ketone C=O.
4. ¹H: no peak at δ 9–10, so it is **not an aldehyde**.
5. Quartet (2H) + triplet (3H) = an ethyl group. The singlet (3H) is a CH₃ with no H neighbours.
6. The singlet at δ 2.1 and the quartet at δ 2.4 are both in the range for H next to C=O.
7. Compound C is **butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃**.

## Common errors

- Counting carbon or hydrogen atoms instead of **environments**. Look for symmetry first.
- Reading splitting as "the number of H in this group". The n+1 rule counts H on the **adjacent**
  carbons.
- Writing the integration ratio as the number of H atoms without scaling to the molecular formula.
- Explaining TMS only as "a peak at zero". Give the **single peak**, **away from other peaks**,
  **inert** and **volatile** points.
- Saying deuterated solvents are used because they are inert. They give **no signal** in ¹H NMR.
- Forgetting that a CH₃ next to C=O and a CH₃ next to O are both singlets. Only δ separates them.
