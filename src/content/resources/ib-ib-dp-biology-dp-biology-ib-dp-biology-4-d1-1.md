## Why cells copy DNA (D1.1.1)

Replication produces two DNA molecules whose base sequences are identical to the original. The guide asks you to link this to three needs:

- **Reproduction:** offspring, or a prokaryote's daughter cells, need a complete genome.
- **Growth:** each cell made by mitosis needs every gene, even ones it never expresses.
- **Tissue replacement:** replacement skin, gut and blood cells each need a full copy.

Replication happens before division (S phase in eukaryotes), giving two identical sister chromatids.

## Semi-conservative replication and its evidence (D1.1.2)

In semi-conservative replication each strand of the parent molecule stays intact and acts as a template; each daughter molecule has one parental strand and one new strand. Accuracy comes from **complementary base pairing**. Adenine forms two hydrogen bonds with thymine, and guanine forms three with cytosine. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, so only the correct base fits the width of the helix and makes the right hydrogen bonds at each position.

### The three hypotheses

Before 1958 there were three possible models. Their predictions differ for DNA labelled with a heavy isotope of nitrogen (¹⁵N) and then copied in light nitrogen (¹⁴N):

| Model | What it proposes | DNA after 1 round | DNA after 2 rounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | Parent double helix stays whole; daughter is entirely new | Heavy band and light band | Heavy and light bands, 1 : 3 |
| Semi-conservative | Each daughter has one old and one new strand | One intermediate band | Intermediate and light bands, 1 : 1 |
| Dispersive | Old and new DNA mixed in patches along both strands | One intermediate band | One band between intermediate and light |

### Worked example: interpreting the Meselson–Stahl result

Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl grew *E. coli* for many generations in ¹⁵N, transferred the cells to ¹⁴N, and extracted DNA after each generation. The DNA was centrifuged in a caesium chloride solution, which forms a density gradient, so each DNA molecule settles at the level matching its own density.

1. **After one generation** all DNA formed a single intermediate band. This rules out conservative replication, which predicts separate heavy and light bands.
2. **After two generations** there were two bands of equal intensity, one intermediate and one light. Dispersive replication predicts a single band, so it is ruled out too.
3. Only the semi-conservative model fits both results.

The general rule: after *n* rounds there are 2ⁿ molecules, and the two original strands are always in exactly two hybrid molecules. After 3 rounds, 2 of 8 molecules are intermediate (25 %) and 6 are light.

A strong answer names which observation rules out which model.

## Helicase and DNA polymerase (D1.1.3)

Keep your SL description to two roles:

- **Helicase** moves along the double helix, unwinding it and breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, so that the two strands separate and expose their bases.
- **DNA polymerase** links free nucleotides, each paired with its complementary base on the template, into a new strand by forming covalent bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next.

Do not confuse the bonds: helicase breaks hydrogen bonds between strands; polymerase makes covalent bonds within a strand.

## PCR in more detail (D1.1.4)

The polymerase chain reaction copies one chosen region of DNA. The guide wants three features: primers, temperature changes and Taq polymerase.

### Primers decide what is copied

A primer is a short single strand of DNA, typically around 20 nucleotides, that binds to a complementary sequence at one end of the target. Two primers are used, one for each strand, and they point towards each other. DNA polymerase can only extend an existing strand, so copying starts at a primer and the region between the two primers is what accumulates.

**Worked example.** A target region begins with the sequence 5' GATTCAGCAT 3' on the top strand. Which sequence will a primer that binds to the bottom strand at this end have?

1. The primer must be identical to the top strand here, because it pairs with the bottom strand, which is complementary to the top.
2. So the forward primer reads 5' GATTCAGCAT 3' (shown short for clarity).
3. It binds the bottom strand antiparallel, and polymerase extends it from its 3' end into the target.

### Why the temperatures change

| Step | Typical temperature | Reason for this temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Denaturation | about 95 °C | High enough to break hydrogen bonds so strands separate; covalent bonds of the backbone survive |
| Annealing | about 50 to 60 °C | Cool enough for primers to form hydrogen bonds with their complementary sequences |
| Extension | about 72 °C | Close to the optimum temperature of Taq polymerase |

**Taq polymerase** comes from *Thermus aquaticus*, a bacterium of hot springs. Its enzyme keeps its shape at 95 °C, so it does not need to be replaced after every denaturation step. The number of copies roughly doubles each cycle, but the increase levels off in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.

### What gel electrophoresis separates on

DNA is negatively charged because of its phosphate groups, and the charge per unit length is the same for any fragment. So charge does not separate fragments; size does. The gel is a mesh, and short fragments slip through it faster. A fragment's position compared with a ladder of known sizes gives its length. A dye that binds DNA makes the bands visible, often under ultraviolet light.

## Applications: reading a DNA profile (D1.1.5)

Profiles use regions where people differ in the number of times a short sequence is repeated (short tandem repeats). PCR amplifies each region, and the fragment length depends on how many repeats the person has. Everyone has two copies of each region, one from each parent, so each region gives one band (if both copies are the same length) or two.

### Worked example: a forensic comparison

Fragment sizes in base pairs at three regions:

| Region | Crime scene | Suspect A | Suspect B |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 112, 128 | 112, 128 | 112, 140 |
| 2 | 96 | 96 | 96, 104 |
| 3 | 201, 213 | 201, 213 | 201, 213 |

1. Suspect B differs at regions 1 and 2, so B is excluded as the source. One mismatch is enough to exclude.
2. Suspect A matches at all three regions. This is consistent with A being the source, but it is not proof, because unrelated people can match at a few regions; each extra region tested makes that less likely.
3. Region 2 shows one band for the scene sample: the person inherited fragments of the same length from both parents.

In a **paternity** test, every band in the child must come from the mother or the father. Identify the child's bands shared with the mother first; any band left over must be present in the father.

## HL only: one replication fork, step by step (D1.1.6 to D1.1.9)

**Directionality.** Each strand has a 5' end (phosphate on carbon 5' of the deoxyribose) and a 3' end (hydroxyl on carbon 3'). DNA polymerase joins the 5' phosphate of an incoming nucleotide to the 3' hydroxyl of the growing strand. New DNA is therefore only made 5' to 3', and the template is read 3' to 5'.

**Leading and lagging strands.** Because the parent strands are antiparallel, at any fork one template runs 3' to 5' towards the fork and the other runs the opposite way.

**Worked example.** A fork moves to the right. The upper template strand runs 3' (left) to 5' (right); the lower one runs 5' to 3'.

1. A new strand on the upper template must run 5' to 3' from left to right, the same direction the fork moves. It can be extended continuously as the fork opens: the **leading strand**, needing one RNA primer.
2. A new strand on the lower template must run right to left, away from the fork. It is made in short sections as each new length of template is exposed: the **lagging strand**, made discontinuously as **Okazaki fragments**, each starting with its own primer.

**The enzyme sequence on the lagging strand (prokaryotes).**

1. **Helicase** unwinds the helix at the fork.
2. **DNA primase** makes a short RNA primer on the newly exposed template.
3. **DNA polymerase III** extends the primer with DNA nucleotides, 5' to 3', until it reaches the previous fragment.
4. **DNA polymerase I** removes the RNA primer nucleotides of that previous fragment and replaces them with DNA.
5. **DNA ligase** makes the final covalent bond in the sugar-phosphate backbone, sealing the nick between fragments.

**Proofreading.** If DNA polymerase III adds a nucleotide whose base does not pair correctly with the template, it removes that nucleotide from the 3' terminal and adds a correctly paired one before continuing. The 3' end is the only place the polymerase is working.

## Common mistakes

- Stating that Meselson and Stahl's first-generation result proved semi-conservative replication. It only ruled out the conservative model; the second generation was needed to rule out dispersive replication.
- Saying gel electrophoresis separates by charge. All fragments have the same charge per unit length.
- Writing that heat "breaks the DNA" in PCR. Only hydrogen bonds break at 95 °C.
- Claiming a matching profile proves guilt.
- (HL) Saying the lagging strand is made 3' to 5'. Both new strands are made 5' to 3'.
- (HL) Giving ligase the job of removing primers. That is DNA polymerase I.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** an enzyme's role: one or two sentences naming the bond it acts on.
- **Evaluate** a profile or hypothesis: what the data support, rule out and cannot show.
- **Deduce** a strand or primer: label 5' and 3' on every sequence.
- **Calculate** copies or fractions: show 2ⁿ before any percentage.
