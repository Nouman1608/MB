## Questions

**1.** Describe how dideoxynucleotides are used to find the base sequence of a DNA template in Sanger sequencing. **[4]**

**2.** In a Sanger reaction the terminal bases of the fragments, from shortest to longest, are T, C, A, G, G, C, A, T, T, C, A, G.

**(a)** State the sequence of the new strand, 5′ to 3′. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the template sequence, written 5′ to 3′. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the shortest fragment reaches the detector first. **[1]**

**3.** Aligned 24-base stretches of one gene from three species, A, B and C, are:

```
A  ATGAAGCTGGACTTCGGTCCAATG
B  ATGAAGCTGGATTTCGGTCCAATA
C  ATGAGGCTAGATTTCGATCCGATA
```

**(a)** Calculate the percentage identity for A and B, A and C, and B and C. **[3]**
**(b)** State which two species are likely to share the most recent common ancestor. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why a comparison of one gene gives limited evidence of evolutionary relationships. **[1]**

**4.** The template strand of a gene reads 3′ TACCTTGGCAAAATT 5′. Codons: AUG Met; GAA Glu; GAC Asp; CCG Pro; CCA Pro; UUU Phe; UAA stop.

**(a)** Give the mRNA sequence and the amino acids it codes for. **[2]**
**(b)** A mutation changes the second codon to GAC. State the effect on the polypeptide. **[1]**
**(c)** A different mutation changes the third codon to CCA. State and explain the effect on the polypeptide. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a predicted amino acid sequence may not show the structure of the working protein. **[1]**

**5.** Sequences of a bacterium were obtained from four patients. The table shows the number of base differences between isolates.

| | P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 38 |
| P2 | | 0 | 2 | 39 |
| P3 | | | 0 | 38 |

**(a)** Identify the patients most likely to be part of one chain of infection and justify your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how patient P4 was infected. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how the sequences could also help a health authority respond to the outbreak. **[1]**

**6.** **(a)** Explain two advantages of high throughput sequencing over Sanger sequencing for sequencing a whole genome. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how sequencing has made the development of synthetic biology possible. **[3]**

**7.** DNA from a crime scene and from two suspects is profiled. The table shows repeat numbers (a single entry means one allele detected).

| Locus | Scene | Suspect A | Suspect B |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | 11, 14 | 11, 14 | 11, 14 |
| L2 | 8 | 8 | 8, 9 |
| L3 | 15, 17 | 15, 17 | 15, 17 |
| L4 | 9, 12 | 9, 12 | 9, 12 |
| L5 | 6, 7 | 6, 7 | 6, 7 |

**(a)** State which suspect can be excluded and explain why. **[2]**
**(b)** The frequencies of the matching pattern at the five loci are 0.07, 0.11, 0.09, 0.16 and 0.12. Calculate the probability that a random person matches at all five loci, and give it as 1 in n to the nearest thousand. **[3]**
**(c)** Give two reasons why a match is not proof that suspect A left the sample. **[2]**

**8.** A forensic sample contains 6 copies of a target sequence. Assume copies double every cycle.

**(a)** Calculate the copies after 22 cycles. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the smallest number of cycles giving more than 1 × 10⁸ copies. Show that one fewer cycle is not enough. **[3]**
**(c)** Each cycle takes 140 seconds. Calculate the time for your answer to (b), in minutes. **[1]**
**(d)** Describe the effect on the reaction of setting the annealing temperature too high. **[1]**

**9.** A ladder of DNA fragments ran as follows: 1200 bp 16 mm; 800 bp 23 mm; 600 bp 28 mm; 400 bp 35 mm; 200 bp 47 mm. An unknown fragment ran 31 mm.

**(a)** Describe how to use the ladder to estimate the size of the unknown fragment. **[3]**
**(b)** The best-fit line gives log₁₀(size) = 3.48 − 0.0251 × distance (distance in mm). Calculate the size of the unknown fragment to the nearest 10 bp. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why shorter fragments travel further. **[2]**

**10.** Protein standards ran in SDS-PAGE: 120 kDa at 12 mm, 80 kDa at 19 mm, 50 kDa at 27 mm, 30 kDa at 38 mm, 20 kDa at 48 mm. A protein ran 33 mm.

**(a)** Explain the role of SDS. **[2]**
**(b)** Estimate the mass of the protein, to the nearest 5 kDa. **[2]**
**(c)** State what would determine separation if SDS were left out. **[1]**

**11.** A late-onset disorder is linked to the number of 3-base repeats at one locus. PCR with primers either side of the repeat gives a product of 96 bp plus 3 bp per repeat. A repeat count of 40 or more on one allele is classed as high risk (data supplied). A patient's products are 150 bp and 237 bp.

**(a)** Calculate the number of repeats on each allele. **[2]**
**(b)** State the patient's risk category. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe how PCR and electrophoresis give these sizes. **[4]**
**(d)** Discuss one ethical issue raised by predictive testing for the disorder. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The mixture has template, primer, DNA polymerase, ordinary nucleotides and four dideoxynucleotides with different fluorescent labels [1]. A dideoxynucleotide has no 3′ hydroxyl group, so no phosphodiester bond can form with the next nucleotide and the strand stops [1]. Termination is random, so strands of every length are made, each ending in a labelled base [1]. Fragments are separated by length and the labels read from shortest to longest give the sequence [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say why the chain stops: the missing 3′ hydroxyl group means the next bond cannot form. "Modified" alone is vague.

**2. (a)** TCAGGCATTCAG [1].
**(b)** The template is complementary and antiparallel: 3′ AGTCCGTAAGTC 5′ [1], so 5′ to 3′ it is CTGAATGCCTGA [1].
**(c)** Shorter fragments move through the capillary gel faster [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State which end you are writing from. A complement left unreversed is an easy loss.

**3. (a)** A and B differ at 2 bases: 22/24 = 91.7% [1]. A and C differ at 6: 18/24 = 75.0% [1]. B and C differ at 4: 20/24 = 83.3% [1].
**(b)** A and B, because they are most similar [1].
**(c)** Genes change at different rates, so one gene may not represent the genome [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the count of differences before each percentage, so that your method is visible.

**4. (a)** mRNA 5′ AUGGAACCGUUUUAA 3′ [1]; Met, Glu, Pro, Phe, then stop [1].
**(b)** Glu becomes Asp, so the polypeptide has a different amino acid [1].
**(c)** CCA still codes for Pro, so there is no change [1], because the genetic code is degenerate (several codons can specify one amino acid) [1].
**(d)** The sequence does not show folding, or changes made after translation [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link "no change" to the degenerate code, not only state it, because the explanation is what answers "explain".

**5. (a)** P1, P2 and P3 [1], because they differ by only 1 or 2 bases, so they probably share a recent source [1].
**(b)** P4 differs by 38 or 39 bases, so it probably came from a different source [1].
**(c)** Sequences show whether resistance alleles are spreading, so treatment can be chosen [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from the table. "Closely related" with no figures is weaker than "1 to 2 differences".

**6. (a)** Millions of reactions run together, so a genome is done much faster [1]. The cost per base is lower [1].
**(b)** Known sequences let designers plan new DNA [1], which can be made chemically [1] and checked by sequencing [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two distinct advantages. Repeating "faster" in two ways makes one point.

**7. (a)** Suspect B [1], because at L2 B has a second allele (9) that is not in the scene sample [1].
**(b)** 0.07 × 0.11 × 0.09 × 0.16 × 0.12 [1] = 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ [1], about 1 in 75 000 [1].
**(c)** Any two: contamination or a mixed sample; a degraded, incomplete profile; a relative with a similar pattern [1] [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* One mismatch is enough to exclude. For the probability, show the product before the "1 in n" statement.

**8. (a)** 6 × 2²² [1] = 2.52 × 10⁷ (25 165 824) [1].
**(b)** 6 × 2²⁴ = 1.01 × 10⁸, more than 1 × 10⁸, so 24 cycles [1]. For 23 cycles, 6 × 2²³ = 5.03 × 10⁷ [1], below 1 × 10⁸ [1].
**(c)** 24 × 140 = 3360 s = 56 minutes [1].
**(d)** Primers cannot bind, so little or no product forms [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Test the cycle number below your answer as well, and compare it with 1 × 10⁸.

**9. (a)** Plot distance against log₁₀(fragment size) for the ladder [1]. Draw a best-fit straight line [1]. Read log₁₀(size) at 31 mm and take the antilog to find the size [1].
**(b)** log₁₀(size) = 3.48 − 0.0251 × 31 = 2.70, so the size is about 500 bp [1].
**(c)** DNA is negatively charged and moves through a gel mesh [1]. Shorter fragments pass through the pores more easily, so they move further in the same time [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the line and the reading you used, as a value read from a graph is only approximate.

**10. (a)** SDS unfolds (denatures) the protein [1] and coats it with negative charge in proportion to its length, so separation depends on mass [1].
**(b)** Plot distance against log₁₀(mass), draw a best-fit line [1] and read 33 mm: about 40 kDa [1].
**(c)** Charge, size and shape [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Gives the proteins a charge" is incomplete. Say the charge is negative and proportional to length.

**11. (a)** (150 − 96) ÷ 3 = 18 [1]; (237 − 96) ÷ 3 = 47 [1].
**(b)** High risk, because one allele has 47 repeats, which is 40 or more [1].
**(c)** PCR amplifies the region across the repeat using primers on either side, heat-stable polymerase and repeated denaturation, annealing and extension [1]. The products are loaded into gel wells beside a ladder [1]. Under current, negatively charged DNA moves towards the anode, shorter fragments travelling further [1]. Band positions are compared with the ladder to find sizes [1].
**(d)** Any one issue, developed: state the issue, such as insurers or employers using the result, anxiety about a disorder with no certain onset date, or relatives who share alleles learning of their own risk without asking [1]; give a consequence for the person [1]; say who is affected or how the harm or benefit could be reduced [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* For a discuss command, state the issue, then its consequence, then who is affected.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Reading the template from the detector, or not reversing the complement to write it 5′ to 3′.
* Saying a dideoxynucleotide "stops DNA polymerase", without the missing 3′ hydroxyl group.
* Giving a match probability as a decimal when "1 in n" is asked for.
* Forgetting that negative charge moves DNA, and that SDS gives proteins that charge.
