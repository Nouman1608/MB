This guide covers **9.4 Sustainable management in hazardous environments** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus wording is a case study: candidates must study **some of the problems of sustainable management of a hazardous environment and evaluate attempted or possible solutions**. It is assessed in Paper 3, where the syllabus describes the management element of each option as synoptic, combining physical and human factors.

The [Topic 9 hazardous environments study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-hazardous-environments/) uses coastal Bangladesh as its case study. This page sets out the ideas you need to evaluate any hazardous environment and gives two further case studies, a volcanic island and a tsunami coast, which you can use alongside or instead of your class example. ## What makes a hazardous environment hard to manage sustainably

A hazardous environment is a place where people live with a recurring threat: an active volcano, a seismic zone, a cyclone coast, or a landslide-prone hillside. Sustainable management aims to let people continue to live and earn a living there **while reducing risk now and for future generations**, without simply moving the risk elsewhere or creating new environmental damage.

Typical problems:

- **The hazard cannot be removed**, only its effects reduced.
- **Uncertainty:** the timing and size of the next event are unknown, so it is hard to justify spending.
- **Competing needs:** fertile soils, ports, tourism and cheap land attract people to risky places.
- **Unequal vulnerability:** the poorest often live in the most exposed locations.
- **Cost and dependence:** engineering and monitoring may need outside funding.
- **False security:** defences can encourage development behind them.
- **Changing risk:** population growth, urbanisation and climate change can raise exposure faster than protection improves.

## Frameworks for evaluating management

**The disaster management cycle.** Management is not a single response but a cycle:

1. **Mitigation:** reducing the hazard's effects before it happens (building codes, land-use zoning, defences).
2. **Preparedness:** plans, warning systems, drills, stockpiles.
3. **Response:** rescue, shelter, medical care in the hours and days after.
4. **Recovery:** rebuilding, ideally "building back better" to reduce future risk.

A sustainable system invests most in mitigation and preparedness, because they reduce the cost of response and recovery.

**Modify the event, modify vulnerability, modify the loss.** Approaches can be grouped as attempts to change the hazard itself (for example diverting lava or building sea walls), to change people's vulnerability (education, warnings, land-use planning, stronger buildings), or to change how losses are shared (insurance and aid).

**International frameworks.** The **Hyogo Framework for Action (2005-2015)** and its successor, the **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015-2030)**, shifted emphasis from responding to disasters towards reducing risk. Sendai's four priorities are: understanding disaster risk; strengthening disaster risk governance; investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience; and enhancing preparedness for effective response and to "build back better" in recovery.

**Four tests for any solution:** Is it **effective** (does it reduce deaths and losses)? Is it **affordable** over time? Is it **fair** to different groups? Is it **resilient** to larger events and to change?

## Case study 1: Montserrat, a volcanic island

Montserrat is a small British Overseas Territory in the Caribbean. The **Soufrière Hills volcano**, dormant for centuries, began erupting in July 1995.

**Problems.**

- The volcano produced dome collapses and pyroclastic flows that buried much of the south of the island, including the capital, **Plymouth**, which was abandoned and later buried.
- On 25 June 1997, pyroclastic flows killed 19 people, most of whom had returned to farm inside the evacuated zone.
- The airport and the port were in the danger zone and were lost.
- The population fell sharply as many people left for other Caribbean islands and the UK, reducing the island's workforce and tax base.
- The south, which held most of the best farmland and infrastructure, was closed as an exclusion zone.

**Attempted solutions and evaluation.**

| Solution | What was done | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring | The Montserrat Volcano Observatory was set up to monitor the volcano continuously | Gives the scientific basis for zoning; allows warnings; ongoing cost |
| Hazard zoning | The island was divided into zones with controlled access; the south became an exclusion zone | Saved lives once enforced; the 1997 deaths happened when people re-entered |
| Resettlement | Development moved to the safer north, with a new town centre planned around Little Bay and Brades | Gives the island a future, but on less land and at high cost |
| External aid | The UK funded relocation, housing and infrastructure | Essential but creates dependence |

**Is it sustainable?** Lives have been protected since 1997 by accepting the loss of land, but the economy and population remain smaller, and recovery depends on external funding. It shows that sometimes the most sustainable option is **retreat** from the most dangerous zone rather than defence.

## Case study 2: Japan's Sanriku coast and the 2011 tsunami

The north-east coast of Honshu faces the Japan Trench subduction zone and has a long history of tsunami. Japan invested heavily in **sea walls**, warning systems, evacuation routes and drills.

**What happened on 11 March 2011.** The Tohoku earthquake generated a tsunami larger than many defences had been designed for. In the town of Taro (now part of Miyako), a sea wall about 10 m high, among the largest in Japan, was overtopped and partly destroyed.

**Evaluation.**

- **Hard engineering:** sea walls reduce smaller, more frequent tsunami but can be overtopped by rare large ones, and they may encourage people to feel safe and to evacuate later.
- **Warnings and education:** the warning system issued alerts within minutes, and many people survived by moving quickly to high ground. In some schools, practised evacuation saved almost all pupils, showing the value of drills.
- **Rebuilding:** reconstruction has combined higher sea walls with raising land, moving housing to higher ground and planting coastal forests as buffers. This is a **multiple-lines-of-defence** approach rather than relying on one wall.

**Is it sustainable?** Japan's approach is effective for frequent events and saved many lives in 2011, but the cost is very high, the loss of sea views and access divides communities, and no defence can be sized for every possible event.

## Comparing the case studies

| Issue | Montserrat | Sanriku coast |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard | Volcanic: pyroclastic flows, ash | Tsunami from subduction earthquakes |
| Main approach | Retreat and zoning | Defence, warnings, then retreat to higher ground |
| Funding | Mainly external aid | National government |
| Lesson | Zoning works only if enforced | Engineering needs to be combined with evacuation |

## Worked paragraph

*"Management on Montserrat has been successful in protecting life, because the 1997 deaths occurred among people who had re-entered the evacuated zone, and enforcing the exclusion zone removed that exposure [evidence]. However, it has not been sustainable economically, because the loss of Plymouth, the airport and the southern farmland left a smaller population dependent on aid [evaluation]. This suggests that in a small island, where retreat means losing most usable land, sustainability depends on outside support as much as on local management [judgement]."*

## Common mistakes

- Describing the hazard event instead of the management of the environment.
- Judging solutions by deaths alone; economic and environmental tests count too.
- Assuming engineering is always the best answer, or always the worst.
- Forgetting that people choose to stay, and why.
- Omitting dates, places and named schemes.

## Exam technique

**Evaluate** questions need you to weigh success against problems and reach a supported judgement. Structure the answer around the problems first, then each solution with evidence, then a conclusion that answers "sustainable for whom and over what timescale".
