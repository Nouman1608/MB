This guide covers the **letter**, one of the five forms named for **Section A, Directed Writing**, of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2. The syllabus says candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, **letter** or article", writing "about 250–350 words". The [directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/) sets out the use-develop-evaluate method and includes a model letter paragraph to a council. This page goes deeper into the form itself: who letters are written to, how to open and close them, how to sound firm without being rude, how a letter to a newspaper differs from one to an official, and how to plan the whole thing.

## Who the letter is to

The recipient decides almost everything about register (**W4**, "use register appropriate to context"). The usual recipients in directed writing are:

| Recipient | Relationship | Register |
|---|---|---|
| A council, company or organisation | you are a member of the public or a customer | formal, courteous, precise |
| A head teacher or principal | you are a student or parent | formal, respectful, constructive |
| A newspaper editor | you are a reader responding to an article or issue | formal but more openly persuasive, because other readers will see it |
| A relative or friend you are advising | you know them well | semi-formal or informal, warm, but still organised and accurate |

Read the task for the recipient's role and for your own. "You are a parent of a student at the school" changes what you can claim and how you sound.

## Openings and closings

**Salutation and sign-off.** The usual British conventions are:

| You address | Salutation | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|
| a named person | "Dear Mr Hussain", "Dear Ms Lee", "Dear Dr Khan" | "Yours sincerely" |
| an unnamed person or role | "Dear Sir or Madam", "Dear Editor" | "Yours faithfully" |
| a friend or relative | "Dear Sana" | "Best wishes", "With love" |

The syllabus does not mention addresses or dates; your marks come from the content and the writing, so keep the time for those.

**The opening paragraph** should tell the reader, in two or three sentences, who you are, what you are writing about and what you think. For example: *"I am writing as a regular passenger on the number 14 bus to object to the proposal to end the evening service after 8 p.m."*

**The closing paragraph** should state what you want the reader to do, and why it is reasonable. For example: *"I would therefore ask the company to delay its decision until passengers have been consulted."* Then thank them briefly, and sign off.

## Polite firmness

A letter that complains or objects must stay courteous: rudeness weakens an argument and loses register marks. Firmness comes from evidence and clear requests, not from insults.

| Too weak | Too aggressive | Polite and firm |
|---|---|---|
| "Maybe you could think about it?" | "This is a ridiculous plan." | "I would urge you to reconsider." |
| "Sorry to bother you, but..." | "You obviously don't care about us." | "I am concerned that the proposal overlooks..." |
| "It might be a bit unfair." | "Everyone knows you're only after money." | "The proposal appears to put savings ahead of passengers' safety." |

Useful phrases: "I would be grateful if...", "I would ask you to consider...", "It is difficult to see how...", "I hope you will agree that...".

## Letters to an editor

A letter to a newspaper is addressed to the editor but written for the paper's readers. That allows a more openly persuasive style:

- refer to the article or event that prompted you ("Your report last week on...");
- state your view in the first paragraph;
- appeal to readers' shared interests ("Anyone who has waited at a dark bus stop...");
- end with a clear point that readers will remember, or a call to the community.

It is still formal: no slang, no personal attacks on the article's writer.

## Building the body from the sources

Each body paragraph should contain one main point, with the source material **used**, **developed** or **evaluated**:

1. A topic sentence stating the point.
2. The source detail, in your own words.
3. Development: a consequence, example or application to the recipient.
4. Evaluation where relevant: is the claim supported, biased or incomplete?
5. A link back to your request.

A letter usually needs three or four body paragraphs within about 250–350 words. A paragraph answering the strongest opposing view makes the letter more convincing to an official, who will have heard that view too.

## Worked example

Sources (original, shortened):

> **Text 1, from a bus company notice:** From next month, the number 14 service will end at 8 p.m.
> instead of 11 p.m. Few passengers use the late buses, and the change will allow us to run more
> frequent services at peak times, when buses are often full.
>
> **Text 2, from a nurse's post on a community website:** I finish my hospital shift at 9.30 p.m. The
> late number 14 is my only way home. Several of my colleagues, and students at the evening college,
> are in the same position. A taxi costs more than I earn in an hour.

**Task:** *Write a letter to the manager of the bus company, giving your views on the change.*

**Model opening:**

> Dear Sir or Madam,
>
> I am writing as a regular passenger on the number 14 route to express my concern about the plan to
> end the service at 8 p.m. While I understand the need to improve peak-time services, I believe this
> change would cause serious hardship to people who depend on the late buses.

**Model body paragraph:**

> Your notice states that few passengers use the late buses. However, the people who do use them are
> often those with no alternative. Hospital staff finishing evening shifts, and students at the evening
> college, cannot simply leave early, and for many of them a taxi would cost more than they earn in an
> hour. A service with fewer passengers may therefore be more essential, not less. It would also help
> to know how "few" is defined: has the company published passenger figures for these journeys?

**Model close:**

> I would therefore ask the company to keep at least one late service, perhaps at 10 p.m., and to
> consult passengers before making a final decision. Thank you for considering my views.
>
> Yours faithfully,
>
> A. Rahman

**Commentary.** The opening states who is writing, the issue and the view. The body paragraph **uses** Text 1's claim, **evaluates** it (vague evidence; who the passengers are) and **develops** it with Text 2's details applied to the recipient's decision. The close makes a specific, reasonable request (one late bus) that neither source suggested. "Dear Sir or Madam" and "Yours faithfully" match an unnamed recipient. The tone is firm and courteous throughout.

## Common mistakes

- **Mismatched sign-off**: "Yours sincerely" after "Dear Sir or Madam".
- **No purpose** in the first paragraph, so the reader does not know what the letter is about until halfway through.
- **Rudeness or sarcasm** towards the recipient.
- **Vague requests** such as "please do something".
- **Writing to the wrong person**: addressing the newspaper's readers when the task says the head teacher.
- **Retelling the sources** paragraph by paragraph instead of building an argument.
- **Informal phrasing** in a formal letter ("loads of", "a bit rubbish").

## Exam technique

Underline the recipient, your role and the purpose in the task. Decide the salutation and sign-off before you write. In planning, write your request first, then choose the three or four source points that best support it. Aim for about 300 words, and check that every paragraph moves towards the request. Leave a few minutes to check spelling, punctuation and grammar (W5).
