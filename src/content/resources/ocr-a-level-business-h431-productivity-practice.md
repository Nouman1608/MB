## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by productivity. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between productivity and production. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** way in which training could improve a business's productivity. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Ashgrove Bakery makes 3,000 loaves a day with 12 staff, each paid £96 a day, and has capital employed of £200,000. It buys a new oven, which raises capital employed to £260,000 and output to 3,600 loaves a day with the same 12 staff.

**(a)** Calculate labour productivity before and after the new oven. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate labour cost per loaf before and after, to the nearest tenth of a penny. **[3]**

**(d)** Calculate capital productivity before and after, to three significant figures. **[2]**

**(e)** The oven cost £60,000. The extra 600 loaves a day are sold for 300 days a year with a contribution of £0.30 a loaf. Calculate the payback period, to two decimal places. **[3]**

**(f)** Staff training is an alternative: it costs £20,000 and raises output by 5%. Evaluate whether Ashgrove should buy the oven or train staff. **[8]**

**5.** Hallam Garments employs 60 people and makes 7,200 garments a month. A competitor makes 150 garments per worker per month. Hallam's managers say that morale is low, machines are old and the layout of the factory causes delays. Recommend and justify methods to improve Hallam's productivity. **[9]**

**6.** A call centre owner says that raising productivity always benefits everyone. Evaluate the impact of a productivity drive on the call centre's stakeholders. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** How efficiently a business turns inputs into outputs [1]; output compared with the resources used, such as output per employee or per hour [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Production is the total amount a business makes [1]. Productivity compares that output with the inputs used [1]. A business can raise production by hiring more staff without raising productivity [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Training gives staff the skills to do tasks faster [1] and with fewer errors [1], so more output is made per employee in the same time, raising output per worker [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Before: 3,000 ÷ 12 = **250 loaves** per member of staff. After: 3,600 ÷ 12 = **300 loaves** [1 + 1]. **[2]**

**(b)** (300 ÷ 250 − 1) × 100 [1] = **20%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Labour cost = 12 × 96 = £1,152 a day [1]. Before: 1,152 ÷ 3,000 = **£0.384** (38.4p) [1]. After: 1,152 ÷ 3,600 = **£0.320** (32.0p) [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** Before: 3,000 ÷ 200,000 = **0.0150** [1]. After: 3,600 ÷ 260,000 = **0.0138** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** Extra loaves a year = 600 × 300 = 180,000 [1]; extra contribution = 180,000 × 0.30 = £54,000 [1]; payback = 60,000 ÷ 54,000 = **1.11 years** [1]. **[3]**

**(f)** Oven: labour productivity rises 20% and labour cost per loaf falls by 6.4p [1]; payback is 1.11 years, with extra contribution of £54,000 a year [1]; but capital productivity falls (0.0150 to 0.0138), so each pound of capital makes less [1].
Training: 5% of 3,000 is 150 loaves a day, or 45,000 a year, worth £13,500 of contribution [1]; payback = 20,000 ÷ 13,500 = 1.48 years [1]; it requires no new capital and the benefit stays only while staff stay [1].
Judgement: the oven gives the larger and faster return if the extra 600 loaves a day can be sold, which needs demand evidence [1]; if sales are limited, training is the lower-risk choice, and the two could be combined [1]. **[8]**

**5.** Diagnosis: Hallam's productivity is 7,200 ÷ 60 = 120 garments per worker, 20% below the competitor's 150 (30 ÷ 150) [1].
Morale: low morale reduces effort, so improve it with team working, better communication and recognition, and possibly an incentive scheme, but compare any recurring cost of a bonus with its yearly gain [2]. Machines: replacing old machines would raise output per worker but at high cost, so appraise the payback, and check that the extra output can be sold [2]. Layout: reorganising the factory to cut delays (lean methods) is relatively cheap and may produce quick gains [1].
Staff: automation may threaten jobs, so consultation and retraining are needed to avoid resistance [1].
Priority: start with layout and morale, which are cheap, then invest in machines if orders justify it [1]. Reaching 150 garments per worker would raise output to 9,000 a month (60 × 150), a rise of 25% [1]. **[9]**

**6.** Benefits: lower cost per call and higher profit for owners [1]; the business can offer lower prices or better service to clients [1]; employees may gain higher pay and security if the business competes well [1].
Costs: staff may face pressure to take more calls and have less time for each customer, so stress and absence may rise [1]; quality may fall if speed is rewarded over customer satisfaction [1].
Judgement: the owner is wrong to say that all stakeholders benefit; productivity gains help owners and clients most, and employees only if the drive is managed fairly and quality is also measured [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total output instead of output per employee.
- Forgetting that a recurring cost needs a yearly comparison with the benefit.
- Ignoring capital productivity when machinery is added.
- Treating higher productivity as good for all stakeholders.
