## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene expression | The process by which gene information has effects on phenotype |
| Promoter | DNA sequence next to a gene where RNA polymerase attaches |
| Enhancer | DNA sequence where regulatory proteins bind to raise transcription of a gene |
| Transcription factor | Protein that binds a specific base sequence and helps or blocks transcription |
| Nuclease | Enzyme that breaks down mRNA |
| Epigenesis | Process by which cells of a multicellular organism develop different patterns of differentiation |
| Genome | All the DNA of a cell |
| Transcriptome | All the mRNA in a cell at a given time |
| Proteome | All the proteins in a cell |
| Epigenetic tag | Chemical group (such as a methyl group) on DNA or histones that changes expression without changing the base sequence |
| Monozygotic twins | Twins from one zygote, with the same genome |

## The pathway in one line

Gene → **transcription** → mRNA → **translation** → polypeptide → **protein function** (for example an enzyme) → phenotype. Control can act at several points: whether transcription starts, how long mRNA lasts, and how the protein works.

## Control points at a glance

| Level | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Transcription | Transcription factors at promoters and enhancers | Raise or lower the rate |
| Transcription | Methylated cytosine in a promoter | Represses; gene downstream silenced |
| Transcription | Methylated amino acids in histones | Repress or activate, depending on site |
| After transcription | Nucleases degrade mRNA | Shorter mRNA life, less translation |

## Genome, transcriptome, proteome

- Cells of one organism share the same genome.
- No cell expresses all its genes.
- The pattern of expression decides the cell type.
- So the **transcriptome and proteome differ** between cells even though the genome is the same.
- The proteome does not exactly mirror the transcriptome: mRNA lifetime and translation also matter.

## Epigenetic inheritance in steps

1. A tag (DNA methylation or histone modification) is in place on a gene.
2. The tag **stays in place during mitosis**, so daughter cells show the same expression pattern.
3. If tags stay in place through **meiosis**, offspring could show the changed phenotype.
4. The base sequence never changed, so genotype is unaltered.

## Tigons and ligers in steps

1. Most epigenetic tags are removed from ovum and sperm, resetting the zygote.
2. Not all are removed, so some tags carry information about the parent of origin.
3. Retained tags on growth-related genes differ between lion and tiger.
4. Lion father and tiger mother give ligers; tiger father and lion mother give tigons.
5. The same genes are expressed differently, giving different phenotypes.

State it as an outline, with careful wording ("it is proposed that").

## Method boxes

**Half-life from mRNA data**
1. Find the time for the amount to halve.
2. Check against a second pair of values.
3. For the fraction left after time t: (1/2) raised to the power t / half-life.

**Percentage change**
(new − old) / old × 100. Quote it with a unit, such as %.

**Fold change**
new / old. State which is divided by which.

**Interpreting a methylation or twin data set**
1. Quote data for both groups with units.
2. Calculate the difference or ratio.
3. Link the trend to the biology (more methylation, less expression).
4. Add one limitation: correlation is not cause, group sizes, confounding variables.

## Small worked reminders

**Half-life.** mRNA falls from 200 to 100 units in 4 h. After 12 h (three half-lives) it is 200 × (1/2)³ = **25 units**.

**Genes expressed.** A cell transcribes 7 500 of 20 000 genes (data supplied): 7 500 / 20 000 × 100 = **37.5%**.

**Fold change.** Gene activity of 18 with a drug and 90 without: the drug lowers it by (90 − 18) / 90 × 100 = **80%**, or 5-fold.

## Twin studies

- Monozygotic twins share a genome, so differences in expression come from the environment.
- Older pairs, and pairs who spent more years apart, may show larger differences in methylation.
- Check whether the data follow the same twins over time or compare different groups.

## Hormone and bacterial examples in steps

**Steroid hormone (one hormone example)**
1. The hormone passes through the cell-surface membrane, because it is lipid-soluble.
2. It binds a specific receptor protein.
3. The hormone-receptor complex enters the nucleus.
4. It binds a specific base sequence and acts as a transcription factor.
5. Transcription of the target genes rises, so more of the protein product is made.

Only cells with the receptor respond. This is why a hormone reaches every cell but changes only some.

**Lactose in bacteria (one biochemical example)**
1. With no lactose, the repressor sits on the operator and RNA polymerase is blocked.
2. Lactose arrives and a lactose-derived molecule binds the repressor.
3. The repressor changes shape and leaves the operator.
4. RNA polymerase transcribes the genes and the enzymes for lactose use appear.

**Tryptophan in bacteria (the alternative biochemical example)**
1. Without tryptophan, the repressor cannot bind and the genes are transcribed.
2. With tryptophan, the repressor binds the amino acid and now attaches to the operator.
3. Transcription stops, so the cell stops making what is already supplied.

Learn one clearly rather than three vaguely. The guide asks for one hormone and one biochemical.

## Data interpretation reminders

**Reporter-gene data with methylation.** If activity falls when the promoter is methylated and rises when a methyl-removing drug is added, the intervention supports a causal role for methylation. If the drug restores only part of the activity, say that some methyl groups probably remain, or that other factors are involved.

**Cells with and without a receptor.** If cells lacking the receptor show no response, the receptor is needed. If cells with the receptor respond only when the hormone is present, the hormone is needed too.

**Mutant repressor data.** A strain with high activity whether or not the inducer is present has lost control. The repressor no longer blocks the operator.

**Twin data.** Differences that rise with age fit the idea that environments add up over time. A cross-sectional study (different pairs at each age) cannot track change within one pair.

**Air pollution data.** A higher methylation level in a polluted district fits environmental alteration of tags, but check age, smoking and sample size before claiming cause.

## Quick self-test

1. State the three common stages of gene expression.
2. Name the enzymes that degrade mRNA.
3. What is the effect of methylating cytosine in a promoter?
4. mRNA starts at 160 units with half-life 2 h. How much is left after 6 h?
5. A cell transcribes 5 000 of 20 000 genes. What percentage is that?
6. Why do the transcriptomes of two cell types differ when the genome is the same?
7. Why can histone methylation not be called always repressive?
8. Why are monozygotic twins useful for studying the environment?
9. A gene's activity is 40 without a drug and 10 with it. Give the percentage decrease.
10. What does a lactose-derived molecule do to the repressor in *E. coli*?
11. Why are tigons and ligers different in phenotype?
12. State what stays unchanged in epigenesis.

### Answers

1. Transcription, translation, function of the protein product.
2. Nucleases.
3. It represses transcription, so the gene downstream is not expressed.
4. Three half-lives: 160 × 1/8 = **20 units**.
5. 5 000 / 20 000 × 100 = **25%**.
6. Different genes are transcribed in each cell type.
7. It can repress or activate, depending on the amino acid and site.
8. They share a genome, so differences in expression come from the environment.
9. (40 − 10) / 40 × 100 = **75%**.
10. It binds the repressor, which releases the operator, so the genes are transcribed.
11. Retained epigenetic tags differ, so gene expression differs depending on the parent species.
12. The DNA base sequence (genotype).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying epigenetic changes are mutations or alter the DNA sequence.
- Saying all tags are erased in gametes.
- Writing that the genome differs between cell types.
- Quoting a half-life without checking that the amount halves each time.
- Giving a percentage change with the wrong baseline (divide by the old value).
- Claiming a correlation proves that pollution or methylation causes an effect.
- Writing "the gene is switched off" without saying which step (transcription) is affected.
- Mixing up the repressor action of lactose and tryptophan.
- Forgetting a unit or the words "fold" or "%".

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between the genome, transcriptome and proteome?**
The genome is all the DNA of a cell and is the same in nearly all cells of an organism. The transcriptome is all the mRNA present at a given time, and the proteome is all the proteins; both vary between cell types.

**What is the difference between a promoter and an enhancer?**
A promoter is the DNA sequence next to a gene where RNA polymerase attaches. An enhancer is a DNA sequence where regulatory proteins bind to raise transcription of the gene.

**Does histone methylation always repress a gene?**
No. Methylation of cytosine in a promoter represses transcription, but methylation of amino acids in histones can either repress or activate transcription depending on the site.

**Why are tigons and ligers different?**
Most epigenetic tags are removed from gametes, but some are retained and differ between lion and tiger, so the same genes are expressed differently depending on which species supplied them; this is a proposed explanation.

**How does lactose affect gene expression in E. coli?**
A lactose-derived molecule binds the repressor, which changes shape and leaves the operator, so RNA polymerase can transcribe the genes for lactose use.
