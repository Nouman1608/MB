This guide covers **3.2.1.1 Operations management** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1. It sits in Unit 2 Managing operations, human resources and finance (International AS). The [Operational performance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-operational-performance/) introduces all three operations sub-sections; this page goes further on the first one, with new worked examples on comparing sites, service measures and the cost logic behind process choices. Afterwards, use the [operations management revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725-revision-notes/) and the [operations management practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

- **Operations objectives, including calculation**: total costs, unit costs, measures of quality (waiting times, delivery times, proportion of returned items, proportion of defects), labour productivity, capacity and capacity utilisation.
- **Other operational objectives**: flexibility and environmental objectives.
- **Trade-offs**: for example costs versus speed of response, and the fact that objectives vary in different operational contexts.
- **Operations processes**: advantages and disadvantages of job, batch, flow and mass customisation, and of labour-intensive and capital-intensive processes.

## The formulas (9725 Appendix: Formulae and Key data)

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total costs | total fixed costs + total variable costs |
| Unit cost | total costs ÷ output |
| Waiting times | total waiting time ÷ number of customers |
| Delivery times | total delivery time ÷ number of deliveries |
| Proportion of returned items (%) | number of returned items ÷ number of sold items × 100 |
| Proportion of defects (%) | number of defective items ÷ number of items produced × 100 |
| Labour productivity | output in a period of time ÷ average number of employees during the period |
| Capacity | maximum potential output |
| Capacity utilisation (%) | actual output ÷ maximum potential output × 100 |

Note the two different bases: returns are divided by items **sold**, defects by items **produced**.

## Worked example 1: comparing two workshops

Hollin Joinery makes kitchen doors in two workshops. Figures are for one month.

| | Workshop A | Workshop B |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | $36,000 | $52,000 |
| Variable cost per door | $14 | $11 |
| Doors produced | 2,400 | 4,000 |
| Maximum potential output | 3,000 | 4,200 |
| Average employees | 15 | 16 |
| Defective doors | 72 | 40 |

```
Workshop A
Total costs  = 36,000 + (14 × 2,400) = 36,000 + 33,600 = $69,600
Unit cost    = 69,600 ÷ 2,400 = $29.00
Utilisation  = 2,400 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 80%
Productivity = 2,400 ÷ 15 = 160 doors per employee
Defects      = 72 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 3%

Workshop B
Total costs  = 52,000 + (11 × 4,000) = 52,000 + 44,000 = $96,000
Unit cost    = 96,000 ÷ 4,000 = $24.00
Utilisation  = 4,000 ÷ 4,200 × 100 = 95.2% (1 d.p.)
Productivity = 4,000 ÷ 16 = 250 doors per employee
Defects      = 40 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 1%
```

**Interpretation.** Workshop B beats A on every cost and quality measure. But B has only 200 doors of spare capacity; A has 600. If a builder places a rush order for 500 doors, only Workshop A can take it without overtime or delay. A good answer uses both sets of figures: B is the efficient site, A is the flexible one.

**Why A's unit cost would fall if it were busier.** At full capacity of 3,000 doors, A's total costs would be 36,000 + (14 × 3,000) = $78,000 and its unit cost 78,000 ÷ 3,000 = $26.00. The fixed costs are spread over more doors. So part of A's cost disadvantage is low utilisation, not poor working methods.

## Worked example 2: service measures

Operations objectives apply to services too.

- A clinic records 3,450 minutes of total waiting time for 230 patients in a day: average waiting time = 3,450 ÷ 230 = **15 minutes**.
- A courier records 4,320 minutes of total delivery time for 96 deliveries: average delivery time = 4,320 ÷ 96 = **45 minutes**.
- An online clothes shop sold 8,000 items and 960 were returned: proportion returned = 960 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = **12%**.

Returns are not always a quality failure: in online clothing, many items come back because of fit. The figure means most when compared with past months, with rivals, or with the reasons customers give.

## Flexibility and environmental objectives

- **Flexibility**: the ability to change the volume of output (handle a sudden large order) or the variety (switch products or customise). Flexibility usually needs spare capacity, multi-skilled staff or adaptable equipment, all of which cost money.
- **Environmental objectives**: targets such as cutting energy use per unit, reducing waste sent to landfill, or lowering the carbon footprint of deliveries. They can lower costs over time (less energy, less waste) and appeal to customers, but often need investment first.

## Trade-offs and context

Operations objectives pull against one another:

| Objective pursued | What it can cost |
|---|---|
| Lowest unit cost | Less flexibility; long runs of one product; little spare capacity |
| Fast response | Spare capacity or extra inventory, raising unit costs |
| Highest quality | Slower output, more inspection, more training |
| Environmental targets | New equipment or dearer materials in the short run |

The right balance depends on context. A budget airline puts unit cost first; a hospital emergency department puts waiting time first; a wedding dressmaker puts quality and customisation first. In an exam, identify which objective matters most to the business in the case and why.

## Operations processes

| Process | Typical example | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | A bespoke staircase | Meets exact needs; premium price | Highest unit cost; slow; needs skilled staff |
| Batch production | A bakery making trays of one loaf type, then another | Some variety at lower cost than job | Idle time while switching; inventory between stages |
| Flow | A bottling line | Lowest unit cost at high volume; consistent | Inflexible; costly to set up; a fault stops the whole line |
| Mass customisation | Trainers ordered online in chosen colours, made on an automated line | Personal product near flow cost | Complex ordering and production systems; high set-up cost |

**Choosing a process.** Ask how many units are needed, how much variety customers want, and how stable demand is. High volume and low variety point to flow; low volume and high variety to job; mass customisation suits markets where customers will pay a little more for a personal product and order online.

## Labour-intensive or capital-intensive: the cost logic

A capital-intensive process has higher fixed costs (machinery) but lower variable costs; a labour-intensive process is the reverse. Which is cheaper depends on volume.

**Worked example 3.** A furniture maker compares two methods (monthly figures):

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | $10,000 | $40,000 |
| Variable cost per unit | $20 | $8 |

```
At 2,000 units: labour = 10,000 + 40,000 = $50,000 → $25.00 per unit
                capital = 40,000 + 16,000 = $56,000 → $28.00 per unit
At 5,000 units: labour = 10,000 + 100,000 = $110,000 → $22.00 per unit
                capital = 40,000 + 40,000 = $80,000 → $16.00 per unit
```

The two methods cost the same at 2,500 units ($60,000 each). Below that, labour-intensive is cheaper; above it, capital-intensive is. So the decision depends on the sales forecast and how certain it is. Capital-intensive production also gives more consistent quality, but it is harder to scale down if demand falls, and the investment must be financed.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing total costs by capacity instead of actual output.
- Using items sold as the base for defects, or items produced for returns.
- Leaving capacity utilisation without a % sign, or calculating it above 100%.
- Calling the cheapest site "best" without considering flexibility or quality.
- Describing processes without linking them to the volume and variety in the case.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: write the formula, substitute, and give units (dollars, minutes, units per employee, %).
- **Explain** a trade-off by naming both objectives and showing how improving one worsens the other in the case.
- **Analyse** a process choice by linking volume, variety and cost per unit.
- **Evaluate** by deciding which objective matters most to this business and whether its figures support the decision.
