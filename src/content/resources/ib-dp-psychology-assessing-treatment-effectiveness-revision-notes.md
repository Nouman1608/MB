These notes cover the effectiveness of treatment, part of "Prevention and treatment" in the health and well-being context of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027), where students study the explanation and effectiveness of biological and psychological treatments of mental health disorders. Full explanations and an extended-response plan are in the [assessing treatment effectiveness study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-assessing-treatment-effectiveness/). Major depressive disorder is the example disorder.

## Four questions to ask of any treatment study

1. **Better than what?** Placebo, wait-list, treatment as usual or another treatment?
2. **Measured how?** Clinician-rated or self-report scale; response, remission or relapse?
3. **Measured when?** End of treatment, or months later?
4. **Who was studied?** Age, severity, other conditions, culture.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Efficacy | whether a treatment works under ideal, controlled trial conditions |
| Effectiveness | whether it works in ordinary clinical practice |
| Randomised controlled trial (RCT) | participants randomly allocated to treatment or comparison conditions |
| Placebo | an inactive treatment that controls for expectations |
| Wait-list control | a group that receives treatment later, controlling for the passage of time |
| Response | a substantial fall in symptoms, often half or more on a rating scale |
| Remission | symptoms reduced to a near-normal level |
| Relapse | return of symptoms after improvement |
| Spontaneous remission | recovery without treatment, over time |
| Regression to the mean | extreme scores tend to move towards average on retesting |
| Intention to treat | analysing all randomised participants, including those who dropped out |
| Publication bias | positive results are more likely to be published than negative ones |
| Researcher allegiance | a tendency for results to favour the treatment the researchers prefer |
| Meta-analysis | statistical combination of results from many studies |

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Design | Finding | Assessment point |
|---|---|---|---|
| TADS Team (2004) | adolescents with depression randomly allocated to fluoxetine, CBT, both, or placebo for 12 weeks | combination best; fluoxetine better than placebo; CBT alone not clearly better than placebo at 12 weeks; treatments more similar at later follow-up | results depend on the time point; CBT arms not blind |
| DeRubeis et al. (2005) | adults with moderate to severe depression: cognitive therapy, medication or placebo for 16 weeks | cognitive therapy as effective as medication | challenges the view that severe depression needs medication |
| Hollon et al. (2005) | follow-up of responders from DeRubeis et al. | after stopping, cognitive therapy responders relapsed less than those switched from medication to placebo | relapse as an outcome favours CBT |
| Kirsch et al. (2008) | meta-analysis of regulator trial data, including unpublished trials | small advantage over placebo except in severe depression | avoids publication bias; averages hide differences |
| Turner et al. (2008) | compared registered antidepressant trials with published papers | negative trials often unpublished or presented positively | published literature overstates effectiveness |
| Luborsky et al. (1999) | analysis of psychotherapy comparisons | results tended to favour the researchers' preferred therapy | researcher allegiance |

## Design strengths and problems

| Feature | Strength | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Random allocation | balances participant variables | trial volunteers may differ from clinic patients |
| Double-blind drug trial | controls expectations | side effects can reveal who is on the drug |
| Therapy trial | tests real psychological change | cannot be double-blind; therapist skill varies |
| Short follow-up | quick, cheaper, less drop-out | misses relapse |
| Long follow-up | shows lasting effects | attrition; other life events intervene |
| Meta-analysis | large combined sample | quality and comparability of included studies |

## Exam traps

- **"Symptoms fell, so it works."** Without a comparison group, spontaneous remission or regression to the mean could explain it.
- **Describing therapy instead of evidence.** The content point is assessment, not description.
- **One time point.** Contrast end-of-treatment and follow-up results.
- **Therapy as double-blind.** Only the rater can be blind.
- **Invented figures.** State the direction of a finding if you cannot recall the numbers.
- **No disorder named.** Name it in the first sentence.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish efficacy from effectiveness.
2. Why is a comparison group needed in a treatment study?
3. What is the difference between response and remission?
4. Why can a psychological therapy not be tested double-blind?
5. What were the four conditions in TADS (2004)?
6. Which condition did best at 12 weeks in TADS?
7. What did Hollon et al. (2005) show about relapse?
8. Define publication bias and name a study that showed it in antidepressant research.
9. How did Kirsch et al. (2008) reduce the effect of publication bias?
10. What is researcher allegiance?
11. What does intention-to-treat analysis protect against?
12. Give one reason trial results may overestimate effectiveness in ordinary clinics.

**Answers**

1. Efficacy is whether a treatment works under ideal trial conditions; effectiveness is whether it works in ordinary clinical practice.
2. Symptoms may improve anyway through spontaneous remission, regression to the mean or expectation, so improvement must be compared with a group that did not receive the treatment.
3. Response is a substantial fall in symptoms; remission is a fall to a near-normal level.
4. Both the client and the therapist know which therapy is being delivered.
5. Fluoxetine, CBT, fluoxetine combined with CBT, and placebo.
6. The combination of fluoxetine and CBT.
7. Patients who responded to cognitive therapy and stopped were less likely to relapse than those switched from medication to placebo.
8. Positive results are more likely to be published than negative ones; Turner et al. (2008).
9. By analysing the regulator's trial data, including unpublished trials.
10. A tendency for comparisons to favour the treatment the researchers prefer or developed.
11. It stops a treatment looking better than it is because patients who did badly dropped out.
12. For example: trials exclude complex patients; therapists in trials are highly trained; monitoring is more frequent than in normal care.
