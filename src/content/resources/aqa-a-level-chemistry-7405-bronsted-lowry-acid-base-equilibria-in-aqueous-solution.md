This guide teaches section **3.1.12.1 Brønsted–Lowry acid–base equilibria in aqueous solution** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. The [Acids and bases study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/) introduces the whole topic. This page goes deeper on one idea that every later calculation depends on: what an acid and a base are, what happens to the proton, and how to write the equilibrium for any acid or base in water. Quick recall is in the [Acids and bases revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Brønsted–Lowry acid–base equilibria practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bronsted-lowry-acid-base-equilibria-in-aqueous-solution-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content | What it means |
|---|---|
| An acid is a proton donor | A species that gives away an H⁺ ion |
| A base is a proton acceptor | A species that takes an H⁺ ion, using a lone pair |
| Acid–base equilibria involve the transfer of protons | Write reactions as proton transfer, usually reversible |

The specification wording is short, but exam questions use it in several ways: identifying acids and bases in an equation, naming conjugate pairs, and writing equations that show water acting as an acid or a base.

## Protons, H⁺ and H₃O⁺

A hydrogen atom has one proton and one electron. Remove the electron and you have H⁺, which is just a proton. A free proton does not exist in water. It bonds to a lone pair on a water molecule and forms the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺. In equations, chemists often write H⁺(aq) as a shorthand for H₃O⁺(aq). Either is acceptable, but be consistent in one answer.

## Acids as proton donors

An acid donates H⁺ to something else. The acid must contain a hydrogen atom that can leave as H⁺. When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water, it donates a proton to water:

```
HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
```

Here HCl is the acid. Water accepts the proton, so water is acting as a base. Many sources simplify this to HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻, which hides the role of water, but the simplified form is acceptable when the question does not ask about the role of water.

An acid with one H⁺ to donate is **monoprotic** (HCl, HNO₃, CH₃COOH). One with two is **diprotic** (H₂SO₄). In ethanoic acid, only the hydrogen in the –COOH group is acidic. The three hydrogens in the CH₃ group are bonded to carbon and are not donated.

## Bases as proton acceptors

A base accepts H⁺ using a lone pair of electrons, forming a new covalent bond. Ammonia reacts with water:

```
NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
```

Ammonia is the base. Water donates a proton, so water is acting as an acid. The lone pair on nitrogen forms the bond to the proton. Hydroxide ions, OH⁻, are also bases because oxygen's lone pair accepts a proton to form water.

The word **alkali** means a base that dissolves in water to give OH⁻(aq). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis: copper(II) oxide accepts protons but is insoluble in water.

## Conjugate acid–base pairs

Acid–base reactions are equilibria, so the products can react back. This leads to the idea of a conjugate pair. Two species that differ by one proton form a **conjugate acid–base pair**. The species with the extra proton is the acid.

```
CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)
acid 1        base 2     base 1        acid 2
```

The pairs are CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺ / H₂O. Write them with the acid first. In an equilibrium there are always two conjugate pairs.

### A reliable way to identify the pairs

1. Look for the species that has lost an H⁺ between the left and right sides. The left species is the acid, and the right species is its conjugate base.
2. Look for the species that has gained an H⁺. The left species is the base, and the right species is its conjugate acid.
3. Check that each pair differs by exactly one H⁺, in both formula and charge.

### Worked example 1

Identify the acids, bases and conjugate pairs in: HCO₃⁻ + NH₃ ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + NH₄⁺.

- HCO₃⁻ loses H⁺ to become CO₃²⁻, so HCO₃⁻ is the acid and CO₃²⁻ is its conjugate base.
- NH₃ gains H⁺ to become NH₄⁺, so NH₃ is the base and NH₄⁺ is its conjugate acid.
- Pairs: HCO₃⁻ / CO₃²⁻ and NH₄⁺ / NH₃. Charge changes by one unit each time, as it must.

### Worked example 2: ignoring spectators

Hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. The ionic equation is H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O. In this, H₃O⁺ is the acid and OH⁻ is the base. The conjugate base of H₃O⁺ is H₂O, and the conjugate acid of OH⁻ is H₂O. Sodium and chloride ions are spectators and take no part in proton transfer.

## Water: acid, base or both

Water can accept a proton (making H₃O⁺) and donate one (making OH⁻). A substance that can act as either is **amphoteric**. Water is amphoteric, and so is the hydrogencarbonate ion:

```
HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ → H₂CO₃ + H₂O      (HCO₃⁻ acts as a base)
HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻  → CO₃²⁻ + H₂O      (HCO₃⁻ acts as an acid)
```

Whether a species acts as an acid or a base depends on what it is reacting with. Water is a base towards HCl and an acid towards NH₃. In water itself, one water molecule donates a proton to another:

```
H₂O + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻
```

This self-ionisation is the start of the ionic product of water, Kw, which later pages build on.

## Strong and weak acids and bases

Position of equilibrium tells you strength.

| Type | Behaviour in water | Example equation |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid | Dissociates almost completely; the equilibrium lies far to the right | HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ |
| Weak acid | Dissociates only slightly; the equilibrium lies to the left | CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ |
| Strong base | Accepts protons almost completely or releases OH⁻ fully | NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ |
| Weak base | Accepts protons only slightly | NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ |

A useful pattern: the conjugate base of a strong acid is a very weak base (Cl⁻ barely accepts protons), and the conjugate base of a weak acid is a stronger base (CH₃COO⁻ does accept protons). Strength is about how far the equilibrium goes, not about concentration. A dilute solution of a strong acid is still a strong acid.

### Worked example 3: writing an equilibrium for a base

Write an equation showing methylamine, CH₃NH₂, acting as a base in water.

- Methylamine has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it accepts a proton from water.
- The product is the conjugate acid CH₃NH₃⁺. Water, having lost H⁺, becomes OH⁻.
- Equation: CH₃NH₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
- Conjugate pairs: CH₃NH₃⁺ / CH₃NH₂ and H₂O / OH⁻.

### Worked example 4: sulfuric acid in steps

Sulfuric acid is diprotic and loses protons in two stages.

```
H₂SO₄ + H₂O → HSO₄⁻ + H₃O⁺       (essentially complete)
HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ SO₄²⁻ + H₃O⁺       (partial)
```

HSO₄⁻ is the conjugate base of H₂SO₄ in the first step and the acid in the second. In A-level calculations, the second step is usually ignored unless the question says otherwise.

## Common mistakes

- Defining an acid as "a substance that produces H⁺ ions" without saying proton donor. Use the Brønsted–Lowry words.
- Describing a base as "a substance that neutralises acid" or only as OH⁻. The definition is proton acceptor.
- Writing a conjugate pair that differs by more than one proton, or by the wrong direction of charge.
- Writing → for a weak acid equilibrium. Use ⇌.
- Forgetting water in the equation when the question asks about acting as acid or base.
- Confusing strong with concentrated. Strength is the extent of dissociation. Concentration is the amount of acid per dm³.
- Saying the H on carbon in ethanoic acid is acidic. Only the O–H hydrogen is.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State**: give the definition in one line, using "proton donor" or "proton acceptor".
- **Identify the conjugate pairs**: write two pairs, each with the acid first.
- **Write an equation to show X acting as an acid / base**: include water and use state symbols if asked.
- **Explain why X is amphoteric**: give one equation where it donates a proton and one where it accepts one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is H⁺ the same as a proton?**
Yes. A hydrogen ion H⁺ is a bare proton, which is why Brønsted–Lowry theory talks about protons. In water, it exists as H₃O⁺.

**Can a species be an acid and a conjugate base at the same time?**
Yes, in different pairs. HSO₄⁻ is the conjugate base of H₂SO₄ and the acid of the SO₄²⁻ pair. It depends on which equilibrium you look at.

**Why is the arrow different for weak acids?**
Only a small fraction dissociates, and the reverse reaction occurs constantly. The double arrow shows a dynamic equilibrium that lies to the left.
