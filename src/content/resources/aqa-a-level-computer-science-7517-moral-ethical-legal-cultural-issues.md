This guide covers **4.8.1 Individual (moral), social (ethical), legal and cultural issues and opportunities** in AQA A-Level Computer Science (7517). The specification asks for **awareness of current opportunities and risks** of computing, and for you to understand four ideas. Digital technologies have changed communication and information flows, so it is now possible to **monitor behaviour, amass and analyse personal information, and distribute, publish and communicate personal information**. Computer scientists and software engineers therefore have **power and responsibility** for the algorithms and code they produce. Software and algorithms **embed moral and cultural values**. And the **issue of scale**, with software used across the world, means individuals can do great good or great harm. You must also be able to **discuss the challenges facing legislators in the digital age**.

There is no fixed list of facts to memorise. Questions give a scenario or statement and ask you to discuss it, so the skill is structured, balanced argument. The wider [consequences of uses of computing study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-computer-science-consequences-of-uses-of-computing-study-guide/) covers the topic as a whole. This page sets out the vocabulary, a method for answering, a worked case study, and the legal background.

Condensed notes are in the [issues and opportunities revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-computer-science-7517-moral-ethical-legal-cultural-issues-revision-notes/), and exam-style questions are in the [issues and opportunities practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-computer-science-7517-moral-ethical-legal-cultural-issues-practice/).

## The four lenses

| Lens | The question it asks | Examples of what it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Moral (individual)** | Is this right or wrong for a person? What would I be willing to do? | Honesty, consent, a developer deciding whether to build a feature, whistleblowing |
| **Ethical (social)** | Is this fair and beneficial for groups of people and for society? | Fairness, bias, jobs, access, accountability |
| **Legal** | What do the laws and regulations permit or require? | Data protection, computer misuse, copyright, interception |
| **Cultural** | How does this fit or clash with the values, customs and expectations of different communities? | Different attitudes to privacy, language, imagery, censorship |

An issue often touches more than one lens. Naming the lens shows the examiner you are organising your argument, but do not spend marks on definitions alone.

## The three capabilities highlighted by the specification

### 1. Monitoring behaviour

Technology can record where we are, what we buy, what we search for and who we talk to. Examples include CCTV with automatic number plate or face recognition, location data from phones, workplace monitoring software and vehicle trackers.

- **Opportunities:** crime detection and prevention, safer workplaces, finding lost people, insurance premiums linked to safe driving, helping elderly people live independently.
- **Risks:** loss of privacy, a feeling of being watched that discourages lawful behaviour, misuse of recordings, mistaken identification, and monitoring that is not agreed to.

### 2. Amassing and analysing personal information

Large collections of personal data can be searched, combined and analysed with algorithms and machine learning to reveal patterns that no single record would show.

- **Opportunities:** personalised medicine, spotting disease outbreaks, fraud detection, better public services, recommendations people find useful.
- **Risks:** profiling that people do not know about, decisions made about them (loans, jobs, insurance) by algorithms that may be biased, data breaches, and data collected for one purpose being used for another.

### 3. Distributing, publishing and communicating personal information

The internet lets anyone publish to the whole world instantly, and what is published is hard to remove.

- **Opportunities:** free speech, community, education, whistleblowing that exposes wrongdoing, help for people who are isolated.
- **Risks:** harassment, publishing private details without consent, misinformation that spreads faster than correction, permanent records of youthful mistakes, and information that is accurate but harmful in the wrong hands.

## Power, responsibility, values and scale

**Power and responsibility.** The people who write the algorithms and code decide what the software does, often without the people affected ever knowing. With that power comes responsibility to consider who will be affected, test for harmful side effects, be honest about what the system can and cannot do, and refuse or raise concerns about work that would cause serious harm. Professional bodies publish codes of conduct that set out these duties.

**Software embeds moral and cultural values.** Every design choice reflects a judgement. A route planner that chooses the quickest route values time over quietness. A recommendation system that maximises time spent on screen values attention over wellbeing. A face recognition system trained mainly on images of one group may work worse for others, which embeds a bias even if nobody intended it. A car that must choose between two harmful outcomes in an emergency has had somebody decide, in advance, how it should choose.

**The issue of scale.** A mistake in a lesson-planning tool at one school affects one school. A mistake in software used by millions can affect millions at the same moment, and the same is true of a benefit: one well designed tool can give access to information, healthcare or education to people all over the world. So a single programmer, or a small team, can do very great good or very great harm.

## Challenges facing legislators

Lawmakers must write rules for technology that changes faster than laws can be passed. Typical challenges:

- **Pace of change:** a law written for today's technology may be out of date before it comes into force, or may be too specific to cover new methods.
- **Jurisdiction:** the internet crosses borders. A company in one country can serve users in another, and an offence may involve computers in several countries, so it is unclear whose law applies and how it is enforced.
- **Anonymity and evidence:** it can be difficult to identify who carried out an action, and digital evidence can be changed or deleted.
- **Balancing rights:** privacy against security and crime prevention; freedom of speech against protection from harm; innovation against regulation.
- **Technical understanding:** laws must be written clearly enough to apply but without requiring every lawmaker to understand the technology in depth.
- **Enforcement and scale:** monitoring billions of messages or transactions for illegal activity is a huge task and raises further privacy concerns.
- **Different cultures:** what is acceptable speech, content or privacy differs between countries, so global services face conflicting rules.

## UK law you can use as examples

The specification does not name particular Acts, so treat these as supporting examples, not as lists to learn word for word.

- **Data Protection Act 2018 and the UK GDPR:** rules on how organisations collect, use and keep personal data, including that data should be processed lawfully and fairly, collected for specified purposes, kept accurate and secure, and not kept longer than needed. Individuals have rights, such as seeing the data held about them.
- **Computer Misuse Act 1990:** makes it an offence to access a computer without authorisation, to do so intending to commit a further offence, or to modify computer material without authorisation (for example by spreading malware).
- **Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988:** protects creators' rights in software, music, film and written work, including against unlicensed copying.
- **Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000:** governs how public bodies may intercept communications and carry out surveillance.

## A method for discussion questions

1. **Identify the stakeholders:** who gains, who bears the risk, who is not asked?
2. **State the opportunities and the risks** for each group, using specific wording from the scenario.
3. **Link to a lens:** is the main issue moral, ethical, legal or cultural?
4. **Consider safeguards:** consent, transparency, limits on data use, testing for bias, human review, legislation.
5. **Weigh the trade-off** and reach a **reasoned conclusion**. Say what would change your view.

## Worked case study: a face-recognition doorbell scheme

*Scenario (invented for practice).* A town council offers every household a free doorbell camera with face recognition. It flags people who are on a police watch list and shares recordings with the council to reduce crime. Residents are not asked beforehand.

- **Stakeholders:** householders, visitors, delivery workers, police, the council, the company supplying the cameras, people wrongly flagged.
- **Opportunities:** faster detection of offenders; reduced burglary; evidence for investigations; reassurance for vulnerable residents.
- **Risks:** visitors are monitored without consent (monitoring); recordings and faces build a large store of personal data (amassing); recordings might be shared or leaked (distribution); the recognition may be less accurate for some groups, causing false accusations (embedded values); the data could later be used for purposes never announced (scale and function creep).
- **Legal:** the scheme must comply with data protection law on lawful purpose, security and retention; the council may need to show it is necessary and proportionate.
- **Cultural:** some communities may be more affected, or have different views about being watched outside their homes.
- **Safeguards:** consultation before launch; clear notices; a short retention period; independent testing for accuracy; human checks before anyone is accused; the right to opt out.
- **Conclusion (sample):** the scheme could reduce crime, but without consent, accuracy checks and limits on data use the risks to privacy and fairness outweigh the benefits, so it should be introduced only after consultation and independent testing.

## Common mistakes

- Writing **only opportunities or only risks**. Discussion questions need both.
- **Listing** issues without explaining the consequence for a person or group.
- Using **generic statements** ("it invades privacy") without linking to the scenario.
- Treating a law as the **only** question: something can be legal and still unethical.
- Giving an **opinion with no reason**, or a conclusion that does not follow from the points made.
- Stating facts about a law that you are unsure of. If unsure, describe the principle and avoid dates or section numbers.

## Exam technique

- Read the scenario and **underline the capability** involved: monitoring, amassing, or distributing.
- Give **balanced points** with a link: point, scenario evidence, effect.
- Write a short **conclusion**; it often separates the top mark band.
- Use the correct technical words (personal data, consent, profile, algorithmic bias, jurisdiction).
