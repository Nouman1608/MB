This guide covers **3.2.2.4 Motivation** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 (International AS). The [Human resources study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-human-resources/) summarises the three theories and the methods; this page shows how to apply the theories to a case, how to cost a financial method, and how to judge whether a method has worked. Then condense it with the [motivation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-motivation-9725-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [motivation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-motivation-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

- The benefits of motivated employees.
- Theories of motivation: **Taylor's scientific management**; **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** (self-actualisation, esteem, social, safety, basic); **Herzberg's two-factor theory** (hygiene and motivators).
- How to improve employee motivation.
- Financial methods: **wages, piece rate pay, commission, salaries, performance-related pay, fringe benefits**.
- Non-financial methods of motivation and engagement: **job enlargement, job enrichment, job rotation, empowerment, teamwork**.
- Influences on the choice and assessment of the effectiveness of financial and non-financial methods.

## Why motivation matters

Motivated employees work harder and more carefully, so **productivity** and **quality** rise. They are less likely to be absent or to leave, so **labour turnover** and recruitment costs fall. In services, they give better **customer service**, which builds loyalty. Each benefit can be measured using the HR data in the [human resource data guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-human-resource-data-9725/).

## The three theories, applied

### Taylor's scientific management

Taylor argued that managers should study each task scientifically, find the single most efficient way to do it, train workers in that method, supervise them closely, and pay them according to output. The assumption is that workers are motivated mainly by money.

**Apply it when** tasks are repetitive and output is easy to count, such as packing or assembly. **Its limits**: it ignores social and psychological needs, repetitive work can bore staff, and pay by output may push speed over quality.

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Maslow set out five levels of need: **basic** (food, shelter: pay), **safety** (secure job, safe conditions), **social** (belonging, teamwork), **esteem** (recognition, status) and **self-actualisation** (reaching one's potential). Lower needs must be largely met before higher needs motivate.

| Level | What a business can offer |
|---|---|
| Basic | Pay enough to live on; breaks |
| Safety | Permanent contracts; safe working conditions |
| Social | Teamwork; social events; good communication |
| Esteem | Recognition schemes; job titles; promotion |
| Self-actualisation | Challenging projects; training; empowerment |

**Apply it** by identifying which level is not being met. A worker on a zero hours contract who fears losing shifts is unlikely to be motivated by a team-building day.

### Herzberg's two-factor theory

Herzberg separated **hygiene factors** (pay, working conditions, supervision, company policy, relationships at work) from **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement).

- Poor hygiene factors cause **dissatisfaction**; improving them removes dissatisfaction but does not create lasting motivation.
- Motivators create **satisfaction** and lasting motivation.

**Apply it** in two steps: first fix the hygiene factors that cause complaints, then redesign jobs to include motivators, through **job enrichment** and **empowerment**.

### Comparing the theories

| | Taylor | Maslow | Herzberg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Role of money | The main motivator | Meets basic (and some safety) needs | A hygiene factor |
| Key method | Piece rate, close supervision | Meet each level in turn | Enrich jobs once hygiene factors are sound |
| Best suits | Routine, measurable tasks | Explaining why needs change | Designing jobs |

## Financial methods, costed

**Worked example 1: introducing piece rate.** At Corriemoor Packaging, a packer earns an hourly wage of $15 for a 40-hour week and packs 1,000 units, of which 1% are faulty. The firm switches to piece rate of $0.55 per unit. Output rises to 1,200 units a week, but faults rise to 4%.

```
Before: pay = 15 × 40 = $600; good units = 1,000 × 99% = 990
        labour cost per good unit = 600 ÷ 990 = $0.61 (2 d.p.)
After:  pay = 0.55 × 1,200 = $660; good units = 1,200 × 96% = 1,152
        labour cost per good unit = 660 ÷ 1,152 = $0.57 (2 d.p.)
```

The packer earns $60 more a week and the cost per good unit falls, so both gain. But faults have quadrupled: if customers notice, the hidden cost could exceed the saving. Piece rate needs quality checks.

**Worked example 2: commission and performance-related pay.**

- A sales adviser earns a basic salary of $1,200 a month plus 3% commission on sales. With sales of $40,000: 1,200 + (0.03 × 40,000) = 1,200 + 1,200 = **$2,400**.
- A manager on a $45,000 salary receives a performance-related bonus of 8% for meeting targets: 0.08 × 45,000 = **$3,600**.

| Method | Works best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Wages (hourly) | Hours vary; output hard to measure | No reward for extra effort |
| Piece rate pay | Output is countable and quality easy to check | Speed over quality |
| Commission | Sales effort drives results | Pressure selling; unstable income |
| Salaries | Work is varied or long-term | No direct link to effort |
| Performance-related pay | Targets can be set fairly | Appraisal felt to be unfair |
| Fringe benefits | Retention and loyalty matter | Cost; not valued equally by all |

## Non-financial methods

- **Job enlargement**: more tasks at the same level (horizontal). Adds variety but may feel like extra work.
- **Job enrichment**: more responsibility and more demanding tasks (vertical). Matches Herzberg's motivators; needs training.
- **Job rotation**: moving between tasks. Reduces boredom and creates cover for absence, but tasks may be equally dull.
- **Empowerment**: authority to make decisions about one's own work. Speeds up service and builds esteem; risk of errors.
- **Teamwork**: working in groups with shared goals. Meets social needs and spreads ideas; risk of conflict.

**Example.** A hotel lets reception staff resolve complaints up to $100 without asking a manager (empowerment). Guests get faster answers, staff feel trusted (esteem), and managers save time. The hotel must train staff and accept some mistakes.

## Influences on the choice of method

- **Nature of the work**: countable output suits piece rate; creative or team work does not.
- **Cost and cash available**: non-financial methods can be cheaper, though training for enrichment costs money.
- **Employees' needs and expectations**: low-paid staff may value pay most; well-paid professionals may value responsibility.
- **Management style and culture**: empowerment needs managers willing to let go.
- **Skills of staff**: enrichment and empowerment need capable, trained employees.
- **Competition for labour**: if rivals pay more, financial methods may be needed first.

## Assessing effectiveness

Measure before and after, using data the specification already names: labour productivity, labour turnover, retention, absence, quality measures and customer satisfaction. Allow time for effects to show, and look for other causes: productivity may rise because of new equipment, not the new bonus. Compare costs with benefits: a scheme that lifts productivity by 5% while raising labour costs by 8% has raised labour cost per unit.

## Common mistakes

- Saying Herzberg treats pay as a motivator.
- Mixing up job enlargement and job enrichment.
- Listing theories without applying them to the workers in the case.
- Recommending piece rate for work where output cannot be counted.
- Claiming a method "will motivate" without saying how success would be measured.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: name the method, show how it meets a need or motivator, and give the effect on the business.
- **Analyse**: use a theory to explain *why* a method works or fails for these employees.
- **Evaluate**: weigh cost against measurable benefit, consider the nature of the work and staff, and say what the result depends on.
