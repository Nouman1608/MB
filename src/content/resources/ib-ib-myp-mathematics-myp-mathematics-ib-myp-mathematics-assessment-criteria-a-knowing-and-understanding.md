This guide goes deep on one criterion: Criterion A, knowing and understanding, in the IB *Mathematics guide for the MYP* (for use from September 2020 and January 2021, first assessment 2022). It is about the thinking that happens between reading a problem and writing a correct answer. ## What Criterion A assesses

Criterion A is marked out of 8 and has three strands. In Year 5 they ask you to:

| Strand | What it asks | What a teacher looks for on the page |
|---|---|---|
| i | select appropriate mathematics when solving problems in both familiar and unfamiliar situations | a method that suits the problem, often named or visible in the first line |
| ii | apply the selected mathematics successfully when solving problems | the method carried through without errors in algebra, arithmetic or notation |
| iii | solve problems correctly in a variety of contexts | a correct final answer, in the form and units the question needs |

The strands form a chain. A wrong choice at strand i usually breaks strands ii and iii, but a correct choice with a slip in strand ii still shows understanding, which is why visible working matters. An answer alone gives the teacher evidence for strand iii only.

## Familiar and unfamiliar situations

The top band needs challenging problems in **unfamiliar** situations as well as familiar ones. "Unfamiliar" does not mean new mathematics. It means the problem does not announce which mathematics to use. Problems usually become unfamiliar in one of these ways:

| Feature | Familiar version | Unfamiliar version |
|---|---|---|
| Cue words removed | "Use Pythagoras' theorem to find x" | "How far is the foot of the ladder from the wall?" |
| Reversed | Find the area of a circle of radius 5 cm | A circle has area 50 cm²; find its circumference |
| Topics combined | Solve x² + 7x − 60 = 0 | A right-angled triangle has legs x and x + 7 and hypotenuse 13 |
| Hidden step | Find the volume of a cylinder | How many days will the water in this tank last? |
| New context | Percentage decrease of a price | Percentage drop in a bacterial count after treatment |

Practising only exercises that tell you the method trains strand ii, not strand i. To prepare for unfamiliar problems, cover the heading of an exercise and decide the method before you look.

## Strand i: selecting mathematics

Ask three questions before writing anything:

1. **What is unknown, and what kind of quantity is it?** A length, a count, a probability, a rate, a time.
2. **What links the information to that unknown?** A formula, a relationship such as proportion, a geometric fact, a statistical measure.
3. **Is there more than one route, and which is safer?** Choose the one with fewer steps or easier checks.

Writing a short line that names the choice ("the cost scales with the number of items, so use a unit rate") makes strand i visible. It also helps if a later slip spoils the answer, because the teacher can still see that your selection was sound.

## Strand ii: applying successfully

Successful application means accurate execution. The habits that protect it:

- Keep full calculator values in the middle of a calculation and round only at the end.
- Write each step on its own line so an error can be found and the rest still credited.
- Carry units through: area in cm², volume in m³, then convert at the end (1 m³ = 1000 litres).
- Use correct notation, for example 3.2 × 10⁴ rather than calculator notation.

## Strand iii: solving correctly in a variety of contexts

A correct solution answers the question asked. Check that the final answer has the right form (a whole number of days, a percentage, a probability between 0 and 1), the right degree of accuracy, and a sentence where the context calls for one. Then test it: substitute back, estimate, or solve a second way.

## Worked example 1: a hidden step (annotated)

A cylindrical water tank has internal diameter 1.2 m. The water in it is 0.9 m deep. A household uses 35 litres of water a day. For how many full days will the water last?

| Step | Working | Strand shown |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The water forms a cylinder, so find its volume, then divide by the daily use. | i: selects volume and division |
| 2 | Radius = 1.2 ÷ 2 = 0.6 m | ii |
| 3 | V = π × 0.6² × 0.9 = 1.0179 m³ | ii |
| 4 | 1.0179 m³ = 1017.9 litres | ii: unit conversion |
| 5 | 1017.9 ÷ 35 = 29.08 | ii |
| 6 | The water lasts **29 full days**. | iii: answers "full days" by rounding down |

The hidden step is the conversion from cubic metres to litres. A response that divides 1.0179 by 35 has selected the right mathematics but not applied it successfully. A response that rounds 29.08 up to 30 has applied it but not solved the problem as asked, because the 30th day is not full.

## Worked example 2: topics combined

A right-angled triangle has shorter sides x cm and (x + 7) cm and hypotenuse 13 cm. Find its perimeter.

Selection: the sides of a right-angled triangle are linked by Pythagoras' theorem, and the unknown appears squared twice, so expect a quadratic.

```
x² + (x + 7)² = 13²
x² + x² + 14x + 49 = 169
2x² + 14x − 120 = 0
x² + 7x − 60 = 0
(x + 12)(x − 5) = 0
x = 5 or x = −12
```

A length cannot be negative, so x = 5. The sides are 5 cm, 12 cm and 13 cm, and the perimeter is **30 cm**. Check: 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13².

What makes this unfamiliar is that neither "quadratic" nor "Pythagoras" appears in the question. Rejecting x = −12 with a reason shows the solution is correct in context, not just algebraically.

## Worked example 3: two routes, one choice

A coat costs 80 after a 20% discount. What was the price before the discount?

- Route 1 (inverse percentage): 80 is 80% of the original, so the original is 80 ÷ 0.8 = **100**.
- Route 2 (equation): let p be the original price. 0.8p = 80, so p = 100.

A common wrong selection is to add 20% of 80 to get 96. That treats 20% of the *sale* price as the discount. Checking the answer by applying the discount to it (100 × 0.8 = 80) catches the error immediately, and 96 × 0.8 = 76.8 shows why 96 is wrong.

## Common mistakes

- Starting to calculate before deciding what the unknown is, then answering a different question.
- Choosing a method because it matches the last exercise, not the problem.
- Rounding intermediate values, so the final answer drifts (for example using π = 3 or a radius rounded to 1 s.f.).
- Leaving a quadratic with both roots when the context rules one out.
- Giving a final answer without units, or in the wrong units after a conversion was needed.
- Rounding a count the wrong way: tins of paint and buses round up, full days and complete items round down.
- Writing an answer with no working, which gives evidence for strand iii only.

## Exam technique and command terms

Criterion A tasks are usually tests or examination questions. The command term tells you how much to write:

| Command term | What it expects |
|---|---|
| State / write down | A brief answer; little or no working needed |
| Calculate | A numerical answer with the relevant stages of working |
| Find / determine | An answer obtained by working; "determine" implies only one possible answer |
| Solve | Every value that satisfies the equation, by algebra, a graph or numerical methods |
| Show that | Every step of a calculation or derivation leading to the given result |
| Estimate | An approximate value, usually from rounded figures |

When the answer is given ("show that the radius is 4.2 cm to 1 d.p."), you must reach a more accurate value first, such as 4.18, and then round it. Starting from the given answer earns nothing. When a question has several parts, an answer to an earlier part is often needed later: use your own value if you are unsure of it, because follow-through marking can credit correct method.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three strands of MYP Mathematics Criterion A?**
Strand i is selecting appropriate mathematics in familiar and unfamiliar situations, strand ii is applying the selected mathematics successfully, and strand iii is solving problems correctly in a variety of contexts.

**What makes a problem unfamiliar in Criterion A?**
An unfamiliar problem does not tell you which mathematics to use. It may hide a step, combine topics, reverse a standard question or place the mathematics in a new context.

**Why does working matter in Criterion A?**
Working shows which mathematics you selected and how well you applied it. A bare answer gives evidence only for whether the solution is correct.
