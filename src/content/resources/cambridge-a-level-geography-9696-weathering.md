This guide covers **3.2 Weathering** in Topic 3, Rocks and weathering, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **Physical (mechanical) weathering processes:** freeze-thaw, heating/cooling, salt crystal growth, pressure release (dilatation) and vegetation root action.
- **Chemical weathering processes:** hydrolysis, hydration and carbonation.
- **General factors affecting the type and rate of weathering:** climate, rock type, rock structure, vegetation and relief.
- **Specific factors affecting the type and rate of weathering:** temperature and rainfall (Peltier diagram).

It is examined in Paper 1, often with a Peltier diagram, a photograph of weathered rock or a table of climate data. The [3.2 weathering revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-weathering-revision-notes/) list each process and the main controls; this guide explains the mechanisms, the chemistry at the level the syllabus needs, and how to read a Peltier diagram. Practise with the [3.2 weathering practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-weathering-practice/).

## Weathering, not erosion

Weathering is the breakdown of rock **in situ**, at or near the Earth's surface, by physical, chemical and biological processes. Nothing is transported. Erosion involves removal by an agent such as water, ice or wind. The distinction matters because weathering prepares material that slope processes (3.3) and rivers then move.

## Physical weathering: how each process breaks rock

- **Freeze-thaw:** water in a joint or pore freezes and expands by about 9%, exerting pressure on the rock. Repeated cycles widen the crack until fragments break off. It needs water and frequent fluctuations around 0°C, so it is most effective in mountain and periglacial environments with many freeze-thaw cycles each year. A place that stays frozen all winter has few cycles and less freeze-thaw.
- **Heating/cooling:** rocks are poor conductors, so in climates with a large diurnal temperature range the outer layer heats and expands by day and contracts at night more than the interior. Repeated stress may cause outer layers to peel off (**exfoliation**) or individual mineral grains to loosen, because dark and light minerals expand by different amounts (**granular disintegration**). Laboratory experiments suggest that heating alone is slow and that moisture greatly increases its effect, so it is best described as working alongside other processes.
- **Salt crystal growth:** saline water enters pores; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and exert pressure on the pore walls. Some salts also expand when they absorb water. It is important in hot deserts where groundwater rises by capillary action and on coasts exposed to spray.
- **Pressure release (dilatation):** rocks formed at depth, such as granite, are under great pressure from overlying rock. When that rock is removed by erosion, the pressure falls, the rock expands and cracks develop parallel to the surface. This produces **sheeting** and curved slabs and helps form domed hills.
- **Vegetation root action:** roots growing into joints widen them as they thicken, and plants can lift and split blocks of rock. Root action also opens pathways for water, helping chemical weathering.

## Chemical weathering: the three named processes

- **Carbonation** affects rocks containing calcium carbonate, especially limestone and chalk. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form a weak carbonic acid. This reacts with calcium carbonate to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble and carried away in solution:
  calcium carbonate + carbonic acid → calcium bicarbonate.
  Carbonation widens joints and bedding planes into the features of karst scenery. Cold water can hold more dissolved carbon dioxide than warm water, so carbonation can be effective in cool climates as well as warm ones.
- **Hydrolysis** is a reaction between minerals and water, in which hydrogen ions replace other ions in the mineral. Its classic effect is on **feldspar** in granite, which is converted into **kaolinite (china clay)**, releasing other ions in solution. Because quartz is resistant, hydrolysis of granite leaves loose quartz grains and clay, and the rock crumbles. It is the dominant chemical process in hot, wet climates and can rot granite to depths of tens of metres in the humid tropics.
- **Hydration** is the absorption of water into the structure of a mineral, which makes it expand. For example, anhydrite becomes gypsum when it takes up water. The expansion stresses the rock and makes it more open to other processes. Hydration is chemical, but its effect is partly mechanical.

As a general rule, the rate of most chemical reactions increases as temperature rises, and all three processes need water. Hot, humid climates therefore have the deepest weathering profiles.

## General factors affecting type and rate

| Factor | How it affects weathering |
|---|---|
| Climate | Temperature and moisture determine which processes are possible and how fast they run |
| Rock type (mineral composition) | Limestone and chalk are vulnerable to carbonation; granite to hydrolysis of feldspar; quartz-rich sandstone is resistant to chemical attack |
| Rock structure | Joints, faults and bedding planes let water in and increase surface area; porous rocks absorb water for freeze-thaw and salt growth; coarse-grained rocks suffer granular disintegration |
| Vegetation | Roots split rock; decaying plants add carbon dioxide and organic acids to soil water; vegetation also holds moisture against rock surfaces |
| Relief | Steep slopes shed weathered material, exposing fresh rock to attack; gentle slopes and valley floors accumulate deep regolith that keeps water in contact with rock; aspect and altitude change temperature and freeze-thaw frequency |

## Specific factors: the Peltier diagram

The **Peltier diagram** plots **mean annual temperature** on one axis and **mean annual rainfall** on the other, and divides the graph into zones of weathering type and intensity. In general terms:

- **Strong chemical weathering** occurs where temperatures and rainfall are both high, as in humid tropical climates.
- **Moderate chemical weathering** occupies warm and temperate climates with moderate to high rainfall.
- **Frost action** (freeze-thaw) is strongest where mean temperatures are low, around or a little below freezing, with enough moisture to supply water to joints.
- **Weak (slight) weathering** of all kinds occurs where rainfall is very low, whether hot or cold, because water is needed for almost every process.

The diagram is a model. It uses annual means, so it hides the number of freeze-thaw cycles, the diurnal temperature range and the seasonal timing of rain, all of which matter. It also ignores rock type and structure. A strong answer uses it and then states its limits.

## Worked example: reading climate data against the Peltier diagram

*Three fictional stations have these annual means:*

| Station | Mean annual temperature (°C) | Mean annual rainfall (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 26 | 2400 |
| Q | -4 | 700 |
| R | 24 | 120 |

- **P:** hot and very wet, so it falls in the zone of **strong chemical weathering**. Expect hydrolysis of feldspar-rich rocks and deep weathering profiles.
- **Q:** cold, with a mean below freezing and moderate precipitation, so it falls in the zone of **strong frost action**. Expect freeze-thaw to dominate, with slow chemical weathering.
- **R:** hot but very dry, so weathering is **weak** overall. Salt crystal growth and heating/cooling may act locally, especially where dew or groundwater supply moisture, but the Peltier zones do not show these.

**Comment on limitations:** station Q's annual mean does not tell you how often temperatures cross 0°C. A site with many freeze-thaw cycles in spring and autumn could weather faster than one that stays frozen for months, even with the same mean.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain how rock structure affects the rate of weathering." [5]*

> Joints and bedding planes are lines of weakness that let water penetrate the rock **(feature)**. Water in joints allows freeze-thaw where temperatures fluctuate around 0°C, and carries carbonic acid into limestone, widening the joints by carbonation **(processes linked to structure)**. Densely jointed rock has a larger surface area exposed to attack, so it weathers faster than massive rock **(rate)**. Porous rocks such as some sandstones absorb water, making them prone to salt crystal growth and frost shattering **(second feature)**. Coarse-grained rocks such as granite can break down by granular disintegration because their large crystals expand at different rates **(third feature)**.

## Common mistakes

- Defining weathering in a way that includes transport.
- Saying freeze-thaw is greatest where it is coldest; it needs repeated freezing and thawing.
- Writing "carbonation dissolves all rocks"; it attacks carbonate minerals.
- Treating hydration as the same as hydrolysis.
- Reading the Peltier diagram without naming the axes or using the station values.
- Ignoring rock type when explaining weathering in a given climate.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a process: state the conditions it needs, the mechanism and the result for the rock.
- **Describe** a Peltier diagram: name the axes, the zones and where given places lie.
- **Assess** the importance of climate: compare it with rock type, structure, vegetation and relief, and reach a judgement.
