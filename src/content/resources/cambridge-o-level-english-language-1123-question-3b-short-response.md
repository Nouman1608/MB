This guide covers **Question 3b, the Short response task**, in Section B of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 1 Reading. The syllabus says: "Candidates give a short response to show understanding of implicit meanings and attitude in response to Text B." It is worth **5 marks** and tests one objective, **R2**: "demonstrate understanding of implicit meanings and attitudes". The [Paper 1 Reading study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-1-reading/) places the question in the paper as a whole, and the [Question 3a summary guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-question-3a-summary/) covers the task that comes just before it on the same text. This page is about 3b alone: what "attitude" means, how writers reveal it without stating it, and how to write a short response that proves what you claim.

## What makes 3b different from 3a

Both questions use Text B, which the syllabus describes as "approximately 550–600 words long". The two tasks pull in opposite directions:

| | Question 3a Summary | Question 3b Short response |
|---|---|---|
| Objectives | R1 explicit meanings, R5 select and use information, plus W2 and W3 | R2 implicit meanings and attitudes only |
| What you look for | stated points relevant to a focus | what is suggested but not stated |
| What you write | continuous prose, no more than 150 words | a short response; no word limit is set in the syllabus |
| Main risk | copying or adding detail | retelling content instead of interpreting it |

After writing a summary, many candidates keep summarising. In 3b, a sentence that only repeats what the text says, however accurately, shows understanding of explicit meaning, which this question does not test.

## What "implicit meanings and attitudes" covers

- An **implicit meaning** is something the reader works out from clues: a suggestion, an implication, a hint about motive or consequence.
- An **attitude** is a stance towards something: the writer's feelings or opinion about the subject, or the feelings of a person described in the text. An attitude is usually not announced ("I disapprove of...") but built up through language and selection.

So 3b questions are likely to ask about what a writer or a person in the text thinks or feels about something, or what a detail suggests. Read the question to find out **whose** attitude it asks about and **towards what**.

## How writers show attitude without stating it

| Signal | What to look for | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| Connotation | words carrying approval or disapproval beyond their literal meaning | "the old market *sprawls*" against "the old market *spreads out*" |
| Evaluative modifiers | adjectives and adverbs that judge | "a *modest* success", "*predictably* late" |
| Selection of detail | what the writer chooses to include or dwell on | three sentences on litter, one on the new park |
| Omission | what the writer leaves out | benefits of a scheme never mentioned |
| Hedging | words that show doubt or caution | "perhaps", "it seems", "supposedly" |
| Concession | granting a point before turning against it | "Admittedly the view is fine, but..." |
| Irony | saying the opposite of what is meant | "Another triumph of planning: the bus stop faces a wall." |
| Placement | what comes last, or is given its own short paragraph | a final one-line verdict |
| Contrast | two things set side by side so one looks better | the quiet village then, the traffic now |

Most attitudes are **mixed or shifting**. A writer may admire a place but regret what it has become, or be sceptical at first and then won over. A response that notices the mixture, or the change, shows fuller understanding than one that names a single feeling.

## Naming the attitude precisely

Vague labels such as "positive" or "negative" are a start, not an answer. Build a vocabulary of attitude words and choose the one that fits the evidence.

| Vague | More precise options |
|---|---|
| positive | admiring, grateful, affectionate, enthusiastic, proud, approving, hopeful |
| negative | critical, scornful, resentful, disappointed, sceptical, anxious, dismissive |
| mixed | ambivalent, grudgingly respectful, nostalgic but realistic, cautiously optimistic |
| detached | neutral, objective, ironic, wry, amused |

"Nostalgic" says more than "positive", because it explains *why* the writer feels warmly: the thing belongs to the past.

## A four-step method

1. **Find the target.** Underline in the question whose attitude and towards what.
2. **Gather clues.** Re-read Text B for that target only. Mark two or three short phrases with strong connotations, hedges, ironies or concessions.
3. **Name and prove.** For each point: state the attitude in a precise word, quote briefly (a word or short phrase), explain how the wording shows it.
4. **Check for a turn.** Does the attitude change, or is it qualified somewhere? If so, say where and how.

Short quotations work best. A phrase of two to five words lets you point to the exact word doing the work; a long quotation hides it.

## Worked example

The extract below is original, written for this guide in the style of a Text B passage.

> When the town council announced that the old cinema would become a "multi-purpose leisure hub", I
> confess I rolled my eyes. The building had stood empty for years, its velvet seats slowly surrendering
> to damp, and I assumed the hub would be another glass box selling coffee. I was half right. There is
> coffee, and it is expensive. But on a wet Tuesday I found the old screen still in place, and forty
> children sitting cross-legged in front of a puppet show, shrieking with delight. Someone had kept the
> chandelier. I left grudgingly impressed, though I still cannot say "leisure hub" without wincing.

**Question:** *What is the writer's attitude towards the new "leisure hub"?* (practice tariff: **[5]**)

> **Model response.** At first the writer is sceptical and rather scornful: admitting that they "rolled
> my eyes" shows they expected the scheme to be pointless, and they dismiss modern buildings as "another
> glass box". Their complaint that the coffee "is expensive" keeps this critical note. However, their
> attitude shifts to reluctant admiration when they see the old screen in use; the detail of children
> "shrieking with delight" shows they value what the hub offers the community, and noticing that
> "someone had kept the chandelier" suggests gratitude that the building's character survived. By the
> end the attitude is mixed: "grudgingly impressed" concedes the success, while "wincing" at the phrase
> "leisure hub" shows they still dislike the council's jargon.

**Commentary.** The response names several precise attitudes (sceptical, scornful, reluctant admiration, gratitude, mixed), supports each with a short quotation and explains how the wording shows it. It tracks the turn signalled by "But on a wet Tuesday". It never retells the story of the visit for its own sake.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising again**: listing what the hub contains instead of how the writer feels about it.
- **One-word answers**: "positive" with no evidence.
- **Long quotations**: copying whole sentences so the key word is buried.
- **Your opinion**: writing about whether *you* like leisure hubs.
- **Missing the turn**: naming only the opening attitude when the writer changes their mind.
- **Answering about the wrong person**: giving the council's attitude when the question asks for the writer's.

## Exam technique

The syllabus defines **explain** as "set out purposes or reasons / make the relationships between things clear / say why and/or how and support with relevant evidence", and **identify** as "name/select/recognise". If the question asks you to explain, every claim needs evidence and a reason. Keep 3b in proportion: it carries 5 of Section B's 25 marks, so allow it a share of your time after the summary, rather than squeezing it into the final minutes. A useful working rule for a 5-mark response is to make several distinct, supported points, including any change of attitude.
