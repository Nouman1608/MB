## The accounts at a glance

| Account | Records |
|---|---|
| Current | Goods, services, primary income, secondary income |
| Capital | Small capital transfers and non-produced assets |
| Financial | Investment flows: FDI, portfolio investment, bank lending |
| Net errors and omissions | The balancing item |

- Credits are money in. Debits are money out.
- The whole balance of payments sums to zero. "A deficit" normally refers to one account, usually the current account.
- A current account deficit is financed by net inflows on the capital and financial accounts (borrowing, or sales of assets to foreigners).
- Detailed knowledge of the capital and financial accounts is not required.

## Key calculations

| Calculation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Balance on an item | Credits - debits |
| Current account | Goods + services + primary income + secondary income |
| Share of GDP | Balance / GDP × 100 |
| Missing item | Net errors and omissions = -(current + capital + financial) |
| Import fall needed | Fall in income = fall in imports / marginal propensity to import |
| AD cut needed | Fall in income / multiplier |
| Steady-state foreign liabilities | Deficit as % of GDP / nominal growth rate in % |
| Volume response | % change in quantity = elasticity × % change in price |

### Method in steps: a lower currency and the trade balance

1. Write the starting export and import values.
2. Export value in home currency rises by export volume change, since home-currency prices are unchanged.
3. Import value = old value × (1 + price rise) × (1 - volume fall).
4. Compare the new balance with the old one.
5. Comment on elasticities and time lags.

Small reminder: exports 90, imports 100, currency falls 8%. Export PED 1.5, import PED 0.5. Exports = 90 × 1.12 = 100.8. Imports = 100 × 1.08 × 0.96 = 103.68. New balance = -2.88, an improvement of 7.12 from -10.

## Deficits and surpluses

| | Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|---|
| Possible causes | Uncompetitive exports, fast growth at home, strong currency, foreign slowdown | Competitive exports, low home spending, weak currency, foreign boom |
| Significance | Rising foreign claims, income flowing out, confidence risk, leakage from AD | Weak consumption, inflation risk, tension with partners, pressure for a higher currency |
| Less serious when | Small, temporary, financed by stable inflows, imports raise future output | Reflects temporary commodity booms or high saving put to good use |

Judge by size relative to GDP, duration, cause and ease of financing.

## FDI and portfolio investment

| | FDI | Portfolio investment |
|---|---|---|
| What | Real operations abroad with control | Shares and bonds, no control |
| Stability | Long-term, hard to reverse | Can leave quickly |

- **Benefits to the recipient:** capital, technology, jobs, productivity, exports, tax revenue, financing of a deficit.
- **Costs to the recipient:** profit outflows, loss of control, local firms squeezed, volatility from portfolio flows, exchange rate swings.
- **For the sending country:** returns come back as primary income, and firms reach new markets, but investment and jobs may be lost at home.
- Profit outflow = investment × rate of return. For example, 80 million × 7% = 5.6 million a year on primary income debits.

## Policies

| Type | Tools | Mechanism | Side effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expenditure-reducing | Tax rises, spending cuts, dearer borrowing | Lower income, so fewer imports | Lower growth, higher unemployment, lower inflation |
| Expenditure-switching | Lower exchange rate, tariffs, quotas, export subsidies | Move spending from imports to home goods | Imported inflation, retaliation, higher prices |
| Supply-side | Skills, infrastructure, innovation | Improve quality and cost competitiveness | Slow and costly |

- **Elasticities decide effectiveness.** If demand for exports and imports is price elastic, switching works. If inelastic, the import bill can rise more than exports, so the deficit gets worse first. Low supply elasticity limits export growth.
- A formal Marshall-Lerner condition is not required.
- For a surplus, use the reverse: expansionary policy, a higher exchange rate or fewer trade barriers.

### Evaluation chain for any policy

1. Say what it does to the balance of payments, and how.
2. Say what it does to growth, inflation, unemployment and equality.
3. Check the economy's position: spare capacity or overheating?
4. Check elasticities and time lags.
5. Reach a judgement about which policy fits this cause, and why.

## Side effects of correcting a deficit

| Policy | Helps | Hurts |
|---|---|---|
| Higher taxes, lower spending, higher rates | Inflation falls | Growth, employment, investment |
| Lower exchange rate | Exports and jobs | Import prices and inflation, real incomes |
| Tariffs and quotas | Protected home jobs | Prices, choice, efficiency, trade relations |
| Supply-side measures | Growth and productivity | Cost and long delay |

For a surplus, expansionary policy or a higher currency reduces the surplus but can raise inflation or hurt exporters.

## Chains of reasoning to learn

- **Deficit to lost confidence:** persistent deficit, then more borrowing and asset sales, then larger income outflows, then doubts among lenders, then higher interest rates or a falling currency, then lower growth.
- **Spending cut:** higher taxes, lower disposable income, lower consumption and imports, smaller deficit, but lower output and more unemployment.
- **Currency fall:** cheaper exports and dearer imports, then a change in volumes that depends on elasticities, then a change in the balance, with imported inflation as a side effect.
- **FDI inflow:** capital and technology, higher productivity and exports, but profit outflows later.

## Must-know distinctions

- Expenditure-reducing cuts total demand. Expenditure-switching changes where demand goes.
- Deficit on one account versus the whole balance of payments, which always sums to zero.
- FDI (control, long-term) versus portfolio (financial, mobile).
- Short-run elasticities (low) versus long-run elasticities (higher).

## Quick self-test

1. Country R has goods -30, services +12, primary income -9, secondary income +5, all in billions of R-units, and GDP of 400. Find the current account and its size as a percentage of GDP.
2. The capital account is +1 and the financial account is +19. Find net errors and omissions.
3. Give two ways in which FDI differs from portfolio investment.
4. Imports rise 0.25 for each 1 rise in income. Imports must fall by 6. By how much must income fall? With a multiplier of 2, by how much must AD fall?
5. A 20% tariff is placed on imports with price elasticity of demand 0.5. Find the change in import volume. If the pre-tariff import bill was 150, find the new bill before tariff.
6. Classify as expenditure-reducing or expenditure-switching: (a) a rise in interest rates, (b) export subsidies.
7. A deficit equal to 3% of GDP is run each year with nominal growth of 6%. Find the long-run foreign liabilities as a share of GDP.
8. An investor puts 80 million into a plant and earns a 7% return that is sent home. Find the yearly effect on primary income.
9. Give one reason why a currency fall may worsen the trade balance at first.
10. Give one conflict between correcting a deficit by cutting spending and another macroeconomic objective.

### Answers

1. Current account = -30 + 12 - 9 + 5 = **-22**. As a share of GDP: -22 / 400 × 100 = **-5.5%**.
2. -22 + 1 + 19 = -2, so net errors and omissions = **+2**.
3. FDI involves control and a long-term presence, whereas portfolio investment is a financial holding with no control. FDI is harder to reverse, whereas portfolio investment can leave quickly.
4. Income falls by 6 / 0.25 = **24**. AD falls by 24 / 2 = **12**.
5. Volume falls by 0.5 × 20 = **10%**. The bill before tariff is 150 × 0.90 = **135**.
6. (a) Expenditure-reducing. (b) Expenditure-switching.
7. 3 / 6 × 100 = **50% of GDP**.
8. 80 × 0.07 = **5.6 million** a year, a debit on primary income.
9. Short-run demand for imports and exports is inelastic, so the higher import bill outweighs the volume gains.
10. Cutting spending lowers output and raises unemployment, which conflicts with growth and employment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the balance of payments is in deficit" instead of naming the account.
- Giving a policy list with no explanation of how it changes the balance.
- Describing a currency fall as always beneficial, with no mention of elasticities or time lags.
- Using vague terms such as "bad for the economy" instead of a chain: higher claims abroad, more income outflow, lower confidence.
- Treating FDI and portfolio investment as the same, or ignoring profit outflows.
- Forgetting side effects on inflation, growth and unemployment in an evaluation.
- Reaching no judgement. Strong answers weigh size, cause, elasticities and time horizon, then decide.
- Mixing up the sign of a balance or dividing by the wrong total when finding a percentage of GDP.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three main accounts of the balance of payments?**
The current account records goods, services, primary income and secondary income. The capital account records small capital transfers and non-produced assets, and the financial account records investment flows such as FDI, portfolio investment and bank lending.

**How is a current account deficit financed?**
It is financed by net inflows on the capital and financial accounts, through borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners. The whole balance of payments sums to zero once net errors and omissions are included.

**What is the difference between expenditure-reducing and expenditure-switching policies?**
Expenditure-reducing policies cut total spending, so fewer imports are bought. Expenditure-switching policies, such as a lower exchange rate or tariffs, move spending from imports to home-produced goods.

**Do I need the Marshall-Lerner condition for OxfordAQA 9640?**
No. A formal Marshall-Lerner condition is not required, but you must understand how the elasticities of demand and supply affect whether policies to correct a deficit work.

**Is a current account surplus always good?**
No. A persistent surplus can reflect weak domestic consumption, add to inflation when exports boom, create tension with trading partners and push the currency up.
