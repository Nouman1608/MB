This guide covers content item 2.5.3, Effective training and development, in Theme 2 Topic 2.5 Making human resource decisions of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0), Issue 2, July 2022. The page on [training and motivation](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-training-and-motivation/) introduces each method. This page focuses on choosing between methods for a given business and on measuring whether training has worked. Every business and figure is invented.

Companion pages: the [training and development revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-effective-training-and-development-revision-notes/) and the [training and development practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-effective-training-and-development-practice/).

## What 2.5.3 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| How businesses train and develop employees | formal and informal training, self-learning, ongoing training for all employees, use of target setting and performance reviews |
| Why businesses train and develop employees | the link between training, motivation and retention; retraining to use new technology |

The two headings fit together: the first is *how*, the second is *why*. A good answer about a method usually ends with one of the reasons in the second heading.

## Training and development are not the same

Training gives an employee the skills for their present job, such as using a till. Development builds the person for the future, such as preparing a supervisor to become a manager. The specification uses both words without defining them; this distinction is the standard one. Target setting and performance reviews are mainly development tools, because they look at how a person is progressing over time.

## Choosing a training method

Each method suits a different kind of need. Read the case for four facts: what must be learned, how many people need it, how quickly, and how much the business can spend.

| What the case shows | Method that usually fits | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A skill that must be done exactly the same way by everyone, or a legal or safety requirement | Formal training | Structured, consistent and can lead to a recognised certificate |
| A practical task best learned by watching and doing | Informal training | Learned at the workstation from an experienced colleague, at little cost |
| Motivated staff who want to learn at their own pace, or knowledge that is easy to read up on | Self-learning | Cheap and flexible, but harder to check |
| A business where products, methods or rules change often | Ongoing training for all employees | Keeps every employee, not just new starters, up to date |
| Staff whose progress needs direction and checking | Target setting and performance reviews | Shows what is expected and finds gaps in skills |

Methods are usually combined. A new chef might attend a formal food-hygiene course, learn the restaurant's dishes informally from the head chef, and agree targets at a review after three months.

### Target setting and performance reviews working together

These two tools form a cycle:

1. The manager and employee agree targets, such as "handle 40 customer calls a day with no more than two complaints a month".
2. The employee works towards them, with support or training where needed.
3. At a performance review the manager compares results with the targets, gives feedback and identifies skill gaps.
4. Training is arranged to close the gaps, and new targets are set.

A target that is unrealistic, or reviews that only criticise, can lower motivation rather than raise it. The specification asks for the use of these tools, so be ready to explain both the benefit and the risk.

## Why businesses train and develop employees

### The link between training, motivation and retention

The specification links three ideas in one chain:

**training → employees more skilled and feel valued → motivation rises → employees more likely to stay (retention)**

- Skilled employees find the job easier and gain satisfaction from doing it well.
- Being offered training or development shows the business is investing in them and sees a future for them.
- Motivated employees are less likely to leave, so the business keeps their experience and avoids the cost of replacing them.

The chain can break. A business that trains staff well but pays below its rivals may find trained staff are recruited by competitors. In an Evaluate answer, this is the counter-argument to use.

### Worked example: measuring retention at Linmore Hotels

Linmore Hotels employs 80 staff. In one year, 20 left. The business introduced ongoing training for all employees at a cost of £9,000. The next year, 12 left. Replacing each leaver costs about £1,800 in advertising, interviewing and lost output.

A standard way to measure this is the percentage of staff who leave in a year (the formula is not in the specification's Appendix 3, so this is the standard method):

- Before: 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = **25%**.
- After: 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = **15%**, a fall of 10 percentage points.
- Leavers avoided: 20 − 12 = 8, saving 8 × £1,800 = **£14,400**.
- Net benefit: £14,400 − £9,000 = **£5,400**.

Using the figures: the training more than paid for itself in one year. The limitation is that other changes, such as a pay rise or fewer job openings elsewhere, may also have cut the number of leavers, so the whole fall cannot be credited to training.

### Retraining to use new technology

When a business introduces new technology, existing skills can become out of date. Retraining lets current employees use the new equipment or software rather than the business replacing them.

### Worked example: a new booking system at Linmore

Linmore introduces a new online booking system that 25 reception and reservations staff must use. Two options:

- **Formal training for everyone:** the supplier trains each employee for £180, a cost of 25 × £180 = **£4,500**.
- **Train two staff formally, who then train the rest informally:** 2 × £180 = £360, plus 3 hours of colleague training for each of the other 23 staff, costed at £12.50 an hour: 23 × 3 × £12.50 = £862.50. Total **£1,222.50**.

The second option is £3,277.50 cheaper, but the quality depends on how well the two staff teach, and errors in bookings would cost money and upset guests. For a system that every guest's booking passes through, a business might accept the higher cost for consistency. Either recommendation can be justified if it uses the figures and this risk.

Retraining also supports motivation and retention: staff who are not trained on new technology may fear for their jobs or struggle and leave.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (no context): "Ongoing training keeps employees' skills up to date. This means they can cope with changes in products and methods, so the business does not fall behind competitors."
- **Outline**: two linked points about the case, such as cost and consistency at Linmore.
- **Analyse**: follow the chain from method to motivation to retention, using the business.
- **Justify**: choose between two training options with figures for both.
- **Evaluate**: decide whether training is worthwhile and what that depends on, such as pay at rival firms.

### Annotated model answer

*Question (Analyse):* Analyse the impact on Linmore Hotels of introducing ongoing training for all employees.

*Answer:* "Ongoing training means staff keep learning new skills, such as handling complaints, so they feel more confident and valued. As a result they are more motivated and less likely to leave: leavers fell from 25% to 15%. This saved £14,400 in replacement costs, £5,400 more than the training cost, and guests are more likely to be served by experienced staff, which could improve reviews."

*Why it works:* one chain of reasoning, developed step by step, with the case figures used to support it.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "train the staff" without naming the method.
- Treating informal training as no-cost. It takes up experienced staff's time.
- Forgetting that self-learning is hard for the business to check.
- Saying training always improves retention. Trained staff may leave for better pay.
- Mixing up target setting (agreeing goals) with performance reviews (checking progress against them).
- Ignoring the cost of staff time away from work when comparing training options.
