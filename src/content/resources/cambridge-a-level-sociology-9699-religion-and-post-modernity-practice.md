These questions cover sub-topic 12.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699). Paper 4
itself contains only 35-mark essays; Section A here checks the concepts those essays depend on.

## Section A

**1.** Define *privatised religion*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what Hervieu-Léger meant by *cultural amnesia* and how it links to spiritual
shopping. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between *religion online* and *online religion*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **two** reasons why people may join new religious movements. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what Barker's study of the Unification Church suggests about membership of NRMs. **[3]**

**6.** Identify Heelas's **two** core features of New Age thought and explain one of them. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the *disengagement* explanation for the growth of fundamentalism. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which cultural defence differs from marginality as an explanation of
fundamentalism. **[4]**

## Section B

**9.** "Spiritual shopping shows that religion is changing rather than declining." Evaluate this
view. **[35]**

**10.** "Resacralisation is a defining feature of post-modern societies." Evaluate this view.
**[35]**

**11.** "New religious movements and New Age ideas are of little significance for understanding
religion today." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "The growth of fundamentalist religions is best explained as a response to
globalisation." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Religion practised and believed privately by individuals [1] rather than through public
institutions such as churches, so it becomes a personal matter of meaning (Luckmann) [1].

**2.** Religion was once passed down through families and communities as a "chain of memory" [1];
cultural amnesia is the breaking of that chain [1], so people no longer inherit a fixed religion
and instead assemble beliefs for themselves, which is spiritual shopping [1].

**3.** Religion online: organisations use the internet to send information to followers [1], a
top-down extension of traditional authority [1]. Online religion: users create religious practice
and community interactively [1], which allows self-directed, privatised belief [1].

**4.** Any two, 2 marks each:
- Marginality or relative deprivation: those on the edges of society seek status, community or
  hope (world-rejecting movements).
- Social change and anomie: rapid change weakens norms, and movements offer certainty.
- Search for self-improvement: successful people seek techniques for fulfilment (world-affirming
  movements).

**5.** Barker found that most people who attended recruitment workshops did not join [1], which
challenges claims that members are brainwashed [1] and suggests that membership is an active
choice even in demanding groups [1].

**6.** Self-spirituality and detraditionalisation [1 for both]. Self-spirituality: the sacred is
found within the self rather than in an external God [1], so spiritual practice aims at inner
development [1]. Or detraditionalisation: personal experience replaces external authority [1],
so individuals judge truth for themselves [1].

**7.** Believers see the wider society as corrupt or secular [1] and withdraw from it into
separate communities [1], so fundamentalism grows as a way of protecting a distinct moral way of
life [1].

**8.** Any two, 2 marks each:
- Cultural defence centres on group identity against an external threat, while marginality
  centres on individual or class deprivation.
- Cultural defence can unite all classes in a threatened group, while marginality predicts
  support mainly from the poor and excluded.
- Cultural defence is often linked to nationalism, while marginality is linked to Weber's theodicy
  of disprivilege.

**9.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Luckmann's invisible religion; Hervieu-Léger's cultural amnesia; Roof's spiritual marketplace.
- Kendal Project: holistic spirituality growing.
- Lyon: media and consumer culture create new forms rather than ending religion.

**Against the view:**
- Bruce: pick-and-mix belief is weakly held, has little effect on behaviour and is rarely passed
  on, so it is secularisation in another form.
- Kendal Project: holistic growth was far too small to replace congregational decline.
- Shopping may describe affluent Western groups, not religion worldwide.

**Evaluation:** "changing rather than declining" may be a false choice: change in form can
accompany decline in intensity. Judge by level (individual versus societal) and by place.

**10.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Pentecostal and charismatic growth (Martin, 1990); NRMs and New Age; fundamentalism.
- Post-modern uncertainty and the collapse of metanarratives create demand for meaning (Bauman,
  Giddens).
- Religion's public and political role in many societies.

**Against the view:**
- Much growth is in societies undergoing modernisation, not post-modernity.
- In Western Europe institutional decline continues; alternative spirituality remains small.
- Revival may show secularisation was never universal, rather than a new post-modern trend.

**Evaluation:** "defining" is the key word; resacralisation is a significant trend in places, but
not a universal feature.

**11.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Membership of NRMs and New Age groups is small; Niebuhr predicts many sects are short-lived.
- Bruce: New Age belief is weakly held and individualistic, with little social impact.

**Against the view:**
- Significance lies in what they reveal: persistent demand for meaning; a shift from obedience to
  self-development (Heelas).
- Barker shows active choice in membership.
- Wallis's typology links types of movement to social change and marginality, explaining wider
  religious trends.

**Evaluation:** distinguish size from sociological significance.

**12.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Giddens: fundamentalism is "tradition defended in the traditional way", a reaction to the
  uncertainty globalisation brings.
- Castells (1997): resistance identity among groups who feel excluded or threatened by global
  change.
- Bruce: outside the West, fundamentalism often reacts to change imposed from outside, which
  links to cultural defence.
- Fundamentalist movements use global media to spread their message, showing they are shaped by
  globalisation as well as opposing it.

**Against the view, using the syllabus explanations:**
- Cultural transition: migration is part of globalisation, but the need it meets is local (coping
  in a new community).
- Marginality: deprivation and exclusion can drive support with or without global change
  (theodicy of disprivilege).
- Disengagement: withdrawal from a society seen as corrupt can respond to internal secular
  culture rather than to globalisation.
- Religious revival: renewal movements recur throughout history, long before contemporary
  globalisation.
- Bauman: the anxiety of post-modern choice, not globalisation as such.

**Evaluation:** globalisation is a strong framing explanation because it connects several of the
syllabus explanations, but "best" needs testing against specific cases; define fundamentalism
and judge which explanation fits which movement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Wallis's typology needed in every NRM essay?** It is useful, but always connect it to why
NRMs matter for understanding religion today.

**Do I need to name all five fundamentalism explanations?** The syllabus lists them all, so a
strong essay uses each, judging where it fits best.
