## 4.10.4.1 The Haber process

The **Haber process** manufactures **ammonia**, which is used to produce **nitrogen-based fertilisers**.

### Raw materials

| Raw material | Source |
|---|---|
| **Nitrogen** | **The air** (about 80% nitrogen), separated by fractional distillation of liquid air |
| **Hydrogen** | **Natural gas** (methane), reacted with steam |

### The reaction

The purified gases are passed over a **catalyst of iron** at a **high temperature (about 450 °C)** and a **high
pressure (about 200 atmospheres)**. Some of the hydrogen and nitrogen reacts to form ammonia. The reaction is
**reversible**, so some of the ammonia breaks down again:

**N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)**

On **cooling**, the ammonia **liquefies** and is removed. The remaining **hydrogen and nitrogen are recycled**
back into the reactor, so none is wasted.

### Process summary

1. Nitrogen and hydrogen are mixed in a 1 : 3 ratio by volume, purified and compressed to about 200 atmospheres.
2. The mixture passes over an iron catalyst at about 450 °C.
3. The gas leaving the reactor contains ammonia, nitrogen and hydrogen.
4. It is cooled in a condenser; ammonia liquefies (it has a higher boiling point than nitrogen and hydrogen).
5. Liquid ammonia is run off. Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen go back to the reactor.

### Worked example 1: volumes of gases

*100 dm³ of nitrogen reacts completely. What volume of hydrogen is needed and what volume of ammonia forms (all
gases at the same temperature and pressure)?*

```
N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3   (1 : 3 : 2 by volume)
hydrogen = 3 x 100 = 300 dm3
ammonia  = 2 x 100 = 200 dm3
```

The total volume falls from 400 dm³ to 200 dm³, which is why high pressure favours ammonia.

### Higher tier only: conditions, rate and equilibrium

You must be able to **interpret graphs of reaction conditions versus rate**, **apply the principles of dynamic
equilibrium** to the process, **explain the trade-off** between rate and position of equilibrium, and **explain
how the conditions are related to the availability and cost** of raw materials and energy, and control of the
equilibrium position and rate.

**Pressure.** There are 4 gas molecules on the left and 2 on the right. A higher pressure shifts the position to
the right, so a **higher yield**. A higher pressure also increases the **rate** (more frequent collisions). But
high pressure needs **strong, expensive equipment** and a lot of energy to compress the gases. About **200
atmospheres** is a compromise.

**Temperature.** The forward reaction is **exothermic**. A **lower** temperature shifts the position to the
right, giving a **higher yield**. But a lower temperature makes the reaction **too slow**. About **450 °C** is a
**compromise**: a reasonable rate with a reasonable yield. This is the **trade-off** between rate and position of
equilibrium.

**Catalyst.** The iron catalyst **increases the rate** and lets a lower temperature be used. It **does not change
the position of equilibrium** or the yield. It provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

**Removing ammonia and recycling.** Removing ammonia by liquefying it shifts the equilibrium to the right, and
**recycling** unreacted gases gives a high overall conversion without waste.

### Worked example 2: interpreting data (illustrative)

The table shows the percentage of ammonia at equilibrium. The numbers are invented to show the trends.

| Temperature / °C | 100 atm | 200 atm | 300 atm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 350 | 40 | 55 | 63 |
| 450 | 16 | 28 | 38 |
| 550 | 6 | 11 | 17 |

1. At any pressure, the percentage **falls as temperature rises**: the forward reaction is **exothermic**.
2. At any temperature, the percentage **rises with pressure**: fewer gas molecules on the right.
3. The highest yield is at **350 °C and 300 atm**, but the rate at 350 °C is very slow, and 300 atm is costly.
4. The commercial conditions (**450 °C, 200 atm**) give a yield of about 28% per pass in this table. The
   remainder is **recycled**, so the overall use of reactants is high.

## 4.10.4.2 Production and uses of NPK fertilisers

Compounds of **nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium** are used as **fertilisers** to improve **agricultural
productivity**. **NPK fertilisers** contain compounds of **all three** elements. They are **formulations**: mixtures
of various salts in carefully chosen percentages.

| Element | Why plants need it |
|---|---|
| **N** (nitrogen) | Growth of leaves and proteins |
| **P** (phosphorus) | Root growth and energy transfer |
| **K** (potassium) | Healthy flowers, fruit and disease resistance |

### Making the ingredients

Industrial production uses a variety of raw materials in several **integrated processes**.

- **Ammonia** (from the Haber process) is used to make **ammonium salts** and **nitric acid**.
  - ammonia + nitric acid → **ammonium nitrate**: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃
  - ammonia + sulfuric acid → **ammonium sulfate**: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄
- **Potassium chloride**, **potassium sulfate** and **phosphate rock** are obtained by **mining**.
- **Phosphate rock cannot be used directly** as a fertiliser, because it is **insoluble**, so plants cannot absorb
  it. It is treated with acid to produce **soluble salts**:

| Phosphate rock treated with | Products |
|---|---|
| **Nitric acid** | Phosphoric acid and calcium nitrate |
| **Sulfuric acid** | Calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate |
| **Phosphoric acid** | Calcium phosphate |

### Worked example 3: percentage of nitrogen

*Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, and in ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄ (Ar: H 1,
N 14, O 16, S 32).*

```
NH4NO3:    Mr = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80;      %N = 28 / 80  x 100 = 35 %
(NH4)2SO4: Mr = 36 + 32 + 64    = 132;      %N = 28 / 132 x 100 = 21.2 %
```

Ammonium nitrate has a higher percentage of nitrogen, which is better value for farmers where nitrogen is the
need.

### Industrial and laboratory production compared

| | Industry | Laboratory |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | Very large (thousands of tonnes) | Small (grams) |
| Process | **Continuous**, integrated processes | **Batch**: titration, evaporation, crystallisation |
| Raw materials | Ammonia from the Haber process, nitric acid made on site | Bought solutions |
| Purity and cost | Lower cost per tonne, tight control | Pure crystals, higher cost per gram |
| Energy | Heat recovered and reused | Heating on a hot plate |

A laboratory preparation of ammonium sulfate: neutralise ammonia solution with dilute sulfuric acid, using a
titration with indicator to find the volumes needed, then repeat without the indicator, evaporate some of the
water and leave to crystallise.

### Problems of fertilisers (extension)

Excess fertiliser washes into rivers and lakes (**eutrophication**), where algae grow, die and use up oxygen. This
is not required in 4.10.4 but helps in evaluation questions.

## Exam technique

- Give the **source** of each raw material: nitrogen from air, hydrogen from natural gas.
- Quote the numbers: **iron, about 450 °C, about 200 atmospheres**.
- Mention that the reaction is **reversible** and unreacted gases are **recycled**.
- At Higher tier, for each condition give the effect on **yield** and on **rate**, then say it is a **compromise**.
- For NPK, name the three elements and say that phosphate rock is insoluble so must be treated with acid.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the catalyst increases the yield.
- Saying a high temperature increases yield (it lowers it for an exothermic forward reaction).
- Forgetting the recycle of nitrogen and hydrogen.
- Saying hydrogen comes from water or air in the Haber process; it comes from natural gas.
- Writing NH₃ as the fertiliser; the fertilisers are ammonium salts.
- Saying phosphate rock is used directly.
