Condensed recall for **9.3 Responding to global climate change** (Unit 2: Global climate --
vulnerability and resilience). For the full explanation, read the
[Global Climate study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-global-climate/).

## Adaptation versus mitigation

| | Mitigation | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Target | The **causes** of climate change | The **effects** of climate change |
| Aim | Reduce emissions | Deliberate change in response to actual or expected impacts |
| Examples | Reducing greenhouse gas emissions | Flood defences, drought-resistant crops |

Never use the two terms interchangeably.

More examples to quote:

| Mitigation | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Switching from coal to renewable electricity | The Netherlands' Room for the River programme |
| Energy-efficient buildings and transport | Raised homes and plinths in flood-prone Bangladesh |
| Protecting and planting forests | Drought-tolerant seed varieties and water harvesting in the Sahel |
| Carbon capture and storage | Hulhumalé, a raised artificial island built by the Maldives |

Some actions do both: restoring mangroves stores carbon (mitigation) and protects coasts from storm
surges (adaptation).

## Government-led responses

- Global geopolitical efforts, recognising that sources of emissions may be spatially distant from the
  countries most affected.
- Carbon emissions **offsetting and trading**.
- **Technology**, including geo-engineering.

### Global geopolitical efforts: timeline

| Year | Agreement or meeting | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1992 | UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (Rio Earth Summit) | Set the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities" |
| 1997 | Kyoto Protocol | Binding targets for developed countries only; in force from 2005; the USA never ratified it |
| 2009 | Copenhagen summit | Widely seen as a failure to agree a binding successor |
| 2015 | Paris Agreement | Every party sets its own nationally determined contribution; aim to keep warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C; targets reviewed and strengthened every five years |
| 2023 | COP28, Dubai | First agreement calling for a transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems |

The USA withdrew from the Paris Agreement under President Trump, rejoined in 2021 and announced a
second withdrawal in 2025, showing how national politics affects global efforts. Evaluation: Paris
includes everyone but its targets are voluntary, and current pledges are not enough to meet its goals.

### Carbon trading and offsetting

- **Cap and trade:** the EU Emissions Trading System (from 2005) caps total emissions from power
  stations and heavy industry and lets firms buy and sell allowances. Early over-allocation of permits
  kept prices low; later reforms tightened the cap.
- **Carbon tax:** a direct price per tonne of carbon dioxide, used for example in Sweden since 1991.
- **Offsetting:** paying for emission cuts elsewhere, such as forest protection, under the Kyoto
  Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism or voluntary markets. Critics question whether the cuts are
  real, permanent and additional (would they have happened anyway?).

### Technology and geo-engineering

| Type | Examples | Concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide removal | Afforestation, direct air capture, bioenergy with carbon capture | Expensive and unproven at scale; may compete for land |
| Solar radiation management | Injecting reflective aerosols into the stratosphere, mimicking a large volcanic eruption | Does not stop ocean acidification; could alter rainfall; sudden stopping would cause rapid warming; who decides? |

## Civil society and corporate strategies

The IB guide asks for a case study of one country's response to climate change, focusing on the
actions of **non-governmental stakeholders**.

Non-governmental stakeholders include NGOs, youth movements, scientists, courts acting on citizens'
claims, businesses, investors, faith groups and local communities.

- **Youth and protest movements:** Fridays for Future grew from Greta Thunberg's school strike in
  Sweden in 2018; Extinction Rebellion began in the UK the same year.
- **Corporate action:** company net-zero pledges, commitments to 100 per cent renewable electricity
  and investor pressure to divest from fossil fuels. Watch for **greenwashing**, where claims exceed
  real action.

**Case-study outline: the Netherlands.**

- The **Urgenda Foundation**, an NGO, sued the Dutch state; in 2019 the Supreme Court upheld an order
  for emissions to be cut by at least 25 per cent from 1990 levels by the end of 2020.
- The environmental group **Milieudefensie** sued Shell; a district court ordered deep emission cuts in
  2021, but an appeal court overturned the order in 2024 while confirming that companies have a
  responsibility to reduce emissions.
- Businesses, cities and water boards also act on adaptation, building on centuries of flood
  management.
- Evaluation: litigation forced government action, but corporate cases show the limits of courts, and
  some groups, such as farmers opposing nitrogen rules, have pushed back against environmental policy.

## Disparities in exposure and vulnerability

Differences in climate risk relate to location, wealth, social differences such as age, gender and
education, and risk perception. The guide asks for detailed examples of **two or more societies with
contrasting vulnerability**.

A useful pair is the Netherlands and Bangladesh: both are low-lying deltas, but the Netherlands has
the wealth and institutions to fund advanced defences, while Bangladesh relies more on community
preparedness and international finance.

## Evaluating a response

Ask whether it targets cause or effect, who pays, who benefits, how long it lasts and whether it is
fair between richer and poorer places.

## Exam traps

- Mixing up adaptation and mitigation.
- Describing responses with no named place or stakeholder.
- Forgetting that responders and those most affected can be in different countries.
- Offering technology as a complete answer.
- Treating the Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement as the same kind of agreement.
- Using a government policy as the case study when the question asks for non-governmental stakeholders.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish adaptation from mitigation.
2. Give an example of each.
3. Name two government-led responses.
4. What should a civil society case study focus on?
5. Which factors explain disparities in climate risk?
6. How did the Paris Agreement differ from the Kyoto Protocol?
7. How does a cap-and-trade system work?
8. Give one criticism of carbon offsetting.
9. Name one carbon dioxide removal method and one solar radiation management method.
10. What did the Urgenda case achieve?
11. Give one action that is both mitigation and adaptation.

**Answers:** 1. Mitigation reduces the causes; adaptation manages the effects. 2. For example reducing
emissions (mitigation) and flood defences (adaptation). 3. For example emissions trading and
geo-engineering technology. 4. Actions of non-governmental stakeholders in one country. 5. Location,
wealth, age, gender, education and risk perception. 6. Kyoto set binding targets for developed
countries only; Paris asks every party to set its own contribution. 7. A cap limits total emissions and
firms trade allowances within it. 8. The cuts may not be real, permanent or additional. 9. For example
direct air capture; stratospheric aerosol injection. 10. A court order requiring the Dutch state to cut
emissions by at least 25 per cent from 1990 levels by the end of 2020. 11. Restoring mangroves.
