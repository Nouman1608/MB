This set covers section 10.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: how
groups are represented, the media and gender socialisation, moral panics, and the media and
popular culture. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *stereotype*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between under-representation and misrepresentation. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what Hall meant by *inferential racism*. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what Connell meant by *hegemonic masculinity*. **[2]**

**5.** Identify **three** of Goode and Ben-Yehuda's criteria of a moral panic. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** way in which older people are represented in the media. **[3]**

**7.** Explain what Adorno and Horkheimer meant by the *culture industry*. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by *othering*, with an example. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which the media may contribute to gender socialisation. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why the concept of moral panic may be less useful in today's
media. **[8]**

**11.** "The media represent social groups in stereotypical ways." Evaluate this view with
reference to class and ethnicity. **[35]**

**12.** "Popular culture keeps audiences passive." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** A simplified, generalised image of a group [1] applied to all its members regardless of
individual differences [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Under-representation means a group appears less often in the media than its share of the
population [1]. Misrepresentation means a group does appear but in distorted or narrow roles [1];
for example, older women may be both under-represented and, when shown, misrepresented as frail
[1]. **[3]**

**3.** Racist assumptions built into apparently neutral reporting [1], unlike overt racism, which
openly states racist views [1]; for example, coverage that treats the presence of minority groups
as itself a social problem [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The dominant, culturally idealised form of masculinity in a society [1], associated with
strength, control and heterosexuality, against which other masculinities are judged [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any three: concern [1]; hostility [1]; consensus [1]; disproportionality [1];
volatility [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Identify [1], explain [1], develop or example [1]: older people are under-represented [1],
appearing less often than their share of the population, especially older women [1]; when they do
appear they are often stereotyped as frail or dependent, though advertising sometimes shows an
"active, affluent pensioner" [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The standardised production of entertainment by capitalist industries [1], producing
formulaic products for profit [1], which Adorno and Horkheimer argued keeps audiences passive and
distracted from inequality [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Presenting a group as fundamentally different from "us" [1], often as a problem or threat
[1]; for example, Poole's analysis of British press coverage that framed Muslims through conflict
and cultural difference [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study or concept [1],
evaluate or example [1].
- **Role models and repeated images** [1]: media repeatedly show men and women in particular roles
  [1], teaching norms such as hegemonic masculinity (Connell) [1]; but audiences may reject these
  images, especially as representations diversify [1].
- **Advertising and body ideals** [1]: advertisements present idealised, sexualised images of
  women [1]; Kilbourne's *Killing Us Softly* analysed how these treat women's bodies as objects [1];
  girls may judge themselves against them, linking to effects in 10.3 [1].
- Also creditable: Butler's performativity — media supply scripts for doing gender; online media
  spreading alternative images. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example [1].
- **Folk devils can answer back** [1]: in a multi-mediated world, groups have their own media and
  supporters [1]; McRobbie and Thornton (1995) argued this weakens the one-way model [1]; for
  example, a youth subculture can respond on social media [1].
- **Fragmented audiences reduce consensus** [1]: with many outlets, there is less agreement that a
  threat is real [1], so the consensus criterion (Goode and Ben-Yehuda) is harder to meet [1];
  panics may be confined to some audiences [1].
- Also creditable: some groups welcome hostile coverage as publicity; panics are so frequent they
  lose impact. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level uses concepts (stereotype, under-representation, othering,
normalisation), applies them to both class and ethnicity, and evaluates change and audience
response. Indicative content:
- **Class:** middle class normalised; working class as comic, criminal or dependent; *Benefits
  Street* (2014) criticised as "poverty porn"; Tyler (2013) on social abjection; Marxist view that
  such images legitimate inequality.
- **Ethnicity:** Hall (1981) on overt and inferential racism; Poole (2002) on Muslims; Hall et al.
  (1978) on mugging; under-representation in positions of authority.
- **Against or qualifying:** representations have diversified in some media; minority producers and
  online media create alternative images; audiences read texts actively (Fiske); pluralists argue
  content reflects audience demand.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish media types (news versus drama); stereotypes may be decreasing but
  persist in news framing; effects depend on audience reading.
- **Judgement:** for example, stereotyping is still common, especially in news, though less
  uniform than in the past. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level contrasts the culture industry thesis with active-audience
and postmodern views and considers class and global dimensions. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Adorno and Horkheimer's culture industry — standardised, formulaic products
  that distract from inequality; commercial concentration (link to 9.1); passive consumption of
  streaming and social feeds.
- **Against the view:** Fiske (1989) on resistant readings; fan cultures and participatory
  production; Bourdieu shows the high/popular distinction is itself a class judgement; postmodern
  blurring of high and popular culture; global popular culture such as South Korean pop shows
  creative, non-Western production.
- **Evaluation:** activity does not equal power — audiences can reinterpret but rarely control
  production; varies by audience group and medium.
- **Judgement:** for example, popular culture is produced industrially but consumed actively, so
  "passive" overstates the case. **[35]**
