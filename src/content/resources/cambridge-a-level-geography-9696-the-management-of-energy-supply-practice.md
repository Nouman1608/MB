Places and data in resource questions are fictional; other questions ask for your own case studies of a country's strategy and a named scheme. Revise first with the [12.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-energy-supply/) and the [12.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-energy-supply-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "electrical energy strategy". **[2]**

**2.** Outline **two** factors that influence the choice of site for a nuclear power station. **[4]**

**3.** The table shows electricity supply in Velmont, a fictional HIC, in TWh.

| Source | 2005 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | 120 | 5 |
| Gas | 152 | 110 |
| Nuclear | 70 | 45 |
| Wind | 3 | 95 |
| Solar | 0 | 20 |
| Imports | 5 | 15 |
| Total (equal to demand) | 350 | 290 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in total demand from 2005 to 2025. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in Velmont's electricity supply. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** issues that these changes may create for Velmont. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how the location of power production changes when a country replaces inland coal power stations with offshore wind farms. **[4]**

**5.** A fictional offshore wind farm has a Contract for Difference with a strike price of 90 currency units per MWh. In one year it generates 2 000 000 MWh, and the average market price is 60 units per MWh.

**(a)** Calculate the extra payment the wind farm receives for the year. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why governments use contracts of this kind to manage energy supply. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** With reference to one country you have studied, describe the changes in its demand for electricity and explain their causes. **[6]**

**7.** With reference to one named scheme to produce electricity, explain the reasons for its location. **[6]**

**8.** Read the information about a fictional proposal.

> An energy company plans a 600 MW solar farm on 1 100 ha of farmland near Ashbury, a village of 2 000 people. A farmer who rents part of the land supports it because the company will pay more than farming earns. Villagers fear the loss of open views and two years of construction traffic. A wildlife trust says the scheme could include new hedgerows and meadows between panels. The grid operator warns that the nearest substation can accept only 400 MW until it is upgraded in five years.

Assess whether the scheme should go ahead as proposed. **[8]**

**9.** Explain why the success of a power station can be judged differently by different groups of people. **[5]**

**10.** "No country's electricity strategy can achieve energy security, affordability and lower emissions at the same time." With reference to one country you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A country's overall plan [1] for how its electricity will be produced, transmitted and used, including the mix of sources and targets such as emissions cuts [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A large, reliable supply of cooling water, so most sites are on the coast or large rivers [1], because reactors produce a great deal of waste heat [1]. Stable geology with low earthquake and flood risk [1], to reduce the danger of an accident [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit access to the grid, distance from large populations, or an existing nuclear site with local acceptance, each with a reason.

**3. (a)** (290 − 350) ÷ 350 × 100 [1] = −17.1%, a fall of about 17% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Coal almost disappeared, falling from 120 TWh to 5 TWh [1]. Wind rose from 3 TWh to 95 TWh and solar from zero to 20 TWh [1]. Gas and nuclear both fell, gas from 152 to 110 TWh and nuclear from 70 to 45 TWh [1]. Imports tripled from 5 to 15 TWh, while total supply fell by 60 TWh [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** Wind and solar output varies, so Velmont needs storage or back-up for calm, dark periods [1], which keeps gas plants open or raises costs [1]. Higher imports increase dependence on neighbouring countries [1], which reduces energy security if supplies are interrupted or prices rise [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Coal stations were built inland near coalfields or along rivers for fuel supply and cooling water [1]. Offshore wind farms are located at sea where winds are strongest and steadiest [1], so generation shifts to the coast and to particular sea areas [1]. New transmission links are needed from coastal landing points to inland demand centres [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** (90 − 60) × 2 000 000 [1] = 60 000 000 currency units [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The guaranteed price reduces the risk to investors [1], so large, expensive low-carbon projects can raise finance [1]. Governments can steer the energy mix towards chosen technologies to meet emissions targets [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Credit any country. Example for the UK: demand has fallen since the mid-2000s [1]. More efficient lighting and appliances reduce use per household [1]. The decline of heavy industry removed large industrial users [1]. Higher prices encourage households and firms to use less [1]. Demand is now expected to rise again [1] as heat pumps and electric vehicles replace gas boilers and petrol cars [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Credit any named scheme. Example for Hinkley Point C: it is on an existing nuclear site beside Hinkley Point A and B [1], where land is already in energy use and local people are used to the industry [1]. It is on the coast of the Bristol Channel [1], which provides large volumes of cooling water [1]. An existing grid connection point could be reinforced to carry its output [1], and the site has firm foundations and can be protected from flooding [1]. **[6]**

**8.** For: 600 MW of low-carbon electricity would help meet national targets [1], and the farmer and landowner gain a higher, more secure income [1]. The wildlife trust's suggestion could increase biodiversity compared with arable farming [1]. Against: the substation can take only 400 MW for five years, so a third of the capacity could not be used [1]. Villagers face lost views and construction traffic, with few direct benefits [1], and 1 100 ha of food-producing land would be lost [1]. On balance it should go ahead only if modified, for example built in phases to match grid capacity [1], with habitat planting and community benefits for Ashbury [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Different groups bear different costs and benefits [1]. Government and the energy company judge success by output, emissions savings and security of supply [1]. Consumers judge it by the price they pay [1]. Local people judge it by jobs, disruption and risk near their homes [1]. Environmental groups judge it by its effects on habitats, waste and emissions over its lifetime [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define energy security, affordability and emissions reduction [1], and name the country, for example the UK [1].

*Agree:* the UK cut emissions by removing coal [1], but relied more on gas, so the 2021–22 gas price rise raised bills [1]. Subsidies and contracts for low-carbon power add to consumer costs [1]. Wind farms are paid to switch off when the grid is constrained, adding cost [1]. Nuclear replacement has been delayed, threatening future security [1]. In Germany, closing nuclear before coal kept emissions higher than they might have been [1].

*Disagree:* the cost of offshore wind has fallen, so low-carbon power can be cheap [1]. Domestic renewables reduce dependence on imported fuel, improving security [1]. Interconnectors share supply between countries, improving security without new fossil plants [1]. Countries rich in HEP, such as Norway, combine all three goals [1]. Efficiency measures that reduce demand help all three goals at once [1].

*Analysis:* trade-offs are sharpest during the transition, when old and new systems overlap [1]. The balance depends on resource endowment [1]. Short-term costs may bring long-term gains [1].

*Use of case study:* specific evidence from the strategy, with dates and places [1], linked to at least one named scheme within it [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion on "how far", for example agreeing for the transition period but not as a permanent rule [1]. A clear final view [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest essays use the three goals as criteria in every paragraph rather than discussing them separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using MW and TWh interchangeably.
- Describing supply changes without figures from both years.
- Location answers that name a site but give no reasons.
- "Assess" answers with no final recommendation.
- Essays based on a scheme when the question asks about a national strategy.
