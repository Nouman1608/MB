These notes condense sub-topic 1(a) States of matter (points 1.1 to 1.7C) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). Points 1.1 to 1.4 can appear on Paper 1 and Paper 2; points 1.5C to 1.7C carry a C reference and are examined on Paper 2 only. For full explanations and worked examples use the [states of matter study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-states-of-matter/), then try the [states of matter practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-states-of-matter-practice/).

## The particle model in one table (1.1)

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Touching, regular, fixed positions | Touching, irregular | Far apart, random |
| Movement | Vibrate about fixed positions | Slide past one another | Fast, random, all directions |
| Energy | Lowest | Intermediate | Highest |
| Forces between particles | Strongest | Weaker | Very weak |
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape and volume | Takes container's shape; fixed volume | Fills the container |
| Compressible? | No | No | Yes: large spaces between particles |

## Changes of state (1.2)

| Change | Name | Achieved by | Particle change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid → liquid | Melting | Heating | Energy taken in; some forces overcome; particles can move past one another |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing | Cooling | Energy given out; particles settle into fixed positions |
| Liquid → gas | Boiling (throughout, at the boiling point) or evaporating (surface, below it) | Heating | Energy taken in; remaining forces overcome; particles move far apart |
| Gas → liquid | Condensing | Cooling | Energy given out; particles move close together |
| Solid → gas | Sublimation | Heating | Particles leave the solid directly |

**Flat sections on heating and cooling curves.** While a pure substance changes state, its temperature stays constant. On heating, the energy is used to overcome forces of attraction. On cooling, energy released as forces form balances the heat lost.

### Method: state at a given temperature

```
temperature < melting point            -> solid
melting point < temperature < boiling point -> liquid
temperature > boiling point            -> gas
```

Watch negative values: a boiling point of −34 °C means a gas at 25 °C.

## Dilution and diffusion (1.3)

| Experiment | Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Repeated dilution of potassium manganate(VII) solution | Colour fades but is visible after several dilutions | A crystal contains a very large number of very small particles |
| Bromine vapour under a jar of air | Brown colour spreads through both jars without stirring | Gas particles move randomly and mix by diffusion |
| Same experiment, but the top jar is a vacuum | Colour fills the top jar almost at once | Air particles normally get in the way (collisions slow mixing) |
| Ammonia and hydrochloric acid at opposite ends of a tube | White ring of ammonium chloride forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end | Lighter particles (NH₃, Mr 17) diffuse faster than heavier ones (HCl, Mr 36.5) |

Diffusion is faster at higher temperature (particles have more energy) and for gases of lower relative molecular mass.

Dilution arithmetic: each "1 cm³ made up to 10 cm³" step divides the concentration by 10, so n steps leave 1/10ⁿ of the original.

## Key terms (1.4, 1.5C)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solvent | The liquid in which a substance dissolves |
| Solute | The substance that dissolves |
| Solution | The mixture formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent |
| Saturated solution | A solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature |
| Solubility (Paper 2 only) | The mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a stated temperature, in g per 100 g of solvent |
| Diffusion | Net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration, caused by random motion |
| Sublimation | Change from solid directly to gas |

## Solubility curves (1.6C) — Paper 2 only

- Axes: temperature (°C) on x; solubility (g per 100 g water) on y. Smooth curve of best fit.
- On the curve = saturated; below = unsaturated.
- Crystals formed on cooling = (solubility at high T − solubility at low T) × mass of water ÷ 100.
- To find when crystals first appear: convert the actual solution to g per 100 g of water, then read the temperature where the curve reaches that value.

Reminder: 15 g of solute in 25 g of water is 15 × 100 ÷ 25 = 60 g per 100 g of water.

## Solubility practical (1.7C) — Paper 2 only

```
mass of solid    = (basin + dry solid) - basin
mass of water    = (basin + solution) - (basin + dry solid)
solubility       = mass of solid / mass of water x 100   (g per 100 g water)
```

Checks: excess solid present (saturated); temperature held constant in a water bath; only clear solution taken; heated to constant mass.

## Exam traps

- "Particles in a solid don't move" loses the mark: they vibrate.
- "No energy goes in while melting" is wrong: energy goes in but overcomes forces.
- Solubility given without a temperature, or in g/dm³, is not the specification's unit.
- Dividing by the mass of solution instead of the mass of water.
- In the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube, writing that the heavier gas travels faster.
- Mixing up evaporation (surface, any temperature) with boiling (throughout, at the boiling point).

## Self-test

1. Give two differences between the particles in a liquid and in a gas.
2. Explain why a gas can be compressed but a liquid cannot.
3. Name the change of state when iodine crystals turn directly into a purple vapour.
4. A substance melts at −7 °C and boils at 59 °C. State its physical state at 25 °C and at 80 °C.
5. Explain why the temperature of a pure liquid stays the same while it boils, even though it is still being heated.
6. Describe how the movement and energy of particles change when steam condenses.
7. A solution is diluted by taking 1 cm³ and making it up to 10 cm³, four times. What fraction of the original concentration remains?
8. Give one change that would make bromine vapour diffuse faster, and explain why.
9. Explain why a white ring forms nearer one end of a tube with ammonia at one end and hydrogen chloride at the other.
10. State what is meant by a saturated solution.
11. (Paper 2 only) 18 g of a salt dissolves in 40 g of water at 50 °C to make a saturated solution. Calculate its solubility at 50 °C.
12. (Paper 2 only) A salt's solubility is 80 g per 100 g water at 70 °C and 30 g per 100 g water at 20 °C. Calculate the mass of crystals formed when a saturated solution in 25 g of water is cooled from 70 °C to 20 °C.

### Answers

1. Liquid particles are close together; gas particles are far apart. Liquid particles slide past one another; gas particles move quickly in all directions (gas particles also have more energy).
2. Gas particles have large spaces between them, so they can be pushed closer; liquid particles are already touching.
3. Sublimation.
4. Liquid at 25 °C (between −7 and 59); gas at 80 °C (above 59).
5. The energy taken in is used to overcome the forces of attraction between particles, not to increase their speed.
6. The particles lose energy, move more slowly and come close together, sliding past one another.
7. 1/10 × 1/10 × 1/10 × 1/10 = 1/10 000.
8. Raise the temperature: the particles have more energy and move faster. (Or remove the air: fewer collisions.)
9. Ammonia particles are lighter, so they diffuse faster and travel further before meeting hydrogen chloride; the ring forms nearer the hydrogen chloride end.
10. A solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature.
11. 18 × 100 ÷ 40 = 45 g per 100 g of water at 50 °C.
12. (80 − 30) × 25 ÷ 100 = 12.5 g.
