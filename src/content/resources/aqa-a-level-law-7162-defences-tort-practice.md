These questions test the section 3.3 row on defences: contributory negligence, consent, and the defences specific to private nuisance and *Rylands v Fletcher*. Any sums of money are invented for practice.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the effect of a successful plea of contributory negligence. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the two requirements of the defence of consent (volenti non fit injuria). **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the defence of consent failed in *Smith v Baker* (1891). **[2]**

**4.** A claimant's damages are assessed at £60,000. The court finds her 25% responsible for her injuries. Calculate the damages she will receive, showing your working. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the defence of prescription in private nuisance. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the defence of act of a stranger in *Rylands v Fletcher*, with **one** case. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the courts apply contributory negligence to children and to people acting in an emergency. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why consent is rarely successful for rescuers and car passengers. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why coming to the nuisance and public benefit are not defences to private nuisance. **[4]**

**10.** Sid, an experienced scaffolder, removes his safety harness against his employer's clear rules to work faster, and falls when a board negligently fixed by a colleague gives way. Advise Sid's employer which defences it may raise. **[6]**

**11.** Tia accepts a lift home from Ross, who she knows has drunk several pints. She does not wear a seat belt. Ross crashes and Tia suffers head injuries that a seat belt would have prevented. Advise Ross on any defences. **[6]**

**12.** A council reservoir overflows after the heaviest rainfall recorded in the area for a century, flooding Uma's farm. Separately, a disused coach in the council's yard catches fire when trespassing teenagers drop a lit cigarette into its fuel tank, damaging Uma's barn. Advise the council on defences under *Rylands v Fletcher*. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The claimant's damages are reduced to reflect their share of responsibility for the damage, rather than the claim being defeated [1].

**2.** The claimant must have had full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk [1], and must have voluntarily agreed to accept that risk, giving up any claim against the defendant [1].

**3.** The worker knew that stones were being swung over his head by a crane [1], but knowledge of a risk is not consent; as an employee with no real choice, he had not freely agreed to accept it [1].

**4.** 25% of £60,000 is £15,000 [1]; damages awarded are £60,000 − £15,000 = £45,000 [1].

**5.** A defendant may acquire a right to commit a private nuisance [1] if it has been an actionable nuisance to the claimant for 20 years without objection [1]. In *Sturges v Bridgman* (1879) the defence failed because the confectioner's noise only became a nuisance when the doctor built his consulting room [1].

**6.** The defendant is not liable if the escape was caused by the deliberate, unforeseeable act of a third party over whom it had no control [1]. In *Rickards v Lothian* (1913) an unknown person blocked a basin and turned on a tap, flooding the floor below [1], and the occupier was not liable [1]. (Credit *Perry v Kendricks Transport*.)

**7.** Children are judged against a reasonable child of the same age [1]: in *Gough v Thorne* (1966) a 13-year-old who crossed when a lorry driver waved her on was not contributorily negligent [1]. A person who makes a reasonable choice in the agony of the moment is not at fault even if the choice proves wrong [1]: in *Jones v Boyce* (1816) a passenger who jumped from a coach he feared would overturn was not contributorily negligent [1].

**8.** Rescuers act under a moral or legal duty, so their choice is not truly voluntary [1]: in *Haynes v Harwood* (1935) a police officer injured stopping runaway horses was not volenti [1], and in *Baker v TE Hopkins* (1959) the defence failed against a doctor [1]. For car passengers, *Dann v Hamilton* (1939) held that accepting a lift from a drinking driver was not consent [1], and s149 Road Traffic Act 1988 now prevents the defence being used against passengers where insurance is compulsory [1].

**9.** Coming to the nuisance is not a defence because the claimant's right to enjoy land is not lost by moving near an existing activity [1]: *Bliss v Hall* (1838); *Miller v Jackson* (1977); *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014) [1]. Public benefit is not a defence to liability [1]: in *Adams v Ursell* (1913) a fish and chip shop was a nuisance despite its value to the community, although public benefit may affect whether an injunction is granted [1].

**10.** **Consent:** Sid is experienced and deliberately broke clear safety rules, as in *ICI v Shatwell* (1965), where volenti succeeded [1]. However, Sid accepted the risk of falling without a harness, not the risk of a colleague's negligently fixed board, so he may not have full knowledge of the precise risk [1], and courts are reluctant to apply volenti to employees (*Smith v Baker*) [1]. **Contributory negligence:** Sid failed to take reasonable care for his own safety by removing his harness [1], and this contributed to his injuries [1]. The employer's best argument is that damages should be reduced substantially, perhaps by analogy with *Jones v Livox Quarries*, but not defeated entirely [1].

**11.** **Consent:** Tia knew Ross had been drinking, but *Dann v Hamilton* (1939) shows accepting a lift is not consent [1], and s149 Road Traffic Act 1988 prevents volenti against a passenger [1]. **Contributory negligence:** travelling with a driver known to have been drinking is contributory negligence (*Owens v Brimmell*, 1977: 20%) [1]. Not wearing a seat belt also contributed to the damage (*Froom v Butcher*, 1976) [1], and since a belt would have prevented her head injuries, the guidance suggests 25% [1]. The court will make a combined just and equitable reduction rather than simply adding the two figures, but a substantial reduction is likely [1].

**12.** **Flood:** Act of God applies only to natural forces no human could foresee or guard against [1]. In *Nichols v Marsland* (1876) extraordinary rainfall was an Act of God, but in *Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway* (1917) heavy rain was not [1]. Rainfall heaviest for a century is arguable, but reservoirs are designed against extreme rain, so the defence may fail [1]. **Barn:** the teenagers are trespassers, so their deliberate act may be an act of a stranger outside the council's control [1], as in *Perry v Kendricks Transport* (1956), where children threw a match into a coach's petrol tank [1]. However, if the council knew teenagers often entered the yard, the act may be foreseeable and the defence may fail; there is also doubt whether a disused coach is a non-natural use at all [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that contributory negligence defeats the claim.
- Confusing knowledge of a risk with consent.
- Forgetting the Road Traffic Act 1988 for passengers.
- Treating any rainfall as an Act of God.
