## What 8.2 asks you to do

The syllabus has three learning outcomes:

1. Define activation energy, Eₐ, as *the minimum energy required for a collision to be effective*.
2. Sketch and use the Boltzmann distribution to explain the significance of activation energy.
3. Explain qualitatively, in terms **both** of the Boltzmann distribution **and** of frequency of effective collisions, the effect of temperature change on the rate of a reaction.

The word "both" in outcome 3 matters. An answer that only mentions the curve, or only mentions collisions, is incomplete.

## Activation energy: the definition and what it means

Learn the definition word for word: **the minimum energy required for a collision to be effective**. Two features earn the credit: "minimum", and a reference to a **collision** being effective. "The energy needed to start a reaction" mentions neither and is not accepted.

Activation energy is a property of the reaction pathway. It is the energy barrier between reactants and the highest point on the pathway. Raising the temperature does **not** change Eₐ. What changes is how many particles have enough energy to get over it.

### Activation energy on a reaction pathway diagram

A reaction pathway diagram plots enthalpy (vertical axis) against progress of reaction (horizontal axis). Eₐ is drawn as a vertical arrow from the **reactants' level up to the peak**. It is never measured from the products, and never from zero.

- For an **exothermic** reaction the products sit below the reactants, so ΔH is negative and Eₐ is the climb from the higher, reactant level.
- For an **endothermic** reaction the products sit above the reactants. The peak must be at least as high as the products, so Eₐ is always greater than or equal to ΔH.

The barrier for the reverse reaction follows directly from the diagram: Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH.

**Worked example (invented values).** A reaction has ΔH = −60 kJ mol⁻¹ and Eₐ(forward) = +85 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Eₐ for the reverse reaction.

Eₐ(reverse) = 85 − (−60) = **+145 kJ mol⁻¹**. The reverse climb starts from the lower product level, so it must be larger than the forward barrier by the size of ΔH.

## Sketching the Boltzmann distribution properly

The Boltzmann distribution shows how kinetic energy is spread among the particles in a sample of gas at one temperature. Mark schemes check the same features every time:

| Feature | What to draw |
|---|---|
| Axes | Vertical: number of molecules (or fraction of molecules) with a given energy. Horizontal: energy. Never "rate" or "temperature" on an axis |
| Start | The curve begins at the origin: no molecule has zero energy |
| Shape | Rises to a single peak (the most probable energy), then falls more slowly. The curve is not symmetrical |
| Tail | Approaches the energy axis but **never touches or crosses it**: there is no maximum energy |
| Eₐ | A vertical line well to the right of the peak, labelled Eₐ |
| Shading | The area under the curve to the right of Eₐ, labelled as molecules with energy ≥ Eₐ |

Two quantities are read from the curve as areas, not heights. The **total area** is the total number of molecules. The **area beyond Eₐ** is the number of molecules able to react on collision. Because most molecules sit near the peak, which is far below Eₐ, the shaded area is a small fraction of the whole. That is why most collisions are not effective.

### Drawing two temperatures on one set of axes

When the question asks for a second curve at a higher temperature, T₂:

- The T₂ peak is **lower** and **further to the right** than the T₁ peak.
- The T₂ curve is broader. It starts at the origin as well.
- The two curves **cross once**, to the left of Eₐ. Beyond the crossing point, the T₂ curve lies above the T₁ curve all the way along the tail.
- The total area under each curve is the **same**, because the number of molecules has not changed.
- Eₐ stays where it is. Only the curve moves.

A common error is a T₂ curve that is simply the T₁ curve shifted to the right at the same height. That increases the total area and loses the mark for a lower peak.

## Why temperature has such a large effect on rate

Build the explanation in this order:

1. At a higher temperature the molecules have a higher average kinetic energy.
2. The Boltzmann distribution shifts to higher energy, so a **greater proportion** of molecules have energy **greater than or equal to Eₐ** (the shaded area increases).
3. So a greater proportion of collisions are effective.
4. The molecules also move faster, so collisions are **more frequent**.
5. Together, these give a greater **frequency of effective collisions**, so the rate increases.
6. The increase in the proportion with energy ≥ Eₐ is by far the larger effect.

Point 6 is what separates a full answer from a partial one. A small temperature rise causes only a small increase in collision frequency. The shaded tail, however, is very sensitive to the position of the curve, so its area can grow by a large factor.

**Worked example: an annotated model answer.** *Explain, with reference to the Boltzmann distribution, why the rate of a gas reaction increases when the temperature is raised from 300 K to 310 K.*

> "At 310 K the molecules have a higher average energy, so the Boltzmann distribution moves to the right and its peak is lower. A larger proportion of molecules now have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1], so a larger proportion of collisions are effective [1]. The molecules also move faster, so collisions are more frequent [1]. The frequency of effective collisions increases, and the rate increases; the main reason is the larger proportion of molecules with E ≥ Eₐ [1]."

To show the scale, take an illustration with invented figures. Suppose 1.2% of molecules have E ≥ Eₐ at 300 K and 2.6% at 310 K. That fraction has more than doubled (2.6 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 2.2). Over the same rise, the average speed, and so the collision frequency, goes up by under 2%. The size of the shaded area is what controls the rate.

## From practical data to a rate

Temperature practicals often time how long a reaction takes to reach a fixed point. In the reaction of sodium thiosulfate with dilute hydrochloric acid, a precipitate of sulfur forms:

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + S(s) + SO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

The flask stands on a cross drawn on paper, and the time, t, for the cross to disappear is recorded. The same amount of sulfur forms each time, so the **rate is proportional to 1/t**.

**Worked example (invented data).**

| Temperature / °C | t / s | 1/t / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 84 | 0.0119 |
| 30 | 43 | 0.0233 |
| 40 | 22 | 0.0455 |

Each 10 °C rise roughly doubles 1/t (0.0233 ÷ 0.0119 ≈ 1.96 and 0.0455 ÷ 0.0233 ≈ 1.95). A plot of 1/t against temperature curves upwards rather than giving a straight line. This is the Boltzmann argument appearing in data: equal steps in temperature give larger and larger increases in rate.

Keep the other variables constant: the same volumes and concentrations of both solutions, the same flask and the same cross. Warm each solution separately to the temperature before mixing. Record the temperature of the mixture, not of the water bath.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining Eₐ without "collision"**, or as "the energy given to start a reaction".
- **Saying Eₐ decreases when temperature increases.** Temperature moves the curve. Eₐ stays fixed. Only a catalyst gives a route with a lower Eₐ.
- **Drawing the Eₐ line at or near the peak.** It belongs well into the tail.
- **A curve that meets the energy axis**, or starts above the origin.
- **A higher-temperature curve with a larger area**, or a higher peak.
- **Giving only "the particles collide more often"** for the effect of temperature. That is the minor effect.
- **Measuring Eₐ from the products** on a pathway diagram for an exothermic reaction.

## Exam technique: command words in 8.2

- **Define**: give the exact definition. Two marks normally depend on "minimum energy" and "collision ... effective".
- **Sketch**: a clear freehand curve with both axes labelled. Exact values are not needed, but the shape features in the table above are checked.
- **Explain**: give a chain of cause and effect. For temperature, include both the Boltzmann point and the collision-frequency point, and say which is more important.
- **Use** (as in "use the Boltzmann distribution"): refer to what your diagram shows, especially the **area** beyond Eₐ, not just the word "energy".
- **Calculate** (from practical data): show 1/t, or a gradient, with units, and keep the significant figures consistent with the data.
