These notes condense **3.1.1 Economic methodology and the economic problem** in AQA A-level Economics (7136).
For explanations and worked examples, read the [study guide for this sub-section](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-methodology-and-the-economic-problem-aqa-alevel-economics/),
then test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-methodology-and-the-economic-problem-aqa-alevel-economics-practice/).
All countries and figures are invented.

## Economics as a social science (3.1.1.1)

| Like natural sciences | Unlike natural sciences |
|---|---|
| Builds models from simplifying assumptions | Studies people, who change behaviour in response to incentives and forecasts |
| Forms hypotheses and tests them against evidence | Controlled experiments on whole economies are rarely possible |
| Uses ceteris paribus to isolate one cause | Many variables change together in real data, so cause is hard to isolate |
| Seeks general relationships | Findings are tendencies, not exact laws, and are used to recommend policy, where values enter |

## Positive versus normative

- **Positive:** can be tested against evidence, even if it turns out false.
- **Normative:** contains a value judgement about what ought to be; evidence alone cannot settle it.
- **Test:** could data in principle decide it?

Tricky cases:

- "Economists believe X" is positive (a survey could check it).
- "If the aim is X, policy Y works faster" is positive: the value judgement is in the condition.
- "Too high", "excessive", "fair", "sensible", "should", "ought" signal normative statements.
- A positive statement about a policy is still positive: "the tax raised 2 billion" is checkable.

## Value judgements in policy

Views on the best option depend on two things:

1. **Positive consequences:** predicted effects on output, prices, jobs, costs and who gains.
2. **Moral and political judgements:** fairness, freedom, the proper role of the state.

People who agree on (1) can disagree because of (2). A good evaluative conclusion names the value judgement
it rests on.

## The nature and purpose of economic activity (3.1.1.2)

- Purpose: **production of goods and services to satisfy needs and wants**.
- The key decisions: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **who** benefits.
- "How" depends on factor costs and productivity; "who" raises questions of distribution.

## Economic resources (3.1.1.3)

| Factor | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources | Farmland, minerals, fish stocks |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental | Nurses, engineers |
| Capital | Produced goods used to make other goods | Machinery, broadband networks |
| Enterprise | Organising the factors and taking risks | A founder starting a firm |

**The environment is a scarce resource:** non-renewable resources are depleted by use; renewable resources can
be exhausted if used faster than they regenerate; unpriced environmental resources tend to be overused (the
tragedy of the commons, in 3.1.8).

## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost (3.1.1.4)

- **Fundamental economic problem:** scarcity, from limited resources and unlimited wants.
- **Scarcity forces choice** about how to allocate resources between uses.
- **Opportunity cost:** the **next best** alternative forgone, not the sum of all alternatives.
- With data: opportunity cost of one more unit of good X = units of good Y given up ÷ extra units of X.
- Using unemployed resources has **no** opportunity cost in terms of other output.

## Production possibility diagrams (3.1.1.5)

| Feature | How it appears |
|---|---|
| Resource allocation | The chosen point on the boundary |
| Opportunity cost and trade-offs | Movement along the boundary; the fall in one good |
| Unemployed resources | A point inside the boundary |
| Economic growth | Outward shift of the boundary |

- **Concave boundary:** rising opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods.
- **Straight-line boundary:** constant opportunity cost.
- **Pivot:** technology improves in one good only; only that intercept moves.
- **Inward shift:** loss of resources (war, disaster, a fall in the labour force).
- **Productive efficiency:** every point on the boundary.
- **Allocative efficiency:** only the point whose mix matches what society most wants; not every boundary
  point.

## Worked reminder (invented)

| Point | Food (million tonnes) | Machines (thousand) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 15 | 40 |
| Q | 25 | 32 |
| R | 35 | 20 |

- P to Q: 8 thousand machines for 10 million tonnes = **0.8** per million tonnes.
- Q to R: 12 thousand for 10 million tonnes = **1.2** per million tonnes.
- Rising opportunity cost, so the boundary is concave.

## Must-know distinctions

- Movement along the boundary (choice) versus shift of the boundary (capacity change).
- Moving from inside to the boundary (lower unemployment) versus outward shift (growth in capacity).
- Productive efficiency versus allocative efficiency.
- Positive and false versus normative.
- Capital as a factor of production (machines) versus financial capital (money).

## Quick self-test

1. Give two ways in which economics differs from natural sciences in its methods.
2. Classify: "A 10% rise in fuel duty would cut fuel use by about 2%."
3. Classify: "Fuel duty is unfairly high for rural drivers."
4. Classify: "Most voters think fuel duty is too high."
5. Name the three key economic decisions.
6. Why is the environment described as a scarce resource?
7. Define opportunity cost.
8. Using the worked reminder, what is the opportunity cost of moving from R back to Q?
9. What does a point inside a production possibility boundary show?
10. Show on a production possibility diagram an improvement in technology that affects only one good.
11. Why is every point on the boundary productively efficient?
12. Why is not every point on the boundary allocatively efficient?

### Answers

1. Controlled experiments on whole economies are rarely possible; human behaviour responds to incentives
   and forecasts; many variables change together in data, so isolating a cause is hard.
2. **Positive**: it can be tested, whether or not the estimate is correct.
3. **Normative**: "unfairly" is a value judgement.
4. **Positive**: a survey of voters could test it, even though the opinion itself is normative.
5. What to produce, how to produce it, and who benefits.
6. Natural resources such as clean air, water and fish stocks are limited, some are depleted by use, and
   unpriced ones tend to be overused.
7. The next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
8. Moving from 35 to 25 million tonnes of food gains 12 thousand machines, so each thousand machines costs
   10 ÷ 12 ≈ **0.83 million tonnes of food**.
9. Unemployed or inefficiently used resources; more of both goods could be produced.
10. The boundary **pivots** outward along the axis of the good whose technology improved; the other intercept
    does not move.
11. More of one good can be produced only by producing less of the other, so no resources are wasted.
12. Allocative efficiency requires the output mix that best matches society's preferences; most boundary
    points have the wrong mix.
