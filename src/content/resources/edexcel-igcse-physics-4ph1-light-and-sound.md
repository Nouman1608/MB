This guide goes deep on one sub-topic: **3(d) Light and sound**, statements 3.14 to 3.29 of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4 specification. Statements 3.24P to 3.29P (hearing range, the speed of
sound practical and the oscilloscope work) are Physics-only, so they are assessed only on Paper 2. For the whole of
Topic 3 in one place, see the [Waves study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-waves/).

## What 3(d) asks you to do

The light half of the sub-topic is mostly about **ray diagrams and two equations**: you must use the law of reflection,
draw rays being reflected and refracted, carry out two named practicals (refraction through rectangular blocks,
semi-circular blocks and triangular prisms; the refractive index of glass using a glass block), use `n = sin i / sin r`
and `sin c = 1/n`, and describe how total internal reflection is used in optical fibres and prisms. The sound half asks
you to know that sound is longitudinal, and (Physics only) to measure the speed of sound in air, use a microphone and
oscilloscope to display a sound and find its frequency, and link pitch to frequency and loudness to amplitude.

## Drawing ray diagrams that earn the marks

Every ray diagram is built around the **normal**: a dashed line drawn at 90° to the surface where the ray meets it.
All angles of incidence, reflection and refraction are measured between the ray and the normal.

**Reflection (3.15, 3.16).** The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Draw the incident ray with a
ruler, add the normal with a protractor, measure the angle and mark the same angle on the other side of the normal.
Put an arrowhead on each ray showing the direction light travels.

**Refraction (3.16).** Light changes speed when it crosses into a different material, and that change of speed makes
it change direction unless it meets the surface along the normal. Going from air into glass or water it slows and
bends **towards** the normal; coming out it speeds up and bends **away** from the normal.

## The refraction practical with three block shapes (3.17)

Each block shape shows something different, which is why the specification names all three.

- **Rectangular block.** Trace the block outline on paper, aim a narrow ray from a ray box at one long face, mark the
  ray going in and coming out with crosses, then join them up through the outline. The emerging ray is **parallel to
  the incident ray** but shifted sideways, because the bending on the way in is undone on the way out.
- **Semi-circular block.** Aim the ray at the centre of the flat face from the curved side. The ray enters the curved
  face along a radius, so it meets that surface along the normal and does not bend there. All the refraction happens
  at the flat face, which makes this the cleanest set-up for watching the refracted ray get closer to the surface as
  the angle increases, then disappear into total internal reflection at the critical angle.
- **Triangular prism.** The ray refracts at two faces that are not parallel, so it is deviated towards the base of the
  prism and does not come out parallel to the way it went in. With white light, each colour refracts by a slightly
  different amount, so the emerging light spreads into the colours of the visible spectrum.

## Measuring the refractive index of glass (3.18, 3.19)

Using the rectangular block, measure the angle of refraction `r` for several angles of incidence `i` (for example 20°,
30°, 40°, 50°, 60°). Plot `sin i` on the y-axis against `sin r` on the x-axis. Because `n = sin i / sin r`, the graph is
a straight line through the origin and its **gradient is the refractive index**. A graph uses every reading at once, so
one badly drawn ray has less effect than it would in a single calculation.

**Worked example 1.** A student's readings give `sin r` = 0.225, 0.342, 0.423, 0.515, 0.574 for `sin i` = 0.342,
0.500, 0.643, 0.766, 0.866. Using the line of best fit, two well-separated points on it are (0.225, 0.342) and
(0.574, 0.866).

```
gradient = (0.866 − 0.342) / (0.574 − 0.225) = 0.524 / 0.349 = 1.50
n = 1.50 (no unit: refractive index is a ratio)
```

Errors worth naming in an evaluation: a wide ray, a normal not at exactly 90°, and angles read only to the nearest
degree. A narrow slit, crosses marked far apart and repeated readings all help.

## Total internal reflection and the critical angle (3.20 to 3.22)

When light inside glass meets the glass-to-air boundary, some is reflected and some refracts out. As the angle of
incidence inside the glass grows, the refracted ray bends further from the normal until, at the **critical angle c**,
it travels along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°). For any angle of incidence greater than c, no light refracts
out and all of it is reflected: **total internal reflection**. Two conditions must both hold: the light is going from
the material with the higher refractive index towards the lower one, and the angle of incidence is greater than c.

**Worked example 2.** Acrylic has a refractive index of 1.49. Find its critical angle, then decide what happens to a
ray inside the acrylic that meets the surface at 40° and at 50°.

```
sin c = 1/n = 1/1.49 = 0.671      c = 42.2°
40° is less than 42.2°: the ray refracts out into the air (bending away from the normal)
50° is greater than 42.2°: total internal reflection
```

**Optical fibres.** A fibre is a thin strand of glass. Light entering one end strikes the side wall at an angle greater
than the critical angle, is totally internally reflected, crosses the fibre, and is reflected again, many times, so
it follows the fibre even round gentle bends. Pulses of light carry information along the fibre with very little
escaping through the sides.

**Prisms.** In a right-angled glass prism, light entering one short face at 90° meets the long face at 45°. This only
works if 45° is greater than the critical angle, which needs `n` greater than `1/sin 45° = 1.41`. Glass (about 1.5)
qualifies. One reflection turns the ray through 90°; entering through the long face instead gives two reflections
and turns it through 180°, sending it back the way it came, displaced sideways.

## Sound waves (3.23 to 3.29)

Sound is a **longitudinal** wave: air particles vibrate back and forth along the direction the sound travels, making
compressions and rarefactions. Like light, sound reflects (an echo) and refracts (it changes speed and direction when
it passes into a different medium, or into air at a different temperature).

**Hearing range (3.24P).** Humans can hear frequencies from **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz**. A frequency outside that range is
still sound, but we cannot hear it.

**Measuring the speed of sound in air (3.25P).** Two common methods:

- **Echo method.** Stand a measured distance from a large flat wall and clap. Clap again each time the echo returns,
  so the claps and echoes fall into a steady rhythm. Time a large number of intervals and divide to find the time for
  one interval, which is the time for sound to travel to the wall **and back**.
- **Two microphones.** Place two microphones a measured distance apart in line with the sound source and connect
  them to an oscilloscope or a fast timer. The time delay between the two signals is the time for sound to travel the
  gap between them.

**Worked example 3.** A wall is 80 m away. Twenty clap-to-echo intervals take 9.6 s.

```
time for one interval = 9.6 / 20 = 0.48 s
distance travelled in that time = 2 × 80 = 160 m
speed = distance / time = 160 / 0.48 = 330 m/s (2 s.f.)
```

Timing twenty intervals instead of one cuts the effect of human reaction time, which would be large compared with
0.48 s.

**The oscilloscope (3.26P, 3.27P).** A microphone turns the pressure changes of a sound into a changing voltage; the
oscilloscope plots that voltage against time. The trace looks like a transverse wave even though sound is
longitudinal, because it is a **graph**, not a picture of the wave. To find the frequency, count the horizontal
squares (divisions) for one complete wave, multiply by the timebase setting to get the period, then use `f = 1/T`.

**Worked example 4.** One complete wave spans 5.0 divisions and the timebase is 0.20 ms per division.

```
T = 5.0 × 0.20 ms = 1.0 ms = 0.0010 s
f = 1/T = 1/0.0010 = 1000 Hz
```

**Pitch and loudness (3.28P, 3.29P).** A higher-frequency vibration of the source gives a higher pitch, seen as more
waves across the screen. A larger amplitude of vibration gives a louder sound, seen as a taller trace. Changing one
does not change the other.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal. Examiners look at the angle, not just the bend.
- Dividing the angles instead of their sines: `n = 40/25` is not the refractive index of anything.
- Forgetting to halve (or double) the distance in an echo calculation: the sound goes there and back.
- Stating total internal reflection happens "when the angle is big". Say greater than the critical angle, and say
  the light is travelling from glass (or water) towards air.
- Reading the timebase as the period. The period is the timebase setting multiplied by the number of divisions per
  wave.

## Exam technique for 4PH1 command words

- **Draw** a ray diagram: ruler, normals, arrowheads, and angles that look right (a refracted ray bending the wrong way
  loses the mark however neat it is).
- **Describe** a practical: give the equipment, what you measure, and how you use the readings, in order. For the
  refractive index practical, say you plot `sin i` against `sin r` and find the gradient.
- **Explain** total internal reflection or optical fibres: link the cause (angle greater than the critical angle)
  to the effect (all light reflected, so it stays inside the fibre).
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute, give the answer with a unit (or say it has none, as for `n`).
