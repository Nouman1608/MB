Recall notes for **4.2.5 Static electricity** (4.2.5.1 Static charge and 4.2.5.2 Electric fields) in AQA GCSE
Physics (8463). Physics only, Paper 1, both tiers. No equations to learn.

For full explanations and model answers, read the [static electricity study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-static-electricity/).
Then practise with the [static electricity practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-static-electricity-practice/).

## The one rule behind everything

**Only electrons move.** Electrons are negatively charged. Protons stay in the nuclei of atoms.

| What happened | Result |
|---|---|
| Object gains electrons | Becomes negatively charged |
| Object loses electrons | Left with an equal positive charge |
| Rod and cloth rubbed together | Charges equal in size and opposite in sign |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Static charge | Charge that stays on an insulator rather than flowing |
| Insulator | Material in which charge cannot move freely, so rubbed-on charge stays put |
| Repulsion | Force pushing apart two objects with the same type of charge |
| Attraction | Force pulling together two objects with different types of charge |
| Non-contact force | A force that acts across a gap without the objects touching |
| Electric field | Region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force |
| Earthed conductor | A conductor connected to earth (0 V) |
| Spark | Electrons jumping across a gap when the electric field there is strong enough |

## Charging by rubbing in three lines

1. Certain insulating materials are rubbed together.
2. Electrons are rubbed off one material and on to the other.
3. The gainer becomes negative; the loser is left equally positive.

## Forces

- Same type of charge → **repel**. Different types → **attract**.
- Both are **non-contact** forces.
- Evidence: a charged rod hanging on a thread turns away from a like-charged rod and towards an unlike-charged rod,
  without being touched.

## Electric fields

- Created by any charged object.
- **Strongest close to** the object; **weaker further away**.
- A second charge in the field feels a force; the force is **stronger at smaller distances**.
- The field explains the non-contact force: each object is in the other's field.

**Sphere field pattern:** straight radial lines, evenly spaced, starting at the surface. Arrows **out** for
positive, **in** for negative. Lines are closest together near the surface.

## Sparking in three steps

1. Charge builds up on an insulated object.
2. The pd between it and a nearby earthed conductor rises, so the field in the gap gets stronger.
3. When the field is strong enough, electrons jump the gap: a spark.

## Worked reminders

- Acetate strip rubbed with a cloth becomes positive → the strip **lost** electrons to the cloth → the cloth is
  **negative**.
- Polythene rubbed with wool becomes negative → it **gained** electrons from the wool → the wool is **positive**.
- Two strips both rubbed with the same cloth → same type of charge → they **repel**.
- A positive strip and a negative cloth → different types → they **attract**.

## Weak answer, strong answer

| Question focus | Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|---|
| How the cloth became charged | "It got static." | "Electrons moved from the cloth to the rod, so the cloth lost electrons and is left positive." |
| Why two strips repel | "Like charges repel." | "Both strips gained electrons from the same cloth, so they have the same type of charge and repel with a non-contact force." |
| Why the spark happens | "The charge escapes." | "The pd and the field in the gap grow until the field is strong enough for electrons to cross the gap." |
| Why the force grows as charges get closer | "They are nearer." | "Each object is in a stronger part of the other's electric field, so the force on it is larger." |

## Exam traps

- "Positive charges moved on to the rod": wrong; electrons moved off it.
- Unequal charges on rod and cloth: they are always equal and opposite.
- Field arrows pointing into a positive sphere: they point outwards.
- Curved or crossing lines round a single sphere: the lines are straight and radial.
- "Static jumps": say electrons cross the gap because the field is strong enough.
- Forgetting "non-contact" when asked what type of force acts.
- Starting an explanation with "like charges repel" before explaining how the charge arose.

## Self-test

1. Which particles move when an insulator is charged by rubbing?
2. A rod gains electrons. What charge does it have?
3. A cloth loses electrons. What charge is it left with?
4. Compare the size of the charges on a rod and the cloth used to rub it.
5. What happens when two negatively charged objects are brought close?
6. What type of force acts between charged objects?
7. Describe one piece of evidence that charged objects exert forces without touching.
8. Define an electric field.
9. Where is the electric field of a charged sphere strongest?
10. Describe the field lines of an isolated negatively charged sphere.
11. How does the force between two charged objects change as they move closer?
12. Explain how a spark forms between a charged object and a nearby earthed pipe.

### Answers

1. Electrons.
2. **Negative.**
3. **Positive.**
4. Equal in size, opposite in type.
5. They repel.
6. A non-contact force.
7. A charged rod on a thread turns away from (or towards) another charged rod brought near it, without touching.
8. The region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force.
9. Close to its surface.
10. Straight, evenly spaced radial lines with arrows pointing in towards the sphere.
11. It gets stronger.
12. Charge builds up, so the pd between the object and the pipe and the field in the gap increase; when the field
    is strong enough, electrons jump across the gap.
