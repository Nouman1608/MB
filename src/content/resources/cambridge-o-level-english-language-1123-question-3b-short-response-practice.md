> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for practice on 1123 Paper 1 Question 3b,
> where candidates "give a short response to show understanding of implicit meanings and attitude in
> response to Text B". All passages are original and much shorter than the real Text B. Questions 10,
> 11 and 12 use the syllabus tariff of 5 marks; the others practise the separate skills with practice
> marks. The way marks are split in the answers is a teaching guide, not the official mark scheme.

## Section A

**1.** Give a precise attitude word for each sentence (original): (a) "The new stadium, we are told, will 'transform' the area." (b) "I still keep the ticket from that first concert in my drawer." **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the word "lurks" in this sentence shows the writer's attitude: "A vending machine now lurks at the end of the library." **[2]**

**3.** Identify the hedge in this sentence and explain what it suggests: "The scheme has, apparently, been a great success." **[2]**

**4.** Explain the attitude shown by the concession in this sentence: "Admittedly, the new road is quicker; it is also louder, uglier and more dangerous." **[2]**

**5.** Rewrite this weak answer so that it shows attitude: "The writer says the park has new benches and a café." **[2]**

**6.** Explain why "positive" is a weaker answer than "nostalgic" for a writer describing a childhood holiday with warmth and regret. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Read the passage (original).

> Everyone told me that volunteering at the animal shelter would be "rewarding". On my first morning I
> was handed a mop, a bucket and a list of thirty kennels. By lunchtime I smelled like a farmyard and
> my back ached. Then a nervous grey dog called Pebble, who had not let anyone touch her for a week,
> pushed her nose into my hand.

Identify the writer's attitude at the start of the passage and at the end, quoting one word or phrase for each. **[4]**

**8.** In the passage in Question 7, explain what the inverted commas around "rewarding" suggest. **[2]**

**9.** Read the passage (original).

> The town's annual food festival has grown enormously. Twenty years ago it was a handful of stalls
> run by grandmothers. Now there are queues, celebrity chefs and a car park charging by the hour. The
> food is, I suppose, more varied.

What does the last sentence suggest about the writer's attitude to the festival? **[2]**

**10.** Using the passage in Question 9, explain the writer's attitude towards how the festival has changed. **[5]**

**11.** Read the passage (original).

> Our school's new rule is simple: no phones from the first bell to the last. Teachers report that
> corridors are calmer and that students talk to each other at lunch. Some parents, however, say they
> cannot reach their children in an emergency, and older students complain that they are treated like
> infants. The head teacher's response, that "emergencies are what the office telephone is for", has
> not settled the argument. Still, I notice that the library has never been so full.

Explain the writer's attitude towards the new phone rule. **[5]**

**12.** Read the passage (original).

> My grandfather's workshop still smells of oil and sawdust, though no one has worked there for ten
> years. His tools hang in neat rows, each outline painted on the wall so that he would know at once
> if anything was missing. My father says we should sell them; they are worth something now. I have
> not said anything yet. I go in sometimes and just stand there.

What attitudes do the writer and the writer's father have towards the workshop? **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Sceptical: "we are told" and the inverted commas around "transform" distance the writer from the claim **[1]**. (b) Nostalgic or affectionate: keeping the ticket shows the memory is treasured **[1]**.

**2.** "Lurks" suggests something hiding and slightly threatening **[1]**, so the writer disapproves of the machine and sees it as out of place in the library **[1]**.

**3.** "Apparently" **[1]**; it suggests the writer is doubtful about the claim and has only heard it from others **[1]**.

**4.** The writer grants one advantage ("Admittedly... quicker") **[1]** but then lists three disadvantages, showing that their attitude is strongly critical despite the concession **[1]**.

**5.** For example: "The writer is pleased with the improvements, welcoming the 'new benches' as signs that the park is cared for." **[2]** One mark for an attitude word, one for linking it to the evidence.

**6.** "Nostalgic" names the specific feeling, warmth mixed with longing for the past, while "positive" misses the regret **[1]**.

**7.** Start: sceptical or reluctant, shown by the inverted commas around "rewarding" or by "smelled like a farmyard" **[2]**. End: touched or moved, shown by Pebble, "who had not let anyone touch her", choosing to push her nose into the writer's hand **[2]**.

**8.** That the writer doubted the word or found it a cliché **[1]**, and that the first morning did not seem rewarding at all **[1]**.

**9.** "I suppose" is grudging **[1]**, so the writer concedes one benefit only reluctantly and still prefers the old festival **[1]**.

**10.** Award one mark per developed, supported point, up to five:
- The writer is nostalgic about the past, describing the early festival warmly as "a handful of stalls run by grandmothers" **[1]**.
- "Grown enormously" seems neutral but introduces a list of changes the writer dislikes **[1]**.
- "Queues" and "celebrity chefs" suggest the writer finds the festival crowded and commercial, having lost its homely character **[1]**.
- "A car park charging by the hour" shows irritation at the festival becoming about money **[1]**.
- The grudging "I suppose" concedes that the food is more varied, so the attitude is mainly regretful but not wholly negative **[1]**.

**11.** Award one mark per developed, supported point, up to five:
- The writer presents the rule's benefits first and without irony ("calmer", "talk to each other"), suggesting cautious approval **[1]**.
- The writer is fair to critics, reporting the parents' worries and older students' complaints, which shows a balanced, not one-sided, attitude **[1]**.
- "Has not settled the argument" suggests the writer is unconvinced by the head teacher's brisk reply **[1]**.
- Placing the head teacher's words in quotation marks distances the writer from them **[1]**.
- "Still" signals a final turn: the full library suggests the writer, on balance, approves of the rule's effect **[1]**.

**12.** Award one mark per developed, supported point, up to five:
- The writer feels deep affection and reverence for the workshop: the sensory detail "still smells of oil and sawdust" shows its memories remain alive for them **[1]**.
- The "neat rows" and painted outlines suggest admiration for the grandfather's care and order **[1]**.
- The father's attitude is practical and financial: "they are worth something now" treats the tools as objects to sell **[1]**.
- "I have not said anything yet" implies the writer disagrees but is reluctant to argue, showing an unspoken conflict **[1]**.
- "Just stand there" suggests quiet grief or a wish to stay close to the grandfather, attitudes the writer does not state directly **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 5:** replacing one fact with another fact.
- **Question 10:** listing the changes without saying how the writer feels about them.
- **Question 11:** deciding the writer is simply "for" or "against" and ignoring the balance.
- **Question 12:** giving only the writer's attitude when the question asks about two people.
