This guide teaches section **3.1.3.4 Bonding and physical properties** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The earlier subtopics cover ionic, covalent and metallic bonding one at a time. This one asks you to put them together: to name the type of structure from its properties, to relate melting point and conductivity to the particles and forces present, to explain what changes at a change of state, and to draw the six named structures. The [Bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding/) gives a wider survey. Recall is in the [Bonding and physical properties revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bonding-and-physical-properties-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Bonding and physical properties practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bonding-and-physical-properties-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content and skills | What you must be able to do |

| Four types of crystal structure: ionic, metallic, macromolecular (giant covalent), molecular | Classify a substance from its properties and bonding |
| Structures of diamond, graphite, ice, iodine, magnesium and sodium chloride as examples | Describe and sketch each |
| Relate melting point and conductivity to the type of structure and the bonding present | Explain the properties of each type |
| Explain the energy changes associated with changes of state | Say which forces are overcome when a substance melts or boils |
| Draw diagrams to represent these structures involving specified numbers of particles | Produce labelled sketches on request |

## A method for any "explain the property" question

1. Name the **type of structure** (ionic, metallic, macromolecular or molecular).
2. Name the **particles** (ions, atoms, molecules) and the **forces** between them.
3. State whether the forces are strong or weak, and in what direction.
4. Link to the property (energy needed to overcome the forces; availability of mobile charged particles).

## The four types

| Type | Particles | Forces overcome on melting | Conducts when solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Positive and negative ions | Strong electrostatic attraction between ions | No | Yes |
| Metallic | Positive ions and delocalised electrons | Strong electrostatic attraction between ions and electrons | Yes | Yes |
| Macromolecular | Atoms | Strong covalent bonds | No (graphite is the exception) | Usually not |
| Molecular | Molecules | Weak intermolecular forces | No | No |

The key difference between the last two types: a macromolecular solid must have **covalent bonds broken** to melt it. A molecular solid needs only the **intermolecular forces** overcome. The covalent bonds within each molecule are not broken.

## The six named structures

### Sodium chloride (ionic)

A giant lattice of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a cubic arrangement, each ion surrounded by six of the opposite charge. Melting point 801 °C. Does not conduct when solid, conducts when molten or dissolved.

### Magnesium (metallic)

A lattice of Mg²⁺ ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. High melting point (650 °C) and conducts when solid and liquid.

### Diamond (macromolecular)

Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds to four other carbon atoms, arranged tetrahedrally in a giant three-dimensional lattice. All the outer electrons are used in bonds, so none are free to move. It is very hard, has a very high melting point, and does not conduct electricity.

### Graphite (macromolecular)

Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in flat hexagonal layers. The fourth outer electron of each carbon is **delocalised** along the layer. The layers are held together only by weak London forces. Consequently:

- very high melting point, because covalent bonds within a layer are strong;
- conducts electricity along the layers, because delocalised electrons are free to move;
- soft and slippery, because the layers slide over one another, overcoming only weak forces;
- lower density than diamond, because the layers are far apart.

### Ice (molecular, with hydrogen bonds)

Each water molecule forms hydrogen bonds with four others: two through its hydrogen atoms and two through its oxygen lone pairs. The molecules form an open, tetrahedral, hexagonal-ring network. This arrangement holds the molecules further apart than they are in liquid water, so ice is **less dense** than water and floats. Ice has a low melting point (0 °C) because hydrogen bonds are much weaker than covalent bonds, and only these are overcome on melting.

### Iodine (molecular)

I₂ molecules, each with a strong covalent bond, arranged in a regular lattice. The molecules are held by weak London forces. Iodine is a dark solid that sublimes easily, melts at 114 °C and does not conduct, because there are no ions or delocalised electrons.

## Explaining melting and boiling points

### Worked example 1: diamond and iodine

Explain why diamond has a melting point above 3500 °C but iodine melts at 114 °C.

Diamond is macromolecular: every atom is joined to four others by strong covalent bonds in a giant lattice, and a lot of energy is needed to break these bonds. Iodine is molecular: its I₂ molecules are held by weak London forces, and only these forces are overcome when it melts. The strong I–I covalent bonds within each molecule are not broken.

### Worked example 2: sodium chloride and ice

Explain why sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and ice at 0 °C.

Sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice, with strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in all directions. A lot of energy is needed to overcome them. Ice is molecular. Melting overcomes only the hydrogen bonds between water molecules, which are much weaker than the attraction between ions, so far less energy is needed.

### Worked example 3: the conductivity of graphite

Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not.

In graphite each carbon uses three of its four outer electrons for bonding, so one electron per atom is delocalised in the layer and is free to move. In diamond all four outer electrons are used in covalent bonds, so no mobile charged particles are present.

## Energy changes at a change of state

When a solid melts, or a liquid boils, the energy supplied **overcomes the forces between particles**; it does not raise the temperature during the change. The size of the energy needed depends on the strength of the forces.

| Change | What happens |
|---|---|
| Melting | The particles gain enough energy to break out of fixed positions, but they stay in contact |
| Boiling | Particles gain enough energy to overcome the remaining forces and separate completely |
| Sublimation | A solid goes straight to a gas; this happens in iodine because the intermolecular forces are weak |
| Freezing or condensing | Energy is released as forces form |

The substance must be identified before saying what is broken: ionic bonds (ions separate), metallic bonding (ions are freed from the lattice), covalent bonds in macromolecular substances (atoms are separated) or intermolecular forces in molecular substances (molecules separate but remain intact). A common error is to write that "covalent bonds break" when ice or iodine melts or when water boils.

## Identifying an unknown from properties

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts solid | Conducts liquid | Soluble in water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 1610 | No | No | No |
| Q | 801 | No | Yes | Yes |
| R | 98 | Yes | Yes | Reacts |
| S | 114 | No | No | No |

**Deduction:** P is macromolecular (very high melting point, no conduction); Q is ionic (high melting point, conducts only when molten or dissolved); R is metallic (conducts when solid, relatively low melting point for a metal); S is molecular (low melting point, no conduction). Always give both the type **and** the evidence.

## Drawing the structures

Examiners may specify the number of particles, for example "draw eight ions of sodium chloride". Use these guides.

- **Sodium chloride:** a cube, with alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ at the corners. Label the ions and make clear which are which.
- **Magnesium:** at least three rows of at least three circles, each labelled 2+, with electrons shown between.
- **Diamond:** a central carbon bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement, with bonds continuing outwards.
- **Graphite:** at least three joined hexagons, with the second layer shown, and weak forces indicated between layers.
- **Ice:** a central water molecule with four hydrogen bonds to others, with the angle at oxygen shown.
- **Iodine:** several I₂ molecules as pairs of atoms, in a regular arrangement.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts or boils.
- Writing that "intermolecular forces are weaker than covalent bonds, so the melting point is low" without stating which are overcome.
- Stating that graphite conducts because "it has free electrons", without saying they are delocalised in the layers.
- Failing to say that diamond has no mobile charged particles.
- Describing ice as less dense because molecules are "further apart" with no mention of hydrogen bonds holding an open lattice.
- Using "molecules" for sodium chloride or diamond.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain**: name the type of structure, the particles, the forces and the consequence, in that order.
- **Identify** or **deduce**: give the type plus evidence from the data.
- **Draw**: label the particles and show enough of the lattice for the pattern to be clear.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is graphite a conductor in all directions?**
No. It conducts along the layers, where the delocalised electrons are free to move. Conduction between layers is poor.

**Does silicon dioxide fit into these types?**
Yes. It is macromolecular, with each silicon atom bonded to four oxygen atoms in a giant lattice, so it has a very high melting point.

**Why does ice float?**
Hydrogen bonding holds the molecules in an open lattice, which makes ice less dense than liquid water.
