These notes condense the [Psychology of sleep study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-psychology-of-sleep/). Read that first if anything here is new.

They follow section 3.3.1 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Psychology (9685) specification, Version 3.1 (International A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards). The specification places Psychology of sleep in Section A of Unit 3: Advanced topics and research methods 2, a 1 hour 30 minute written exam worth 90 marks and 30% of the International A-level. Section A is worth 30 marks and its questions are compulsory.

Practise with the [sleep practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-psychology-of-sleep-practice/). See the [9685 course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/), the [9685 checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/) and the [Unit 2 biopsychology revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-biopsychology-development-revision-notes/) for the nervous and endocrine systems.

## What the specification lists

1. Biological rhythms: circadian, infradian and ultradian; endogenous pacemakers and exogenous zeitgebers and the sleep/wake cycle.
2. Disruption of biological rhythms: shift work and jet lag.
3. The nature of sleep: types of sleep, non-REM and REM.
4. Functions of sleep: evolutionary explanations; restoration theory; memory consolidation.
5. Sleep disorders: insomnia, including personality factors and genetics; narcolepsy.

The assessment objectives are AO1 (knowledge), AO2 (application) and AO3 (analysis and evaluation). The specification gives Unit 3 approximate weightings of 9% AO1, 10% AO2 and 11% AO3 of the full A-level, so evaluation and application matter as much as description. Research methods and maths skills are also assessed in Unit 3.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm | A cycle of about 24 hours, e.g. sleep/wake, body temperature |
| Infradian rhythm | A cycle longer than 24 hours, e.g. menstrual cycle, seasonal affective disorder |
| Ultradian rhythm | A cycle shorter than 24 hours, e.g. the 90-minute sleep stage cycle |
| Endogenous pacemaker | Internal clock; mainly the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus |
| Exogenous zeitgeber | External cue that resets the clock; mainly light, also meals and social routines |
| Entrainment | Bringing the internal clock into line with external cues |
| Free-running rhythm | A rhythm that continues with no zeitgebers; in humans, slightly over 24 hours |
| Melatonin | Hormone released by the pineal gland in darkness; promotes sleep |
| Desynchronisation | Internal rhythms out of step with each other or with the outside world |
| Phase advance / delay | Moving the clock earlier (harder) / later (easier) |
| Slow-wave sleep (SWS) | NREM stages 3 and 4; delta waves; deepest sleep |
| REM sleep | Rapid eye movements, waking-like EEG, muscle paralysis, vivid dreams |
| Core sleep | Horne's term for the SWS and REM needed for normal function |
| Hyperarousal | Raised physical and mental arousal that blocks sleep |
| Cataplexy | Sudden loss of muscle tone, often after strong emotion; a feature of narcolepsy |
| Hypocretin (orexin) | Hypothalamic neurotransmitter that stabilises wakefulness; reduced in narcolepsy with cataplexy |

## Studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Siffre (1962) | About two months alone in a cave | Sleep/wake cycle continued but ran slightly over 24 hours | Single case; cannot generalise alone |
| Aschoff and Wever | Volunteers in an underground bunker | Most free-ran a little over 24 hours | Artificial light may have reset the clock |
| Ralph et al. (1990) | SCN transplants in hamsters | Recipients took the donor's 20-hour rhythm | Animal study; generalising to humans needs care |
| DeCoursey et al. (2000) | SCN damaged in chipmunks, returned to the wild | More killed by weasels than controls | Ethical concerns about harm to animals |
| Campbell and Murphy (1998) | Light on back of the knee | Rhythm shifted | Later work failed to replicate |
| Stern and McClintock (1998) | Armpit compounds applied to other women | Menstrual cycle length changed | Many other factors affect cycle length |
| Czeisler et al. (1982) | Rotas of rotating shift workers | Forward, slower rotation improved schedule satisfaction, health ratings and productivity | Real workplace, so high ecological validity |
| Recht et al. (1995) | Baseball results after travel | Teams flying east did worse than those flying west | Correlational; travel stress is a confound |
| Dement and Kleitman (1957) | Woke sleepers in REM and NREM | REM wakings usually produced dream reports | Relies on self-report |
| Zepelin and Rechtschaffen (1974) | Sleep across species | Smaller, faster-metabolism animals sleep more | Many species do not fit |
| Allison and Cicchetti (1976) | Sleep across 39 mammal species | Species in more danger from predators had less REM sleep | Correlational comparison |
| Shapiro et al. (1981) | Runners after a 92 km race | More SWS and total sleep afterwards | Supports body restoration |
| Randy Gardner (1964) | 264 hours awake | Recovered a fraction of lost sleep, but more SWS and REM | Single case |
| Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924) | Recall after sleep vs waking | Better after sleep | May reflect less interference, not consolidation |
| Plihal and Born (1997) | Early vs late night sleep | SWS-rich helped facts; REM-rich helped a motor skill | Lab tasks may lack everyday validity |
| Rasch et al. (2007) | Odour cue during SWS | Improved later recall | Strong experimental control |
| Siegel (2001) | Review of REM and memory | REM-suppressing antidepressants without clear memory loss | Challenges a REM role |
| Lin et al. (1999) | Narcoleptic dogs | Mutation in a hypocretin receptor gene | Human narcolepsy is rarely single-gene |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Circadian vs ultradian sleep rhythms.** The sleep/wake cycle is circadian. The cycle of sleep stages within a night is ultradian.
- **Pacemaker vs zeitgeber vs hormone.** The SCN is the pacemaker, light is the zeitgeber, and melatonin is the hormone that carries the signal.
- **Phase advance vs phase delay.** East travel and backward shift rotation need an advance (harder). West travel and forward rotation need a delay (easier).
- **SWS vs REM.** SWS is deep, with delta waves and body restoration. REM has waking-like EEG, a paralysed body and vivid dreams.
- **Evolutionary vs restoration.** Evolutionary accounts explain *why sleep evolved* (safety, saving energy). Restoration explains *what sleep does* for the body and brain.
- **Primary vs secondary insomnia.** Secondary has another cause, such as pain. Primary has no clear separate cause.
- **Personality vs genetics.** Personality factors (neuroticism, worry) act through hyperarousal. Genetic factors are many small-effect variants, shown by family, twin and genome-wide studies.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Discuss restoration theory as an explanation of the function of sleep." Your teacher will confirm the mark tariff.

1. **AO1:** Oswald: SWS restores the body (growth hormone, tissue repair); REM restores the brain. Horne: core and optional sleep.
2. **AO2 (if a scenario is given):** use its details, for example an athlete's longer, deeper sleep after a race.
3. **AO3 support:** Shapiro et al. (1981), more SWS after an ultramarathon; Randy Gardner recovered more SWS and REM than lighter sleep.
4. **AO3 limitations:** repair also happens while awake; exercise does not always increase sleep; Gardner was a single case.
5. **AO3 alternatives:** evolutionary explanations (energy conservation, safety); memory consolidation evidence from Plihal and Born (1997).
6. **Judgement:** sleep probably serves several functions. Restoration fits SWS well but cannot explain all of sleep alone.

The same shape works for other extended questions on this unit. For shift work and jet lag, describe desynchronisation and the phase advance/delay difference, then judge the evidence from Czeisler et al. and Recht et al. For insomnia, give the personality and genetic factors, then weigh correlational evidence and shared family environments.

## Quick self-test

1. Give one example of an infradian rhythm.
2. Where is the SCN?
3. Which gland releases melatonin, and when?
4. Why is a phase delay easier than a phase advance?
5. What did Ralph et al. (1990) transplant?
6. Which shift rotation did Czeisler et al. (1982) find better?
7. Name two EEG features of NREM stage 2.
8. Why is REM called paradoxical sleep?
9. What is Horne's core sleep?
10. Which sleep stage did Plihal and Born link to declarative memory?
11. Name the three Ps in Spielman's model.
12. Which neurotransmitter is lost in narcolepsy with cataplexy?

### Answers

1. The menstrual cycle, or seasonal affective disorder.
2. In the hypothalamus.
3. The pineal gland, mainly in darkness.
4. The free-running rhythm is slightly over 24 hours, so staying up later suits the clock.
5. SCN tissue from mutant hamsters with 20-hour rhythms.
6. Forward rotation (days to evenings to nights), changing less often.
7. Sleep spindles and K-complexes.
8. The brain looks awake on the EEG but the body is almost paralysed.
9. SWS and some REM, which are needed for normal function.
10. Slow-wave sleep (early-night sleep).
11. Predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating.
12. Hypocretin (orexin).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the sleep/wake cycle ultradian.
- Naming light as the pacemaker and the SCN as the zeitgeber.
- Saying west-bound travel causes worse jet lag.
- Describing REM as deep sleep.
- Giving evaluation that could fit any study, such as "it lacks ecological validity", without saying why.
- Using Siffre as if he were a large sample.
- Writing about insomnia without personality factors and genetics, which the specification names.
- Ignoring the scenario in application questions and writing a general definition instead.
- Treating correlational evidence, such as personality and insomnia, as proof of cause.
- Describing narcolepsy symptoms but giving no explanation.
