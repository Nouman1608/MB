## Unit 2 at a glance

2 hours, **open book**, 50 marks = 50% of the International AS, 20% of the full International A-level.

| Section | Focus | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| A | Essay, chosen prose text | 25 |
| B | Essay, chosen poetry selection | 25 |

One prose text and one poet's selection in total — open book, but the specification does not permit an annotated copy.

## The seven "place" angles

Learn these as a checklist of *angles to bring evidence for*, not a definition of place itself:

1. Specific geographical locations and their significance
2. The location and time period a story is set in
3. The natural world — its beauty, or its despoliation
4. Social identity and how people are positioned within society
5. Social class as expressed through place
6. Home and homeland
7. The language a text uses to represent place

A strong essay ranges across several angles rather than settling on geography alone, since "place" is deliberately left open by the specification.

## Prose text options

Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*, F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's *Americanah*, Andrea Levy's *Small Island*, Aravind Adiga's *Last Man in Tower*, Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*.

## Poetry selection options

A William Wordsworth selection, a Robert Frost selection, a Thomas Hardy selection, or a Seamus Heaney selection — each is a body of poems, not a single poem.

## Open-book technique

Because candidates may bring a clean copy of the set texts into the exam, the exam itself is not a memory test of quotations. Preparation time is better spent building an *analytical framework* — a set of angles with supporting moments already identified — that can be applied quickly once the paper starts, rather than memorising exact wording. Practise locating relevant passages fast under timed conditions, since the open-book advantage is wasted if a candidate cannot find the right material quickly.

## Poetry: compare across the selection

Because Section B assesses a poet's *selection* rather than one poem, strong answers draw evidence from at least two or three poems, showing how the writer's treatment of place varies or develops across their work — a single-poem answer under-uses the available marks even if the analysis of that one poem is strong.

## Worked example: applying the angles to a prose text

Take *The Great Gatsby* as a model of how several angles combine in one text. The geographical split
between West Egg and East Egg maps onto social class (new money versus old money), the Valley of
Ashes sits between them as a third space of human cost, and the green light across the water works as
a place-based symbol of an unreachable home. A condensed exam answer does not need to cover every
angle exhaustively — it needs to show the *language* constructing the place (recurring imagery of
light, water, distance) and connect that construction explicitly to class or aspiration, rather than
describing the settings as background detail.

## Unit 1 vs Unit 2: don't mix up the conditions

| | Unit 1 | Unit 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Genre | Tragedy | Place |
| Text types | Two plays | One prose text, one poetry selection |
| Book policy | Closed book | Open book (clean copy only) |
| Preparation focus | Memorised quotation bank | Analytical framework, fast retrieval |

Revising the two units together risks blending their quite different exam conditions — keep a clear
mental separation between "closed book, plays, tragedy conventions" and "open book, prose and poetry,
place angles."

## Exam traps

- Reducing "place" to physical geography only, ignoring class, identity, politics and language angles.
- Writing the poetry answer from a single poem in the selection.
- Bringing an annotated copy of the text into the open-book exam, which is not permitted.
- Confusing Unit 1 (tragedy, closed book) revision with Unit 2 (place, open book) — the two units assess different genres and different exam conditions.
- Spending unequal revision time on Sections A and B despite both being worth 25 marks.

## Timing under exam conditions

With 2 hours for two 25-mark essays, aim for roughly equal time on each section, leaving a short
window at the end to reread both answers against the question actually asked. Because the exam is
open book, resist the temptation to spend exam time searching for quotations from scratch — that
search should already be done in preparation, with only quick confirmation needed on the day.
