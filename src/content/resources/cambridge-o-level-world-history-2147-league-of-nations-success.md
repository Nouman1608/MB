**Key question 2 of Core Content Option B in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks "To what extent was the League of
Nations a success?"** "To what extent" means a balance sheet: you need named successes and failures, an explanation of
why each turned out as it did, and a judgement on the whole record. The specification names exactly which disputes and
which humanitarian areas to know, so learn those cases thoroughly rather than every event of the period. The question
was the Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2025 and is available on Paper 1 throughout 2024–2026.

Condensed recall is in the [League of Nations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-league-of-nations-success-revision-notes/),
and exam questions with mark schemes are in the
[League of Nations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-league-of-nations-success-practice/).
The [O Level twentieth century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-twentieth-century-1919-1989/) shows how
the League fits Option B.

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. How far did weaknesses in the League's organisation and membership make failure inevitable?
2. How successful were the League's attempts at peacekeeping in the 1920s?
3. How important was the League's humanitarian work?
4. How far did the Depression make the work of the League more difficult in the 1930s?

Specified content: the structure, aims and membership of the League; successes and failures in peacekeeping in the
1920s (Vilna 1920, the Aaland Islands 1920–21, Corfu 1923, Bulgaria 1925); humanitarian work on refugees, health,
working conditions and slavery; and the causes, events and consequences of the Manchurian and Abyssinian crises.

## Structure, aims and membership

**Aims:** to prevent war through collective security, to encourage disarmament, to improve working and living
conditions, and to settle disputes by discussion. Its headquarters were in Geneva.

| Body | Role | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Assembly | Every member had one vote; met once a year | Decisions needed unanimity, so action was slow |
| Council | Met more often; permanent members Britain, France, Italy and Japan, plus elected members | Each permanent member had a veto; the great powers put their own interests first |
| Secretariat | Civil service that kept records and ran the agencies | Small for the work it had |
| Permanent Court of International Justice | Judges at The Hague who gave rulings on legal disputes | No power to enforce its rulings |
| Commissions and agencies | International Labour Organisation, Health Organisation, Mandates, Refugees, Slavery | Relied on governments' co-operation |

**Sanctions** came in three stages: moral condemnation, economic sanctions, and military action. The League had no army
of its own, so the last depended on members' willingness to send troops.

**Membership problems:** the USA never joined after the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles. Germany was excluded
until 1926 and left in 1933. The USSR was excluded until 1934 and was expelled in 1939 after attacking Finland. Japan
left in 1933 and Italy in 1937. Britain and France were therefore left to lead, and both were weakened by the war and
had empires to defend.

**Did weakness make failure inevitable?** These weaknesses made the League dependent on the great powers. But it did
succeed in several disputes in the 1920s, so the structure alone did not doom it. What mattered was whether the powers
involved were willing to accept its decisions.

## Peacekeeping in the 1920s: the four named disputes

| Dispute | What happened | Outcome | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vilna, 1920 | Polish forces under General Żeligowski seized Vilna, claimed as capital by Lithuania | Poland kept it | Britain and France would not act against Poland, a likely ally against Germany and Soviet Russia |
| Aaland Islands, 1920–21 | Sweden and Finland both claimed the islands, whose people were Swedish-speaking | Islands to Finland, demilitarised, with protection for Swedish language and culture; both accepted | Two small states willing to accept a ruling |
| Corfu, 1923 | An Italian general, Tellini, was murdered while marking the Greek–Albanian border; Mussolini bombarded and occupied Corfu | The Conference of Ambassadors made Greece apologise and pay compensation; Italy then left Corfu | A great power bullied a small one, and the League's ruling was bypassed |
| Bulgaria, 1925 | After a border incident, Greece invaded Bulgaria | The League ordered Greece to withdraw and pay compensation; it obeyed | Greece was a small power and Britain and France backed the League |

**Pattern:** the League succeeded when the states involved were small or willing to compromise, and failed when a
powerful state, or a state protected by one, refused to accept its decision. Corfu showed this most clearly, and
Mussolini drew the lesson that aggression could pay.

## How important was the humanitarian work?

- **Refugees.** Under Fridtjof Nansen the League returned hundreds of thousands of prisoners of war home after 1920 and
  issued "Nansen passports" so that stateless refugees could travel and work.
- **Health.** The Health Organisation co-ordinated work against diseases such as leprosy and malaria, collected data on
  epidemics and helped fight typhus in Eastern Europe.
- **Working conditions.** The International Labour Organisation, led by Albert Thomas, collected information and
  persuaded governments to adopt standards, for example on working hours and child labour, although many states would
  not accept all of them.
- **Slavery.** The Slavery Commission investigated slavery and forced labour, and the League's Slavery Convention of
  1926 committed signatories to suppress the slave trade and slavery.

**Importance:** this work improved millions of lives and continued after the League's political failure; the World
Health Organisation and the ILO, which survived into the United Nations era, built on it. But the League was judged by
its main aim, preventing war, and humanitarian success could not make up for failure in the 1930s.

## The Depression and the crises of the 1930s

**How the Depression made things harder:** after the Wall Street Crash of 1929, trade collapsed and unemployment
rose. Governments turned to economic nationalism, so economic sanctions became harder to apply. Nationalist and
militarist leaders gained support, as in Japan and Germany, and Britain and France were less willing to spend money
enforcing collective security. The World Disarmament Conference (1932–34) failed, and Germany left the League in 1933.

**Manchuria, 1931–33.** The Japanese army staged the Mukden Incident in September 1931, blaming Chinese forces for an
explosion on the South Manchurian Railway, and occupied Manchuria, creating the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932. The
**Lytton Commission** took about a year to report and condemned Japan. When the Assembly accepted the report in
February 1933, Japan left the League. No sanctions followed: the main trading powers were not willing to act, and the
USA, an important trading partner of Japan, was not a member.

**Abyssinia, 1935–36.** After a clash at Wal Wal in December 1934, Mussolini invaded Abyssinia in October 1935. The
League quickly condemned Italy and imposed sanctions, but they did not include oil, and Britain did not close the Suez
Canal to Italian supply ships. In December 1935 the **Hoare–Laval Plan**, a secret British and French proposal to give
Italy much of Abyssinia, leaked and caused outrage. Italy captured Addis Ababa in May 1936; Emperor Haile Selassie
appealed to the League in person in June 1936; sanctions were dropped in July.

**Consequences:** the League lost credibility as a peacekeeper; Mussolini moved closer to Hitler; Hitler remilitarised
the Rhineland in March 1936 while attention was on Abyssinia. After 1936 the League played almost no part in the
crises that led to war.

## Annotated model answer: a (c) question

*"The League of Nations failed because of weaknesses in its structure." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.*

> Structural weaknesses mattered because the League had no army and needed unanimity, so it could act only through
> the great powers. In the Abyssinian crisis, sanctions were slow and excluded oil. **[Agree, with evidence.]**
> However, the same structure worked in the 1920s, for example over the Aaland Islands and Bulgaria in 1925, which
> suggests the problem was not the structure itself. **[Counter-evidence.]** The deeper cause of failure was the
> unwillingness of Britain and France to act against great powers, made worse by the Depression, which encouraged
> aggression and made sanctions economically painful. The Hoare–Laval Plan shows the two leading members working
> against the League's own policy. **[Alternative factor explained.]** So structural weakness made failure possible,
> but the decisions of the great powers in the 1930s made it happen. **[Judgement.]**

## Common mistakes

- Treating every 1920s dispute as a success. Vilna and Corfu were failures.
- Saying the League imposed no sanctions on Italy. It did, but they excluded oil.
- Writing about the humanitarian agencies vaguely. Name Nansen, the ILO and the Slavery Convention.
- Confusing the Conference of Ambassadors with the League itself in the Corfu case.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe**: the events of one named dispute in sequence.
- **Explain**: why one dispute succeeded or failed, linked to the powers involved.
- **How far / to what extent**: build a balance sheet, then judge using a criterion such as the League's main aim.
- **Sources**: cartoons about the League often show it as weak or ignored; identify which crisis is meant.
