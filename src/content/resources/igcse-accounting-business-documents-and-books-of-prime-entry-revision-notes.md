## The eight documents at a glance

| Document | Think of it as | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice | The bill | Sales journal (seller) or purchases journal (buyer) |
| Credit note | A reduction of the bill | Sales returns journal (seller) or purchases returns journal (buyer) |
| Debit note | A request for credit, or an extra charge | Followed by a credit note or an extra invoice |
| Statement of account | A periodic summary of one customer's account | Used to check the ledger account |
| Receipt | Proof that money was handed over | Cash book receipts |
| Cheque counterfoil | The payer's stub | Cash book payments |
| Paying-in slip | The depositor's record of a deposit | Cash book receipts in the bank column |
| Bank statement | The bank's version of the bank account | Charges, interest and direct debits not yet in the cash book |

**Use as a source:** the document supplies the date, the party, the amount and the nature of the transaction. You record from it; you do not guess.

**Manual or digital:** documents may be written by hand with carbon copies or made, sent and stored by software. Digital documents can be emailed and searched; handwritten ones can be lost or misread.

## Which book for which event

| Event | Book of prime entry |
|---|---|
| Goods sold on credit | Sales journal |
| Goods bought on credit for resale | Purchases journal |
| Customer returns goods | Sales returns journal |
| Goods sent back to a supplier | Purchases returns journal |
| Any receipt or payment through cash or bank | Cash book |
| Small payments from the float | Petty cash book |
| Non-current asset bought on credit; opening entries; correction of errors | General journal |

**Test:** was money received or paid at once? Then cash book. Is it a small payment from a float? Petty cash book. Otherwise ask whether it is a sale, purchase or return on credit, and use the matching journal. If it fits none, use the general journal.

## Posting rules

| Book | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Sales journal | Each customer | Sales (total) |
| Sales returns journal | Sales returns (total) | Each customer |
| Purchases journal | Purchases (total) | Each supplier |
| Purchases returns journal | Each supplier | Purchases returns (total) |
| Cash book, receipt | Cash or bank column (already in the book) | The customer or income account |
| Cash book, payment | The supplier or expense account | Cash or bank column (already in the book) |
| Discount columns | Discount allowed (total) | Discount received (total) |
| Petty cash book | Each expense column total | Petty cash account (restored from the bank through the cash book) |

Customers' and suppliers' accounts are in the sales and purchases ledgers. Everything else is in the nominal (general) ledger.

## Discounts: the two rules

- **Trade discount:** taken off the list price on the invoice. Record only the net figure. No discount account.
- **Cash discount:** a reward for paying early, entered in the cash book when the money moves. Discount allowed is given by you to customers. Discount received is given to you by suppliers.

**Worked reminder.** Wintergill Cycles sells 25 helmets, list price 40 each, to Darrowby Sports with 20% trade discount and an allowance of 2.5% if it settles within 7 days.

```
Gross 25 x 40                       1,000.00
Less trade discount 20%              (200.00)
Net invoice (sales journal)            800.00
Cash discount if paid in time         20.00
Cash received in the bank            780.00
```

Darrowby then returns 3 helmets. The credit note is 3 x 40 less 20% = 96.00, so the balance is 704.00. If Darrowby pays in time on that balance, the discount is 17.60 and the bank receives 686.40. The discount applies to what is actually owed, not the original invoice.

## The cash book

**Dual function:** a book of prime entry **and** the ledger account for cash and bank. Because of this, the ledger has no cash or bank account to post to.

**Payment methods and columns**

| Method | Column |
|---|---|
| Notes and coins | Cash |
| Cheque | Bank |
| Debit card or credit card | Bank |
| Online payment or bank transfer | Bank |

**Balancing method**

1. Total each of the cash and bank columns on both sides.
2. Take the smaller total from the larger to find the balance carried down.
3. Put the balance on the smaller side so both totals agree.
4. Bring the balance down on the opposite side on the next day.
5. Total the discount columns separately and post them: allowed as a debit, received as a credit.

**Contra:** cash paid into the bank (or drawn out) appears on both sides of the cash book and is not posted anywhere else.

## Petty cash and the imprest system

**Purpose:** to control small payments by giving one person a fixed amount and checking all spending against vouchers.

**Method**

1. Start with the float.
2. Record each voucher and analyse it to an expense column.
3. Total the columns and check they equal the total column.
4. Restore: the amount received equals the total spent.
5. Check: cash left plus vouchers equals the float.

Worked reminder: a float of 80, with vouchers of 12.40, 9.85, 15.00, 6.30 and 17.75. Total spent 61.30, cash left 18.70, restoring payment 61.30, float back to 80.

**Imprest versus non-imprest:** under imprest the top-up changes each period and the opening float stays the same. If the top-up were a round figure the opening float would drift, and the control would be lost.

## Benefits and limitations

| | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Cash kept on the premises | Immediate small payments, change for customers | Theft, loss, miscounting, storage and insurance cost, no interest |
| Manual original entry | Cheap; no equipment; not affected by system failure | Slow; copying and arithmetic errors; harder to back up and search |
| Digital original entry | Quick; sums and postings done by the software; simple to back up and report from | Installation and staff training cost; breakdowns; one wrong input flows everywhere |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Credit note and debit note:** a credit note lowers the amount owed. A debit note requests that, or adds a charge.
- **Trade discount and cash discount:** one is on the invoice and unrecorded; the other depends on payment and is recorded.
- **Discount allowed and discount received:** which side of the deal are you on?
- **Book of prime entry and ledger:** first record versus final account; the cash book is both.
- **Invoice and statement:** one transaction versus a summary of many.
- **Receipt and paying-in slip:** proof of payment handed over versus a record of money deposited.

## Quick self-test

1. Which document does a seller send to summarise a customer's account at the month end?
2. Which book records goods bought on credit for resale?
3. An invoice shows 60 items at 15 each, with 10% trade discount. What is entered in the sales journal?
4. The buyer in question 3 earns a 4% cash discount. How much is paid?
5. In which cash book column is a debit card receipt entered?
6. State the cash book's dual function.
7. A cash book bank column has a debit balance of 1,200 before receipts of 350 and payments of 475. What is the closing balance?
8. A float of 150 has 112.60 spent. What is restored, and what is left in the tin?
9. Name the book used when a business buys office furniture on credit.
10. Give one limitation of digital original entry.

### Answers

1. A statement of account.
2. The purchases journal.
3. 810 (900 less 90).
4. 777.60 (810 less 32.40).
5. The bank column.
6. It is a book of prime entry and the ledger account for cash and bank.
7. 1,075 (1,200 + 350 - 475).
8. 112.60 is restored; 37.40 is left.
9. The general journal.
10. Any one: cost and training, system failure or data loss, errors spread automatically.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a document correctly but not saying what it is used for.
- Entering the gross invoice figure in the sales journal.
- Calculating cash discount on the original invoice after a return.
- Using the cash column for a cheque or card receipt.
- Treating trade discount as a ledger entry.
- Writing the cash left in the tin as the petty cash top-up.
- Giving a benefit only (or a limitation only) when the question asks for both.
- Mixing up discount allowed and discount received.
