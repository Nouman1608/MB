## What "methods in context" tests

The methods in context question asks you to apply your knowledge of a **specific research
method** (e.g. questionnaires, unstructured interviews, participant observation) to a
**specific research topic within the Education component** (e.g. researching pupils' attitudes,
or teachers' labelling practices). It rewards genuine methodological reasoning applied to a
concrete situation, not memorised definitions of methods in isolation.

## The two things you must always combine

```
METHOD           TOPIC/CONTEXT
(practical,       (education-specific: age of pupils,
 ethical,          power relationships in a classroom,
 theoretical        sensitivity of the topic, school
 issues of the      setting, timetabling)
 method itself)
        \\           //
         COMBINE THEM
```

A method-only answer (e.g. "questionnaires are cheap and quick") scores low. A context-only
answer (e.g. "pupils might lie to a teacher") also scores low. The highest marks go to answers
that explain how a **specific feature of the method** interacts with a **specific feature of
researching that topic in an educational setting**.

## Common practical issues, applied to education

| Issue | Applied to schools/education |
|---|---|
| Access | Needing gatekeeper permission from head teachers, parents, and often ethics committees |
| Power/status | Pupils may see researchers as authority figures, similar to teachers |
| Time constraints | School timetables limit when research can take place |
| Vulnerability | Working with children raises informed consent and safeguarding issues |

## Practical, ethical and theoretical issues

- **Practical** — cost, time, access, the skills a method requires.
- **Ethical** — informed consent (especially with minors), protection from harm, confidentiality.
- **Theoretical** — validity (does the method capture the true picture?), reliability (could it be
  repeated with the same results?), representativeness.

## Exam traps

- Writing generic method strengths/weaknesses without linking them to education specifically.
- Discussing the topic's sensitivity without connecting it to a specific feature of the named method.
- Forgetting the power imbalance between adult researchers and child/teenage participants — a
  recurring, education-specific issue examiners reward when it is explained, not just mentioned.

## Self-test

1. What are the two elements that must always be combined in a methods-in-context answer?
2. Give one practical issue specific to researching inside a school.
3. Why is informed consent a particularly significant ethical issue in education research?
4. Name the three broad categories of issue methods in context can test.

**Answers:** 1. The named research method's own strengths/limitations, and a specific feature of the education topic or setting being researched. 2. Any one: needing gatekeeper permission from head teachers or parents, working within timetabling constraints, or the power imbalance between researcher and pupils. 3. Because participants are often minors, meaning consent typically also needs to be obtained from a parent or guardian, and children may not fully understand what they are consenting to. 4. Practical, ethical and theoretical issues.
