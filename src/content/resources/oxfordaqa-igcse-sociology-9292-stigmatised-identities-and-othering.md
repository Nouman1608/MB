This guide covers the content statement "**Stigmatised identities; othering**" in **3.2.2.3** of
Paper 2 in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). The specification names **Goffman** and
**Fanon** among the sociologists students "would benefit from studying" for 3.2.2, and both wrote
directly on these ideas. The [Socialisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation/)
explains how identities are socially constructed; this page explains how some identities come to be
devalued and how whole groups are defined as outsiders. It connects to
[formal and informal social control](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-formal-and-informal-social-control/),
because stigma and othering are powerful forms of control.

## Why stigma and othering belong under social control

Social control works not only through laws and sanctions but through the way people are seen. When a
characteristic is treated as shameful, people who have it may be excluded, and others are discouraged
from behaving in the same way. When a group is defined as fundamentally different, it can be treated
unequally without most people questioning it. Both processes therefore keep existing norms and power
relationships in place.

## Stigmatised identities: Goffman

**Erving Goffman**, in *Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity* (1963), defined
**stigma** as an attribute that is deeply discrediting: it leads others to see a person as tainted or
less than whole, so that one characteristic overshadows everything else about them. He called the
result a **spoiled identity**.

Goffman described three broad types of stigma:

1. **Physical:** visible bodily differences or disabilities.
2. **Character:** attributes seen as moral failings, such as a criminal record, addiction, mental
   illness or unemployment.
3. **"Tribal":** stigma attached to race, nation or religion, which can be passed down through
   families.

He also distinguished:

- **Discredited** people, whose stigma is visible or already known;
- **Discreditable** people, whose stigma is hidden and could be revealed.

Discreditable people may try **passing**, concealing the stigma, for example hiding a prison record
from an employer. Discredited people may try **covering**, reducing how noticeable the stigma is in
interaction. This shows the interactionist focus of Goffman's work: stigma is something managed in
everyday encounters, not simply a fixed fact.

### Stigma and labelling

Goffman's ideas link to **Becker's** labelling theory. Becker argued that a deviant label can become a
**master status**, overriding all other statuses: a person becomes "an ex-prisoner" first and a
parent, worker or neighbour second. This can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy, because a person
excluded from work and respect may find it harder to stay within the law.

### Total institutions

In *Asylums* (1961), Goffman studied **total institutions**: places such as prisons, psychiatric
hospitals and military training camps, where people live cut off from wider society under one
authority. On entry, inmates experience a "mortification of the self": personal possessions, clothes
and sometimes names are removed. This shows formal social control reshaping identity, and links to
re-socialisation in 3.2.2.2.

## Othering

**Othering** is the process of defining a group as fundamentally different from, and usually inferior
to, "us". The group becomes "them": strange, threatening or less human. Othering simplifies diverse
people into a single stereotype, and it justifies treating them unequally.

Othering can be seen in:

- media representations of migrants or refugees as a threat;
- historical justifications of colonial rule as a "civilising" mission;
- religious or ethnic minorities blamed for social problems;
- caste-based exclusion, such as the treatment of Dalits in India, where "untouchability" was
  abolished by the Constitution of 1950 but discrimination has persisted.

### Fanon and colonial racism

**Frantz Fanon**, a psychiatrist born in Martinique, examined the effects of colonial racism. In
*Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), he argued that colonialism taught Black colonised people to see
themselves through the eyes of the white coloniser, so that they came to absorb the coloniser's view
of them as inferior. Othering therefore damages not only how a group is treated but how its members
see themselves. In *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), Fanon analysed colonialism as a system that
dehumanised colonised peoples. His work links 3.2.2.3 to the "legacies of colonialism and
enslavement" in 3.2.1.5.

### A related idea: Said

Students may also meet **Edward Said's** *Orientalism* (1978), which argued that Western writing and
art portrayed "the East" as exotic, backward and irrational, which supported colonial power. The
specification credits appropriate use of sociologists beyond those named.

## Perspectives on stigma and othering

The specification asks for functionalist, Marxist, feminist and social action/interactionist
perspectives on social control, and each can be applied here.

- **Functionalist:** stigmatising those who break norms marks the boundaries of acceptable behaviour,
  in the way Durkheim argued punishment does, so stigma reinforces shared values.
- **Marxist:** othering divides the working class. When migrants or minorities are blamed for
  unemployment or crime, attention is drawn away from inequalities created by capitalism; the othered
  group becomes a scapegoat.
- **Feminist:** women are stigmatised for breaking gender norms, for example through judgements about
  dress, sexuality or motherhood, which acts as informal control over women's behaviour.
- **Interactionist:** Goffman and Becker show that stigma is created and managed in interaction, and
  that labels can be resisted or renegotiated.

## Consequences and resistance

| Consequence | Example |
|---|---|
| Discrimination | Being refused housing or jobs |
| Exclusion | Being avoided socially or segregated |
| Internalisation | Coming to see oneself as inferior (Fanon) |
| Self-fulfilling prophecy | A master status pushing someone towards further deviance |

People also **resist**: social movements reclaim stigmatised identities (for example campaigns for
disability rights or against caste discrimination), and individuals challenge labels in everyday
interaction, as interactionists would predict.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain what Goffman meant by a "discreditable" stigma. [4 marks]*

> Goffman distinguished stigma that is visible or known from stigma that is hidden (1). A discreditable
> stigma is one that others do not yet know about, such as a past prison sentence (1). The person
> could be discredited if it were revealed (1), so they may try "passing", concealing it, for example
> by not mentioning it to employers (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far othering helps to explain inequalities between groups. Use examples from your own
society or a society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **For:** othering justifies unequal treatment; Fanon on colonial racism and internalised inferiority;
  Said on Orientalism; media portrayals of migrants; caste-based exclusion.
- **Against or qualifying:** inequalities also have material and economic causes (Marxist view);
  laws against discrimination; groups resist and reclaim identities.
- **Application:** a named society, for example how a particular minority is represented in its
  media.
- **Judgement:** othering is central to how inequalities are justified and sustained, but it works
  alongside economic and legal structures.

## Common mistakes

- Treating stigma as a fixed fact rather than a social judgement.
- Confusing discredited and discreditable.
- Describing othering simply as "being different" rather than being defined as inferior.
- Naming Fanon without his argument about seeing oneself through the coloniser's eyes.
- Forgetting the link to social control: both processes enforce norms and power.
- No example from a named society in an extended answer.
