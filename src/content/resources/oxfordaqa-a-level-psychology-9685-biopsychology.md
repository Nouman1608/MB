This guide covers **3.2.1 Biopsychology** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Psychology (9685) specification, Version 3.1. It is Section A of the Unit 2 exam: 30 marks of compulsory questions within a 1 hour 30 minute, 90-mark paper. The specification lists four areas: the **divisions of the nervous system**; the **structure and function of sensory, relay and motor neurons** and **synaptic transmission**, including neurotransmitters, excitation and inhibition; the **endocrine system** and the **fight or flight response**, including adrenaline; and **localisation of function and hemispheric lateralisation**, including the motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory and language centres, Broca's and Wernicke's areas, split-brain research, and **plasticity and functional recovery** after trauma.

The [Unit 2 study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-psychology-biopsychology-development-research-methods/) summarises each area. This guide adds the structural detail, sequences and evaluation needed for higher-mark questions. Condense it with the [biopsychology revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-biopsychology-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [biopsychology practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-biopsychology-practice/).

## Divisions of the nervous system

```
Nervous system
├── Central nervous system (CNS): brain and spinal cord
└── Peripheral nervous system (PNS)
    ├── Somatic: voluntary movement; sensory information from skin, muscles and joints
    └── Autonomic: involuntary control of internal organs and glands
        ├── Sympathetic: arousal ("fight or flight")
        └── Parasympathetic: calming ("rest and digest")
```

The **brain** processes information and controls behaviour; the **spinal cord** relays messages between the brain and the body and controls simple reflexes. The two autonomic branches work antagonistically: the sympathetic branch speeds the heart, the parasympathetic branch slows it.

## Neurons

All neurons have **dendrites** (receiving signals), a **cell body** (containing the nucleus), an **axon** (carrying the electrical impulse) and **axon terminals** (passing signals on). Many axons are covered in a fatty **myelin sheath**, with gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**, which speeds conduction.

| Neuron | Function | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory** | Carries impulses from receptors to the CNS | Long dendrites, short axon; cell body to one side |
| **Relay** | Connects neurons within the CNS | Short dendrites, short axon |
| **Motor** | Carries impulses from the CNS to muscles and glands | Short dendrites, long axon |

A reflex arc shows all three in sequence: a sensory neuron carries a signal from a pain receptor to the spinal cord, a relay neuron passes it on, and a motor neuron makes a muscle withdraw the hand -- before the brain registers pain.

## Synaptic transmission

1. An electrical impulse (action potential) reaches the **presynaptic terminal**.
2. **Synaptic vesicles** containing neurotransmitter move to the membrane and release it into the **synaptic cleft**.
3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to **receptor sites** on the **postsynaptic** neuron.
4. This changes the electrical charge of the postsynaptic membrane.
5. The neurotransmitter is then removed: taken back into the presynaptic neuron (**reuptake**) or broken down by enzymes.

Transmission is **one-way**, because vesicles exist only in the presynaptic terminal and receptors only on the postsynaptic membrane.

**Excitation and inhibition.** **Excitatory** neurotransmitters raise the chance that the postsynaptic neuron will generate an action potential; **inhibitory** neurotransmitters lower that chance. Serotonin and GABA usually have inhibitory effects, calming activity; adrenaline and noradrenaline usually have excitatory effects. Whether a neuron fires depends on **summation** of all the excitatory and inhibitory inputs it receives: if the net effect reaches the threshold, an action potential is triggered.

## The endocrine system

The endocrine system uses **glands** to release **hormones** into the bloodstream; hormones act on target cells with matching receptors. It acts more slowly than the nervous system but its effects last longer.

| Gland | Hormone (example) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothalamus | Releasing hormones | Controls the pituitary; links nervous and endocrine systems |
| Pituitary | Many, including ACTH | "Master gland" controlling other glands |
| Adrenal medulla | Adrenaline, noradrenaline | Rapid fight or flight changes |
| Adrenal cortex | Cortisol | Longer-term stress response |
| Thyroid | Thyroxine | Metabolic rate |
| Pineal | Melatonin | Sleep and wake cycles |
| Testes and ovaries | Testosterone, oestrogen | Sexual development and behaviour |

## The fight or flight response

1. The **amygdala** detects a threat and signals the **hypothalamus**.
2. The hypothalamus activates the **sympathetic** branch of the autonomic nervous system.
3. Sympathetic nerves stimulate the **adrenal medulla**, which releases **adrenaline** into the bloodstream.
4. Adrenaline produces changes that prepare the body for action: increased heart rate and blood pressure, faster breathing with dilated airways, dilated pupils, glucose released for energy, blood diverted to muscles, and digestion and salivation inhibited.
5. When the threat passes, the **parasympathetic** branch restores the resting state.

**Evaluation.** The response helped our ancestors survive physical threats, but modern stressors such as exams rarely require physical action, so repeated activation may damage health. Gray (1988) argued the first response is often to **freeze** -- staying still and alert -- before fighting or fleeing. Taylor et al. (2000) proposed that females more often respond with **tend and befriend**, protecting offspring and seeking social support, which suggests early research based mainly on male participants did not represent everyone.

## Localisation of function

The idea that specific areas of the brain have specific functions.

- **Motor area** (frontal lobe, at the back): controls voluntary movement on the **opposite** side of the body.
- **Somatosensory area** (parietal lobe, at the front): processes touch, temperature and pain from the opposite side of the body; body parts needing more sensitivity, such as the lips, have larger areas.
- **Visual area** (occipital lobe): receives input from the eyes; the **right visual field** of each eye is processed by the **left hemisphere**, and vice versa.
- **Auditory area** (temporal lobe): processes sound.
- **Broca's area** (usually left frontal lobe): speech production. Broca (1861) studied a patient who could understand language but could say little except "tan"; after his death, damage was found in this region. Damage causes slow, effortful speech.
- **Wernicke's area** (usually left temporal lobe): language comprehension. Wernicke (1874) described patients who spoke fluently but meaninglessly and had trouble understanding speech.

**Evaluation.** Brain scans support localisation: language tasks reliably activate left-hemisphere areas. However, Lashley (1950) removed areas of rats' cortex and found that learning a maze was not tied to any one area, suggesting some functions are distributed (**equipotentiality**). Modern scans of the brains of Broca's patients have suggested damage extended beyond Broca's area, so language may depend on networks rather than single areas.

## Hemispheric lateralisation and split-brain research

**Lateralisation** means some functions are processed mainly by one hemisphere: language is usually lateralised to the left, while the right is often better at spatial tasks and recognising faces.

Sperry's split-brain research tested people who had undergone surgery severing the **corpus callosum** to control severe epilepsy, so the hemispheres could not communicate. An image was flashed briefly to one visual field while the patient fixed on a central point.

- An object shown to the **right visual field** (left hemisphere) could be **named**.
- An object shown to the **left visual field** (right hemisphere) could not be named, but the patient could pick it out by touch with the **left hand**.

**Evaluation.** The procedure was highly controlled. But samples were very small, the patients had a history of epilepsy that may have affected brain organisation, and in everyday life the effects are less obvious because eye movements let both hemispheres see most things.

## Plasticity and functional recovery

**Plasticity** is the brain's ability to change its structure and function with experience. Brain scans by Maguire et al. (2000) showed that the posterior hippocampus -- a region involved in spatial memory -- was larger in licensed London taxi drivers than in controls, and was larger still in those with more years in the job.

**Functional recovery** after trauma, such as a stroke, occurs as the brain reorganises:

- **Axonal sprouting:** new nerve endings grow to connect with undamaged areas.
- **Recruitment of homologous areas:** the equivalent area in the opposite hemisphere takes over a function, for example language.
- **Reformation of blood vessels** and **denervation supersensitivity**, where remaining neurons become more responsive.

Recovery tends to be greater in younger people and with rehabilitation such as physiotherapy and speech therapy.

**Evaluation.** Evidence of plasticity supports rehabilitation programmes. However, much of the evidence is correlational (Maguire's drivers may have chosen the job because of their memory) or from animals, and recovery varies greatly between individuals.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the autonomic system in the CNS.
- Confusing sensory and motor neuron structure.
- Saying the left hemisphere controls the left side of the body.
- Writing that adrenaline is released by the pituitary.
- Describing split-brain research without stating which hemisphere processed which visual field.
