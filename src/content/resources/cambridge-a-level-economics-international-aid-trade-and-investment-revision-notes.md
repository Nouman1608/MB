## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aid | Transfer of money, goods or expertise on easy terms or free, for development or emergencies |
| Bilateral aid | From one government directly to another |
| Multilateral aid | Pooled by many donors and passed on through an international body |
| Non-governmental aid | From charities and other voluntary organisations |
| Grant | Aid that need not be repaid |
| Concessional loan | Loan with below-market interest or long repayment; part of it is a gift |
| Tied aid | Aid that must be spent on the donor's goods or services |
| Technical assistance | Training, advice and expertise rather than money |
| Terms of trade | Export price index divided by import price index, times 100 |
| Income terms of trade | Terms of trade multiplied by the export volume index, divided by 100 |
| Tariff escalation | Higher import tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials |
| Portfolio investment | Purchase of shares or bonds abroad without taking control of a firm |

## Forms of aid (11.5.1)

- By source: bilateral, multilateral, non-governmental.
- By terms: grants or concessional loans.
- By conditions: tied or untied.
- By purpose: project, programme or budget support, technical assistance, emergency relief, food aid.

Match the form to the purpose. Emergency relief meets short-run needs. Project aid and technical assistance build long-run capacity. Budget support relies on the recipient's institutions.

## Reasons for giving aid

| Motive | Idea | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Humanitarian | Relieve suffering after disaster or famine | Hard to help long-run growth |
| Developmental | Fill the savings gap and foreign exchange gap | Assumes the funds are invested |
| Political and strategic | Influence, alliances | Aid may follow donor interests, not need |
| Commercial | Tied aid supports donor exporters | Raises the cost of what is bought |
| Shared problems | Disease, instability, migration | Benefits are slow and uncertain |

## Effects of aid: two-sided chains

**Positive chain:** aid for infrastructure and schools, then higher capital and human capital, then greater productive capacity (long-run aggregate supply shifts right), then higher potential output and living standards.

**Negative chains**
- Large inflow, then higher demand for the local currency, then stronger exchange rate, then dearer exports and cheaper imports, then loss of competitiveness.
- Free food aid, then supply shifts right, then lower local food prices, then lower farm incomes and less incentive to grow food.
- Aid replaces tax revenue, then a weak tax system and less government accountability.
- Fungibility: aid for one use releases domestic funds for another, perhaps unproductive.

## Importance of aid

Measure by size, not total.

Aid as % of GNI = (aid / GNI) x 100. Aid per head = aid / population.

Aid matters most for small, low-income economies with a weak tax base and limited private finance. Then evaluate: type, predictability, governance, and what else is available (trade, private investment, remittances).

## Calculation reminders

**Grant element and loans.** Grant element = (face value - present value of repayments) / face value. Discount repayments at a market rate. A loan of 50 at 0% interest repaid in one lump after 10 years, discounted at 8%: present value = 50/1.08^10 = 23.16. Grant element = (50 - 23.16)/50 = 53.7%.

**Aid ratio.** Country C has GNI of 5,000 and aid of 150, population 6 million. Aid/GNI = 150/5,000 = 3.0%. Per head = 150/6 = 25 units.

**Tied aid.** A tied grant of 90 where donor prices are 20% above world prices. Effective value = 90/1.20 = 75. Loss = 15.

**Savings gap.** Target growth 4%, capital-output ratio 3, so investment needed = 4 x 3 = 12% of GDP. Saving is 9%, so the gap is 3% of GDP. With GDP of 2,000 the gap is 60.

**Real value.** Nominal aid of 200 where the price index has risen from 100 to 125: real aid = 200/1.25 = 160, a fall of 20%.

## Trade and investment (11.5.2)

**Terms of trade method.**
1. Divide the export price index by the import price index.
2. Multiply by 100.
3. Compare with the base year. A fall means more exports are needed per unit of imports.
4. For income terms, multiply by export volume / 100.

Example: export prices 110, import prices 125. ToT = 110/125 x 100 = 88.0. With export volume at 120, income terms = 88.0 x 120/100 = 105.6, so the country can afford more imports in total even though each export buys fewer.

**Primary product volatility.** Price elasticity of demand is low, so a supply change moves price a lot. Example: PED = -0.25 and sales rise 5%. Price change = -5/0.25 = -20%. Revenue = 1.05 x 0.80 = 0.84, a fall of 16%.

**Barriers faced.** Tariff escalation, quotas, rich-country farm subsidies and standards that raise costs.

**Evaluating liberalisation**

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Comparative advantage, larger markets | Dependence on a few exports |
| Imported technology and capital goods | Infant industries face strong competition |
| Competition lowers prices and raises efficiency | Adjustment costs, job losses in some sectors |

**Investment flows.** Richer countries' savers invest in poorer countries for higher returns. Portfolio flows can be sudden. Stable flows that fund productive capacity are more useful than short-term flows.

## Method in steps: an evaluation question on aid

1. Define the form of aid in the question (grant, loan, tied, emergency).
2. Give one developed benefit chain, ending with a macroeconomic effect such as higher potential output.
3. Give one developed cost chain, such as the exchange rate effect or dependency.
4. Use a figure if one is supplied, such as aid as a share of GNI.
5. Judge. Say what the outcome depends on (size relative to the economy, governance, type of aid, predictability, alternatives) and then reach a conclusion that follows from it.

The same method works for trade and investment questions. Replace the aid chains with access to markets, terms of trade and capital flows, and judge which matter most for the country described.

## Must-know distinctions

- Bilateral and multilateral: who delivers it, not who pays.
- Terms of trade and income terms of trade: price only against price and volume.
- Nominal and real aid.
- Aid amounts and aid as a share of GNI.
- Tied aid (the cost is a price premium) and conditional aid (the cost is loss of policy choice).
- Portfolio investment and bank loans: portfolio is a purchase of financial assets.

## Quick self-test

1. Country D has GNI of 4,000 and receives aid of 200. State aid as a percentage of GNI.
2. Tied aid of 120 is spent where prices are 25% above world prices. Calculate the effective value.
3. Export price index 90 and import price index 120: calculate the terms of trade.
4. Using question 3, explain what the figure says about the country's trade.
5. State two reasons why aid might not raise growth.
6. Sales of a primary product rise 8% when PED is -0.5. Calculate the price change and the revenue change.
7. Define tariff escalation and state its effect on a poor exporter.
8. Nominal aid is 300 and the price index for aid purchases rises from 100 to 120. Calculate real aid.
9. A target growth rate is 6% and the capital-output ratio is 3. Calculate the investment needed as a percentage of GDP.
10. Give one benefit and one risk of portfolio investment inflows.

### Answers

1. 200/4,000 x 100 = 5.0%.
2. 120/1.25 = 96.
3. 90/120 x 100 = 75.0.
4. Each unit of exports buys 25% fewer imports than in the base year. Volume could offset this.
5. Aid may be spent on consumption, may be lost to corruption, may strengthen the exchange rate and hurt exporters, or may create dependency.
6. Price: -8/0.5 = -16%. Revenue: 1.08 x 0.84 = 0.9072, a fall of 9.3%.
7. Higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials; it discourages processing in the poor country and keeps it as a raw-material supplier.
8. 300/1.20 = 250.
9. 6 x 3 = 18% of GDP.
10. Benefit: finance for investment above domestic saving. Risk: sudden outflows weaken the currency.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting aid in total with no comparison to GNI or population.
- Listing positive and negative effects of aid with no judgement.
- Describing tied aid but not the price premium.
- Reversing the terms of trade fraction.
- Calling a terms of trade fall a fall in export revenue.
- Presenting the aid-growth link as automatic.
- Not using a figure from the data when asked to use the source.
- Giving only one side of liberalisation.
