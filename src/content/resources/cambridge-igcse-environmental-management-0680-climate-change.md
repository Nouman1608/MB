Sub-topic 4.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680) is the longest in Topic 4. It runs from a definition of a greenhouse gas, through six named causes of rising greenhouse gas concentrations and the enhanced greenhouse effect, to twelve named impacts, twelve strategies for reducing carbon footprints, four adaptation strategies, and a final "discuss the benefits and limitations" point. The best preparation is to learn each list as cause and effect rather than as separate items. The [atmosphere and human activities study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-the-atmosphere-and-human-activities-study-guide/) gives the topic overview, and the [atmosphere study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-0680-the-atmosphere/) covers the natural greenhouse effect this sub-topic builds on.

## Greenhouse gases (4.2.1 and 4.2.2)

The syllabus defines a **greenhouse gas as a gas that absorbs radiation and emits the energy as thermal (heat) energy**. You must be able to state that **carbon dioxide (CO₂), water vapour (H₂O) and methane (CH₄)** are greenhouse gases.

## Causes of increased concentrations (4.2.3)

| Cause | Gas | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Combustion of fossil fuels | CO₂ | Burning coal, oil and gas in power stations, vehicles and industry releases carbon stored for millions of years |
| Agriculture | CH₄ | Digestion by cattle, decomposition of manure and flooded rice fields release methane |
| Deforestation | CO₂ | Fewer trees means less carbon dioxide removed by photosynthesis; burning cleared trees also releases it |
| Changes in land use | CH₄ | Draining wetlands releases methane |
| Cement manufacture | CO₂ | Calcium carbonate (limestone) is heated to produce calcium oxide (lime), releasing carbon dioxide |
| Increasing population and energy use | CO₂ | More people using more energy, mostly from fossil fuels |

For cement, the chemistry is simple enough to quote: calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide. The kiln is also usually heated by burning fossil fuels, so cement releases carbon dioxide in two ways.

## The enhanced greenhouse effect (4.2.4)

The syllabus states two linked points: **greenhouse gases absorb solar radiation causing global warming**, and **global warming can result in climate change**. Higher concentrations of greenhouse gases mean more of the radiation given off by the Earth is absorbed and re-emitted back to the surface, so average temperatures rise. That warming is global warming; the resulting long-term changes in temperature, rainfall, wind and extreme weather are climate change. Keep the order: gases → warming → climate change.

## Impacts of climate change (4.2.5)

Grouping the twelve impacts makes them easier to recall and to link in extended answers.

**Physical changes**
- Increase in temperature of the Earth's surface.
- Increase in temperature of the ocean surface. (Knowledge of ENSO, El Niño and La Niña is not required.)
- Melting of ice sheets, glaciers and permafrost leading to a rise in sea level. Water from melting land ice adds to the ocean; warmer water also expands.
- Ocean acidification: more carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater, lowering its pH, which makes it harder for corals and shell-forming organisms to build their skeletons and shells.
- Increased frequency and severity of extreme weather events, leading to flooding and loss of land, drought and wildfires.

**Effects on living things**
- Disruption to food chains, for example when the timing of plankton blooms or insect emergence no longer matches the needs of the animals that feed on them.
- Change in biodiversity, as species unable to move or adapt decline and others spread.
- Loss of habitat, such as sea ice, coral reefs and low-lying coastal wetlands.
- Forced migration of humans and other animals away from areas that become too hot, dry or flooded.
- Increased pest outbreaks and invasive species, as warmer winters let pests survive and spread to new areas.

**Effects on food**
- Change in crop yields: some cold regions may gain a longer growing season, while hotter, drier regions lose yield.
- Food shortages where yields fall, extreme weather destroys crops and fisheries decline.

## Reducing carbon footprints (4.2.6)

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product, organisation or country, usually expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent. The syllabus names twelve strategies.

| Strategy | How it reduces emissions |
|---|---|
| Reduced use of fossil fuels | Switch to renewable energy, walk, cycle, use public transport |
| Sustainable food choices | Plant-based diet, seasonal food and locally produced food reduce emissions from livestock, heated greenhouses and transport |
| Reduction in livestock farming | Fewer cattle means less methane from digestion and manure |
| Reduction in number of children per woman | Slower population growth reduces future energy demand |
| Increase in energy efficiency | Insulation, efficient appliances and vehicles give the same service with less energy |
| Reforestation and afforestation | Replanting lost forest and planting new forest removes carbon dioxide by photosynthesis |
| Carbon sequestration | Capture, remove and store carbon dioxide, for example from power station chimneys, storing it underground |
| Transport policies | Congestion charges, low-emission zones, better public transport, cycle lanes |
| International agreements and policies | Countries agree targets to cut emissions and report progress |
| Taxation | Taxes on fuels or emissions make high-carbon choices more expensive |
| Research and climate models | Computer models predict future climate, helping governments plan and test policies |
| Use of artificial intelligence (AI) | Predicting impacts of climate change and developing strategies to reduce them |

## Adaptation (4.2.7 and 4.2.8)

The syllabus defines **climate change adaptation as altering behaviour, practices and infrastructure to adapt to the impacts of climate change**. Reducing footprints tackles the cause; adaptation copes with the effects. The four named strategies are:

- **Crops that resist warmer, drier or wetter conditions**, such as drought-tolerant or flood-tolerant varieties.
- **Improve flood defences**: sea walls, embankments, flood barriers, restored wetlands that store water.
- **Legislation about building near coasts and on floodplains**, limiting new homes in areas at risk.
- **Building design and materials**: raised floors, flood-resistant materials, shading, insulation and reflective roofs to keep buildings cool.

## Worked example 1: a carbon footprint calculation

The figures are invented for practice. A family's yearly car travel is 15,000 km. Their car emits 0.18 kg of carbon dioxide per km. They switch 40% of these journeys to a bus that emits 0.05 kg per passenger-km.

1. Original car emissions = 15,000 × 0.18 = 2,700 kg.
2. Distance switched = 15,000 × 40 / 100 = 6,000 km.
3. Remaining car emissions = 9,000 × 0.18 = 1,620 kg.
4. Bus emissions for one person = 6,000 × 0.05 = 300 kg.
5. New total = 1,620 + 300 = 1,920 kg, a saving of 780 kg.
6. Percentage saving = 780 / 2,700 × 100 = 28.9%.

## Worked example 2: an annotated "discuss" answer

Question: discuss the benefits and limitations of taxation and reforestation as strategies to manage climate change. [6]

Taxing fossil fuels makes high-emission choices more expensive, so people and businesses use less fuel or switch to cleaner energy (benefit). The money raised can fund renewable energy (benefit). However, fuel taxes hit poorer households hardest and may be politically unpopular, so governments may keep them low (limitation).

Reforestation removes carbon dioxide by photosynthesis and also restores habitat (benefit). But trees take decades to grow, need land that may be wanted for farming, and the stored carbon is released again if the forest burns or is cleared (limitation).

Overall, taxation works faster across a whole economy, while reforestation gives long-term storage; both are needed, alongside international agreements (judgement).

Why it scores: benefits and limitations for each strategy, each explained, and a reasoned conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Treating global warming and climate change as the same thing. Warming is the temperature rise; climate change is the wider result.
- Confusing ozone depletion with climate change. The ozone hole does not cause global warming.
- Saying deforestation releases methane. The syllabus links deforestation to reduced removal of carbon dioxide; wetland drainage releases methane.
- Mixing up mitigation strategies (reducing footprints) and adaptation strategies.
- Writing "sea levels rise because the ice melts" without saying it is land ice: ice sheets and glaciers. Melting floating sea ice does not significantly raise sea level.

## Exam technique

**State** and **define** questions need the syllabus wording. **Describe and explain** impact questions want a chain: cause → change → effect on people or ecosystems. For **discuss the benefits and limitations**, cover each named strategy, link points to the context (a low-lying country, a farming region, a wealthy city) and give a judgement. In data questions on carbon dioxide or temperature, quote values with units and calculate percentage change from the earlier value.
