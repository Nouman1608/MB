## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mean | Sum of values divided by the number of values |
| Range | Largest value minus smallest value |
| Standard deviation | A measure of spread about the mean; formula provided |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the pattern of the rest |
| Null hypothesis | There is no difference or correlation; any apparent one is due to chance |
| Critical value | The value a test statistic must reach for significance at the chosen probability |
| Gradient | Change in y divided by change in x |
| Intercept | The point where a line crosses an axis |
| Tangent | A straight line touching a curve at one point, used to find the rate there |
| Correlation | Two variables change together |

## Processing data: 1.1.3(a) and (b)

1. Check for **anomalies** and say if you leave them out.
2. Calculate the **mean** of the repeats.
3. Convert to a **rate**: quantity ÷ time.
4. Calculate **percentage change**: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
5. Describe **spread** with range or standard deviation.

Standard deviation (formula provided): s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ].

Example: 8.0, 8.5, 9.0 cm³ in 2 min. Mean 8.5 cm³. Rate 4.25 cm³ min⁻¹. Σ(x − x̄)² = 0.50, ÷ 2 = 0.25, square root gives s = 0.50 cm³.

## Significant figures: 1.1.3(c)

- Report a result to the same number of significant figures as the **least precise** data, or one more.
- 17.6 ÷ 3.0 = 5.8667 → **5.9**.
- 2.40 g ÷ 15 cm³ = 0.16 g cm⁻³.
- Round at the end only.
- 2.40 has three significant figures. 0.0050 has two.

## Choosing a statistical test

| Test | Data | Question | Result means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chi-squared | Counts in categories (observed and expected) | Do observed numbers fit the expected ratio? | Calculated ≥ critical: significant difference |
| Student's t-test | Two sets of continuous measurements | Do the means differ significantly? | Calculated ≥ critical: means differ |
| Spearman's rank | Paired measurements | Is there a correlation? | rₛ, ignoring its sign, ≥ critical: significant correlation |

Formulae and critical-value tables are provided in the assessment where needed. Know which test to use and what the result means.

**Degrees of freedom**

- Chi-squared: number of categories − 1.
- Unpaired t-test: n₁ + n₂ − 2.
- Spearman's: use n (number of pairs) in the table.

**Standard conclusion:** If the calculated value is greater than or equal to the critical value at p = 0.05, reject the null hypothesis. There is only a 5 % probability or less that the result is due to chance alone.

**Worked values**

- Chi-squared: observed 62 and 18, expected 60 and 20 → χ² = 0.27, 1 d.f., critical 3.84 → not significant.
- Spearman: Σd² = 68, n = 6 → rₛ = 1 − 408 ÷ 210 = −0.94; critical 0.886 → significant negative correlation.
- t-test: means 12.14 and 13.16, s = 0.32 and 0.29, n = 5 each → t = 5.3, 8 d.f., critical 2.31 → significant.

## Graphs: 1.1.3(d)

| Data | Graph |
|---|---|
| Two continuous variables | Line graph or scatter graph |
| Categorical independent variable | Bar chart |
| Frequency of continuous values | Histogram |
| Correlation | Scatter graph |

**Plotting checklist**

1. Independent variable on the x-axis.
2. Axis labels with quantity and unit, such as "Time / s".
3. Scale uses more than half the grid; steps of 1, 2, 5 or 10.
4. Points plotted to within half a small square.
5. Line of best fit (smooth) or ruled lines between points, as the data suggest.

**Gradient:** (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁), using two points far apart on the line, not two data points. Unit: unit of y per unit of x.

Example: (30, 4.5) and (150, 22.5) gives 18.0 ÷ 120 = 0.15 cm³ s⁻¹.

**Rate from a curve:** draw a tangent and find its gradient. A tangent through (0, 0) and (20, 16) gives an initial rate of 0.80 cm³ s⁻¹.

**Intercept:** where the line meets the axis. A zero intercept can show no product at the start.

## Writing a valid conclusion

1. State the **relationship** shown by the data.
2. Quote **values**.
3. Use "supports" or "does not support".
4. Explain with **biology**.
5. Add a **statistical** statement if a test was done.
6. Avoid "proves". **Correlation is not causation.**

## Exam traps

- Gradient from two data points that are not on the line.
- Independent variable on the y-axis.
- No units on the gradient.
- Mean taken including an anomaly without comment.
- Too many significant figures.
- Dividing by n, not n − 1, for standard deviation.
- Comparing a negative rₛ with the critical value without ignoring the sign.
- Saying a significant correlation shows cause.

## Self-test

1. Calculate the mean of 4.1, 4.3 and 4.2.
2. A mass falls from 3.50 g to 3.15 g. Calculate the percentage change.
3. Calculate 12.4 ÷ 3.1 to an appropriate number of significant figures if the data are 12.4 and 3.1.
4. Which test compares observed and expected phenotype numbers?
5. Which test compares the means of two samples?
6. A chi-squared test has 3 categories. State the degrees of freedom.
7. A calculated χ² of 5.1 is compared with a critical value of 3.84 (1 d.f.). State the conclusion.
8. A line of best fit passes through (20, 3) and (80, 15). Calculate its gradient.
9. A tangent at t = 0 passes through (0, 2) and (10, 14). Calculate the initial rate if the y-axis is volume in cm³ and the x-axis is time in s.
10. Which variable should go on the x-axis?
11. State what an intercept of zero shows on a graph of product against time.
12. A student finds a significant positive correlation between two variables. Can she conclude that one causes the other?

### Answers

1. (4.1 + 4.3 + 4.2) ÷ 3 = 4.2.
2. (3.15 − 3.50) ÷ 3.50 × 100 = −10 %.
3. 12.4 ÷ 3.1 = 4.0 (two significant figures).
4. Chi-squared.
5. Student's t-test.
6. 3 − 1 = 2.
7. 5.1 is greater than 3.84, so the difference is significant and the null hypothesis is rejected.
8. (15 − 3) ÷ (80 − 20) = 0.20.
9. (14 − 2) ÷ (10 − 0) = 1.2 cm³ s⁻¹.
10. The independent variable.
11. No product was present at the start of the reaction.
12. No. A correlation shows that the variables change together, not that one causes the other.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to remember the t-test formula?**
No. The formula and the critical-value tables are provided where needed. You need to know when to use each test and how to interpret the result.

**When do I use a line of best fit and when do I join the points?**
Use a line of best fit when the data show a trend with some scatter. Join the points with ruled lines when the exact shape between points is not known.

**Which significance level should I use?**
Use p = 0.05 unless the question says otherwise.
