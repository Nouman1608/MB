This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 8.3 of Unit 1, Changing population, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): challenges and opportunities. It is part of the core studied by every SL and HL student and examined in Paper 2. The [8.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-challenges-and-opportunities-revision-notes/) already set out challenges and opportunities by population situation, define the dependency ratio and demographic dividend, and cover policies in China, France and Singapore, Japan's ageing and Kerala. This page adds what those notes leave short: when the demographic dividend window opens and closes, how to measure the burden of ageing, how sex ratios at birth become distorted and why it matters, how countries respond to people trafficking, and a framework for judging any population policy. Test yourself with the [8.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-8-3-practice/); the [Population distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/) gives the wider picture of the unit.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the challenges and opportunities created by population change and structure.
- Explain global and regional trends in family size, sex ratios and ageing.
- Evaluate policies that respond to population change, including those on fertility, ageing, gender equality and trafficking.
- Judge how far challenges can be turned into opportunities.

## The demographic dividend window

The demographic dividend is not automatic. It depends on a **window**: a period during which the working-age share of the population is unusually high.

1. **Before the window:** high fertility means a large share of children, so dependency is high.
2. **The window opens** as fertility falls: fewer children are born, while the large earlier cohorts reach working age.
3. **The window closes** as those large cohorts reach old age and the share of older dependants rises.

### Worked example 1: a closing gap

An invented country at two dates:

| | Earlier | Later |
|---|---|---|
| Aged 0-14 (%) | 45 | 30 |
| Aged 15-64 (%) | 52 | 64 |
| Aged 65 and over (%) | 3 | 6 |
| Dependency ratio | (45 + 3) / 52 x 100 = 92.3 | (30 + 6) / 64 x 100 = 56.3 |

The dependency ratio falls from about 92 to about 56 dependants per 100 people of working age. Each worker supports fewer dependants, so households can save more and governments can spend more per child on education. Whether that becomes growth depends on **jobs, education, health and governance**: a large working-age population without jobs brings unemployment, informal work and emigration pressure instead. The window typically lasts a few decades, so timing matters.

## Measuring the burden of ageing

The **old-age support ratio** is the number of people aged 15-64 for each person aged 65 and over. It shows how many potential workers support each older person.

### Worked example 2: funding pensions

An invented ageing country:

| | Now | Projected |
|---|---|---|
| Aged 15-64 (millions) | 60 | 52 |
| Aged 65 and over (millions) | 20 | 30 |
| Old-age support ratio | 60 / 20 = 3.0 | 52 / 30 = 1.73 |

If each older person receives a pension of 12,000 a year paid from current taxes (a **pay-as-you-go** system):

- Now: total cost = 20 million x 12,000 = 240,000 million; per working-age person = 240,000 million / 60 million = **4,000**.
- Projected: 30 million x 12,000 = 360,000 million; per working-age person = 360,000 million / 52 million = **6,923**.

The cost per working-age person rises by about 73%. Responses each change one part of the calculation: raising the pension age moves people from the dependent group into the working group; encouraging more women and older people into work raises the number who actually pay taxes; immigration adds working-age people; automation raises output per worker; and reducing pension levels cuts the cost per pensioner, which is the least popular option.

## Sex ratios at birth

The **sex ratio at birth** is the number of male births per 100 female births. Without intervention it is naturally about **105**. Higher values point to sex-selective practices.

### Worked example 3: missing births

An invented region records 118,000 male and 100,000 female births in a year, a sex ratio of **118**.

- At the natural ratio of 105, 118,000 boys would be matched by 118,000 / 1.05 = about 112,381 girls.
- The shortfall is 112,381 - 100,000 = about **12,400 girls** in a single year.

Causes include strong son preference, linked to inheritance, old-age support or marriage costs, combined with access to prenatal sex determination, and pressure to have fewer children. Consequences appear two decades later: a surplus of young men who cannot find partners, with concerns about social instability and trafficking of women for marriage. Responses include banning sex-selective practices and, more effectively, raising the status of girls and women through education, inheritance rights and old-age pensions that reduce dependence on sons.

## Responding to people trafficking

Trafficking is the recruitment and movement of people by force, fraud or coercion for exploitation, such as forced labour or sexual exploitation. It differs from smuggling, in which migrants pay to cross a border and the relationship ends on arrival. The main international agreement is the UN protocol on trafficking in persons, adopted in 2000 alongside the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime and often called the Palermo Protocol. It commits states to:

- **prevent** trafficking, for example through awareness campaigns and reducing the poverty that makes people vulnerable;
- **protect** victims, treating them as victims rather than as illegal migrants;
- **prosecute** traffickers.

Evaluation points: trafficking is hidden, so its scale is hard to measure; enforcement varies widely between countries; and tough border controls can push migrants towards smugglers and traffickers. Effective responses combine law enforcement with safe, legal migration routes and support for victims.

## Judging a population policy

Use the same five questions for any policy, whether pro-natalist, anti-natalist, ageing, gender or migration:

| Criterion | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Did the target indicator change, and can the change be attributed to the policy? |
| Timescale | How long until effects appear, and do they last? |
| Unintended consequences | Did it create new problems, such as a distorted sex ratio or rapid ageing? |
| Rights and equity | Did it respect individual choice, and who bore the costs? |
| Cost | Was it affordable and sustainable for the state? |

Attribution is the hardest part. Fertility fell in many countries without coercive policy, as female education, urbanisation and falling infant mortality spread, so a fall after a policy does not prove the policy caused it. Singapore's pro-natalist incentives show the reverse: generous measures have not lifted fertility far, suggesting that once low fertility is established, the costs of housing, childcare and careers matter more than one-off payments.

## Annotated model answer

*Discuss the extent to which an ageing population is a challenge rather than an opportunity.* **(sample paragraph)**

"An ageing population raises the cost of pensions and health care while the working-age population shrinks, so the old-age support ratio falls and the tax burden per worker rises **[challenge with a measure]**. However, healthier older people can work longer and volunteer, and their spending supports a silver economy of care, leisure and technology **[opportunities]**. Policy can shift the balance: raising the pension age, increasing women's employment and admitting working-age migrants all improve the ratio **[responses]**. Ageing is therefore mainly a challenge where pension systems are pay-as-you-go and labour markets are rigid, but a manageable transition where policy adapts early **[conditional judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating the demographic dividend as automatic once fertility falls.
- Using the overall dependency ratio when the question is about ageing.
- Calling any sex ratio above 100 abnormal, forgetting the natural value of about 105.
- Confusing trafficking with smuggling.
- Judging a policy only by whether a number changed, ignoring attribution and rights.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** challenges and opportunities with a measure, such as a support ratio, and a mechanism.
- **Evaluate** policies with criteria and named examples, then a judgement.
- **Discuss** and **to what extent** need both challenges and opportunities, for different groups.
- Use unseen data by calculating ratios first, then interpreting what they mean for the place.
