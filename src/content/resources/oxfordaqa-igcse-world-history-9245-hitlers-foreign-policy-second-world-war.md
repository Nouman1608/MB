**This guide covers the whole of the Section B option Hitler's foreign policy and the origins of the Second World War, 1933-41 in OxfordAQA International GCSE History (9245).** The specification divides it into two parts: "Increasing international tensions, 1933-38" and "Appeasement, the outbreak of war and the global conflict 1938-41". Section B is assessed through a 3-mark knowledge question, a 6-mark question explaining one cause or consequence, a 9-mark question explaining two, and a 12-mark question that ends in a judgement. The [Paper 1 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-paper-1-section-b/) explains the question types for all four options. Follow up with the [Hitler's foreign policy revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-hitlers-foreign-policy-second-world-war-revision-notes/) and the [Hitler's foreign policy practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-hitlers-foreign-policy-second-world-war-practice/).

## What the specification says

The option "focuses on the causes of the Second World War and seeks to show how and why conflict occurred and why it proved difficult to resolve the issues which caused it". It looks at "the legacy of the First World War, the nature of interwar militarism and aggression, the breakdown of diplomacy and the final crisis before the outbreak of war".

## Increasing international tensions, 1933-38

### Hitler's aims

Hitler's foreign policy had four main aims. He wanted to **overturn the Treaty of Versailles**, which he regarded as unjust. He wanted to **rearm** Germany. He wanted to unite all German-speaking peoples in one state, including Austria and the Germans of Czechoslovakia. And he wanted **Lebensraum** (living space) in the east, which meant taking land from Poland and the USSR. He also hated communism. The Treaty had limited the German army to 100,000 men, banned an air force and conscription, demilitarised the Rhineland and forbade union with Austria.

### The origins of tension

In October 1933 Hitler withdrew from the **Disarmament Conference** and from the **League of Nations**, arguing that other nations were not disarming and that Germany was being treated unequally. This allowed him to rearm without restriction, and it showed that the League had no power to stop him. Rearmament began in secret and grew rapidly.

### The development of tensions

- **The German-Polish Non-Aggression Pact (1934).** Hitler made a ten-year pact with Poland. It eased fears of Germany and weakened Poland's links with France.
- **The Saar (January 1935).** A plebiscite under League supervision gave about 90 per cent of the vote for return to Germany. It was legal and a propaganda success.
- **Conscription (March 1935).** Hitler announced conscription and the existence of an air force, the Luftwaffe, breaking Versailles. Britain, France and Italy met at Stresa in April 1935 and formed the **Stresa Front** to oppose further treaty breaking.
- **The Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935).** Britain allowed Germany a navy of up to 35 per cent of the size of the British navy. Britain made the deal without consulting France or Italy, which broke the Stresa Front and implied that Britain accepted treaty-breaking.

### The escalation of tension

- **Abyssinia and the Axis.** Italy's invasion of Abyssinia in 1935 divided the western powers from Mussolini, who then moved closer to Hitler. The **Rome-Berlin Axis** (1936) was a friendship agreement. Japan, which had invaded Manchuria in 1931, signed the **Anti-Comintern Pact** with Germany in 1936, directed against communism; Italy joined in 1937.
- **The Rhineland (March 1936).** Hitler sent troops into the demilitarised Rhineland, breaking Versailles and the Locarno Treaties. France would not act without Britain, and Britain felt Germany was entering its own back garden. The success gave Hitler confidence and weakened France's alliances.
- **The Spanish Civil War (1936-39).** Germany sent aircraft and the Condor Legion to help General Franco, testing new weapons; the bombing of Guernica (1937) shocked the world. Britain and France followed a policy of non-intervention. Cooperation with Mussolini grew closer.
- **The Anschluss (March 1938).** Hitler forced the Austrian chancellor to resign and German troops entered Austria on 12 March, banned by Versailles. A plebiscite later claimed overwhelming support. Britain and France protested but did nothing.

## Appeasement, the outbreak of war and the global conflict, 1938-41

### Appeasement

**Appeasement** meant giving way to reasonable demands to avoid war. Neville Chamberlain, British Prime Minister from 1937, was its leading supporter. Britain and France had different priorities: Britain was concerned about its empire and its weak defences, France about its security against Germany and its army.

**Arguments for appeasement.** Many people remembered the horror of the First World War and feared that bombers would destroy cities. Britain's rearmament was not yet complete. Some thought that Versailles had been too harsh and that Hitler's claims to unite Germans were fair. Some feared communism more than Nazism. The Dominions did not want another war. Appeasement could buy time for rearmament.

**Arguments against.** It encouraged Hitler to make more demands, abandoned small states, and wasted the chance to stand with Czechoslovakia and the USSR. Churchill was a leading critic.

### The Sudetenland Crisis and Munich, 1938

About three million Germans lived in the Sudetenland, in western Czechoslovakia. Encouraged by Hitler, their leader Konrad Henlein demanded autonomy. Hitler demanded that the region join Germany and prepared to invade. Chamberlain met Hitler twice in September, at Berchtesgaden and Godesberg. At the **Munich Conference** (29-30 September 1938), Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed that Germany would take the Sudetenland. Czechoslovakia and the USSR were not invited. Hitler promised he had no further territorial demands in Europe.

The agreement was welcomed in Britain and France as a way of avoiding war. But it left Czechoslovakia without its border defences and made the USSR doubt the West's willingness to stand against Hitler.

### The ending of appeasement

In March 1939 Hitler broke the Munich Agreement by occupying the rest of Bohemia and Moravia. Slovakia became a puppet state. This was not a case of uniting Germans, so it showed that Hitler could not be trusted. British public opinion hardened. Britain began to introduce conscription in April and gave guarantees to Poland on 31 March 1939, joined by France, promising to defend it if attacked.

### The outbreak of war

In May 1939 Germany and Italy signed the **Pact of Steel**, a military alliance. Hitler then demanded Danzig and a route through the Polish Corridor. Talks between Britain, France and the USSR in the summer failed through mistrust. On 23 August 1939 Germany and the USSR signed the **Nazi-Soviet Pact**, which promised not to attack each other. In a secret part they agreed to divide Poland and the Baltic states. For Hitler the pact meant that he could attack Poland without fear of a war on two fronts. For Stalin it gave time to prepare and some territory, and followed his distrust of the West after Munich. Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and Britain and France declared war on 3 September.

**Responsibility.** The main responsibility lies with Hitler, whose aims led to war. But the failure of the League, the harshness of Versailles, appeasement, and Stalin's pact all played a part.

### A regional crisis becomes a global conflict

By 1941 the war had spread. Germany conquered Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and France in 1939-40, and failed to defeat Britain in the Battle of Britain. Italy entered the war in 1940 and fighting spread to North Africa. In Asia, Japan had been at war with China since 1937. On 22 June 1941 Germany invaded the USSR in **Operation Barbarossa**. On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked the American base at **Pearl Harbor**, bringing the USA into the war; Germany and Italy declared war on the USA a few days later. The conflict now covered Europe, Asia, the Pacific and Africa.

## Writing the answers

**Worked example: a 6-mark answer.**

*Explain one way in which the Rhineland remilitarisation of 1936 affected international relations.* **[6]**

> The remilitarisation increased Hitler's confidence and weakened the system of security in Europe **[way]**. In March 1936 German troops marched into the demilitarised Rhineland, breaking the Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno Treaties **[knowledge]**. France, which would not act without British support, did nothing, and Britain thought Germany was only entering its own territory **[knowledge]**. This showed Hitler that the western powers would not use force, so he was encouraged to go further with the Anschluss, and France's allies in eastern Europe began to doubt its support **[explanation]**.

**The 12-mark judgement.** For "Appeasement was the main reason why war broke out in 1939", cover appeasement in depth (the arguments for it, Munich, and the effect on Hitler and Stalin), then other factors (Hitler's aims, the failure of the League, the Nazi-Soviet Pact, the Polish guarantee). Compare them, and reach a judgement, for example that Hitler's aims made war likely, but appeasement made it easier for him to act.

## Common mistakes

- Dating the Rhineland in 1938 (it was March 1936) or the Anschluss in 1936 (March 1938).
- Saying that Czechoslovakia was invited to Munich. It was not.
- Confusing the Stresa Front (1935) with the Rome-Berlin Axis (1936).
- Treating appeasement as simple cowardice. Explain the reasons.
- Forgetting the secret terms of the Nazi-Soviet Pact.
- Writing that Britain declared war because of Czechoslovakia. It was because of the invasion of Poland.
