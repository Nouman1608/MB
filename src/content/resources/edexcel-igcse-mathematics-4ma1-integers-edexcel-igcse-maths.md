This study guide covers one section of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017): **section 1.1, Integers**, in topic 1, Numbers and the number system. Section 1.1 is Foundation Tier content, and the Higher Tier entry for 1.1 says "See Foundation Tier", so everything here is needed on both tiers.

Integers are the whole numbers: positive, negative and zero. Almost every other topic in the course relies on handling them quickly and without sign errors. ## Place value

Each position in a whole number is worth ten times the position to its right. In 4 365 210, the 6 is in the ten-thousands column, so its value is 60 000, not 6.

| Millions | Hundred thousands | Ten thousands | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Units |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 3 | 6 | 5 | 2 | 1 | 0 |

**Reading and writing large numbers.** Split the digits into groups of three from the right, and read each group with its name: 7 040 090 is "seven million, forty thousand and ninety". A zero holds a place: leaving out the zero in the thousands column would change the number completely.

**Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, 1000.** The digits move one, two or three places; they do not "gain a zero". 360 × 100 = 36 000 and 52 000 ÷ 1000 = 52. Thinking in terms of moving digits is what makes the same idea work later for decimals.

## Ordering integers

On a number line, numbers increase to the right. A negative number further from zero is **smaller**: −15 < −4, even though 15 > 4. To order a mixed list, place the negatives first (most negative first), then zero, then the positives.

The symbols matter: −2 > −9 reads "negative two is greater than negative nine", and it is true because −2 is to the right of −9.

## Directed numbers in practical situations

A directed number has a sign showing direction from zero. The specification names temperatures, but questions also use bank balances (an overdraft is a negative balance), heights above and below sea level, floors above and below ground, and goal differences.

**Worked example 1.** Leila's bank account is overdrawn by £45. She pays in £120, then pays a bill of £96. What is her balance now, and how much must she pay in to reach a balance of £50?

```
start           −45
pay in 120      −45 + 120 = 75
pay bill 96     75 − 96 = −21      -> overdrawn by £21
to reach 50     50 − (−21) = 50 + 21 = £71
```

Write the final answer in the language of the context: "overdrawn by £21" or "a balance of −£21" are both clear. A difference between two directed numbers is always "larger minus smaller", which is why the gap from −21 to 50 is 71.

## The four rules with negative numbers

**Adding and subtracting.** Adding a negative moves left; subtracting a negative moves right.

- 8 + (−13) = 8 − 13 = −5
- −6 − (−10) = −6 + 10 = 4
- −9 − 7 = −16 (both move left)

**Multiplying and dividing.** Same signs give a positive answer; different signs give a negative answer.

| Signs | Product or quotient | Example |
|---|---|---|
| + and + | + | 6 × 4 = 24 |
| − and − | + | (−6) × (−4) = 24 |
| + and − | − | 6 × (−4) = −24 |
| − and + | − | −24 ÷ 6 = −4 |

With several factors, count the negatives: an even number of negative signs gives a positive answer, an odd number gives a negative one. (−2) × (−3) × (−5) = −30, because there are three negatives.

## Brackets and the hierarchy of operations

Work in this order: **brackets**, then **indices** (powers and roots), then **division and multiplication** from left to right, then **addition and subtraction** from left to right. Multiplication does not come before division; they have equal rank, as do addition and subtraction.

**Worked example 2.** Work out [(−3)² + 4 × (−5)] ÷ (−11).

```
(−3)² = 9                  index inside the bracket first
4 × (−5) = −20             then multiply
9 + (−20) = −11            then add
−11 ÷ (−11) = 1            finally divide: same signs, positive
```

A fraction line acts as a bracket around the top and around the bottom. (36 − 4²)/(2 − 7) means (36 − 16) ÷ (−5) = 20 ÷ (−5) = −4. On a calculator, type the brackets in, or use the fraction key.

## Odd, even, prime, factors and multiples

- **Even** numbers are divisible by 2; **odd** numbers are not. Odd + odd = even, odd × odd = odd, and even × any integer = even.
- A **factor** of n divides n exactly. List factors in pairs so none are missed: the factors of 84 are 1 and 84, 2 and 42, 3 and 28, 4 and 21, 6 and 14, 7 and 12.
- A **multiple** of n is in the n times table: 7, 14, 21, … Every number is a factor and a multiple of itself.
- A **prime number** has exactly two factors, 1 and itself. So 1 is not prime, and 2 is the only even prime.

**Testing whether a number is prime.** Divide by the primes 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, … only as far as the square root of the number. If none divides exactly, it is prime.

**Worked example 3.** Is 221 prime? Is 233 prime?

```
√221 ≈ 14.9: test 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13
221 ÷ 13 = 17 exactly          -> 221 = 13 × 17, not prime

√233 ≈ 15.3: test 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13
none divides exactly           -> 233 is prime
```

You do not need to test 15, because 15 = 3 × 5, and 3 and 5 have already been tested.

## Prime factors, common factors and common multiples

The **prime factors** of a number are the primes that divide it: the prime factors of 90 are 2, 3 and 5. A **common factor** of two numbers divides both; a **common multiple** is in both times tables.

**Worked example 4.** Two runners start together at the start line of a track. Amira completes a lap every 6 minutes and Ben every 8 minutes. When are they next at the start line together, and how many laps has each run?

```
multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, …
multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, …
first common multiple: 24
```

They meet after **24 minutes**: Amira has run 24 ÷ 6 = 4 laps and Ben 24 ÷ 8 = 3 laps. Listing works well for small numbers; for larger numbers, prime factorisation (section 1.4) is faster.

"Together again" and "at the same time" point to common multiples. "Share equally", "largest possible group" and "cut into equal lengths" point to common factors.

## Common mistakes

- Writing −15 > −4 because 15 is bigger than 4.
- Treating −3² as (−3)²: the index applies only to 3 unless the bracket is there, so −3² = −9.
- Working strictly left to right and ignoring the order of operations, or doing all multiplications before any divisions.
- Calling 1 a prime number, or listing 9, 15 or 21 as primes because they are odd.
- Confusing factors with multiples: 6 is a factor of 18; 18 is a multiple of 6.
- Answering a context question with a bare number, such as −21, without saying what it means.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **Write down**: the answer can be read straight off, such as the value of a digit or the primes in a list; no working is needed.
- **Work out** or **Calculate**: show each step, especially sign changes, so method marks can be given if the final answer is wrong.
- **Show that**: every step must be written down and the final line must match the given answer exactly.
- **Explain** or **Give a reason**: a short sentence using the correct word, for example "9 is not prime because it has three factors: 1, 3 and 9".
- A calculator may be used on every 4MA1 paper, but directed-number work is quicker and safer if you can do it mentally and use the calculator to check.
