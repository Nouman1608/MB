Intertextuality: connecting texts is where, as the [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/) puts it, an exclusively literary course
can draw comparisons across a wide span of literary history and form. These notes go deeper into how
to build that comparison, alongside the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-subject-guide/) and
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-assessment-revision-notes/) already
on the site.

## What "intertextuality" actually means for revision

At its simplest, intertextuality is the idea that no literary work exists in isolation -- every text
is written and read against a backdrop of other texts, whether the writer is deliberately echoing,
responding to, subverting, or unconsciously working within a tradition established by earlier works.
Revise the distinction between these different relationships: an **echo** or **homage** borrows form
or content admiringly; a **response** engages critically or in dialogue with an earlier work's ideas;
a **subversion** deliberately overturns or undermines the conventions or assumptions of an earlier
work or genre.

## Building a genuine intertextual comparison

A weak comparative answer places two works side by side and describes each separately. A strong one
identifies a specific, genuine point of connection -- shared theme, shared form, one work
deliberately referencing or reacting against the other -- and uses that connection to say something
neither work would reveal fully on its own. Structure comparative practice around three questions:
what specifically connects these two works (not just "both deal with loss," but a more precise shared
concern or technique); how does each work treat that shared element differently; and what does
comparing them reveal that reading either work alone would not?

## Working across literary traditions and periods

Because this course draws from a genuinely international range of literary traditions, and often
includes works originally written in languages other than the one being studied (read in
translation), intertextual comparison here can span very different historical periods and cultural
contexts -- a modern novel read alongside a much older play, for instance. Revise being able to
account for context explicitly in a comparison: two works can share a theme while treating it very
differently because of when, where and in what literary tradition each was written, and a strong
comparative answer accounts for that difference rather than treating both works as though they were
produced in the same cultural moment.

## Connecting this component to Time and space

Intertextuality and [Time and space](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/) are not
separate skills to revise in isolation -- understanding a work's own historical and cultural context
(Time and space) is often the key to explaining why it echoes, responds to or subverts another work
in the specific way it does (Intertextuality). Revising these two components together, rather than as
unconnected blocks, produces stronger comparative essays.

## How this feeds Paper 2 and the individual oral

Paper 2's comparative essay draws directly on the skill this component develops -- structured,
evidence-based comparison across two studied literary works. Practising timed comparative writing
specifically (not just single-text close reading) is essential preparation, since comparing under
time pressure is a distinct skill. For the individual oral, which pairs a work originally written in
the studied language with a work studied in translation, this component's comparative habits transfer
directly -- the oral itself is, in effect, a spoken intertextual and contextual analysis of two works
connected around a common global issue.

## A worked mini-example

Consider comparing a modern novel that reimagines events from a much older epic poem's minor
character's point of view. A surface-level comparison might simply note that "both works deal with
war and its aftermath." A stronger intertextual comparison identifies the specific relationship: the
modern novel is a deliberate **response** to the epic, deliberately subverting whose perspective is
centred -- shifting attention from the epic's celebrated hero to a figure the original text treats as
peripheral. This shift is not just thematic overlap; it is the modern work actively arguing with the
older one about whose experience of war deserves narrative attention. A comparative essay that names
this specific relationship, rather than a generic shared topic, demonstrates exactly the kind of
analysis this component and Paper 2 both reward.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish an intertextual "echo," a "response," and a "subversion."
2. What three questions should structure a genuine comparative analysis of two literary works?
3. Why might two works sharing the same broad theme still need to be compared with attention to their
   different historical and cultural contexts?
4. How does practising this component's skills prepare you specifically for the individual oral?

**Answers:** 1. An echo or homage borrows form or content admiringly; a response engages critically or
in dialogue with an earlier work's ideas; a subversion deliberately overturns or undermines the
conventions or assumptions of an earlier work or genre. 2. What specifically connects the two works;
how each work treats that shared element differently; and what comparing them reveals that neither
work would show fully alone. 3. Because two works can share a theme while treating it very differently
due to when, where and in what literary tradition each was written -- ignoring context risks
flattening genuine differences into a superficial similarity. 4. Because the individual oral pairs a
work in the studied language with a work studied in translation around a common global issue, which is
itself a spoken exercise in the same evidence-based, context-aware comparison this component
practises in writing.
