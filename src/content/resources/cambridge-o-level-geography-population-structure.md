This guide covers **sub-topic 1.3 Population Structure** of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217),
2026 (Version 3). The
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/)
introduces pyramid shapes across the whole theme; this page goes further into the calculations,
the less obvious pyramid shapes and the case study you need for this sub-topic.

## What 1.3 asks you to do

You need to be able to:

- identify and give **reasons** for different types of population structure;
- explain the **implications** (consequences) of those structures;
- interpret **population pyramids** for countries at different levels of economic development;
- understand the idea of a **dependent population** and the **dependency ratio**;
- use a named case study of a **country with a high dependent population**.

The skill is a chain: describe the shape, explain it with birth rates, death rates and migration,
then say what it means for the country.

## Key idea 1: what a pyramid shows

A population pyramid is a pair of horizontal bar charts placed back to back: males usually on the
left, females on the right, and age groups (often five-year bands) rising from the youngest at the
bottom. The horizontal axis shows either **numbers** or **percentages** of the total population.

Read the axis before anything else. If the pyramid shows percentages, two countries of very
different size can be compared directly. If it shows numbers, a wider bar in one country may just
mean that country is larger.

Four parts of the pyramid tell you different things:

| Part of the pyramid | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Width of the **base** | Birth rate in recent years |
| How quickly the sides **narrow** | Death rate and life expectancy |
| Width of the **top** | Proportion of elderly people and life expectancy |
| **Bulges** and **notches** | Past events: baby booms, wars, epidemics or migration |

## Key idea 2: structures at different levels of development

**Low-income, youthful structure.** A wide base and steep, concave sides that narrow quickly.
Birth rates are high and life expectancy is relatively low. **Niger**, which has one of the highest
fertility rates in the world, is a well-known example of a very young population.

**Middle-income, transitional structure.** The base has started to narrow because the birth rate
is falling, but there is a broad band of young adults born when birth rates were still high. This
"bulge" of people entering working age can be an advantage, sometimes called a demographic dividend,
if there are enough jobs for them.

**High-income, ageing structure.** Nearly vertical sides, a narrow base and a wide top. Low birth
rates and long life expectancy. **Japan** has one of the oldest populations in the world, with more
than a quarter of its people aged 65 or over, and its total population has been shrinking for more
than a decade.

**Migration-shaped structure.** Some countries have a large bulge of working-age males caused by
labour migration. Gulf states such as the **United Arab Emirates** show far more men than women in
the 20–49 age groups because most migrant workers are men. A source area shows the reverse: a
"waist" in the young-adult bands, often more pronounced on the male side.

Other details to spot:

- More females than males at older ages, because women have a longer life expectancy.
- Slightly more boys than girls at birth in most countries; a much larger imbalance can indicate
  sex-selective practices, as in China during the one-child policy.
- A notch in one cohort, then a smaller notch about 25–30 years later, as the missing generation
  has fewer children of its own.

## Key idea 3: the dependent population

People aged **under 15** and **65 and over** are usually treated as **dependants**: they are less
likely to be in paid work and rely on the economically active population (15–64) for income,
taxes and care.

```
dependency ratio      = (0-14 + 65 and over) / (15-64) x 100
youth dependency      = (0-14) / (15-64) x 100
old-age dependency    = (65 and over) / (15-64) x 100
```

Splitting the ratio matters, because two countries can have similar overall ratios for opposite
reasons, and the consequences are completely different.

## Worked example: two countries compared

Illustrative data, in percentages of the total population:

| | 0–14 | 15–64 | 65+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country A | 43 | 54 | 3 |
| Country B | 12 | 59 | 29 |

- Country A: dependency ratio = (43 + 3) ÷ 54 × 100 = **85.2**; youth 79.6; old-age 5.6.
- Country B: dependency ratio = (12 + 29) ÷ 59 × 100 = **69.5**; youth 20.3; old-age 49.2.

Country A's dependants are almost all **children**. Country B's are mostly **elderly**. A good
answer then states the implications of each, which is where most of the marks are.

## Key idea 4: implications of a high dependent population

**Youthful population**
- Heavy demand for **schools, teachers, maternal and child health care, vaccination**.
- Future need for **jobs and housing** as children reach working age; unemployment and emigration
  if the economy cannot grow fast enough.
- A large future **workforce** and future parents, so **population momentum**: the population keeps
  growing even if fertility falls, because so many people are entering childbearing age.

**Ageing population**
- Rising costs of **pensions, health care and residential care**, paid by a shrinking workforce.
- **Labour shortages**, particularly in care work and manual jobs.
- Lower demand for schools; some close or merge.
- Positives: older people often provide childcare and volunteer work, and many have savings that
  support spending in the economy.

**Responses** depend on the type of dependency. For ageing: raising the retirement age, encouraging
more women and older people into work, pro-natalist incentives, immigration and investment in
technology. Japan, for example, introduced a new visa category for skilled workers in 2019 and has
invested in robotics for care. For youth dependency: investment in education and family planning,
and job creation for the young adults to come.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a pyramid bar by bar without any overall statement.
- Reading numbers as if they were percentages.
- Forgetting migration as a cause of an irregular shape.
- Calculating the dependency ratio as a percentage of the total population rather than of the
  15–64 group.
- Giving only problems of an ageing population and no positives or responses.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pyramid: overall shape first, then two or three specific features with figures
  read from the axis, such as "about 8% of the population are males aged 0–4".
- **Compare** two pyramids: use comparative words ("wider", "a larger proportion") and give figures
  for both.
- **Explain** a shape: link each feature to birth rate, death rate, life expectancy or migration.
- **Suggest** implications: one consequence plus why it follows.
- For the case study, name the country and include specific features of its structure and its
  government's response.
