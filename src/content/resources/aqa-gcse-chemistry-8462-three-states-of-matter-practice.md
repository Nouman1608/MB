These questions cover section **4.2.2.1 The three states of matter** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). The content is examined on Paper 1.
Question 8 and part (b) of Question 9 test the limitations of the particle model, which the specification marks
(HT only), and are labelled **Higher tier only**. Everything else is for both tiers.

Learn the content first in the [three states of matter study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-three-states-of-matter/)
and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-three-states-of-matter-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** **(a)** Name the change of state from solid to liquid. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the change of state from gas to liquid. **[1]**

**2.** A pure substance melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.

**(a)** State the temperature at which the liquid freezes. **[1]**
**(b)** State the state of the substance at 60 °C. **[1]**

**3.** Describe how the particles are arranged and how they move in a solid. **[2]**

**4.** The table gives data for three substances.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| X | -7 | 59 |
| Y | 1085 | 2562 |
| Z | -210 | -196 |

**(a)** Which substance is a liquid at 20 °C? **[1]**
**(b)** Which substance is a gas at 20 °C? **[1]**
**(c)** Which substance has the strongest forces between its particles? Give a reason. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A student heats a solid pure substance steadily. The thermometer reading rises, then stays at 78 °C for several
minutes while the solid melts, and then rises again.

**(a)** Explain why the temperature stays at 78 °C while the solid melts. **[2]**
**(b)** State the name of the temperature 78 °C for this substance. **[1]**

**6.** Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Hydrogen chloride melts at -114 °C.

**(a)** Name the type of particle in sodium chloride and the type of particle in hydrogen chloride. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of forces, why sodium chloride has a much higher melting point. **[4]**

**7.** A student says: "When water boils, the covalent bonds between the hydrogen and oxygen atoms break, which is
why a lot of energy is needed."

**(a)** Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the correct explanation of why liquid water changes to steam at 100 °C. **[2]**

**8.** (Higher tier only) The simple particle model represents particles as small solid spheres with no forces between
them. Explain **two** limitations of this model in relation to changes of state. **[4]**

**9.** A teacher says that a single atom of silicon does not melt at 1410 °C, but a lump of silicon does.

**(a)** Explain what this tells you about the properties of atoms and of materials. **[2]**
**(b)** (Higher tier only) A diagram of the lump of silicon shows touching spheres. Explain why this diagram cannot
show why silicon has a high melting point. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Melting** [1]
**(b)** **Condensing** [1]

**2. (a)** **0 °C**, because freezing happens at the melting point [1]
**(b)** **Liquid**: 60 °C is above 0 °C and below 100 °C [1]

**3.** The particles are in a **regular pattern**, touching each other [1]; they **vibrate** about fixed positions [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** **X**: 20 °C is above -7 °C and below 59 °C [1]
**(b)** **Z**: 20 °C is above its boiling point of -196 °C [1]
**(c)** **Y** [1]; it has the highest melting and boiling points, so the most energy is needed to overcome the forces
[1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** The energy supplied is used to **overcome the forces between the particles** [1]; it does not increase
the kinetic energy of the particles, so the temperature does not rise [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The **melting point** [1]

**6. (a)** Sodium chloride: **ions**; hydrogen chloride: **molecules** [1] [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Marking points, up to 4:
- Sodium chloride has a giant lattice with strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions [1].
- These act in all directions, so there are many strong bonds to overcome [1].
- Hydrogen chloride has only weak intermolecular forces between molecules [1].
- Much more energy is needed to overcome the forces in sodium chloride, so its melting point is higher [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** The covalent bonds **inside** each molecule are **not broken** when water boils [1]; the **weak
intermolecular forces between** the molecules are overcome instead [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The molecules gain enough energy to overcome the intermolecular forces [1] and move apart from each other
as a gas [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Any two limitations, each with an explanation, [2] [2]:
- The model has **no forces** between particles [1], so it cannot show that stronger forces mean a higher melting
  or boiling point [1].
- All particles are shown as **spheres** [1], but real atoms, molecules and ions have different shapes and sizes
  [1].
- The spheres are **solid** [1], but atoms have a nucleus and electrons and are mostly empty space [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Properties such as melting point belong to a **bulk material**, not to single atoms [1]; they depend on
how very many atoms are bonded and arranged together [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The diagram shows **no forces or bonds** between the spheres [1], so it cannot show the strong bonds that
must be overcome to melt silicon [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "the bonds break" when a molecular substance melts or boils.
- Giving a state without quoting the comparison of temperatures.
- Explaining a constant temperature as "the heat stops", rather than energy overcoming forces.
- Forgetting to name the particles (ions or molecules) in a comparison.
- Describing limitations of the model without saying what they prevent it from explaining.
