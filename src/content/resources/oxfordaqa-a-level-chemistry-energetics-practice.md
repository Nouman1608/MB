Take the specific heat capacity of water and of every solution as 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, with solution density 1.00 g cm⁻³. Ar: Mg 24.3. The enthalpy data are invented for practice.

## Questions

**1.** Give the meaning of the term *standard enthalpy of combustion*. **[3]**

**2.** (a) Write the equation, with state symbols, for the reaction whose enthalpy change is the standard enthalpy of formation of ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH(l). **[2]**
(b) Explain why the standard enthalpy of formation of I₂(s) is zero. **[1]**

**3.** 60.0 cm³ of 0.800 mol dm⁻³ silver nitrate solution is poured into a polystyrene cup. Excess copper powder is added and the temperature rises by 14.0 K. Calculate the enthalpy change, in kJ mol⁻¹, for the reaction of one mole of silver ions. **[4]**

**4.** A student burns 0.74 g of butan-1-ol (Mr = 74.0) in a spirit burner. The flame warms 250 g of water in a metal can and the temperature goes up by 20.0 K.
(a) Work out the enthalpy of combustion of butan-1-ol, in kJ mol⁻¹, from these results. **[4]**
(b) A data table gives −2676 kJ mol⁻¹. Give two reasons why the student's result is different. **[2]**
(c) Propose a modification to the equipment that would bring the result nearer to the table value, and say why. **[2]**

**5.** 50.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid at 19.6 °C is added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide at the same temperature. The enthalpy change of neutralisation is −57.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the highest temperature the mixture should reach, assuming no heat is lost. **[4]**

**6.** Describe how you would carry out an experiment to measure the enthalpy change when a solid dissolves in water, so that the temperature change can be found reliably. Include how you would treat the temperature readings. **[4]**

**7.** Use these enthalpies of combustion to calculate the standard enthalpy of formation of propan-2-ol, C₃H₇OH(l): carbon −394, hydrogen −286, propan-2-ol −2006 kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**8.** (a) Calculate ΔH for 2NaHCO₃(s) → Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) from these standard enthalpies of formation in kJ mol⁻¹: NaHCO₃(s) −948, Na₂CO₃(s) −1131, H₂O(l) −286, CO₂(g) −394. **[3]**
(b) The reaction 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g) has ΔH = −114 kJ mol⁻¹. ΔfH° of NO(g) is +90 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔfH° of NO₂(g). **[3]**

**9.** Calculate ΔH for C₆H₁₂(l) → C₆H₆(l) + 3H₂(g) from these enthalpies of combustion in kJ mol⁻¹: C₆H₁₂(l) −3920, C₆H₆(l) −3267, H₂(g) −286. State whether the reaction is endothermic or exothermic. **[4]**

**10.** (a) Define *mean bond enthalpy*. **[2]**
(b) Using the mean bond enthalpies below, in kJ mol⁻¹ (C–H 413, C–C 347, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464) to calculate ΔH for C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g). **[3]**
(c) Use ΔfH° values of C₃H₈(g) = −105, CO₂(g) = −394 and H₂O(g) = −242 kJ mol⁻¹ to calculate ΔH for the same reaction. **[2]**
(d) Explain why your answers to (b) and (c) are different. **[2]**

**11.** (a) ΔH for H₂(g) + Br₂(g) → 2HBr(g) is −103 kJ mol⁻¹. Mean bond enthalpies: H–H 436 and Br–Br 193 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the H–Br bond enthalpy. **[3]**
(b) Explain why bond enthalpies cannot be used directly to find ΔH for the combustion of ethanol, C₂H₅OH(l), forming H₂O(l). **[2]**

**12.** The enthalpy of formation of magnesium oxide cannot be measured directly with a calorimeter. A student measures two enthalpy changes using 100 cm³ of excess hydrochloric acid each time.
Experiment A: 0.486 g of magnesium ribbon, temperature rise 22.3 K.
Experiment B: 0.806 g of magnesium oxide (Mr = 40.3), temperature rise 6.9 K.
(a) Calculate the enthalpy change per mole of magnesium in Experiment A. **[3]**
(b) Calculate the enthalpy change per mole of magnesium oxide in Experiment B. **[2]**
(c) Use these results and ΔfH°(H₂O(l)) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹ to calculate the standard enthalpy of formation of magnesium oxide. Show your cycle. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The heat change at constant pressure when **exactly one mole** of a substance [1] reacts **fully with oxygen** [1] at **standard conditions** (100 kPa, stated temperature), all species in standard states [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A version missing "one mole" or "completely" is likely to lose credit.

**2.** (a) 2C(s) + 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → CH₃COOH(l): correct species, balanced, one mole of product [1] and correct state symbols [1] **[2]**
(b) Solid iodine is itself an element in its standard state, so nothing has to change to make it [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* For formation equations, check that you have one mole of product and elements as reactants. Fractions on the left are allowed in a formation equation.

**3.** q = 60.0 × 4.18 × 14.0 [1] = 3511 J = 3.51 kJ [1]
moles of Ag⁺ = 0.800 × 0.0600 = 0.0480 mol [1]
ΔH = −3.511 ÷ 0.0480 = **−73.2 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The sign is a separate decision from the number. State that the temperature rose and so the reaction is exothermic.

**4.** (a) moles = 0.74 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0100 mol [1]
q = 250 × 4.18 × 20.0 = 20 900 J [1] = 20.9 kJ [1]
ΔcH = −20.9 ÷ 0.0100 = **−2090 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
(b) Any two from: heat lost to the surroundings; incomplete combustion; heat absorbed by the can; evaporation of fuel. [2] **[2]**
(c) Add a draught shield or lid (or lag the can) [1] so that less heat is lost to the surroundings [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Human error" is vague. Naming a specific process, such as heat loss or incomplete combustion, is how you earn the reason marks.

**5.** moles = 1.20 × 0.0500 = 0.0600 mol [1]
q = 57.0 × 1000 × 0.0600 = 3420 J [1]
ΔT = 3420 ÷ (100 × 4.18) = 8.18 K [1]
final temperature = 19.6 + 8.18 = **27.8 °C** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* This reverses the usual calculation, so write the formula as q = mcΔT first and then rearrange it, rather than working from memory.

**6.** Use a polystyrene cup with a lid, in a beaker for stability, and measure a known volume of water [1]. Record the water temperature at fixed time intervals for a few minutes before adding the solid [1]. Add a weighed mass of solid, stir, and keep recording at the same intervals [1]. Plot temperature against time and extrapolate the line back to the time of addition to find ΔT, which allows for heat exchange with the surroundings [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both what you measure (mass, volume, temperature) and what you do with the readings. Your own school's method may differ in detail.

**7.** Cycle: 3C(s) + 4H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₃H₇OH(l), with all species burned to CO₂ and H₂O.
ΔfH = 3ΔcH(C) + 4ΔcH(H₂) − ΔcH(C₃H₇OH) [1]
= 3(−394) + 4(−286) − (−2006) [1]
= −1182 − 1144 + 2006 = **−320 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplying numbers (3 and 4) explicitly. A cycle drawn first makes the sign choices easy to check.

**8.** (a) ΔH = [ΔfH(Na₂CO₃) + ΔfH(H₂O) + ΔfH(CO₂)] − [2 × ΔfH(NaHCO₃)] [1]
= (−1131 − 286 − 394) − (2 × −948) = −1811 + 1896 [1]
= **+85 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
(b) −114 = [2x] − [2(+90)] [1]
−114 = 2x − 180, so 2x = 66 [1]
x = **+33 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* When the unknown is inside the formula, write the equation with x in it before rearranging, so the method is visible even if the algebra slips.

**9.** ΔH = ΔcH(C₆H₁₂) − [ΔcH(C₆H₆) + 3ΔcH(H₂)] [1]
= −3920 − [−3267 + 3(−286)] [1]
= −3920 − (−4125) = **+205 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
Endothermic, because ΔH is positive [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Combustion data reverse the order of subtraction compared with formation data. Writing the formula down first helps you notice this.

**10.** (a) The enthalpy change when one mole of a given type of bond is broken in the gaseous phase [1], averaged over a range of different molecules containing that bond [1]. **[2]**
(b) broken: 8 × 413 + 2 × 347 + 5 × 498 = 6488 kJ [1]
formed: 6 × 805 + 8 × 464 = 8542 kJ [1]
ΔH = 6488 − 8542 = **−2054 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
(c) ΔH = [3(−394) + 4(−242)] − [−105] [1] = −2150 + 105 = **−2045 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
(d) Mean bond enthalpies are averages over many molecules, not values for these specific molecules [1], whereas formation values are measured for these actual compounds [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (d) asks for the cause. A statement such as "bond enthalpies are less accurate" repeats the question without explaining why.

**11.** (a) 436 + 193 − 2x = −103 [1]
629 + 103 = 2x [1]
x = **366 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
(b) Ethanol and the water formed are liquids, and bond enthalpies are for gaseous species [1]. The energy needed to change between liquid and gas states is not included in the bond enthalpy calculation [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the final answer is a bond enthalpy, so it is positive. Write the equation with the unknown before rearranging.

**12.** (a) q = 100 × 4.18 × 22.3 = 9321 J [1]; moles Mg = 0.486 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0200 mol [1]
ΔH = −9.321 ÷ 0.0200 = **−466 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
(b) q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.9 = 2884 J, moles MgO = 0.806 ÷ 40.3 = 0.0200 mol [1]
ΔH = −2.884 ÷ 0.0200 = **−144 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
(c) Equations: A is Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂; B is MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O. The target is Mg + ½O₂ → MgO. Route: A, then reverse B, then form H₂O from H₂ and ½O₂ [1]
ΔfH(MgO) = ΔH(A) − ΔH(B) + ΔfH(H₂O) = −466 − (−144) + (−286) [1]
= **−608 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Reversing B changes its sign. State clearly which equation you are reversing, so the sign change is easy to follow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting "one mole" or "standard conditions" from a definition.
- Using the mass of the solid or fuel in q = mcΔT instead of the mass of the liquid that heated up.
- Dividing joules by moles without first converting to kilojoules.
- Forgetting the negative sign on an exothermic molar enthalpy change.
- Writing wrong state symbols in formation equations.
- Not multiplying each enthalpy by the number of moles in the balanced equation.
- Subtracting in the wrong order in Hess's law, especially when switching between combustion and formation data.
- In a bond enthalpy calculation, missing a bond because the structure was not drawn out in full.
- Giving "experimental error" as the reason for a difference rather than naming the heat loss or incomplete combustion.
