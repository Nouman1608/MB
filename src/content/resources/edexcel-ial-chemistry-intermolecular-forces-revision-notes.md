## The three forces at a glance (7.1)

| Force | Found between | Origin | Gets stronger with |
|---|---|---|---|
| London forces | All molecules | Instantaneous dipole in one molecule induces a dipole in a neighbour | More electrons; larger contact area |
| Permanent dipole-permanent dipole | Polar molecules | δ+ end of one molecule attracts δ− end of another | Greater overall molecular dipole |
| Hydrogen bond | H on N, O or F, with a lone pair on N, O or F nearby | δ+ H attracted to a lone pair on a very electronegative atom | Number of hydrogen bonds each molecule can form |

Strength order for a single interaction: London < permanent dipole < hydrogen bond, all much weaker than a
covalent bond. In larger molecules the **total** London forces can outweigh permanent dipole effects.

Key definitions to write word for word:

- **London force**: an attraction between an instantaneous dipole in one molecule and the dipole it induces in a
  neighbouring molecule.
- **Hydrogen bond**: an attraction between a δ+ hydrogen atom bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on an N, O or F
  atom of another molecule. The X-H···Y arrangement is close to linear (about 180°).

## Hydrogen bonding in H₂O, NH₃ and HF (7.2 and 7.4)

| Molecule | H on N/O/F | Lone pairs | What limits hydrogen bonding |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 2 | 2 | Nothing; balanced, so four per molecule in ice |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 | Only one lone pair to accept |
| HF | 1 | 3 | Only one H to donate; molecules form zig-zag chains |

Boiling order: **H₂O > HF > NH₃**. Water wins on the number of hydrogen bonds, not on the strength of each one.

### Method: does it hydrogen bond with itself?

1. Draw or picture the displayed formula.
2. Find any H bonded **directly** to N, O or F. If none, the answer is no.
3. Check for a lone pair on N, O or F. If present, the answer is yes.
4. For "with water" questions, a lone pair on O or N is enough, because water supplies the H.

Quick sort: yes for alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines with N-H, NH₃, HF, H₂O₂. No for ethers, aldehydes, ketones,
halogenoalkanes, CH₃F and tertiary amines (all their H atoms are on carbon).

## Water's anomalous properties (7.3)

- **High boiling and melting temperatures.** Group 16 hydrides H₂S to H₂Te follow a London-force trend (more
  electrons, higher boiling temperature). Water has the fewest electrons yet boils highest, because extra energy
  is needed to break its hydrogen bonds.
- **Ice is less dense than water.** Each molecule in ice is hydrogen bonded to four others in a tetrahedral
  arrangement. The near-linear, relatively long hydrogen bonds hold molecules apart in an open structure with
  empty space. On melting, some hydrogen bonds break, the structure partly collapses and the molecules move closer.
- Ice at 0 °C: 0.917 g cm⁻³. Liquid at 0 °C: 1.00 g cm⁻³. Volume rises by about 9% on freezing.

## Boiling temperature trends (7.5)

| Comparison | Example data | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Longer alkane chain | Propane 231 K, pentane 309 K | More electrons and more contact: stronger London forces |
| Branched isomer | Pentane 309 K, 2,2-dimethylpropane 283 K | Same electrons; compact shape, less contact, weaker London forces |
| Alcohol vs alkane with equal electrons | Ethanol 352 K, propane 231 K (26 electrons each) | Similar London forces; alcohol also has hydrogen bonds |
| HCl to HI | HCl 188 K, HBr 206 K, HI 238 K | More electrons, stronger London forces outweigh falling dipole |
| HF | 293 K | Hydrogen bonding |

### Method: explaining any boiling temperature difference

1. Name the forces present in **each** substance.
2. Compare electron numbers (for London forces) and say whether they are similar or different.
3. Name the extra or stronger force in the higher-boiling substance.
4. Finish with energy: "more energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular forces".

## Solvents (7.6)

| Solute | Good solvent | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Many ionic solids (e.g. KCl) | Water | **Hydration**: δ− O attracted to cations, δ+ H attracted to anions; energy released compensates for breaking the lattice |
| Short-chain alcohols | Water | Hydrogen bonds form between the O-H group and water |
| Halogenoalkanes | Non-aqueous (e.g. ethanol, other halogenoalkanes) | Polar, but cannot hydrogen bond with water; breaking water's hydrogen bonds is not repaid |
| Non-polar molecules (I₂, alkanes, fats) | Hexane, cyclohexane | Similar London forces in solute and solvent |

Rule of thumb: a substance dissolves well in a solvent whose intermolecular forces are similar to its own.

## Suggested practicals (reminders)

- **Enthalpy change of vaporisation of water**: ΔvapH = energy supplied (power × time) ÷ moles evaporated
  (mass lost ÷ 18.0). Because some heat escapes rather than evaporating water, expect an answer above the true value.
- **Temperature change on dissolving**: q = m × c × ΔT using the mass of water; ΔH = q ÷ moles of solute, with
  sign from the direction of temperature change.
- **Solubility of simple molecules in different solvents**: match the forces.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Intramolecular vs intermolecular**: boiling a simple molecular substance overcomes forces **between**
  molecules only.
- **Polar molecule vs hydrogen bonding**: CH₃Cl is polar but cannot hydrogen bond.
- **Strength of one hydrogen bond vs number of hydrogen bonds**: explains H₂O > HF.
- **Electron number vs contact area**: chain length changes both; branching changes only contact area.
- **Accepting vs donating**: methoxymethane cannot hydrogen bond with itself, but its oxygen lone pairs can accept
  hydrogen bonds from water, which helps it dissolve.

## Quick self-test

1. Which type of intermolecular force acts between all molecules?
2. Which intermolecular force acts in liquid ICl but not in liquid Br₂?
3. State the two conditions needed for hydrogen bonding between molecules of one substance.
4. Can propanone, CH₃COCH₃, form hydrogen bonds between its own molecules?
5. Explain why liquid NH₃ forms fewer hydrogen bonds per molecule than water.
6. Hexane boils at 342 K and 2-methylpentane at 333 K. Give the reason in one sentence.
7. How many electrons does a molecule of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, have?
8. Why does HI boil higher than HCl even though HCl is more polar?
9. Using densities 0.917 g cm⁻³ (ice) and 1.00 g cm⁻³ (water), what fraction of a floating block of ice is
   below the surface of fresh water?
10. Why does 1-bromobutane not dissolve in water?
11. What attractions form when potassium chloride dissolves in water?
12. Over 300 s, a 400 W heater evaporates 52.0 g of boiling water. Calculate ΔvapH in kJ mol⁻¹.

### Answers

1. London forces.
2. Permanent dipole-permanent dipole interactions (London forces act in both).
3. An H atom bonded directly to N, O or F, and a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule.
4. No. Its O has lone pairs, but every H is bonded to carbon.
5. NH₃ has only one lone pair, so it can accept only one hydrogen bond; water has two H atoms and two lone pairs.
6. Branching reduces surface contact between molecules, so London forces are weaker.
7. 26 (2 × 6 + 6 × 1 + 8).
8. HI has more electrons (54 vs 18), so its London forces are stronger, and this outweighs HCl's larger dipole.
9. 0.917 / 1.00 = **0.917**, so about 92% is below the surface.
10. It cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so the energy needed to break water's hydrogen bonds is not
    released again by new attractions.
11. Ion-dipole attractions (hydration): δ− O of water around K⁺, δ+ H of water around Cl⁻.
12. Energy = 400 × 300 = 120 000 J = 120 kJ; moles = 52.0 / 18.0 = 2.89 mol; ΔvapH = 120 / 2.89 =
    **+41.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "van der Waals forces" when the question asks you to name London forces precisely.
- Describing a London force without the word "induced" or without saying the dipole is instantaneous.
- Saying hydrogen bonding happens "because there is hydrogen in the molecule".
- Drawing a hydrogen bond to an H atom rather than to a lone pair, or with a bent X-H···Y angle.
- Forgetting to compare electron numbers before arguing about hydrogen bonds in alcohol vs alkane questions.
- Writing that ice has "more hydrogen bonds so it is heavier" instead of describing the open structure.
- Explaining the HCl to HI trend using dipoles alone.
- Saying a halogenoalkane is non-polar to explain its insolubility in water.
- Missing the positive sign on an enthalpy change of vaporisation.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three intermolecular forces in Edexcel International A Level Chemistry?**
Topic 7 of the Edexcel IAL Chemistry specification (YCH11) covers London forces, permanent dipole-permanent dipole interactions and hydrogen bonds. For a single interaction the order of strength is London < permanent dipole < hydrogen bond, all much weaker than a covalent bond.

**Why does water have a higher boiling temperature than hydrogen fluoride?**
Each water molecule has two δ+ hydrogen atoms and two lone pairs, so it forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule than HF, which has only one hydrogen atom. The number of hydrogen bonds, not the strength of each one, explains the order.

**Why is ice less dense than liquid water?**
In ice each water molecule is hydrogen bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement, holding the molecules apart in an open structure. On melting, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules move closer together.

**Why does branching lower the boiling temperature of an alkane?**
Branched isomers have the same number of electrons, but their compact shape reduces the surface contact between molecules. The London forces are weaker, so less energy is needed to separate the molecules.

**Can propanone form hydrogen bonds?**
Propanone cannot hydrogen bond with itself, because all of its hydrogen atoms are bonded to carbon. Its oxygen lone pairs can still accept hydrogen bonds from water molecules.
