## Section A

**1.** Which case established that an innocent party may sue immediately for an anticipatory breach? A: Hochster v De la Tour. B: Taylor v Caldwell. C: Cutter v Powell. D: Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC. **[1]**

**2.** In which case was a contract not frustrated because the fleet could still be viewed? A: Krell v Henry. B: Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton. C: Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl. D: Avery v Bowden. **[1]**

**3.** State the general rule on discharge by performance and name one case that illustrates it. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the two options available to the innocent party after a repudiatory breach. **[2]**

**5.** State two situations in which a contract will not be frustrated. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the doctrine of substantial performance, referring to two cases. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between actual breach and anticipatory breach. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the effect of frustration on money paid and payable under the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943. **[4]**

**9.** *Kemi agrees to tile Bruno's kitchen for £3,000, payable on completion. She finishes the job, but six tiles are cracked and will cost £150 to replace. Bruno refuses to pay anything.* Suggest whether Kemi is entitled to payment. **[4]**

**10.** *Harbour Events Ltd hires the Lido Hall for a three-day jazz festival in August, paying a deposit of £5,000 with £15,000 due on the first day. In July the council closes the hall after finding dangerous structural faults that neither party knew of. Harbour Events has spent £2,000 printing tickets, and the hall's owner has spent £1,500 preparing the stage.* Advise Harbour Events. **[8]**

**11.** *Ivo agrees to deliver 500 crates of apples to Fresco Ltd on 1 October. In September he tells Fresco he has found a better buyer and will not deliver. Fresco tells him it will wait and still expects delivery. On 25 September the government bans all apple exports from the region to Fresco's area because of a plant disease.* Consider Fresco's position. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss whether the doctrine of frustration is too narrow. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A: Hochster v De la Tour. **[1]**

**2.** B: Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton. **[1]**

**3.** Performance must be **complete and exact** before the contract is discharged and payment is due [1]; for example **Cutter v Powell (1795)** or **Re Moore and Landauer (1921)** [1].

**4.** **Terminate** the contract and claim damages [1]; or **affirm** the contract and continue to perform [1].

**5.** Any two of: performance has become only **more expensive or difficult** (Davis Contractors) [1]; the purpose is **not wholly defeated** (Herne Bay); the frustration is **self-induced** (Maritime National Fish); the risk was **foreseen or provided for** by a term [1].

**6.** Where a party has performed **substantially**, with only minor defects, they can claim the price **less the cost of remedying the defects** [1]. In **Hoenig v Isaacs (1952)** decorating and furnishing a flat with defects costing about £56 to fix, on a price of £750, was substantial performance [1]. Where the defects are **serious**, there is no substantial performance [1], as in **Bolton v Mahadeva (1972)**, where a heating system that gave out fumes and heated poorly entitled the contractor to nothing [1].

**7.** Actual breach occurs **when performance is due** [1] and a party fails to perform, performs defectively, or makes performance impossible [1]. Anticipatory breach occurs **before performance is due** [1], when a party indicates expressly or by conduct that it will not perform, allowing the innocent party to sue at once, as in **Hochster v De la Tour (1853)** [1].

**8.** Under **s1(2)**, money **paid** before frustration is **recoverable** [1] and money **payable** before frustration **ceases to be payable** [1]. The court may allow the payee to keep or recover a sum for **expenses incurred** in performance [1], but only **up to the amount paid or payable** before frustration [1].

**9.** The general rule requires **complete and exact performance**, which suggests Bruno need not pay (Cutter v Powell) [1]. However, the job is finished and the defect costs only £150 on a £3,000 contract, so there is likely **substantial performance** [1], as in **Hoenig v Isaacs** rather than Bolton v Mahadeva [1]. Kemi is entitled to **£3,000 less £150, which is £2,850** [1].

**10.** The closure is an event **after formation**, **without the fault** of either party [1], which makes performance **impossible** or illegal, as in **Taylor v Caldwell (1863)** [1]. The contract is **frustrated** and ends automatically [1]. Under **s1(2) of the 1943 Act**, Harbour Events can recover the **£5,000 deposit** [1], and the **£15,000 ceases to be payable** [1]. The court may allow the owner to keep up to **£1,500 for expenses**, since this is less than the sum paid [1]. Harbour Events' own **£2,000** ticket costs are not recoverable from the owner under s1(2) [1]. Conclusion: Harbour Events is likely to recover between £3,500 and £5,000, depending on the court's view of the owner's expenses [1].

**11.** Ivo's statement before 1 October is an **anticipatory breach** [1]. Fresco could have accepted it and sued at once (**Hochster v De la Tour**) [1]. Instead it **affirmed**, keeping the contract alive for both parties (White and Carter v McGregor) [1]. The government ban is a **supervening illegality** after formation and without fault, likely to **frustrate** the contract (Dick Kerr) [1]. As in **Avery v Bowden (1855)**, Fresco's affirmation means it **loses its claim** for the earlier breach [1]. Conclusion: Fresco has no claim in damages, though any money paid could be recovered under the 1943 Act [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The doctrine reflects **sanctity of contract**: parties should bear the risks they agreed to [1].
- It is narrow because parties can **allocate risk themselves** through force majeure clauses, so courts should not rewrite bargains [1].
- **Davis Contractors** and **Tsakiroglou** show that hardship or cost is not enough, which gives **certainty** [1].
- But narrowness can be **harsh** where a radical change in costs was not foreseeable and no clause was drafted [1].
- **Self-induced** frustration (The Super Servant Two) can seem unfair where a party had to choose between contracts [1].
- The **Krell v Henry** and **Herne Bay** contrast shows the "radically different" test can be **hard to apply** consistently [1].
- The **1943 Act** softens the effect of frustration by adjusting money and benefits, so the narrowness matters less in practice [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that the doctrine is appropriately narrow for commercial parties, though less suited to consumers who rarely negotiate risk clauses [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating the strict performance rule without considering substantial performance.
- Assuming the innocent party must accept an anticipatory breach.
- Treating increased cost as frustration.
- Forgetting the 1943 Act when deposits or expenses are mentioned.
- Not calculating the sum due where the facts give figures.
