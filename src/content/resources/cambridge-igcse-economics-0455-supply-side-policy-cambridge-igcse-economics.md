The section has three parts: 4.5.1 the "definition of supply-side policy"; 4.5.2 supply-side policy measures, which "include education and training, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, improving incentives to work and invest, and privatisation"; and 4.5.3 "how supply-side policy measures may enable the government to achieve its macroeconomic aims". ## 4.5.1 Definition

**Supply-side policy** consists of government measures that aim to increase the productive capacity of the economy by improving the quantity, quality and efficiency of the factors of production and the way markets work. On a production possibility curve diagram, successful supply-side policy shifts the curve outwards.

### Supply-side or demand-side?

Fiscal and monetary policy mainly change **spending** in the economy. Supply-side policy changes **what the economy can produce**. Some measures do both, so look at the purpose and the route.

| Policy (invented) | Main route | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Cut in interest rates to encourage borrowing | Raises spending | Monetary (demand-side) |
| Extra government spending on unemployment benefits during a recession | Raises spending | Fiscal (demand-side) |
| Government-funded apprenticeships in engineering | Raises skills and productivity | Supply-side (education and training) |
| Cut in income tax to make work more rewarding | Raises spending *and* incentives to work | Fiscal measure with a supply-side aim |
| Selling a state-owned airline | Changes ownership and incentives for efficiency | Supply-side (privatisation) |
| Removing licences that limit the number of taxis | Increases competition | Supply-side (deregulation) |

A good answer says which route the question is about. A tax cut explained only as "people spend more" is a demand-side explanation; explained as "people work more hours and firms invest more" it is supply-side.

## 4.5.2 The six measures in depth

### 1. Education and training

Raises the quality of labour. Better-skilled workers produce more per hour and can move into new jobs as industries change, reducing unemployment caused by a mismatch of skills.

**Worked example 1 (invented).** A government course costs 3,000 per trainee. Each trainee's output rises by a value of 1,500 a year from the second year onwards.

```
Value gained by end of year 2 = 1,500
Value gained by end of year 3 = 3,000, equal to the cost
Value gained by end of year 6 = 5 x 1,500 = 7,500, two and a half times the cost
```

The course pays back only after three years. Education and training work, but slowly; a government facing an election or a crisis may prefer faster policies.

### 2. Labour market reforms

Aim to make the labour market more flexible: making it easier for firms to hire, helping workers move between regions and jobs (job centres, information, help with moving costs), and reducing restrictive practices. Workers are matched to vacancies more quickly, so unemployment falls and output rises.

### 3. Lower direct taxes

Direct taxes are taxes on income and profit. Lower income tax increases the reward for working; lower corporation tax increases the profit firms keep for investment.

### 4. Deregulation

Removes rules that restrict entry or competition. **Worked example 2 (invented).** A city limits taxi licences to 400. Each taxi completes 20 trips a day at an average fare of 15. After deregulation, 600 taxis operate and the average fare falls to 12.

```
Taxis: up 50%; fare: down 20%
Trips if each taxi still does 20 a day = 600 x 20 = 12,000 (up from 8,000)
```

More competition lowers fares and raises output, though the city must still enforce safety rules.

### 5. Improving incentives to work and invest

**Incentive to work.** **Worked example 3 (invented).** Sami receives benefits of 180 a week. A 35-hour job pays 250 a week before income tax of 10%. If he takes the job, his benefit stops.

```
Take-home pay = 250 x 0.90 = 225
Gain from working = 225 - 180 = 45 a week
Gain per hour = 45 / 35 = 1.29
```

A gain of 1.29 an hour gives little reason to work. If the government lets him keep 60 a week of benefit for his first year in work, the gain becomes 105 a week (3.00 an hour), a stronger incentive.

**Incentive to invest.** **Worked example 4 (invented).** A firm earns profit of 400,000 and pays corporation tax at 25%. It buys a machine for 80,000. Under a new investment allowance, the cost of the machine is deducted from taxable profit.

```
Tax without allowance = 400,000 x 0.25 = 100,000
Tax with allowance    = (400,000 - 80,000) x 0.25 = 80,000
Tax saved = 20,000, so the machine effectively costs 60,000
```

Cheaper investment encourages firms to buy more capital, raising productivity.

### 6. Privatisation

Transfers state-owned firms to private owners. The profit motive gives owners a reason to cut waste and improve service, and the government receives sale revenue. The benefit is greatest when the privatised firm faces competition; a private monopoly may simply raise prices.

## 4.5.3 Effects on the macroeconomic aims

| Aim | How supply-side policy helps | Speed | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | More and better resources shift the PPC outwards | Slow (training, investment) to medium (deregulation) | Extra capacity raises output only if there is demand for it |
| Low unemployment | Skills and flexible markets match workers to jobs | Slow to medium | Little help when unemployment comes from weak demand |
| Stable prices | Lower costs per unit and more output ease pressure on prices | Slow | Small effect on inflation caused by excessive spending |
| Balance of payments | Lower costs and better quality make exports more competitive | Slow | Depends on demand abroad and exchange rates |
| Redistribution | Education and training can raise low earners' wages | Slow | Lower taxes and benefit cuts may widen inequality |

### Why supply-side policy can ease conflicts

Section 4.2.2 shows that faster growth or lower unemployment can raise inflation or worsen the balance of payments when spending outruns capacity. Supply-side policy raises capacity itself, so the economy can grow with less pressure on prices and with more competitive exports. This is why many governments combine it with demand-side policies.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Discuss whether lower direct taxes will increase economic growth. [6]

> Lower income tax raises take-home pay for each hour worked, so some people choose to work more hours or join the labour force [incentive to work]. Lower corporation tax leaves firms with more profit to invest in machinery, raising capacity [incentive to invest]. More labour and capital shift the PPC outwards, which is economic growth [link to the aim].
> However, some workers may keep the same income by working fewer hours [counter-argument]. Lower tax revenue may force cuts in spending on education or roads, which also support growth [opportunity cost].
> Lower direct taxes are most likely to raise growth when firms have profitable investment opportunities and many people are deterred from working by high tax, but the effect will be small if the lost revenue is taken from productive public spending [judgement with condition].

## Common mistakes

- Explaining a supply-side measure through extra spending only, which is a demand-side route.
- Forgetting time lags: most measures take years to work.
- Claiming privatisation always improves efficiency, without asking whether the firm faces competition.
- Ignoring the effect on income distribution when discussing tax and benefit changes.
- Listing measures without the chain from measure to productive capacity to the aim.
