## Section A - multiple choice

**1.** Which case established that a learner driver is judged by the standard of a reasonably competent
driver? **[1]**
A Bolton v Stone   B Nettleship v Weston   C Mullin v Richards   D Latimer v AEC

**2.** Under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984, which type of loss can a trespasser claim for? **[1]**
A Damage to clothing   B Personal injury   C Loss of earnings from a damaged van   D All of these

**3.** Which statement about secondary victims of psychiatric injury is correct? **[1]**
A Watching a live television broadcast satisfies the own-senses requirement
B A close tie of love and affection is presumed between parent and child
C Rescuers are always primary victims
D Foreseeability of physical injury is sufficient

## Section B - short answers

**4.** Explain the rule on liability for pure economic loss caused by negligent acts. **[5]**

**5.** Explain the requirements for liability for a negligent misstatement. **[5]**

**6.** Explain how the courts decide whether a person is an employee for the purposes of vicarious
liability. **[5]**

**7.** Explain the factors a court considers when deciding whether to grant an injunction in private

## Section C - applied questions

**8.** *Priya runs a garden centre. She stores large quantities of fertiliser chemicals in a shed. During
a storm the shed roof leaks and the chemicals wash into next-door farmland owned by Callum, killing his
crops.* Advise Callum whether he could claim under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher. **[10]**

**9.** *Noor, a delivery driver for Fastline Ltd, is told never to give lifts. She gives a lift to a
hitch-hiker, Ben, and crashes through careless driving. Ben is injured.* Advise Ben whether Fastline is
vicariously liable. **[10]**

## Section D - extended problem and evaluation

**10.** *Harbourside Leisure runs a swimming complex. A cleaner leaves a wet floor without a warning sign
and Jodie, a customer, slips and breaks her wrist; she was running at the time. Jodie's husband Leo, at
home, hears about the accident on the radio and arrives two hours later to see her in pain in the
first-aid room; he develops anxiety disorder. That night, Tariq climbs the locked perimeter fence to use
the diving pool, which has been drained for repairs behind a "Danger - pool empty" sign, and is injured
diving in.*

Advise Harbourside of its liability in tort to Jodie, Leo and Tariq, and evaluate whether the law in this
area strikes a fair balance. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** B. **[1]**

**2.** B - s1(8) excludes property damage. **[1]**

**3.** B. **[1]**

**4.** Pure economic loss is financial loss not caused by physical damage to the claimant or their
property **[1]**. It is generally not recoverable in negligence **[1]**. Economic loss consequential on
physical damage is recoverable **[1]**. In Spartan Steel v Martin (1973) the claimant recovered for the
damaged melt and the profit on it, but not for profit lost while the power was off **[1]**. Policy:
avoiding indeterminate liability (floodgates) and leaving such loss to contract and insurance **[1]**.

**5.** Liability comes from Hedley Byrne v Heller (1964) **[1]**: a special relationship in which the
defendant has special skill or knowledge **[1]**; the defendant knew or ought to have known the claimant
would rely on the statement **[1]**; reliance was reasonable **[1]**; and the defendant voluntarily
assumed responsibility (not effectively disclaimed). Caparo (1990): auditors owe no duty to members of the
public who buy shares, because the statement was not made for that purpose **[1]**.

**6.** No single test **[1]**. Control test - whether the employer controls how the work is done (Yewens v
Noakes, 1880) **[1]**. Integration test - whether the worker is part of the organisation (Stevenson,
Jordan and Harrison, 1952) **[1]**. Economic reality/multiple test - wages, control, and whether other
terms are consistent with employment, such as who provides equipment and bears the financial risk (Ready
Mixed Concrete, 1968) **[1]**. Relationships "akin to employment" can also qualify (Various Claimants v
Catholic Child Welfare Society, 2012), but not genuine independent contractors (Barclays, 2020) **[1]**.

**7.** An injunction is discretionary **[1]**. The court balances the claimant's right to enjoy land against
the defendant's activity and the public interest **[1]** - in Miller v Jackson (1977) damages were given
instead because of the cricket club's value to the community **[1]**; in Kennaway v Thompson (1981) the
injunction limited the times of powerboat racing **[1]**. Damages in lieu are available where Shelfer
(1895) criteria apply, though Coventry v Lawrence (2014) favours a more flexible approach **[1]**.

**8. [10]** Apply each element, with authority:
- Bringing on to land and accumulating: the chemicals were stored there deliberately.
- Likely to do mischief if it escapes: large quantities of chemicals are dangerous.
- Non-natural use: storage in bulk is arguably "extraordinary and unusual" (Transco, 2003), compared with
  ordinary domestic use (Rickards v Lothian, 1913). A garden centre storing some fertiliser may be an
  ordinary use - a strong answer weighs both.
- Escape: the chemicals passed from Priya's land to Callum's (Read v J Lyons, 1947).
- Foreseeable type of damage: crop damage is foreseeable (Cambridge Water, 1994).
- Claimant's standing: Callum owns the land (Transco).
- Defence: Act of God requires a natural event no human foresight could guard against (Nichols v
  Marsland, 1876); an ordinary storm is unlikely to qualify.
- Conclusion: Callum is likely to succeed if the use is non-natural; otherwise he may claim in negligence
  or nuisance.

**9. [10]**
- Stage 1: Noor is an employee on the multiple test (wages, control, Fastline's van).
- Stage 2: driving is the job she is employed to do; giving a lift is a prohibited act.
- Limpus v London General Omnibus (1862): a prohibited way of doing an authorised act is within the course
  of employment.
- Rose v Plenty (1976): employer liable where the passenger was helping the employer's business, contrast
  Twine v Bean's Express (1946), where a hitch-hiker given a prohibited lift could not claim.
- Ben confers no benefit on Fastline, so Twine is the closer analogy; Fastline is probably not
  vicariously liable, though Noor is personally liable in negligence.
- Credit discussion of the close-connection test (Lister, 2001; Mohamud, 2016).

**10. [30]** Indicative content:

*Jodie (OLA 1957).* Harbourside is the occupier; Jodie is a visitor owed the common duty of care (s2(2)).
A wet floor without a sign falls below the standard of reasonable care. Factual causation: but for the
missing sign she would not have slipped. Defence: contributory negligence for running (Law Reform
(Contributory Negligence) Act 1945), with damages reduced by a just and equitable proportion. Remedy:
special damages (lost earnings, treatment) and general damages (pain, loss of amenity); she must mitigate.
Vicarious liability for the cleaner is an alternative route.

*Leo (psychiatric injury).* Anxiety disorder is a recognised illness. He is a secondary victim, so Alcock
(1992) applies. Close tie: husband, presumed. Proximity: arriving two hours later, seeing her in the
first-aid room, is like McLoughlin v O'Brian (1983); hearing on the radio alone is not enough.
Evaluate: a broken wrist may not be a sufficiently horrifying event, and the outcome is uncertain.

*Tariq (OLA 1984).* A trespasser. s1(3): Harbourside knew of the danger and fenced it; it may not have had
reasonable grounds to believe someone would climb in at night (Ratcliff v McConnell, 1999). A warning
sign (s1(5)) and the obviousness of the risk (Tomlinson v Congleton BC, 2003) make liability unlikely;
volenti (s1(6)) may also apply.

*Evaluation.* The 1957 and 1984 Acts balance protection of visitors against the freedom of occupiers, with
less protection for trespassers who chose to enter; Tomlinson emphasises personal responsibility. The
Alcock mechanisms are criticised as arbitrary, but justified by floodgates and fraud concerns. A reasoned
conclusion is needed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming Alcock without applying each control mechanism to the facts.
- Treating an employee's prohibited act as automatically outside the course of employment.
- Ignoring the claimant's own fault when calculating outcome.
- Evaluation tacked on in one sentence at the end instead of running through the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a 30-mark answer take?** On a 2-hour, 100-mark paper you have a little over a minute per
mark, so roughly 35-40 minutes including planning.

**Do I need to quote section numbers?** Accurate section numbers, such as s2(2) OLA 1957 and s1(3) OLA
1984, show precise knowledge and are credited.

**Can a single scenario test several torts?** Yes. Question 10 combines occupiers' liability, psychiatric
injury and defences, which is typical of an extended AQA problem.
