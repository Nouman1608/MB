**Trade is Section 6 of Paper 2 in Cambridge O Level Pakistan Studies 2059.** It asks what Pakistan
sells to and buys from the world, how that mix has changed, what GNP and GDP mean, why the balance
of trade is usually in deficit, who Pakistan's main partners are, and what helps or holds back its
trade. Questions are often built on a bar chart or table of exports and imports, so data
interpretation is central.

## How is Trade examined in 2059 Paper 2?

You answer three 25-mark questions out of five in 1 hour 30 minutes. A Trade question usually
gives a graph of export values by product, a pie chart of partners or a table of exports and
imports over several years. Early parts ask you to read and calculate from it; later parts ask you
to explain trends; part (d) asks you to analyse or evaluate, for example whether Pakistan can
increase its share of world trade. Trade links to Section 4 (rice, cotton, fruit), Section 5
(textiles, sports goods, surgical instruments) and Section 3 (fuel imports).

## How has the mix changed?

- **From raw materials to processed goods.** In the 1950s Pakistan's exports were mainly raw cotton,
  and jute from East Pakistan. Today raw cotton exports are small; most cotton leaves as yarn, cloth
  and finished garments, which add value.
- **Rice has grown.** Basmati and non-basmati rice exports reached a record value in 2023–24 after
  India restricted some of its own rice exports, showing how a competitor's policy can change
  Pakistan's earnings.
- **Fuel dominates imports.** The import bill swings with world oil and gas prices. It jumped in
  2021–22 when world prices rose, then fell in 2022–23 when the government restricted imports to
  protect foreign-exchange reserves.
- **New growth areas:** information technology services (counted in services trade, roughly US$3
  billion), surgical instruments, pharmaceuticals and seafood.

## What are GDP and GNP, and how do they differ?

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the total value of all goods and services produced inside
Pakistan in a year, whoever owns the business. **Gross national product (GNP)** is the value
produced by Pakistan's residents and companies wherever they are: it equals GDP plus income earned
abroad by Pakistanis (such as profits and interest), minus income paid to foreign owners of
businesses in Pakistan (such as profits sent home by foreign firms). The difference is "net income
from abroad". Both are often divided by population to give a per-person figure for comparing
living standards.

## How do trade trends affect the balance of trade and the economy?

The **balance of trade** is the value of exports of goods minus the value of imports of goods.
When imports exceed exports there is a **deficit**, which Pakistan has run almost every year.

A widening deficit means:

1. More foreign currency is needed than exports earn, so reserves fall.
2. The gap is filled by remittances from overseas Pakistanis (about US$30 billion in 2023–24),
   loans, and sometimes help from the IMF or friendly countries.
3. Pressure on reserves weakens the rupee, which makes fuel and machinery imports dearer and raises
   inflation.
4. Repaying loans takes money away from schools, health and development projects.

Rising exports of higher value-added goods narrow the gap, create jobs and allow imports of the
machinery industry needs.

## What promotes and what hinders trade?

**Promoting factors**
- **Trading agreements and blocs:** the EU's GSP+ scheme (from 2014) lets most Pakistani exports
  enter the European Union tariff-free, conditional on human-rights and labour conventions; the
  China–Pakistan Free Trade Agreement (in force from 2007, extended in a second phase); the South
  Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA); the Economic Cooperation Organization (ECO) with Iran, Turkey and
  Central Asia.
- **Location:** Karachi, Port Qasim and Gwadar on the Arabian Sea, close to Gulf markets, and land
  routes to China and Afghanistan.
- **A weaker rupee** can make Pakistani goods cheaper abroad.

**Hindering factors**
- **Narrow export base:** heavy reliance on textiles and rice exposes earnings to falls in world
  prices, poor cotton harvests and floods.
- **Competition** from Bangladesh, Vietnam, India and China, which produce similar goods at scale.
- **Trade barriers** in richer markets: tariffs, quotas, and strict quality, safety and labelling
  standards that small firms struggle to meet.
- **High production costs:** expensive and unreliable power and gas, and slow port clearance.
- **Exchange-rate swings:** the rupee fell from roughly Rs 160 to the US dollar in mid-2021 to
  around Rs 280 by 2024, raising the cost of imported inputs that exporters themselves rely on.
- **Political tension with neighbours**, which limits trade with India in particular.

## Why is it hard for a developing country to raise its share of world trade?

Pakistan exports mostly low-to-medium value goods whose prices are set by world markets, while it
imports high-value machinery and fuel whose prices it cannot control. Its firms are often small,
short of capital and technology, and face standards and branding they cannot easily meet. Rich
countries' tariffs on processed goods can be higher than on raw materials, which discourages
adding value. Without a broader range of products, Pakistan's share of world exports has stayed
well under one per cent.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between the balance of trade and the balance of payments?**
The balance of trade covers only exports and imports of goods. The balance of payments is wider and
also includes services, remittances, investment and loans.

**Why do remittances matter for trade?**
They bring in foreign currency that helps pay for imports, so they reduce the pressure that a trade
deficit puts on reserves and the rupee.

**What is GSP+?**
A European Union scheme that removes tariffs on most exports from qualifying developing countries
that ratify and implement international conventions. Pakistan has used it since 2014.

**Does a weak rupee help or hurt Pakistan?**
Both. Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, but imported fuel, machinery and raw materials
become more expensive, which raises costs and inflation.
