## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain **one** reason why payback and net present value may rank two projects differently. **[3]**

**2.** A project costs £60,000 and is forecast to bring net cash inflows of £25,000, £25,000, £20,000 and £15,000 in years 1 to 4. Calculate the payback period. **[2]**

**3.** For the project in Question 2, calculate the average rate of return. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business might reject a project with a positive net present value. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between an internal and an external reason for the failure of a business. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Orrin Robotics can spend £150,000 on one of two projects.

Project P, a new robot for warehouses: a decision tree gives a 0.5 chance of total net inflows of £400,000 and a 0.5 chance of £100,000. The expected inflows by year are £20,000, £30,000, £50,000, £70,000 and £80,000 for years 1 to 5.

Project Q, an upgrade for existing customers: inflows are almost certain, at £80,000, £70,000, £40,000 and £20,000 for years 1 to 4.

Discount factors at 10% are 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683 and 0.621 for years 1 to 5.

**(a)** Calculate the expected net gain of Project P using the decision tree, and the total net gain of Project Q. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the payback period of each project. **[4]**

**(c)** Calculate the net present value of each project at 10%. **[4]**

**(d)** Evaluate which project Orrin Robotics should choose. **[9]**

**7.** Using Question 6, explain why the decision tree and payback period give different results for Project P. **[4]**

**8.** Linley Bookshops has five shops. It proposes opening a small café in each. Break-even analysis shows each café needs 1,100 customers a month; the forecast is 1,500. Ansoff's matrix classifies the plan as product development. A SWOT analysis lists loyal customers as a strength, no catering experience as a weakness and many cafés nearby as a threat. Evaluate the likely success of this proposal using the results of these decision making tools. **[9]**

**9.** Wrenfield Fashion, a chain of 14 clothing shops, closed this year. The evidence:

- Online clothing sales in the UK grew rapidly over the previous five years, and Wrenfield had no website until two years before it closed.
- It signed 15-year leases on all its shops, with rents that rose every five years.
- Its stock was chosen by the founder alone; unsold stock rose for three years in a row.
- A rise in energy prices increased its costs in its final year.

Evaluate the reasons for the failure of Wrenfield Fashion. **[12]**

**10.** Evaluate the usefulness of decision making tools to the owner of a small business. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Payback measures only how quickly the outlay is recovered and ignores cash after that point [1], whereas NPV counts all cash flows, discounted to today's value [1]. So a project with large inflows late in its life can have a slow payback but a higher NPV than a project that recovers its cost quickly and then earns little [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Cumulative 25,000, 50,000, 70,000; after 2 years £10,000 is still needed, and 10,000 ÷ 20,000 = 0.5 [1]; payback = **2.5 years** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Total profit = 85,000 − 60,000 = 25,000; average a year = 25,000 ÷ 4 = 6,250 [1]; ARR = 6,250 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **10.4%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It might conflict with the business's mission or CSR policy, for example using a supplier with poor working conditions [1], so the damage to reputation and sales could outweigh the financial gain shown by the NPV [1]. Also accept staff opposition, strategic fit or risk, with development. **[2]**

**5.** An internal reason is within the business's control, such as poor cash-flow management or weak forecasting [1]; an external reason arises outside it, such as a recession or a new competitor [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** P: EV = (0.5 × 400,000) + (0.5 × 100,000) = £250,000; net gain **£100,000** [1]. Q: total inflows 210,000 − 150,000 = **£60,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** P: cumulative 20,000, 50,000, 100,000, 170,000 [1]; after 3 years £50,000 is needed, 50,000 ÷ 70,000 = 0.71, so payback = **3.71 years** [1]. Q: cumulative 80,000, 150,000 [1], so payback = **2.0 years** [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** P: 18,180 + 24,780 + 37,550 + 47,810 + 49,680 = 178,000 [1]; NPV = **£28,000** [1]. Q: 72,720 + 57,820 + 30,040 + 13,660 = 174,240 [1]; NPV = **£24,240** [1]. **[4]**

**(d)** Results: the decision tree and NPV favour P; payback favours Q [1].
For P: its expected net gain is £40,000 higher than Q's [1], and its NPV is £3,760 higher [1].
For Q: it pays back in 2 years against 3.71 [1]; its inflows are almost certain, while P has a 50% chance of only £100,000 of inflows, which would mean a loss of £50,000 [1]. P's NPV advantage is small and depends on the expected inflows, which are averages of very different outcomes [1].
Qualitative: Q serves existing customers, so the market is known; P is a new robot for a new market, which is riskier in Ansoff terms [1].
Judgement: Q is the better choice for Orrin unless it can afford a 50% chance of losing £50,000 [1], because P's extra value in today's money is small compared with its much greater risk and slower payback; if Orrin has strong reserves and a growth objective, P becomes more attractive [1]. **[9]**

**7.** The decision tree adds up all inflows over five years without discounting [1], so P's large inflows in years 4 and 5 count fully, giving a high net gain [1]. Payback looks only at how quickly the £150,000 is recovered [1]; because P's cash arrives late, payback is slow, at 3.71 years, so it makes P look worse [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Break-even: the forecast of 1,500 customers is 400 above break-even, a margin of safety of about 27% [1], which suggests each café can cover its costs if the forecast is accurate [1].
Ansoff: product development uses Linley's existing customers [1], so the market risk is lower than for a new market, and loyal customers are likely to try the cafés [1].
SWOT: the lack of catering experience is a serious weakness, because food safety, staffing and waste control are new skills [1]; many nearby cafés threaten the forecast, since customers have alternatives [1].
Evaluation: the tools broadly agree that the plan can work, but the break-even result depends on a forecast that the SWOT threat makes uncertain [1]. The weakness in catering could be reduced by partnering with an established café operator [1]. Judgement: the proposal is likely to succeed if Linley trials one café first to test the 1,500 forecast and to build catering skills before opening in all five shops [1]. **[9]**

**9.** External: the rapid growth of online clothing sales reduced footfall in high-street shops [1]; higher energy prices raised costs in the final year [1].
Internal: Wrenfield was slow to respond to online competition, having no website until two years before closure [1]; 15-year leases with rising rents fixed its costs even as sales fell, so it could not cut its shop numbers [1]; stock was chosen by one person, without data or challenge, so unsold stock grew for three years [1], tying up cash and forcing discounts [1].
Linking: the external shift to online buying exposed the internal weaknesses [1]: with inflexible leases and poor stock control, Wrenfield could neither cut costs nor match what customers wanted [1].
Evaluation: the energy price rise was a final pressure but was not the main cause, since it came only in the last year [1]. The most important reason was the failure to adapt to online shopping, because rivals faced the same market change and some survived [1]; the long leases made this failure much more costly by locking in high fixed costs [1]. Judgement: Wrenfield failed mainly through slow decision making in response to a change it could see coming, rather than through bad luck [1]. **[12]**

**10.** Usefulness: tools such as break-even and cash-flow forecasts help the owner see whether an idea covers its costs before money is committed [1]; payback is simple and focuses on cash, which matters most to a small business [1]; using more than one tool shows where the uncertainty lies [1].
Limitations: a small business owner may lack the time or skills to use tools such as NPV properly [1]; the forecasts that feed every tool may be little more than guesses for a new or small business [1]; tools ignore qualitative factors such as the owner's knowledge of local customers, which may be the most reliable information available [1].
Evaluation: simple tools are more useful to a small business than complex ones, because they need less data and their results are easier to act on [1]. Judgement: tools are useful as a check on the owner's judgement, especially for decisions that commit large sums or borrowing, but they cannot replace that judgement [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting tool results without explaining why they differ.
- Assuming NPV is always the deciding tool.
- Forgetting to consider the risk behind an expected value.
- Listing reasons for failure without linking or prioritising them.
- Ignoring the size and circumstances of the business when judging tools.
