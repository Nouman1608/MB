## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between capacity and duration as features of a memory store. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the type of long-term memory shown in each example.

> A: Tomás knows that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.
> B: Leila remembers the party for her tenth birthday.
> C: Kofi types quickly without looking at the keyboard.

**[3]**

**3.** Explain how chunking can increase the amount of information held in short-term memory. **[2]**

**4.** A fictional researcher repeated a trigram study in which participants counted backwards in threes before recall.

| Delay (s) | 3 | 9 | 15 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correct recall (%) | 88 | 46 | 18 | 9 |

**(a)** Calculate the fall in correct recall, in percentage points, between 3 s and 18 s. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what these results suggest about short-term memory. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why participants counted backwards in threes. **[2]**

**5.** In a fictional study, participants recalled a list of digits while doing a second task. Mean errors were: digits alone 2.9; digits while repeating "the, the, the" aloud 8.4; digits while tracking a moving dot on a screen 3.1.

**(a)** Use the working memory model to explain these results. **[3]**
**(b)** All participants did all three conditions. Explain why the researcher should counterbalance the order. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between response bias and substitution as explanations of the effect of leading questions. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Two passengers saw a bag snatched on a bus. While waiting for the police, one said, "He had a red jacket, didn't he?" In her statement, the other passenger described a red jacket, although the thief's jacket was brown. Explain this with reference to research into post-event discussion. **[4]**

**8.** Evaluate the working memory model. **[8]**

**9.** A police officer is about to interview a witness to a shop robbery. Explain how the officer could use **two** techniques of the cognitive interview, and why each should improve recall. **[4]**

**10.** In a fictional leading-question study, five participants who heard the verb "crashed" estimated speeds of 38, 44, 41, 36 and 46 mph. Five who heard "touched" estimated 30, 28, 35, 31 and 26 mph.

**(a)** Calculate the mean estimate for each group. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** limitation of using filmed crashes to study eyewitness testimony. **[2]**

**11.** Discuss research into the effects of leading questions and post-event discussion on the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss the use of case studies of people with brain damage as evidence for different types of long-term memory. **[8]**

## Answers

Questions 8, 11 and 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** Capacity is how much information a store can hold, such as about 7 plus or minus 2 items in STM [1]. Duration is how long information stays in a store, such as about 18 to 30 seconds in STM without rehearsal [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A: semantic [1]. B: episodic [1]. C: procedural [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Chunking groups separate items into larger meaningful units, such as reading 1, 9, 4, 5 as the year 1945 [1]. Because STM capacity is limited by the number of chunks rather than items, more total information can be held [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 88 - 9 = 79 percentage points [1].
**(b)** Information is lost rapidly from STM when rehearsal is prevented [1], so STM has a short duration of well under 30 seconds [1].
**(c)** Counting backwards occupies the articulatory process [1], so participants cannot rehearse the trigram and the true duration of STM is measured [1]. **[5]**

**5. (a)** Repeating "the" uses the phonological loop, the same component as rehearsing digits, so the tasks interfere and errors rise sharply to 8.4 [1]. Tracking a dot uses the visuo-spatial sketchpad, a separate component [1], so errors (3.1) barely differ from digits alone (2.9), supporting separate components with their own capacity [1].
**(b)** In a repeated measures design, performance may improve with practice or worsen with fatigue across conditions [1]. Counterbalancing (for example, varying the order between participants) spreads these order effects evenly across conditions [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Response bias means the wording of the question influences the answer given, but the original memory is unchanged [1]. Substitution means the misleading wording alters the stored memory itself, so the witness genuinely remembers wrongly [1]. **[2]**

**7.** This is post-event discussion: talking about the event with a co-witness before giving a statement [1]. The second passenger may have shown memory conformity, adopting the other's detail to agree [1], or the suggested detail may have been incorporated into her own memory [1]. Gabbert et al. (2003) found that 71% of witnesses who discussed an event reported details they had only learned from their partner [1]. **[4]**

**8.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Brief description: central executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad and episodic buffer [1]. Strength: dual-task studies show tasks using the same component interfere while different components do not [1]. Strength: the word length effect supports a limited-capacity phonological loop [1]. Strength: KF's impaired verbal but better visual STM fits separate components [1]. Strength: it explains active processing, such as mental arithmetic, better than a single store [1]. Limitation: the central executive is vaguely defined and hard to test [1]. Limitation: case-study evidence comes from unusual individuals whose brains may have been damaged in several areas [1]. Judgement: a well-supported improvement on the single STM store, though incomplete [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Reinstate the context: ask the witness to picture the shop, the weather and how she felt [1], because cues present at encoding help retrieve the memory [1]. Report everything: ask her to mention every detail, however trivial [1], because minor details may trigger recall of more important information [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* reverse order and change of perspective earn the same credit; each technique needs its rationale.

**10. (a)** "Crashed": 205 / 5 = 41.0 mph [1]. "Touched": 150 / 5 = 30.0 mph [1].
**(b)** Watching a film lacks the stress and consequences of a real event [1], so participants may pay less attention or care less about accuracy, which lowers ecological validity [1]. **[4]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Leading questions: Loftus and Palmer (1974) found "smashed" produced a mean estimate of 40.5 mph against 31.8 mph for "contacted" [1]. A week later, 32% of the "smashed" group reported broken glass against 14% for "hit" and 12% for controls [1], supporting substitution rather than only response bias [1]. Post-event discussion: Gabbert et al. (2003) found 71% of discussing witnesses reported unseen details, against 0% of controls [1]. This suggests memory conformity [1]. Strength: laboratory control allows cause and effect to be established [1]. Strength: findings have practical value, for example keeping witnesses apart before interview [1]. Limitation: films lack real emotional consequences [1]. Yuille and Cutshall (1986) found real witnesses stayed accurate months later despite misleading questions [1]. Limitation: student samples may not represent all witnesses [1]. Demand characteristics may lead participants to give the answer they think is expected [1]. Judgement: research shows real risks, but laboratory findings may overstate them [1]. **[12]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Clive Wearing lost most episodic memory but kept procedural skills such as playing the piano [1]. He also retained much semantic knowledge, suggesting separate stores [1]. HM learned mirror drawing without remembering practising it, separating procedural from episodic memory [1]. Strength: case studies give rich, detailed evidence of rare conditions that could not ethically be created [1]. Limitation: damage is often widespread, so it is hard to link one store to one area [1]. Limitation: findings from unusual individuals are hard to generalise [1]. Limitation: there is usually no record of the person's memory before the damage [1]. Judgement: case studies give strong support but need neuroimaging evidence alongside them [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations without working, or missing units.
- Application answers that describe a theory without using the scenario.
- Naming a cognitive interview technique without its rationale.
- Studies described without findings, or findings without the study.
- Extended answers with no judgement at the end.
