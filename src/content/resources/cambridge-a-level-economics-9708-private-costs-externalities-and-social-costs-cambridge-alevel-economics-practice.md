## Section A

**1.** Define an external cost. **[2]**

**2.** At a firm's current output, marginal private cost is 30, marginal external cost is 12 and marginal private benefit is 45. What is the marginal social cost?

A: 18

B: 30

C: 42

D: 57 **[1]**

**3.** For a cement factory, state whether each cost is private or external.

**(a)** The factory's electricity bill. **[1]**

**(b)** Dust from the factory that damages crops on a nearby farm. **[1]**

**4.** A positive externality of consumption means that:

A: marginal social benefit is greater than marginal private benefit

B: marginal social benefit is less than marginal private benefit

C: marginal social cost is greater than marginal private cost

D: marginal social cost is less than marginal private cost **[1]**

**5.** Distinguish between asymmetric information and moral hazard. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a negative production externality leads to over-production in a free market. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A paper mill pollutes a river. Output is in thousands of tonnes and values are per tonne. The marginal external cost is a constant 16. There is no external benefit.

| Output | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MPB | 60 | 52 | 44 | 36 | 28 |
| MPC | 12 | 20 | 28 | 36 | 44 |

**(a)** Calculate marginal social cost at each output. **[1]**

**(b)** State the free-market output. **[1]**

**(c)** State the socially optimal output. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the deadweight welfare loss. **[2]**

**(e)** State the tax per tonne that would move the market to the social optimum. **[1]**

**8.** A firm trains its workers, and some later move to other firms, taking their skills with them. Using a diagram, explain why the free market may provide too little training. **[4]**

**9.** An insurer covers 2,000 phones, each worth 400, against accidental damage. Before cover, the chance of a claim on each phone in a year is 5%. Once insured, owners take less care and the chance rises to 8%.

**(a)** Calculate the expected annual claims before and after owners change their behaviour. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the premium per phone needed to cover the new expected claims. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why this is an example of moral hazard, and why it can cause market failure. **[2]**

**10.** A government is considering a toll bridge. Values are in millions: toll revenue 40, time saved by users 25, fewer accidents 5, construction and running costs 55, environmental damage 12. Toll revenue is the only private benefit and construction and running costs are the only private cost.

**(a)** Calculate the net private benefit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the net social benefit. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how the decision would change if the value of time saved were 20% lower. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of cost-benefit analysis when a government decides whether to build a large public project. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A cost arising from production or consumption [1] that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction and is not compensated [1].

**2.** **C** [1]. MSC = MPC + MEC = 30 + 12 = 42.

**3.** **(a)** Private [1].

**(b)** External [1].

**4.** **A** [1]. Third parties gain, so the benefit to society exceeds the benefit to the consumer.

**5.** Asymmetric information means one party to a transaction knows more than the other [1]; moral hazard is a change in behaviour after an agreement, taking more risk or less care because someone else bears the cost [1].

**6.** The producer considers only private costs, so the market settles where MPB = MPC [1]. Because MSC is above MPC, units are produced whose social cost exceeds their social benefit, so output is above the social optimum where MSB = MSC [1].

**7.** **(a)** MSC = MPC + 16: **28, 36, 44, 52, 60** [1].

**(b)** MPB = MPC at **20 thousand tonnes** [1].

**(c)** MSB (= MPB) = MSC at **15 thousand tonnes** [1].

**(d)** At 20 thousand tonnes, MSC = 52 and MSB = 36, a gap of 16 [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 16 × 5,000 = **40,000** [1].

**(e)** **16 per tonne**, equal to the marginal external cost [1].

**8.** Training gives the firm a private benefit, but other firms that hire the trained workers gain an external benefit, so this is a positive production externality [1]. The diagram shows MSC below MPC, with demand as MPB = MSB [1]. The market provides training where MPB = MPC, which is below the optimum where MSB = MSC [1]. Units between the two quantities are worth more to society than they cost, so there is a welfare-loss triangle between MSB and MSC [1].

**9.** **(a)** Before: 0.05 × 2,000 × 400 = **40,000** [1]. After: 0.08 × 2,000 × 400 = **64,000** [1].

**(b)** 64,000 ÷ 2,000 = **32 per phone**, compared with 20 before [1].

**(c)** Owners take less care because the insurer, not they, bears the cost of damage, which the insurer cannot observe [1]. Premiums must rise, so careful owners may find insurance poor value and leave the market, and more resources are used repairing avoidable damage, so resources are misallocated [1].

**10.** **(a)** 40 − 55 = **−15 million** [1].

**(b)** SB = 40 + 25 + 5 = 70; SC = 55 + 12 = 67; net social benefit = **+3 million** [1].

**(c)** Time saved falls to 25 × 0.8 = 20, so SB = 65 and net social benefit = 65 − 67 = **−2 million** [1]. The bridge would no longer be worth building on social grounds, which shows the decision depends on the most uncertain value [1].

**11.** Useful: it counts external costs and benefits that a private decision ignores, so projects with net social benefit can go ahead even if they are unprofitable [1]; it gives a clear basis for comparing options and spending public money [1]; it makes assumptions explicit, so they can be challenged [1].

Limitations: intangible items such as noise, health or the loss of a landscape are hard to value in money [1]; forecasts of future use and costs can be wrong [1]; the result may flip when one uncertain value changes [1]; it adds up gains and losses without considering who receives them, so a project may harm a poor group while benefiting a richer one [1].

Judgement: cost-benefit analysis is a useful framework for organising a decision, but its result should be tested against changes in uncertain values and combined with judgements about distribution before a government relies on it [1].
