## The specification points

1. Distinguish between methods of production: job, batch, flow and cell.
2. Evaluate the impact and importance of the choice of production method to a business and its stakeholders.
3. Evaluate the reasons for the choice of production method.
4. Explain what is meant by specialisation and the division of labour.
5. Explain the reasons for the introduction of specialisation and division of labour into a business.
6. Evaluate the impact and importance of specialisation and division of labour to a business and its stakeholders.

## Core recall

### The four methods

| Method | Description | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job | One-off, to order | Bespoke, specialist | Flexible; customised; premium price | High unit cost; slow; skilled labour |
| Batch | Groups of identical items | Bread, paint, clothing | Some flexibility; lower cost than job | Changeover time; stock between stages |
| Flow | Continuous, standardised, often automated | Cars, drinks, chemicals | Lowest unit cost at high volume; consistent | Costly set-up; inflexible; breakdown stops all |
| Cell | Self-contained teams, each making a product family | Components, assemblies | Teamwork; multi-skilled; less work in progress | Training and layout change; less efficient than flow at very high volume |

### Factors in the choice

Volume and variety; customer requirements; capital available; unit cost at expected output; skills; flexibility needed; quality and speed; technology.

### Unit cost method

Unit cost = (fixed cost + variable cost per unit × output) ÷ output.

To find the output where two methods cost the same, set total costs equal and solve for output. The method with the lower fixed cost is cheaper at low output; the method with the lower variable cost is cheaper at high output.

### Impact and importance of the choice

- Costs and prices; quality and consistency; flexibility and lead time.
- Staff: skilled (job), machine minders (flow), multi-skilled teams (cell).
- Customers: tailored or low-price goods.
- Suppliers: regular or irregular orders.
- Image: luxury brands may use job production as a mark of quality.
- A wrong choice is costly: heavy fixed costs if demand falls; undercut if job is used for a mass product.

### Specialisation and division of labour

- Specialisation: concentrating on a limited set of tasks. Division of labour: splitting production into tasks done by different workers.
- Reasons: faster and more accurate through repetition; less time moving between tasks; less training per task; machines used for tasks; lower unit costs.
- Disadvantages: boredom, lower motivation, errors, absence; line stops if someone is absent; less flexibility; narrow skills.
- Responses: job rotation, cell production, training.

## Quick worked reminder

Fenwick Cabinets (invented): job £20,000 + £45 a unit; batch £60,000 + £32; flow £300,000 + £18. At 2,000 units: £55, £62, £168. At 10,000: £47, £38, £48. At 50,000: £45.40, £33.20, £24. Job/batch cross at 3,077 units; batch/flow at 17,143.

Division of labour: 8 workers, 32 minutes a unit, 480-minute day: 120 units, labour £6.40 a unit at £96 a day. Specialised, 24 minutes: 160 units if balanced, 144 at 90% efficiency (+20%); labour £5.33.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Job production | Making one-off items to a customer's order |
| Batch production | Making groups of identical items one batch at a time |
| Flow production | Continuous production of standardised items |
| Cell production | Production in self-contained teams |
| Changeover | Time to change a process between batches |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a limited set of tasks |
| Division of labour | Splitting work into tasks done by different people |
| Job rotation | Moving workers between tasks |

## Evaluation prompts

- What volume and variety does demand require?
- At the expected output, which method has the lowest unit cost?
- Can the business afford the fixed cost, and what if demand falls?
- Does the customer value a tailored product?
- Does specialisation raise output more than it harms motivation and flexibility?
- Finish with a judgement for the product and business in the case.

## Exam traps

- Flow is not always best: it is cheapest only at high volume.
- Batch involves changeovers; flow does not.
- Include fixed costs in unit-cost calculations.
- Consider employees as well as costs.
- Link the method to the product, volume and brand image.

## Command words

- **Distinguish:** state the difference between methods with an example of each.
- **Calculate:** find unit cost including fixed cost; find the output where two methods cost the same.
- **Evaluate:** match the method to volume, variety, capital and customer needs, then judge.
- **Explain:** link division of labour to faster, more accurate work and lower unit costs.

## Self-test

1. Define job production.
2. Give one product suited to batch production.
3. Give two disadvantages of flow production.
4. What is cell production?
5. Fixed cost £30,000, variable £10 a unit, output 3,000. Find the unit cost.
6. Which method is likely cheapest at very high volume?
7. Define division of labour.
8. Give two reasons for introducing division of labour.
9. Give two disadvantages of specialisation for workers.
10. Method X: £10,000 + £20 a unit. Method Y: £30,000 + £10 a unit. Find the output at which they cost the same.
11. Why might a luxury brand choose job production?
12. What is job rotation for?

### Answers

1. Making one-off items to a customer's order.
2. Any one: bread; paint; clothing ranges; pharmaceuticals.
3. Any two: very high set-up cost; inflexible; breakdown stops everything; repetitive work.
4. Production in self-contained teams, each making a complete product or family of products.
5. (30,000 + 10 × 3,000) ÷ 3,000 = 60,000 ÷ 3,000 = **£20**.
6. Flow production.
7. Splitting the production process into separate tasks done by different workers.
8. Any two: faster and more accurate; less time moving between tasks; less training; machines for tasks; lower unit costs.
9. Any two: boredom; lower motivation; errors; absence; narrow skills.
10. 10,000 + 20q = 30,000 + 10q, so 10q = 20,000 and q = **2,000 units**.
11. It allows customisation and quality that support a premium price and exclusive image.
12. To reduce boredom by moving workers between tasks, and to give wider skills.
