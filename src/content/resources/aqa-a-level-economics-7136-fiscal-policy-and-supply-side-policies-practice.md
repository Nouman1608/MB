## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a direct tax and an indirect tax, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between supply-side policies and supply-side improvements. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** reasons why governments levy taxes. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why transfer payments are not counted as government spending on goods and services in aggregate
demand. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** principles of taxation. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** microeconomic function of fiscal policy. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Country X's income tax has these bands: 0% on the first 12,000 of income; 25% on income from 12,000 to
50,000; 45% on income above 50,000.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid and the average tax rate for incomes of 20,000 and 70,000. **[4]**

**(b)** State the marginal tax rate at each income and explain whether the tax is progressive. **[2]**

**8.** Two households each pay excise duty on the fuel they buy. Household A has an income of 18,000 and pays 360;
household B has an income of 72,000 and pays 540. Explain whether the duty is progressive, proportional or
regressive. **[3]**

**9.** Country Y's MPC is 0.8. The government is choosing between raising spending by 6 billion and cutting income
tax by 6 billion.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum increase in national income from each option. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the government might still choose the tax cut. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how government spending on infrastructure can affect both aggregate demand and aggregate supply.
**[4]**

**11.** Explain how supply-side policies can reduce the natural rate of unemployment. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate whether free market supply-side policies are more effective than interventionist supply-side
policies in raising the trend rate of economic growth. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A direct tax is levied on **income or wealth** and paid directly by the person taxed, for example income tax
[1]. An indirect tax is levied on **spending** and collected through sellers, for example VAT [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Supply-side policies are **government measures** designed to raise productive capacity, such as training
subsidies [1]. Supply-side improvements are **increases in productive capacity from any source**, including
private-sector innovation and investment without government action [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two [2]: to raise revenue for public spending; to redistribute income; to correct market failures such
as negative externalities; to manage aggregate demand.

**4.** Transfer payments such as pensions **move income from taxpayers to recipients** [1] without the government
buying any output in return; they affect AD only when recipients spend them, which is counted in consumption [1].
**[2]**

**5.** Any two [2]: equity; certainty; convenience; economy of collection; efficiency; flexibility.

**6.** Fiscal policy can **change the pattern of economic activity** [1], for example a tax on sugary drinks
reduces their consumption, or a subsidy to apprenticeships raises training in a particular industry [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Income 20,000: tax = 25% × 8,000 = **2,000** [1]; average rate = 2,000 ÷ 20,000 = **10%** [1]. Income
70,000: tax = (25% × 38,000) + (45% × 20,000) = 9,500 + 9,000 = **18,500** [1]; average
rate = 18,500 ÷ 70,000 = **26.4%** [1].

**(b)** Marginal rates: **25%** at 20,000 and **45%** at 70,000 [1]. The average rate rises with income, so the tax is
**progressive** [1].

**8.** Household A pays 360 ÷ 18,000 = **2.0%** of income [1]; household B pays 540 ÷ 72,000 = **0.75%** [1]. B pays
more in total, but the share of income falls as income rises, so the duty is **regressive** [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5 [1]. Spending: 6 × 5 = **30 billion** [1]. Tax cut: first-round spending is
0.8 × 6 = 4.8 billion, so 4.8 × 5 = **24 billion** [1].

**(b)** Any one, explained [2]: lower income tax may raise **incentives to work** or to take on extra hours, shifting
LRAS right; it lets households choose how to spend their income; it may be quicker to introduce than new spending
projects.

**10.** *AD:* infrastructure spending is part of **government spending**, an injection [1]; through the multiplier,
national income rises by more than the initial spending [1]. *AS:* better roads, rail or broadband **reduce firms'
costs** and raise productivity [1], shifting **LRAS right** and raising potential output over time [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The natural rate consists mainly of **frictional and structural** unemployment [1]. **Education and
training** give workers the skills employers need, reducing structural mismatch [1]. **Better job-matching
information** shortens searches, reducing frictional unemployment [1]. **Help with mobility**, such as housing
support, lets workers move to regions with vacancies [1]. **Reforms to benefits or tax** that raise the gain from
working increase the incentive to accept jobs [1]. With fewer people unemployed at labour market equilibrium, the
natural rate falls and the long-run Phillips curve shifts left [1]. **[6]**

**12.** *Free market policies:* tax cuts may raise incentives to work, save and invest [1]; deregulation and
privatisation can increase competition and efficiency [1]; they may cost the government less in direct spending
[1]. *Weaknesses:* the response of work effort to tax cuts may be small, and benefits may go mainly to high earners,
raising inequality [1]. *Interventionist policies:* education, training and R&D subsidies correct **market
failures** such as short-termism and under-provision of training [1]; infrastructure raises productivity across the
economy [1]. *Weaknesses:* they are costly, take years to work and risk **government failure** if poorly targeted
[1]. *Judgement:* the more effective approach depends on the cause of weak growth; where skills and infrastructure
are lacking, interventionist policies are likely to do more, but the two are often complementary [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taxing the whole income at the top marginal rate.
- Calling a tax progressive because richer people pay more in total.
- Using the same multiplier effect for a tax cut and a spending rise.
- Calling any government spending a supply-side policy.
- Ignoring time lags in evaluating supply-side policies.
- Evaluation that ends without a judgement about which policy fits the circumstances.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I calculate tax with several bands?**
Apply each rate only to the slice of income within its band, add the amounts, then divide by total income for the
average rate.

**Is fiscal policy only about aggregate demand?**
No. The specification says fiscal policy can influence both aggregate demand and aggregate supply, and can have
microeconomic as well as macroeconomic functions.
