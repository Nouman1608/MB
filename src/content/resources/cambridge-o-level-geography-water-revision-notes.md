## The syllabus in brief

- Describe **supply methods** and the **agricultural, domestic and
  industrial** shares of use at different levels of development.
- Explain **why shortages occur** and why **careful management** is
  needed.
- Case study: **water supply in a country or area**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aquifer | Rock that stores and lets water pass through it |
| Water table | The upper level of saturated ground |
| Borehole | A narrow, deep shaft drilled to pump groundwater |
| Reservoir | A lake, often made by a dam, that stores water |
| Desalination | Removing salt from seawater to make fresh water |
| Over-abstraction | Taking groundwater or river water faster than it is replaced |
| Physical water scarcity | Not enough water because of climate or geography |
| Economic water scarcity | Water exists but there is no money or infrastructure to supply it safely |
| Irrigation | Artificial watering of crops |
| Drip irrigation | Pipes that drip water directly onto plant roots, cutting waste |

## Supply methods at a glance

- **Dams and reservoirs**: big store; flood land, evaporation losses.
- **Wells and boreholes**: cheap; can lower the water table.
- **Desalination**: unlimited seawater; expensive, energy-hungry, brine
  waste.
- **Recycling**: reuses waste water; treatment cost.
- **Transfers**: move water to dry areas; costly.
- **Rainwater harvesting**: cheap, local; depends on rain.

## Use and development

- Globally, **agriculture takes most** fresh water (around seven-tenths).
- **Low income**: agriculture dominates; little industry; low domestic
  use per person.
- **Industrialising**: industry share rises (cooling, processing).
- **High income**: industry and domestic shares higher; high domestic
  use per person.
- Always separate **share** (%) from **volume**.

## Causes of shortages

| Physical | Human |
|---|---|
| Low or unreliable rainfall | Population growth and urbanisation |
| High evaporation | Expanding irrigation |
| Drought | Over-abstraction of aquifers |
| Little storage space or few rivers | Pollution of rivers and groundwater |
| | Lack of investment in pipes and treatment |
| | Leaks and waste |

## Management

- **Increase supply**: reservoirs, desalination, recycling, transfers,
  rainwater tanks.
- **Reduce demand**: meters and pricing, fixing leaks, efficient
  appliances, drip irrigation, education.
- **Protect quality**: sewage treatment, pollution controls.

## Case study outline: Singapore

- Small, crowded island city-state; heavy rain but little storage land.
- PUB's **Four National Taps**: local catchment, imported water from
  Johor (Malaysia) under an agreement to 2061, NEWater (highly treated
  used water, mostly for industry), desalination (for example at Tuas).
- Marina Barrage (2008) created a city-centre reservoir and helps
  control floods.
- Demand managed by pricing, a conservation tax and campaigns.
- Limits: desalination and NEWater need a lot of energy and money.

## Second case study outline: Pakistan and the Indus

- **Supply**: the Indus and its tributaries feed one of the world's largest canal irrigation
  systems, through barrages and link canals; large storage dams include **Mangla** on the Jhelum
  (1967) and **Tarbela** on the Indus (1976), which also generate HEP.
- **Sharing**: the **Indus Waters Treaty** (1960, brokered by the World Bank) gave the eastern
  rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) mainly to
  Pakistan. In 2025 India announced it was holding the treaty in abeyance, showing how water
  supply can become a political issue between countries.
- **Use**: agriculture takes by far the largest share, matching the low- and middle-income
  pattern above.
- **Problems**: reservoirs lose capacity as silt builds up; seepage from unlined canals causes
  waterlogging and salinity; over-pumping by tube wells lowers the water table, for example
  around Lahore.
- **Urban shortage, Karachi**: most of the city's water comes from the Indus via Keenjhar Lake,
  with some from the Hub Dam. Leaking pipes, illegal connections and fast population growth mean
  many households rely on tankers or irregular supply, an example of **economic** scarcity.
- **Management**: canal lining, drip and sprinkler irrigation, metering, repairing leaks and new
  storage are all proposed; each needs money and agreement.

## Worked example: share or volume?

*A pie chart shows that agriculture takes 90% of water use in Country A and 40% in Country B.
Does Country A use more water for farming?* Not necessarily: a pie chart gives **shares**. If
Country B uses far more water in total, its 40% could be a larger **volume**. State the share,
then add that total use per person must be known before comparing volumes.

## Model evaluation sentence

"Desalination gives Singapore a supply that does not depend on rainfall or imports, but it is
expensive and uses large amounts of energy, so it suits a wealthy city-state better than a
low-income country such as South Sudan."

## Exam traps

- Percentages read as volumes.
- Only physical causes given.
- "Build a desalination plant" with no mention of cost or energy.
- No named schemes in the case study.
- Confusing physical scarcity (no water) with economic scarcity (no infrastructure).
- Management measures listed with no explanation of how they save or add water.
- Forgetting that the same scheme can solve one problem and cause another, such as dams trapping
  silt.

## Self-test

1. Name three methods of water supply.
2. Which sector uses most fresh water globally?
3. Why does the industrial share of water use rise with development?
4. Give two human causes of water shortage.
5. Distinguish physical and economic water scarcity.
6. Name Singapore's Four National Taps.
7. Give one disadvantage of desalination.
8. Name the treaty that divides the rivers of the Indus basin, and the year it was signed.
9. Give two problems of Pakistan's canal irrigation system.
10. Why is Karachi's water shortage mainly economic rather than physical?
11. Why does a pie chart of water use not show which country uses the most water?

**Answers:** 1. Any three of: reservoirs and dams, wells and boreholes,
river abstraction, desalination, recycling, transfers, rainwater
harvesting. 2. Agriculture. 3. Industrialisation brings factories and
power stations that need water for processing and cooling. 4. Any two
of: population growth, irrigation, over-abstraction, pollution, lack of
investment, leaks. 5. Physical: not enough water because of climate or
geography; economic: water exists but there is not the money or
infrastructure to supply it. 6. Local catchment water, imported water,
NEWater, desalinated water. 7. High energy use and cost, or salty brine
waste. 8. The Indus Waters Treaty, 1960. 9. Any two: silting of reservoirs; seepage from unlined
canals causing waterlogging and salinity; falling water tables from over-pumping. 10. Water is
available from the Indus, but leaks, illegal connections and too little investment in pipes and
treatment stop it reaching many homes. 11. It shows percentage shares, not volumes; total water
use per country or per person is also needed.
