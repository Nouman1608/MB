This guide covers outcomes **1.6.1 The implications of accounting decisions in the social context of the local community, environment, workforce, health and safety, and use of natural resources**, **1.6.2 Significance of non-financial factors** and **1.6.3 Effect of accounting decisions on different stakeholders**. They make up the first half of Topic 1.6, Social and ethical accounting, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Use it with the [social and ethical accounting revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-social-and-ethical-accounting-revision-notes/) and the [social accounting practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-social-accounting-practice/). Ethical principles are covered in the [ethics guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-ethics/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.6.1 | Implications of accounting decisions in the social context | Explain how a decision affects the community, environment, workforce, health and safety, and natural resources |
| 1.6.2 | Significance of non-financial factors | Identify and weigh factors that cannot be measured in money |
| 1.6.3 | Effect of accounting decisions on different stakeholders | Say who gains and who loses from a decision, and how |

Accounting is not only about profit. A decision that looks best on the numbers may harm people or the environment. A business that ignores these effects can lose customers, staff, goodwill and in the end money. The exam expects you to use both financial evidence and social evidence, and to reach a reasoned judgement.

## 1.6.1 The social context of accounting decisions

| Area | Examples of decisions with social effects | Possible consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Local community** | Closing a factory, relocating, expanding a site | Job losses, loss of local spending, traffic and noise, new jobs |
| **Environment** | Choice of packaging, waste disposal, energy source | Pollution, emissions, recycling, regulatory fines, reputation |
| **Workforce** | Redundancies, automation, pay and training | Loss of morale, skills, productivity, staff turnover |
| **Health and safety** | Spending on machine guards, training, protective equipment | Fewer accidents, lower insurance costs, risk of injury or prosecution if skimped |
| **Use of natural resources** | Water, timber, energy, raw materials | Resource depletion, higher future costs, sustainability |

Accounting information drives these decisions, because managers use cost figures, budgets and appraisals to choose between options. If the only number counted is cost, the cheapest option wins, even where it creates a social cost. Social accounting asks that the wider effects are also considered.

## Worked example: automating a production line

Marlow Textiles is considering a machine that will replace eight workers.

- Cost of machine: 160,000, with a life of 5 years and no residual value
- Running costs: 22,000 a year
- Wages saved: 8 workers × 19,500 = 156,000 a year
- Redundancy costs: 8 × 4,000 = 32,000 (once)

**Financial analysis**

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Wages saved | 156,000 |
| Less running costs | (22,000) |
| **Annual cash saving** | **134,000** |
| Less annual depreciation (160,000 ÷ 5) | (32,000) |
| **Annual profit improvement** | **102,000** |

Total initial outlay is 160,000 + 32,000 redundancy = 192,000, so the payback period is 192,000 ÷ 134,000 = **1.43 years**. On financial grounds, the machine looks attractive.

**Social analysis**

- *Workforce:* eight people lose their jobs, and the remaining staff may fear further cuts, which could lower morale and productivity.
- *Local community:* if Marlow is a major local employer, lost wages reduce spending in local shops, and the company's reputation in the town may suffer.
- *Health and safety:* a machine may reduce repetitive manual work but needs trained operators and guards.
- *Environment and resources:* the machine uses electricity (already in the running costs), but may reduce waste. The use of natural resources may change.
- *Non-financial gains:* more consistent quality, faster delivery and a safer working environment.

**Judgement.** A balanced answer would say the financial case is strong but the company should reduce harm, for example by retraining some workers for the new machine, offering support to those who leave, and informing staff early. This keeps the benefit and reduces the damage to morale and reputation.

## 1.6.2 The significance of non-financial factors

A **non-financial factor** is a consideration that cannot easily be measured in money, or that is not shown in the financial statements. Examples are reputation and brand image, employee morale and loyalty, customer satisfaction, relations with the local community, safety, the environment and the quality of service.

Why they matter:

- They can have a **large effect on future profit**. A good reputation attracts customers and staff. A damaged reputation can reduce revenue for years.
- They may create **legal and financial risks**, such as fines for pollution or claims after an accident.
- Many are **hard to measure**, so they are easy to ignore. This is why the exam asks for them directly.
- They matter differently to different **stakeholders**.

When you evaluate, do not just list them. Weigh them against the financial figures: "A saving of 134,000 a year is large, but if the loss of morale reduced output by 5%, the benefit could fall."

## 1.6.3 Effect on different stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is any person or group affected by, or with an interest in, the activities of the business.

| Stakeholder | Interest | Effect of the automation decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Owners / shareholders** | Profit, return on capital | Gain from the saving; may lose if reputation suffers |
| **Employees** | Jobs, pay, safety | Eight lose jobs; others fear more cuts; may gain safer work |
| **Customers** | Price, quality, delivery | May gain from better quality and faster delivery |
| **Suppliers** | Orders and payment | May gain if output increases; may lose if materials are used less |
| **Lenders** | Security of loan, ability to pay | Gain if cash flow improves |
| **Government and tax authorities** | Tax receipts, employment | Lose income tax if jobs are lost; gain if profits rise |
| **Local community** | Employment, environment | Lose spending power; may notice less traffic or noise |

Decisions create winners and losers. A good answer names the stakeholder, states the effect, and gives a reason. It then says which stakeholders have the most power or the strongest claim, and gives an overall judgement.

## Another example: a safety decision

A workshop can spend 15,000 on guards for its saws, which would reduce the risk of accidents. Without them, the owner estimates that one serious accident every five years is possible, costing 60,000 in compensation, lost production and insurance.

Annual expected cost of accidents = 60,000 ÷ 5 = 12,000. The guards cost 15,000, spread over, say, a 10-year life, which is 1,500 a year. On numbers alone the guards are cheap. The non-financial reasons are stronger still: a worker's health, the owner's legal duty, and morale. This shows why a decision should not be made on the lowest cost alone.

## Common mistakes

- Giving only financial arguments in a question that asks about social effects.
- Listing stakeholders without saying how each one is affected.
- Writing general statements such as "it is bad for the environment" with no link to the scenario.
- Treating all non-financial factors as less important than profit, without a reason.
- Confusing social effects with ethical principles (those are in outcome 1.6.4).
- Stating a judgement that does not follow from the evidence given.
- Forgetting that some social decisions bring financial gains, such as lower insurance or a better reputation.

## Exam technique

- Use the scenario. Name the business, the decision, the people and the figures.
- **Explain** questions need a point, a link to the scenario and a consequence.
- **Evaluate** or **recommend** questions need both sides: a financial case and a social case. Then reach a judgement and say what would change your mind.
- Quantify where you can. A figure from the question makes a strong point.
- Make a table of stakeholders (who, interest, effect) when the question asks about several of them.
