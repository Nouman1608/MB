## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregate demand | Total planned spending on a country's output at a given price level in a given period |
| Consumption (C) | Household spending on goods and services |
| Investment (I) | Firms' spending on capital goods (and stocks) |
| Government expenditure (G) | State spending on goods and services; excludes transfer payments |
| Net trade (X−M) | Exports minus imports |
| Disposable income | Income after direct taxes and plus benefits |
| Savings ratio | Savings as a percentage of disposable income |
| Gross investment | Total spending on capital goods |
| Net investment | Gross investment minus depreciation |
| Depreciation | Fall in the value of capital through wear and obsolescence |

## Formulas

| Result | Formula |
|---|---|
| AD | C + I + G + (X−M) |
| Disposable income | C + S (income is spent or saved) |
| Savings ratio | savings ÷ disposable income × 100 |
| Net investment | gross investment − depreciation |
| Change in capital stock (%) | net investment ÷ opening capital stock × 100 |
| Percentage change | change ÷ original × 100 |
| Income elasticity effect | % change in quantity = YED × % change in income |
| Price elasticity effect | % change in quantity = PED × % change in price (sign: price fall raises quantity) |
| Post-tax return | pre-tax profit × (1 − tax rate) ÷ cost |

## The AD curve

- Axes: price level (vertical), real output (horizontal). It slopes down.
- Reasons: wealth effect, interest rate effect, international trade effect.
- **Movement along:** triggered solely by the general price level changing.
- **Shift right:** a rise in C, I, G or X−M at every price level. **Shift left:** a fall.

Quick check. Country with C = 700, I = 120, G = 180, X = 160, M = 190: AD = 700 + 120 + 180 + (160 − 190) = **970**.

## Consumption: method in steps

Chain for any influence: change in influence, then change in disposable income or willingness to spend, then change in C, then shift in AD, then effect on output, jobs and prices.

| Influence | Rise in the influence | Effect on C |
|---|---|---|
| Disposable income | More to spend | Rises |
| Interest rates | Saving rewarded, loans dearer | Falls |
| Consumer confidence | Less fear of job loss | Rises |
| Welfare payments | Higher incomes for low earners | Rises |
| Wealth (house or share prices) | Owners feel richer | Rises |
| Availability of credit | Easier borrowing | Rises now, debt later |

**Savings ratio.** Example: savings 3,000 on disposable income 50,000 gives 6.0%. Savings 5,000 on 52,000 gives 9.6%, so the ratio rose by 3.6 percentage points.

- Causes of a rise: higher interest rates, low confidence, tighter credit, falling asset prices, ageing population, benefit cuts.
- Effects: lower C and AD in the short run; more funds for lending in the longer run.
- Evaluate with: size of rise, spare capacity, whether banks lend the savings, whether firms want to borrow, how long it lasts.

## Investment: must-know distinctions

| Gross | Net |
|---|---|
| All new capital spending | Gross minus depreciation |
| Can be positive while capacity falls | Only positive net investment raises capacity |

Example: gross 80, depreciation 50, net = **30**. If gross were 40, net = −10 and the capital stock shrinks.

**Influences:** growth (firms need capacity; the pace of growth matters), interest rates, business confidence and expectations, availability of credit, tax on company profits.

**Policy to promote I:**

| Tool | How it works | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Tax relief | Lets firms offset spending against tax | Costs revenue; some would have invested anyway |
| Subsidy | Cuts project cost directly | Government may pick poorly; costly |
| Lower corporation tax | Raises post-tax return | Only helps profitable firms; slow |

Method for a hurdle-rate question: post-tax return = pre-tax profit × (1 − t) ÷ cost, then compare with the borrowing rate. Example: profit 60,000 on cost 500,000 is 12.0% pre-tax; at 30% tax, 8.4%; at 20% tax, 9.6%. A firm borrowing at 9% invests only under the lower rate.

## Government expenditure: four influences

1. Fiscal policy (deliberate choices)
2. Level of economic activity (automatic stabilisers: benefits up, taxes down in a downturn)
3. Correction of market failures (public goods, merit goods, externalities)
4. Political priorities

Remember: benefits are transfers, not part of G, but they support C.

## Net trade: method in steps

1. Say which component moves (X, M or both).
2. Give the reason: income, price, global conditions, trade barriers or quality.
3. State the effect on X − M and so on AD.
4. Qualify with elasticity, time lags or retaliation.

| Factor | Effect on X − M |
|---|---|
| Domestic real income rises | M rises, X−M falls |
| Foreign real income rises | X rises, X−M rises |
| Exchange rate falls | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer; effect depends on elasticities |
| World slowdown | X falls |
| Higher tariffs by partners | X falls |
| Higher tariffs at home | M falls, but retaliation risk |
| Better quality, design, reliability, branding | Demand rises at any price |

**Reminder calculation.** Exports 600, imports 700. Foreign income +3% with income elasticity of demand for exports 2.0: exports +6% = 636. Home income +2% with income elasticity for imports 1.5: imports +3% = 721. Balance moves from −100 to −85.

## Evaluation prompts to reuse

- **Size and share:** how large is the component in AD? A 5% fall in a component that is 60% of AD matters more than a 10% fall in one that is 20%.
- **Spare capacity:** a rise in AD lifts output when there is slack, and prices when there is not.
- **Time lags:** investment decisions and trade responses take months or years, so the short run can differ from the long run.
- **Elasticity:** the price and income elasticities of exports and imports decide how far net trade changes.
- **Expectations:** confidence can reinforce or cancel a policy. A tax cut that firms expect to be reversed may not change plans.
- **Cost to government:** tax relief, subsidies and extra spending all reduce revenue or raise the deficit.
- **Other components:** a fall in one component can pull others down, because lower output cuts incomes and profits.

End each evaluation with a verdict and say what would change it.

## Must-know distinctions

- Movement along AD (price level) versus shift (anything else).
- Gross versus net investment.
- Savings (a flow) versus the savings ratio (a proportion of income).
- Transfers (not in G) versus government spending on output.
- Income effect on imports versus price effect of a currency change.

## Quick self-test

1. State the AD formula.
2. A country has C = 400, I = 90, G = 130, X = 110, M = 140. Calculate AD.
3. Give two things that cause a shift of AD to the left.
4. Disposable income is 20,000 and savings are 1,400. Calculate the savings ratio.
5. Gross investment is 70 and depreciation is 45. Calculate net investment.
6. Give two influences on investment.
7. A project costing 200,000 earns 24,000 profit before tax. Calculate the post-tax return at 25% tax.
8. Name two tools to promote investment.
9. Why do rising house prices raise consumption?
10. Give two non-price factors that raise exports.
11. Imports are 500 and income elasticity of demand for imports is 2.0. Home income rises 4%. Calculate the new level of imports.
12. State one reason why a rise in the savings ratio may be good for investment.

### Answers

1. AD = C + I + G + (X−M).
2. 400 + 90 + 130 + (110 − 140) = **590**.
3. For example, a fall in consumer confidence; a rise in interest rates; cuts in G; a world slowdown.
4. 1,400 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **7.0%**.
5. 70 − 45 = **25**.
6. For example, interest rates and business confidence (or growth, credit availability, tax on profits).
7. 24,000 × 0.75 = 18,000; 18,000 ÷ 200,000 = **9.0%**.
8. For example, tax relief and subsidies (or lower corporation tax).
9. Owners feel wealthier, and can borrow against property, so they spend more.
10. For example, quality and reliability (or design, branding, after-sales service).
11. Imports rise 2.0 × 4 = 8%; 500 × 1.08 = **540**.
12. More savings can be lent by banks to firms, which can lower interest rates and fund projects.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the AD curve and shifting it when the cause was a change in the price level.
- Describing an influence without linking it to AD.
- Treating a rise in savings as only bad (or only good) with no evaluation.
- Mixing up gross and net investment, or forgetting depreciation.
- Applying the wrong sign when an exchange rate falls (the home price of imports rises).
- Quoting a percentage-point change as a percentage change.
- Evaluating with a list of generic "it depends" phrases rather than a named factor such as spare capacity or elasticity.
- Leaving out the diagram's axis labels.
