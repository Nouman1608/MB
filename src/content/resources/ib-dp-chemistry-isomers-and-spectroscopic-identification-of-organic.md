## Stereoisomers (3.2.7)

**Stereoisomers** share the same constitution (atoms, connectivity and bond multiplicities) but differ in the arrangement of atoms in space. You need two kinds.

### Cis-trans isomerism

A compound shows cis-trans isomerism when both of these are true:

- **Rotation is restricted.** Twisting a C=C bond would destroy the sideways p-orbital overlap of the π bond. In a three- or four-membered ring, the ring itself locks the carbons.
- **Each of the two key carbons carries two different groups.** If either carbon has two identical groups, only one form exists.

**Cis**: matching groups on the same side of the double bond (or face of the ring). **Trans**: opposite sides. Rings are limited to C₃ and C₄, and E-Z naming is not assessed.

**Worked example 1: which compounds show cis-trans isomerism?**

| Compound | What each key carbon carries | Cis-trans? |
|---|---|---|
| 2-bromobut-2-ene, CH₃CBr=CHCH₃ | C2: CH₃ and Br; C3: H and CH₃ | **Yes** |
| 2-methylpent-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₂CH₃ | C2: two CH₃ groups | **No** |
| 1-ethyl-2-methylcyclopropane | C1: H and C₂H₅; C2: H and CH₃ | **Yes**: the ethyl and methyl groups share a face (cis) or not (trans) |
| 1,1-dichlorocyclobutane | C1: two Cl atoms; the other ring carbons: two H atoms | **No** |

### Chiral carbons and enantiomers

A **chiral** carbon is bonded to four different atoms or groups. Because its bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, the molecule and its mirror image cannot be superimposed. The two forms are **enantiomers**. Build both with a model kit or modelling software: no rotation turns one into the other.

**Drawing them.** Use tapered wedge-dash bonds: two plain lines in the plane of the paper, a solid wedge pointing towards you and a hashed wedge pointing away. Then draw the mirror image beside it.

**Worked example 2: 3-bromo-3-methylhexane, CH₃CH₂CBr(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃**

1. Carbon 3 carries Br, CH₃, C₂H₅ and C₃H₇. All four differ, so carbon 3 is chiral. A chiral carbon does not need a hydrogen.
2. No other carbon qualifies: each carries at least two H atoms.
3. First enantiomer: C₂H₅ as a plain line up-left, C₃H₇ as a plain line up-right, Br on a solid wedge, CH₃ on a hashed wedge.
4. Mirror image: imagine a vertical mirror. C₂H₅ moves to the right and C₃H₇ to the left, while Br stays on the solid wedge and CH₃ on the hashed one.

Shortcut: exchanging the positions of two groups also produces the partner enantiomer.

**Optical activity.** Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarized light by the same angle, one clockwise and one anticlockwise: both are **optically active**. In a **racemic mixture** the two enantiomers are present in a 1 : 1 ratio, so the rotations cancel, even though every molecule is chiral.

**Chemical properties.** Enantiomers share boiling points and densities and react alike with non-chiral reagents. They behave differently only in a **chiral environment**, such as an enzyme or a receptor. The two enantiomers of carvone smell different, one of spearmint and one of caraway, because the smell receptors in your nose are chiral.

## Mass spectrometry of organic compounds (3.2.8)

In the mass spectrometer, a molecule loses an electron to form the **molecular ion**, M⁺. The peak with the highest m/z (ignoring small isotope peaks) usually gives the relative molecular mass.

The molecular ion can **fragment** into a positive ion and a neutral species. Only the positive ion is detected, so the gap between M⁺ and a fragment peak tells you which neutral piece broke off.

| Ion | m/z |
|---|---|
| CH₃CO⁺ or C₃H₇⁺ | 43 |
| C₆H₅⁺ | 77 |
| CH₃⁺ | 15 |
| CHO⁺ or C₂H₅⁺ | 29 |
| CH₃O⁺ or CH₂OH⁺ | 31 |

| Neutral piece lost | Mass below M⁺ |
|---|---|
| CH₃ | 15 |
| OH | 17 |
| C₂H₅ or CHO | 29 |
| OCH₃ or CH₂OH | 31 |
| COOH | 45 |

The guide notes that the data booklet includes values for particular MS fragments; in a question, use the values given.

**Worked example 3: methyl 2-methylpropanoate, (CH₃)₂CHCOOCH₃**

Account for peaks at m/z 102, 87, 71, 59 and 43.

- 102: M⁺. C₅H₁₀O₂ has M = 5(12) + 10(1) + 2(16) = 102.
- 87: 102 − 15, loss of CH₃.
- 71: 102 − 31, loss of OCH₃, leaving (CH₃)₂CHCO⁺.
- 59: COOCH₃⁺ (12 + 16 + 16 + 15).
- 43: (CH₃)₂CH⁺, a C₃H₇⁺ ion.

Together, the peaks at 71 and 59 point to a methyl ester.

## Infrared spectroscopy (3.2.9)

Bonds vibrate at particular frequencies and absorb infrared radiation of matching frequency. Spectra plot transmittance against **wavenumber** (1/wavelength, in cm⁻¹).

| Bond | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| C=O | 1700–1750 | strong, sharp |
| C=C | 1620–1680 | |
| C≡C | 2100–2260 | |
| O–H, alcohol | 3200–3600 | broad |
| N–H | 3300–3500 | |
| O–H, carboxylic acid | 2500–3000 | very broad, overlapping C–H |
| C–H | 2850–3100 | in almost all organic molecules |
| C–O | 1050–1410 | |

The guide says IR data are given in the data booklet; use the table the question supplies. Interpret the **functional group region**, roughly above 1500 cm⁻¹.

**Worked example 4.** An unknown absorbs very broadly across 2500–3000 cm⁻¹, strongly at 1705 cm⁻¹ and at 1650 cm⁻¹. Interpret the spectrum.

- 2500–3000 cm⁻¹, very broad: O–H of a carboxylic acid.
- 1705 cm⁻¹: C=O, confirming a carboxyl group.
- 1650 cm⁻¹: C=C.

It is an **unsaturated carboxylic acid**, such as but-2-enoic acid, CH₃CH=CHCOOH, which also has cis and trans forms that the functional group region cannot tell apart.

### IR activity and greenhouse gases

A vibration absorbs IR only if it **changes the dipole moment**. The symmetric stretch of CO₂ keeps the molecule non-polar, so it is IR inactive; the asymmetric stretch and the bend change the dipole and absorb. N₂ and O₂ never gain a dipole as they vibrate, so they do not absorb IR.

Earth's surface emits infrared radiation. CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ absorb some of it and re-emit it in all directions, partly back towards the surface: they are greenhouse gases. A gas's **global warming potential** compares its warming effect with that of the same mass of CO₂ over a set period. It is higher when the gas absorbs IR strongly, especially at wavelengths other gases leave unabsorbed, and when it stays in the atmosphere for a long time.

## ¹H NMR: signals, shifts and integration (3.2.10)

In a strong magnetic field, hydrogen nuclei absorb radio waves, at slightly different frequencies in different **chemical environments**.

- **Number of signals** = number of different hydrogen environments. Equivalent hydrogens, such as the three in a CH₃ group or two CH₃ groups on the same carbon, give one signal.
- **Chemical shift**, δ in ppm, is measured relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm. Hydrogens near electronegative atoms or a C=O group are deshielded and appear further from 0.
- **Integration trace**: the step heights over the signals are in the ratio of hydrogen atoms in each environment.

| Type of hydrogen | Approximate δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| CH₃ or CH₂ in an alkyl chain | 0.9–1.7 |
| H on carbon next to C=O | 2.0–2.7 |
| H on carbon bonded to O (alcohol, ether, ester) | 3.3–4.3 |
| O–H in an alcohol | 1–6, variable |
| H on a benzene ring | 6.5–8.0 |
| –CHO | 9.4–10 |
| –COOH | 10–13 |

The guide says ¹H NMR data are given in the data booklet; these ranges are approximate.

**Worked example 5: 3-methylpentan-3-ol, CH₃C(OH)(CH₂CH₃)₂**

Integration steps of 9, 12, 18 and 3 mm divided by 3 give 3 : 4 : 6 : 1: the CH₃ on carbon 3 (3 H), the two CH₂ groups (4 H), the two end CH₃ groups (6 H) and the OH (1 H).

## Splitting patterns (3.2.11)

A signal is split by hydrogen atoms on **adjacent** carbons. With n such neighbours, it splits into **n + 1** peaks.

| Neighbouring H (n) | Pattern | Peak ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | singlet | 1 |
| 1 | doublet | 1 : 1 |
| 2 | triplet | 1 : 2 : 1 |
| 3 | quartet | 1 : 3 : 3 : 1 |

Equivalent hydrogens do not split each other, and an alcohol's O–H signal usually appears as a singlet.

**Worked example 6.** In 3-methylpentan-3-ol, carbon 3 has no hydrogen, so its CH₃ is a **singlet**. Each CH₂ has three neighbours: a **quartet**. Each end CH₃ has two: a **triplet**. The OH is a **singlet**. A 2H quartet with a 3H triplet signals an ethyl group.

## Combining techniques (3.2.12)

MS gives the molar mass and fragments, IR the functional groups, ¹H NMR the carbon-hydrogen framework.

**Worked example 7.** Compound Z contains 54.5% C, 9.2% H and 36.3% O by mass. Its mass spectrum shows M⁺ at 88 with peaks at 73, 45 and 43. IR: broad 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ and strong 1715 cm⁻¹. ¹H NMR: 1.4 ppm (doublet, 3H), 2.2 ppm (singlet, 3H), 3.6 ppm (singlet, 1H), 4.2 ppm (quartet, 1H). Identify Z.

1. Moles in 100 g: C 54.5/12.01 = 4.54; H 9.2/1.01 = 9.11; O 36.3/16.00 = 2.27. The ratio is 2 : 4 : 1, so the empirical formula is C₂H₄O (M = 44).
2. 88/44 = 2, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈O₂.
3. IR: alcohol O–H and C=O. With no 2500–3000 band and no signal at 10–13 ppm, Z is not an acid.
4. NMR: the 2.2 ppm singlet (3 H) is CH₃ on C=O with no neighbours, so CH₃CO–. The 4.2 ppm quartet (1 H) is a CH on a carbon bonded to O, next to a CH₃. The 1.4 ppm doublet (3 H) is that CH₃. The 3.6 ppm singlet is the OH.
5. MS: 43 is CH₃CO⁺; 45 is CH₃CHOH⁺; 73 is 88 − 15.

Z is **3-hydroxybutan-2-one**, CH₃COCH(OH)CH₃. Carbon 3 carries four different groups, so Z is also chiral.

## Common errors

- Calling an alkene cis-trans when one carbon of the C=C has two identical groups.
- Drawing two wedges, or two dashes, on a chiral carbon instead of one of each.
- Saying a racemic mixture is inactive because its molecules are not chiral; the rotations cancel instead.
- Counting hydrogens on the same carbon, or the O–H hydrogen, as splitting neighbours.
- Treating a fragment's m/z as the mass lost; subtract it from M⁺.
