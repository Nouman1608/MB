## Stereoisomers (3.2.7)

**Stereoisomers** share the same constitution: the same atoms, joined in the same order, by bonds of the same type. Only the arrangement of atoms in space differs. You need two kinds.

### Cis-trans isomerism

Two features are needed:

1. **Restricted rotation.** The π bond in C=C stops rotation about that bond, because turning one end would break the sideways overlap of p orbitals. In a small ring, the ring itself prevents rotation.
2. **Two different groups on each of the two carbons.** If either carbon carries two identical groups, there is only one form.

In the **cis** isomer, matching groups sit on the same side of the double bond or ring; in the **trans** isomer, they sit on opposite sides. For rings, the guide limits you to cyclopropane and cyclobutane derivatives. E-Z naming is not assessed.

**Worked example 1: which compounds show cis-trans isomerism?**

| Compound | Check each carbon | Cis-trans? |
|---|---|---|
| 1-bromoprop-1-ene, CHBr=CHCH₃ | C1 has H and Br; C2 has H and CH₃ | **Yes** |
| 2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ | C2 carries two CH₃ groups | **No** |
| 1,2-dimethylcyclopropane | each ring carbon has H and CH₃ | **Yes**: both CH₃ above the ring (cis) or one above, one below (trans) |
| 1,3-dichlorocyclobutane | C1 and C3 each have H and Cl | **Yes** |

### Chiral carbons and enantiomers

A **chiral** carbon is bonded to four different atoms or groups. The four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, so the molecule and its mirror image cannot be laid on top of each other. These two forms are **enantiomers**: non-superimposable mirror images. A model kit or modelling software shows that no rotation turns one into the other.

**Drawing them.** Use the guide's wedge-dash convention: two bonds in the plane of the page, one tapered solid wedge coming towards you and one hashed dash going away. Draw the mirror image beside it.

**Worked example 2: 3-methylhexane**

CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃. Carbon 3 carries H, CH₃, C₂H₅ and C₃H₇. Four different groups, so carbon 3 is chiral. Carbon 2 (two H atoms) and carbon 4 (two H atoms) are not.

```
        CH3         :          CH3
         |          :           |
 C3H7 -- C <| H     :     H |> C -- C3H7
         :          :           :
        C2H5     (mirror)      C2H5
```

Key: "--" and "|" lie in the plane; "<|" and "|>" are wedges towards you; ":" below each carbon is a dash going away. On paper, draw real tapered wedges and hashed dashes.

**Optical activity.** Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarized light by the same angle, but in opposite directions: both are **optically active**. A **racemic mixture** holds equal amounts of both enantiomers. Their rotations cancel, so the mixture shows no net rotation.

**Chemical properties.** Enantiomers share boiling points and densities and react identically with non-chiral reagents. They behave differently only in a **chiral environment**, such as an enzyme or receptor. The two enantiomers of carvone smell different, one of spearmint and one of caraway, because the receptors in your nose are chiral.

## Mass spectrometry of organic compounds (3.2.8)

In the mass spectrometer, a molecule loses an electron to form the **molecular ion**, M⁺. The peak with the highest m/z (ignoring small isotope peaks) usually gives the relative molecular mass.

The molecular ion can **fragment**: it breaks into a positive ion and a neutral species. Only the positive ion is detected, so the gaps between peaks show which neutral pieces were lost.

| Peak at m/z | Likely ion |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ |
| 31 | CH₂OH⁺ or CH₃O⁺ |
| 43 | C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺ |
| 77 | C₆H₅⁺ |

| Gap below M⁺ | Likely group lost |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃ |
| 17 | OH |
| 29 | C₂H₅ or CHO |
| 31 | OCH₃ or CH₂OH |
| 45 | COOH |

The guide states that data on specific MS fragments are provided in the data booklet; the table above is a practice set of common values.

**Worked example 3: methyl 2-methylpropanoate, (CH₃)₂CHCOOCH₃**

Peaks appear at m/z 102, 87, 71, 59 and 43. Explain them.

- 102: M⁺, since C₅H₁₀O₂ has M = 5(12) + 10(1) + 2(16) = 102.
- 87: 102 − 15, loss of CH₃.
- 71: 102 − 31, loss of OCH₃, leaving (CH₃)₂CHCO⁺.
- 59: COOCH₃⁺ (12 + 16 + 16 + 15).
- 43: (CH₃)₂CH⁺, a C₃H₇⁺ ion.

Together, 71 and 59 point to a methyl ester.

## Infrared spectroscopy (3.2.9)

Bonds stretch and bend at particular frequencies and absorb IR radiation of matching frequency. IR spectra plot transmittance against **wavenumber** in cm⁻¹, where wavenumber = 1/wavelength.

| Bond | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| O–H in alcohols | 3200–3600 | broad |
| N–H | 3300–3500 | |
| C–H | 2850–3100 | present in nearly every organic compound |
| O–H in carboxylic acids | 2500–3000 | very broad |
| C≡C | 2100–2260 | |
| C=O | 1700–1750 | strong, sharp |
| C=C | 1620–1680 | |
| C–O | 1050–1410 | |

The guide states that IR data are given in the data booklet; use whatever table a question supplies. You interpret the **functional group region**, above about 1500 cm⁻¹.

**Worked example 4.** An unknown has absorptions at 2500–3000 cm⁻¹ (very broad) and 1705 cm⁻¹. Its third absorption is quoted as a wavelength of 6.06 μm.

Convert: 6.06 μm = 6.06 × 10⁻⁴ cm, so wavenumber = 1/(6.06 × 10⁻⁴) = **1650 cm⁻¹**, which is C=C. The broad band is O–H in a carboxylic acid and 1705 cm⁻¹ is C=O. The compound is an **unsaturated carboxylic acid**, for example propenoic acid, CH₂=CHCOOH.

### IR activity and greenhouse gases

A vibration absorbs IR only if it **changes the dipole moment** of the molecule. The symmetric stretch of CO₂ keeps the molecule non-polar throughout, so it is IR inactive. The asymmetric stretch and the bend do change the dipole, so they absorb. N₂ and O₂ have no dipole at any point in their only vibration, so they do not absorb IR.

Earth's surface emits infrared radiation. CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ absorb some of it and re-emit it in all directions, some back towards the surface. That is why they are greenhouse gases.

## ¹H NMR: signals, shifts and integration (3.2.10)

In a magnetic field, hydrogen nuclei absorb radio waves; those in different **chemical environments** absorb at slightly different frequencies.

- **Number of signals** = number of different hydrogen environments. Equivalent H atoms, such as the three in a CH₃ group or two CH₃ groups on the same carbon, give one signal.
- **Chemical shift**, δ in ppm, is measured relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm. H atoms near electronegative atoms or C=O are deshielded and appear further from 0.
- **Integration trace**: the step heights over the signals are in the ratio of hydrogen atoms in each environment.

| Type of hydrogen | Approximate δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| CH₃ or CH₂ in an alkyl chain | 0.9–1.7 |
| H on carbon next to C=O | 2.0–2.7 |
| H on carbon bonded to O (alcohol, ether, ester) | 3.3–4.3 |
| O–H in an alcohol | 1–6, variable |
| H on a benzene ring | 6.5–8.0 |
| –CHO | 9.4–10 |
| –COOH | 10–13 |

The guide states that ¹H NMR data are given in the data booklet; these ranges are approximate.

**Worked example 5: 3-methylpentan-3-ol, CH₃C(OH)(CH₂CH₃)₂**

The integration trace has steps of 9 mm, 12 mm, 18 mm and 3 mm. Dividing by 3 gives 3 : 4 : 6 : 1. That matches four environments: the CH₃ on carbon 3 (3 H), the two CH₂ groups (4 H), the two end CH₃ groups (6 H) and OH (1 H).

## Splitting patterns (3.2.11)

A signal is split by hydrogen atoms on **adjacent** carbons. With n such neighbours, the signal splits into **n + 1** peaks.

| Neighbouring H (n) | Pattern | Peak ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | singlet | 1 |
| 1 | doublet | 1 : 1 |
| 2 | triplet | 1 : 2 : 1 |
| 3 | quartet | 1 : 3 : 3 : 1 |

Equivalent hydrogens do not split each other, and an alcohol's O–H signal usually appears as a singlet.

**Worked example 6.** Back to 3-methylpentan-3-ol: the CH₃ on carbon 3 has no H on carbon 3 next to it, so it is a **singlet**. Each CH₂ has three neighbours (its CH₃), so it is a **quartet**. Each end CH₃ has two neighbours, giving a **triplet**. The OH is a **singlet**. A quartet (2 H) paired with a triplet (3 H) is the fingerprint of an ethyl group.

## Combining techniques (3.2.12)

Use MS for molar mass and fragments, IR for functional groups and ¹H NMR for the carbon-hydrogen framework.

**Worked example 7.** Compound Z contains 54.5% C, 9.2% H and 36.3% O by mass. Its mass spectrum shows M⁺ at 88 with peaks at 73, 45 and 43. IR: broad 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ and strong 1715 cm⁻¹. ¹H NMR: 1.4 ppm (doublet, 3H), 2.2 ppm (singlet, 3H), 3.6 ppm (singlet, 1H), 4.2 ppm (quartet, 1H). Identify Z.

1. Moles in 100 g: C 54.5/12.01 = 4.54; H 9.2/1.01 = 9.11; O 36.3/16.00 = 2.27. Ratio 2 : 4 : 1, so the empirical formula is C₂H₄O (M = 44).
2. 88/44 = 2, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈O₂.
3. IR: alcohol O–H and C=O. No 2500–3000 band and no NMR signal at 10–13 ppm, so not an acid.
4. NMR: 2.2 ppm singlet (3 H) is CH₃ next to C=O with no neighbours, so CH₃CO–. The 4.2 ppm quartet (1 H) is a CH on a carbon bonded to O, next to a CH₃. The 1.4 ppm doublet (3 H) is that CH₃. The 3.6 ppm singlet is the OH.
5. MS: 43 is CH₃CO⁺; 45 is CH₃CHOH⁺; 73 is 88 − 15.

Z is **3-hydroxybutan-2-one**, CH₃COCH(OH)CH₃. Carbon 3 carries four different groups, so Z is chiral as well.

## Common errors

- Calling an alkene cis-trans when one carbon of C=C has two identical groups.
- Drawing two wedges, or two dashes, on a chiral carbon instead of one of each.
- Saying a racemic mixture is inactive because the molecules are not chiral. Each molecule is chiral; the rotations cancel.
- Counting hydrogens on the same carbon, or on O–H, as splitting neighbours.
- Treating the fragment's m/z as the mass lost. Subtract from M⁺ to find the lost group.
