This guide covers **3.1.2 Methods of language analysis** in AQA A-level English Language (7702): the analytical
toolkit for Paper 1. The specification says you must "identify and describe salient features of language in the
texts" using methods of language analysis, in order to study textual variations and representations. The same
toolkit describes children's language data in Section B. For what you *do* with the features (representation,
positioning, comparison), see the
[textual variations and representations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-7702-section-a-textual-variations-and-representations/).

## The six levels in the specification

| Level | Specification wording | What it gives you in Paper 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Phonetics, phonology and prosodics | How speech sounds and effects are articulated and analysed | Sound patterning in written texts; stress, pitch, pace and pauses in transcripts |
| Graphology | The visual aspects of textual design and appearance | Layout, typography, colour, images and their relation to words |
| Lexis and semantics | The vocabulary of English, including social and historical variation | Word choice, connotation, semantic fields, register, older lexis |
| Grammar, including morphology | Structural patterns at sentence, clause, phrase and word level | Sentence functions, clause structure, noun and verb phrases, word formation |
| Pragmatics | The contextual aspects of language use | Implied meaning, politeness, speech acts, assumed knowledge |
| Discourse | Extended stretches of communication in different genres, modes and contexts | Overall structure, cohesion, openings and closings, turn-taking |

## "Salient" is the key word

Salient means *prominent and significant*. You are not asked to find every feature at every level; you are asked to
find the features that do most to create the text's meanings. A practical test: if this feature were changed, would
the representation or relationship change? If yes, it is salient. Repeated patterns (a semantic field running
through a text, a run of imperatives) and marked choices (a sudden shift in register, a single minor sentence) are
usually more salient than isolated features.

## Level by level: the terminology that earns credit

**Phonology and prosodics.** In written texts: alliteration, sibilance, assonance, rhyme, rhythm and onomatopoeia,
which matter most in slogans, headlines and speeches written to be heard. In transcripts: stress (often shown in
capitals or underlining), rising and falling intonation, pauses and micropauses, changes of pace and volume, and
features such as elision ("gonna"). Always use the transcript's own key.

**Graphology.** Typeface (serif, sans serif, handwritten-style), size and weight, colour, layout and white space,
the positioning of headings and images, logos, and the relationship between image and text: does the image anchor
the meaning of the words, or do the words direct how we read the image? In an older printed text, dense columns and
few images are themselves evidence of context.

**Lexis and semantics.** Semantic fields; connotation; formal versus informal register; jargon and specialist
lexis; colloquialisms; euphemism and dysphemism; collocation (words that habitually go together, and deliberate
breaks in collocation); figurative language; lexis that has changed meaning or fallen out of use in an older text.

**Grammar, including morphology.** Work up through the ranks:

- *Word:* word classes (concrete and abstract nouns, dynamic and stative verbs, modal verbs, adverbs of degree);
  morphology (prefixes, suffixes, compounds, coinages such as "staycation").
- *Phrase:* noun phrase structure (determiner, pre-modifier, head, post-modifier); verb phrase (tense, aspect,
  modality, voice).
- *Clause:* clause elements (subject, verb, object, complement, adverbial); main and subordinate clauses; fronted
  adverbials.
- *Sentence:* functions (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamative) and structures (simple, compound,
  complex, compound-complex, minor).

**Pragmatics.** Implicature (meaning implied beyond what is said, often explained through Grice's maxims of quantity,
quality, relation and manner); presupposition; speech acts (the action an utterance performs: apologising,
warning, promising); politeness and face; deixis (words such as "here", "now", "we" whose meaning depends on
context); assumed shared knowledge.

**Discourse.** Overall structure (narrative, problem and solution, question and answer, chronological); cohesion
through reference (pronouns pointing back or forward), lexical repetition, synonyms, ellipsis and conjunctions;
openings and closings; in speech, adjacency pairs, turn-taking, topic shifts and repair.

## Worked example: describing salient features precisely

The text below is **original**, written for this page: a leaflet for a community café.

> *"Pull up a chair. At The Kettle, nobody's a stranger for long. Our volunteers bake fresh every morning, our tea is
> always brewing, and every penny goes back into the neighbourhood. Pop in. You'll be glad you did."*

| Level | Salient feature, precisely described | Contribution to meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Grammar | Opening and closing imperatives ("Pull up a chair", "Pop in"), short simple sentences | Direct, friendly invitation framing the text |
| Grammar | Tricolon of parallel clauses with "our" as determiner | Builds a rhythm of collective pride and generosity |
| Lexis | Domestic, informal lexis ("Pull up a chair", "Pop in", "brewing") | Represents the café as an extension of home |
| Pragmatics | "nobody's a stranger for long" implies that newcomers are welcomed quickly | Reassures a lonely or new reader without naming loneliness |
| Pragmatics | Future "You'll be glad you did" presupposes the reader will visit | Positions the reader as already persuaded |
| Discourse | Circular structure: invitation, reasons, invitation | Cohesion through repeated directive address |
| Graphology | (In the real leaflet you would analyse font, colour and images) | Whether design reinforces warmth and informality |

Notice that the table never says "short sentence for effect". It names the structure, quotes it and states the
meaning it helps create.

## Worked example: getting grammar right

*"The tired volunteers who had worked all night finally closed the doors."*

```
Noun phrase   the (determiner) + tired (pre-modifier) + volunteers (head)
              + who had worked all night (post-modifying relative clause)
Verb phrase   had worked: past perfect, placing the work before the closing
              closed: simple past, main verb; dynamic, material process
Adverbial     finally: adverb signalling the end of a long effort
Clause        main clause "The tired volunteers ... closed the doors" with an
              embedded relative clause; sentence structure is complex
Effect        the modifiers and the past perfect foreground the volunteers'
              effort before the action, building sympathy and admiration
```

Accurate description like this lets you make confident claims. Calling this a "long sentence" earns nothing; calling
it a complex sentence with a post-modifying relative clause that delays the verb, so that effort is foregrounded,
earns credit for method and for meaning.

## Applying methods to children's data (Section B)

The same levels describe children's speech and writing: phonological processes (substitution, deletion,
reduplication), lexical and semantic development (over- and underextension), grammatical development (inflections,
negatives, questions), pragmatic development (turn-taking, politeness) and, for writing, graphology and orthography.
Description comes first; theory follows. See the
[children's language development study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-7702-section-b-childrens-language-development/).

## Common mistakes

- Using "short sentence" or "long sentence" instead of naming structure or function.
- Calling every question "rhetorical", or every "we" "inclusive" without checking who it includes.
- Confusing simple sentences (one main clause) with short ones.
- Listing word classes ("there are adjectives and verbs") with no pattern or purpose.
- Ignoring graphology in a multimodal text, or prosody in a transcript.
- Applying a level mechanically when it adds nothing to the text's meaning.
- Mislabelling: calling a noun phrase a clause, or a modal verb an adverb.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** means name the feature accurately; **describe** means say what it is and how it is formed;
  **analyse** means explain how it creates meaning in context. Paper 1 analysis questions need all three.
- Plan by meaning first, then select levels: decide what the text represents, then find the salient features that
  build it.
- Range matters, but depth matters more. Aim to use several levels across an answer, and develop each point.
- Quote briefly and precisely, embedding quotations in your sentences.
