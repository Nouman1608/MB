## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Value consensus | Broad agreement among members of a society on shared values |
| Social solidarity | A sense of belonging to, and commitment to, society |
| Role allocation | Sorting people into the jobs that suit their talents |
| Particularistic standards | Judging a person as a unique individual (as in the family) |
| Universalistic standards | Judging everyone by the same rules (as at school) |
| Ascribed status | Status fixed at birth |
| Achieved status | Status gained through effort and ability |
| Ideological state apparatus | An institution that controls people through ideas, not force (Althusser) |
| Correspondence principle | School life mirrors work life (Bowles and Gintis) |
| Cultural reproduction | Education passing on dominant-class culture and so reproducing class inequality |
| Marketisation | Making schools compete for pupils, like firms in a market |
| Human capital | Skills and knowledge treated as an investment that raises productivity |
| Meritocracy | Rewards based on ability and effort, not background |
| Life chances | A person's chances of gaining good things such as income, health and secure work |
| Absolute mobility | The total amount of movement between classes |
| Relative mobility | The chances of people from different class backgrounds compared with each other |
| Qualification inflation | Employers asking for higher credentials as more people hold them (Dore) |
| Social construction of knowledge | What counts as valuable knowledge is decided by people, not fixed by nature |
| Ethnocentric curriculum | A curriculum that gives priority to one ethnic group's culture, history and language |
| Gendered curriculum | Content and subject choices that follow gender stereotypes |
| Hidden curriculum | What pupils learn from how school is organised, not from formal lessons |
| Cultural capital | Knowledge, tastes, language and attitudes valued by the dominant class |
| Habitus | Tastes and dispositions learned early in life (Bourdieu) |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Key idea or finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Durkheim (1858-1917) | Functionalist | Education passes on shared values and builds social solidarity; school is a society in miniature; teaches specialist skills | Assumes one set of shared values in diverse societies |
| Parsons | Functionalist | School is the bridge from particularistic family to universalistic society; status becomes achieved | Ignores class barriers to achievement |
| Davis and Moore (1945) | Functionalist | Education sifts the most able into the most important, best-rewarded jobs | Tumin: high pay does not prove importance |
| Althusser (1970; English 1971) | Marxist | School is the dominant ideological state apparatus in capitalist societies | Sees pupils as passive |
| Bowles and Gintis (1976) | Marxist | Correspondence principle; New York study of 237 students: grades linked to perseverance and punctuality, not creativity | Based on 1970s American schooling |
| Willis (1977) | Marxist | 12 working-class "lads" resisted school yet still entered manual jobs | Small sample of boys in one school |
| Bourdieu (with Passeron) | Cultural reproduction | Schools reward middle-class cultural capital | Some working-class pupils succeed |
| Halsey | Social democratic | Equality of opportunity through state action, such as comprehensive schools | Expansion has not closed class gaps |
| Schultz; Becker (1964) | Human capital theory | Education is an investment that raises productivity | Wolf (2002): spending does not automatically drive growth |
| Chubb and Moe (1990) | New Right | Democratic control causes poor schools; choice and competition improve them | Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz (1994): markets favour skilled, mainly middle-class choosers |
| Young (1958) | Meritocracy | Merit = intelligence + effort; satire warning of a new elite | Written as a warning, not a blueprint |
| Dore (1976) | Credentials | Qualification inflation weakens the value of each credential | Qualifications still matter for many jobs |
| Goldthorpe (1980) | Mobility | Absolute mobility rose; relative mobility stayed broadly stable | Focuses on Britain |
| Krueger (2012), Corak's data | Mobility | "Great Gatsby curve": more unequal countries tend to have less mobility | Correlation, not proof of cause |
| Michael F. D. Young (ed.), *Knowledge and Control* | New sociology of education (1970s) | School knowledge is socially organised | Others argue some knowledge is genuinely more powerful |
| Apple (1979) | Curriculum | Asks whose knowledge becomes "official" | Teachers and pupils can reshape content |
| Coard (1971) | Ethnocentric curriculum | Schools treated West Indian children's culture as second-rate | A campaigning booklet, about Britain in one period |
| Spender (1982) | Gendered curriculum | Curriculum left out or played down women's contributions | Many curricula now include more women |
| Jackson (1968) | Hidden curriculum | Coined the term in *Life in Classrooms* | Rules are now often written down openly |
| Illich (1971) | Hidden curriculum | Schooling as an institution cannot deliver true education for all | Critics doubt his informal "learning webs" could replace schools at scale |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Functionalist vs Marxist hidden curriculum.** Functionalists: it teaches shared values everyone needs. Marxists: it trains a compliant workforce for capitalism.
- **Social democratic vs New Right.** Both link education to the economy. Social democrats want state action for equal opportunity; the New Right wants markets and choice.
- **Formal vs substantive equal opportunity.** Formal removes legal barriers; substantive also tackles hidden barriers such as poverty.
- **Absolute vs relative mobility.** More upward movement (absolute) can happen while class chances stay just as unequal (relative).
- **Cultural reproduction vs cultural capital.** Cultural reproduction is the process; cultural capital is the resource that makes it work.
- **Formal vs hidden curriculum.** Formal is the planned subjects; hidden is what the organisation of school teaches.

## Mini essay plan

**Question:** "Evaluate the view that the main role of education is to serve the needs of the economy." (Extended-response style; the real Question 4 is worth 26 marks.)

1. **Introduction.** Define the economic role (skills, role allocation, human capital). Name the perspectives: functionalist, Marxist, New Right, social democratic.
2. **For: functionalism.** Durkheim on specialist skills; Davis and Moore on role allocation. Apply to a modern economy that needs engineers and nurses.
3. **For: human capital and the New Right.** Schultz and Becker; Chubb and Moe on competition. Apply to vocational and science-focused policy.
4. **For, but critical: Marxism.** Bowles and Gintis: education serves capitalism, not "the economy" as a whole. The correspondence principle.
5. **Against: other roles.** Durkheim's social solidarity; Althusser's ideological control; cultural reproduction (Bourdieu); the hidden curriculum.
6. **Against: evidence.** Wolf (2002) on growth; Dore (1976) on qualification inflation; Willis (1977) on resistance.
7. **Conclusion.** Weigh the evidence: education is linked to the economy, but whose interests it serves is contested. Reach a judgement based on the evidence you used.

## Quick self-test

1. What did Durkheim mean by school as a "society in miniature"?
2. Name Parsons's two types of standards.
3. What year did Davis and Moore publish "Some Principles of Stratification"?
4. What is the difference between the repressive and ideological state apparatus?
5. What traits did Bowles and Gintis find linked to higher grades?
6. Which book by Chubb and Moe argued for school choice, and when?
7. Who is widely credited with coining "meritocracy"?
8. Define relative mobility.
9. What is qualification inflation?
10. Give the three forms of cultural capital.
11. Who coined the term "hidden curriculum"?
12. What is an ethnocentric curriculum?

### Answers

1. Pupils follow rules that apply to all, learning self-discipline and cooperation with people who are not family.
2. Particularistic (family) and universalistic (school and wider society).
3. 1945.
4. The repressive state apparatus controls by force (police, army, courts); ideological state apparatuses, such as education, control through ideas.
5. Perseverance, consistency and punctuality.
6. *Politics, Markets, and America's Schools* (1990).
7. Michael Young, in *The Rise of the Meritocracy* (1958).
8. The chances of people from different class backgrounds reaching a given position, compared with each other.
9. As more people gain a credential, employers ask for higher ones, so each one is worth less (Dore, 1976).
10. Embodied, objectified and institutionalised.
11. Philip Jackson, in *Life in Classrooms* (1968).
12. A curriculum that gives priority to the culture, history and language of one ethnic group.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a theory without applying it to the question or the quotation.
- In a quotation question, writing arguments that support the view instead of arguments against it.
- Giving one explanation in depth when the question asks for two.
- Writing "Marxists say schools are bad" instead of naming the mechanism: ideological control, the correspondence principle or cultural reproduction.
- Confusing the New Right and social democratic views, which both link education to the economy.
- Claiming rising absolute mobility proves a system is meritocratic.
- Treating the hidden curriculum as a Marxist idea only, when functionalists also use it.
- Using cultural capital to mean money or income.
- Essays that list studies with no judgement; AO3 is 36% of Paper 3.
