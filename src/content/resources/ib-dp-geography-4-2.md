This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 4.2 of Option D, Geophysical hazards, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): geophysical hazard risks. The [4.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-geophysical-hazard-risks-revision-notes/) already define hazard, exposure, vulnerability and risk, set out hazard profiles, compare monitoring of volcanoes and earthquakes, and summarise Pinatubo, Nevado del Ruiz and L'Aquila. This page adds the tools that let you **quantify and explain** risk: separating primary from secondary hazards, the magnitude-frequency relationship, expected annual loss, why the same earthquake shakes some ground far harder than other ground, and how people perceive and respond to risk. Practise with the [4.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-4-2-practice/); the [Geophysical hazards study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Describe the characteristics of volcanic, earthquake and mass movement hazards.
- Explain the physical and human factors that affect the risk a hazard poses.
- Explain how risk varies with magnitude, frequency and the perception of people exposed.

## Primary and secondary hazards

**Primary hazards** are caused directly by the event; **secondary hazards** are triggered by it. Secondary hazards often cause more deaths than the primary event.

| Event | Primary hazards | Secondary hazards |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake | Ground shaking, surface rupture | Landslides, liquefaction, tsunamis, fires from broken gas lines, dam failure |
| Volcanic eruption | Lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash (tephra) fall, volcanic gases | Lahars, landslides and flank collapse, tsunamis, crop failure and famine |
| Mass movement | The moving mass itself | Blocked rivers forming landslide-dammed lakes that later burst |

Each hazard has a different **footprint**, the area it can affect:

- Pyroclastic flows and lava flows are confined to the volcano's slopes and valleys.
- Lahars follow river valleys and can travel tens of kilometres.
- Ash fall is spread by the wind, so its footprint is elongated **downwind** and can cover large areas. A hazard map for ash should therefore be read with a map of prevailing winds.
- Earthquake shaking decreases with distance from the epicentre but varies sharply with ground conditions (see below).
- Tsunamis can cross whole oceans.

## Magnitude and frequency

Small events are common and large events rare. For earthquakes, a widely observed rule (the Gutenberg-Richter relationship) is that, in a given region and period, there are roughly **ten times as many** earthquakes for each **one unit lower** magnitude.

**Worked example.** A region records on average 2 earthquakes of magnitude 7 or more each decade.

```
Magnitude 6 or more: about 2 x 10 = 20 per decade
Magnitude 5 or more: about 2 x 100 = 200 per decade
```

Because each magnitude unit also means roughly 32 times more energy, the rare large events release most of the total energy. Planning for "typical" events therefore leaves people exposed to the rare ones that cause most damage.

## Expected annual loss

Risk can be expressed as the **probability** of an event multiplied by its **consequences**. Insurers and planners use **expected annual loss**:

**expected annual loss = annual probability x loss if the event happens**

**Worked example (invented city).**

| Scenario | Annual probability | Loss if it happens | Expected annual loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderate earthquake | 1 in 20 = 0.05 | 40 million | 0.05 x 40 = 2.0 million |
| Major earthquake | 1 in 200 = 0.005 | 1,200 million | 0.005 x 1,200 = 6.0 million |
| Total | | | 8.0 million |

The major earthquake is ten times less likely but contributes three times as much to expected loss. If retrofitting halves the loss from a major event, its expected loss falls to 3.0 million, saving 3.0 million a year on average. Compare that with the annual cost of the retrofit programme to judge whether it is worthwhile.

Limits: probabilities for rare events are uncertain; losses to life, culture and wellbeing are hard to express in money.

## Why ground conditions change the hazard

Two places the same distance from an earthquake can experience very different shaking.

- **Bedrock** transmits seismic waves with relatively little amplification.
- **Soft sediments**, such as old lake beds, river deposits and reclaimed land, slow the waves and amplify their amplitude, so shaking is stronger and lasts longer. Saturated sands may also **liquefy**.
- When the natural period of shaking of the sediments matches that of buildings, damage is magnified (**resonance**).

**Example: Mexico City, 1985.** The magnitude 8.0 earthquake had its epicentre off the Pacific coast, several hundred kilometres from the capital. Yet Mexico City suffered severe damage, because much of it is built on the soft sediments of a drained lake bed, which amplified the shaking. Medium-height buildings were especially affected. The case shows that **site conditions**, not only distance and magnitude, determine the hazard at a location, and it is why seismic hazard maps include ground type.

## Human factors that increase risk

- **Population growth and urbanisation** increase exposure: more people and buildings in the hazard footprint.
- **Land pressure** pushes informal settlement onto steep slopes, river banks and reclaimed land, the most hazardous sites.
- **Economic ties** keep people near hazards: fertile volcanic soils, geothermal energy, tourism and jobs.
- **Building practices**: heavy, unreinforced construction increases the danger of collapse.

## How people perceive risk

People respond to hazards according to how they perceive them, which may differ from the scientific estimate.

| Response | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Fatalism | Accepting losses as inevitable, sometimes for religious or cultural reasons; little preparation |
| Adaptation | Living with the hazard by changing behaviour or buildings, such as preparing evacuation plans or retrofitting |
| Risk reduction or "fight" | Trying to control the hazard or its effects through engineering or monitoring |
| Moving away | Leaving the hazard zone, which is often not an option for the poor |

Perception is shaped by **experience** (people who have lived through an event usually take risk more seriously), **time since the last event** (memory fades), **education**, **trust in authorities**, **wealth** (which gives options) and **culture**. A low perceived risk is not always ignorance: people may know the risk and judge that their livelihood outweighs it.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine why the risk posed by a volcano depends on more than how often it erupts.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Frequency matters, but risk combines the probability of eruption with the consequences, so a rarely erupting volcano near a large city can pose a greater risk than a frequently erupting one in a remote area **[risk defined as probability and consequence]**. Consequences depend on the type of hazard: lahars can travel tens of kilometres down valleys, and ash fall spreads downwind, so exposure extends well beyond the volcano's slopes **[hazard footprint]**. They also depend on vulnerability and perception: communities that have not experienced an eruption for generations may underestimate the danger or depend on fertile soils that keep them close **[human factors]**. Risk is therefore a product of the hazard, exposure, vulnerability and perception together **[synthesis]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating secondary hazards as less important than primary ones.
- Drawing an ash fall hazard zone as a circle, ignoring wind direction.
- Assuming shaking depends only on magnitude and distance.
- Calculating expected loss by adding probabilities and losses rather than multiplying.
- Describing low risk perception as ignorance without considering economic necessity.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** primary and secondary hazards with an example of each from the same event.
- **Explain** spatial variation in risk using hazard footprint, ground conditions and exposure.
- **Examine** risk by considering physical, human and perception factors and how they interact.
- When given probabilities and losses, multiply them and comment on what the calculation omits.
