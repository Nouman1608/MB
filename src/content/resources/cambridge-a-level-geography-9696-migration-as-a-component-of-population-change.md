This guide covers section 5.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: "Movements of populations (excluding all movements of less than one year's duration)", with their causes (push and pull factors), processes (including chain migration), patterns (by distance and by age) and "the role of constraints, obstacles and barriers (e.g. cost, national borders)". It is part of Topic 5, Migration, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [5.1 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-migration-population-change-revision-notes/) already list the key terms, Ravenstein's laws and Lee's model. This guide goes further: how migration feeds through into population size and structure, how to use the models on unfamiliar data, and how to write the answers Paper 2 rewards. Test yourself afterwards with the [5.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-migration-as-a-component-of-population-change-practice/).

## What 5.1 asks you to know

| Syllabus wording | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Movements of populations (excluding movements under one year) | A change of usual residence that lasts at least a year; commuting, tourism and most seasonal work are excluded |
| Causes: push and pull factors | Reasons to leave the origin and reasons to choose a destination, judged by the migrant |
| Processes, including chain migration | How a move actually happens: who goes first, who pays, who follows |
| Patterns by distance and by age | Who moves, and how far |
| Constraints, obstacles and barriers | What stops or filters movement: cost, borders, information, family ties |

Section 5.1 is the toolkit for the rest of Topic 5. Sections 5.2 and 5.3 apply it to internal and international moves, and 5.4 needs it for the case study.

## Migration in the population equation

Population change has two components: natural change (births minus deaths) and net migration (in-migrants minus out-migrants). At world scale net migration is zero, so it only matters at national, regional and local scale. The smaller the area, the larger migration's share of change usually is, because natural change is slow while a single factory opening or closing can move thousands of people.

Useful measures:

- **Gross migration** = in-migrants + out-migrants. It shows turnover, the total amount of movement.
- **Net migration** = in-migrants − out-migrants. It shows the overall gain or loss.
- **Net migration rate** = net migration ÷ mid-year population × 1000, expressed per 1000 per year.
- **Migration effectiveness** = net migration ÷ gross migration × 100. A value near 0 means flows in and out almost cancel; a value near 100 (or −100) means movement is mostly one-way.

Two areas can have the same net migration but very different gross flows. A university city may gain and lose tens of thousands of students each year with little net change; a remote rural district may have small flows that are almost all outward.

## How migration changes population structure

Migration is **selective**. Because movers are not a random sample of the population, migration reshapes age-sex pyramids in both places.

- **Source areas** lose young adults, often more of one sex. The pyramid narrows at ages 20-39, the dependency ratio rises, and fewer births follow because the people of child-bearing age have left. The population ages faster than natural change alone would cause.
- **Destination areas** gain young adults. The pyramid bulges at 20-39 and, a few years later, at 0-9 as migrants have children. The crude birth rate rises and the crude death rate falls, so migration indirectly raises natural increase.
- **Sex selectivity** depends on the type of work. Labour migration to construction and oil work in the Gulf states is dominated by men, so countries such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates have strongly male-skewed adult populations. Where domestic service and factory work recruit women, the imbalance is reversed in the destination.
- **Retirement migration** in some HICs adds older people to coastal and rural destinations, widening the top of the pyramid there.

This is why 5.1 is described as a "component of population change": migration changes not just how many people live in a place, but its future birth and death rates too.

## Causes: using push and pull factors properly

A list of push and pull factors earns little on its own. Stronger answers do three things:

1. **Link factors to a particular group.** Low farm incomes push young landless men differently from older landowners.
2. **Weigh push against pull.** Some moves are push-dominated (fleeing conflict), others pull-dominated (a graduate taking a job in a capital city).
3. **Show that factors are perceived.** People act on what they believe about a destination. Information from earlier migrants, social media and recruiters can be accurate or misleading.

Causes also operate at different scales. **Structural causes** are the large economic and political forces, such as wage gaps between regions, land shortage, or a war. **Individual causes** are life events, such as finishing school, marrying, being made redundant or retiring. A full explanation connects the two: a wage gap exists for everyone in a region, but it is the 20-year-old school-leaver who acts on it.

## Processes of migration

- **Chain migration.** Pioneers send back information and money, and later migrants follow through family and village networks. A well-known example is the movement from the Mirpur district of Pakistan-administered Kashmir to British industrial towns from the 1950s and 1960s, strengthened when the Mangla Dam reservoir displaced many villages. Networks explain why people from one district cluster in particular neighbourhoods of particular towns.
- **Stepped migration.** Moves up the settlement hierarchy in stages, village to town to city.
- **Counter-streams and return migration.** Every major stream produces a smaller flow in the opposite direction, including people returning home after retirement, failure or success.
- **Circulation.** Repeated short moves that are not counted as migration under the one-year rule, but which often come before a permanent move.

## Patterns by distance and by age

**Distance.** The number of migrants usually falls as distance increases (distance decay). Longer moves cost more, require more information and cross more barriers. The pattern is weaker for skilled workers, whose labour market is national or global, and for refugees, whose destination is set by safety rather than distance. Stouffer's idea of intervening opportunities explains why a nearer town can capture migrants who would otherwise have gone further.

**Age.** Migration rates peak in the late teens and twenties, fall through middle age, and show a smaller rise at retirement in some HICs. A further small rise among the very old reflects moves into care or to live with relatives.

## Constraints, obstacles and barriers

| Type | Examples | Who is most affected |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Cost of travel, agent fees, rent deposit at the destination | The poorest households |
| Political | Visas, quotas, border controls, internal registration systems | Low-skilled workers; nationals of some countries |
| Social and cultural | Language, family duties, discrimination | Women in some societies; older people |
| Physical | Mountains, seas, deserts, distance | Those without access to transport |
| Information | Not knowing about jobs or routes | First-generation migrants without networks |

Barriers are selective, so they shape who moves as well as how many. Because the cost of a long-distance move is high, international migrants often come from middle-income households rather than the very poorest. Networks lower many barriers at once, which is one reason chain migration is so persistent.

## Worked example: data interpretation

*A district has a mid-year population of 80 000. In one year it records 1 600 births, 700 deaths, 2 400 in-migrants and 3 500 out-migrants. Calculate (a) natural change, (b) net migration rate, (c) total change and (d) migration effectiveness.*

- (a) Natural change = 1 600 − 700 = **+900**.
- (b) Net migration = 2 400 − 3 500 = −1 100. Rate = −1 100 ÷ 80 000 × 1000 = **−13.75 per 1000**.
- (c) Total change = 900 + (−1 100) = **−200**. The district shrinks even though births exceed deaths.
- (d) Gross migration = 2 400 + 3 500 = 5 900. Effectiveness = −1 100 ÷ 5 900 × 100 = **−18.6%**. Most movement cancels out; the net loss is a small part of a large turnover.

An examiner would then expect you to *suggest* why: perhaps young adults leave for education and work while older people and returning migrants arrive.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why migration is often described as selective. [5]*

> Migrants are not a cross-section of the population (**definition of selective**). Young adults move most because they have fewer ties and a long working life in which to gain from the move (**age**). Some streams are dominated by one sex, such as men moving to construction jobs, depending on the work available (**sex**). Long-distance moves are made more often by educated and better-off people, who can pay the costs and find information (**barriers filter movers**). As a result, the source area ages while the destination gains young adults (**consequence for population structure**).

## Common mistakes

- Counting commuters, tourists or seasonal workers as migrants. The syllabus excludes moves of under a year.
- Writing that migration "increases the population" without saying where. Net migration is zero globally.
- Treating push and pull factors as a checklist rather than explaining how they combine for one group.
- Forgetting the indirect effect: migrants change future births and deaths, not just population size.
- Losing the sign in calculations. A net loss is negative; say so in words as well.
- Describing barriers as purely physical. Cost and borders are the syllabus's own examples.

## Exam technique

- **Define** and **state** questions need one precise sentence; the one-year rule is the commonest missing element.
- **Describe** questions on graphs or maps need figures from the resource, the overall pattern and an anomaly.
- **Explain** needs reasons linked to the pattern: "because…, so…".
- **Suggest** asks you to apply 5.1 ideas to an unfamiliar place; there is no single right answer, but each reason must fit the data given.
- **Assess** or **To what extent** needs a judgement, for example whether push or pull factors matter more for a named stream, supported by evidence.
