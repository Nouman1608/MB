## Questions

**1.** Define the term *working capital*. **[2]**

**2.** Norrisby Dairy starts June with £6,000 in the bank. It sells £15,000 of cheese on credit, and customers will pay in August. During June it pays £9,500 for milk, wages and rent.

**(a)** Calculate the profit for June. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the bank balance at the end of June. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a profitable business like Norrisby Dairy may still be at risk of failure. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Brantwick Signs has inventory of £9,000, trade receivables of £16,000 and cash of £2,500. Trade payables are £12,000 and the overdraft is £6,500. Credit sales are £146,000 a year, about £400 a day, and customers take 45 days to pay.

**(a)** Calculate working capital. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cash released if customers are persuaded to pay in 30 days. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** risk to Brantwick Signs of insisting on payment in 30 days. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by liquidation and by administration. **[4]**

**6.** Kellerby Foods sells its warehouse for £340,000 and rents it back for £2,500 a month on a ten-year agreement.

**(a)** Calculate the rent paid in one year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the rent paid over ten years. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse **one** benefit and **one** drawback of this decision for Kellerby Foods. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why a public limited company usually has more sources of finance than a sole trader. **[4]**

**8.** A small caterer needs £12,000 for 3 months to buy ingredients for a large contract. An overdraft would charge 14% a year on the amount used. A 12-month bank loan would charge 10% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the interest on the overdraft if £12,000 is used for 3 months. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the interest on the loan for 12 months. **[1]**
**(c)** Justify which source is more appropriate. **[2]**

**9.** Distinguish between leasing and hire purchase. **[4]**

**10.** Haverdon Cycles earns a profit of £21,000 a year before finance costs. It already pays £9,600 a year in interest on existing loans. The owner needs £60,000 for vans, which would raise annual profit before finance costs by £14,000. She is considering: a new bank loan at 9% a year (assume interest of 9% of £60,000 each year); leasing the vans at £1,350 a month for four years; or a new partner who invests £60,000 for 30% of profit after existing interest. Ignore tax.

**(a)** Calculate annual profit after all interest if the bank loan is used. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the owner's share of annual profit if the partner invests. **[2]**
**(c)** Recommend which option the owner should choose. **[5]**

**11.** Evaluate whether retained earnings are the best source of finance for an established business. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Working capital is the finance available for day-to-day operations [1], equal to current assets minus current liabilities [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving the formula as well as the idea secures both marks; "money in the bank" confuses it with cash.

**2. (a)** £15,000 − £9,500 = **£5,500** [1].
**(b)** £6,000 − £9,500 [1] = **−£3,500**, so the account is overdrawn by £3,500 [1].
**(c)** Customers do not pay until August, so cash is short for two months [1]. If Norrisby cannot pay suppliers or wages, or the bank refuses more overdraft, it can fail despite the profit [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the cash balance separate from the profit figure; quoting £5,500 as the bank balance is the usual loss.

**3.** Capital expenditure is spending on non-current assets used for more than a year [1], for example a delivery van [1]. Revenue expenditure is spending on the everyday running costs of the business [1], for example wages or repairs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each half needs its own example; two definitions without examples cap the answer.

**4. (a)** Current assets = £9,000 + £16,000 + £2,500 = £27,500; current liabilities = £12,000 + £6,500 = £18,500 [1]. Working capital = **£9,000** [1].
**(b)** 15 days fewer × £400 [1] = **£6,000** released [1].
**(c)** Customers may dislike the tighter terms and buy from a rival [1], so sales revenue could fall by more than the cash gained [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show current assets and liabilities separately before subtracting, so a slip in one line is still visible.

**5.** Liquidation is the closing of the business [1], with its assets sold to pay creditors as far as they allow [1]. Administration is when an outside administrator takes charge [1] to try to rescue the business or sell it as a going concern [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The contrast is that liquidation ends the business and administration tries to save it; describe the outcome of each.

**6. (a)** £2,500 × 12 = **£30,000** [1].
**(b)** £30,000 × 10 = **£300,000** [1].
**(c)** Benefit: Kellerby gains £340,000 of cash now without borrowing [1], which could pay debts or fund growth [1]. Drawback: it must pay £300,000 over ten years and no longer owns the warehouse [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the stem to make each point specific to Kellerby.

**7.** A public limited company can sell shares to the public [1], raising large sums that never have to be repaid [1]. A sole trader relies on the owner's savings, retained earnings and loans [1], and lenders may see a single owner as a greater risk [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both businesses. Saying a plc is "bigger" without naming the sources gains nothing.

**8. (a)** £12,000 × 14% × 3/12 [1] = **£420** [1].
**(b)** £12,000 × 10% = **£1,200** [1].
**(c)** The overdraft is more appropriate [1] because the need lasts only 3 months and interest is charged only on the time used, so it costs £780 less than the loan [1]. (A reasoned alternative on risk of recall may also be credited.)
*Examiner insight:* A justification must reach a choice and use the numbers; "overdrafts are flexible" alone is thin.

**9.** Leasing is renting an asset for regular payments [1], and the business never owns it [1]. Hire purchase is paying instalments for an asset [1], which the business owns after the final payment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Ownership at the end is the distinction examiners look for.

**10. (a)** Profit before finance costs = £21,000 + £14,000 = £35,000. Interest = £9,600 + 9% × £60,000 (£5,400) = £15,000 [1]. Profit = **£20,000** [1].
**(b)** £35,000 − £9,600 = £25,400 [1]. Partner takes 30% = £7,620, so the owner keeps **£17,780** [1].
**(c)** Leasing costs £1,350 × 12 = £16,200 a year, leaving profit of £9,200 [1]. The loan leaves £20,000 and the partner option £17,780 for the owner [1]. The loan gives the highest profit and keeps full control [1], but adds to existing debt and needs repayment of principal of about £12,000 a year if repaid over five years [1]. The partner option avoids debt but shares control and 30% of profit [1]. Recommend the loan if profit is stable enough to cover the interest; otherwise the partner. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare all three options with figures and end in a clear recommendation. Stating the assumption about interest shows awareness of its limits.

**11.** For: retained earnings carry no interest and need no repayment [1], and ownership and control are not shared [1]. They are available quickly without lender approval [1]. Against: they exist only if the business has made profit and kept it [1], they may not be enough for a large project such as a new factory [1], and keeping profit means lower dividends for owners [1]. Judgement: they are often the best first choice for a profitable business with a modest need, but a large or urgent need, or a low-profit year, requires external finance such as a loan [1]. The conclusion depends on the size of the need and the profit history [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs both sides and a reasoned conclusion that depends on the situation; "it is best" with only advantages stays at analysis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving profit as the bank balance, or the other way round.
- Defining working capital as cash.
- Mixing up liquidation (closure) and administration (rescue attempt).
- Failing to apply the figures from the stem in an explanation.
- Ignoring the length of time when calculating interest.
- Recommending a source without rejecting at least one alternative.
- Listing sources of finance with no link to cost, control or the use.
- Describing hire purchase as renting, or leasing as ownership.
