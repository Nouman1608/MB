These questions cover section 2.6, Production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [2.6 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production/) or the [2.6 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain the difference between production and productivity. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each of these costs for a pizza takeaway as fixed or variable: rent, flour, delivery boxes, insurance. **[2]**

**3.** A firm's total cost is £4,800 and its total revenue is £6,000 when it makes and sells 600 units.

**(a)** Calculate its average cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its average revenue. **[1]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why the government acts as a producer. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** types of economies of scale. **[2]**

**6.** Last year 12 workers produced 1,080 units. This year 15 workers produced 1,200 units. Calculate output per worker in each year and state whether productivity rose. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A candle maker has fixed costs of £2,000 a month and a variable cost of £3 per candle. It sells each candle for £8.

**(a)** For outputs of 300 and 500 candles, calculate total cost, average cost, total revenue and the profit or loss. **[4]**
**(b)** Use average revenue and average cost to check the profit at 500 candles. **[1]**

**8.** The price of wax rises, so the candle maker's variable cost rises to £3.50 per candle. It still sells 500 candles at £8.

**(a)** Calculate the new profit and the percentage change in profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how a rise in costs like this, across all candle makers, affects the market supply of candles. **[3]**

**9.** A firm increases output from 10,000 to 40,000 units by opening a larger factory. Total cost rises from £50,000 to £160,000.

**(a)** Calculate average cost at each output and the percentage change. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** economy of scale that could explain the change. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how a rise in productivity in a country's manufacturing firms could affect those firms and consumers. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why a firm might continue producing for a time even though it is making a loss. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of cost, revenue, profit and loss for producers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Production is the total amount of goods and services produced [1]; productivity is output per unit of input, such as output per worker or per hour [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Question 6 shows why the difference matters: output can rise while productivity falls.

**2.** Fixed: rent and insurance. Variable: flour and delivery boxes. Any two correct [1]; all four correct [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Ask whether the cost rises with each extra pizza. If not, it is fixed.

**3. (a)** 4,800 ÷ 600 = **£8.00** [1].
**(b)** 6,000 ÷ 600 = **£10.00** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Average revenue equals the price when all units sell at the same price.

**4.** The government provides services such as state schools or hospitals [1] so that everyone can use them, whatever their income, which a market might not ensure [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs the reason, not a second example.

**5.** Any two of: technical [1]; purchasing [1]; financial; managerial; marketing; risk-bearing. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Bulk buying" is accepted as purchasing.

**6.** Last year 1,080 ÷ 12 = 90; this year 1,200 ÷ 15 = 80 units per worker [1]. Productivity fell, even though production rose [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Extra workers raised output but each worker produced less.

**7. (a)** 300 candles: TC = 2,000 + 900 = £2,900; AC = £9.67; TR = £2,400; loss of £500. 500 candles: TC = 2,000 + 1,500 = £3,500; AC = £7.00; TR = £4,000; profit of £500. Both TC correct [1]; both AC correct [1]; both TR correct [1]; both profit or loss correct, with "loss" stated [1].
**(b)** (8 − 7) × 500 = **£500** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing −500 as "profit" loses the mark; say "loss of £500".

**8. (a)** TC = 2,000 + (3.50 × 500) = £3,750 [1]. Profit = 4,000 − 3,750 = **£250** [1]. Change = (250 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **−50%** [1].
**(b)** Higher costs reduce the profit made at every price [1], so producers are willing to supply fewer candles at each price [1] and the market supply curve shifts left [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A cost change shifts the curve; it is not a movement along it.

**9. (a)** 50,000 ÷ 10,000 = £5.00 and 160,000 ÷ 40,000 = £4.00 [1]; change = (4 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = **−20%** [1].
**(b)** For example, technical: the larger factory can use bigger, faster machines [1], so more units are produced for each pound spent and cost per unit falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Total cost rose, but average cost fell. Your explanation must be about cost per unit.

**10.** Higher productivity means more output per worker [1], so labour cost per unit falls [1]. Firms can then cut prices to win sales at home and abroad, or keep prices and earn more profit [1]. Higher profit can fund investment or higher wages [1]. Consumers may benefit from lower prices or better products [1], although if machines replace workers, some consumers lose income from jobs, which reduces the benefit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse both groups named. A chain from productivity to unit cost to price is the core.

**11.** The firm may expect the loss to be temporary, for example during a quiet season [1]. It may be able to cover the loss from savings or a loan for a while [1], and closing would mean losing customers and paying some fixed costs anyway, such as rent on a lease [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It hopes things improve" earns one mark only if it says why things might improve.

**12.** Costs and revenue determine profit or loss [1], which tells a producer whether the business is worth continuing [1]. Profit rewards the entrepreneur's risk [1] and funds investment in new machinery, which can lower future costs [1]. A loss signals the need to cut costs, raise revenue or leave the market [1]. Changes in cost affect supply: higher costs cut profit and shift supply left [1]. However, a firm may accept low profit or a short-term loss to win market share or survive a downturn, so profit is not the only aim [1]. Judgement: these measures are central to producers' decisions, but they must be read over time, not from one month, before deciding to expand or leave [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each measure to a decision the producer makes; that is what "importance" means here.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using average cost where total cost is needed.
- Writing a loss as a profit.
- Treating higher production as higher productivity.
- Explaining economies of scale without "per unit".
- Moving along the supply curve after a cost change.
