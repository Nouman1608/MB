**How to use this set.** This page practises the skills needed when your Section B set text is a
selection of short stories, as the 2026 syllabus allows ("From Stories of Ourselves Volume 2, the
following 10 stories"). Every passage below is original, written for practice. Section A builds
single skills; Section B uses the two longer passages for extended analysis.

**Passage 1: from "The Weighing Room"** *(original passage; the opening of a story)*

> Nobody in the market used Mr Okafor's scales any more, but he polished them every morning before
> he unlocked the shutter. The brass pans hung level, as they always had. "A scale must never lie,"
> he told the boy who swept the stalls, and the boy nodded, because he had heard it every day since
> spring. Across the aisle, the new shop had a machine with red numbers that changed faster than
> anyone could read them, and a queue that reached the door.

**Passage 2: from "Low Tide"** *(original passage; the ending of a story)*

> Mira walked out across the wet sand to the place where her brother's boat used to be moored. The
> rope was still tied to the post, frayed and green, leading nowhere. She had come to untie it. She
> had told her mother so, and her mother had nodded and said nothing, which was how her mother
> said most things now.
>
> The knot was harder than she expected. Salt had set it like stone. She worked at it until her
> fingers ached, and then she stopped, and sat down on the cold sand beside the post, and let the
> sea come back in around her ankles, and did not untie it at all.

## Section A

**1.** Read Passage 1. Identify **two** things the reader is made to wonder about in this opening,
and explain how the writer creates each. **[4]**

**2.** In Passage 1, explain the effect of the contrast between the brass scales and "a machine with
red numbers". **[3]**

**3.** "A scale must never lie." Give **two** possible meanings of this line beyond its literal sense,
as it might be developed in the rest of the story. **[2]**

**4.** In Passage 2, what is implied by "which was how her mother said most things now"? Explain how
the writer implies it. **[3]**

**5.** Rewrite this weak sentence as analysis of implication: "The rope is still tied to the post,
which shows the brother is gone." **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a story card should include a quotation from the turning point and one from the
ending, not only from the opening. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Read Passage 2.
**(a)** Explore how the writer uses the rope and the knot as symbols in this ending. **[6]**
**(b)** Explain how sentence structure in the final sentence shapes the reader's response. **[4]**

**8.** Write the introduction (three to four sentences) to an answer on the question: "How does the
writer make this ending of 'Low Tide' so moving?" **[4]**

**9.** Read Passage 1. Explore how the writer presents Mr Okafor in this opening. **[8]**

**10.** A question on a short-story text names one story and asks about it more widely, with no
passage printed. Write a four-point plan for the question: "Explore the ways in which the writer
presents loss in 'Low Tide'." Use only what Passage 2 tells you, and indicate where each point would
come from in the story's structure. **[4]**

**11.** Passage 1 is an opening and Passage 2 an ending. Explain **two** differences between how you
would introduce an answer on a printed opening and one on a printed ending. **[4]**

**12.** Explain how a reader's view of the opening line of "Low Tide" ("Mira walked out across the wet
sand…") might change once the story's last sentence is known. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, [2] each (one for the question raised, one for the method):
- Why no one uses his scales [1]: the opening sentence states the fact without explaining it, so the
  reader must read on [1].
- Why he still polishes them [1]: the daily ritual ("every morning") suggests loyalty or stubbornness
  the story has not yet explained [1].
- What will happen between him and the new shop [1]: the final sentence sets up a rivalry, with the
  "queue that reached the door" hinting at conflict to come [1].
Maximum 4.

**2.** The brass scales are old, steady and "level", suggesting honesty and patience [1]; the machine's
numbers change "faster than anyone could read them", suggesting speed, and perhaps that it cannot be
checked or trusted [1]. The contrast sets up a conflict between tradition and modernity, and invites
the reader to side with Mr Okafor even though the crowd does not [1].

**3.** Any two reasonable readings, [1] each: that honesty matters more than success; that Mr Okafor
himself refuses to pretend or compromise; that the new shop may be cheating its customers; that the
story will test whether he can stay truthful about his own failing business. Maximum 2.

**4.** Her mother has fallen silent through grief and now communicates mainly by gestures [1]. The
writer implies this through the word "now", which marks a change from how she used to be [1], and by
placing the comment casually at the end of a sentence, so the reader discovers the extent of her
grief almost by accident [1].

**5.** A point about implication [1], the quotation embedded [1], the effect explained [1]. For example:
"The rope 'still tied to the post' but 'leading nowhere' implies the brother's absence without
stating it; an object that once held his boat now holds nothing, so the reader feels the loss
through what is missing."

**6.** The turning point and the ending are where the meaning of a short story is usually decided [1];
quotations from them let you explain how a printed passage connects to the story's change and
outcome, which an opening quotation alone cannot do [1].

**7. (a)** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- The rope "leading nowhere" stands for the brother's absence: a connection to someone who is no
  longer there [2].
- "frayed and green" suggests time passing and neglect, as if grief has been left untouched [2].
- Untying the rope would mean letting go of him; "She had come to untie it" presents the visit as a
  deliberate attempt to accept the loss [2].
- The knot "set like stone" by salt suggests that grief has hardened and cannot be undone by effort
  alone [2].
- Leaving the knot tied at the end shows she chooses, or is unable, to let go, so the symbol keeps
  the brother present [2].
Maximum 6.
**(b)** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- The long sentence joined by repeated "and" slows the pace, mirroring her exhaustion and giving up [2].
- The sequence of actions (stopped, sat, let the sea come back) shows her becoming passive, letting
  the tide act instead of her [2].
- The decision is held back until the last words, "did not untie it at all", so the ending lands as a
  quiet surprise that overturns her stated purpose [2].
Maximum 4.

**8.** A direct answer to the question [1]; a focus on "moving" [1]; placing the passage as the
story's ending and saying what it resolves or leaves open [1]; a sense of the argument to follow [1].
For example: "This ending is moving because Mira fails, or refuses, to do what she came for. The
rope she meant to untie is the last link to her brother's boat, and by leaving it tied the story
ends not with acceptance but with a quiet holding on. I will show how the writer uses the rope and
knot as symbols, the mother's silence and the slow final sentence to make her grief felt."

**9.** Any four developed points, [2] each:
- Loyalty and routine: he polishes the scales "every morning" although "Nobody" uses them [2].
- Pride in honesty: "A scale must never lie" presents him as principled, almost proverbial [2].
- Repetition: the boy has heard the saying "every day since spring", which adds gentle humour and
  suggests Mr Okafor repeats it to reassure himself [2].
- Isolation: the queue at the new shop "reached the door", so he is left alone with his ideals [2].
- The scales "hung level, as they always had" link him to the past and steadiness, but also to a
  refusal to change [2].
- The narrator presents him through actions and one line of speech, inviting sympathy without
  stating it [2].
Maximum 8.

**10.** One mark for each of four relevant, sequenced points tied to structure [4], for example:
1. Before the ending: the rope "leading nowhere" shows how the brother's absence marks familiar
   places.
2. The family: the mother's silence shows loss changing how people speak to each other.
3. The turning point: the knot "set like stone" shows that grief resists her effort to move on.
4. The ending: she sits as the sea returns and "did not untie it at all", so the story presents loss
   as something carried rather than resolved.

**11.** Any two developed differences, [2] each:
- For an opening, explain what the reader does not yet know and what the passage sets up; for an
  ending, explain what has happened before and what the passage resolves or leaves open [2].
- For an opening, point forward to how details will develop later; for an ending, point back to how
  earlier details gain their full meaning [2].
- For an ending, you can comment on the story's overall shape (circular, open, reversed); for an
  opening, you comment on how expectations are created [2].
Maximum 4.

**12.** At first the line reads as a purposeful walk to complete a task [1]. Once the reader knows she
does not untie the rope, the walk becomes a journey towards a moment of grief she cannot complete
[1], so the opening's calm tone looks, in hindsight, like a brave front that the ending breaks down
[1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining what happens rather than what is implied.
- Treating a symbol as having one fixed meaning instead of tracing how it develops.
- Losing track of which story a detail belongs to.
- Ignoring the final sentence of a printed ending.
