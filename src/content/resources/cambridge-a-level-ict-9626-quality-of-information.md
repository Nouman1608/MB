This guide teaches **section 1.2 Quality of information** of Topic 1 Data processing and information in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, examined in Paper 1 Theory. The [Data processing and information overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-data-processing-and-information/) lists the factors briefly; this page explains each one, shows how poor quality arises, and practises judging a source in a scenario. Use it with the [quality of information practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-quality-of-information-practice/).

## What section 1.2 covers

The syllabus asks for the **factors that affect the quality of information**, for example:

| Factor | Short meaning |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | The information is correct and free from errors |
| Relevance | The information is suitable for the purpose and the person using it |
| Age | The information is up to date for the decision being made |
| Level of detail | The information has the right amount of detail, neither too little nor too much |
| Completeness | Nothing needed for the decision is missing |

The word "for example" means other factors, such as the reliability or bias of the source, can also be credited when they are explained properly. Quality matters because decisions are only as good as the information behind them. Bad information leads to wrong decisions, wasted money and, in some cases, harm to people.

## Accuracy

**Accurate** information is correct. Every figure, name and date matches the real situation.

**Causes of inaccuracy:**
- Typing errors when data is entered, such as 5,100 typed as 51,000.
- Faulty sensors or equipment giving wrong readings.
- Errors in calculation or in a formula.
- Out-of-date data being treated as current.
- Deliberate falsification or bias, for example a source that wants to make a product look better.

**Effects:** a customer is billed the wrong amount, a medicine is prescribed at the wrong dose, or a business orders too much stock.

**Improving accuracy:** validation and verification when data is entered (section 1.4), checking against a second source, calibrating sensors, and using trusted sources.

## Relevance

**Relevant** information is useful for the specific purpose and the person who will read it. Accurate information can still be of poor quality if it is not relevant.

**Causes of irrelevance:**
- Information collected for a different purpose.
- Too much unrelated material, so the important points are hidden.
- Information about the wrong place, group or period.

**Effects:** the reader wastes time searching, may base a decision on the wrong evidence, or may miss the point that matters.

**Improving relevance:** state the purpose before collecting data, ask the right questions, filter and summarise before presenting, and tailor reports to their audience.

## Age

**Age** is about how up to date the information is. Information that was correct when it was gathered can become unreliable if circumstances change.

**Examples:**
- A bus timetable from last year may not show the current routes.
- A stock list that is updated once a week can show an item as available when it sold out yesterday.
- A map that predates a new bypass sends drivers the wrong way.

**Effects:** orders are placed for goods that no longer exist, customers are told something that is no longer true, and trends are missed.

**Improving age:** update data regularly, record the date of collection, use real-time systems where decisions are urgent, and discard or archive old records.

Age does not always mean "newer is better". Information for a historical study can be old and still of high quality, because the purpose is a past period. Always judge age **against the purpose**.

## Level of detail

The **level of detail** is how much specific information is provided. Quality is highest when the detail matches what the reader needs.

- **Too little detail:** "Sales fell last month" does not say which products, which shops or by how much, so no action can be taken.
- **Too much detail:** a 400-page printout listing every single sale hides the trend that the manager wanted to see.

**Improving level of detail:** summarise for managers and give detailed breakdowns to the staff who need them, use tables, charts and totals, and allow users to drill down to the detail when they ask for it.

## Completeness

**Complete** information includes everything necessary for the decision. Missing items can be as damaging as wrong ones.

**Examples:**
- A job advert that does not give the salary, location or closing date.
- A patient record with no list of allergies.
- A survey report that gives only the answers of people who responded and does not say that 70% did not reply.

**Effects:** the user guesses the missing parts, makes the wrong decision, or has to spend time finding the rest.

**Improving completeness:** use forms with required fields (a presence check), check lists before publishing, and compare against a specification of what must be included.

## Other factors that can be credited

Although the five above are the syllabus examples, an answer may also explain:

- **Reliability and source:** is the source trusted and independent?
- **Bias:** is the information presented to favour one view?
- **Presentation:** is it clear, in a suitable format and easy to understand?
- **Cost and timeliness of delivery:** does the reader get it when they need it?

If you use one of these, define it and link it to the scenario.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: judging a report

Harbourside Cycles is deciding which of its four shops to expand. The manager receives this summary.

> Sales in the third quarter were good in all shops. The Eastgate shop sold 412 bikes. A survey of customers shows 85% are happy. The survey was carried out in 2019.

Judge the quality using the five factors.

1. **Accuracy:** only one figure (412) is given, and nothing shows it is correct or how it was counted. The "85% are happy" figure cannot be checked.
2. **Relevance:** the customer survey measures happiness, not whether a shop could sell more with a larger floor space. It does not help the expansion decision.
3. **Age:** the survey is from 2019, so it is likely to be out of date for a decision made now.
4. **Level of detail:** "good in all shops" is too vague. The report needs sales per shop, per month and by type of bike.
5. **Completeness:** figures for the other three shops are missing, so the shops cannot be compared.

**Conclusion:** the report is poor quality for this decision. The manager should ask for current sales for all four shops, with the number of customers turned away and the cost of the premises.

### Worked example 2: improving a poor source

A school office keeps a list of parents' phone numbers. Some numbers are missing, some are three years old and one has a digit missing.

| Problem | Factor affected | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Digit missing | Accuracy | Use a length check and read-back verification when the number is entered |
| Numbers from three years ago | Age | Ask parents to confirm details each September |
| Some entries empty | Completeness | Make the phone number a required field on the form |
| The list also holds each pupil's dinner choice | Relevance | Keep it on a separate list used only by the canteen |

Each improvement is directly linked to a factor. This is the pattern for a six-mark question.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "correct" for accuracy and "right" for relevance. Use the word the examiner expects and add what it means in the scenario.
- Saying that old information is always bad. Judge age against the purpose.
- Thinking more detail is always better. Too much detail can hide the message.
- Mixing up **accuracy** (is it right?) and **completeness** (is it all there?).
- Giving a factor without an effect. State what goes wrong if the factor is poor.
- Naming a factor but not linking it to the scenario given in the question.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **state**: give the factor name with a short definition.
- **Describe** or **explain** the effect: use "because" and "so", for example "Because the price list is out of date, customers may be charged the wrong price, so the shop may lose money or trust."
- **Suggest** how to improve: pair each improvement with the factor it fixes.
- **Evaluate** a source: look at all five factors, say which matter most for the purpose, and reach a conclusion.
