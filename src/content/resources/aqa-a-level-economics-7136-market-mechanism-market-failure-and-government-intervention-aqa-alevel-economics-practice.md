## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Explain the signalling function of prices, using an example. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a public good and a private good. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the free-rider problem. **[2]**

**4.** Define government failure and state **one** possible cause. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the absence of property rights can lead to an externality. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of providing a merit good free of charge through the state.
**[4]**

### Section B

**7.** In a market for fertiliser, marginal private benefit is P = 120 − 2Q and marginal private cost is
P = 30 + Q. Production causes an external cost of 15 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the free-market output and price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the socially optimal output and price. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss at the free-market output. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the tax revenue raised by a tax of 15 per unit once the market reaches the optimum. **[1]**

**8.** In a market for a crop, Qd = 400 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 100. The government sets a minimum price of 12.
Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity before the intervention and the size of the surplus it creates.
**[3]**

**9.** Explain how asymmetric information can cause market failure in the market for second-hand cars. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a system of tradable pollution permits works, and why it may reduce pollution at a lower total
cost than requiring every firm to cut emissions by the same amount. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** arguments for and **one** argument against privatising a state-owned railway company. **[6]**

**12.** Country S plans an indirect tax on sugary drinks to reduce obesity. Evaluate whether an indirect tax is the
best way to reduce consumption of sugary drinks. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A rising price **signals** to producers that consumers want more and to buyers that the good is scarcer [1].
For example, a rise in the price of electric bikes signals growing demand, and firms move resources into making
them [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A public good is **non-rival and non-excludable**, such as street lighting [1]. A private good is **rival and
excludable**: one person's consumption reduces what is left, and non-payers can be kept out, such as a sandwich
[1]. **[2]**

**3.** Because a public good is non-excludable, people can benefit **without paying** [1]. Each person has an
incentive to wait for others to pay, so a private firm cannot raise enough revenue and the good is not provided
[1]. **[2]**

**4.** Government failure is when **intervention leads to a misallocation of resources** [1]. A cause: any one of
inadequate information, conflicting objectives, administrative costs or unintended consequences [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Without property rights, nobody **owns** a resource such as a river or the air [1], so the person harmed
cannot charge the polluter or demand compensation, and the cost is ignored in market decisions [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Advantage: consumption rises towards the **socially optimal level**, because price no longer deters people
who undervalue the benefits [1]; poorer households gain access, improving equity [1]. Disadvantage: there is an
**opportunity cost**, since the taxes used could fund other services [1]; with no price, demand may exceed
capacity, leading to queues or waiting lists [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** 120 − 2Q = 30 + Q, so 90 = 3Q, **Q = 30** [1]; P = 30 + 30 = **60** [1].

**(b)** MSC = 45 + Q. 120 − 2Q = 45 + Q, so 75 = 3Q, **Q = 25** [1]; P = 45 + 25 = **70** [1].

**(c)** At Q = 30, MSC = 75 and MSB = 60, a gap of 15 [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × (30 − 25) × 15 = **37.5** [1].

**(d)** 15 × 25 = **375** [1].

**8.** 400 − 20P = 30P − 100, so 500 = 50P, **P = 10** and **Q = 200** [1]. At P = 12: Qd = 400 − 240 = 160 and
Qs = 360 − 100 = 260 [1]. **Surplus = 100** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Sellers know more about a car's condition than buyers: **asymmetric information** [1]. Buyers cannot tell good
cars from bad, so they will pay only an average price [1]. Owners of good cars are unwilling to sell at that price
and withdraw from the market, so the average quality offered falls [1]. Fewer good cars are traded than buyers and
sellers would want, a misallocation of resources [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The government sets a **cap** on total emissions and issues permits equal to that cap [1]. A firm must hold
a permit for each unit it emits and can **buy or sell** permits [1]. Firms that can cut emissions cheaply do so
and sell spare permits [1]; firms facing high abatement costs buy permits instead [1]. The required cut is made
where it is **cheapest**, so the total cost of meeting the cap is lower than under a uniform rule that forces
high-cost firms to cut as much as low-cost ones [1]. The permit price also gives a continuing incentive to invest
in cleaner technology [1]. **[6]**

**11.** *For:* any two of the following, 2 marks each (1 for the argument, 1 for its development) [2] [2]: the
**profit motive** encourages cost-cutting and better service; shareholders and access to private finance may
fund investment without public borrowing; sale proceeds give the government revenue for other uses.
*Against:* a railway route is often a **natural monopoly**, so a private owner may raise fares or cut
unprofitable but socially valuable services unless regulated [2]. **[6]**

**12.** *For a tax:* it raises the price and, along the demand curve, reduces quantity demanded [1]; it
**internalises** some of the external costs, such as health-care costs [1]; revenue can fund health education
[1]. *Against:* demand may be **price inelastic** because of habit, so consumption falls little [1]; the tax is
**regressive**, taking a larger share of income from poorer households [1]; consumers may switch to other sugary
foods that are not taxed [1]. *Alternatives:* information campaigns tackle the imperfect information behind
over-consumption; regulation of sugar content works directly on producers [1]. *Judgement:* a tax is useful if
demand is reasonably elastic and the revenue is used well, but it works best combined with information and
regulation rather than alone [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a good "public" because the state provides it, rather than by its characteristics.
- Calculating the welfare loss with the wrong height: use the gap between MSC and MSB at market output.
- Forgetting that a minimum price causes a surplus and a maximum price a shortage, and not the reverse.
- Explaining asymmetric information without saying why fewer trades occur.
- Arguing for or against privatisation without mentioning natural monopoly or regulation.
- Evaluating a tax without considering elasticity, equity or the alternatives.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the welfare loss of an externality calculated?**
For straight-line curves it is a triangle: half of the gap between market and optimal output, multiplied by the
gap between marginal social cost and marginal social benefit at the market output.

**Does government failure mean governments should never intervene?**
No. It means intervention must be judged on whether its likely gains exceed its costs and risks in the specific
market.
