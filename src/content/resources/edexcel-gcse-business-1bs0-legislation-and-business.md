This guide covers content item 1.5.3, Legislation and business, in Topic 1.5 (Understanding external influences on business) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It belongs to Theme 1, examined in Paper 1. The broader [guide to legislation, the economy and external influences](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-legislation-the-economy-and-external-influences/) gives an overview of each area of law. This page goes into how each principle shapes everyday decisions in a small business, and how to weigh the cost of compliance against its benefits. The specification names no particular Acts, so the law is described here in general terms; you are not expected to quote statutes. All businesses and figures are invented, including any wage rates.

## What 1.5.3 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| The purpose of legislation | Principles of consumer law: quality and consumer rights. Principles of employment law: recruitment, pay, discrimination and health and safety |
| The impact of legislation on businesses | Cost; consequences of meeting and not meeting these obligations |

Legislation means laws passed by government. For a business, the key word in the specification is **obligations**: things the business must do, whether or not it wants to.

## Why governments make laws for business

The purpose of legislation is to protect people who are in a weaker position than the business and to keep competition fair.

- **Customers** usually know less about a product than the seller does. Consumer law stops sellers taking advantage of that.
- **Employees** usually depend on the employer for their income. Employment law stops employers treating them unfairly or unsafely.
- **Fair competition:** a business that cuts corners on safety or pay could undercut honest rivals. Laws set a minimum standard every business must meet.

## Consumer law in practice

The specification names two principles.

**Quality.** Goods should be of a reasonable standard, fit for the purpose they are sold for, and as described. Services should be carried out with reasonable care and skill. For a business this affects:

- **Product decisions:** choosing suppliers and materials that will meet the standard.
- **Promotion:** descriptions and adverts must be accurate; a claim that a jacket is "waterproof" must be true.
- **Operations:** checking goods before they are sold.

**Consumer rights.** When goods or services fall short, customers are entitled to a remedy, such as a repair, a replacement or a refund. For a business this means:

- having a returns and complaints process;
- budgeting for refunds and replacements;
- training staff to handle complaints correctly.

### Worked example: Northlight Lamps

Northlight Lamps sells 2,000 lamps a year at £45. Currently 4% are returned faulty and refunded in full.

1. Returns = 4% × 2,000 = 80 lamps
2. Refunds = 80 × £45 = £3,600 a year

A better supplier would charge £1.20 more per lamp but cut faults to 1%.

3. Extra cost = 2,000 × £1.20 = £2,400
4. New refunds = 1% × 2,000 × £45 = 20 × £45 = £900
5. Refunds saved = £3,600 − £900 = £2,700, so the change saves £2,700 − £2,400 = £300 a year

The money saved is small, but 60 fewer unhappy customers means fewer bad reviews and more repeat buying. Meeting the quality obligation more fully pays off in reputation as well as in pounds.

## Employment law in practice

The specification lists four principles. For each, ask what the business must do and what it costs.

| Principle | What the business must do (in general terms) | Cost or effect for the business |
|---|---|---|
| Recruitment | Advertise and select fairly, judging applicants on their ability to do the job; check that people have the right to work | Time on fair selection and record keeping |
| Pay | Pay at least the legal minimum for each worker's age and situation, accurately and on time | Wage bill rises when legal minimums rise |
| Discrimination | Not treat staff or applicants less favourably because of characteristics such as sex, race, age, disability or religion | Training managers; reviewing pay and promotion decisions |
| Health and safety | Assess risks, provide safe equipment, training and protective clothing where needed | Equipment, training and staff time |

### Worked example: a rise in a legal minimum wage

Corner Crust Pizza employs 8 staff on the legal minimum hourly rate. Each works 25 hours a week for 50 weeks a year. Suppose (invented figures) the minimum rises from £11.00 to £11.60 an hour.

1. Hours a year = 8 × 25 × 50 = 10,000 hours
2. Wage bill before = 10,000 × £11.00 = £110,000
3. Wage bill after = 10,000 × £11.60 = £116,000
4. Increase = £6,000, which is £6,000 ÷ £110,000 × 100 = 5.5% (to one decimal place)

Corner Crust must pay this; it cannot choose not to. Its options are to accept lower profit, raise prices, cut staff hours or find savings elsewhere. Each option affects a stakeholder group, which is a useful link to 1.5.1.

## The impact of legislation: cost

Complying costs money in two ways:

- **One-off costs:** new safety equipment, changes to premises, setting up new procedures.
- **Ongoing costs:** higher wages, training, inspections, record keeping and the time managers spend on rules.

Small businesses feel these costs more heavily, because the same task (a risk assessment, for example) takes as much time for a firm of 5 people as for a firm of 50, but the small firm has less revenue to spread it over.

## Consequences of meeting and not meeting obligations

| Meeting obligations | Not meeting obligations |
|---|---|
| Fewer accidents, so fewer days lost | Fines or other legal penalties |
| Good reputation with customers and staff | Compensation paid to customers or employees |
| Easier to recruit and keep staff | Bad publicity and lost sales |
| Fewer complaints and returns | Staff leave; recruitment becomes harder |
| Customers trust the business | In serious cases, the business may be ordered to stop an activity or close |

Meeting obligations costs money now; failing to meet them risks much larger costs later. An evaluative answer weighs the size and certainty of each.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain style):** Explain **one** consequence for a business of not meeting health and safety obligations. [3]

> A worker could be injured because equipment is unsafe *(statement)*. The business may then have to pay compensation to the worker and may be fined *(expansion 1)*, which raises its costs and could damage its reputation, making it harder to recruit staff *(expansion 2)*.

## Common mistakes

- Inventing the names or dates of Acts. The specification does not require them; general principles are enough.
- Saying legislation is "bad for business". It brings costs, but also benefits such as trust and fair competition.
- Writing only about fines. Reputation, staff and customers are consequences too.
- Mixing up consumer law (protects customers) with employment law (protects workers).

## Exam technique

- **Give / State:** name a principle (for example, discrimination) or a consequence.
- **Explain:** a statement of fact and two expansions.
- **Analyse:** use the business in the source: its staff numbers, its products, its costs.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the cost of meeting a new obligation against the consequences of not meeting it, and against the benefits.
