**Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881-1924 is the third AS topic in the European option of Cambridge International History 9489.** It starts with the reaction under Alexander III after his father's assassination in 1881 and ends with Lenin's death in 1924. The four key questions ask what challenged the regime in 1881-94, what caused the 1905 Revolution and what changed up to 1914, how the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, and how they consolidated it.

## Where this fits in 9489

This topic is assessed on **Paper 2 (Outline Study)** in 2027 and 2028 and on **Paper 1 (Historical Sources)** in 2029, under the 2027-2029 rotation. Paper 1 sets a 15-mark comparison of two sources and a 25-mark question using all four sources; Paper 2 sets two-part questions of 10 marks (explain why) and 20 marks (a comparative judgement). ## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL HISTORY (9489), 2027-2029 -- TOPIC 3: RUSSIA FROM AUTOCRACY TO REVOLUTION, 1881-1924**

- **3.1 What challenges faced the Tsarist regime in 1881-94?** Autocracy (the Tsar, State Council, ministries, army and Church); Alexander III's reform and repression, Russification; the economy (Bunge, industrialisation, taxation and famine); social change and the middle class; peasant and worker opposition and the growth of Marxism.
- **3.2 What were the causes and outcomes of the 1905 Revolution up to 1914?** Discontent with Nicholas II; the Socialist Revolutionaries and Social Democrats; harvests, unemployment, Witte's policies, taxation; the Russo-Japanese War; Bloody Sunday, the October Manifesto and the Duma; reasons for survival (opposition, the Fundamental Laws, repression); change to 1914 (Stolypin's agrarian reforms, industry).
- **3.3 How and why did the Bolsheviks seize power in October 1917?** The war's impact on the Tsar; the February Revolution and the home front; the abdication; the Provisional Government's aims and failure (the war, land, the July Days, the Kornilov Revolt); Bolshevik promises, Lenin, the Petrograd Soviet, Trotsky and the Military Revolutionary Committee; the October Revolution.
- **3.4 How did the Bolsheviks consolidate power up to 1924?** Sovnarkom and the first decrees; Brest-Litovsk; the Cheka and the closure of the Constituent Assembly; the Civil War (Bolshevik strengths, White weaknesses, War Communism); Kronstadt and the New Economic Policy.

This topic follows the syllabus in using **February** and **October** for the 1917 revolutions (the Russian calendar of the time), which fell in March and November by the Western calendar.

## 3.1 What challenges faced the regime in 1881-94?

**How Russia was ruled.** The Tsar held unlimited power, advised by an appointed State Council and ministers responsible only to him; the army and the Orthodox Church were pillars of the system. After revolutionaries of the People's Will killed **Alexander II** in March 1881, **Alexander III** abandoned further reform. Guided by **Pobedonostsev**, Procurator of the Holy Synod, he passed emergency security laws (1881), strengthened the police, introduced **land captains** (1889) to supervise peasants and reduced the powers of the elected zemstvos (1890).

**Russification.** Non-Russian peoples -- Poles, Baltic Germans, Finns, Ukrainians -- faced compulsory Russian in schools and government. Jews suffered pogroms in 1881-82 and the restrictive May Laws (1882). Russification bought loyalty from some Russians but created lasting national grievances.

**Economy.** Finance minister **Bunge** (1881-86) began abolishing the poll tax, set up a Peasant Land Bank and introduced the first factory laws limiting child labour. His successor pushed grain exports to balance the budget, and the **famine of 1891-92** exposed the cost: hundreds of thousands died and the government's slow response discredited it. Industrialisation began to accelerate, and with **Witte** at the finance ministry from 1892, railway building -- including the Trans-Siberian line, begun in 1891 -- drove growth.

**Opposition and social change.** Towns grew, and with them a professional middle class and a factory working class living in poor conditions. Peasants remained burdened by redemption payments. Populism was driven underground, but **Marxism** spread: Plekhanov's Emancipation of Labour group (1883) argued that the new proletariat, not the peasantry, would make revolution.

## 3.2 What caused the 1905 Revolution, and what changed to 1914?

**Causes.** Nicholas II (from 1894) dismissed hopes of political reform. New parties organised discontent: the **Socialist Revolutionaries** (founded 1901-02), who appealed to peasants and used terrorism, and the **Social Democrats** (1898), who split in 1903 into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks. **Witte's policies** -- foreign loans, protective tariffs, high indirect taxes, the gold standard (1897) -- built industry but squeezed peasants, and the slump after 1900 brought unemployment. Bad harvests added to rural unrest. Humiliating defeat in the **Russo-Japanese War (1904-05)** -- the loss of Port Arthur and the destruction of the fleet at Tsushima -- discredited the government.

**Events.** **Bloody Sunday (January 1905)** led to strikes, peasant risings, national revolts and mutinies through the year, culminating in a general strike in October. Nicholas issued the **October Manifesto**, promising civil rights and a legislative **Duma**.

**Why did the regime survive?** Opposition was divided: liberals accepted the Manifesto, while socialists did not. The army stayed loyal once the war ended and was used to crush the Moscow rising in December 1905. The **Fundamental Laws (1906)** reaffirmed the Tsar's "supreme autocratic power"; the first two Dumas were dissolved, and in 1907 Stolypin changed the electoral law to produce more conservative Dumas. Repression included field courts-martial and executions.

**Change to 1914.** **Stolypin's agrarian reforms** (from 1906) let peasants leave the commune and consolidate land, aiming to create a loyal class of prosperous farmers; uptake was real but partial, and the "wager on the strong" was far from complete when he was assassinated in 1911. Industry grew strongly from about 1908, but working conditions stayed harsh; the shooting of strikers at the Lena goldfields (1912) began a new wave of strikes up to 1914.

## 3.3 How and why did the Bolsheviks seize power in October 1917?

Defeats in 1914-15, shell shortages and millions of casualties weakened the Tsar, especially after he took personal command in 1915 and left government to the Tsarina and Rasputin. Ministers changed rapidly, and the Duma's Progressive Bloc was ignored. On the home front, **inflation**, **food shortages** in the cities and **land seizures** spread disorder.

In **February 1917**, strikes and bread riots in Petrograd became revolution when the garrison mutinied. Nicholas abdicated, and the **Provisional Government**, formed from Duma leaders, promised civil liberties and elections to a Constituent Assembly, but intended to continue the war. It shared power with the **Petrograd Soviet** ("dual power").

It failed because it **did not end the war** (the June offensive collapsed), **delayed land reform** until a Constituent Assembly could meet, and lost authority in the **July Days** and the **Kornilov Revolt** (August), when Kerensky had to arm the Bolsheviks' Red Guards. The Bolsheviks offered simple **promises** -- "Peace, Bread, Land", "All power to the Soviets" -- while **Lenin** insisted on armed insurrection, and **Trotsky**, chairman of the Petrograd Soviet, directed its **Military Revolutionary Committee**. On 24-25 October the MRC seized the bridges, stations and telegraph and then the Winter Palace, with little resistance, and the Second Congress of Soviets was presented with power.

## 3.4 How did the Bolsheviks consolidate power up to 1924?

**Policies.** A Bolshevik government, **Sovnarkom**, chaired by Lenin, issued decrees on peace, on land (approving peasant seizures), on workers' control and on the rights of the peoples of Russia. The **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918)** ended the war at the cost of huge territories, including Ukraine and the Baltic lands.

**Dictatorship.** The **Cheka** (December 1917) under Dzerzhinsky arrested opponents. The **Constituent Assembly**, in which the Socialist Revolutionaries won the most seats, was closed after one day in January 1918.

**Civil War.** The Reds won through leadership (Lenin's ruthlessness, Trotsky's Red Army using former Tsarist officers under political commissars), enough peasant support from fear of the Whites returning land to landlords, control of the central heartland, railways and arms factories, and unity of command. **War Communism** -- grain requisitioning, nationalisation and labour discipline -- supplied the army but caused famine. The Whites suffered divided leadership and aims, relied on conscription that produced desertion, had stretched supply lines on the periphery, and were tainted by foreign intervention.

**Kronstadt and the NEP.** In March 1921 the sailors of Kronstadt, once Bolshevik heroes, revolted, demanding free soviets; the Red Army crushed them. The same month, at the Tenth Party Congress, Lenin introduced the **New Economic Policy**, replacing requisitioning with a tax in kind and allowing private trade, while banning factions inside the party. Recovery followed, but political control tightened. Lenin's death in January 1924 ends the topic.

## Worked example: a Paper 1 part (a) approach

*Compare two sources as evidence about the October Manifesto.* **[15]**

Identify points of agreement and disagreement in content, then explain them using provenance and context: a liberal newspaper might welcome the promise of a Duma, while a Social Democrat leaflet dismisses it as a trick to split the opposition. Explaining *why* they differ -- each writer's political aim in late 1905 -- is what lifts the answer.

## Common mistakes

- Using 0470-style narrative of 1917 without the 1881-1905 background, which this topic examines in detail.
- Treating the Fundamental Laws as a minor detail: they explain how autocracy survived 1905.
- Saying the Bolsheviks won the Civil War only through terror.
- Forgetting the political tightening that came with the NEP.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Stalin in this topic?**
No. The topic ends in 1924. Stalin's Russia is a Paper 4 depth study (European option topic 2).

**How should I explain 1905's survival?**
Use the syllabus headings: the nature and extent of opposition, the Fundamental Laws and Dumas, and repression -- then judge which mattered most.
