Condensed recall for **10.1 Global trends in consumption** (Unit 3: Global resource consumption and
security). For the full explanation and worked examples, read the
[Global resource consumption study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-global-resource-consumption-study-guide/).

## Resource distinctions

- **Renewable** (replenish naturally within a human timescale) versus **non-renewable** (finite).
- **Stock** versus **flow** resources.
- **Natural capital** versus **human-made** capital.
- The three focus resources are **food, water and energy**, with attention to land and minerals as
  inputs.
- Consumption is shaped by population, income, technology and culture.

A **stock** resource exists as a fixed quantity that is used up (coal, oil, groundwater in a fossil
aquifer); a **flow** resource is continuously renewed (sunlight, wind, river flow). Some renewables
behave like stocks if used faster than they regenerate: an over-pumped aquifer or an overfished
stock can collapse.

## The new global middle class

Rising incomes, especially in China, India and other parts of Asia, have created a large **new
global middle class**. Researchers at the Brookings Institution estimated that around 2018, for the
first time, just over half of the world's population lived in middle-class or richer households.
Middle-class consumers buy more meat, dairy, processed food, cars, appliances and air travel, so
the growth of this group is a major driver of resource demand. Extreme poverty has fallen sharply
since the 1990s, most of all in East Asia, though progress has been slower in sub-Saharan Africa.

## Trends

- Total consumption has risen with population growth and rising incomes, particularly in emerging
  economies.
- **Per-capita** consumption is very unequal: higher-income countries generally use far more energy,
  water and food (including meat) per person, though the gap has narrowed in places.
- Diets shift toward animal products as incomes rise; these need more land and water per unit of
  food energy.
- Energy demand is still dominated by **fossil fuels**, though renewables have grown quickly in many
  countries.
- Water use is dominated by **agriculture**, then industry and domestic use.

Detail for each focus resource:

| Resource | Pattern to describe |
|---|---|
| Energy | Fossil fuels supply roughly 80 per cent of world primary energy. Energy mixes differ: France gets most of its electricity from nuclear power, Norway from hydroelectricity and Iceland from hydro and geothermal sources. Solar and wind capacity has grown fastest, led by China |
| Water | Agriculture accounts for roughly 70 per cent of global freshwater withdrawals. **Physical water scarcity** means too little water for demand; **economic water scarcity** means water exists but there is no money or infrastructure to supply it |
| Food | The **nutrition transition**: as incomes rise, diets shift from staple grains towards meat, dairy, sugar and processed foods. China's meat consumption has risen strongly since the 1980s |

## Footprints

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ecological footprint | Productive land and water needed to supply consumption and absorb waste |
| Biocapacity | The area available |
| Ecological deficit | Footprint greater than biocapacity |
| Virtual water | Water embedded in traded goods |

**Worked example (invented figures):** population 5 million, footprint 3.2 gha and biocapacity 1.8
gha per person. Footprint = 16 million gha; biocapacity = 9 million gha; deficit = 7 million gha;
ratio = 16 / 9, about 1.78.

The unit "gha" is a **global hectare**: a hectare with world-average productivity, used so that land
of different quality can be added together. The Global Footprint Network calculates that humanity as
a whole has been in ecological deficit since the early 1970s; its "Earth Overshoot Day", the date when
humanity has used a year's worth of biocapacity, has fallen in late July or early August in recent
years.

A **water footprint** measures the total water used to produce what a person or country consumes,
including virtual water in imports. Beef is often cited as needing around 15,000 litres of water per
kilogram, far more than cereals.

## Total versus per-capita: worked example (invented figures)

- Country A: 50 million people use 200 million tonnes of oil equivalent (toe) a year: 4 toe per person.
- Country B: 1,000 million people use 1,000 million toe: 1 toe per person.
- Country B's total is five times A's, but its per-capita use is a quarter of A's.
- Which matters more depends on the question: total use drives global emissions; per-capita use
  reflects living standards and fairness.

## Describing a trend from a graph

State the overall direction, quote start and end values with units, identify rates of change, note
anomalies and compare regions; then **explain** with reasons such as population growth, urbanisation,
a rising middle class, technology, trade and policy.

**Model description:** *"Global solar and wind electricity generation rose slowly until the late 2000s,
then accelerated sharply, with the steepest growth after 2015. Growth was fastest in Asia, led by
China, reflecting falling solar panel costs and government policy."* Swap in the figures from the
graph you are given.

## Environmental Kuznets idea

The Environmental Kuznets curve suggests environmental pressure rises as an economy industrialises
and then falls as wealth funds cleaner technology. It fits some local pollutants (such as sulphur
dioxide in high-income cities) better than global ones such as carbon dioxide, and richer countries
may partly "export" pollution through imports.

## Exam traps

- Describing totals when the question asks about per-capita use, or the reverse.
- Assuming renewable resources cannot be overused; water and forests can be depleted.
- Describing a graph without figures and units.
- Confusing physical with economic water scarcity.
- Treating the ecological footprint as an exact measure rather than a modelled estimate.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish renewable from non-renewable resources.
2. Define ecological footprint.
3. Footprint 10 million gha and biocapacity 6 million gha: what is the deficit?
4. Which sector dominates global water use?
5. Give two reasons per-capita use differs between countries.
6. Distinguish a stock from a flow resource.
7. What is the nutrition transition?
8. Distinguish physical from economic water scarcity.
9. What is a global hectare?
10. Country C has 20 million people using 60 million toe. What is its per-capita use?
11. Why does the new global middle class matter for resource demand?

**Answers:** 1. Renewable resources replenish within a human timescale; non-renewable ones are finite.
2. The area of productive land and water needed to supply consumption and absorb waste. 3. 4 million
gha. 4. Agriculture. 5. For example income and industrial structure. 6. A stock is a fixed quantity
used up; a flow is continuously renewed. 7. The shift in diets towards meat, dairy and processed food
as incomes rise. 8. Physical: too little water for demand; economic: water exists but money or
infrastructure to supply it is lacking. 9. A hectare of world-average productivity. 10. 3 toe per
person. 11. Middle-class households consume more meat, energy and manufactured goods per person.
