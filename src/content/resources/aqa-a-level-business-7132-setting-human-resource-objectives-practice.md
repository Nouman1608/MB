These questions cover section 3.6.1 Setting human resource objectives of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [HR objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-human-resource-objectives/) and the [HR objectives revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-human-resource-objectives-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A factory must make 72,000 units a month and each employee makes 1,200 units a month. How many employees are needed? **[1]**

A: 50

B: 60

C: 72

D: 86

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a feature of soft human resource management? **[1]**

A: Close supervision and tight output targets

B: Zero-hours contracts to match demand

C: Training and consultation to develop employees

D: Monitoring every employee's output each hour

**3.** Define the term "talent development". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit to a business of having a diverse workforce. **[3]**

**5.** Garner Garage employs 25 mechanics who each complete 40 jobs a month. A training programme costs £800 per mechanic and is expected to raise output per mechanic by 5%. Each extra job earns £90 contribution.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of the training. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of extra jobs completed each month. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the extra contribution in a year. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** reason why the actual gain might be lower than forecast. **[2]**

**6.** Fennel Foods needs to make 45,000 units a month. Each of its 54 production workers makes 750 units a month.

**(a)** Calculate the number of workers needed. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the shortfall. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate whether raising productivity to 834 units per worker would remove the shortfall. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** feature of hard human resource management. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a hotel of having engaged employees. **[6]**

**9.** A software company with highly skilled developers is deciding on its approach to HRM. Assess whether it should use a soft HRM approach. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how the growth of remote working might change a business's human resource objectives. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** difficulties a business might face in achieving a diversity objective. **[4]**

**12.** A retailer has high staff turnover. Evaluate whether training should be its main human resource objective. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 72,000 ÷ 1,200 = 60. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Soft HRM values development and involvement; the other options are hard HRM. **[1]**

**3.** Identifying employees with potential [1] and giving them the training and experience to take on more senior roles [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A diverse workforce understands a wider range of customers [1], so the business can design products, marketing and service that suit them [1], which can raise sales and customer loyalty [1]. Accept: wider talent pool; new ideas; reputation; legal compliance. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 25 × £800 = **£20,000** [1].

**(b)** 5% of 40 = 2 extra jobs each; 2 × 25 = **50 jobs** a month [1].

**(c)** 50 × £90 = £4,500 a month [1]; × 12 = **£54,000** a year [1].

**(d)** Trained mechanics may leave for better-paid jobs [1], so the business loses the benefit; or the productivity gain may be less than 5% because demand for jobs is limited, so extra capacity is not used [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** 45,000 ÷ 750 = **60 workers** [1].

**(b)** 60 − 54 = **6 workers** [1].

**(c)** 54 × 834 = 45,036 [1], which is above 45,000, so yes, it would remove the shortfall without recruiting [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Close supervision and tight targets to control output [1], treating employees as a resource to be used efficiently, for example monitoring each worker's output and using flexible contracts to match demand [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: better customer service [1]. Engaged staff care about guests' experience and go beyond their duties [1], which leads to better reviews and repeat bookings [1]. Benefit 2: lower costs of turnover and absence [1]. Engaged staff are more likely to stay, so the hotel spends less on recruiting and training replacements [1], and lower absence reduces the need for expensive cover [1]. **[6]**

**9.** For: skilled developers are hard to recruit and keep, so involvement, development and trust help retention [1]. Creative work needs staff who feel free to suggest ideas, and soft HRM supports that [1]. Engaged developers are more likely to produce quality work and stay with projects [1]. Developers have many job offers, so a soft approach helps the company compete for talent [1]. Against: it is costly in training, time for consultation and management effort [1]. Some deadlines and standards still need clear targets and monitoring, which is a hard HRM tool [1]. Not all managers are comfortable sharing decision-making [1]. Judgement: a mainly soft approach is likely to suit the company because of the labour market and the nature of the work [1], with clear targets and accountability to keep projects on time [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Remote working widens the area from which a company can recruit, so location objectives change [1] and the company may set targets to hire the best people wherever they live [1]. Engagement and communication become harder without daily contact [1], so objectives may focus on regular virtual meetings and measures of wellbeing [1]. New skills are needed, such as digital collaboration [1], so training objectives change; the firm may also need fewer office staff and lower property costs [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Difficulty 1: unconscious bias in recruitment and promotion [1]. Managers may favour candidates like themselves without realising, so progress is slow until processes and training are changed [1]. Difficulty 2: cost and culture [1]. Changing practices, adjusting workplaces and training managers costs money, and existing staff may resist change, so the objective takes time and effort to achieve [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: training improves skills and confidence, and staff who feel developed are more likely to stay [1]. Better trained staff give better service, raising sales [1]. Training is something the business controls [1], and it can be started quickly [1]. A career path linked to training gives staff a reason to stay [1]. Against: the main cause of turnover may be low pay, poor management or inflexible shifts, which training does not solve [1]. Trained staff may be more attractive to rivals and leave [1]. Training costs time and money and takes staff off the shop floor [1]. Other objectives such as engagement, pay and flexible working may matter more [1]. Factors: why staff leave (found through exit interviews), the cost of turnover, and the local labour market [1]. Judgement: training should be one objective but not the only one; the retailer first needs to find the reasons for turnover [1], and combine training with engagement and fair pay [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Link the HR approach to the type of work and the labour market in the case, and weigh costs against benefits.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing soft HRM as "kind" and hard HRM as "unkind".
- Listing objectives without applying them to the business.
- Counting the benefits of training without its costs.
- Forgetting that trained staff can leave.
- Giving objectives with no measure.
- Ignoring the labour market in the case.
