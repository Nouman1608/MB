## Section A

**1.** Define the term *activation energy*. **[2]**

**2.** State what is represented by the area under a Boltzmann distribution curve that lies to the right of the activation energy. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why a Boltzmann distribution curve starts at the origin and never meets the energy axis at high energy. **[2]**

**4.** A student writes: "Increasing the temperature lowers the activation energy, so more collisions succeed." Identify the error and correct it. **[2]**

**5.** A reaction has ΔH = −45 kJ mol⁻¹ and an activation energy for the forward reaction of +80 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the activation energy for the reverse reaction. **[2]**

**6.** For an endothermic reaction, explain why the activation energy must be at least as large as ΔH. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A sample of gas is at temperature T₁.

**(a)** Sketch, or describe fully, the Boltzmann distribution for this sample. Label both axes and mark the activation energy, Eₐ. **[3]**

**(b)** On the same axes, describe the curve for the same sample at a higher temperature, T₂. **[3]**

**8.** Explain, in terms of both the Boltzmann distribution and the frequency of effective collisions, why the rate of a reaction increases when the temperature is raised. **[4]**

**9.** A student investigates the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid by timing how long a cross takes to disappear beneath the flask.

| Temperature / °C | Time, t / s |
|---|---|
| 20 | 120 |
| 30 | 64 |
| 40 | 33 |

**(a)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why 1/t can be used as a measure of the rate. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate 1/t at each temperature, giving units, and describe what happens to the rate for each 10 °C rise. **[3]**

**(d)** State two variables that must be kept constant. **[2]**

**10.** Two reactions, X and Y, take place at the same temperature. Reaction X has Eₐ = +50 kJ mol⁻¹ and reaction Y has Eₐ = +120 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Using the Boltzmann distribution, explain which reaction is faster, assuming the collision frequencies are similar. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why a fuel and oxygen can be mixed at room temperature without reacting, but react rapidly once a spark is applied. **[2]**

**11.** A student says: "When a gas is heated from 300 K to 310 K, the particles collide more often, and that is why the rate doubles."

**(a)** State the approximate effect of this temperature rise on the frequency of collisions. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the student's reasoning does not account for the size of the rate increase. **[2]**

**12.** Draw, or describe, a labelled reaction pathway diagram for an exothermic reaction. Show the reactants, products, ΔH and Eₐ, and state where Eₐ is measured from. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The **minimum energy** [1] required for a **collision to be effective** (to lead to reaction) [1].

**2.** The number of molecules with energy **greater than or equal to Eₐ**, which are able to react on collision [1].

**3.** No molecule has zero energy, so the curve starts at zero at the origin [1]. There is no maximum energy: a small number of molecules always have very high energies, so the tail approaches but never meets the axis [1].

**4.** Temperature does not change Eₐ, which is fixed for a given reaction pathway [1]. A higher temperature increases the **proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Eₐ**, so more collisions are effective [1].

**5.** Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH [1] = 80 − (−45) = **+125 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**6.** The products are at a higher enthalpy than the reactants [1]. The peak of the pathway must be at or above the products' level, so the climb from reactants to the peak is at least ΔH [1].

**7. (a)** Vertical axis: number (or fraction) of molecules; horizontal axis: energy [1]. The curve starts at the origin, rises to a single peak and falls in a long tail that does not meet the energy axis [1]. Eₐ is marked well to the right of the peak, with the area beyond it shaded [1].

**(b)** The peak is lower and moves to higher energy [1]. The curve is broader, starts at the origin, and lies above the T₁ curve beyond the crossing point, so the area beyond Eₐ is larger [1]. The total area under the curve is the same as for T₁ [1].

**8.** At the higher temperature the molecules have more kinetic energy, and the Boltzmann distribution shifts to higher energy [1]. A greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ, so a greater proportion of collisions are effective [1]. The molecules move faster, so collisions are also more frequent [1]. The frequency of effective collisions increases, so the rate increases; the increase in the proportion with E ≥ Eₐ is the main reason [1].

**9. (a)** Na₂S₂O₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + S + SO₂ + H₂O [1].

**(b)** The same amount of sulfur must form for the cross to disappear each time, so the rate is proportional to 1/t [1].

**(c)** 20 °C: 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹; 30 °C: 1/64 = 0.0156 s⁻¹; 40 °C: 1/33 = 0.0303 s⁻¹ [1 for all three values, 1 for the unit s⁻¹]. The rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (ratios about 1.9) [1].

**(d)** Any two of: volume of sodium thiosulfate solution; its concentration; volume of acid; concentration of acid; the same flask and cross; total volume of mixture [2].

**10. (a)** Reaction X is faster [1]. Its Eₐ lies further to the left on the distribution, so a much larger area of the curve, and so a much greater proportion of molecules, has energy ≥ Eₐ [1].

**(b)** At room temperature the activation energy is large compared with the energy of most molecules, so very few collisions are effective [1]. The spark supplies energy so that some molecules exceed Eₐ; the reaction is exothermic, so the energy released raises the temperature of the surrounding mixture and keeps the reaction going rapidly [1].

**11. (a)** It increases only slightly, by about 2% (accept "a small increase") [1].

**(b)** A small rise in collision frequency cannot double the rate [1]. The main effect is that the proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Eₐ increases greatly, so a much larger proportion of collisions are effective [1].

**12.** Axes: enthalpy (vertical) and progress of reaction (horizontal) [1]. Products drawn at a lower level than reactants, with ΔH shown as a downward arrow and labelled negative [1]. A single hump between them [1]. Eₐ shown as the vertical arrow from the **reactants' level to the top of the hump** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "collision" out of the activation energy definition.
- Shifting the higher-temperature curve sideways without lowering its peak.
- Saying that temperature, rather than a catalyst, lowers Eₐ.
- Explaining the temperature effect with collision frequency alone.
- Using t, not 1/t, when comparing rates in timing experiments.
