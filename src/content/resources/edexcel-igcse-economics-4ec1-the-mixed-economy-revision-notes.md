These notes condense section 1.1.5, The mixed economy, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, examined in Paper 1 with a single tier of entry. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked cases, read the [mixed economy study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-mixed-economy/); to practise, use the [mixed economy practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-mixed-economy-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mixed economy | Resources allocated by both the private sector (markets) and the public sector (government) |
| Private sector | Part of the economy owned and controlled by individuals and firms |
| Public sector | Part of the economy owned and controlled by government, central or local |
| Market failure | The free market leads to an inefficient allocation of resources |
| Public good | A good that is non-excludable and non-rival |
| Non-excludable | Non-payers cannot be stopped from benefiting |
| Non-rival | One person's use does not reduce the amount available to others |
| Free rider | Someone who benefits from a good without paying for it |
| Privatisation | Transfer of ownership from the public sector to the private sector |

## Sector comparison: use "whereas"

| | Private sector | Public sector |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Individuals, partners, shareholders | The state, on behalf of citizens |
| Control | Owners or appointed managers | Ministers, councils, appointed officials |
| Aims | Profit, growth, survival, market share | Service to the public, fair access, meeting need |
| Funding | Sales revenue, owners' capital, loans | Taxation, government borrowing, some charges |

Write a difference as one comparison: "A private firm aims for profit, *whereas* a public organisation aims to provide a service."

## What, how, for whom

- **What:** markets produce what consumers will pay for; government provides what the market will not (public goods) and changes quantities through taxes, subsidies and rules.
- **How:** firms choose the lowest-cost method; government sets standards and runs its own services.
- **For whom:** markets allocate to those who can pay; government redistributes through taxes, benefits and free or subsidised services.

## Market failure and intervention

- Market failure = inefficient allocation of resources.
- Three reasons to intervene: nothing provided (public goods); wrong quantity provided (externalities, see 1.1.6); basic needs unmet by allocation through ability to pay.
- Intervention has costs: it must be funded, and officials may lack information.

## Public goods

**Two tests:** non-excludable **and** non-rival. Both must hold.

**Free rider chain:** non-excludable → people can benefit without paying → firms cannot raise enough revenue → no private firm supplies the good → government provides it, funded by taxes.

| Public goods | Not public goods (and why) |
|---|---|
| National defence | School places (excludable and rival) |
| Street lighting | Toll roads (excludable by toll) |
| Lighthouses | Concerts in a ticketed hall (excludable) |
| Flood defences | Food (excludable and rival) |

**Free rider calculation (invented):** a park costs 9 000 a year; 3 000 households value it at 5 each (total 15 000). Only 800 pay voluntarily: 800 × 5 = 4 000, a shortfall of 5 000. Tax of 9 000 ÷ 3 000 = 3 each leaves each household 5 − 3 = 2 better off.

## Relative importance of the sectors

Measures:

- government spending as a percentage of national output
- public-sector employment as a percentage of all workers
- share of output made by state-owned organisations.

Method: part ÷ whole × 100. *Example:* 4.2 million public-sector workers out of 28 million gives 4.2 ÷ 28 × 100 = **15%**.

Reasons the share differs: political choices about taxes and services; income levels; history of state ownership.

## Privatisation effects

| Group | Possible gains | Possible losses |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Lower prices and better service if competition rises | Higher prices if a private monopoly forms; loss of unprofitable services |
| Workers | Higher pay if the firm grows; share schemes | Job losses as costs are cut; less job security |
| Businesses | Profit motive drives efficiency; new rivals can enter | Shareholder pressure; regulation |
| Government | Sale revenue; subsidies saved; less to manage | Loss of control and future profits; one-off revenue only |

**Deciding factor:** whether the privatised firm faces competition or effective regulation.

## Exam traps

- A **difference** needs both sectors in the same sentence.
- A public good needs **both** characteristics.
- "Provided by government" does not make something a public good.
- The free rider problem is **using without paying**, not refusing to use.
- Privatisation effects **depend** on competition and regulation; avoid "always".
- In calculations, show part ÷ whole before × 100.
- One-sided "differences".
- Public goods identified by who provides them, not by their characteristics.
- No chain from non-excludability to the lack of private provision.
- Privatisation answers without saying what the outcome depends on.

## Quick self-test

1. Define mixed economy.
2. State one difference between the public and private sectors in terms of aims.
3. What is meant by market failure?
4. Give the two characteristics of a public good.
5. Explain the free rider problem in one sentence.
6. Is a ticketed concert a public good? Give a reason.
7. Give one reason why governments might need to intervene in markets.
8. Define privatisation.
9. Give one possible effect of privatisation on workers.
10. Give one possible benefit of privatisation for the government.
11. Government spending is 96 billion and national output is 320 billion. Calculate spending as a percentage of output.
12. Why might privatisation not lower prices for consumers?

### Answers

1. An economy in which resources are allocated by both the private sector and the public sector.
2. Private firms usually aim for profit, whereas public organisations aim to provide a service to the public.
3. When the free market leads to an inefficient allocation of resources.
4. Non-excludable and non-rival.
5. People can benefit without paying, so firms cannot collect enough revenue and do not supply the good.
6. No: non-payers can be excluded by tickets (and seats are rival).
7. Any one: public goods would not be provided; externalities lead to the wrong quantity; basic needs may go unmet.
8. Transfer of ownership of a business or asset from the public sector to the private sector.
9. Job losses as the firm cuts costs (or: higher pay if the firm grows).
10. Revenue from the sale (or: no longer paying a subsidy).
11. 96 ÷ 320 × 100 = **30%**.
12. If the privatised firm is a monopoly with no competition, it may raise prices to increase profit.
