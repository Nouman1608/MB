This study guide teaches the writing skills behind **Paper 1** of IB Diploma Programme Language B:
choosing a task, reading its context, audience and purpose, and producing a text with the right
register and conventions. It follows the IB Language B subject brief for first assessment 2020,
which remains the examined course until the new syllabus is first assessed in May 2029, so it
applies to the 2026, 2027 and 2028 sessions. It covers SL and HL and draws on all five prescribed
themes, because a Paper 1 task can come from any of them. It is written in English; apply each
point to your target language.

Use it with the [Paper 1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-paper-1-writing-text-types-revision-notes/)
and the [Paper 1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-paper-1-writing-text-types-practice/).
For the whole course, see the [Language B course hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-b/) and the
[printable Language B checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-b/).

## What the brief says about Paper 1

The subject brief gives these facts, and only these, about the paper:

- It is **Paper 1 (productive skills)**, externally assessed.
- You complete **one writing task from a choice of three**.
- It is worth **25%** of the final grade and is marked out of **30 marks** ("Writing—30 marks").
- The outline is the same at SL and HL. At HL "the tasks set will require more complex language
  and structures and demand higher-order thinking skills", and "a higher word range has been
  provided".

The brief does not print the word ranges, the list of text types or the marking criteria. Your
teacher will share these from the full subject guide.

Two of the brief's assessment objectives describe the task exactly: "Communicate clearly and
effectively in a range of contexts and for a variety of purposes" and "Understand and use language
appropriate to a range of interpersonal and/or intercultural contexts and audiences". Paper 1
rewards language that **fits**, not only language that is correct.

## Five questions before you write

The brief names five conceptual understandings for the course: **context, audience, purpose,
meaning and variation**. Turn them into five questions and answer each one in your plan.

1. **Context.** Where and why does this text exist? Context sets the format.
2. **Audience.** Who reads it, and what is your relationship to them? A friend, a manager, the
   general public. Audience sets the register.
3. **Purpose.** What must the text do? Inform, persuade, request, complain, advise, reflect.
   Most tasks have a main purpose and a second one.
4. **Meaning.** What single message should the reader take away? If you cannot say it in one
   sentence, your text will drift.
5. **Variation.** Which form of the language suits this reader? Languages vary by region, age
   group and situation. Choose forms that suit the reader and stay consistent.

## Register: the scale you write on

Register is how formal or informal your language is: a scale, not a switch.

| | Informal | Neutral | Formal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical reader | Friend, family, peers online | Wide public, school community | Manager, official, organisation |
| Address | First names, familiar "you" form | "you" to readers in general | Titles and surnames, polite "you" form |
| Vocabulary | Everyday, some slang, emotive | Clear, standard | Precise, impersonal |
| Sentences | Short, questions, exclamations | Varied | Complete, linked, fewer exclamations |
| Tone | Chatty, personal | Engaging but controlled | Polite, measured |

Most languages mark register in ways English does not. French chooses between *tu* and *vous*,
German between *du* and *Sie*, Spanish between *tú* and *usted*. Once you choose a form of address,
keep it for the whole text, including verb endings and possessives. Switching halfway is one of the
most visible register errors.

### Worked example: one message, three registers

The message: a school event has moved from Friday to Monday. Original texts written for Marlbridge.

> **Informal (message to a friend):** Hey! Bad news, the quiz night's moved to Monday. Still up for
> it? Let me know!

> **Neutral (school newsletter):** Please note that this term's quiz night will now take place on
> Monday rather than Friday. Tickets already bought remain valid.

> **Formal (letter to a guest speaker):** I am writing to inform you that the quiz night has been
> rescheduled to Monday. I apologise for any inconvenience and would be grateful if you could
> confirm whether you are still able to attend.

What changes, step by step:

1. **Opening.** "Hey!" becomes "Please note" becomes "I am writing to inform you". The formal
   version states its purpose in its first clause.
2. **Vocabulary.** "moved" becomes "will now take place" becomes "has been rescheduled".
3. **Structure.** Fragments ("Still up for it?") give way to full sentences and a passive.
4. **Request.** "Let me know!" becomes "would be grateful if you could confirm". Conditional and
   polite forms carry formality in most languages.

## Three families of text types

This page sorts text types into three families by audience: **personal**, **professional** and
**mass media**. Your teacher's list from the full guide is the one to learn. The tables show the
conventions that most often decide whether a text looks like the type it claims to be.

### Personal texts: for yourself or someone you know

| Text type | Layout and opening | Register | Typical moves | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diary or journal entry | Date, sometimes a time | Informal, first person | Events of the day, feelings, reflection | A thought about tomorrow or what you learned |
| Personal email or letter | Greeting by first name | Informal to neutral | Shared references, news, questions to the reader | Friendly sign-off and name |
| Blog post (personal) | Title, engaging first line | Informal to neutral | Direct address to followers, anecdote, opinion | Question or invitation to comment |

### Professional texts: to an organisation or in a working role

| Text type | Layout and opening | Register | Typical moves | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal letter | Addresses, date, formal salutation | Formal | Purpose in the first paragraph; one point per paragraph; a clear request | Formal closing formula, name, role |
| Formal email | Subject line, formal salutation | Formal to neutral | Shorter than a letter; purpose first; action requested | Polite close and name |
| Report | Title, "to/from/date" or an introduction stating the aim | Neutral to formal, impersonal | Headings, findings, evidence | Recommendations |
| Proposal | Title and aim | Formal, persuasive | Problem, plan, costs or resources, benefits | Request for approval |

In your target language, learn the fixed formulas. French letters often open "Madame, Monsieur,"
and close with a "Veuillez agréer…" formula; German uses "Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren" and "Mit
freundlichen Grüßen"; Spanish uses "Estimado/a" and "Atentamente". Check yours with your teacher.

### Mass-media texts: for a wide public audience

| Text type | Layout and opening | Register | Typical moves | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Article (news or feature) | Headline, often a subheading, byline | Neutral | Who, what, where, when, why; quotations from people | A final quotation or look ahead |
| Opinion piece or editorial | Headline that signals a view | Neutral to formal, persuasive | Clear stance, evidence, counterargument | Restated view or call to action |
| Speech | Greeting to the audience | Depends on audience; spoken features | Signposting, rhetorical questions, inclusive "we" | Thanks and a memorable last line |
| Leaflet or brochure | Title, short sections under subheadings | Neutral, direct | Bullet points, imperatives, contact details | Call to action |
| Review | Title, what is being reviewed | Neutral, evaluative | Brief summary, strengths, weaknesses, rating or verdict | Recommendation |

## Choosing one task from three

You get three tasks and write one. Spend the first minutes choosing well.

1. **Read all three to the end.** The text type and audience are often in the last line.
2. **Underline five things in each:** your role, the reader, the purpose, the text type and the
   content points you must cover.
3. **Ask two questions.** Do I control this text type's conventions? Do I have the topic
   vocabulary in my target language?
4. **Choose the task where both answers are yes.**
5. **Commit.** Changing task halfway wastes time.

## Worked example: decoding and planning a professional task

An original task written for Marlbridge (theme: Social organization, topic: the working world):

> A local community library is looking for young volunteers to run a weekend reading club for
> children. Write to the library manager to offer your help, explaining what you could bring to
> the role and asking about practical details.

**Step 1 — decode.**

- Role: a student offering to volunteer.
- Reader: the library manager, someone you do not know. **Formal register.**
- Purpose: to offer help (main), to persuade them you are suitable, and to request information.
- Text type: the task says "write to the library manager", so a formal letter or formal email.
- Content points: what you could bring; practical questions.

**Step 2 — plan the paragraphs.**

1. Salutation and purpose: why you are writing and where you saw the notice.
2. What you bring: two specific strengths, each with an example (reading to younger siblings; a
   club you already help run).
3. A short idea for the reading club, to show initiative.
4. Practical questions: hours, training, whether a reference is needed.
5. Closing: availability, thanks, formal formula, name.

**Step 3 — model opening and close** (original text written for Marlbridge).

> Dear Ms Varga,
>
> I am writing in response to your notice asking for volunteers to run a weekend reading club. I am
> seventeen, I read to my two younger brothers every evening, and I would very much like to help.
>
> …
>
> I am available every Saturday morning from next month. Thank you for considering my offer. I look
> forward to hearing from you.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Daniel Okafor

The opening names the purpose, then gives a concrete reason. The close gives availability, thanks
the reader and uses a fixed formula.

## SL and HL

The outline is identical at both levels; HL tasks demand more complex language and higher-order
thinking, with a higher word range. So an HL plan should do more than report or describe:

- **Weigh** two sides before reaching a view.
- **Anticipate** the reader's objection and answer it.
- **Use complex structures** you control: conditionals, subordinate clauses, the passive,
  structures of concession ("although…", "even if…").

SL writers should aim for the same where they can control the language. Complexity you cannot
control costs more than it earns.

## Common errors

- **Right content, wrong text type.** A general essay when the task asked for a speech, with no
  greeting, no audience and no closing appeal.
- **Register drift.** A formal letter that ends "Anyway, see you soon!", or switches from the polite
  to the familiar "you".
- **Conventions only at the start.** A headline, then paragraphs that read like a diary.
- **Missing content points.** The task asks for two things; the text answers one.
