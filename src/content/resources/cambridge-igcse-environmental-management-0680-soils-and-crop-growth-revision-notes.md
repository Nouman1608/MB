## Soil composition (2.1.1): four components

1. **Mineral particles**: sand, silt and clay, from weathered rock.
2. **Organic content**: living organisms (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria) and organic matter from decomposition (humus).
3. **Gases**: air in pore spaces, including oxygen and carbon dioxide.
4. **Water**: in pore spaces and as films around particles, carrying dissolved ions.

Percentage composition is not required.

## Sand, silt and clay compared

| | Sand | Silt | Clay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle size | Largest | Medium | Smallest |
| Feel | Gritty | Smooth | Sticky |
| Pore spaces | Large | Medium | Tiny |
| Drainage | Fast | Moderate | Slow |
| Water retention | Low | Moderate | High |
| Nutrient retention | Low (leaching) | Moderate | High |
| Cultivation | Easy, light | Can compact | Heavy when wet, hard when dry |
| Main risk | Drying out, nutrient loss | Packing down | Waterlogging |

## Why composition matters (2.1.2)

- **Mineral particles**: control drainage, water and nutrient retention.
- **Nutrient content**: organic content releases ions as it decomposes; inorganic ions:
  - **Nitrate (NO₃⁻)**: nitrogen for proteins and leaf growth; lack gives stunted growth and yellow leaves.
  - **Phosphate (PO₄³⁻)**: phosphorus for root growth; lack gives poor roots and purple-tinged leaves.
  - **Potassium (K⁺)**: for flowers and fruit; lack gives yellow leaf edges and poor fruit.
- **pH**: acidic (below 7), neutral (7), alkaline (above 7). Most crops prefer near neutral; extremes make some nutrients less available.
- **Pore spaces**: hold air and water; room for roots.
- **Gas content**: oxygen for aerobic respiration of roots and soil organisms.
- **Water content and drainage**: water for photosynthesis and ion transport; excess must drain to avoid waterlogging.
- **Ease of cultivation**: how easily soil can be ploughed and planted.

## Loam (2.1.3): five reasons, each with its benefit

| Reason | Benefit to the crop |
|---|---|
| Ideal combination of mineral particles and pore spaces | Both air and water available to roots |
| Retains moisture | Water between rainfall or irrigation |
| Contains organic content and inorganic ions | Supply of nitrate, phosphate and potassium |
| Allows excess water to drain | No waterlogging; roots get oxygen |
| Easy to cultivate | Less time, fuel and labour to plough and plant |

## Weather and seasons (2.1.4 and 2.1.5)

- **Weather**: the day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere in a location. (Climate is the long-term average.)
- **Wet and dry seasons**: some areas have them; crops are planted at the start of the wet season; dry-season growing needs irrigation; a failed wet season reduces harvests.

## Optimum photosynthesis and crop growth (2.1.6)

**Word equation:** carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light energy, chlorophyll).

- **Length of growing season**: more days warm and wet enough → slower crops can mature, or more harvests.
- **Optimum weather conditions**: warm but not extreme heat, enough rain, sunshine, no frost.
- **Daylight hours**: longer days → more hours of photosynthesis per day.

**Reading graphs**

- Rate against light intensity: rises, then levels off (another factor now limiting).
- Rate against temperature: rises to an optimum, then falls (enzymes work less well).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Humus | Dark organic matter from decomposition |
| Leaching | Nutrients washed down out of reach of roots by draining water |
| Waterlogging | Pore spaces filled with water, leaving little oxygen |
| Loam | Soil with a balanced mix of sand, silt and clay plus organic matter |
| Growing season | Period of the year when conditions allow crops to grow |
| Limiting factor | The condition in shortest supply that holds back the rate of photosynthesis |

## Exam traps

- Organic content has **two** parts: living organisms and decomposed matter.
- Clay is not simply "good": it holds water and nutrients **but** drains poorly.
- Sand is not simply "bad": it drains well **but** loses water and nutrients.
- Nitrate = nitrogen; phosphate = phosphorus; potassium ions = potassium.
- Weather is day to day, not the average over years.
- More heat does not always mean faster growth.
- For loam, explain what each property does for the crop.

## Self-test

1. Name the four components of soil.
2. Which mineral particle is the smallest?
3. Name the two parts of soil organic content.
4. Which ion supplies nitrogen to plants?
5. What is the main use of phosphate in plants?
6. Why do roots need oxygen from the soil?
7. Why can a clay soil become waterlogged?
8. Why does a sandy soil often lack nutrients?
9. Give three reasons why loam is a good medium for crop growth.
10. Define weather.
11. Why are crops in areas with wet and dry seasons usually planted at the start of the wet season?
12. On a graph of photosynthesis rate against light intensity, why does the line level off?

## Answers

1. Mineral particles, organic content, gases and water.
2. Clay.
3. Living organisms (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria) and organic matter from decomposition.
4. Nitrate (NO₃⁻).
5. Healthy root growth.
6. For aerobic respiration, which releases energy for growth and for absorbing mineral ions.
7. Its tiny pore spaces drain slowly, so they fill with water.
8. Water drains quickly through large pores and washes nutrients down (leaching).
9. Any three: ideal mix of particles and pore spaces; retains moisture; contains organic content and inorganic ions; allows excess water to drain; easy to cultivate.
10. The day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere in a location.
11. So that the crop has water while it grows, and can be harvested as the dry season starts.
12. Another factor, such as temperature or carbon dioxide, has become the limiting factor.
