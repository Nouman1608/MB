## The functional group and the series (4.34C and 4.35C)

The **carboxylic acids** have the functional group **-COOH**, called the carboxyl group. In the displayed formula, the carbon atom of the group has a **double bond to one oxygen** and a **single bond to an -OH group**, as well as a single bond to the rest of the molecule:

```
      O
     //
 - C
     \
      O - H
```

The general formula of the series is **CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH**, where n is the number of carbons in the chain **not** counting the carbon of the carboxyl group. The names end in **-oic acid**, and the carbon of the -COOH group counts as carbon 1 in the chain length.

| Name | Molecular formula | Structural formula | Mr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methanoic acid | CH₂O₂ | HCOOH | 46 |
| Ethanoic acid | C₂H₄O₂ | CH₃COOH | 60 |
| Propanoic acid | C₃H₆O₂ | CH₃CH₂COOH | 74 |
| Butanoic acid | C₄H₈O₂ | CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH | 88 |

The name counts all the carbons, including the one in -COOH: ethanoic acid has two carbons in total, so it is the "eth" stem. A very common slip is to call CH₃COOH "methanoic acid".

**Displayed formula of ethanoic acid:**

```
    H   O
    |   ||
H - C - C - O - H
    |
    H
```

Check the carbons: the CH₃ carbon has four bonds (three C-H and one C-C); the carboxyl carbon has four bonds (C-C, C=O counts two, and C-O). Each oxygen has two bonds: the double-bonded one has the C=O, and the other has one bond to carbon and one to hydrogen. The molecular formula is C₂H₄O₂: two carbons, four hydrogens (three on CH₃ and one on O), two oxygens.

**Properties.** Carboxylic acids are colourless liquids with sharp smells. The first members mix with water. In water, they ionise only a little, so they are **weak acids**, with a pH of about 2 to 4 in typical solutions, compared with 0 to 1 for a strong acid of the same concentration. They have the usual properties of acids: they turn universal indicator orange or yellow and react with metals, bases and carbonates.

## Reactions of aqueous carboxylic acids (4.36C)

An aqueous solution of a carboxylic acid reacts like other dilute acids. The salts they form are called **ethanoates**, **propanoates** and so on (formed from ethanoic acid, propanoic acid and so on).

### With metals

Reactive metals such as magnesium, zinc and iron react with aqueous carboxylic acids to give **a salt and hydrogen**:

```
Mg + 2CH3COOH → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2
magnesium + ethanoic acid → magnesium ethanoate + hydrogen
```

Observations: the metal dissolves and **bubbles of hydrogen** are given off. The reaction is slower than with a strong acid of the same concentration, because there are fewer hydrogen ions at any moment. Hydrogen is confirmed by a lit splint, which gives a squeaky pop.

### With metal carbonates

Carbonates react with the acid to give **a salt, water and carbon dioxide**:

```
CaCO3 + 2CH3COOH → (CH3COO)2Ca + H2O + CO2
calcium carbonate + ethanoic acid → calcium ethanoate + water + carbon dioxide
```

Observations: the carbonate dissolves and the mixture **fizzes** because carbon dioxide is given off. The gas turns limewater milky. This is a test for a carboxylic acid (although any acid will give the same result).

**Worked example 1: reaction with magnesium.** 0.24 g of magnesium (Ar = 24) reacts with excess ethanoic acid.

1. Moles of Mg = 0.24 / 24 = **0.010 mol**.
2. The equation shows 1 mol Mg gives 1 mol H₂, so 0.010 mol of hydrogen forms.
3. Volume of hydrogen = 0.010 x 24,000 = **240 cm³** (at room temperature and pressure).
4. Mr of magnesium ethanoate, (CH₃COO)₂Mg = 24 + 2 x 59 = **142**; mass formed = 0.010 x 142 = **1.42 g**.

(The ethanoate ion, CH₃COO, has a mass of 12 + 3 + 12 + 16 + 16 = 59.)

**Worked example 2: reaction with calcium carbonate.** 1.0 g of calcium carbonate (Mr = 100) reacts with excess ethanoic acid.

1. Moles of CaCO₃ = 1.0 / 100 = **0.010 mol**.
2. 1 mol CaCO₃ gives 1 mol CO₂, so the gas volume = 0.010 x 24,000 = **240 cm³**.
3. Mr of calcium ethanoate = 40 + 2 x 59 = 158; mass formed = 0.010 x 158 = **1.58 g**.
4. The acid needed: the equation shows 2 mol of CH₃COOH per mole of CaCO₃, so 0.020 mol, a mass of 0.020 x 60 = **1.2 g** of pure ethanoic acid.

## Vinegar (4.37C)

**Vinegar** is an **aqueous solution containing ethanoic acid**, usually about 4 to 8% by mass. It is used in cooking, to preserve food (pickles) and as a flavouring. It is made when ethanol in wine or cider is oxidised by bacteria in air.

**Worked example 3.** A bottle of vinegar contains 5.0% ethanoic acid by mass. In 100 g of vinegar there is 5.0 g of ethanoic acid, which is 5.0 / 60 = **0.083 mol**. Because vinegar is acidic, it can be used to remove limescale (calcium carbonate): the acid reacts with the carbonate and the mixture fizzes.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the carboxyl group as -COH or -CHO (that is an aldehyde).
- Calling CH₃COOH methanoic acid. It is ethanoic acid, because both carbons count.
- Forgetting the hydrogen on the -OH when drawing the displayed formula.
- Writing the salt of ethanoic acid as "ethanate". It is ethanoate.
- Omitting water from the carbonate reaction, or writing hydrogen as H.
- Forgetting the factor of 2 for the acid in the equations (2CH₃COOH).
- Saying a carboxylic acid is "strong" because it has the word acid in its name.

## Exam technique

When asked to name an acid, count all the carbons including the carboxyl carbon. When asked for a reaction, give the word equation or balanced symbol equation and the observations (fizzing for carbonates; bubbles and the metal dissolving for metals). For calculations, use the ratio from the balanced equation and the molar volume 24 dm³. The whole sub-topic is on Paper 2 only.
