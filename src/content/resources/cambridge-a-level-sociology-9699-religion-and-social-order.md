Section 11.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Religion and social order*, is
the theoretical core of heading 11 in Paper 4. The syllabus lists three points: functionalist
accounts of how religion contributes to social order and social solidarity; Marxist accounts of the
relationship between religion, oppression and capitalism; and debates about the strengths and
limitations of functionalist and Marxist perspectives on religion. Heading 11 links this to
*Power, control and resistance* and asks for a contrast between structural and interactionist
theories.

The [religion, social order and social change guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-religion-and-social-order/)
introduces Durkheim, Malinowski, Parsons, Bellah, Marx and Maduro. This page goes further into how
each argument works, the evidence for it and the criticisms that matter in a 35-mark essay. Use it
with the [religion and social order revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-religion-and-social-order-revision-notes/)

## Functionalist accounts

### Durkheim's argument step by step

Émile Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) is the starting point.

1. **Sacred and profane.** Every religion divides the world into the sacred, things set apart and
   treated with awe, and the profane, ordinary things of everyday life.
2. **Totemism.** Durkheim used ethnographic reports on Aboriginal Australian clans, whose totems
   (often an animal or plant) were sacred symbols of the clan.
3. **The totem symbolises society.** When clan members worship the totem, they are in effect
   worshipping their own society, the power that existed before them and will outlast them.
4. **Collective effervescence.** At collective ceremonies, the shared intense emotion makes people
   feel part of something greater than themselves.
5. **Collective conscience.** Ritual reinforces the shared norms, values and beliefs that make
   social life possible, producing **social solidarity**.
6. **Cognitive functions.** Durkheim also argued that religion gave humans the basic categories of
   thought, such as time, space and cause, so it is the origin of shared ways of thinking.

**Durkheim on the future.** He expected traditional religion to weaken as societies became more
complex and individualistic, but predicted that new forms of shared belief, such as a "cult of the
individual" stressing human rights, would take its place.

### Later functionalists

- **Malinowski:** religion helps individuals cope with situations of emotional stress and
  uncertainty that threaten social order, such as death or danger. Funeral rites, for example,
  reintegrate the bereaved and reaffirm the group's continuity.
- **Talcott Parsons:** religion provides core values that become the basis of norms (for example,
  Christian ideas reflected in law) and answers "ultimate questions" about suffering and injustice,
  helping people adjust to events that might otherwise undermine commitment to society.
- **Robert Bellah:** civil religion integrates a religiously diverse society around shared sacred
  symbols of the nation.

### Evidence that supports functionalism

- National ceremonies, remembrance days and royal or state funerals show ritual creating collective
  emotion.
- Religion's role in rites of passage (birth, marriage, death) continues even where attendance has
  fallen.
- Religious communities often provide support and solidarity in times of crisis.

### Criticisms of functionalism

- **Peter Worsley** (1956) argued that Durkheim's evidence was flawed: there is no sharp division
  between sacred and profane among Aboriginal Australians, and different clans can share the same
  totems, so totems cannot simply symbolise one clan.
- **Religious conflict.** Religion can divide societies rather than unite them. Samuel Huntington's
  *The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order* (1996) argued that after the Cold War
  conflict would increasingly occur between civilisations defined largely by religion. Critics
  dispute his thesis, but religious conflict between and within societies challenges the idea that
  religion is a source of solidarity.
- **Diversity.** Durkheim's theory fits small societies with one religion; it is harder to apply to
  modern societies with many faiths and many non-believers.
- **Change.** Functionalism neglects religion as a source of change (11.3).

## Marxist accounts

### Marx's argument

Karl Marx wrote that "religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless
world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people" (1844). The fuller
passage matters: religion is a response to real suffering, but one that dulls awareness of its
causes.

| Function in Marx's account | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideology** | Presents inequality as natural or God-given | Divine right of kings; hierarchy presented as sacred order |
| **Compensation** | Promises reward in an afterlife for suffering now | "The meek shall inherit the earth" as consolation |
| **Legitimation of the ruling class** | Religious institutions ally with the powerful | Established churches owning land and supporting rulers |
| **Product of alienation** | Workers who lack control over their lives project their hopes onto a god | Religion flourishes where people feel powerless |

**Lenin** (1905) called religion "a kind of spiritual gin" in which the slaves of capital drown
their human image. Marx expected religion to disappear once a classless society removed the
suffering that produced it.

### Historical evidence

- **The Halévy thesis:** the historian Élie Halévy argued that Methodism helped prevent a
  revolution in England by channelling working-class discontent into religious enthusiasm. E.P.
  Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963) also discussed Methodism's role in
  disciplining workers.
- **Caste:** religious ideas about purity and rebirth can present a rigid social hierarchy as part of
  a sacred order.

### Neo-Marxist developments

- **Engels** noted that early Christianity began as a movement of oppressed people, so religion can
  have a radical side.
- **Antonio Gramsci** saw religion as part of the struggle for hegemony: it can support ruling-class
  ideas, but clergy can also act as "organic intellectuals" who help the working class develop
  alternative ideas.
- **Dwight Billings** (1990) compared coal miners and textile workers in Kentucky. Among miners,
  some lay preachers supported union struggles and used religious language to justify them, while
  textile workers in company-dominated communities were more conservative. Religion therefore
  supported both resistance and accommodation, depending on who controlled it.
- **Otto Maduro** (1982) argued that religion has relative autonomy from the economy.

### Criticisms of Marxism

- Ignores religion's positive functions for individuals and communities.
- Treats believers as victims of false consciousness, denying their own understanding.
- Cannot easily explain religion as a force for change, such as liberation theology (11.3).
- Religion persists in societies that tried to abolish class or repress religion.

## Weighing the two perspectives

| | Functionalism | Marxism |
|---|---|---|
| **What religion does** | Integrates society, creates solidarity | Legitimates inequality, dulls resistance |
| **Who benefits** | Society as a whole | The ruling class |
| **Shared assumption** | Religion is conservative: it maintains the existing order | Religion is conservative: it maintains the existing order |
| **Main weakness** | Ignores conflict and inequality | Ignores consensus and individual meaning |

**Key evaluative points**
- Both are **structural** theories that underplay how believers interpret religion. Peter Berger's
  *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) offers a phenomenological alternative: religion provides a "canopy" of
  meaning that makes the world feel ordered and secure. This still supports order, but explains it
  through individuals' need for meaning rather than society's needs.
- Both see religion as **conservative**, so both are challenged by evidence that religion drives
  change.
- Feminists argue both ignore **patriarchy**: religion may maintain gender inequality (12.2).
- Secularisation and religious diversity (heading 12) weaken both theories' assumption of a single
  dominant religion.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that religion maintains social order.*

> Durkheim argued that collective rituals create collective effervescence, reinforcing the shared
> values that hold society together. **[AO1]** National ceremonies in many modern societies, where
> religious and civic symbols combine, suggest this still happens. **[AO2: application]** However,
> Worsley showed that Durkheim's own evidence was weak, and Marxists argue that the "order" religion
> maintains is one of inequality. **[AO3: criticism and alternative]** Billings's study of Kentucky
> miners shows that the same religion can also fuel resistance. **[AO3: evidence]** Religion often
> supports order, but whose order and for how long depends on who controls religious institutions.
> **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Quoting "opium of the people" without explaining what Marx meant by it.
- Presenting Durkheim without the steps of his argument (sacred, totem, effervescence, solidarity).
- Forgetting that both theories see religion as conservative.
- Writing two separate descriptions instead of comparing them, which the third syllabus point asks
  for.
- Ignoring neo-Marxist complexity.

## Exam technique

- For **explain** questions, give the mechanism (ritual creates collective conscience; compensation
  dulls resistance) and an example.
- For **evaluate** questions, set functionalism and Marxism against each other, then bring in
  neo-Marxist, interactionist and feminist criticisms.
- Link forward to 11.3: Weber and liberation theology are the main evidence that religion can
  cause change rather than maintain order.
