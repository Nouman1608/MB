## Section A: the aspect in the set texts

**1.** Explain why disguise comedy depends on dramatic irony. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what Kate Hardcastle's disguise allows her to learn in *She Stoops to Conquer*, and why
this matters to the comic resolution. **[3]**

**3.** Identify two ways in which a discovery can be staged in comedy, with an example of each from
different set texts. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how "The Nun's Priest's Tale" both mocks and takes seriously the supernatural power of
dreams. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the escape in "Tam o' Shanter" is comic rather than frightening. **[2]**

## Section B: original extracts and essay skills

**Extract 1 (original drama).** A young woman, Lettice, has disguised herself as a footman to enter
the house of the man she is to marry, Sir Gervase, whom she has never met.

> **SIR GERVASE:** You, boy. What do you think of my intended?
> **LETTICE:** I have not seen her, sir.
> **SIR GERVASE:** Nor I. They say she reads. Imagine it. A wife
> who reads would always know what I had meant to say.
> **LETTICE:** A dreadful thing, sir.
> **SIR GERVASE:** You understand me. Fetch the wine, and stay.
> A man should have one person in the house who listens.
> **LETTICE:** *(aside)* He has two, if he but knew it.

**Extract 2 (original verse).** A farmer walks home past a graveyard at midnight.

> He'd heard the stories: Hob had seen a light,
> and Jen had heard the bell without a hand.
> So Davy sang, to show he felt no fright,
> the loudest hymn the parish ever planned,
> and ran the last half-mile, and swore next day
> the ghost had chased him nearly all the way.

**6.** Analyse how Extract 1 uses disguise to create comedy. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how Extract 1 prepares for a future discovery, and suggest how that discovery might
change Sir Gervase. **[3]**

**8.** Analyse how Extract 2 combines the supernatural with comedy. **[5]**

**9.** Explain one connection and one difference between Extract 2 and "Tam o' Shanter" as treatments
of the supernatural. **[4]**

**10.** A student writes: "Viola is disguised as a boy, which is funny." Explain two ways to develop
this into an analytical point. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer on your two non-Shakespeare texts for the question: "In comedy, disguise
reveals more than it hides." List four elements of your plan. **[4]**

**12.** Explain how context could be used (AO3) in an essay on disguise in one studied text. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The audience knows the disguised character's true identity while other characters do not [1],
so their mistakes, misplaced feelings and double meanings become funny because the audience can see
the gap [1].

**2.** Because Marlow takes her for a barmaid, he speaks freely and shows his confident self [1], so
Kate learns the real man behind his shyness with women of his own rank [1]; this allows her to win
him, and the marriage resolves the play on the basis of true knowledge [1].

**3.** Two methods, each [1] for the method and [1] for a correct example from a different text: for
example, recognition by evidence (Viola and Sebastian questioning each other about their father in
*Twelfth Night*); recognition by object (the handbag in *The Importance of Being Earnest*); discovery
as humiliation (Marlow's discovery of Kate's identity); self-discovery (Emma's recognition of her
errors).

**4.** The long, learned debate between Chauntecleer and Pertelote, with its examples and remedies, is
mock-heroic and comic [1]; Pertelote's practical explanation and Chauntecleer's pompous scholarship
make the argument absurd [1]; yet the dream comes true when the fox appears, so the tale does not
simply dismiss the supernatural [1].

**5.** The danger is undercut by farce: Tam's own drunken shout gives him away [1], and the narrow escape
ends with the mare losing her tail rather than with harm to Tam, so the ending is undignified rather
than tragic [1].

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- Dramatic irony: Sir Gervase talks to his intended wife without knowing it.
- "You, boy" shows how completely the disguise works, and invites laughter at his blindness.
- "I have not seen her, sir" is literally true and comically evasive.
- His fear of a wife who "reads" exposes his vanity and his fear of being understood.
- The joke that she "would always know what I had meant to say" is self-revealing, as he admits his
  speech is unclear.
- "A dreadful thing, sir" is ironic agreement, which the audience hears as mockery.
- "You understand me" deepens the irony: the person who understands him is the one he fears.
- The aside "He has two, if he but knew it" shares the joke directly with the audience.

**7.** The scene establishes that he values being listened to and fears an intelligent wife [1], so the
discovery that the sympathetic footman is Lettice will force him to admit she understands him [1];
it could teach him to value the qualities he mocked, a journey towards knowledge and happiness [1].

**8.** Any five, [1] each: the hearsay of "Hob" and "Jen" builds the ghost from gossip, not evidence;
the light and the bell "without a hand" are classic signs of haunting presented as rumour; Davy's
loud hymn is a comic show of courage that reveals his fear; the exaggeration "the loudest hymn the
parish ever planned" mocks him gently; the shift to running "the last half-mile" undercuts his
bravado; the final couplet's boast that the ghost chased him reveals that the supernatural exists
mainly in his telling.

**9.** Connection [2]: both present a man alone at night meeting, or fearing, the supernatural [1], and
both use a comic narrator's voice that invites laughter at the man's fear or folly [1]. Difference
[2]: in "Tam o' Shanter" the witches are presented as really seen [1], whereas Extract 2 suggests the
ghost may be invented, so its supernatural is doubtful [1].

**10.** Two developments, each [2] for a developed point or [1] for a partial one: analyse the dramatic
irony of Viola wooing Olivia for Orsino while loving him herself; analyse a specific scene and its
language, such as her speeches to Olivia or her double meanings with Orsino; consider how the
disguise lets her speak more freely than she could as a woman; use the context of boy actors to
discuss layered identity.

**11.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis on whether disguise reveals or hides; evidence from both texts
organised by point of connection; a paragraph on what disguise reveals about the disguised character
or those deceived; a paragraph weighing what disguise conceals or protects; relevant context for both
texts; an alternative interpretation; a conclusion comparing the two texts' discoveries.

**12.** A relevant context identified [1] and linked to the effect of disguise in the text [1]: for
example, Victorian respectability explains why Wilde's young men need double lives, so *Earnest*
satirises a society that values appearance over truth.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming disguises without analysing the irony or the gap they create.
- Treating the supernatural as setting rather than asking how far the text believes in it.
- Describing discovery as a single moment instead of analysing how it is delayed and staged.
- In Section C, writing about each text in turn.

For full practice, choose a scene of disguise from your Shakespeare play and write a 25-mark Section A
response in about 50 minutes.
