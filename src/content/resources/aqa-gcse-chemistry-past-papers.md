This page is a guide to AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) past papers for the June 2023, June 2024 and June 2025 series. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from AQA's grade-boundary documents, and a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from AQA's reports on the examination. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on AQA's own site, linked below.

AQA publishes question papers, mark schemes and reports on the examination on the assessment resources page for the subject.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the report on the examination for that paper: it explains why answers that looked reasonable still lost marks. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. GCSE Chemistry 8462 is a linear, tiered qualification first taught in September 2016 and first examined in 2018. Each tier sits its own Paper 1 and Paper 2, each 1 hour 45 minutes and 100 marks, worth 50 per cent of the GCSE. Paper 1 covers topics 1 to 5 (atomic structure and the periodic table; bonding, structure and the properties of matter; quantitative chemistry; chemical changes; energy changes) and Paper 2 covers topics 6 to 10 (the rate and extent of chemical change; organic chemistry; chemical analysis; chemistry of the atmosphere; using resources). Check the specification version on the front cover or in the specification itself before relying on a question from an older paper.

## June 2025

The June 2025 reports say that many candidates gave only part of an answer where the specification statement is short and clear. Atomic models, the structure of graphite, ionic bonding, extraction by electrolysis and the energy and rate ideas in Paper 2 all show the same pattern. Stronger candidates used exact terms (atoms, ions, molecules and outer-shell electrons) and compared properly when asked to. Weaker candidates repeated the question in different words, mixed up particle types or left parts blank where no answer lines were printed.

**Where marks were lost**

- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: describing where non-metals sit in the periodic table, why Mendeleev regrouped elements by their properties, and what an isotope is (atoms of the same element, not elements). Argon's stability was explained only by restating the question.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: the structure of graphite (giant covalent layers, delocalised electrons, no bonds between layers), and why small molecules do not conduct. The word "free" was used where "delocalised" was needed.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: reduction linked to the substance that loses oxygen, not the product; atom economy used without recalling the expression; the role of cryolite in aluminium extraction, and the half-equation at the electrode.
- Paper 8462/1H: titration improvements, concentration calculations that used the mole ratio the wrong way round, and precise language for nanoparticles and for the attraction between nucleus and outer electrons.
- Papers 8462/2F and 2H: flame test and precipitate tests, the test for alkenes, heating to constant mass, equilibrium position under changed conditions, and condensation polymers and thermosetting structures. Rate explanations needed collision frequency, not just "more collisions".

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports pick out precise language as the main dividing line. Candidates who named the right particle, gave a comparison when one was needed and linked cause to effect scored well. Many others lost marks by treating covalent bonds as weak, mixing up intermolecular forces with bonds, or listing a deduction (such as "gas produced") instead of an observation. Practical skills appeared on both tiers: salt preparation, titration, chromatography and electrochemical cells.

**Where marks were lost**

- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: observations for Group 1 metals in water, and the electronic structure of sodium and potassium; "outer shell" means the single outermost shell, so "more outer shells" was wrong.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: preparing a salt from an insoluble base, including how to know the reaction has finished, why to filter, and a water bath or electric heater rather than a bare flame.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: describing ionic bonding in terms of atoms and ions, why ions must be able to move for electrolysis, and the models of the atom. Isotopes were again described loosely.
- Paper 8462/1H: planning an investigation with an electrochemical cell (two different metals, the same pair each time), the temperature-change experiment, and quantitative energy and mole calculations with correct unit conversions.
- Papers 8462/2F and 2H: tests for sodium and chloride ions, gas tests, the Haber process recycling step, reaction profiles, and extended answers on life-cycle comparison and water treatment where no judgement was given.

## June 2023

The June 2023 reports describe a paper that rewarded careful reading of the data supplied. Calculations with several steps separated candidates, and unit conversions between cm³ and dm³ were a repeat weakness. Recall of specification facts, such as the plum pudding model, peer review and the tests for ions, was patchy. Better answers gave specific tests with results; weaker ones named a test without the result, or the result without the test.

**Where marks were lost**

- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: the plum pudding model and the order in which particles were discovered, why new results need peer review, and using the position of an element in the periodic table instead of working out its electron arrangement.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: titration equipment and calculations, with volumes not converted to dm³; also the meaning of uncertainty as a range about the mean, and random error.
- Papers 8462/1F and 1H: evaluating metals for different uses, linking alloy structure to conductivity, redox in terms of oxygen as well as electrons, and atom economy.
- Papers 8462/2F and 2H: the greenhouse effect, sulfur dioxide and particulates, fuel comparisons, and the tests for hydrogen and oxygen gases.
- Papers 8462/2F and 2H: planning a flame test and halide test for a medicine, naming flame emission spectroscopy in full, and equilibrium questions where the number of moles on each side was the same.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find AQA GCSE Chemistry 8462 past papers?**
AQA publishes papers, mark schemes and reports on the examination on the assessment resources page for 8462, and offers a past paper finder on its website.

**Which 8462 papers should I sit when practising a series?**
Sit the two papers for your tier: Paper 1 and Paper 2, Foundation or Higher. Each is 1 hour 45 minutes and 100 marks. Mark them with the official mark scheme.

**How many sets of thresholds are there each year?**
For the three series on this page there is one set per year, because the papers were sat in June.

**Why are there no grade 9 to 6 boundaries for Foundation Tier?**
In the board's documents the Foundation Tier has boundaries for grades 5 to 1 and the Higher Tier for grades 9 to 3, so the tables show a dash where a tier has none.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
