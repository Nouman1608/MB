Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.7 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.
**Case study required:** a rapidly growing urban area in a developing country **and migration
to it**.

## Definitions first

- **Urbanisation** = rising *proportion* of people living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth** = rising *size or population* of urban areas.
- **Rural depopulation** = falling rural population, mainly through out-migration.

## Why cities grow rapidly

**Rural–urban migration + natural increase** (migrants are young adults, so city birth rates
are high). Learn push and pull factors in the syllabus's three groups:

| | Push (rural) | Pull (urban) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Drought, desertification, soil erosion, floods, hazards | Fewer harvest-related risks |
| **Economic** | Low farm incomes, mechanisation, small plots, debt | Factory, construction, service and informal jobs; higher wages |
| **Social** | Few schools and clinics, no electricity, conflict | Better education and health care, family networks, "bright lights" |

**Chain migration:** earlier migrants help relatives follow, so districts fill with people from
the same region.

## Impacts on rural areas

- **+** Remittances; less pressure on land; returning skills.
- **−** Loss of young adults (often men); ageing, female-dominated population; labour
  shortage; abandoned farmland; services close as threshold populations fall.

## Impacts on urban areas

- **People:** housing shortage and squatter settlements; unemployment, underemployment and
  informal work; overloaded water, sanitation, schools, clinics, transport; disease; crime.
  Also: labour supply and economic growth.
- **Natural environment:** air and water pollution; solid waste; loss of farmland, forest and
  wetland to sprawl; landslides on built-up slopes; faster runoff and flooding.

## Squatter settlements: describe with a range of features

Illegal or insecure tenure · self-built from scrap, improved over time · marginal land (steep
slopes, flood plains, beside railways, dumps, city edge) · very high density · narrow unpaved
alleys · little piped water, sanitation, electricity or rubbish collection · active informal
economy. Local names: favelas (Brazil), katchi abadis (Pakistan), bustees (India).

## Strategies and their limits

| Strategy | Key limitation |
|---|---|
| Self-help (materials, loans) | Quality depends on residents' time and skill |
| Site-and-service | Plots may be far from jobs or too costly for the poorest |
| In-situ upgrading (e.g. Favela-Bairro, Rio, 1990s) | Expensive; rents may rise |
| Community sanitation (e.g. Orangi Pilot Project, Karachi, 1980) | Needs strong community organisation |
| Mass transit (e.g. Lagos BRT, 2008) | High cost; incomplete coverage |
| Rural development | Slow; needs long-term funding |
| New towns / satellite cities | Jobs may not move; long commutes |

**Tackle causes vs manage effects:** rural development reduces push factors; upgrading and
transport deal with the results. Demolition usually just moves the problem.

## Case study outline: Karachi, Pakistan

- **Context**: the country's biggest city and chief seaport, on the Arabian Sea; it grew very fast after
  Partition in 1947, when large numbers of migrants arrived from India.
- **Migration to it since**: people from rural Sindh, Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and refugees
  from Afghanistan. Pulls: port, industrial, construction and service jobs, and the informal
  sector. Pushes: small landholdings, low farm incomes, lack of services, floods and drought.
- **Impacts on the city**: large **katchi abadis** such as Orangi Town, often on the city edge;
  water shortages and tanker dependence; overloaded sewers and drains, so streets flood in heavy
  monsoon rain; congestion and air pollution; a large informal economy.
- **Responses**: the **Orangi Pilot Project** (from 1980) helped residents plan, pay for and build
  their own lane sewers, cutting costs by using local labour; regularisation of tenure in some
  settlements; the **Green Line** bus rapid transit (opened 2021).
- **Evaluation**: community schemes are cheap and lasting but depend on strong local
  organisation; transport and water projects are slow and costly compared with the speed of
  growth.

## Worked example: interpreting urban data

*Table 1 shows the urban population of Country Z rising from 20% in 1970 to 45% in 2020. Describe
the change.* Model answer: "The share of people living in urban areas more than doubled, from
20% in 1970 to 45% in 2020, an increase of 25 percentage points. This shows rapid urbanisation,
although most people still lived in rural areas in 2020."

Note: the change is in **percentage points**, not "25%". Quote the start, the end and the change.

## Model paragraph: impacts on the urban area

"Rapid migration means that housing cannot be built fast enough, so many migrants build homes in
squatter settlements on marginal land such as flood plains. These lack piped water and
sanitation, so diseases such as diarrhoea spread easily, especially in the monsoon. Many migrants
find only informal work, which is insecure and untaxed, so the city cannot raise enough money to
provide services."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Push factor | Something that encourages people to leave an area |
| Pull factor | Something that attracts people to an area |
| Remittance | Money sent home by a migrant |
| Informal sector | Unregistered, untaxed work without contracts or protection |
| Underemployment | Working fewer hours or at lower skill than a person wants or is able to |
| Squatter settlement | Housing built on land without the legal right to occupy it |
| Site-and-service scheme | Serviced plots supplied by authorities for residents to build on |
| Self-help scheme | Residents improve their own homes with supplied materials or loans |
| Chain migration | Migration following earlier migrants from the same place |
| Urbanisation | Rise in the proportion of people living in urban areas |
| Rural depopulation | Decline in the population of rural areas, mainly through out-migration |
| Katchi abadi | Informal or squatter settlement in Pakistan |
| Bus rapid transit | Bus system using dedicated lanes and stations to move many people quickly |

## Exam traps

- Using "urbanisation" when the question means urban growth, or the reverse.
- Describing squatter settlements with only one feature.
- Generic impacts with no named city.
- Writing "%" for a change between two percentages; it is percentage points.
- Strategies named with no limitation or judgement.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between urbanisation and urban growth.
2. Give the two processes that together cause rapid urban growth.
3. Give one physical, one economic and one social push factor.
4. State two negative impacts of rural–urban migration on rural areas.
5. Describe three characteristics of squatter settlements.
6. Give one strategy that tackles a cause of urbanisation rather than an effect.
7. Why did Karachi grow very rapidly after 1947?
8. Explain how the Orangi Pilot Project improved sanitation.
9. Give one limitation of community-based schemes.
10. Urban share rises from 32% to 40%. Describe the change correctly.
11. Why do squatter settlements often develop on flood plains or steep slopes?

**Answers:** 1. Urbanisation is a rise in the proportion of people living in urban areas;
urban growth is a rise in the size or population of urban areas. 2. Rural–urban migration and
natural increase. 3. Physical: drought, flood or soil erosion. Economic: low farm incomes or
mechanisation. Social: lack of schools, clinics or electricity. 4. Any two of: loss of young
adults; labour shortage; ageing population; farmland abandoned; services close. 5. Any three
of: illegal tenure; self-built homes from scrap materials; on marginal land; high density;
lacking water, sanitation or electricity; informal economy. 6. Rural development, such as
irrigation or rural electrification, which reduces push factors. 7. Large numbers of migrants
arrived from India after Partition, and its port and industry attracted people from across
Pakistan. 8. Residents organised by lane, paid for and built their own sewers with local labour
and technical advice, so costs were far lower than official schemes. 9. They depend on strong
local organisation and do not solve city-wide problems such as main sewers or water supply.
10. It rose by **8 percentage points**, from 32% to 40%. 11. This land is left empty because it is
unsafe or unsuitable for formal building, so migrants can occupy it, often close to jobs.
