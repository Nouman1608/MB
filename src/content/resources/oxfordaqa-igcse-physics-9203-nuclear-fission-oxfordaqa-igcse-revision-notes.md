These notes condense section **3.7.3 Nuclear fission** (statements a to e) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statement b is marked P (full Physics award only). For the step-by-step explanation and chain reaction diagrams read the [fission study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-nuclear-fission-oxfordaqa-igcse/), then test yourself with the [fission practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-nuclear-fission-oxfordaqa-igcse-practice/).

## Condensed recall

**Fission (a).** The splitting of a large, unstable nucleus, with the release of energy.

**Fuels (b, P).** Uranium-235 and plutonium-239. Most reactors use uranium-235.

**The process (c).**

1. A uranium-235 or plutonium-239 nucleus absorbs a neutron.
2. The nucleus becomes unstable.
3. It splits into two smaller nuclei.
4. Two or three neutrons are released.
5. Energy is released, much more than in a chemical reaction involving a similar mass of material.

**Chain reaction (d).** Neutrons from one fission go on to cause further fissions.

**Control rods (d).** Absorb fission neutrons so that, on average, only one neutron per fission causes another fission. The reaction then proceeds at a steady rate.

- Rods lowered further → more neutrons absorbed → fewer fissions per second.
- Rods raised → fewer absorbed → more fissions per second.

**Waste (e).** Radioactive, possibly for a long time depending on half-lives and products. Disposal must be managed carefully, and this affects decisions about using nuclear power to generate electricity.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nuclear fission | Splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy |
| Fissionable substance | A material whose nuclei can undergo fission in a reactor (uranium-235, plutonium-239) |
| Fission products | The two smaller nuclei formed in fission |
| Chain reaction | Process in which neutrons from fissions cause further fissions |
| Control rods | Rods of neutron-absorbing material that control the rate of fission |
| Nuclear reactor | Device in which a controlled chain reaction releases energy |
| Radioactive waste | Used fuel and fission products that remain radioactive |

## Chain reaction counting

| Neutrons per fission causing new fission | Fissions in generations 1, 2, 3, 4 | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1, 3, 9, 27 | Grows very rapidly |
| 2 | 1, 2, 4, 8 | Grows rapidly |
| 1 (controlled) | 1, 1, 1, 1 | Steady energy output |

If each fission releases 3 neutrons, 2 of every 3 must be absorbed or lost for a steady reaction.

## Labelled diagram checklist

- An incoming neutron.
- A large nucleus labelled uranium-235.
- Two smaller nuclei (fission products).
- Two or three released neutrons, each leading to another uranium-235 nucleus.
- "Energy" labelled at each fission.
- At least two generations shown.

## Nuclear power: points to weigh

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| No carbon dioxide from burning fuel while operating | Long-lived radioactive waste needing careful disposal |
| Large energy from a small mass of fuel | Risk of accidents releasing radioactive material |
| Reliable output, not dependent on weather | Expensive to build and to dismantle |

## Fission or fusion?

| | Fission (this subtopic) | Fusion (next subtopic) |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A large nucleus splits | Two light nuclei join |
| How it starts | A neutron is absorbed | Very high temperature and pressure |
| Where it happens | Nuclear reactors | Stars |

## Answer templates

- **Describe fission:** "A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and becomes unstable. It splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing two or three neutrons and energy."
- **Chain reaction:** "The neutrons released go on to be absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei, which also split, releasing more neutrons."
- **Control rods:** "The control rods absorb neutrons, so that on average only one neutron from each fission causes another fission and the reaction is steady."
- **Waste:** "The waste is radioactive and some of it has a long half-life, so it must be stored securely for a very long time."

## Exam traps

- Absorption of the neutron comes **before** the split.
- Two smaller **nuclei**, two or three **neutrons**: do not swap them.
- Control rods **absorb** neutrons.
- Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei.
- "Much more energy than a chemical reaction" must refer to a **similar mass** of material.

## Quick self-test

1. Define nuclear fission.
2. Name the two fissionable substances in common use in reactors.
3. Which is used in most reactors?
4. What must happen to a uranium-235 nucleus before it can undergo fission?
5. How many neutrons are released in a typical fission?
6. What is a chain reaction?
7. What do control rods do?
8. Explain why lowering control rods reduces the power output.
9. On average, how many neutrons per fission should cause further fission in a steady reactor?
10. If each fission caused 3 more, how many fissions would there be in the fourth generation?
11. Why is nuclear waste difficult to dispose of?
12. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power.

### Answers

1. The splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller nuclei, with the release of energy.
2. Uranium-235 and plutonium-239.
3. Uranium-235.
4. It must absorb a neutron, which makes it unstable.
5. Two or three.
6. Neutrons released by fissions go on to cause further fissions.
7. Absorb fission neutrons so that, on average, only one neutron per fission causes another fission.
8. More neutrons are absorbed, so fewer fissions occur each second and energy is released more slowly.
9. One.
10. 1, 3, 9, 27: **27**.
11. It is radioactive and some of it remains dangerous for a very long time, so it must be stored securely for many years.
12. Advantage: no carbon dioxide from burning fuel (or large energy from a small mass of fuel). Disadvantage: long-lived radioactive waste (or accident risk, or high cost).
