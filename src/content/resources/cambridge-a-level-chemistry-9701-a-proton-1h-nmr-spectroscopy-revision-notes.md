## What each feature tells you

| Feature | Tells you | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Number of signals | number of proton environments | equivalent protons give one signal |
| Chemical shift δ | what the protons are attached to or next to | read from the printed data table |
| Relative peak area | ratio of protons in each environment | scale to the molecular formula |
| Splitting | protons on the adjacent carbon(s): n + 1 lines | named only as singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet or multiplet |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proton environment | A set of hydrogen atoms in the same structural position |
| Integration trace | Stepped line whose step heights are proportional to the number of protons in each signal |
| n + 1 rule | A signal splits into n + 1 lines, n = number of protons on adjacent carbon atoms |
| Multiplet | The 9701 name for any signal with more than four lines or with non-equivalent neighbours |
| TMS | Tetramethylsilane, the δ = 0 reference |
| Deuterated solvent | A solvent such as CDCl₃ in which ¹H is replaced by deuterium, so it gives no proton signal |
| Proton exchange | Replacement of an O–H or N–H proton by deuterium when the sample is shaken with D₂O |

## Shift regions in brief

- **0.9–1.7:** alkyl protons with nothing special nearby.
- **2.2–3.0:** alkyl next to C=O; **2.3–3.0:** alkyl next to a benzene ring.
- **3.2–4.0:** alkyl next to O or Cl.
- **4.5–6.0:** protons on an alkene carbon.
- **6.0–9.0:** protons on a benzene ring.
- **9.3–10.5:** aldehyde CHO; **9.0–13.0:** carboxylic acid COOH.
- **O–H and N–H:** wide, variable ranges (alcohol 0.5–6.0, phenol 4.5–7.0, alkyl amine 1.0–5.0, aryl amine
  3.0–6.0, amide 5.0–12.0).

## Splitting rules to apply every time

1. Count protons on **all** adjacent carbons, both sides.
2. Do not count protons in the **same** environment.
3. A neighbouring O atom or C=O carbon has no H, so it contributes nothing: OCH₃ and CH₃CO are singlets.
4. O–H and N–H protons usually neither split nor are split: broad singlets.
5. More than three neighbours, or two different sets of neighbours: **multiplet**.

## Predicted spectra to recognise

| Compound | Signals: region, area, pattern |
|---|---|
| propanone, CH₃COCH₃ | 2.2–3.0, 6H, singlet |
| chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl | 0.9–1.7, 3H, triplet; 3.2–4.0, 2H, quartet |
| butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃ | 2.2–3.0, 3H, singlet; 2.2–3.0, 2H, quartet; 0.9–1.7, 3H, triplet |
| ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH | 2.2–3.0, 3H, singlet; 9.0–13.0, 1H, singlet |
| methylbenzene, C₆H₅CH₃ | 2.3–3.0, 3H, singlet; 6.0–9.0, 5H, multiplet |
| ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ | 0.9–1.7, 3H, triplet; CH₂ 2H, quartet; 1.0–5.0, 2H, broad singlet |
| 1-chloropropane, CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl | 0.9–1.7, 3H, triplet; 2H, multiplet; 3.2–4.0, 2H, triplet |

## D₂O, TMS and CDCl₃

- **D₂O shake:** run the spectrum, add D₂O, shake, run again. The O–H or N–H signal **disappears** because
  the proton is exchanged for deuterium. C–H signals stay.
- **TMS:** the reference set at δ = 0; it gives one sharp signal because its twelve protons are equivalent.
- **CDCl₃:** used because an ordinary hydrogen-containing solvent would swamp the sample's signals.

## Exam traps

- Writing "three neighbours" when the question asks for the pattern; write "quartet".
- Counting neighbours on only one side of a CH₂ group.
- Splitting the CH₃ of a methyl ester or ethanoate, which are singlets.
- Telling methyl propanoate from ethyl ethanoate by splitting alone; the shift of the quartet decides.
- Leaving an area ratio unscaled: 1 : 1.5 must become 2 : 3.
- Forgetting the aromatic integration: five ring protons means one substituent, four means two.
- Describing the D₂O test without saying which signal disappears.

## Self-test

1. What does the area under a signal tell you?
2. Integration steps measure 12 mm and 18 mm for a compound with 10 hydrogens. Give the number of protons in
   each environment.
3. Predict the splitting of each signal in chloroethane.
4. Why is the CH₃ signal of propanone a singlet?
5. Give the δ region and splitting for the CHO proton of propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO.
6. Name the pattern for the CH proton in 2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CH, using 9701 terminology.
7. A compound has a 5H signal in 6.0–9.0 and a 3H singlet in 2.2–3.0. Suggest a structure with formula C₈H₈O.
8. How would methyl propanoate and ethyl ethanoate be told apart by proton NMR?
9. Why is CDCl₃, not CHCl₃, used as the solvent?
10. Describe how D₂O identifies the OH signal in propan-1-ol.
11. Which signals in ethylamine change after shaking with D₂O?
12. How many signals does 1,4-dimethylbenzene give, and in what area ratio?

### Answers

1. The **relative number of protons** in that environment, as a ratio with the other signals.
2. 12 : 18 = 2 : 3, total 5; doubled to match 10 hydrogens: **4H and 6H**.
3. CH₃: **triplet** (two neighbouring CH₂ protons). CH₂: **quartet** (three neighbouring CH₃ protons).
4. Its neighbour is the **C=O carbon**, which has no protons, so n = 0 (and the two CH₃ groups are
   equivalent).
5. **9.3–10.5 ppm, triplet**, because the adjacent CH₂ has two protons.
6. **Multiplet**: it has nine neighbouring protons.
7. **Phenylethanone, C₆H₅COCH₃**: one substituent on the ring (5H) and a CH₃ next to C=O with no neighbouring
   protons.
8. Both show a triplet, quartet and singlet in a 3 : 2 : 3 ratio, but the **quartet's shift** differs: 2.2–3.0
   (CH₂ next to C=O) in methyl propanoate, 3.2–4.0 (CH₂ bonded to O) in ethyl ethanoate.
9. CHCl₃ contains ¹H and would give a large signal that hides the sample's peaks; **deuterium gives no proton
   signal**.
10. Record the spectrum, shake the sample with D₂O and record it again; the **broad OH singlet disappears**
    because the O–H proton is exchanged for deuterium; the C–H signals are unchanged.
11. Only the **NH₂ signal** disappears; the CH₃ and CH₂ signals remain.
12. **Two** signals: CH₃ protons 6H and ring protons 4H, ratio **3 : 2**.
