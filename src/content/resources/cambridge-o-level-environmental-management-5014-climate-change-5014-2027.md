Sub-topic 4.2 Climate change is the largest part of Topic 4 in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029, and a new heading in this edition. It has nine statements that follow a clear sequence: what a greenhouse gas is (4.2.1 and 4.2.2), why concentrations are rising (4.2.3), how that causes warming (4.2.4), the impacts (4.2.5), ways to reduce carbon footprints (4.2.6), adaptation (4.2.7 and 4.2.8), and a discussion of the strategies (4.2.9). If you can follow that sequence in one answer, you can tackle almost any question on this sub-topic.

## Greenhouse gases

The syllabus defines a **greenhouse gas** as a gas that absorbs radiation and emits the energy as thermal (heat) energy. You must be able to state three examples: **carbon dioxide (CO2)**, **water vapour (H2O)** and **methane (CH4)**. Learn the formulae as well as the names, because a question may use either.

## Why concentrations are increasing

Statement 4.2.3 lists six causes. For each, know the gas and the process.

| Cause | Gas | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Combustion of fossil fuels | Carbon dioxide | Coal, oil and gas are burned in power stations, vehicles, industry and homes |
| Agriculture | Methane | Digestion by cattle, decomposition of manure and flooded rice fields |
| Deforestation | Carbon dioxide | Fewer trees remove less carbon dioxide by photosynthesis; burning cleared forest also releases it |
| Changes in land use | Methane | Draining wetlands releases methane |
| Cement manufacture | Carbon dioxide | Calcium carbonate (limestone) is heated to produce calcium oxide (lime), releasing carbon dioxide |
| Increasing human population and energy use | Carbon dioxide | More people using more energy, much of it from fossil fuels |

Deforestation is the one that works differently: it is about **less removal** of carbon dioxide rather than a new source, so describe it carefully. Cement manufacture is another frequent question, because the carbon dioxide comes from the chemical change in the limestone as well as from the fuel used to heat it.

## From more gas to climate change

Statement 4.2.4 is a two-step chain:

1. Increased concentrations of greenhouse gases absorb more radiation, leading to the **enhanced greenhouse effect** and **global warming**, a rise in the average temperature of the Earth.
2. Global warming can result in **climate change**, a long-term change in temperature, rainfall and other weather patterns.

Keep the terms in order. Global warming is the temperature rise; climate change is the wider, long-term change it causes.

## The impacts of climate change

The syllabus lists twelve impacts. They are easier to explain if you see how they link.

**Temperature and ice**
- Earth's surface temperature increases.
- Ocean surface temperature increases. (You do not need to know about El Niño, La Niña or ENSO.)
- Ice sheets, glaciers and permafrost melt. Water from melting land ice flows into the oceans, so sea level rises.

**Ocean chemistry**
- Ocean acidification: more carbon dioxide in the atmosphere means more dissolves in sea water, making it more acidic. This makes it harder for corals and shelled organisms to build their skeletons and shells.

**Living things**
- Disruption to food chains, for example when the timing of plankton growth no longer matches the needs of fish larvae.
- Change in biodiversity, as species that cannot cope decline and others spread.
- Loss of habitat, such as sea ice for animals that hunt on it, or coastal wetlands drowned by rising seas.
- Increased pest outbreaks and invasive species, as warmer conditions let pests survive winters and spread to new areas.

**People**
- Forced migration of humans and other animals away from areas that become too hot, dry or flooded.
- Increased frequency and severity of extreme weather events, leading to flooding and loss of land, drought and wildfires.
- Change in crop yields: some cooler regions may gain a longer growing season, but many regions lose yield to heat, drought or flooding.
- Food shortages, the end of a chain from extreme weather, pests and falling yields.

When you explain an impact, use a chain: cause → change → consequence. For example: higher ocean temperatures → coral stress and acidification → coral death → loss of reef fish → less food and income for coastal communities.

## Reducing carbon footprints

A **carbon footprint** is the total amount of greenhouse gases released by a person, organisation, product or country. Statement 4.2.6 lists twelve strategies. They operate at different scales.

**Individuals and households**
- Reduce the use of fossil fuels, for example by walking, cycling or using public transport.
- Make more sustainable food choices: a plant-based diet, seasonal food, and locally produced food, which needs less transport and fewer heated greenhouses.
- Increase energy efficiency with insulation, efficient lighting and appliances.

**Farming and land**
- Reduce livestock farming, cutting methane from digestion and manure and reducing land cleared for grazing.
- Reforestation (replanting trees where forest was lost) and afforestation (planting trees where there was no forest) remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis.

**Population**
- A reduction in the number of children per woman slows population growth and so the growth in energy demand.

**Technology and science**
- Carbon sequestration: capture, remove and store carbon dioxide, for example from power station chimneys, storing it underground.
- Research and climate models predict future conditions and test strategies.
- Artificial intelligence (AI) can be used to predict the impacts of climate change and to develop strategies to reduce them.

**Governments**
- Transport policies, such as investment in public transport, cycle lanes and low-emission zones.
- International agreements and policies that commit countries to emission targets.
- Taxation, such as taxes on fuel or on carbon emissions, which make high-emission choices more expensive.

## Adaptation

The syllabus defines **climate change adaptation** as altering behaviour, practices and infrastructure to adapt to the impacts of climate change. Strategies that reduce emissions tackle the cause; adaptation deals with the effects. The four named adaptation strategies are:

- **Crops that resist warmer, drier or wetter conditions**, so farmers keep harvests.
- **Improved flood defences**, such as sea walls, raised embankments and restored coastal wetlands.
- **Legislation about building near coasts and on floodplains**, so new homes are not built where flooding will increase.
- **Building design and materials**, such as raised floors, shading, reflective roofs and materials that cope with heat or flooding.

## Worked example: a household carbon footprint

*All figures, including emission factors, are invented for practice.*

| Source | Use | Emission factor | Emissions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electricity | 3,600 kWh | 0.5 kg CO2 per kWh | 1,800 kg |
| Car travel | 8,000 km | 0.2 kg CO2 per km | 1,600 kg |
| One return flight | 1 | 900 kg CO2 | 900 kg |
| **Total** | | | **4,300 kg = 4.3 tonnes** |

If the household switches half its electricity to a renewable supply with no emissions, electricity emissions fall by 1,800 × 0.5 = 900 kg. The new total is 4,300 − 900 = 3,400 kg. The percentage reduction is 900 ÷ 4,300 × 100 = 20.9%. Replacing the car journeys with public transport, or dropping the flight, could cut the footprint further.

## Discussing the strategies (4.2.9)

A balanced discussion usually weighs scale, speed, cost and fairness.

- **International agreements and taxation** can cut emissions across whole economies, but agreements depend on every country keeping its promises, and taxes can fall hardest on low-income households.
- **Individual choices** such as diet and energy saving are cheap and immediate, but their effect is small unless many people take part.
- **Reforestation and afforestation** remove carbon dioxide and bring other benefits, such as habitats and flood control, but trees take decades to grow and need land that may be wanted for farming.
- **Carbon sequestration, climate models and AI** can be powerful, but they are expensive and depend on investment, data and expertise.
- **Adaptation** protects people now, but it does not reduce emissions, and the costs of sea walls and new crops are hardest for poorer countries to meet.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Discuss the benefits and limitations of afforestation as a strategy to manage the impacts of climate change.* **[6]**

> Planting trees on land that was not forest removes carbon dioxide from the air through photosynthesis, so it reduces the enhanced greenhouse effect. ✔
> It also provides new habitats and reduces soil erosion and flooding, so there are benefits beyond climate. ✔
> However, trees take many years to grow, so the carbon dioxide removed in the first years is small. ✔
> Land used for forest cannot be used for crops, which could reduce food supply where land is scarce. ✔
> If the forest is later burned or cleared, the stored carbon dioxide is released again. ✔
> Afforestation is useful as a long-term strategy, but it must be combined with reducing fossil fuel use, because it cannot remove carbon dioxide as fast as burning fuels releases it. ✔

## Common mistakes

- Using "global warming" and "climate change" as if they meant the same thing.
- Saying deforestation "releases methane". The syllabus links it to reduced carbon dioxide removal.
- Linking ozone depletion or CFCs to climate change in answers on this sub-topic.
- Describing adaptation strategies when the question asks how to reduce carbon footprints.
- Listing impacts without explaining how each follows from warming.

## Exam technique

**Define** and **state** need the syllabus wording and the three named gases. **Describe and explain** impacts need the link from warming to the impact. **Describe** strategies need what the strategy is and how it reduces emissions or helps people adapt. **Discuss** needs both sides and a judgement; naming the scale (individual, national, international) helps you organise the answer.
