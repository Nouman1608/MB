These questions cover part of topic 11, "Elementary ideas of probability", in the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: independent events, the multiplication rule, and selections made with or without replacement. The course is not tiered. Learn the method first from the [study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-independent-events-and-selection-with-or-without/) or the [revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-independent-events-and-selection-with-or-without-revision-notes/). Give answers as exact fractions or as decimals to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** The events A and B are independent, with P(A) = 0.6 and P(B) = 0.35. Find

**(a)** P(A ∩ B) **[1]**
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) **[2]**

**2.** A club has 60 members. 24 swim, 15 cycle and 6 do both. Show, by calculation, whether "swims" and "cycles" are independent events. **[3]**

**3.** A fair six-sided die is rolled and a fair coin is flipped. Find the probability of

**(a)** a six and a tail **[1]**
**(b)** neither a six nor a tail **[2]**

**4.** Each battery from a factory is defective with probability 0.03, independently of the others. Four batteries are chosen. Find the probability that, giving both answers to 3 significant figures,

**(a)** none is defective **[1]**
**(b)** at least one is defective **[2]**

**5.** A bag contains 4 black and 5 white counters. Two counters are picked with replacement. Draw a tree diagram, and find the probability that

**(a)** both are black **[2]**
**(b)** they are different colours **[2]**

**6.** A bag of 15 sweets holds 6 mint and 9 toffee sweets. Two sweets are taken at random without replacement. Find the probability that

**(a)** both are mint **[1]**
**(b)** one of each type is taken **[2]**

**7.** A drawer holds 8 socks: 5 grey and 3 navy. Three socks are taken without replacement. Find the probability that

**(a)** all three are grey **[2]**
**(b)** exactly two are grey **[2]**
**(c)** at least one is navy **[1]**

**8.** A class has 20 students, of whom 8 are girls. Two students are chosen as monitors. Find the probability that both are girls if

**(a)** the same student may be chosen twice **[2]**
**(b)** the two monitors must be different students **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why your answer to (b) is smaller than your answer to (a). **[1]**

**9.** A box holds 10 components, of which 3 are faulty. Components are tested one at a time, without replacement, until a faulty one is found. Find the probability that the first faulty component is found

**(a)** on the second test **[2]**
**(b)** on the third test **[2]**
**(c)** after more than three tests **[2]**

**10.** Bag A holds 4 red and 6 white counters. Bag B holds 3 red and 2 white counters. One counter is taken from bag A. Then two counters are taken from bag B without replacement. Find the probability that

**(a)** all three counters are red **[2]**
**(b)** exactly one of the three counters is red **[3]**
**(c)** at least two of the three counters are red **[3]**

**11.** A bag contains 10 counters, r of them red and the rest green. Two counters are taken without replacement, and the probability that both are red is 2/15.

**(a)** Show that r² − r − 12 = 0. **[2]**
**(b)** Solve the equation and state the value of r. **[2]**
**(c)** Using this value of r, find the probability that exactly one red counter is taken if the two counters are taken with replacement. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) P(A ∩ B) = 0.6 × 0.35 [1] = **0.21**. (b) P(A ∪ B) = 0.6 + 0.35 − 0.21 [1] = **0.74** [1].
*Examiner insight:* the addition rule for "or" needs the overlap subtracted, and a solution that adds 0.6 and 0.35 gives 0.95 and shows the rule has been misremembered.

**2.** P(swim) = 24/60 = 0.4 and P(cycle) = 15/60 = 0.25 [1]. The product is 0.4 × 0.25 = 0.1 [1]. P(swim and cycle) = 6/60 = 0.1, which equals the product, so the events are **independent** [1].
*Examiner insight:* a conclusion needs both values written out and a comparison stated in words. Saying "yes" with no figures, or comparing only one pair of numbers, leaves the argument incomplete.

**3.** (a) 1/6 × 1/2 [1] = **1/12**. (b) 5/6 × 1/2 [1] = **5/12** [1].
*Examiner insight:* for "neither", each factor is the chance of the event not happening. Using 1/6 × 1/2 again, or subtracting 1/12 from 1, answers a different question.

**4.** (a) 0.97⁴ [1] = **0.885**. (b) 1 − 0.97⁴ [1] = 1 − 0.8853 = **0.115** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "at least one" is quickest as 1 − P(none). Rounding the answer to part (a) before subtracting can shift the third significant figure of part (b), so keep the unrounded value in your working.

**5.** Tree: first pick B (4/9) or W (5/9); second pick B (4/9) or W (5/9) from each point, the same numbers because the counter is replaced [1]. (a) P(BB) = 4/9 × 4/9 = **16/81** [1]. (b) P(BW) + P(WB) = 4/9 × 5/9 + 5/9 × 4/9 [1] = 40/81 [1].
*Examiner insight:* "different colours" has two paths, black then white and white then black. An answer that includes only one path is a common loss of credit.

**6.** (a) 6/15 × 5/14 [1] = 30/210 = **1/7**. (b) 6/15 × 9/14 + 9/15 × 6/14 [1] = 54/210 + 54/210 = 108/210 = **18/35** [1].
*Examiner insight:* write the two orders separately before adding. If the second-stage denominator is 15 rather than 14, the working shows a replacement model was used where none was stated.

**7.** (a) 5/8 × 4/7 × 3/6 [1] = 60/336 = **5/28** [1]. (b) Orders GGN, GNG, NGG each have probability 5/8 × 4/7 × 3/6 = 60/336 [1], so the total is 3 × 60/336 = 180/336 = **15/28** [1]. (c) 1 − 5/28 = **23/28** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "at least one navy" is the complement of "all grey", which was found in part (a). Using that result is quicker than adding three separate cases, and a correctly identified complement keeps the working short.

**8.** (a) 8/20 × 8/20 = 64/400 [1] = **4/25** = 0.16 [1]. (b) 8/20 × 7/19 [1] = 56/380 = **14/95** ≈ 0.147 [1]. (c) Without replacement, once one girl has been chosen there are fewer girls and fewer students left, so the second choice is less likely to be a girl: 7/19 is less than 8/20 [1].
*Examiner insight:* an explanation scores when it refers to the changed numbers in the second pick. A statement such as "it is different" without saying why does not explain anything.

**9.** (a) Non-faulty then faulty: 7/10 × 3/9 [1] = 21/90 = **7/30** [1]. (b) Non-faulty, non-faulty, faulty: 7/10 × 6/9 × 3/8 [1] = 126/720 = **7/40** [1]. (c) More than three tests means the first three are all non-faulty: 7/10 × 6/9 × 5/8 [1] = 210/720 = **7/24** [1].
*Examiner insight:* each path must show the right number of "not yet faulty" factors before the faulty one. A useful check is that the probabilities of finding the first faulty component on test 1, 2, 3 and after three tests add to 1: 3/10 + 7/30 + 7/40 + 7/24 = 1.

**10.** (a) P(red from A) = 4/10 and P(both red from B) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 3/10 [1]. The stages are independent, so P = 4/10 × 3/10 = 12/100 = **3/25** [1]. (b) From bag B, P(no red) = 2/5 × 1/4 = 1/10 and P(exactly one red) = 3/5 × 2/4 + 2/5 × 3/4 = 6/20 + 6/20 = 3/5 [1]. Exactly one red overall is red from A with no red from B, or white from A with one red from B: 4/10 × 1/10 + 6/10 × 3/5 [1] = 4/100 + 36/100 = **2/5** [1]. (c) At least two red is: all three red, or red from A with exactly one red from B, or white from A with two red from B. Using 3/25 from part (a): red from A with one red from B = 4/10 × 3/5 = 6/25 [1]; white from A with two red from B = 6/10 × 3/10 = 9/50 [1]. Total = 6/50 + 12/50 + 9/50 = 27/50 [1]. So P = **27/50** = 0.54.
*Examiner insight:* in a multi-stage question, first find the probabilities for each source separately, then combine. A list of every case that fits the wording, with no overlap, is easier to mark and easier to check than one long expression.

**11.** (a) P(both red) = r/10 × (r − 1)/9 [1] = 2/15, so r(r − 1) = 90 × 2/15 = 12, giving r² − r − 12 = 0 [1]. (b) (r − 4)(r + 3) = 0 [1], so r = 4 or r = −3, and r must be positive, so **r = 4** [1]. (c) P(red) = 4/10 = 2/5 and P(green) = 3/5 on each pick; P(exactly one red) = 2 × 2/5 × 3/5 [1] = **12/25** [1].
*Examiner insight:* in a "show that" question every step from the first expression to the final equation must appear. When solving, rejecting the negative root with a reason is part of the answer, since a number of counters cannot be negative.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the same denominator for the second pick when the question says the counter is not replaced.
- Reducing the denominator but not the count of the colour just removed.
- Giving only one of the two orders for "one of each" or "different colours".
- Adding along a branch when the branch probabilities should be multiplied.
- Claiming independence without testing it, or testing it with figures that are not written down.
- Treating "mutually exclusive" as the same thing as "independent".
- Computing "at least one" by adding many cases and missing one, when 1 − P(none) is shorter.
- Rounding early, then giving a final answer that differs in the third significant figure.
- Giving a "show that" answer that jumps from the setup to the result.
- Writing an explanation that restates the answer instead of pointing to what changed between the first and second pick.
