## What quality means

Quality is not the same as luxury. A product is of good quality when it meets the customer's expectations for its price. A £1 pen that writes smoothly and lasts is good quality; a £50 pen that leaks is not.

### Expectations for goods

Customers expect goods to:

- work properly and do what they claim;
- last a reasonable time;
- look and feel right, with a good finish;
- be safe;
- match their description and be consistent, so each unit is the same as the last.

### Expectations for services

Services cannot be inspected before they are delivered, so quality depends on the people and systems providing them. Customers expect:

- reliability: the service happens when promised;
- speed: little waiting;
- friendly, knowledgeable staff;
- accuracy: the right order, the right bill;
- a clean, pleasant environment where relevant.

Consistency matters most for services. A customer who has a great meal on Friday and a poor one on Saturday at the same restaurant may not return.

## Identifying and measuring quality problems

Businesses find problems in several ways:

- **Inspection and testing:** checking samples of products or all of them at stages of production.
- **Customer complaints and returns:** a rise is an early warning.
- **Customer feedback and reviews:** surveys, comment cards and online reviews.
- **Mystery shoppers:** people paid to use a service as ordinary customers and report on it.
- **Staff reports:** workers who notice faults at their own stage.

Common measures include:

- Defect rate = number of faulty items ÷ total items produced × 100
- Complaint rate = number of complaints ÷ number of customers × 100
- Return rate = items returned ÷ items sold × 100
- Average review score, or the percentage of customers rating the service highly
- On-time rate for a service, such as deliveries arriving when promised

A single figure means little alone. Compare it with the business's target, with last month, or with rivals.

## Consequences of quality problems

- **Returns, refunds and replacements**, which add costs.
- **Waste:** faulty items scrapped or reworked.
- **Lost sales**, as customers switch to rivals.
- **Damaged reputation**, spread by word of mouth and online reviews.
- **Product recalls** if faulty goods are unsafe: collecting, replacing and possibly destroying stock, plus publicity costs.
- **Lower staff morale** when staff deal with complaints all day.

## Total quality management (TQM)

TQM is an approach in which every employee, in every department, is responsible for quality at every stage. The aim is to get things right first time and to keep improving, rather than to find faults at the end.

**How it differs from relying on inspection at the end:**

| | Inspection at the end | TQM |
|---|---|---|
| Who is responsible | Quality inspectors | Every employee |
| When faults are found | After the product is made | At each stage, as they occur |
| Main aim | Stop faulty items reaching customers | Stop faults happening at all |
| Effect on waste | Faulty items already made are scrapped | Fewer faulty items are made |

**Advantages of TQM:**

- Fewer defects, so less waste and lower costs over time.
- Quality is consistent, building reputation and loyalty.
- Fewer complaints, returns and recalls.
- Staff involvement can raise motivation.
- Quality reaches every department, including customer service and finance.

**Drawbacks:**

- Training every employee costs money and time.
- It takes time to change the culture of a business.
- It only works if all staff are committed.

## Costs and benefits of maintaining quality

The specification lists seven items. Sort them as you write.

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Additional sales: satisfied customers return and recommend | Inspection costs: staff time, testing equipment |
| Image/reputation: a name for quality attracts customers | Staff training: teaching employees to work to standard |
| Higher price: customers pay more for reliable quality | The provision of services: extra staff or time to keep service high |
| Avoiding product recalls: fewer faults means fewer costly recalls | Product recalls: a cost when quality fails |

Product recalls appear on both sides depending on the argument: spending on quality is partly a way of avoiding the much larger cost of a recall.

## Worked example: is quality worth paying for?

Figures invented. Linwood Lamps makes 20,000 desk lamps a year, selling at £35. Its defect rate is 4%, and each faulty lamp costs £22 in replacement and handling.

- Faulty lamps = 4% of 20,000 = 800.
- Cost of faults = 800 × £22 = £17,600 a year.

Linwood could introduce TQM, with training costing £9,000 in the first year and £2,000 a year after that. It expects the defect rate to fall to 1%.

- Faulty lamps = 1% of 20,000 = 200.
- Cost of faults = 200 × £22 = £4,400.
- Saving in year one = £17,600 − £4,400 − £9,000 = £4,200.
- Saving in later years = £17,600 − £4,400 − £2,000 = £11,200.

TQM pays for itself in the first year on these estimates. There are further benefits not in the figures: fewer unhappy customers, better reviews, and possibly a higher price. The main risk is that the defect rate does not fall as far as hoped, because staff do not commit to the new approach.

## Quality as a business grows

The specification highlights two methods of growth that put quality at risk.

**Outsourcing** means paying another business to carry out an activity, such as manufacturing, cleaning or customer support. The business no longer controls the work directly. If the outsourced firm cuts corners, customers blame the brand they bought from.

**Franchising** means allowing franchisees to run their own outlets using the brand name and methods. Each outlet is run by a different owner, so standards can vary from one to another.

Ways to protect quality:

- clear written standards in the contract;
- training for the outsourced firm's staff or franchisees;
- regular inspections and mystery shopper visits;
- monitoring complaints and reviews by outlet;
- the right to end the agreement if standards fall.

Growth can still bring quality benefits, such as specialist suppliers who do the job better, but only if they are managed well.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name a method of finding problems, a measure, a cost or a benefit.
- **Explain:** link a quality issue to its consequence for the business.
- **Analyse:** chain, such as poor quality → bad reviews → fewer new customers → lower revenue.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the costs of maintaining quality against the benefits and the cost of failure, using the business's context. Consider how the business is growing.

## Common mistakes

- Equating quality with expensive or luxury.
- Forgetting services: quality applies to haircuts and hotel stays as well as goods.
- Describing TQM as "more inspectors". It makes every worker responsible.
- Listing costs and benefits without applying them to the business.
- Ignoring the specification's point about outsourcing and franchising.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is total quality management?**
An approach in which every employee in every department is responsible for quality at every stage, aiming to get things right first time and keep improving.

**How do businesses measure quality?**
Using measures such as the defect rate, complaint rate and return rate, and customer feedback, reviews and mystery shopper reports.

**Why can franchising cause quality problems?**
Each outlet is run by a different owner, so the brand has less direct control and standards may vary between outlets.
