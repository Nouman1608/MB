## Where the water is

About 97% of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans. Of the small freshwater fraction, roughly two-thirds is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers and almost all the rest is groundwater. Lakes, rivers, soil moisture, the atmosphere and living things together hold only about 1% of freshwater. The consequence: the water people can most easily use, in rivers and lakes, is a tiny, fast-moving part of the system that depends on continuous renewal by precipitation.

Two further terms are useful when you link water systems to food and ecosystems:

- **Blue water**: liquid freshwater in rivers, lakes and aquifers, which can be pumped or diverted.
- **Green water**: water held in the soil and available to plants, which returns to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration.

Most rain-fed farming and all natural land ecosystems run on green water; irrigation draws on blue water.

## The drainage basin as an open system

A **drainage basin** is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries, separated from neighbouring basins by a **watershed**. It is an open system: water enters as precipitation and leaves as evapotranspiration and river discharge, and its stores (interception, surface, soil, groundwater, channel) rise and fall.

Its **water budget** balances inputs and outputs:

```
P = Q + E ± ΔS
P = precipitation, Q = runoff (river discharge),
E = evapotranspiration, ΔS = change in storage
```

### Worked example: a basin water budget

A fictional basin of 600 km² receives 850 mm of precipitation in a year. Evapotranspiration is 520 mm and river discharge is equivalent to 290 mm over the basin.

```
ΔS = P − Q − E = 850 − 290 − 520 = +40 mm

volume added to storage:
depth = 40 mm = 0.040 m
area  = 600 km² = 600 × 10⁶ m²
ΔS    = 0.040 × 600 × 10⁶ = 24 × 10⁶ m³ (24 million m³)
```

**Interpretation.** Storage rose, so soils and aquifers in the basin gained water that year. In a drought year ΔS is negative: discharge continues for a time because groundwater keeps feeding the river (**baseflow**), even though rainfall is low. Over many years ΔS should average close to zero unless people are abstracting groundwater faster than it is recharged.

## Storm hydrographs

A **storm hydrograph** plots river discharge against time after a rainfall event, with rainfall shown as a bar chart. Learn its parts:

- **Rising limb**: discharge increases as quick flows (surface runoff, throughflow) reach the channel.
- **Peak discharge**: the highest flow.
- **Lag time**: the time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
- **Falling (recession) limb**: discharge declines as stores drain.
- **Baseflow**: the slow groundwater contribution that continues between storms.

A "flashy" hydrograph (short lag time, high peak) means flood risk. Factors that make a basin flashier: impermeable urban surfaces, drains that pipe water straight to channels, steep slopes, saturated or frozen soil, thin soils, deforestation and a high density of streams. Factors that slow the response: forest cover, permeable rock and soils, gentle slopes and floodplain wetlands that store water.

### Worked example: comparing hydrographs

In a fictional catchment, the same storm (peak rainfall at 10:00) was recorded before and after a large housing development.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Time of peak discharge | 16:00 | 12:30 |
| Peak discharge / m³ s⁻¹ | 30 | 48 |

```
lag time before = 16:00 − 10:00 = 6.0 hours
lag time after  = 12:30 − 10:00 = 2.5 hours
change in peak  = (48 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +60 %
```

**Interpretation.** Roofs, roads and drains reduced infiltration, so more rain became surface runoff and reached the river faster. The lag time fell by 3.5 hours and the peak rose by 60%, increasing the chance that the channel overflows. Sustainable drainage (green roofs, permeable paving, retention ponds) aims to reverse these changes.

## Ocean circulation and heat transport

The oceans store and move vast amounts of heat, so they shape climate on land.

**Surface currents** are driven mainly by wind. Because of Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect) and the shape of the continents, they form large circular systems called **gyres**: clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern. Warm currents carry heat from the tropics towards the poles along the western sides of ocean basins; cold currents return water towards the equator along the eastern sides. The Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, help explain why north-west Europe has milder winters than places at the same latitude in eastern Canada.

**Deep ocean circulation** (thermohaline circulation) is driven by differences in density, which depend on temperature and salinity. In the North Atlantic, surface water cools and becomes saltier as sea ice forms (ice leaves salt behind), so it becomes dense and sinks. This deep water spreads through the world's oceans and eventually rises again, completing a loop that takes centuries. Scientists are concerned that meltwater from Greenland, by making surface water fresher and less dense, could weaken the Atlantic part of this circulation.

## Upwelling and El Niño

**Upwelling** happens where winds push surface water away from a coast and colder, nutrient-rich water rises from below to replace it. The nutrients support high phytoplankton productivity and therefore large fisheries, such as the anchoveta fishery off Peru.

During an **El Niño** event, the trade winds across the Pacific weaken. Warm surface water spreads east towards South America and upwelling off Peru weakens, so nutrient supply and fish catches fall. Rainfall patterns shift across the Pacific: heavy rain and flooding in parts of western South America, drought in parts of Australia and Indonesia. In the opposite phase, **La Niña**, the trade winds strengthen and upwelling increases. The whole cycle is called the El Niño-Southern Oscillation.

## Stratification of water bodies

Water is densest at about 4 °C. In summer, the surface of a temperate lake warms and floats on colder, denser water below, so the lake becomes **stratified** into layers:

- **Epilimnion**: the warm, well-mixed, sunlit surface layer, rich in oxygen from the air and photosynthesis.
- **Thermocline** (metalimnion): a layer where temperature falls rapidly with depth and acts as a barrier to mixing.
- **Hypolimnion**: the cold, dark bottom layer, where decomposers use oxygen that cannot be replaced while the lake is stratified.

In autumn the surface cools until the whole lake reaches a similar temperature and density. Wind can then mix it from top to bottom: this is **turnover**. Turnover carries oxygen down to the bottom and brings nutrients from the bottom up to the surface, often triggering a burst of phytoplankton growth. Many temperate lakes also turn over in spring as ice melts and surface water warms towards 4 °C.

The oceans are stratified too, with a warm surface layer over cold deep water. Warming strengthens stratification, which reduces mixing: less oxygen reaches deep water and fewer nutrients reach the sunlit surface, lowering productivity.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the water budget without the storage term, or with the wrong sign.
- Forgetting to convert km² to m² (× 10⁶) and mm to m (÷ 1000) when turning a budget into a volume.
- Measuring lag time from the start of the rain instead of from peak rainfall.
- Saying the thermohaline circulation is driven by wind. Surface currents are wind-driven; deep circulation is density-driven.
- Describing El Niño without its effect on upwelling and productivity.
- Saying the hypolimnion loses oxygen "because it is cold". It loses oxygen because the thermocline stops mixing while decomposers keep respiring.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a hydrograph: quote times, discharges and units from the graph.
- **Explain** a change in lag time: link a basin feature to infiltration or runoff speed, then to the hydrograph.
- **Draw** or **annotate**: label rising limb, peak, lag time, falling limb and baseflow precisely.
- **Calculate**: show the equation, substitute with units and state what the answer means.
- **Evaluate** a flood-management measure: weigh how much it reduces peak flow against cost and side effects.
