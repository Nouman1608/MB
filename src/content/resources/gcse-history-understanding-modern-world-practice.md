## Section A

**1.** Give **two** reasons why examiners reward a timeline before thematic
revision begins for either a period study or a wider world depth study. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the three categories a strong causation answer should sort
its evidence into. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **two** ways in which the internal three-part structure
(opening phase, middle phase of crisis or diplomacy, final phase) helps you
plan revision for your chosen period study or depth study. **[8]**

**4.** Using an example from your own period study or depth study, explain
how long-term, short-term and trigger causes can be connected to explain a
single outcome. **[8]**

## Section C

**5.** "A historian's account of an event is shaped more by the questions
they chose to ask than by the evidence available to them." How far do you
agree, using an example of two differing interpretations from your studied
content? **[16]**

**6.** (Alternative Section C question) "Change and continuity are equally
important to understanding the modern world." How far would you agree,
using your own period study or depth study as evidence? **[16]**

## Answers (worked approach, since content depends on your specified options)

**1.** A timeline **establishes the correct sequence of events** before
thematic revision begins, which is a precondition for explaining causation
accurately [1]; it also **reveals gaps** in a student's knowledge of the
period early, while there is still time to address them, rather than
discovering the gap for the first time in the exam [1].

**2.** **Long-term causes** — underlying conditions built up over years or
decades [1]; **short-term causes** — events in the run-up to the outcome
being explained [1]; **the trigger** — the specific event that precipitated
the outcome [1].

**3.** Any two, 4 marks each: knowing the shared three-part shape means a
student can **allocate revision time evenly** across all three phases
rather than concentrating only on the most dramatic middle section, which
is a common and costly imbalance [1] [1] [1] [1]. It also helps a student
**recognise which phase a given exam question is really testing**, since
questions on the opening phase typically test causation of how a system
was established or a conflict began, while questions on the final phase
more often test the lived consequences or the breakdown of a diplomatic
effort [1] [1] [1] [1].

**4.** Using AB Germany 1890–1945 as one illustrative example: the
**long-term cause** is resentment at the terms of the Treaty of Versailles
and structural weaknesses built into the Weimar constitution [1] [1]; the
**short-term cause** is the economic impact of the Depression from 1929,
which sharply increased unemployment and support for extremist parties
[1] [1]; the **trigger** is Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January
1933 [1]. A top-mark answer explicitly explains how the categories
**reinforced each other** — for example, that long-term resentment created
a receptive audience for extremist politics, which the short-term economic
crisis then activated, culminating in the specific trigger event — rather
than listing all three causes with no stated connection between them [2].
(Full marks are equally available using a correctly structured example
from any other period study or depth study option.)

**5.** A top-band answer takes a clear position on the statement [2] and
supports it with a **specific example of two historians reaching different
conclusions** about the same event from their studied content, explaining
the difference in terms of what each historian emphasised or the questions
each set out to answer, rather than the raw evidence available to them
(which was often similarly accessible to both) [8]. Strong answers also
consider the counter-case — that the evidence itself, or the historical
context in which a historian was writing, can equally shape an
interpretation — before reaching a **substantiated judgement** that
weighs both factors rather than asserting the statement is simply true or
false [6].

**6.** A top-band answer takes a clear position [2] and supports it with
**specific evidence of both change and continuity** from the student's own
period study or depth study — for example, in AD America 1920–1973,
political rights expanded significantly for African Americans through the
Civil Rights movement, while economic inequality persisted in many
respects across the same period [8]. The strongest answers explicitly
weigh whether change or continuity was more significant overall for their
chosen content, reaching a judgement rather than simply describing
instances of each side-by-side [6].

## Where marks are usually lost

Revising or answering with content from a period study or depth study
option your school did not actually teach — always confirm your two exact
options first. Naming long-term, short-term and trigger causes without
explaining how they reinforced each other, which caps causation answers at
the lower half of the top level. Describing change and continuity, or two
differing interpretations, side by side without reaching an explicit,
substantiated judgement at the end. Treating a source's origin and purpose
as identical to evaluating a historian's interpretation — these are
related but distinct AO3 skills tested in different question types on this
paper.
