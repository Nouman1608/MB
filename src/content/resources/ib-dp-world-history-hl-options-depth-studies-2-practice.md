Section A checks precise knowledge of the US Civil War, the Mexican Revolution and civil rights. Section B builds explanation and then full essays: allow about 50 minutes each for questions 11 and 12.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** events of the 1850s that increased sectional tension in the United States. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** effects of the Emancipation Proclamation. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** articles of the Mexican Constitution of 1917 and what each established. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** reasons for the outbreak of the Cristero War. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** differences between the methods of the SCLC and the Black Panther Party. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain why Lincoln's election in 1860 led to secession. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** weaknesses of the Confederacy during the war. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Explain why Reconstruction failed to secure lasting equality for African Americans in the South. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **one** lasting achievement of Reconstruction. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why Zapata broke with Madero in 1911. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the Constitutionalists defeated Villa and Zapata by 1915. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why the Montgomery bus boycott succeeded. **[4]**

**11.** "Cárdenas fulfilled the aims of the Mexican Revolution." To what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of the federal government in the successes of the civil rights movement between 1954 and 1965. **[15]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Compromise of 1850 and its Fugitive Slave Act; publication of *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852); the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854); violence in "Bleeding Kansas" (1856); the caning of Senator Sumner (1856); the *Dred Scott* decision (1857); John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (1859). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: it declared enslaved people in rebel-held areas free, making abolition a Union war aim; it discouraged British and French recognition of the Confederacy; it allowed Black men to enlist in Union forces, and many thousands did; enslaved people fled to Union lines in greater numbers. **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: Article 3, secular, free public education; Article 27, national ownership of land, water and subsoil, the basis for land reform and control of oil; Article 123, labour rights including an eight-hour day and the right to strike; Article 130, restrictions on the Church and clergy. **[3]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: President Calles enforced the anticlerical articles of the 1917 Constitution, including the "Calles Law" (1926) restricting priests; the bishops suspended public worship in protest; Catholic peasants, especially in west-central Mexico, rose to defend their religion and communities. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: banned segregation in public places such as hotels, restaurants and theatres; banned discrimination in employment on grounds including race and sex (Title VII); created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission; allowed federal funds to be withheld from segregated programmes, including schools. **[2]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: the SCLC was committed to non-violence, the Panthers to armed self-defence; the SCLC sought integration, the Panthers stressed Black self-determination and community control; the SCLC was rooted in Southern churches, the Panthers in Northern and Western cities (founded in Oakland, 1966); the Panthers combined a revolutionary programme with community services such as free breakfasts. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: Lincoln won without any Southern electoral votes, showing that the free states could control the presidency (1); the Republican platform opposed the expansion of slavery into the territories, which Southerners saw as the first step towards abolition (1); Southern leaders argued that states had a right to leave a Union that threatened their institutions (1); South Carolina seceded in December 1860, followed by six more states before Lincoln took office (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed weakness: a much smaller industrial base and railway network, so it struggled to supply its armies; the Union blockade reduced cotton exports and imports; conflict between states' rights and the need for central control of conscription and taxation; inflation and food shortages, including bread riots in Richmond (1863); failure to win foreign recognition. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: Black Codes and later segregation laws restricted freedmen's rights (1); violence and intimidation by the Ku Klux Klan and similar groups deterred Black political participation (1); most freedmen did not receive land and became sharecroppers, so economic dependence continued (1); Northern opinion lost interest, and the Compromise of 1877 removed federal troops, leaving Southern Democrats in control (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for a developed point: the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments remained in the Constitution, and the Fourteenth's equal protection clause became the basis of later civil rights rulings such as *Brown v. Board* (1954); or Black schools, colleges and churches founded in this period endured. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** 1 mark each: Madero did not carry out land restitution after taking power; he asked Zapata's forces to disarm before reforms were made; Zapata issued the Plan of Ayala (November 1911), declaring Madero a traitor and demanding the return of village lands. **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 4 marks: Obregón's modern tactics, using trenches and machine guns, defeated Villa's cavalry at Celaya (1915) (1); Carranza controlled the ports and oil revenues, so could buy arms (1); Villa and Zapata failed to co-ordinate or build a national government after Aguascalientes (1); Carranza's promises of land and labour reform (1915) won support from peasants and urban workers (1). **[4]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks: the Black community organized effectively through the Montgomery Improvement Association and churches, with car pools to sustain the boycott for over a year (1); Martin Luther King Jr provided inspiring leadership and a commitment to non-violence (1); the boycott caused economic loss to the bus company (1); the federal courts ruled that bus segregation was unconstitutional (*Browder v. Gayle*, upheld by the Supreme Court in 1956) (1). **[4]**

**11.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. For: Cárdenas distributed more land than all his predecessors combined, largely through *ejidos*, meeting Zapata's demand; he supported labour unions and strikes (Article 123); the oil expropriation of 1938 fulfilled the economic nationalism of Article 27; secular education expanded; he avoided the personal dictatorship that the revolution had overthrown, leaving office in 1940. Against: Madero's aim of effective suffrage was not met, as the ruling party dominated elections; many *ejidos* lacked credit and became unproductive; reforms slowed under his successors; mass organizations were incorporated into the party, limiting their independence. A strong answer sets out what the revolution's aims were, recognizing they differed between factions, and judges how far each was met by 1940. **[15]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. Federal importance: the Supreme Court's *Brown* (1954) and *Browder v. Gayle* (1956) rulings; Eisenhower sending troops to Little Rock (1957); the Kennedy administration's protection of the Freedom Riders and intervention at the University of Mississippi (1962); Johnson's Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965). Limits and alternatives: federal action was often reluctant and reactive; grassroots campaigns (Montgomery, sit-ins, Birmingham, Selma) forced action by creating crises reported on television; leadership by King, SNCC and local activists; Cold War pressure on US international image. A strong answer judges, for example, that federal action was essential for lasting legal change but was usually a response to the pressure that the movement created. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know Canada or Latin America too?**
Only if your school's sections include them. Many sections allow examples from across the Americas, so check your teacher's list.

**How much detail on battles is needed for the Civil War?**
Enough to explain turning points and the outcome; Paper 3 rewards analysis, not campaign narrative.
