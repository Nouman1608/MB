This guide covers content item 1.5.4, The economy and business, in Topic 1.5 (Understanding external influences on business) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It belongs to Theme 1, examined in Paper 1. The broader [guide to legislation, the economy and external influences](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-legislation-the-economy-and-external-influences/) introduces each economic factor. This page focuses on one skill: working out *which* businesses gain and *which* lose from a change in the economy, using figures where the question gives them. All businesses, figures and rates below are invented.

## What 1.5.4 contains

The specification lists one heading, the impact of the economic climate on businesses, with six factors:

1. unemployment
2. changing levels of consumer income
3. inflation
4. changes in interest rates
5. government taxation
6. changes in exchange rates.

The **economic climate** means the overall state of the economy: whether people are in work, how much they earn and spend, and how prices and borrowing costs are changing. A business cannot control it, so it is an external influence. How a business responds is covered in 1.5.5.

## One change, different effects

The most important idea in this item is that the same change in the economy affects businesses differently. To decide the effect on the business in the source, ask four questions:

- Does it sell **essentials or luxuries**? Spending on luxuries changes more when incomes change.
- Does it **borrow**, or hold savings?
- Does it **import** materials, or **export** its products?
- Are its **costs mainly wages**, materials or rent?

## Unemployment

Unemployment is when people who want to work cannot find a job.

**Rising unemployment** usually means:

- **Lower spending:** unemployed people have less income, and people still in work may save more in case they lose their jobs. Sellers of luxuries, such as restaurants and holidays, are hit hardest.
- **Easier recruitment:** more people apply for each vacancy, so a business may fill jobs faster and with less pressure to raise wages.
- **Some winners:** businesses selling cheaper alternatives, such as discount shops, may gain customers who are trading down.

## Changing levels of consumer income

Consumer income is the money people have to spend. It changes with wages, jobs, prices and taxes.

- **Rising incomes:** more spending, especially on luxuries; customers may trade up to premium products.
- **Falling incomes:** customers cut back on luxuries and switch to cheaper brands.

### Worked example: two businesses, one change

Average household spending in a town falls by 5%.

- Silverbirch Spa sells £90 treatments. Its owner expects bookings to fall by 15%, from 2,000 to 1,700 a year. Revenue falls from £180,000 to £153,000, a fall of £27,000.
- Value Basket, a discount grocer, expects sales to rise by 4% as shoppers switch from dearer supermarkets. Revenue of £400,000 rises to £416,000.

The spa sells a luxury and loses; the discount grocer sells cheaper essentials and gains. A strong answer names the type of product before deciding the effect.

## Inflation

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time.

- **Costs rise:** materials, energy and rent go up, squeezing profit if the business cannot raise its own prices.
- **Wage pressure:** staff ask for pay rises so their wages keep up with prices.
- **Customers' spending power falls** if wages rise more slowly than prices, so they buy less.
- **Pricing decisions:** a business may raise prices, but if rivals do not, it may lose customers.

### Worked example: passing on a cost rise

Stonebridge Candles' variable cost per candle rises by 8%, from £5.00 to £5.40. It sells candles at £14.

- Contribution per candle falls from £14 − £5.00 = £9.00 to £14 − £5.40 = £8.60.
- With fixed costs of £4,300 a month, break even rises from £4,300 ÷ £9.00 = 477.8, so 478 candles, to £4,300 ÷ £8.60 = 500 candles.

To keep the contribution at £9.00, the price would have to rise to £14.40. Whether customers accept that depends on what competitors charge.

## Changes in interest rates

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving.

**A rise in interest rates:**

- increases repayments on loans and overdrafts with variable rates, raising costs;
- makes businesses less willing to borrow to expand;
- reduces customer spending, especially on goods often bought on credit, such as cars and furniture, and leaves households with mortgages less to spend;
- benefits a business with savings, which earn more interest.

**A fall in interest rates** has broadly the opposite effects.

### Worked example: an overdraft

Kestle Garage has an average overdraft of £20,000 over the year. The interest rate rises from 9% to 12% a year.

- Interest before = 9% × £20,000 = £1,800
- Interest after = 12% × £20,000 = £2,400
- Extra cost = £600 a year

## Government taxation

Taxation is money collected by the government.

- **Taxes on business profits** reduce the profit left for owners and for reinvestment.
- **Taxes on income** affect how much customers have to spend: higher income tax means lower consumer income.
- **Taxes on spending** (added to prices) raise what customers pay, which can reduce demand.

A tax cut has broadly the opposite effects. The specification does not ask for tax rates; explain the direction of the effect.

## Changes in exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, for example £1 = €1.20.

| | Pound strengthens (buys more foreign currency) | Pound weakens (buys less) |
|---|---|---|
| UK business importing materials | Imports cheaper | Imports dearer |
| UK business exporting | Exports dearer abroad, so harder to sell | Exports cheaper abroad, so easier to sell |

### Worked example: a weaker pound

Avonmere Bikes imports frames from Germany at €240 each. The exchange rate falls from £1 = €1.20 to £1 = €1.10.

- Cost before = €240 ÷ 1.20 = £200
- Cost after = €240 ÷ 1.10 = £218.18
- Increase = £18.18 per frame, about 9.1%

Avonmere also exports finished bikes priced at £600. In euros, the price was £600 × 1.20 = €720 and is now £600 × 1.10 = €660, so the bikes are cheaper for European customers. The weaker pound hurts Avonmere's costs but helps its export sales. Which effect is larger depends on how much it imports compared with how much it exports.

Memory aid (the writer's own): a **s**trong pound is good for **s**hopping abroad (importing); a **w**eak pound is good for **w**inning sales abroad (exporting).

## Common mistakes

- Saying a change in the economy affects every business the same way.
- Saying inflation means "prices are high". It means prices are **rising**.
- Getting exchange-rate direction wrong: when converting pounds to euros, multiply; euros to pounds, divide.
- Forgetting that higher interest rates affect customers' spending as well as the business's own loans.
- Describing a change without linking it to the business's revenue or costs.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the conversion or percentage step by step.
- **Explain:** state the change, then two linked effects (for example, interest rates rise → repayments rise → less profit to invest).
- **Analyse:** decide which type of business is in the source (luxury or essential, importer or exporter, borrower or saver).
- **Evaluate:** weigh positive and negative effects and say which is likely to matter more for this business.
