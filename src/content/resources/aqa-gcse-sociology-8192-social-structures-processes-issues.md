This guide covers section 3.2, Social structures, social processes and social issues, of the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards. Section 3.2 sets out how you must handle theories, extracts, methods and current debates in every topic. The specification does not attach it to one paper: Paper 1 (The sociology of families and education) and Paper 2 (The sociology of crime and deviance and social stratification) both assess "relevant areas of social theory and methodology", and both expect you to draw on the entire course.

Use this guide with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-structures-processes-issues-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-structures-processes-issues-practice/). ## What the three terms mean

- **Social structures** are the lasting, organised patterns of a society: families, the education system, the class structure, the criminal justice system.
- **Social processes** are the ways those structures work on people: socialisation, social control, labelling, selection in school.
- **Social issues** are patterns people debate and governments may try to change: divorce, underachievement, crime, poverty.

The aims of the specification (section 4.1) talk about the "relationships and tension between social structures and individual agency". Agency means people's ability to make their own choices. Functionalist and Marxist accounts stress structure; interactionist accounts stress meaning and reactions to labels.

## Comparing and contrasting theories in context

The specification asks for the **key features** of each theory "in the context of a specific topic". Show what a theory says about *this* family pattern, school process or crime figure.

| Perspective | Key feature | Families | Education | Crime and deviance | Stratification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | Society rests on value consensus; institutions meet society's needs | Parsons: primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities | Durkheim: passing on norms and values; Parsons: achieved status and meritocracy | Merton: anomie when goals outrun means; Albert Cohen: status frustration | Davis and Moore: unequal rewards fill functionally important roles |
| Marxism | Society is shaped by class conflict and the ruling class's ownership of the means of production | Zaretsky: family props up capitalism and is a unit of consumption | Bowles and Gintis: correspondence principle; Willis: counter school culture | Crime defined and policed in ways that suit the powerful | Marx: bourgeoisie exploit proletariat; polarisation |
| Feminism | Society is patriarchal; men benefit from women's subordination | Delphy and Leonard: men exploit women's labour; Oakley: the idea of the conventional family | Gendered experiences of schooling | Heidensohn: women controlled more closely; Carlen: class deal and gender deal | Walby: six patriarchal structures |
| Interactionism | Society is built in everyday interaction; meanings and labels matter | (named mainly in other topics) | Ball: teacher expectations, banding | Becker: labelling, master status, deviant career | (not a named perspective for this topic) |

The specification names interactionism for education processes and crime only; families and stratification name functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives.

### How to compare well

1. **Same question, different answer.** Put two theories on the same question. Who benefits from the family? Zaretsky says capitalism; Delphy and Leonard say men.
2. **Find the similarity first.** Marxism and feminism are both conflict theories that see an institution as serving a powerful group. Functionalism and Marxism both explain behaviour by social structure, while interactionism starts from small-scale interaction.
3. **Compare within a perspective too.** Willis and Bowles and Gintis are both Marxists, but Willis rejects a direct link between the economy and schooling.
4. **Judge.** Say which account fits the evidence in the question better, and why.

### Evaluation points you can reuse

- **Functionalism** explains how institutions hold society together, but can idealise middle-class life (printed against Parsons) and assume a value consensus (printed against Merton).
- **Marxism** explains why class inequality persists, but can underplay gender and ethnicity; Willis shows people do not simply absorb ruling-class ideas.
- **Feminism** reveals inequality inside "private" institutions, but Heidensohn is criticised for generalising to all women without strong research evidence.
- **Interactionism** shows how labels create outcomes, but can neglect where the power to label comes from.

### Worked example: comparing within a topic

*Question focus: compare Marxist and feminist views of who benefits from family life.*

> Both Zaretsky (1976) and Delphy and Leonard (1992) see the family as a site of exploitation rather than the harmonious unit functionalists such as Parsons describe (**knowledge**). They disagree on who gains. For Zaretsky, the family is a prop to capitalism: housewives' unpaid domestic labour reproduces the next generation of workers, and the family is a vital unit of consumption. For Delphy and Leonard, men rather than capitalists are the main beneficiaries, because the family is an economic system in which men appropriate women's work (**analysis**). A dual-earner couple in which the woman still does most of the housework fits Delphy and Leonard better. Zaretsky cannot explain why the husband, a worker himself, gains from her labour (**application**). Yet Zaretsky explains why family spending supports the economy, so capitalism and patriarchy may both benefit at once (**evaluation**).

## Reading and responding to extracts

Section 3.2 says you will show knowledge of theories "by reading and responding to extracts". Appendix B of the specification summarises the classic texts. For any extract, ask five questions:

1. **Who and when?** Note the author and date. Section 3.1 asks for a "sense of time and place".
2. **Which perspective?** Look for signal words: "value consensus" or "functions" (functionalist), "capitalism" or "ruling class" (Marxist), "patriarchy" or "men benefit" (feminist), "label" or "meanings" (interactionist).
3. **What is the main claim?** Put it in one sentence.
4. **What is the evidence?** Name the method and sample if the extract gives them.
5. **What are the limits?** Ask whether society has changed since, who was left out, and what rival perspectives would say.

The Appendix B summaries model this last step. Oakley's paper predates civil partnerships and same-sex marriage. The Rapoports' work predates the more open acceptance of gay and lesbian households. Halsey's sample excluded females. The Willis summary notes that manual work was relatively easy to obtain in the 1970s.

### Worked example: responding to an original extract

*The extract below was written for this guide. It does not quote a real sociologist.*

> "Schools teach every child the same rules, reward effort with grades and prepare young people to take their place in adult society. Pupils learn that they are judged by what they achieve, not by who their parents are."

> The extract reflects a functionalist view (**knowledge**). Phrases such as "the same rules" and "judged by what they achieve, not by who their parents are" echo Parsons's argument that schools apply universalistic standards and operate on meritocratic principles (**application**). However, the extract assumes the rules are neutral. Bowles and Gintis argue the rules mirror the workplace and produce an obedient workforce (**analysis**). Halsey's study of over 8,000 males found that a service-class boy had about eleven times the chance of a working-class boy of attending university, which suggests family background still mattered greatly (**evaluation**).

## Methods and methodological issues in each topic

Section 3.7 teaches methods in general; here you judge methods *in the context of a topic*. For research design, sampling and ethics, see the [research methods guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-research-methods/).

### Official statistics in four topics

**Official statistics** are numerical data collected by government bodies. They are cheap, cover large populations and show **trends**. Their usefulness differs by topic:

- **Divorce.** Figures record legal divorces but miss separations without divorce and any **empty shell marriage**.
- **Crime.** **Recorded crime** misses the **dark figure** of crime that is unreported or unrecorded. Victim surveys (such as the Crime Survey for England and Wales) and **self-report studies** try to measure it.
- **Poverty.** The figure depends on the definition. Townsend estimated that just over 6% of the population was poor in 1968–69 on the state standard and a little over 9% on relative income. On his own relative deprivation measure, the figure was more than 22%. The definition creates the figure.

Perspectives read these figures differently. For Becker, crime figures result from labelling decisions that depend on who commits an act, where, and who sees it. Marxist critics argue that statistics can over-represent working-class crime and under-represent **white collar crime**, a criticism the specification also prints against Merton's theory. Feminists note that crimes inside the home may stay hidden.

### Qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods in the named studies

| Study (as printed in the specification) | Approach | What it allowed | A limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Willmott and Young: nearly 2,000 people interviewed in Greater London and surrounding areas | Large-scale social survey (quantitative) | Patterns across many households | Feminists saw little evidence of real "symmetry" |
| Halsey, Heath and Ridge: over 8,000 males born 1913–1952 | Large-scale survey (quantitative) | Clear class-based odds of staying on | Excluded females |
| Townsend: questionnaires to over 2,000 households, more than 6,000 individuals | Questionnaires and a deprivation index (quantitative) | National estimate of poverty | Index items may reflect choice, not poverty |
| Ball: three years as a participant observer in "Beachside" school (1981) | Participant observation (qualitative) | Close detail of banding and mixed ability | One school; hard to generalise |
| Willis: one Midlands school | Observation, participant observation, group discussions, informal interviews, diaries (qualitative) | The pupils' own point of view | Small group of boys |
| Carlen: 39 women | In-depth unstructured interviews (qualitative) | Rich detail on class and gender deals | Small sample |

**Quantitative** data are numerical and suit patterns, comparisons and **reliability**. **Qualitative** data are words and meanings and suit **validity**: seeing the world as participants see it. **Mixed methods research** combines both in one study. For example, use official statistics to find areas with high exclusion rates, then interview pupils there. **Triangulation** means using more than one method or source to check that findings agree.

### Worked example: judging a method in context

> To study why some young people resist school, a researcher could use participant observation (**knowledge**). Willis used observation, group discussions and informal interviews to see school from the pupils' point of view, which a questionnaire with closed questions could not capture (**application**). The method gives high validity because it records what the boys did, not only what they claimed (**analysis**). However, one small group cannot show how common counter school culture is; adding exclusion statistics, a mixed methods design, would test this (**evaluation**).

## Contemporary social issues: challenging everyday understandings

Section 3.2 asks you to "challenge everyday understandings of social phenomena from a sociological perspective". Everyday views usually explain issues by individual nature or choice; sociology looks for the structures and processes behind them.

| Everyday understanding | Sociological challenge | Named evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "Crime statistics show who the criminals are." | Figures reflect reporting, recording and labelling decisions | Becker; the dark figure |
| "Women commit less crime because they are naturally law-abiding." | Women are more closely controlled at home, in public and at work | Heidensohn's control theory |
| "School is fair, so the cleverest succeed." | Class background shapes staying on and university entry | Halsey; Bowles and Gintis |
| "The family is a private refuge from the outside world." | The idea of a private life separate from the economy is an illusion | Zaretsky |

Murray's idea of an **underclass**, linked to New Right theories, explains poverty partly by values and benefit dependency. Townsend's **relative deprivation** explains it by lack of resources to take part in the normal life of society. The specification records criticisms of both: Murray for a poor evidence base, Townsend for an index that may confuse choice with deprivation. Present both fairly and let the evidence decide.

### Worked example: a balanced debate paragraph

> Many people assume poverty results from a lack of effort (**everyday view**). Murray's New Right account partly supports this. He argued that generous benefits encouraged dependency and an alternative value system in a growing underclass (**knowledge**). Townsend instead defined poverty as lacking the resources for the diet, activities and living conditions widely available in society. His deprivation index suggested poverty was far more widespread than the state standard showed (**analysis**). For a household in a fictional area where most local jobs have closed, Townsend's view fits better, because the cause lies in the labour market rather than attitudes (**application**). However, missing items may reflect choice, so a full judgement needs data on both resources and attitudes (**evaluation**).

## Drawing connections between topic areas

The specification asks you to "draw connections between the different topic areas". The same concepts appear across the course:

- **Labelling.** Ball on teacher expectations in school; Becker on deviant labels and master status. Both show a **self-fulfilling prophecy**.
- **Social control.** Heidensohn links control in the home to women's lower crime.
- **Class.** Halsey's educational odds, Albert Cohen's status frustration, Carlen's class deal.
- **Patriarchy.** Delphy and Leonard on family labour; Walby's six structures.

A connection earns credit when it strengthens an argument: "Becker's master status explains why Ball's low-band pupils might see themselves as failures".

## Common errors

- Describing a theory in general terms ("Marxists think society is unequal") without its key features in the topic the question names.
- Treating all Marxists, or all feminists, as saying the same thing. Willis does not agree with Bowles and Gintis on how far schools succeed.
- Calling Albert Cohen a Marxist. The specification presents his work as functionalist.
- Treating official statistics as either "true" or "useless". Their usefulness depends on the topic and the definition used.
- Confusing **mixed methods** (qualitative plus quantitative) with **triangulation** (checking findings with more than one method or source).
