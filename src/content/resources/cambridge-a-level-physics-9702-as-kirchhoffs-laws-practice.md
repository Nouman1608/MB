## Section A

**1.** Explain why Kirchhoff's first law is a consequence of the conservation of charge. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why Kirchhoff's second law is a consequence of the conservation of energy. **[2]**

**3.** A current of 4.2 A enters a junction along one wire. Currents of 1.6 A and 3.1 A leave along two other wires. Determine the size and direction of the current in the fourth wire. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the combined resistance of resistors of 15 Ω, 30 Ω and 30 Ω connected in parallel. **[2]**

**5.** Show that n identical resistors, each of resistance R, connected in parallel have a combined resistance of R/n. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Use Kirchhoff's laws to derive the formula for the combined resistance of three resistors R₁, R₂ and R₃ connected in parallel. **[3]**

**8.** A 12 Ω resistor is connected in series with a parallel combination of a 20 Ω and a 30 Ω resistor. The arrangement is connected to an 18 V supply of negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** Calculate the total resistance of the circuit. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the current from the supply. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across the parallel combination. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the current in the 20 Ω and in the 30 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**(e)** Show that your answers are consistent with Kirchhoff's first law. **[1]**

**9.** A single loop contains a 12 V cell, a 4.0 V cell, a 3.0 Ω resistor and a 5.0 Ω resistor in series. The cells are connected so that their e.m.f.s oppose each other. Both cells have negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** Calculate the current in the loop and state its direction relative to the 12 V cell. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the 5.0 Ω resistor. **[1]**

**(c)** State the rate at which energy is transferred to the 4.0 V cell. **[1]**

**10.** Three branches connect junction P to junction Q. Branch 1 contains a 10 V cell (positive terminal towards P) and a 2.0 Ω resistor. Branch 2 contains a 5.0 V cell (positive terminal towards P) and a 1.0 Ω resistor. Branch 3 contains a 1.0 Ω resistor only. The cells have negligible internal resistance. Let I₁ and I₂ be the currents flowing towards P in branches 1 and 2, and I₃ the current flowing from P to Q in branch 3.

**(a)** Write down the equation given by Kirchhoff's first law at P. **[1]**

**(b)** Write down two equations given by Kirchhoff's second law. **[2]**

**(c)** Solve for I₁, I₂ and I₃. **[3]**

**(d)** Calculate the power dissipated in branch 3. **[1]**

**11.** In a different circuit a student assumes that the current through cell X flows out of its positive terminal. Solving the Kirchhoff equations gives this current as −1.2 A. State what the result means and what is happening to cell X. **[2]**

**12.** Two branches are connected in parallel across a 12 V supply of negligible internal resistance. Branch A has a 4.0 Ω resistor (connected to the positive terminal) in series with an 8.0 Ω resistor. Branch B has two 6.0 Ω resistors in series. A high-resistance voltmeter is connected between the junction of the two resistors in branch A and the junction of the two resistors in branch B.

**(a)** Calculate the p.d. across the 8.0 Ω resistor and across the lower 6.0 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**(b)** Determine the voltmeter reading. **[1]**

**(c)** State which junction is at the higher potential. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Current is the rate of flow of charge, and charge cannot be created, destroyed or accumulate at a junction [1]; so the charge flowing into a junction per second must equal the charge flowing out per second, which means the total current in equals the total current out [1].

**2.** E.m.f. is the energy gained per unit charge and p.d. is the energy transferred per unit charge [1]; a charge that travels once round a closed loop returns to the same point with the same energy, so the energy gained (sum of e.m.f.s) must equal the energy transferred (sum of p.d.s) [1].

**3.** Current out = 1.6 + 3.1 = 4.7 A, current in = 4.2 A [1]; so the fourth wire carries 0.5 A **into** the junction [1].

**4.** 1/R = 1/15 + 1/30 + 1/30 = 4/30 [1]; R = 7.5 Ω [1].

**5.** 1/R_total = 1/R + 1/R + … (n terms) = n/R [1]; so R_total = R/n [1].

**6.** There are no junctions, so by the first law the same current I flows through each resistor [1]. By the second law, ε = IR₁ + IR₂ + IR₃ [1]. For the equivalent resistance, ε = IR, so IR = IR₁ + IR₂ + IR₃ and R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ [1].

**7.** By the second law, the p.d. V across each branch is the same, so the branch currents are V/R₁, V/R₂ and V/R₃ [1]. By the first law, I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ [1]. With I = V/R: V/R = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + V/R₃, so 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ [1].

**8. (a)** Parallel: 1/R = 1/20 + 1/30 = 5/60, so R = 12 Ω [1]; total = 12 + 12 = 24 Ω [1].

**(b)** I = 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 A [1].

**(c)** V = 0.75 × 12 = 9.0 V [1].

**(d)** 20 Ω: 9.0 ÷ 20 = 0.45 A; 30 Ω: 9.0 ÷ 30 = 0.30 A [1 each].

**(e)** 0.45 + 0.30 = 0.75 A, equal to the current entering the parallel combination [1].

**9. (a)** Net e.m.f. = 12 − 4.0 = 8.0 V [1]; I = 8.0 ÷ (3.0 + 5.0) = 1.0 A [1]; the current flows out of the positive terminal of the 12 V cell (in the direction driven by the larger e.m.f.) [1].

**(b)** V = 1.0 × 5.0 = 5.0 V [1].

**(c)** P = εI = 4.0 × 1.0 = 4.0 W [1].

**10. (a)** I₃ = I₁ + I₂ [1].

**(b)** Loop through branches 1 and 3: 10 = 2.0I₁ + 1.0I₃ [1]. Loop through branches 2 and 3: 5.0 = 1.0I₂ + 1.0I₃ [1].

**(c)** Substituting: 10 = 3.0I₁ + I₂ and 5.0 = I₁ + 2.0I₂ [1]. From the second, I₁ = 5.0 − 2.0I₂, so 10 = 15 − 6.0I₂ + I₂, giving I₂ = 1.0 A [1]. Then I₁ = 3.0 A and I₃ = 4.0 A [1].

**(d)** P = I₃²R = 4.0² × 1.0 = 16 W [1].

**11.** The current is 1.2 A in the opposite direction, into the positive terminal of X [1]; cell X is being charged (energy is being transferred to it) [1].

**12. (a)** Branch A current = 12 ÷ 12 = 1.0 A, so p.d. across 8.0 Ω = 8.0 V [1]; branch B current = 12 ÷ 12 = 1.0 A, so p.d. across the lower 6.0 Ω = 6.0 V [1].

**(b)** 8.0 − 6.0 = 2.0 V [1].

**(c)** The junction in branch A (it is 8.0 V above the negative terminal, compared with 6.0 V) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining the first law using energy, or the second using charge.
- Deriving the formulas without stating which law gives the same current or the same p.d.
- Forgetting to invert 1/R in parallel calculations.
- Adding opposing e.m.f.s instead of subtracting them.
- Treating a negative current as an arithmetic mistake rather than a direction.
- Not checking a solution with an unused loop or junction.
