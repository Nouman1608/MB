These tasks follow the paper 2 structure for the thematic study of popular movements: a concept question (6 marks), a short response (4 marks) and an essay (15 marks). The study prescribes no examples, so the suggested answers are one route only. Any accurate and relevant examples earn credit.

## Section A: concepts

**1.** Using one example from your thematic study, analyse the concept of significance. **[6]**

**2.** Using one example from your thematic study, analyse the concept of continuity and change. **[6]**

## Section B: short responses

**3.** Explain how non-violent methods were used by one popular movement you have studied. **[4]**

**4.** Explain the role of women in one popular movement you have studied. **[4]**

## Section B: essays

**5.** To what extent were divisions within popular movements the main obstacle to change? Use examples from at least two regions. **[15]**

**6.** Evaluate the importance of political factors in the emergence of popular movements. Use examples from at least two regions. **[15]**

## Section B: a further short response

**7.** Explain one way in which violent opposition affected a popular movement. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Marking points (6). Credit:
- a definition: value judgements about people and events, and the question of which stories should be told **[1]**;
- a specific example, for example the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 to 1956 **[1]**;
- analysis of significance against criteria, such as impact (it helped launch a national movement and brought Martin Luther King Jr to prominence), scale and duration (over a year of sustained action) **[2]**;
- recognition that significance can lie in what the event reveals, for example how Black churches and the Women's Political Council organised the boycott **[1]**;
- a judgement, perhaps noting that historians' judgements differ on whether the boycott or later campaigns were more important **[1]**.
Bands: 1 to 2 describes the concept with minimal analysis; 3 to 4 partial analysis with a relevant example; 5 to 6 clear and accurate analysis with a specific example.

**2.** Marking points (6). Credit:
- a definition: societies becoming different while keeping similarity **[1]**;
- a specific example, for example South Africa after 1994 **[1]**;
- change: universal suffrage, a new constitution, the end of legal apartheid **[1 to 2]**;
- continuity: persistent economic inequality, land ownership patterns and poverty **[1 to 2]**;
- a judgement on the pace and on who experienced the change positively or negatively **[1]**.

**3.** Marking points (4). Award:
- identification of a movement, for example the Indian independence movement **[1]**;
- description of a method, such as the Salt March (March to April 1930), with a walk of about 240 miles to Dandi **[1]**;
- explanation of how it worked, for example that breaking the salt law was a simple act that everyone could join and that it drew national and international attention **[1]**;
- explanation of an effect or limit, for example mass arrests, and that the government did not concede independence immediately **[1]**.

**4.** Marking points (4). Award:
- identification of a movement and role **[1]**;
- description of women's activities, for example the Women's Political Council in Montgomery distributing leaflets for the boycott, or suffragettes in Britain **[1]**;
- explanation of their importance to the movement's methods or organisation **[1]**;
- explanation of a limit, for example that women were often excluded from top leadership or that their role was under-recognised **[1]**.

**5.** Essay plan with marking points. A 13 to 15 answer will:
- state a thesis, for example that divisions weakened movements, but external opposition and the strength of traditional ideas were generally greater obstacles **[2]**;
- use two or more examples from at least two regions, such as the suffragists and suffragettes (Europe) and the Congress and Muslim League in India (Asia) **[3]**;
- analyse divisions with specific evidence **[4]**;
- connect with other obstacles (violent opposition, political opposition, traditional ideas) and compare the examples, including a case where unity was strong but the movement still faced severe repression, such as Solidarity under martial law **[4]**;
- reach a reasoned judgement **[2]**.

**6.** Essay plan with marking points. A 13 to 15 answer will:
- state a thesis, for example that political exclusion was a necessary background but rarely sufficient without ideas, economic grievance and organisation **[2]**;
- use two or more examples from at least two regions, such as the American South (Americas) and India (Asia) **[3]**;
- analyse political factors with specific evidence (denial of voting rights; the Rowlatt Acts) **[4]**;
- connect the examples and weigh economic and social factors, such as price rises in Poland (Europe) **[4]**;
- reach a reasoned judgement **[2]**.

**7.** Marking points (4). Award: identification of a relevant movement and event, such as the attack on marchers at Selma in March 1965 **[1]**; accurate description, such as state troopers using clubs and tear gas on marchers on 7 March, known as Bloody Sunday **[1]**; explanation of the effect, such as national television coverage that increased sympathy and pressure on Congress **[1]**; explanation of a limit or cost, such as injuries and fear for the participants, or that violent opposition could also deter some supporters **[1]**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to cover all four types of movement?** No. The guide lets you choose, but each line of inquiry needs at least two examples from more than one region.

**How should I treat violent methods?** Analyse their causes and effects, and avoid simple moral judgement.
