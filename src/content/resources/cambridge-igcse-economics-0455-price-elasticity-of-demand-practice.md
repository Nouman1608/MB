Revise first with the [PED study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-demand/) and the [PED revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-elasticity-of-demand-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** The price of a product rises by 10% and quantity demanded falls by 4%. What is the PED? **[1]**

A: −0.4

B: −2.5

C: −4.0

D: −14

**2.** Demand for a product is price elastic. A firm cuts its price. What happens to the firm's revenue? **[1]**

A: It falls, because the price is lower

B: It rises, because quantity rises by a larger percentage than price falls

C: It stays the same

D: It rises, because quantity rises by a smaller percentage than price falls

**3.** Define price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**4.** A cinema raises its ticket price from 8 to 10. Weekly ticket sales fall from 2,000 to 1,700.

**(a)** Calculate the PED. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether demand is elastic or inelastic, with a reason. **[1]**

**5.** PED for a brand of trainers is −2. The firm cuts its price by 6%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[1]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why demand for electricity is likely to be price inelastic. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A bakery sells 600 cakes a week at 3 each. It cuts the price to 2.40 and sales rise to 840.

**(a)** Calculate the revenue lost on existing sales and the revenue gained on extra sales. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in total revenue. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the PED and explain how it accounts for your answer to (b). **[3]**

**8.** Explain, using diagrams, the difference between perfectly inelastic and perfectly elastic demand. **[4]**

**9.** A train company has 50,000 passengers a month at a fare of 12. PED is −0.3. It wants 9% more passengers.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fare change needed. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the new fare. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the company might decide not to make this change. **[2]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why demand for one brand of coffee is likely to be more price elastic than demand for coffee in general. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse why a government might choose to place a tax on fuel rather than on restaurant meals if its aim is to raise revenue. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a firm should always raise its price when demand for its product is price inelastic. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **A** [1]. PED = −4 ÷ 10 = −0.4. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. With elastic demand, the percentage rise in quantity outweighs the percentage fall in price. **[1]**

**3.** The responsiveness of quantity demanded [1] to a change in the price of the product [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** % change in price = 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = +25%; % change in quantity = −300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = −15% [1]. PED = −15 ÷ 25 = **−0.6** [1].

**(b)** Inelastic, because quantity changed by a smaller percentage than price (size less than 1) [1]. **[3]**

**5.** −2 × −6 = **+12%** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** It is a necessity with few close substitutes for most uses, such as lighting and refrigeration [1], so households keep buying a similar amount when its price rises [1]. Alternatively: it is needed every day and buyers cannot quickly change appliances in the short run. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Lost = (3 − 2.40) × 600 = **360** [1]; gained = 2.40 × (840 − 600) = **576** [1].

**(b)** 576 − 360 = **+216** (revenue rises from 1,800 to 2,016) [1].

**(c)** % change in price = −20%; % change in quantity = +40% [1]; PED = 40 ÷ −20 = **−2** [1]. Demand is elastic, so the rise in sales outweighs the lower price and revenue increases [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Perfectly inelastic demand is drawn as a vertical line [1]; quantity demanded stays the same whatever the price, PED = 0 [1]. Perfectly elastic demand is drawn as a horizontal line [1]; at any price above the line quantity demanded falls to zero, PED is infinite [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 9 ÷ −0.3 = **−30%** [1].

**(b)** 12 × 0.70 = **8.40** [1].

**(c)** Demand is inelastic, so the fare cut would reduce revenue [1]: from 12 × 50,000 = 600,000 to 8.40 × 54,500 = 457,800, which may not cover the cost of carrying the extra passengers [1]. **[4]**

**10.** One brand has many close substitutes in other brands [1], so if its price rises buyers can switch easily, making quantity demanded fall sharply [1]. Coffee in general has fewer substitutes, and many buyers drink it out of habit [1], so a rise in the price of all coffee changes quantity demanded much less [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Demand for fuel is inelastic, because it is a necessity for transport with few substitutes in the short run [1], so a tax causes only a small fall in quantity bought and revenue is collected on most of the previous sales [1]. Demand for restaurant meals is more elastic, because people can eat at home instead [1], so a tax would cut sales more and raise less revenue [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: with inelastic demand, a price rise increases revenue because quantity falls by a smaller percentage [1]; fewer units sold may also reduce total costs, so profit may rise further [1]. Against: PED is an estimate and may be wrong, or may become more elastic over time as buyers find substitutes [1]; rivals may not follow the rise, making demand for this firm's product more elastic [1]; a higher price may harm the firm's reputation or attract new competitors [1]. Conclusion: a price rise is often sensible in the short run, but the firm should consider competitors' reactions and long-run effects before assuming revenue will keep rising [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use the revenue rule as the starting point and then show why real decisions need more than PED alone.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the price change by the quantity change.
- Forgetting to say elastic or inelastic after calculating PED.
- Calculating revenue changes without the rectangles method when a question asks for revenue lost and gained.
- Describing a steep curve as perfectly inelastic.
- Naming "substitutes" without explaining how buyers' behaviour changes.
