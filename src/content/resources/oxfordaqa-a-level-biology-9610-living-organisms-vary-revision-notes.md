These notes condense section 3.1.6 (Living organisms vary) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1, in Unit 1. For explanations and worked data, use the [living organisms vary study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-living-organisms-vary/); then try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-living-organisms-vary-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intraspecific variation | Variation between members of the same species |
| Interspecific variation | Variation between members of different species |
| Random sample | A sample in which every individual has an equal chance of being chosen |
| Bias | A systematic tendency to favour some individuals in a sample |
| Sample size | The number of individuals measured |
| Normal distribution | A symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution of values about a mean |
| Mean | Sum of values divided by the number of values |
| Standard deviation | A measure of how widely values are spread around the mean |

## Causes of variation

| Cause | Example | How to test |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic | Blood group; alleles for flower colour | Genetically different individuals in the same environment still differ |
| Environmental | Plant height in shade or sun; body mass and diet | Genetically identical individuals in different environments differ |
| Both | Human height; skin colour | Genes set the potential; environment affects how much is achieved |

## Random sampling: steps

1. Define the area or population.
2. Lay out a grid with two tape measures.
3. Use a random number generator or tables to give coordinates.
4. Measure at each coordinate.
5. Use a large enough sample.

**Why random?** To avoid bias, so the sample represents the population.

## Chance and sample size

- Samples from the same population differ by chance.
- Small samples are strongly affected by a few extreme values.
- A larger sample gives a mean closer to the population mean and a more reliable comparison.
- Simulation example: 200 samples of 5 gave means from 38.8 to 49.7 mm; 200 samples of 100 gave means from 43.9 to 46.3 mm.

## Mean, spread and the normal distribution

- A normal curve is symmetrical with the mean at the centre.
- About 68 % of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean.
- About 95 % lie within 2 standard deviations.
- A small SD means the data cluster near the mean. A large SD means they are spread out.
- You are not required to calculate SD in written papers. You must interpret it.

**Same mean, different spread.** Sample A (42, 45, 47, 44, 46, 43, 48, 45, 44, 46) and sample B (38, 52, 45, 41, 49, 36, 54, 45, 47, 43) both have a mean of 45.0 mm. The SD of A is about 1.8 mm and the SD of B is about 5.8 mm. B is much more variable.

## Interpreting two samples

1. Compare means.
2. Compare spread (ranges or standard deviations).
3. If the mean plus or minus 1 SD ranges overlap greatly, the difference may be due to chance.
4. If the ranges are well separated, the difference is more likely to be real.
5. State that more data or a statistical test would increase confidence.

**Using mean plus or minus 2 SD:** species P, mean 62 mm with SD 2 mm, spans 58 to 66 mm. Species Q, mean 70 mm with SD 3 mm, spans 64 to 76 mm. The overlap (64 to 66 mm) means some birds cannot be identified by wing length alone.

## Exam traps

- Mixing up intraspecific and interspecific.
- "Random" does not mean "haphazard". A method with random numbers is needed.
- Comparing only means.
- "A larger sample removes chance." It reduces the effect of chance.
- "Small SD = small values." It means small spread.
- Ignoring overlap when deciding that two species or two treatments differ.
- Concluding that a trait is genetic without comparing individuals in the same environment.

## Quick self-test

1. Define intraspecific variation.
2. A student measures the beak length of finches of two different species. Is the variation inter- or intraspecific?
3. Why should the sampling positions in a field be chosen using random numbers?
4. Why is a sample of 5 less reliable than a sample of 100?
5. What does a large standard deviation tell you?
6. Roughly what percentage of values lie within two standard deviations of the mean in a normal distribution?
7. A trait differs between genetically identical plants in two soils. State the cause.
8. Two samples have the same mean. What further information is needed to compare them?
9. Give one factor that might cause bias in selecting leaves for measurement.
10. Species R has a mean body length of 30 mm with an SD of 2 mm. What range includes about 95 % of individuals?

### Answers

1. Variation between members of the same species.
2. Interspecific.
3. To give every position an equal chance of being chosen and avoid bias.
4. A small sample is more affected by chance and by extreme values, so its mean is less likely to represent the population.
5. The values are widely spread around the mean.
6. About 95 %.
7. Environmental factors (soil).
8. The spread, for example range or standard deviation.
9. Any one from choosing the largest leaves, choosing the most accessible leaves, avoiding damaged leaves.
10. 30 plus or minus 4 mm, so 26 to 34 mm.
