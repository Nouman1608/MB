This guide goes deeper on section 6.5 (Pressure groups) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The section has one content statement, 6.5.1 The role of pressure groups, with one point: "how pressure groups can influence business decisions". The broader [ethical issues and pressure groups guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-and-pressure-groups/) lists the methods groups use and costs a boycott. This page looks at which *decisions* pressure groups change, why some campaigns succeed while others fail, and how a business should decide what to do when it becomes a target. Revise with the [pressure groups revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-pressure-groups-revision-notes/) and practise with the [pressure groups practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-pressure-groups-practice/).

All businesses, groups and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Point in 6.5.1 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| How pressure groups can influence business decisions | Explain the methods a group uses, which business decisions they affect, and how much influence the group is likely to have in a given case |

The wording is about *influence on decisions*. An answer that only describes what a pressure group is, without linking it to a decision the business must make, misses the point of the content.

## What a pressure group is

A pressure group is an organised group of people who share a concern and try to change the decisions of businesses or government. Groups may campaign about the environment, animal welfare, workers' rights, consumer safety or a local issue such as a new building. Pressure groups are not among the stakeholder groups the syllabus lists in section 1.5, and their members may not buy from, work for or live near the business at all. It gains power by affecting people who *are* stakeholders, such as customers, the local community and government.

## Which business decisions they influence

Pressure groups can change almost any decision, but these are the most common.

| Decision | Example of pressure | Possible change |
|---|---|---|
| What to produce | Campaign against a product containing a harmful ingredient | Remove the ingredient or withdraw the product |
| How to produce | Campaign against factory waste reaching a river | Install cleaner equipment, change process |
| Where to locate | Local residents oppose a new warehouse near a school | Choose another site, or change the design and delivery hours |
| Which suppliers to use | Campaign about child labour at a supplier | Change supplier or require improvements |
| How to promote | Complaints that adverts target young children | Withdraw or change the adverts |
| Packaging | Campaign against single-use plastic | Switch to recyclable packaging |

## How they influence decisions

Pressure groups have no legal power over a business. They influence it by working on three things the business cares about.

1. **Sales.** A **boycott** asks customers to stop buying. Even a small fall in sales can matter if the business's profit is small compared with its revenue.
2. **Reputation.** Publicity through news stories, social media posts, petitions and peaceful protests makes the issue visible. A damaged brand can lose customers, employees and investors long after a campaign ends.
3. **The law.** **Lobbying** means trying to persuade government to pass laws or change rules. If it succeeds, every business in the industry must change, not just the one targeted.

Groups may also meet managers directly to negotiate. A business that agrees to change can then be praised by the group, which works in its favour.

## What makes a campaign effective

| Factor | More influence when... | Less influence when... |
|---|---|---|
| Public support | Many people agree and the issue is easy to understand | The issue is complicated or few people care |
| Media attention | The story spreads widely, for example through social media | The campaign receives little coverage |
| The business's brand | It sells directly to the public under a well-known name | It sells to other businesses and the public do not know its name |
| Customers' choices | Customers can easily switch to a rival | There are few alternatives |
| Cost of the change | The change asked for is cheap or clear | The change would be very costly or is vague |
| Size of the group | It is large, well organised and well funded | It is small and has few resources |

### Worked example: two campaigns compared

**Campaign 1.** Greenhaven Watch, a local group, objects to Sallowmere Quarry extending its site towards a village. The quarry sells crushed stone to construction firms, not to the public. The group collects 900 signatures.

**Campaign 2.** A national group criticises Kessock Coffee, a café chain, for using single-use cups. A petition is signed by 4,200 of Kessock's 30,000 loyalty-card members.

```
Share of Kessock's loyalty members who signed = 4,200 / 30,000 x 100 = 14%
```

Kessock is more likely to change. Its customers are the public, they can easily buy coffee elsewhere, 14% of its known regular customers have signed, and switching to reusable or recyclable cups is a clear, affordable change. Sallowmere sells to businesses that are unlikely to boycott it, so the group's best route is to lobby the local council, which decides whether the extension is allowed. The quarry may still change its plans, for example by planting trees as a barrier and limiting working hours, to win the council's approval.

## How a business can respond

| Response | When it may suit | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Make the change asked for | The cost is affordable and the group has strong support | Higher costs; other groups may make further demands |
| Negotiate a partial change | The full demand is too costly | The group may continue the campaign |
| Explain its position publicly | The group's claims are inaccurate | May look defensive; can spread the story further |
| Ignore the campaign | Support is small and the business is little known | Campaign may grow; reputation damage can be sudden |

### Annotated model answer

Question: *Explain how a pressure group could influence Kessock Coffee's decision about the cups it uses.*

Model answer: The pressure group could organise a petition and share it on social media, so many of Kessock's customers hear that its cups cannot be recycled. Some customers may choose to buy coffee at rival cafés instead, which reduces Kessock's sales. To protect its sales and reputation, Kessock may decide to switch to recyclable cups or offer a discount to customers who bring their own.

Why it earns credit: a method (petition and social media), the link to Kessock's customers, the effect on sales, and the decision that results.

## Pressure groups are not always opponents

Some campaigns reward businesses. A group may publish a list of businesses that meet its standards, or praise a firm that changes. A business can use this in promotion. Pressure groups can also warn a business early about an issue that would otherwise grow into a bigger problem, such as a change customers will soon expect.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify:** name a method, such as "a boycott" or "lobbying government".
- **Explain:** method, then effect on customers or government, then effect on the business, then the decision that changes.
- **Consider / justify:** judge how much influence the group will have, using the factors table, or decide how the business should respond.

## Common errors

- Saying a pressure group can force a business to change or shut it down. It can only influence.
- Describing protests without saying how they affect the business's sales, reputation or the law.
- Assuming every campaign succeeds. Use the case to judge how strong the group is.
- Recommending that a business always gives in. Sometimes negotiating or explaining is more sensible.
