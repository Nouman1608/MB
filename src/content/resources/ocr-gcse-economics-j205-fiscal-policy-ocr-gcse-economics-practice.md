## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State two sources of government revenue. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by a budget deficit. **[2]**

**3.** Identify whether each tax is direct or indirect.

**(a)** Income tax **[1]**
**(b)** VAT **[1]**

**4.** A government raises £880bn in revenue and spends £915bn. GDP is £2,000bn.

**(a)** Calculate the budget balance. **[1]**
**(b)** Express it as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**

**5.** Country P taxes income at 0% up to £12,500, 20% from £12,500 to £50,000 and 45% above £50,000. Lucy earns £62,000.

**(a)** Calculate Lucy's income tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate her average tax rate to one decimal place. **[1]**

**6.** Which one of the following is an example of contractionary fiscal policy? **[1]**

- A. A cut in interest rates
- B. A rise in the rate of VAT
- C. An increase in benefit payments
- D. More spending on road building

**7.** Explain why the national debt can rise even when the budget deficit is falling. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** The government places a duty of £0.60 per can on a sugary drink. Before the duty, the price was £1.50 and 2.0 million cans were sold each week. After the duty, the price is £1.95 and 1.7 million cans are sold each week.

**(a)** Calculate how much of the £0.60 duty per can is paid by consumers and how much by producers. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the weekly tax revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what the figures suggest about the price elasticity of demand for the drink. **[2]**

**9.** Country N is in a recession with unemployment of 8%. The government plans to spend an extra £20bn on building railways and schools.

**(a)** Analyse how this spending could reduce unemployment. **[3]**
**(b)** State one opportunity cost of the policy. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason the policy may take time to work. **[1]**

**10.** Using Country P's tax system from question 5, Xavier earns £24,000 and Yasmin earns £120,000.

**(a)** Calculate each person's average tax rate, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain whether the tax is progressive. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a government should cut its spending to reduce a budget deficit. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the economic consequences of raising the top rate of income tax to redistribute income. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: income tax; national insurance; corporation tax; VAT; excise duties; government borrowing (one mark each) [1] [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A budget deficit occurs when government spending is greater than government revenue in a year [1]. The gap must be financed by borrowing, which adds to the national debt [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Direct [1]. **(b)** Indirect [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 880 − 915 = **−£35bn, a deficit** [1].
**(b)** 35 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **1.75%** of GDP [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 20% band: (50,000 − 12,500) × 0.2 = £7,500; 45% band: 12,000 × 0.45 = £5,400 [1]. Total = **£12,900** [1].
**(b)** 12,900 ÷ 62,000 × 100 = **20.8%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each rate only to the income inside its band. Charging 45% on all £62,000 is the most common error.

**6.** **B** [1]. A higher tax reduces spending. A is monetary policy; C and D are expansionary. **[1]**

**7.** The debt is the total amount owed from past borrowing [1]. As long as there is still a deficit, however small, the government is borrowing more, so the debt keeps rising [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Consumers: 1.95 − 1.50 = **£0.45** per can [1]. Producers: 0.60 − 0.45 = **£0.15** per can [1].
**(b)** £0.60 × 1.7 million = **£1.02m** per week [1].
**(c)** Price rose by 30% but quantity fell by only 15% [1], so demand is price inelastic, which is why most of the duty is passed on to consumers [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** The government's spending creates demand for construction firms, which hire more workers [1]. Those workers earn and spend more in shops and services, so other firms see higher sales [1]. Firms across the economy then raise output and employ more people, reducing cyclical unemployment [1].
**(b)** For example, the £20bn could have been spent on health care, or used to cut taxes, or not borrowed [1].
**(c)** Large projects need planning and approval before building starts, so jobs are created months or years later [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** Xavier: (24,000 − 12,500) × 0.2 = £2,300; 2,300 ÷ 24,000 = **9.6%** [1]. Yasmin: £7,500 + (70,000 × 0.45) = £7,500 + £31,500 = £39,000 [1]; 39,000 ÷ 120,000 = **32.5%** [1].
**(b)** Yes: the average tax rate is higher for the higher earner [1]. **[4]**

**11.** For: cutting spending reduces the deficit and so slows the growth of the national debt [1], lowering future interest payments, which frees money for services later [1]. It may increase confidence that the government's finances are under control [1]. Against: spending cuts reduce demand, so output may fall and unemployment rise [1], especially if the economy is weak. Lower output means lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so the deficit may fall by less than the cuts [1]. Cuts to education or infrastructure may reduce future growth, and cuts to benefits may widen inequality [1]. Evaluation: the decision depends on the size of the deficit and the state of the economy [1]. Judgement: cuts are more appropriate when the economy is growing strongly and the deficit is large; in a recession they risk worsening unemployment and may even fail to reduce the deficit much [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For: higher-income households pay more tax, which can fund benefits or services for lower-income households, narrowing the gap in after-tax income [1]. Low-income households spend more of any extra income, so spending in the economy may hold up [1]. Against: a higher top rate may reduce incentives for high earners to work extra hours or take on more responsibility [1], and may encourage tax avoidance, or lead some skilled workers and business owners to move abroad [1]. If that happens, revenue may rise less than expected [1]. Judgement: a moderate rise is likely to raise revenue and reduce inequality, but the larger the rise, the more likely the incentive and avoidance effects are to cut into the gains [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taxing all income at the highest rate rather than band by band.
- Confusing the deficit with the debt.
- Treating interest rate changes as fiscal policy.
- Leaving out opportunity cost when evaluating spending.
- Giving a judgement with no reason.
