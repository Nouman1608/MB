This study guide teaches section 3.1.1, **Structure and calculation** (specification references N1 to N9), of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification, for teaching from September 2015 with exams from May/June 2017 (version 1.0). It goes deeper than the topic overview: every statement in N1 to N9 is taught with a worked example. Most of this content is assessed at both tiers. Four parts sit in the specification's Higher content only column and are labelled **Higher tier only**: the product rule for counting (N5), estimating powers and roots (N6), fractional indices (N7) and surds (N8).

## Ordering and inequality symbols (N1)

To order a mix of fractions, decimals and negatives, convert everything to decimals, then place them on a number line. With negatives, the number further from zero is the **smaller** one: −0.8 < −0.3.

**Worked example 1.** Order these, smallest first: −0.6, −2/3, 5/8, 0.65, −0.06.

```
−2/3 = −0.666...     5/8 = 0.625
−0.666... < −0.6 < −0.06 < 0.625 < 0.65
Order: −2/3, −0.6, −0.06, 5/8, 0.65
```

Know all six symbols. ≠ means "is not equal to". ≤ and ≥ include the end value, so x ≥ −2 includes −2 itself.

## The four operations and place value (N2)

### Formal written methods

You need a written method for each operation. For 58 × 23, split the 23:

```
58 × 20 = 1160
58 × 3  =  174
58 × 23 = 1334
```

To divide by a decimal, multiply both numbers by the same power of 10 so the divisor is a whole number: 7.56 ÷ 0.4 = 75.6 ÷ 4 = **18.9**.

### Place value from a given fact

If you know 58 × 23 = 1334, you can write down related results by counting decimal places.

```
5.8 × 2.3   = 13.34     (one d.p. + one d.p. = two d.p.)
133.4 ÷ 0.23 = 580      (133.4 is 1334 ÷ 10, and 0.23 is 23 ÷ 100,
                          so the answer is 58 × 10 = 580)
```

Check the size with a rough estimate: 6 × 2 = 12, so 13.34 is sensible.

### Fractions, mixed numbers and negatives

Change mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying or dividing.

**Worked example 2.** Work out (a) 2 1/3 × 1 4/5 and (b) −1 5/6 + 3 1/4.

```
(a) 7/3 × 9/5 = 63/15 = 21/5 = 4 1/5
(b) −22/12 + 39/12 = 17/12 = 1 5/12
```

Sign rules: two like signs multiplied or divided give a positive; unlike signs give a negative. Subtracting a negative is adding: 4 − (−3) = 7.

### Household finance terms

The specification expects you to know: **cost price** (what a trader pays), **selling price** (what the customer pays), **profit** (selling price minus cost price, when positive), **loss** (cost price minus selling price), **debit** (money out of an account), **credit** (money in), **balance** (what is in the account; a negative balance means overdrawn), **income tax**, **VAT** (a tax added to the price of many goods and services) and **interest rate**.

**Worked example 3.** A stall buys 40 mugs at £2.15 each. It sells 35 at £4.20 and the other 5 at £1.50. Find the profit.

```
Cost price:   40 × 2.15 = £86.00
Takings:      35 × 4.20 + 5 × 1.50 = 147.00 + 7.50 = £154.50
Profit:       154.50 − 86.00 = £68.50
```

Percentage work with VAT and interest belongs to 3.1.2 and to ratio (R9); here you only need the terms.

## Inverse operations and priority of operations (N3)

Addition and subtraction undo each other, as do multiplication and division, and squaring and square-rooting. Use inverses to check: 18.9 × 0.4 = 7.56 confirms the division 7.56 ÷ 0.4 = 18.9 above.

**Cancellation** simplifies before you multiply:

```
(36 × 25) / (15 × 12)  =  (3 × 5) / (1 × 3)  ... cancel 12 into 36, 5 into 25 and 15
                       =  15 / 3 = 5
```

Priority of operations: brackets, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division (left to right), then addition and subtraction (left to right). The **reciprocal** of a number is 1 divided by it; the reciprocal of 2 1/2 = 5/2 is 2/5.

**Worked example 4.** Work out 18 − 3 × (4 − 6)² ÷ 2.

```
Brackets:   4 − 6 = −2
Power:      (−2)² = 4
× and ÷:    3 × 4 ÷ 2 = 6
Subtract:   18 − 6 = 12
```

## Primes, factors and multiples (N4)

A **prime** has exactly two factors. **Factors** (divisors) divide a number exactly; **multiples** are in its times table. The **unique factorisation theorem** says every integer greater than 1 has exactly one prime factorisation, apart from order. Write it in index form using product notation.

**Worked example 5.** Two ribbons are 90 cm and 126 cm long. Both are cut into pieces of equal length, as long as possible, with nothing left over. Find the piece length and the number of pieces.

```
90  = 2 × 3² × 5
126 = 2 × 3² × 7
HCF = 2 × 3² = 18 cm          (lowest power of each shared prime)
Pieces: 90 ÷ 18 + 126 ÷ 18 = 5 + 7 = 12
```

The LCM uses the highest power of every prime: 2 × 3² × 5 × 7 = 630. Read the context: "largest equal piece" or "biggest group" needs the HCF; "next time together" or "smallest number both divide" needs the LCM.

## Systematic listing and the product rule (N5)

List in a fixed order so nothing is missed or repeated. Choosing two colours from red, green, blue and yellow (order not mattering):

```
RG  RB  RY
    GB  GY
        BY        → 6 pairs
```

Tables and sample-space grids work the same way.

**Higher tier only.** The **product rule**: if one choice can be made in m ways and a second in n ways, together there are m × n ways.

**Worked example 6.** A code is one letter (A to Z) then two digits. How many codes are there? How many if the two digits must be different?

```
26 × 10 × 10 = 2600
26 × 10 × 9  = 2340    (only 9 choices remain for the second digit)
```

## Powers and roots (N6)

Know the squares up to 15 × 15 (1, 4, 9, … 196, 225), the cubes 1, 8, 27, 64, 125 and 1000 = 10³, and 1 million = 10⁶. Recognise powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, …), 3 (3, 9, 27, 81, 243), 4 (4, 16, 64, 256) and 5 (5, 25, 125, 625). Square roots have a positive and a negative value: x² = 49 gives x = 7 or x = −7. Higher roots work the same way: the cube root of −27 is −3, the fourth root of 81 is 3 (3⁴ = 81) and the fifth root of 32 is 2 (2⁵ = 32).

**Higher tier only.** Estimate a root by trapping it between known powers.

```
√40:  6² = 36 and 7² = 49, 40 is nearer 36  → about 6.3
∛70:  4³ = 64 and 5³ = 125, 70 is just above 64 → just over 4
```

## Integer and fractional indices (N7)

The index laws for integer powers are in the [Number study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-mathematics-number/). Apply them to a single base:

```
5³ × 5⁻⁴ ÷ 5⁻² = 5^(3 − 4 + 2) = 5¹ = 5
```

**Higher tier only.** a^(1/n) is the nth root of a, and a^(m/n) = (nth root of a)^m. A negative index means take the reciprocal.

```
32^(−2/5)    = 1 / (fifth root of 32)² = 1 / 2² = 1/4
(125/64)^(2/3) = (5/4)² = 25/16
```

## Calculating exactly (N8)

Exact means no rounding. Leave fractions as fractions and π as π.

**Worked example 7.** A quarter circle has radius 6 cm. Find its area and perimeter in terms of π.

```
Area      = ¼ × π × 6² = 9π cm²
Perimeter = two radii + quarter of circumference
          = 6 + 6 + ¼ × 2 × π × 6 = 12 + 3π cm
```

**Higher tier only.** A **surd** is a root left in exact form. Simplify by taking out the largest square factor; rationalise by multiplying top and bottom by the surd.

```
√98 = √49 × √2 = 7√2
√20 × √15 = √300 = √100 × √3 = 10√3
```

## Standard form (N9)

A × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ A < 10 and n an integer. For × and ÷, handle the numbers and the powers separately, then adjust A.

```
(3.2 × 10⁴) × (5 × 10⁻⁷) = 16 × 10⁻³ = 1.6 × 10⁻²
(4.8 × 10⁵) ÷ (1.2 × 10⁻³) = 4 × 10⁸
```

For + and −, match the powers first: 3.4 × 10⁵ + 6.1 × 10⁴ = 3.4 × 10⁵ + 0.61 × 10⁵ = **4.01 × 10⁵**.

On a calculator, a display such as 2.5E−04 means 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.00025. When you copy it down, write it as A × 10ⁿ, never as "2.5E−04".

## Common errors

- Ordering negatives by size of digits: −0.6 is **greater** than −2/3.
- Losing the decimal point when using a given fact; check with an estimate.
- Working left to right instead of following priority: 18 − 3 × 4 is 6, not 60.
- Squaring a negative without brackets: (−2)² = 4, but −2² = −4.
- Using the LCM when the context asks for the largest equal group (HCF).
- Listing ordered pairs twice (RG and GR) when order does not matter.
- Treating 1 as prime, or leaving the prime factorisation out of index form when asked.
- Leaving standard form as 16 × 10⁻³ instead of adjusting to 1.6 × 10⁻².
- (Higher) Writing √20 × √15 as √35, or not simplifying √300 fully.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you order negative decimals and fractions?**
Convert everything to decimals and place them on a number line. A negative number closer to zero is greater, so −0.6 is greater than −2/3.

**How do you use a given multiplication fact to find related answers?**
Count the decimal places in the numbers you are multiplying and adjust the given answer to match, then check the size with a rough estimate.

**How do you add numbers in standard form?**
Match the powers of 10 first, then add the numbers. For example, 3.4 × 10⁵ + 6.1 × 10⁴ = 3.4 × 10⁵ + 0.61 × 10⁵ = 4.01 × 10⁵.

**What household finance terms do you need for AQA GCSE Maths?**
The AQA 8300 specification expects you to know terms such as cost price, selling price, profit and loss, along with income tax, VAT and interest rate.
