Subtopic 14.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers thermometric properties, the thermodynamic scale, kelvin–Celsius conversion and absolute zero. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Temperature scales study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-temperature-scales/); to apply the ideas, try the [Temperature scales practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-temperature-scales-practice/).

## Thermometric properties

A physical property that varies with temperature can be used to measure temperature. The syllabus names four:

| Property | Change as temperature rises |
|---|---|
| Density of a liquid | Usually decreases |
| Volume of a gas at constant pressure | Increases (∝ T for an ideal gas) |
| Resistance of a metal | Increases |
| E.m.f. of a thermocouple | Depends on the temperature difference between the junctions |

A good thermometric property:

- changes **measurably** with temperature
- has **a single value** for each temperature in the range (water's density fails near 4 °C)
- is **reproducible**

## Empirical scales

- Built from one property of one substance.
- Calibrated at **two fixed points**, divided linearly between them:

```
θ = (X_θ – X₀) / (X₁₀₀ – X₀) × 100 °C
```

- All empirical scales agree **at the fixed points** (by definition).
- Between and beyond the fixed points they **generally disagree**, because different properties do not vary with temperature in the same way.

## The thermodynamic scale

- **Does not depend on the property of any particular substance.**
- Zero at **absolute zero**; unit the **kelvin** (K); a change of 1 K = a change of 1 °C.
- An ideal gas is a model whose behaviour matches the thermodynamic scale; real gases at low pressure come very close.
- Write "K", never "°K"; symbol T (Celsius: θ).

## Absolute zero

- **0 K = –273.15 °C**: the lowest possible temperature; no negative kelvin values.
- Found by extrapolating gas volume (constant pressure) or pressure (constant volume) against θ to zero.

## Conversions

```
T / K = θ / °C + 273.15
```

| Celsius | Kelvin |
|---|---|
| –273.15 °C | 0 K |
| –196 °C | 77 K |
| 0 °C | 273.15 K |
| 20 °C | 293.15 K |
| 100 °C | 373.15 K |

- **Differences**: identical on both scales (Δθ = ΔT). Do not add 273.15 to a change.
- **Ratios, gas equations, ³⁄₂kT**: must use kelvin.
- 20 °C → 40 °C multiplies T by 1.07, not 2.

## Two calculations to be able to do

**Disagreeing scales (invented data).** A thermocouple reads 0.00 mV at 0 °C, 4.10 mV at 100 °C and 2.20 mV in a liquid: 53.7 °C. A resistance thermometer reads 100.0 Ω, 138.5 Ω and 120.0 Ω in the same liquid: 51.9 °C. Both are in equilibrium with the liquid; the scales differ because the properties vary differently between the fixed points.

**Absolute zero by extrapolation (invented data).** Gas at constant pressure: 250 cm³ at 20.0 °C, 318 cm³ at 100.0 °C.

1. Gradient = 68 ÷ 80.0 = 0.85 cm³ per °C.
2. Fall in θ needed to reach V = 0: 250 ÷ 0.85 = 294 °C.
3. Absolute zero ≈ 20.0 – 294 = –274 °C.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Thermometric property | A measurable physical property that varies with temperature |
| Fixed point | A reproducible temperature used to calibrate a scale |
| Empirical scale | A scale based on a particular property of a particular substance |
| Thermodynamic scale | Absolute scale independent of any substance, zero at absolute zero |
| Absolute zero | The lowest possible temperature, 0 K |
| Calibration | Marking a thermometer's readings against known temperatures |

## Exam traps

- Naming a device ("thermocouple") when the question asks for a **property** ("e.m.f. of a thermocouple").
- Saying two calibrated thermometers must agree at all temperatures.
- Writing –273.15 K for absolute zero.
- Adding 273.15 to a temperature **difference**.
- Using Celsius in pV = nRT or in a ratio.
- Writing that the thermodynamic scale depends on the behaviour of a gas.
- Forgetting that a thermocouple's e.m.f. depends on the **difference** between junction temperatures.

## Self-test

1. State three physical properties named in the syllabus that can be used to measure temperature.
2. Give two requirements of a good thermometric property.
3. Why would the density of water be a poor thermometric property between 0 °C and 8 °C?
4. A resistance thermometer reads 100.0 Ω at 0 °C, 138.5 Ω at 100 °C and 115.4 Ω in a liquid. Find the temperature on this empirical scale.
5. Why might a thermocouple, calibrated at the same fixed points, give a different reading in that liquid?
6. What is meant by saying the thermodynamic scale does not depend on any particular substance?
7. Convert 37.0 °C to kelvin.
8. Convert 4.2 K to °C.
9. A liquid is heated from 15 °C to 65 °C. State the temperature rise in kelvin.
10. State the lowest possible temperature in kelvin and in °C.
11. By what factor does the thermodynamic temperature change when water is heated from 0 °C to 100 °C?
12. On what does the e.m.f. of a thermocouple depend?

**Answers:** 1. Any three of: density of a liquid; volume of a gas at constant pressure; resistance of a metal; e.m.f. of a thermocouple. 2. Any two: changes measurably with temperature; has a single value for each temperature in the range; reproducible. 3. Its density is a maximum near 4 °C, so the same density occurs at two different temperatures. 4. θ = (115.4 – 100.0) ÷ (138.5 – 100.0) × 100 = 40.0 °C. 5. The e.m.f. and the resistance do not vary with temperature in the same way between the fixed points. 6. Its definition is not based on how any one material's property changes, so all correct thermodynamic temperatures agree regardless of the thermometer's substance. 7. 310.15 K (310 K). 8. –268.95 °C (about –269 °C). 9. 50 K. 10. 0 K; –273.15 °C. 11. 373.15 ÷ 273.15 = 1.37. 12. The temperature difference between its two junctions (and the pair of metals used).
