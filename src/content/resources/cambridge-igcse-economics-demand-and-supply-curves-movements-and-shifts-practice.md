## Questions

**1.** Define demand. **[2]**

**2.** For each event, state whether it causes a movement along a curve or a shift of a curve, and name the curve (demand or supply) and the term for the change, in the market for bicycles.

**(a)** The price of bicycles falls. **[1]**
**(b)** The price of cycle helmets, which are bought with bicycles, rises sharply. **[1]**
**(c)** A new technology cuts the cost of making bicycle frames. **[1]**
**(d)** The price of bicycles rises, and firms respond. **[1]**

**3.** The table shows the daily demand for sandwiches at a school canteen from two groups of customers. All figures are illustrative.

| Price (cu) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 50 | 40 | 30 | 20 | 10 |
| Staff | 40 | 35 | 30 | 25 | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate market demand at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** The price rises from 3 cu to 5 cu. Use the correct term to describe the change in demand and state the change in quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded in part (b), to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**4.** Town J has 240 identical households. At a price of 3 cu each household buys 2 loaves a day. The number of households rises by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate market demand before the change. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate market demand after the change. **[2]**
**(c)** State the term for the change in demand at 3 cu. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why a rise in the price of butter shifts the demand curve for margarine to the right. **[3]**

**6.** Use the market demand from question 3. A gym opens next to the canteen and demand rises by 15 sandwiches at every price.

**(a)** Complete the new demand schedule. **[2]**
**(b)** State the term for this change. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in quantity demanded at 4 cu and at 6 cu. **[2]**

**7.** Define supply and explain why a supply curve slopes upward. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows the weekly supply of tea (in kg) from three growers at different prices. All figures are illustrative.

| Price (cu) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grower 1 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
| Grower 2 | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| Grower 3 | 10 | 10 | 20 | 20 | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** The price falls from 6 cu to 4 cu. Name the movement and state the change in quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied in part (b), to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why the supply of wooden chairs might increase. **[4]**

**10.** The table shows the supply of bottled water in Country A before a tax. The government then imposes an indirect tax of 2 cu per bottle. All figures are illustrative.

| Price (cu) | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supply before tax | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |

**(a)** Complete the schedule of supply after the tax. **[3]**
**(b)** State the direction of the shift and the term for the change. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the tax causes this shift. **[2]**

**11.** The table shows the market demand for electric fans in Country G. All figures are illustrative.

| Price (cu) | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 500 | 400 | 300 | 200 | 100 |

**(a)** The price falls from 50 cu to 30 cu. State the term and the change in quantity demanded. **[2]**
**(b)** A heatwave raises demand by 25% at every price. Complete the new schedule. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how a fall in the price of air-conditioning units could affect demand for fans. **[3]**
**(d)** A shop sees sales rise from 300 to 400 fans. Give two different explanations, one a movement and one a shift, and say why sales figures alone cannot tell you which happened. **[3]**

**12.** The table shows the daily supply of tomatoes (kg) from three farms in Country H. All figures are illustrative.

| Price (cu) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Farm P | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
| Farm Q | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| Farm R | 0 | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** Farm Q installs irrigation and its supply rises by 50% at every price. Calculate the new market supply at each price. **[3]**
**(c)** State the term for the change in market supply and calculate the percentage change at a price of 4 cu, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain two other conditions that could decrease the supply of tomatoes. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Demand is how much of a good or service [1] buyers are ready and able to purchase at each price over a stated period [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition tends to gain credit when it includes both "willing and able" and the link to price; "what people want" on its own misses the point that demand needs money behind it.

**2. (a)** Movement along the demand curve [1]: an extension of demand. **[1]**
**(b)** Shift of the demand curve for bicycles to the left [1]: a decrease in demand, because helmets are complements. **[1]**
**(c)** Shift of the supply curve to the right [1]: an increase in supply. **[1]**
**(d)** Movement along the supply curve [1]: an extension of supply. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the curve, the movement or shift and the term together is safest. A response that says "demand rises" for part (a) uses the wrong term, because it describes a shift and not an extension.

**3. (a)** Add the two columns at each price [1]: 90, 75, 60, 45, 30 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The price rises, so quantity demanded falls from 75 to 45 [1]: a contraction of demand of 30 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Percentage change = (45 − 75)/75 × 100 [1] = **−40.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In percentage change, the starting quantity is the divisor. Starting from 45 instead of 75 gives a different answer.

**4. (a)** 240 × 2 = **480** loaves [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New households = 240 × 1.10 = 264 [1]. Market demand = 264 × 2 = **528** loaves [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** An increase in demand [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The price did not change, so the term is "increase", not "extension".

**5.** Butter and margarine are substitutes [1]. When butter becomes dearer, some buyers switch to margarine [1]. More margarine is wanted at every price, so the demand curve shifts to the right (an increase in demand) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain of three links (relationship, buyer behaviour, effect on the curve) tends to be stronger than a one-line assertion.

**6. (a)** Add 15 to each market value [1]: 105, 90, 75, 60, 45 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** An increase in demand (the curve shifts to the right) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** At 4 cu: 15/60 × 100 = **25%** [1]. At 6 cu: 15/30 × 100 = **50%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The same shift is a bigger percentage where the original quantity is small.

**7.** Supply is how much of a good or service producers are ready and able to sell [1] at each price in a stated period [1]. A higher price brings more revenue per unit, so selling more is more profitable [1], and it may draw in new sellers or cover higher costs per unit as output grows [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explaining the upward slope tends to gain credit when it ties a higher price to a firm's incentive.

**8. (a)** Add the three columns [1]: 30, 50, 80, 100, 130 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Quantity supplied falls from 130 to 80 [1]: a contraction of supply of 50 kg [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** (80 − 130)/130 × 100 [1] = **−38.5%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A fall in price moves you along the same supply curve, so "contraction" is the term. The starting value is 130, not 80.

**9.** Any two developed reasons, worth two marks each. Example: lower costs of production, such as cheaper timber [1], so each firm can supply more at every price and the curve shifts right [1]. Better technology or new machines [1] raise output per worker, so more chairs are supplied at each price [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain two" needs two separate causes each developed with a consequence. A change in the chair's own price is not a valid reason, because it moves along the curve.

**10. (a)** New supply at price p equals old supply at p minus 2 [1]. At 3, 4 and 5 cu the new supply is 0 [1]. At 6, 7 and 8 cu it is 20, 40, 60 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Leftward shift of the supply curve, called a decrease in supply [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** A tax raises the cost of selling each bottle [1], so sellers need a higher price to supply the same quantity, or supply less at each price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction in words and give the cost reason.

**11. (a)** Quantity demanded rises from 200 to 400 [1]: an extension of demand of 200 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 500 × 1.25 and so on [1]: 625, 500, 375, 250, 125 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Air-conditioning units are substitutes for fans [1]. Cheaper units tempt buyers away from fans [1], so demand for fans decreases at every price and the curve shifts left [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Movement: the price of fans fell, so more were bought along the same curve (extension) [1]. Shift: demand increased because of another condition, such as a heatwave or an advert, so more fans were bought at the same price [1]. Sales show only the quantity sold, not the price or the conditions behind it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (d) tends to gain credit when you name the evidence needed, such as the price at the time.

**12. (a)** Add the three farms [1]: 10, 30, 60, 90, 120 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Farm Q's new supply: 0, 15, 30, 45, 60 [1]. Add P and R to the new Q values [1]: 10, 35, 70, 105, 140 [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** An increase in supply [1]. At 4 cu: (105 − 90)/90 × 100 = **16.7%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Any two developed conditions, worth two marks each. Example: a rise in fertiliser prices [1] raises costs, so farms supply less at each price and the curve shifts left [1]. A drought or flood [1] cuts yields, so less is available at each price [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b) the 50% applies only to Farm Q. Applying it to the whole market is a frequent slip. Show the new column for Farm Q before the totals.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "increase in demand" for a price fall.
- Not naming the curve that moves, or the direction of the shift.
- Mixing up the base of a percentage change. Use the original quantity.
- Applying a percentage change to the whole market when it affects only one firm or group.
- Treating a tax as an increase in supply.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when aggregating.
