## Markets and sub-markets

A **market** is wherever buyers and sellers make contact so that trade can happen. It need not be a place: online trading counts. A **sub-market** is a distinct part of a wider market with its own buyers, sellers and price. The housing market contains sub-markets for flats, family houses and rented homes; the labour market contains one for nurses and another for electricians. Prices can move differently in each.

## Individual and market demand

**Demand** is the set of quantities consumers want and can afford at each price over a set period, other things equal. A wish without the money to pay is not demand. **Market demand** adds every buyer's quantity at each price. On a diagram you add the curves horizontally, so you add quantities, not prices.

Worked example (Country A, illustrative): three households buy cinema tickets each month.

| Price (£) | Household X | Household Y | Household Z | Market demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 14 | 9 | 6 | 29 |
| 6 | 11 | 7 | 4 | 22 |
| 8 | 8 | 5 | 2 | 15 |
| 10 | 5 | 3 | 1 | 9 |
| 12 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 3 |

At £4: 14 + 9 + 6 = 29. Household Z stops buying at £12, but the market still demands 3. A new household in the area shifts market demand right.

## Types of demand

- **Derived demand:** demand for a good because it is needed to make something else. Demand for steel comes from demand for cars and bridges. Labour demand is derived from demand for the output. A fall in demand for the final product cuts demand for the inputs.
- **Joint demand:** complements demanded together, such as printers and ink cartridges. A rise in the price of one reduces the quantity demanded of that good and decreases demand for the other.
- **Composite demand:** demand for a good with several uses, which is the sum of the demands for each use. Illustration: at £0.80 a litre, milk sells 60 (million litres) for drinking, 30 for cheese and 10 for yoghurt, so composite demand is 100. If cheese demand rises by 20 at that price, composite demand becomes 120 and less milk is left for the other uses.
- **Competitive demand:** substitutes that compete for the same spending, such as tea and coffee. A rise in the price of one raises demand for the other.

## Why demand slopes downwards

### Marginal utility

**Utility** is satisfaction. **Marginal utility** is the satisfaction added by consuming one more unit. For most goods it diminishes as you consume more. A consumer pays only up to the value of the last unit's extra satisfaction, so a lower price is needed to sell more.

Worked example: one consumer's marginal utility from cups of tea, measured in money (£), is 3.00, 2.40, 1.80, 1.20 and 0.60 for cups one to five.

- At £1.50 she buys cups one to three, because the third (1.80) is worth more than the price and the fourth (1.20) is not. She buys 3 cups.
- At £1.00 the fourth cup (1.20) is now worth buying. She buys 4 cups.

Across goods, utility is maximised when marginal utility per pound is equal. If good A has marginal utility 12 utils at £2 (12 ÷ 2 = 6 per pound) and good B has 15 utils at £3 (15 ÷ 3 = 5 per pound), she gains by buying more A and less B. A fall in the price of a good raises its marginal utility per pound, so she buys more of it.

### Income and substitution effects

A fall in price has two effects.

- **Substitution effect:** the good is now cheaper relative to others, so consumers switch towards it.
- **Income effect:** the fall raises real income, meaning purchasing power. Take money income of £400 and a price cut from £4 to £3.20: she can now afford 400 ÷ 3.20 = 125 units instead of 400 ÷ 4 = 100, a 25 per cent rise in what her income can buy. For a normal good she uses some of this extra power to buy more.

Both effects raise quantity demanded of a normal good. For an inferior good the income effect works the other way.

## Movement along versus shift of demand

Only the good's **own price** moves you **along** the curve: an **extension** when price falls, a **contraction** when it rises. Any other factor changes demand at every price and **shifts** the curve: an **increase** shifts it right, a **decrease** left.

Worked example (good M, illustrative; quantity in thousands, price in £). Demand is P = 20 − 4Q. At £8, Q = 3. If price falls to £4, Q = 4: an extension, because only price changed. If incomes rise and demand becomes P = 24 − 4Q, then at £8, Q = (24 − 8) ÷ 4 = 4. That is an increase in demand, a shift right, of 1 thousand at every price.

Factors that shift demand:

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Income | Rise increases demand for normal goods, decreases it for inferior goods |
| Price of substitutes | Rise in a substitute's price increases demand |
| Price of complements | Rise in a complement's price decreases demand |
| Tastes, fashion, advertising | Favourable change increases demand |
| Population and its age structure | Growth or a change in make-up shifts demand |
| Expectations | Expecting higher prices later increases demand now |
| Interest rates and credit | Cheaper borrowing increases demand for costly goods |
| Government action | Bans, regulations or information campaigns change demand |

In a diagram answer, label both axes, name each curve and mark the original and new quantity.

## Consumer surplus

**Consumer surplus** is the gain buyers get from paying less than the most they were prepared to pay. In a diagram, look for the region under the demand curve and over the price line, out to the quantity bought. It is a triangle when demand is a straight line.

For good M at £8, quantity is 3 and the triangle has height 20 − 8 = 12:

consumer surplus = ½ × 3 × 12 = £18 (thousand).

**A fall in price from £8 to £4.** Quantity becomes 4 and the triangle's height is 20 − 4 = 16, so surplus = ½ × 4 × 16 = 32. The rise is 14, in two parts:

- Existing buyers pay £4 less on 3 units: a rectangle of 3 × 4 = 12.
- New buyers gain a small triangle: ½ × 1 × 4 = 2.

Check: 12 + 2 = 14. A price rise from £4 to £8 reverses this: surplus falls by 14, or 14 ÷ 32 = 43.75 per cent.

**Evaluation.**

- The gain depends on the size of the price change and the slope of the demand curve.
- The curve is a model. Real willingness to pay is hard to measure, and measuring it in money treats a pound as worth the same to the rich and the poor.
- The cause of the price change matters. If demand increases and price rises, surplus can still rise. Using P = 24 − 4Q, a price of £10 gives Q = 3.5 and surplus = ½ × 3.5 × 14 = 24.5, above the original 18.
- A lower price is not always good: if the good harms others, cheaper consumption can reduce wider welfare, and a cut in quality means consumers gain less than the figure suggests.
- Firms that charge different prices to different buyers can capture some surplus.

## Individual and market supply

**Supply** is the set of quantities producers choose to offer at each price over a set period, other things equal. **Market supply** is the horizontal sum of all firms' supplies. If three firms supply 40, 25 and 15 units at £10, market supply at £10 is 80.

Supply slopes upwards because a higher price covers the higher marginal cost of extra output and draws more firms into the market.

## Types of supply

- **Joint supply:** one process yields two or more products, such as beef and hides. If beef prices rise, more cattle are reared and the supply of hides increases, which pushes hide prices down.
- **Composite supply:** a good supplied for more than one use or market, such as oil going to fuel and plastics. Total supply is the sum across uses, and a rise in supply to one use reduces what is left for another.
- **Competitive supply:** producers can switch resources between alternatives, so more of one product means less of the other. A farmer with 100 hectares earns £480 a hectare from wheat and £450 from barley. If the wheat margin rises to £520 she moves land to wheat and the supply of barley falls.

## Movement along versus shift of supply

If the good's own price is the only thing that moves, you slide along one curve: an **extension** when price rises, a **contraction** when it falls. Other factors shift supply: an **increase** shifts it right (more at every price), a **decrease** left.

Worked example (good N, illustrative). Supply is P = 2 + 2Q (Q in thousands). At £8, Q = 3. A fall in unit costs of £1 gives P = 1 + 2Q, so at £8, Q = 3.5: an increase in supply.

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Costs of production (wages, raw materials, energy) | Rise decreases supply |
| Technology | Better technology increases it |
| Indirect taxes | Rise decreases supply |
| Subsidies | Increase supply |
| Prices of other goods | Higher price of an alternative product decreases supply of this one |
| Number of firms | More firms increase supply |
| Weather and natural events | Poor harvests decrease supply |
| Expectations | Expecting higher prices later can reduce supply now |

## Producer surplus

**Producer surplus** is the gain sellers get from receiving more than the least they would have accepted. On a diagram it is the area above the supply curve and under the price line, out to the quantity sold.

For good N at £8, Q = 3 and the triangle's height is 8 − 2 = 6:

producer surplus = ½ × 3 × 6 = £9 (thousand).

**A rise in price from £8 to £10.** Q = 4 and the height is 8, so surplus = ½ × 4 × 8 = 16. The gain is 16 − 9 = 7:

- Rectangle on existing units: 3 × 2 = 6.
- Triangle on new units: ½ × 1 × 2 = 1.

**Evaluation.**

- Producer surplus is not profit. It covers returns above the cost of the marginal unit, but fixed costs still need paying out of it.
- A price rise helps most if it comes from higher demand. One caused by higher costs shifts the curve up and may not raise surplus.
- A lower price from a cost-saving technology can lower surplus per unit while raising volume, so the net effect needs working out.
- The curves are only models and cannot be read exactly in practice.

## Common errors

- Writing "demand has fallen" for a price rise. That is a contraction; only a non-price factor shifts the curve.
- Adding prices rather than quantities to build market demand.
- Using the wrong triangle height: it is the gap between the price and the curve's intercept.
- Forgetting the rectangle when price changes.
- Giving "income" as a shifter without saying whether the good is normal or inferior.
- Defining composite demand as demand for complements.
