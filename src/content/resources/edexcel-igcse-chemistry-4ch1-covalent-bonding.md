## What a covalent bond is (1.44, 1.45)

A **covalent bond** is a **shared pair of electrons** between two atoms, usually two non-metal atoms. The bond holds the atoms together because there is a strong **electrostatic attraction between the shared pair of electrons (negative) and the nuclei of both atoms (positive)**. Each nucleus is pulled towards the same pair of electrons, so the atoms stay joined.

Atoms share electrons so that each gains a full outer shell: 2 for hydrogen, 8 for the other atoms on the list.

## Building a dot-and-cross diagram (1.46)

Use the number of bonds each atom needs.

| Atom | Outer electrons | Electrons needed | Bonds formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| F, Cl, Br, I | 7 | 1 | 1 |
| O | 6 | 2 | 2 |
| N | 5 | 3 | 3 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 4 |

Method:

1. Join the atoms so every atom forms its number of bonds (two bonds between the same pair of atoms is a double bond; three is a triple bond).
2. Draw each bond as one dot and one cross in the overlap.
3. Add the non-bonding (lone) pairs so each atom except hydrogen has 8 outer electrons.
4. Check: total dots = outer electrons of the "dot" atoms; total crosses = outer electrons of the "cross" atoms.

**Worked example 1 — hydrogen chloride, HCl.**

```
H forms 1 bond; Cl forms 1 bond -> H-Cl
shared pair: one dot (Cl) + one cross (H)
Cl also has 3 lone pairs (6 dots)
check: Cl dots = 1 + 6 = 7; H crosses = 1
```

**Worked example 2 — ethane, C₂H₆.**

```
each C forms 4 bonds: one C-C bond + three C-H bonds
shared pairs: 1 (C-C) + 6 (C-H) = 7 pairs
no lone pairs: each C has 8 outer electrons, each H has 2
check: crosses for C = 4 + 4 = 8; dots for H = 6
```

**Worked example 3 — chloromethane, CH₃Cl.**

```
C forms 4 bonds: three C-H + one C-Cl
Cl forms 1 bond and has 3 lone pairs
total shared pairs = 4; lone pairs = 3 (all on Cl)
```

For the molecules with multiple bonds:

- **O₂**: one double bond (two shared pairs); each O has two lone pairs.
- **N₂**: one triple bond (three shared pairs); each N has one lone pair.
- **CO₂**: two C=O double bonds; each O has two lone pairs; C has no lone pairs.
- **C₂H₄ (ethene)**: one C=C double bond and four C–H bonds; no lone pairs.

A dot-and-cross diagram for a covalent molecule has **no brackets and no charges**. Those belong to ionic diagrams.

## Simple molecular substances (1.47, 1.48)

A **simple molecular** substance is made of small molecules. Inside each molecule the covalent bonds are strong. Between molecules there are only weak **intermolecular forces of attraction**. When the substance melts or boils, only the intermolecular forces are overcome; the covalent bonds do not break. Little energy is needed, so these substances are gases or liquids at room temperature, or solids with low melting points.

**Worked example 4 — the Mr trend.** Explain the trend in boiling points.

| Substance | Formula | Mr | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | 16 | −162 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | 30 | −89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | 44 | −42 |

> As Mr increases, the boiling point increases [1]. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces of attraction [1], so more energy is needed to overcome them [1].

The specification says "in general": the rule works best for molecules of a similar type, as here.

## Giant covalent structures (1.49)

In a **giant covalent** structure, a very large number of atoms are joined by covalent bonds in a continuous network. There are no separate molecules. To melt the solid, many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy, so melting and boiling points are high. Diamond and graphite are giant covalent; silicon dioxide (silica) has the same type of structure.

## Diamond, graphite and C60 fullerene (1.50)

| | Diamond | Graphite | C60 fullerene |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | Giant covalent; each C bonded to 4 others in a rigid 3-D network | Giant covalent layers; each C bonded to 3 others in hexagons | Simple molecular: separate C₆₀ molecules shaped like a hollow ball |
| Forces to overcome | Strong covalent bonds | Strong covalent bonds within layers; weak forces between layers | Weak intermolecular forces between molecules |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft and slippery: layers slide over each other | Soft |
| Electrical conductivity | Does not conduct: no free electrons | Conducts: one electron per C is delocalised and free to move along the layers | Poor conductor |
| Melting point | Very high | Very high | Much lower: only intermolecular forces are overcome |

**Model answer.** Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. **[3]**

> In graphite each carbon atom forms only three covalent bonds [1], so one outer electron per atom is delocalised [1]. These electrons can move through the layers and carry charge, whereas in diamond all four outer electrons are used in bonds, so there are no free electrons [1].

The properties link to uses: diamond's hardness suits cutting tools; graphite's sliding layers suit lubricants and pencils, and its conductivity suits electrodes.

## Conductivity of covalent substances (1.51)

Covalent compounds do not usually conduct, because they contain no ions and their electrons are held in bonds (or in lone pairs), so there are no charged particles free to move. Graphite is the important exception among covalent substances. The full explanation for compounds is extended in electrolysis (1.55C, Paper 2 only).

## Common mistakes

- Saying a covalent bond is "two atoms sharing electrons" without "a pair".
- Saying boiling breaks covalent bonds in a simple molecular substance.
- Putting brackets and charges on a covalent dot-and-cross diagram.
- Forgetting lone pairs on O, N or Cl.
- Calling C60 fullerene a giant structure.
- Explaining graphite's softness with "weak covalent bonds between layers": the forces between layers are not covalent bonds.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** (dot-and-cross): check every atom's outer shell and that dots and crosses add up.
- **Explain** a property: name the structure, say which forces or bonds are overcome, and whether they are weak or strong.
- **Compare** two substances: give the structure of each, not just one.
- **Explain** a trend: state the trend, then link Mr to intermolecular forces and energy.
