## 10.1.1 Statement of profit or loss

- **Meaning:** profit made over a period. **Purpose:** show how revenue becomes profit, for owners, lenders and others.
- **Layout:** revenue − cost of sales = gross profit; − expenses = profit from operations (operating profit); − taxation = profit for the year; − dividends = retained earnings.
- **Amending:** change the line affected, then recalculate every line below it, including tax.
- **Impact of a change:** a price rise or cut changes revenue only. A supplier price change alters cost of sales and gross profit. A new overhead alters expenses and profit from operations. Tax changes with profit.

| Change | First line affected | Then also |
|---|---|---|
| Higher selling price, same volume | Revenue | Gross profit, operating profit, tax, profit for the year |
| Higher material costs | Cost of sales | Gross profit onwards |
| Higher wages in the office | Expenses | Profit from operations onwards |
| Higher dividend | Dividends | Retained earnings only |

## 10.1.2 Statement of financial position

- **Meaning:** assets, liabilities and equity on one date. **Purpose:** show financial strength.
- **Contents:** non-current assets; current assets; current liabilities; net current assets; non-current liabilities; net assets; reserves and equity (issued shares + reserves).
- **Key identities:**
  - Net current assets = current assets − current liabilities.
  - Net assets = non-current assets + net current assets − non-current liabilities.
  - Net assets = equity.
  - Capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities.
- **Amending:** each change has two effects. Buying an asset with cash swaps one asset for another, so net assets do not move. Buying it with a loan raises an asset and a liability. Net assets move only when profit, loss, dividends or new shares change equity.

**Links between the statements**

| Statement of profit or loss | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|
| Profit for the year less dividends | Added to reserves |
| Depreciation expense | Lowers non-current assets |
| Cost of sales and closing inventory | Inventory in current assets |
| Credit sales | Trade receivables |
| Credit purchases | Trade payables |
| Tax charge | Current liability until paid |

## 10.1.3 Inventory valuation

- **Difficulties:** damaged or obsolete stock; changing prices; stock that is hard to count or value; uncertain selling price; judgement is needed.
- **Net realisable value (NRV)** = estimated selling price − costs of selling or finishing. The standard form is to value inventory at the lower of cost and NRV.
- **Effect of a write-down:** inventory and current assets fall; cost of sales rises; profit falls.

## 10.1.4 Depreciation

- **Role:** spreads the cost of a non-current asset over its useful life and matches cost to the revenue it helps earn. It is non-cash.
- **Straight-line:** (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. This is the only method in the syllabus.
- **Impact:** expense in the statement of profit or loss, so profit falls; net book value on the statement of financial position falls by the same amount each year.

## 10.2 Ratios (formulas as given in the syllabus appendix)

| Ratio | Formula | Presented as |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | a ratio |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | a ratio |
| Gross profit margin | gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Operating profit margin (called profit margin in 10.2.2) | profit from operations ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) | profit from operations ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Trade receivables turnover | trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 | days |
| Trade payables turnover | trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 | days |
| Rate of inventory turnover | cost of sales ÷ average inventory | times |
| Gearing | non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Price/earnings ratio | market price per share ÷ earnings per share | a number |
| Dividend yield | dividend per share ÷ market price per share × 100 | % |
| Dividend cover | profit for the year ÷ annual dividend | times |

Earnings per share is not in the appendix. The standard form is profit for the year ÷ number of issued shares.

**Quick reminder.** A cafe chain has current assets 360, of which inventory is 150, and current liabilities 240. Current ratio = 360 ÷ 240 = 1.5 : 1. Acid test = 210 ÷ 240 = 0.875, or 0.88 : 1.

### Meaning, importance, improvement

| Group | Meaning and importance | Methods of improving |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Ability to pay short-term debts; firms fail when cash runs out | Cut inventory; collect receivables sooner; longer supplier terms; long-term instead of short-term borrowing; issue shares; sale and leaseback |
| Profitability | Profit earned from sales and from capital used; pays for growth, dividends and survival | Raise price; cut cost of sales; cut expenses; sell more; reduce capital employed |
| Financial efficiency | How well inventory, receivables and payables are managed; frees cash | Just-in-time; credit control; early-settlement discounts; negotiate longer payment terms |
| Gearing | Reliance on long-term borrowing; high gearing raises interest and risk | Repay loans; issue shares; retain profit |
| Investment ratios | Return to shareholders; affects the share price and ability to raise capital | Raise profit; raise dividend; reinvest for growth; build investor confidence |

## Method in steps: interpreting a ratio

1. State the figure with its unit.
2. Compare it: with last year, with a competitor, or with the business's target.
3. Say what it means for this business (use the context in the case).
4. Give a reason that might explain it.
5. Judge whether it matters, and what other information you would want.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Gross profit margin vs operating profit margin:** the first ignores expenses; the second includes them.
- **Current ratio vs acid test:** the acid test removes inventory.
- **Dividend yield vs dividend cover:** yield is return on the share price; cover shows how safely the dividend is paid from profit.
- **Receivable days vs payable days:** low receivable days and high payable days help cash, but long supplier terms may harm relationships.
- **Profit vs cash:** depreciation lowers profit but does not move cash.

## Quick self-test

1. A machine costs 30,000, has a residual value of 3,000 and a life of 6 years. Find the straight-line charge and the net book value after 2 years.
2. A shop holds 200 units that cost 16 each. They can be sold at 18, after spending 4 each on repairs. State the NRV per unit, the inventory value and the write-down.
3. Non-current assets 1,500; current assets 700; current liabilities 400; non-current liabilities 600. Find net assets.
4. Revenue 2,400; gross profit 900; profit from operations 300. Find both margins.
5. Issued shares 500; reserves 300; non-current liabilities 200; profit from operations 150. Find capital employed and ROCE.
6. Using question 5, find the gearing ratio.
7. Receivables 150, credit sales 1,825; payables 90, credit purchases 1,095. Find both day figures.
8. Cost of sales 1,440; opening inventory 200; closing inventory 280. Find the rate of inventory turnover.
9. Dividend per share 9c; earnings per share 18c; market price 2.25 (dollars); profit for the year 360; annual dividend 180. Find yield, P/E and dividend cover.
10. Current assets 600, inventory 240, current liabilities 400. Find both liquidity ratios.
11. Give two ways to lower gearing.
12. State one reason a depreciation charge does not mean cash has left the business.

### Answers

1. (30,000 − 3,000) ÷ 6 = **4,500** a year; net book value = 30,000 − 9,000 = **21,000**.
2. NRV = 18 − 4 = **14**; cost 3,200 and NRV 2,800 so value at **2,800**; write-down **400**.
3. 1,500 + (700 − 400) − 600 = **1,200**.
4. Gross profit margin 900 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **37.5%**; operating profit margin 300 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **12.5%**.
5. Capital employed = 500 + 300 + 200 = **1,000**; ROCE = 150 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **15%**.
6. 200 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **20%**.
7. 150 ÷ 1,825 × 365 = **30 days**; 90 ÷ 1,095 × 365 = **30 days**.
8. Average inventory = 240; 1,440 ÷ 240 = **6 times**.
9. Yield = 9 ÷ 225 × 100 = **4%** (using 0.09 ÷ 2.25); P/E = 2.25 ÷ 0.18 = **12.5**; cover = 360 ÷ 180 = **2.0 times**.
10. Current ratio **1.5 : 1**; acid test = 360 ÷ 400 = **0.9 : 1**.
11. Repay a loan; issue new shares; retain more profit.
12. The cost was paid when the asset was bought; depreciation only spreads it across years.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong base: credit sales and credit purchases for the days ratios, not total revenue.
- Using closing inventory when average inventory is the data given.
- Leaving non-current liabilities out of capital employed.
- Forgetting to recalculate tax and later lines after an amendment.
- Quoting a ratio with no comparison or meaning, so there is no interpretation.
- Calling depreciation a cash cost or a fund for replacement.
- Suggesting a method of improvement that makes another ratio worse, with no mention of the trade-off.
- Judging a business on one ratio alone.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to draw up a full set of accounts for Cambridge 9609?**
No. The syllabus does not expect you to draw up a set of accounts. It expects you to calculate, amend and interpret the statement of profit or loss, the statement of financial position and the ratios.

**Which depreciation method is needed for Cambridge AS and A Level Business 9609?**
Only the straight-line method: cost minus residual value, divided by useful life. Depreciation is an expense that lowers profit but does not move cash.

**What is net realisable value?**
Net realisable value is the estimated selling price minus the costs of selling or finishing the goods. If it is below cost, inventory is valued at net realisable value.

**What is the difference between the current ratio and the acid test ratio?**
The current ratio divides current assets by current liabilities. The acid test removes inventory from current assets first, because inventory is the hardest current asset to turn into cash.
