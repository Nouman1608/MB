## Section A

**1.** State the total time and total marks for Paper 4, and how many
questions a candidate answers. **[3]**

**2.** State the two forms of text a candidate can choose between on
this paper. **[2]**

**3.** State Cambridge's recommended amount of planning time out of
the 75 minutes available. **[1]**

## Section B

**4.** Explain why Paper 4 is described as testing "no prior content
knowledge at all," unlike Papers 1 to 3. **[3]**

**5.** A candidate spends the full 75 minutes writing, using no
dedicated planning time, and produces an unstructured commentary that
jumps between points without a clear order. Explain what the candidate
should have done differently, and why. **[3]**

**6.** A candidate writes: "The writer uses a metaphor here." Explain
why this sentence alone is unlikely to gain AO4 marks, and rewrite it
as a stronger sentence that would. **[3]**

**7.** A candidate has only ever practised unseen poetry throughout
the course. On the exam day, they find the prose extract would
actually have suited their strengths better, but they choose the poem
anyway out of habit.

**(a)** Explain the risk in this candidate's preparation. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how the candidate should have prepared differently. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why a candidate who writes a detailed, accurate running
summary of what happens in an unseen prose extract is still likely to
score only moderately, even though every stated fact is correct. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why choosing between the poem and prose option should
be based on which extract the candidate "can say more about in 55
minutes of writing," rather than a fixed preference decided before the
exam. **[2]**

**10.** A candidate on the Paper 3 + Paper 4 route assumes Paper 4 is
"lower stakes" because there is no set text to learn for it. Explain
why this assumption is mistaken. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 hour 15 minutes, 25 marks, and the candidate answers one
question from a choice of two [3].

**2.** A passage of literary prose (such as an extract from a novel or
short story), or a poem or extract from a poem [2].

**3.** Around 20 minutes [1].

**4.** Papers 1 to 3 reward detailed, text-specific knowledge built up
over a course of study of set texts, whereas Paper 4 has no set-text
list at all — the poem or prose extract is genuinely unseen, so no
content can be pre-learned [2]. Instead, Paper 4 rewards the reading
and analysis skills a literature student should be able to apply to
any unfamiliar text put in front of them for the first time [1].

**5.** The candidate should have used the recommended 20-minute
planning window to read the extract twice, annotate it for language,
structure, form, tone and shifts in mood, and decide which of the two
options to commit to before writing [2]. Planning first would have
allowed the commentary to be built around a small number of
well-organised, well-supported points instead of jumping between
observations without a clear structure [1].

**6.** This sentence names a technique (a metaphor) without stating
what effect it has on the reader or the candidate's own response to
it, which is what AO4 specifically requires [2]. A stronger version
might read: "The metaphor makes the character's fear feel physical and
overwhelming, which unsettled me as a reader because it withholds any
reassurance that the danger will pass" — connecting the device to both
an effect and a personal response [1].

**7. (a)** The risk is being caught out on exam day by an extract form
the candidate has not practised confidently, even when that form would
actually have suited their strengths better than the one habitually
chosen [2].
**(b)** The candidate should have practised both unseen poetry and
unseen prose regularly throughout the course, so that the choice on
the day can be made based on which specific extract suits them best,
rather than defaulting to whichever form feels more familiar [2].

**8.** Because examiners reward selective, well-supported analysis of
how specific words, images or structural choices create effects, not
a comprehensive but unanalytical retelling of events [2]. A running
summary demonstrates only that the candidate has understood what
happens, which addresses a limited part of AO1, without engaging with
AO3 (the writer's methods) or AO4 (personal response), both of which
are weighted equally alongside content knowledge [1].

**9.** Because both options are marked against the same four
assessment objectives and carry equal marks, so neither is inherently
easier; the best choice on the day depends on which specific extract
gives the candidate the most to analyse in the time available, which
can only be judged after reading both questions, not decided in
advance [2].

**10.** This assumption is mistaken because, for candidates on the
Paper 3 + Paper 4 route, Paper 4 carries the same 25% weighting as an
entire studied set text does on the alternative Paper 2 route — it is
worth exactly as much as a text studied over months of lessons, even
though it requires no content to be learned in advance [2].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Because nothing on this paper can be pre-learned, the highest-value
preparation is regular practice on genuinely unfamiliar poems and
prose extracts throughout the course, not a final revision push before
the exam — the goal is making close reading under time pressure feel
routine. Always use the full 20-minute planning window rather than
diving straight into writing, and build the habit of finishing every
analytical point with a statement of effect and personal response
("...and this makes me feel/think..."), since this is the single most
reliable way to convert accurate technical observation into the AO4
marks that are otherwise easy to leave unclaimed.
