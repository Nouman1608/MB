These questions cover section 6.5 Limitations of accounting statements of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. Give percentages to two decimal places. Revise first with the [limitations study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements/) and the [limitations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limitations-of-accounting-statements-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** State the **three** factors that the syllabus gives as causing limitations of accounting statements. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **one** limitation of recording assets at historic cost. **[2]**

**3.** Equipment has a book value of $14,000 and could be sold today for $22,000. By how much does the statement of financial position understate it? **[1]**

**4.** Which is a non-financial aspect named in the syllabus as a limitation of accounting statements?

A: depreciation

B: location of the business

C: historic cost

D: valuation of inventory

**[1]**

**5.** Explain why the skill of a business's workforce is not shown in its financial statements. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** estimates a business must make when applying its accounting policies. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Ashdown Ltd bought its premises many years ago; they have a book value of $70,000 and a current value of $310,000. Belford Ltd bought similar premises last year. Each company made a profit before interest of $45,000. Ashdown's capital employed is $225,000 and Belford's is $450,000.

**(a)** Calculate the ROCE of each company. **[2]**

**(b)** Recalculate Ashdown's ROCE as if its premises were shown at current value. **[2]**

**(c)** Comment on your results. **[2]**

**8.** Pendle Hardware's revenue was $150,000 in 2027 and $159,000 in 2028. During 2028 its selling prices rose by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in revenue. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate 2028 revenue at 2027 prices. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what this shows about the limitation of comparing money figures over time. **[2]**

**9.** Firm C and Firm D trade identically. Each bought machinery for $80,000 at the start of the year, has trade receivables of $40,000 at the year end and made a profit of $60,000 before depreciation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts (its first year). Firm C depreciates machinery on the straight line basis over 8 years with no residual value and keeps an allowance of 2%. Firm D uses 25% reducing balance and keeps an allowance of 5%.

**(a)** Calculate each firm's depreciation for the year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate each firm's allowance for irrecoverable debts. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate each firm's profit for the year. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain the problem this creates for a lender comparing the two firms. **[2]**

**10.** Two hair salons made a profit of $32,000 each last year and have similar ratios. Salon X has experienced stylists and is on a busy high street. Salon Y employs mainly trainees and is on a street where several shops have recently closed. Economists expect a recession next year. Explain how **three** non-financial aspects limit the usefulness of the salons' statements to someone deciding which to buy. **[6]**

**11.** "A business's financial statements tell a buyer everything needed to decide what the business is worth." Discuss this statement. **[6]**

**12.** A company changes its depreciation method from 20% reducing balance to 10% straight line. As a result, profit for the year is $8,000 higher. The directors tell shareholders that "performance has improved".

**(a)** Explain why the directors' claim may be misleading. **[3]**

**(b)** Name the accounting concept that this change departs from. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Historic cost [1]; application of accounting policies [1]; non-financial aspects [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Assets are shown at original cost less depreciation, not current value [1], so assets bought long ago, such as premises, may be shown far below what they are worth, understating the business and making comparisons misleading [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 22,000 - 14,000 = **$8,000** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** B [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* A and D are accounting policy matters and C is a separate factor; only B is a non-financial aspect.

**5.** The money measurement concept records only items that can be measured reliably in money [1]. The value of staff skill cannot be measured reliably, so it is left out, even though it affects future profit [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two: useful life of non-current assets [1]; residual value [1]; percentage for the allowance for irrecoverable debts; net realisable value of inventory. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Ashdown: 45,000 / 225,000 x 100 = **20.00%** [1]. Belford: 45,000 / 450,000 x 100 = **10.00%** [1].

**(b)** Capital employed = 225,000 + (310,000 - 70,000) = $465,000 [1]. ROCE = 45,000 / 465,000 x 100 = **9.68%** [1].

**(c)** Ashdown appears twice as efficient [1], but once its premises are shown at current value its return is slightly below Belford's; the difference comes from historic cost, not better trading [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 9,000 / 150,000 x 100 = **6.00%** [1].

**(b)** 159,000 / 1.10 [1] = **$144,545** [1].

**(c)** Revenue appears to have risen, but at constant prices it fell by about 3.64%, so Pendle sold less [1]. Statements show money amounts, so rising prices can hide a fall in the amount of business done [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** C: 80,000 / 8 = **$10,000** [1]. D: 80,000 x 25% = **$20,000** [1].

**(b)** C: 40,000 x 2% = **$800** [1]. D: 40,000 x 5% = **$2,000** [1].

**(c)** C: 60,000 - 10,000 - 800 = **$49,200** [1]. D: 60,000 - 20,000 - 2,000 = **$38,000** [1].

**(d)** The firms traded identically, yet C's profit is $11,200 higher [1], purely because of different policies and estimates, so the lender might wrongly judge C the stronger borrower unless it adjusts for the policies [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Skill of the workforce: X's experienced stylists keep loyal customers [1], while Y's trainees may lose customers, so Y's future profit may fall, which the statements do not show [1]. Location: X's busy high street brings passing trade [1], while Y's declining street may cut its revenue in future [1]. Economic climate: in a recession customers may visit salons less often [1], so last year's $32,000 profit may not be repeated by either salon, especially Y [1]. **[6]**

**11.** For: statements show actual revenue, expenses and profit and the assets and liabilities at the year end [1], which give a factual starting point for a valuation [1]. Against: assets are at historic cost, so premises may be worth much more or less than their book value [1]; accounting policies such as depreciation rates affect profit and asset values [1]; non-financial aspects such as staff skill, location and reputation, which affect future profit, are not shown [1]. Conclusion: the statements are useful but not sufficient; a buyer also needs current valuations, several years' results and information about non-financial factors [1]. **[6]**

**12. (a)** The rise in profit comes from a lower depreciation charge, not from higher sales or lower running costs [1]. Trading is unchanged, so the business has not performed better [1]. Comparing this year's profit with last year's is misleading, because the figures are prepared on different bases [1].

**(b)** Consistency [1]. **[4]**
