This guide covers **Paper 1 Introductory Topics in Psychology** for
AQA A-level Psychology (7182), specification version 1.4 (first
teaching September 2025, first A-level exams June 2027). The A-level
Paper 1 assesses four compulsory topics -- social influence, memory,
attachment, and clinical psychology and mental health -- in a 2-hour,
96-mark written exam with four sections of 24 marks each. The rest of
the A-level is assessed in Paper 2 (approaches in psychology,
biopsychology and research methods) and Paper 3 (issues and debates,
plus one topic from each of three option groups).

## Where this fits in 7182

Paper 1 introduces four core areas of psychology -- how other people
influence our behaviour, how memory works, how early relationships
form, and how mental health problems are defined, explained and treated -- that establish foundational psychological concepts and
research studies referenced throughout the rest of the course. A-level
students continuing beyond this content into Biopsychology (Paper 2),
Issues and Debates, and their chosen options (Paper 3) will find the same
description-plus-evaluation essay structure recurring, so mastering it
here pays off across the whole qualification, not just Paper 1.

AS Psychology (7181) and A-level Psychology (7182) are separate linear
qualifications with separate exams: students sit all the AS exams at the
end of the AS course, and all the A-level exams at the end of the A-level
course. Their first papers share a title but are not interchangeable.
The AS Paper 1 is 1 hour 30 minutes, 72 marks, three sections, and
assesses social influence, memory and attachment only; the A-level
Paper 1 is 2 hours, 96 marks, four sections, and adds clinical
psychology and mental health (which the AS assesses in its Paper 2
instead).

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA A-LEVEL PSYCHOLOGY (7182) — PAPER 1 INTRODUCTORY TOPICS IN
PSYCHOLOGY**

- 1.1 Social Influence — types and explanations of conformity
(internalisation, compliance, informational and normative social
influence) and Asch's variables affecting conformity; explanations of
obedience (agentic state, legitimacy of authority) and Milgram's
situational variables, plus the dispositional Authoritarian
Personality explanation; explanations of resistance to social
influence, including social support and locus of control; and minority
influence
- 1.2 Memory — the multi-store model (sensory register, short-term and
long-term memory) and the coding, capacity and duration of each store;
the working memory model (central executive, phonological loop,
visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer); explanations for forgetting
(proactive/retroactive interference, retrieval failure); and factors
affecting eyewitness testimony accuracy, including the cognitive
interview
- 1.3 Attachment — animal studies of attachment (Lorenz, Harlow);
explanations of attachment, including learning theory and Bowlby's
monotropic theory; Ainsworth's Strange Situation and attachment types;
cultural variations in attachment; Bowlby's theory of maternal
deprivation and the effects of institutionalisation; and the influence
of early attachment on later relationships
- 1.4 Clinical Psychology and Mental Health — definitions in the field
of mental health (deviation from ideal mental health, deviation from
social/cultural norms, failure to function adequately, statistical
infrequency); the behavioural, emotional and cognitive characteristics
of phobias, depression and OCD; the behavioural approach to explaining
and treating phobias (the two-process model; systematic
desensitisation and flooding); the cognitive approach to explaining and
treating depression (Beck's negative triad, Ellis's ABC model, CBT);
and the biological approach to explaining and treating OCD (genetic and
neural explanations; drug therapy)

## How to approach it

Each of the four sub-topics is built around named studies and
theories, so structure revision around being able to describe a study
or model accurately and then evaluate it (methodology, real-world
application, alternative explanations), rather than description alone
-- this description-plus-evaluation structure mirrors how AQA
Psychology essay questions are typically marked. A useful discipline is
to build a short "AO1/AO3" split for every named study: a concise,
accurate description (AO1) followed by two or three genuinely distinct
evaluation points (AO3), rather than one long undifferentiated
paragraph mixing description and evaluation together. Social Influence and
Attachment both connect strongly to research methods (assessed
formally in Paper 2), so notice methodological issues -- such as
ecological validity or ethical concerns -- as you revise the named
studies in this paper, since exam questions often draw those
connections explicitly, and a student who revises Paper 1's content in
isolation from Paper 2's methods content misses these deliberately
built connections. Memory pairs particularly well with
Introductory Topics as a whole, since the multi-store model and
reconstructive memory research recur as reference points in later,
more applied topics — a candidate who cannot describe the multi-store
model precisely will find it resurfacing as an obstacle well beyond
this paper's own exam questions.

## Social Influence — the argument in sequence

1.1 moves through a deliberate sequence: first *why* people conform or obey (the explanations),
then the *conditions* that make conformity or obedience more or less likely (Asch's and Milgram's
variables), and finally *how* people resist social influence despite those pressures. Revising this
as a single argument — pressure, explanation, resistance — rather than three disconnected topics
makes it easier to answer synoptic questions that span more than one part of 1.1, and it mirrors how
minority influence (the final part of 1.1) is best understood as the flip side of majority
conformity examined earlier in the same sub-topic.

## Memory — connecting the two models

The multi-store model and the working memory model are not simply two alternative theories to learn
in isolation — the working memory model specifically elaborates on the short-term memory store from
the multi-store model, breaking it down into separate components (central executive, phonological
loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer) that the simpler multi-store model treats as one
undifferentiated store. Understanding this relationship — working memory as a more detailed account
of one part of the multi-store model, not a rival model of memory as a whole — is what a strong
"compare the two models" essay actually needs to demonstrate.

## Attachment — from animal studies to human theory

The animal studies (Lorenz, Harlow) are examined not as isolated curiosities but as evidence that
informed the human attachment theories that follow: Bowlby's monotropic theory draws directly on the
idea of a critical period established in Lorenz's imprinting research. Tracing this influence
explicitly — how animal research shaped human attachment theory — is a stronger answer than treating
the animal studies and the human theories as two separate revision blocks.
