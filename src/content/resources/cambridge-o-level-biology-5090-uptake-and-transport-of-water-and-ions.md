This guide covers subtopic **7.1 Uptake and transport of water and ions** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to relate the structure of root hair cells to their function of water and ion uptake, to outline the pathway taken by water through the root, stem and leaf (limited to root hair cells, root cortex cells, xylem and mesophyll cells), and to investigate, using a suitable stain, the pathway of water in a cut stem.

## Why plants need to take up water and ions

Plants lose water all the time from their leaves, and they need water for photosynthesis, for supporting their cells by turgor pressure and as a solvent. They also need mineral ions, such as nitrate and magnesium, which they can only get from the soil. Plants have no mouth or gut. All of this uptake happens through the **root system**, and the key exchange surface is the **root hair cell**.

## The root hair cell

A root hair cell is an epidermal cell in a young root. It has a long, thin outgrowth, the **root hair**, that reaches between soil particles.

| Feature | Reason it helps uptake |
|---|---|
| Long, thin extension (the root hair) | Gives a **large surface area** for absorbing water and ions, and grows into the spaces between soil particles |
| Thin cell wall and cell membrane | Short distance for water and ions to cross |
| Cell sap with dissolved sugars and ions | Keeps the water potential **lower** than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis |
| Many mitochondria | Release energy by aerobic respiration for **active transport** of ions |
| No chloroplasts | Root hair cells are underground and do not photosynthesise |

Notice how each feature is linked to a process from topic 3. Water enters by **osmosis**, because the soil water has a higher water potential than the cell sap. Mineral ions enter by **active transport** when the concentration in the soil is lower than in the cell (see subtopic 3.2), and by diffusion when the concentration is higher in the soil.

A strong exam answer is of the form: "The root hair cell has a long extension, which gives a large surface area, so more water and ions can be absorbed."

### Why root hairs are always replaced

Root hairs live for only a short time. New ones grow behind the root tip as old ones die. This means that the plant is always absorbing from fresh soil. It also explains why a transplanted seedling wilts if roots are damaged: it loses the root hairs and so loses surface area for uptake.

## The pathway of water through the plant

Water takes a continuous path from the soil to the air. The syllabus limits the pathway to four stages.

1. **Root hair cell**: water enters from the soil by osmosis.
2. **Root cortex cells**: water moves from cell to cell across the cortex, by osmosis, from cells with a higher water potential to cells with a lower water potential.
3. **Xylem**: water enters the xylem vessels in the centre of the root, then travels up through the **stem** to the **leaf**.
4. **Mesophyll cells**: water leaves the xylem in the leaf and enters the mesophyll cells by osmosis. Some water evaporates from the surfaces of these cells and escapes through the stomata (transpiration, subtopic 7.2).

In summary: **soil → root hair cell → root cortex cells → xylem (root, stem, leaf) → mesophyll cells → air spaces → out through stomata.**

### Worked example: which way does the water move?

*Four cells are lined up from the soil inwards. The sap of each cell has the concentration of dissolved substances shown (illustrative figures).*

| Position | Concentration of sap |
|---|---|
| Soil water | Very dilute |
| Root hair cell | 0.20 mol per dm cubed |
| Outer cortex cell | 0.25 mol per dm cubed |
| Inner cortex cell | 0.30 mol per dm cubed |

The more concentrated the sap, the **lower** the water potential. Water moves by osmosis from a higher water potential to a lower one. The soil water has the highest water potential, then the root hair cell, then the outer cortex cell, then the inner cortex cell. So water moves **inwards**: soil, root hair cell, outer cortex cell, inner cortex cell. This **water potential gradient** is what carries water across the root. If the soil became very salty so that the soil water had a **lower** water potential than the root hair cell, water would move **out** of the plant and it would wilt.

## Ions in the pathway

Ions enter the root hair cells by active transport (or diffusion). They are then carried with the water through the cortex and into the **xylem**, which transports water and mineral ions up the stem to the leaves and growing regions. Ions are used, for example, to make amino acids (nitrate) and chlorophyll (magnesium).

## Investigating the pathway of water in a cut stem

**Aim**: to show which tissue in a stem carries water.

**Materials**: a fresh leafy shoot such as celery or a white flower, a beaker of water containing a stain such as red ink or eosin, a scalpel, a hand lens or microscope.

**Method**

1. Cut the base of the stem under water, so air does not enter the xylem.
2. Place the cut end into the beaker of stained water.
3. Leave the shoot in a warm, bright place for 30 minutes to several hours. A warm, breezy place speeds up transpiration and so the movement of water.
4. Remove the shoot. Cut a thin transverse (cross) section of the stem, and a longitudinal section.
5. Look at the sections with a hand lens or low-power microscope.

**Result**: the stain is found only in the **xylem**, which appears as stained dots or a ring in the transverse section, and as stained lines in the longitudinal section. This shows that the xylem carries water up the stem. Veins in the leaves are also stained, which shows the water reaching the leaf.

**Control**: a second shoot kept in unstained water shows what the stem looks like without the stain. Keeping light, temperature and the time the same for each shoot makes the comparison fair.

### Worked example: rate of movement

*In a celery experiment, the stain travelled 45 mm up the stem in 30 minutes. Calculate the rate in mm per minute and in mm per hour.*

1. Rate = distance divided by time = 45 divided by 30 = **1.5 mm per minute**.
2. There are 60 minutes in an hour, so 1.5 x 60 = **90 mm per hour**.

If a second stem, kept in a draught, had stain travelling 60 mm in 30 minutes, the rate would be 2.0 mm per minute. A faster rate in a draught suggests that more transpiration pulls the water up faster (see subtopic 7.2).

## When uptake goes wrong

Understanding the uptake process helps you to explain situations that appear in exams.

- **Waterlogged soil**: little oxygen reaches the roots, so aerobic respiration slows. Less energy is released, so active transport of ions falls. The plant may show mineral deficiency symptoms even though the soil contains the ions.
- **Very salty or very concentrated soil water**: the soil water has a lower water potential than the cell sap. Water leaves the root hair cells by osmosis and the plant wilts.
- **Damaged or cut roots**: fewer root hair cells means less surface area, so less water and ions are absorbed. Transplanted plants are often watered well and shaded to reduce water loss while new root hairs grow.
- **Very dry soil**: there is little water to absorb, so the plant loses more water than it takes in, and the cells become flaccid.

In each case, state the cause, then the effect on uptake, then the effect on the plant.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that root hairs "absorb water by diffusion". Water uptake is by **osmosis**.
- Saying that root hair cells take in ions only by osmosis. Ions enter by active transport or diffusion.
- Giving the feature without the reason: "large surface area" needs "so more water and ions can be absorbed".
- Leaving out the root cortex cells from the pathway, or putting mesophyll cells before the xylem.
- Saying that the stain is carried by the phloem. The xylem carries water.
- Forgetting to cut the stem under water in the practical.

## Command words

- **Relate**: link a structure to its function with a reason.
- **Outline**: set out the main stages in order.
- **Investigate**: plan or carry out a practical, with a control and measurements.
- **Explain**: give the reason for the direction of water movement using water potential.
