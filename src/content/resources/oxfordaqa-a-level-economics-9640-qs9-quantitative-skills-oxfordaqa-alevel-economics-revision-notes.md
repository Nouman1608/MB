These notes condense quantitative skill **QS9** from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: interpret, apply and analyse information in written, graphical and numerical forms. The [QS9 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics/) works through a full extract and table with model answers; the [QS9 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-practice/) give you new sources. Use the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) to find the topics the data will draw on. All data are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interpret | State accurately what the information shows, with figures, units and dates |
| Apply | Link the information to an economic concept or model |
| Analyse | Build a chain of reasoning from the evidence to a conclusion |
| Trend | The general direction of a series over time |
| Turning point | A point where a series changes direction |
| Outlier | A value well away from the rest of the data |
| Percentage point | The unit for the difference between two percentages |
| Real value | A money value adjusted for changes in the price level |
| Correlation | Two variables moving together; not proof that one causes the other |
| Derived figure | A number you calculate from the data, such as a total, share or percentage change |

## Condensed recall

**Interpreting**

- Read the title, units, time period and source before the numbers.
- Describe a trend in four parts: direction, size, shape, exceptions.
- Use percentage changes for comparisons between series of different sizes.
- Distinguish levels from rates of change and percentage points from percentages.

**Applying**

- Name the concept the data illustrate: a shift in supply, elasticity, a negative externality, an output gap.
- Use the right diagram language: a movement along or a shift of a curve.
- Combine columns to create the figure the concept needs, such as total supply or real price.

**Analysing**

- Write a chain: evidence, then concept, then consequence.
- Check timing: does the cause come before or with the effect?
- Look for other explanations in the data.
- Use the extract to explain the numbers and the numbers to test the extract.

**Judging evidence**

- Who wrote it and why? Is it opinion or data?
- Money or real values? Averages or distributions?
- Is the period long enough? Would another start year change the story?
- What is missing that you would need to be sure?

## Diagram information in words

| You read from the diagram | You can calculate |
|---|---|
| Old and new equilibrium price and quantity | Percentage changes, change in consumer spending, rough PED along demand |
| Price paid by buyers and received by sellers after a tax | Tax per unit, share paid by each group, tax revenue |
| Two points on one curve | Gradient, and whether the curve is relatively steep or flat |

Example: equilibrium moves from 40 and 300 to 46 and 260. Price +15%, quantity -13.3%, spending 12,000 to 11,960, rough PED -0.89.

## Exam traps

- Listing every number in a table instead of describing the pattern.
- Theory with no data, or data with no theory.
- Figures without units or years.
- Taking a quoted opinion as fact.
- Treating a correlation as a cause.
- Missing an exception that weakens your point.
- Ignoring inflation when comparing money values over several years.
- Concluding without saying what extra evidence would help.

## Self-test

Use this invented data set for questions 1 to 8.

| Year | Bus fare | Passengers (m) | Fuel price index |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.50 | 40 | 100 |
| 2 | 1.50 | 41 | 104 |
| 3 | 1.80 | 37 | 120 |
| 4 | 1.80 | 38 | 118 |

1. Describe the trend in passenger numbers over the four years.
2. Calculate the percentage change in passengers between year 2 and year 3.
3. Calculate the percentage change in the fare in year 3.
4. Estimate PED from the year 2 to year 3 change.
5. Calculate fare revenue in years 2 and 3 and the percentage change.
6. Suggest a reason, from the data, for the fare rise.
7. Why is your PED estimate only rough?
8. What extra information would you want before judging whether the fare rise was a good decision?
9. What is the difference between "interpret" and "analyse"?
10. Unemployment rises from 5.0% to 6.0%. State the change in two ways.
11. Why might a quotation from a business leader in an extract need testing?
12. Two series rise together over five years. Give one reason this may not show cause and effect.

### Answers

1. Passengers fell overall, from 40m to 38m (-5%). They rose slightly in year 2, fell sharply in year 3 when the fare rose, and recovered a little in year 4.
2. (37 - 41) / 41 = -9.8%.
3. 0.30 / 1.50 = +20%.
4. -9.8 / 20 = about -0.49, inelastic.
5. Year 2: 1.50 x 41 = 61.5m. Year 3: 1.80 x 37 = 66.6m. Rise of 8.3%.
6. Fuel prices rose by 15.4% in year 3, raising operating costs.
7. Other things may also have changed, such as incomes or the quality of service, and it is based on one change only.
8. Costs (including fuel) to judge profit, alternatives available to passengers, and effects on congestion or low-income travellers.
9. Interpret says what the information shows; analyse builds a reasoned chain from it to a conclusion.
10. A rise of 1 percentage point, or a 20% rise in the rate.
11. The speaker may have an interest in a particular conclusion, so the claim should be checked against the data.
12. Both may be driven by a third factor, such as rising incomes.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a trend description be?**
Two or three sentences: the overall change with figures, the shape, and any exception.

**Do I always need to calculate something?**
Not always, but a derived figure such as a percentage change or a real value usually makes the point stronger than a copied number.
