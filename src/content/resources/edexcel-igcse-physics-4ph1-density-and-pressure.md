This guide covers sub-topic **5(b) Density and pressure**, statements 5.3 to 5.7 of the Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. It is core content, so it can appear on either paper. The
[Solids, Liquids and Gases study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-solids-liquids-and-gases/) states the
equations; this page goes further into the density practical for every kind of sample, pressure on surfaces, why
fluid pressure acts in all directions, and multi-step problems with `p = h × ρ × g`.

## What 5(b) covers

- **5.3** know and use `density = mass / volume` (`ρ = m / V`);
- **5.4** practical: investigate density using direct measurements of mass and volume;
- **5.5** know and use `pressure = force / area` (`p = F / A`);
- **5.6** understand how the pressure at a point in a gas or liquid at rest acts equally in all directions;
- **5.7** know and use `pressure difference = height × density × gravitational field strength` (`p = h × ρ × g`).

All three equations are in the specification's list of relationships that are not given in the exam, so learn them.

## Density

Density is the mass of each unit of volume. It is a property of the material, not of the object: a steel nail and a
steel girder have the same density. Units: kg/m³ (or g/cm³; multiply by 1000 to convert to kg/m³).

## The density practical (5.4)

"Direct measurements of mass and volume" means you measure both quantities yourself. The method depends on the sample.

**A regular solid (a cuboid block).**
1. Measure the mass on a top-pan balance.
2. Measure length, width and height with a ruler (or a more precise instrument for small blocks), taking each
   measurement in more than one place and finding the mean.
3. Volume = length × width × height; density = mass ÷ volume.

**An irregular solid (a stone or a key).**
1. Measure the mass on a balance.
2. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and read the volume at the bottom of the meniscus, with your eye level
   with the surface.
3. Lower the object in gently on a thread so that it is fully covered, and read the new volume. The **increase** is
   the object's volume.
4. For an object too big for a cylinder, use a displacement can: fill it to the spout, lower the object in, and catch
   the overflowing water in a measuring cylinder.

**A liquid.**
1. Place an empty measuring cylinder on the balance and record its mass (or zero the balance with the cylinder on it).
2. Pour in a measured volume of liquid, reading the meniscus at eye level.
3. Record the new mass. Mass of liquid = new mass − mass of cylinder.

**Worked example 1: oil.** An empty measuring cylinder has a mass of 82.0 g. With 50.0 cm³ of oil in it, the mass is
128.0 g.

```
mass of oil = 128.0 − 82.0 = 46.0 g
ρ = m / V = 46.0 / 50.0 = 0.920 g/cm³ = 920 kg/m³
```

**Improving the practical.** Using a larger volume of liquid, or a larger object, makes the reading errors a smaller
fraction of the measurement. Repeating and averaging reduces the effect of random errors. Air bubbles clinging to a
stone in the cylinder make its volume look too big, so tap them off.

## Pressure on a surface (5.5)

Pressure is the force acting at right angles to a surface, per unit area. The same force over a smaller area gives a
greater pressure.

**Worked example 2: shoes and snowshoes.** A person weighs 600 N.

```
standing in flat shoes, total area 0.040 m²:   p = 600 / 0.040 = 15 000 Pa
on snowshoes, total area 0.30 m²:              p = 600 / 0.30  = 2000 Pa
all weight on one shoe heel of area 1.0 cm² (1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²):  p = 600 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 6.0 × 10⁶ Pa
```

That is why snowshoes stop you sinking into snow and why narrow heels can dent a wooden floor. The same idea explains
wide tyres on tractors (low pressure on soft ground) and sharp knives and needles (very small area, so a large pressure
from a small force).

## Pressure in a fluid acts in all directions (5.6)

In a liquid or gas at rest, the particles move randomly in every direction and collide with any surface they meet, so
at any point the pressure pushes equally in all directions: up, down and sideways. A small sensor held at one depth
reads the same whichever way it faces.

You can see this with a plastic bottle of water that has several small holes punched around it **at the same
height**: water spurts out sideways from every hole with equal force. Holes punched **lower down** spurt further,
because the pressure is greater at greater depth. Gas pressure behaves the same way, which is why a balloon inflates
into a rounded shape.

## Pressure difference with depth (5.7)

```
p = h × ρ × g
```

`p` here is the pressure **difference** between two points a vertical height `h` apart in a fluid of density `ρ`.
Below a liquid surface, the pressure at depth `h` is the pressure at the surface (usually atmospheric pressure) plus
`h × ρ × g`. The pressure depends only on depth, density and g, not on the shape of the container.

**Worked example 3: a diver.** A diver is 25 m below the surface of the sea. Sea water has a density of 1030 kg/m³;
atmospheric pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa; g = 10 N/kg.

```
pressure difference = h × ρ × g = 25 × 1030 × 10 = 257 500 Pa
total pressure = 1.0 × 10⁵ + 2.575 × 10⁵ = 3.6 × 10⁵ Pa (2 s.f.)
```

So at 25 m the diver experiences about three and a half times atmospheric pressure.

**Worked example 4: force on a window.** A submarine window of area 0.050 m² is 200 m below the surface of the same
sea. Find the extra force on the window caused by the water above it.

```
p = h × ρ × g = 200 × 1030 × 10 = 2.06 × 10⁶ Pa
F = p × A = 2.06 × 10⁶ × 0.050 = 1.03 × 10⁵ N
```

Combining `p = h × ρ × g` with `p = F / A` like this is a common two-step question.

**Worked example 5: a water tower.** A tap is 15 m below the water surface in a storage tank on a hill. The pressure
difference driving water out of the tap is `15 × 1000 × 10 = 150 000 Pa`. A tap in a house further down the hill,
and so further below the tank's surface, has a larger pressure difference and the water flows out faster.

## Common mistakes

- Using the length of a pipe or the distance along a slope as `h`. It is always the **vertical** height difference.
- Forgetting to add atmospheric pressure when the question asks for the **total** pressure, or adding it when the
  question asks only for the pressure **due to the water**.
- Leaving areas in cm²: divide by 10 000 to get m².
- Saying pressure acts "downwards" in a liquid. It acts in all directions.
- In the displacement method, using the total reading instead of the increase in volume.
- Measuring the mass of a liquid without subtracting the mass of the container.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the density practical: list the measurements, the instrument for each, and how you calculate density.
  Include one step that improves accuracy (meniscus at eye level, repeat readings, larger sample).
- **Explain** why pressure is greater at depth: more fluid above, so a greater weight of fluid presses on each unit
  area below.
- **Calculate:** write the equation, convert to m, m², m³ and kg first, and give the unit (Pa or kg/m³).
- **Suggest** why a design uses a large or small area: link area to pressure using `p = F / A`.
