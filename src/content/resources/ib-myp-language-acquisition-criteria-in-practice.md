This guide works through what genuinely top-band evidence looks like
for each of IB Middle Years Programme Language Acquisition's four
assessment criteria, applied to real coursework tasks. The [assessment
revision
notes](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-assessment-revision-notes/)
and [eAssessment exam
preparation](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-eassessment-exam-preparation/)
cover the four criteria and how the two eAssessment components are
structured -- this guide goes further, since a top-band response at the
emergent phase looks very different from a top-band response at the
proficient phase for the same criterion.

## Where this fits in the subject

These four criteria are assessed against a student's own proficiency
phase -- emergent, capable or proficient -- rather than a single fixed
standard for all students. Top-band evidence at the emergent phase
might be a short, simple but accurate response using basic vocabulary
and structures, while top-band evidence at the proficient phase
requires nuanced, extended, spontaneous language use. A common
revision mistake is comparing your own work against an unrealistic
standard drawn from a different, more advanced proficiency phase,
rather than against your own phase's actual descriptors.

## Criterion A: Listening

Top-band evidence interprets and constructs meaning from spoken
multimodal text, and understands how images or other spatial elements
presented alongside the oral text contribute to meaning -- not just
transcribing individual words heard. A capable-phase listening task
might play a short spoken announcement accompanied by a relevant
image. Top-band evidence does not just identify factual details stated
aloud, such as time or place -- it also explains how the accompanying
image reinforces or adds to the spoken message, for example an image
of a closed sign supporting an announcement about a temporary closure,
demonstrating genuine multimodal interpretation rather than audio
comprehension alone.

## Criterion B: Reading

Top-band evidence constructs meaning from written text while also
interpreting its spatial and visual aspects -- layout, images,
formatting -- as sources of meaning in their own right, not just
decoration around the words. Given a public information poster
combining short text with icons and colour coding, top-band evidence
explains what the icons and colour choices themselves communicate --
a red icon signalling urgency or warning, for instance -- in addition
to correctly comprehending the written text, rather than treating the
visual elements as irrelevant to the reading task.

## Criterion C: Speaking

Top-band evidence interacts spontaneously on topics of personal, local
and global interest, applying linguistic and literary concepts with
increasing skill, range and accuracy for the student's proficiency
phase. At the proficient phase, a top-band spoken response to an
unexpected follow-up question does not fall back to a memorised,
unrelated prepared answer -- it responds directly and coherently to
what was actually asked, using a genuine range of vocabulary and
structures appropriate to that phase, demonstrating real interactive
competence rather than rehearsed recitation.

## Criterion D: Writing

Top-band evidence recognises and uses language suitable to audience
and purpose across different registers -- home, classroom,
formal/informal, social and academic -- applying understanding of
language, form, mode and medium to express ideas creatively and
meaningfully. Asked to write both a text message to a friend and a
formal email to a school administrator on a related topic, top-band
evidence produces genuinely different register, vocabulary and
structure for each -- informal, abbreviated language for the text
message; formal greetings, complete sentences and polite requests for
the email -- rather than writing the same content with only minor
wording changes between the two tasks.

## How the four criteria map onto the two eAssessment components

Criteria A, B and D are assessed together in the on-screen paper
(listening, reading, writing), while Criterion C (speaking) is
assessed separately as an internally assessed, interactive task. This
split matters for revision planning: strengthening Criterion A or B
does nothing to prepare Criterion C's genuinely spontaneous, unscripted
speaking demand, and vice versa -- each criterion's top-band evidence
has to be practised in its own right rather than assuming strength in
one skill transfers automatically to another.

## How to approach it

Identify your own current proficiency phase before judging your work
against any descriptor, since the same response can be top-band
evidence at one phase and only mid-band evidence at a more advanced
one. Practise multimodal interpretation for both listening and reading
-- explicitly explaining what an image, layout or colour choice adds to
a spoken or written text, not just the literal content -- since this is
a distinct, separately assessed skill from comprehension alone.
Practise responding to genuinely unexpected follow-up questions in
speaking practice, rather than only rehearsing prepared answers, since
Criterion C specifically rewards spontaneous interaction. For writing,
deliberately practise the same content in two different registers side
by side, checking that vocabulary, structure and tone genuinely differ
between them rather than only the greeting or sign-off changing.

## Common mistakes

Judging a response against the wrong proficiency phase's descriptors, either by underestimating your
own phase's expectations or by comparing yourself unfavourably against a more advanced phase's
standard. Treating listening and reading as purely literal comprehension tasks, missing the
multimodal-interpretation element both criteria specifically credit. Relying on memorised, rehearsed
answers in speaking assessments, which cannot demonstrate top-band Criterion C evidence once a genuine,
unexpected follow-up question is asked. Writing different tasks with only superficial changes -- a
different greeting or sign-off -- rather than genuinely different register, vocabulary and structure
suited to each task's specific audience and purpose.
