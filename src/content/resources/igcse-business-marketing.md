This guide covers **Topic 3 Marketing**, for Cambridge IGCSE Business
Studies 0450, 2026 series (Version 2 — no significant changes affecting
teaching from the prior version). Business Studies at 0450 is not
tiered: all candidates study the same content, assessed across
Paper 1 (Short Answer and Data Response) and Paper 2 (Case Study), each
worth 50% of the final grade.

## Where this fits in 0450

Marketing is the third of six topics in 0450, following [Understanding
business activity](/resources/igcse-business-understanding-business-activity/)
and [People in business](/resources/igcse-business-people-in-business/),
and preceding Operations management, Financial information and
decisions, and External influences on business activity. It is built
around four sub-topics: marketing, competition and the customer;
market research; the marketing mix; and marketing strategy.

## 3.1 Marketing, competition and the customer

This sub-topic sets out why marketing exists before looking at how it
is done. The role of marketing is to identify customer needs, satisfy
those needs, maintain customer loyalty and build customer
relationships. Candidates need to understand why customer spending
patterns change, why some markets become more competitive over time,
and how businesses respond to both.

Two core distinctions are examined here:

- **Niche marketing vs mass marketing** — the benefits and limitations
  of targeting a small, specific segment of the market (niche) versus
  targeting the whole market with a single offer (mass). Niche
  marketing typically allows a business to charge premium prices and
  face less direct competition, but limits the size of the potential
  customer base; mass marketing can achieve economies of scale but
  faces more direct competitors.
- **Market segmentation** — dividing the market into groups of
  customers with similar characteristics, for example by age,
  socio-economic grouping, location or gender, and the benefits this
  brings to a business (more targeted promotion, product development
  that better matches customer needs). Exam questions frequently ask
  you to recommend and justify an appropriate method of segmentation
  for a given business scenario.

## 3.2 Market research

Market research is the process by which businesses gather information
about their customers and markets before making decisions. The
syllabus requires you to know:

- **Primary research** (original research carried out directly by or
  for the business): postal questionnaires, online surveys,
  interviews and focus groups, along with the benefits and limitations
  of each method.
- **Secondary research** (using information already collected by
  someone else): online sources, government statistics and paid
  commercial market research reports.
- **The need for sampling** — since it is rarely practical to survey
  an entire target market, and the factors that influence how accurate
  market research data actually is.
- **Presenting and using market research results** — analysing data
  shown as graphs, charts and diagrams, and drawing simple conclusions
  from it.

A common exam skill tested here is evaluation: weighing the cost and
reliability of primary research against the speed and lower cost of
secondary research for a specific business situation, rather than
treating one as universally "better."

## 3.3 Marketing mix

The marketing mix -- the four Ps -- is the centrepiece of this topic
and the most heavily examined sub-topic in Marketing.

- **Product**: the benefits and limitations of developing new
  products, the role of brand image and packaging, and the **product
  life cycle** -- its main stages (introduction, growth, maturity,
  decline) and extension strategies. You are expected to be able to
  draw and interpret a product life cycle diagram, and to explain how
  its stages influence other marketing decisions such as promotion and
  pricing.
- **Price**: pricing methods -- cost-plus, competitive, penetration,
  skimming and promotional pricing -- with their respective benefits
  and limitations, and the significance of price elasticity of demand
  (understanding the difference between elastic and inelastic demand
  and why it matters for pricing decisions; the syllabus explicitly
  states the PED formula and calculations are **not** assessed).
- **Place**: distribution channels, including the advantages and
  disadvantages of selling through wholesalers, retailers or directly
  to consumers.
- **Promotion**: the aims of promotion, different forms it can take
  (advertising, sales promotion), and the need for cost-effectiveness
  when spending a marketing budget.
- **Technology and the marketing mix**: the concept of e-commerce, its
  opportunities and threats for businesses and consumers, and the use
  of the internet and social media for promotion.

Because each of the four Ps interacts with the others (for example, a
premium pricing strategy usually requires a matching product and
promotion strategy), exam questions often ask you to evaluate how
elements of the mix work together for a given business, not just
define each P in isolation.

## 3.4 Marketing strategy

The final sub-topic asks you to bring the marketing mix together into
a coherent strategy, and covers two further areas:

- **Legal controls related to marketing** — the impact of legal
  restrictions on marketing strategy, such as rules against misleading
  promotion and against selling faulty or dangerous goods.
- **Entering new foreign markets** — the growth potential of overseas
  markets, the problems of entering them (cultural differences, lack
  of local knowledge), and methods used to overcome these problems,
  such as joint ventures and licensing.

Questions on marketing strategy typically present a business scenario
and ask you to recommend and justify which elements of the marketing
mix should be prioritised, drawing on everything covered across 3.1 to
3.4 rather than 3.4 alone.

## How to approach it

Marketing is one of the more scenario-driven topics in 0450: both
papers are built around applying knowledge to a specific business
context (Paper 1's stimulus material, Paper 2's case study), so
practise linking marketing mix decisions back to the type of business
and market segment described in each question, rather than writing
generic definitions. The product life cycle and pricing methods are
the two areas most consistently tested across past papers, so make
sure you can both draw and interpret a life cycle diagram and justify
a pricing method against the specific circumstances given, since
"recommend and justify" questions carry Analysis and Evaluation marks
(AO3 and AO4) that are not available from definitions alone.
