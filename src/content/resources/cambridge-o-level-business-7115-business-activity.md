This guide covers subtopic **1.1 Business activity** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series) in more depth than the combined [Business Activity and Classification study guide](/resources/business-activity-and-classification/). That page introduces the terms. This one shows how to use them on a business described in a question, with calculations of added value and opportunity cost and the kind of reasoning that earns application and analysis marks.

## What the syllabus asks for

Section 1.1.1, the purpose and nature of business activity, lists four ideas:

- concepts of needs, wants, scarcity and opportunity cost
- importance of specialisation
- purpose of business activity
- the concept of adding value and how added value can be increased.

Both papers expect you to apply these to simple business situations, not only to define them.

## Scarcity forces choice

People's wants are unlimited, but the resources used to satisfy them are limited. The resources are usually grouped as the **factors of production**:

| Factor | What it is | Example for a furniture workshop |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources, including the site | Timber, the plot the workshop stands on |
| Labour | The work of people | Carpenters, a delivery driver |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods | Saws, a van, the workshop building |
| Enterprise | Taking the risk of combining the other three | The owner who starts and runs the business |

Because these are scarce, every use of them has an **opportunity cost**: the next best alternative given up.

**Worked example 1: opportunity cost in a business decision.** Hollin Furniture (an invented business) has $18,000 to spend. Its three options, ranked by the owner, are a new cutting machine, a website with online ordering, and training for two apprentices. It chooses the machine. The opportunity cost is the website, the second choice, not the website plus the training. If the owner had ranked training second, the opportunity cost would be the training. In an exam, opportunity cost must be a **single named alternative** drawn from the case.

Opportunity cost also applies to time. If the owner spends Saturday making a table instead of visiting a trade fair, the lost contacts from the fair are the opportunity cost of that day.

## Specialisation

Specialisation means concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products. It happens at three levels:

- **Workers**: each person does one part of a job. Splitting production into separate tasks is called the **division of labour**.
- **Businesses**: a firm makes only one kind of product, or one stage of a product, and buys the rest from others.
- **Regions or countries**: an area concentrates on what its resources suit, such as tea growing in a hilly, wet district.

**Why it matters to a business.** Workers who repeat one task become quicker and make fewer mistakes, so output per worker rises. Less time is lost switching between jobs. Specialist machinery can be bought for each task. Recruiting is easier when each job needs a narrower set of skills.

**The limitations.** Repetitive work can be boring, which may lower motivation and raise absence. If one stage stops (a machine breaks or a worker is ill), the whole process can stop. A specialised business depends on other businesses for everything it does not make, and on demand for its one product. Specialisation is what makes **exchange** necessary: a specialist must trade its output for the goods and services it does not produce, usually using money.

## The purpose of business activity

A business combines the factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy customers' needs and wants. In doing so it:

- produces the output people want to buy, so scarce resources are turned into something more useful
- provides employment and incomes for its workers
- in most private sector businesses, aims to make a profit as the reward for the owner's risk.

Not every business has profit as its purpose. A social enterprise or a public sector organisation may use the same resources mainly to meet a social need. The purpose question in an exam is answered by linking resources, output and the needs or wants that output satisfies.

## Added value

**Added value** is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and components used to make it.

```
added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and components
```

**Worked example 2: calculating added value.** Hollin Furniture sells a dining table for $640. The timber, glue, screws and varnish bought from suppliers cost $230.

```
added value per table = 640 − 230 = $410
In a month it sells 25 tables: total added value = 25 × 410 = $10,250
```

The $410 is **not** profit. It must still pay the carpenters' wages, rent, electricity and other overheads. If those come to $8,600 a month, profit is $10,250 − $8,600 = $1,650.

## How added value can be increased

There are only two routes: **raise the price customers will pay** without an equal rise in input costs, or **reduce the cost of bought-in inputs** without an equal fall in price.

| Method | How it raises added value | Risk to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Better design or quality | Customers pay more for a superior product | Higher input costs may cancel the gain |
| Branding | A trusted name supports a higher price | Building a brand takes time and promotion spending |
| Customer service, such as free delivery or after-sales care | Adds to what the customer receives | Service costs staff time |
| Convenience, such as online ordering | Customers pay for ease of buying | Set-up cost |
| Cheaper suppliers or less waste | Lowers the input cost per unit | Cheaper materials may reduce quality and sales |

**Worked example 3: comparing two methods.** Hollin could (a) switch to a cheaper varnish, saving $15 a table, or (b) add a hand-carved edge that lets it charge $700 but uses $20 more timber.

```
(a) added value = 640 − 215 = $425   (up $15)
(b) added value = 700 − 250 = $450   (up $40)
```

Option (b) gives more added value per table, but only if customers accept the higher price. If sales fell from 25 to 20 tables, total added value would be 20 × 450 = $9,000, lower than the $10,250 it earns now. A good answer states this condition, because it turns a calculation into analysis.

## Common mistakes

- Giving every rejected option as the opportunity cost instead of the single next best.
- Defining scarcity as "having no money"; it is limited resources against unlimited wants.
- Treating added value as profit, or forgetting that it is per unit unless multiplied by sales.
- Saying "raise the price" without explaining why customers would pay more.
- Listing advantages of specialisation without the dependence and boredom it can cause.

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Define**: one precise sentence; for added value include both "selling price" and "bought-in materials".
- **Identify / State**: a short answer is enough, such as naming one way to add value.
- **Calculate**: show the formula, the figures and the unit ($).
- **Explain**: make the chain clear: method, effect on price or cost, effect on added value.
- **Consider / Justify**: weigh at least two options from the case and say which is better for this business and why, for example whether its customers are likely to pay more.
