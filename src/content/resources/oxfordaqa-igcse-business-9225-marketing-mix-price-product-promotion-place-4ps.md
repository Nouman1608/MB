This guide covers section **3.5.4 The elements of the marketing mix: price, product, promotion and place (4Ps)** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 5, Marketing. The specification asks you to understand **pricing methods** (skimming, penetration, competitive, loss-leader and cost-plus) and the factors that influence pricing; the **product**, including developing new products, design, brand image, **product differentiation and the unique selling point (USP)**, the **product life cycle** with extension strategies, and the **product portfolio**; **promotional methods** and the factors that influence the promotional mix; **place**, meaning channels of distribution and intermediaries; **e-commerce** including mobile commerce; and how the elements of the mix **work together** and change over time. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Marketing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-marketing/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-marketing-mix-price-product-promotion-place-4ps-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-marketing-mix-price-product-promotion-place-4ps-practice/).

## The marketing mix

The **marketing mix** is the combination of four decisions a business makes to sell to its target market: **product** (what it sells), **price** (what it charges), **promotion** (how it tells customers) and **place** (how it gets the product to customers). They are often called the **4Ps**. A good mix fits the target market, and the four elements must support one another.

## Price

### Pricing methods

| Method | How it works | Suited to | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost-plus | Add a fixed percentage (mark-up) to the cost of making the product | Businesses wanting a reliable profit on each item | Ignores competitors and what customers will pay |
| Competitive | Set a price similar to or just below rivals | Crowded markets with similar products | Profit squeezed; price wars |
| Penetration | Low launch price to win customers and market share quickly | New products in competitive markets | Low profit at first; hard to raise price later |
| Skimming | High launch price to earn high profit from early buyers, then lower it | New, innovative or fashionable products with little competition | Few customers at first; rivals may enter |
| Loss-leader | Sell some products below cost to attract customers who buy other goods | Shops with many products | Loses money on the item; bargain hunters may buy only the loss-leader |

**Worked example 1: cost-plus.** A product costs $8.00 to make. The business adds a 40% mark-up.

```
Mark-up = 40% x $8.00 = $3.20
Selling price = $8.00 + $3.20 = $11.20
```

**Worked example 2: skimming against penetration.** Orbit Headphones cost $45 to make. Skimming sells 2,000 at $110 and penetration sells 6,000 at $75 in the first year.

```
Skimming:    2,000 x ($110 - $45) = 2,000 x $65 = $130,000 profit
Penetration: 6,000 x ($75 - $45)  = 6,000 x $30 = $180,000 profit
```

Penetration gives more profit on these estimates, and a larger customer base, but skimming gives a higher profit per item and a premium image. The forecasts of sales are uncertain.

**Worked example 3: a loss-leader.** A shop sells milk at $0.90 though it costs $1.10, and sells 5,000 cartons. This loses 5,000 x $0.20 = $1,000. But 800 shoppers who came for the milk also buy other goods, earning $3 profit each: 800 x $3 = $2,400. The net gain is $2,400 - $1,000 = $1,400.

### Factors influencing price

- **Production costs:** the price must usually cover costs, and rise if costs rise.
- **Degree of competition:** with many rivals, prices are pushed down.
- **Stage in the product life cycle:** new products may be priced high or low; prices often fall as products mature.
- **Customers:** what the target market will pay, and how sensitive to price they are.
- **Brand image:** strong brands can charge more.
- **Business objectives:** market share objectives favour lower prices; profit objectives favour higher ones.
- **Growth:** as a business expands, lower unit costs from economies of scale allow lower prices.

## Product

The **product** is the good or service a business sells. Customers consider its features, quality, design, brand and service.

### Developing new products and adapting existing ones

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Fresh sales and a chance to enter new markets | Research and development are expensive |
| Keeps up with competitors and changing tastes | The product may fail; many new products do |
| Reduces reliance on old products | Staff and equipment may need to change |
| Can allow higher prices | Existing products may lose sales |

When designing a product, a business considers **design** (look, feel and function), **image or brand** (the identity customers associate with it) and the **needs of the target market**.

### Product differentiation and the unique selling point (USP)

**Product differentiation** means making a product stand out from similar products. A **unique selling point (USP)** is a feature that competitors do not offer, such as a longer battery life. A strong brand image builds loyalty and may allow a higher price.

### The product life cycle

The **product life cycle** shows how sales change over the life of a product.

| Stage | Sales | Typical actions |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low; costs high | Heavy promotion; limited distribution |
| Growth | Rising quickly | Build brand; widen distribution |
| Maturity | Highest; levels off | Defend share; promotions; improve product |
| Decline | Falling | Reduce costs, withdraw, or use an extension strategy |

**Worked example 4: life cycle sales.** A snack sells 2,000 units in introduction and 9,000 in growth, then 15,000 in maturity.

```
Growth from introduction = (9,000 - 2,000) / 2,000 x 100 = 350%
```

At maturity the business uses an **extension strategy**, such as a new flavour, new packaging or a new market. Suppose this increases sales by 20%: 15,000 x 1.20 = 18,000 units. Extension strategies are worth using when the product still has a loyal market, and the cost is less than the extra profit.

### Product portfolio

A **product portfolio** is the range of products a business sells. A balanced portfolio includes products at different stages of the life cycle, so that profits from mature products pay for developing new ones, and the business is not dependent on a single product. Broadening the range spreads risk, but may spread resources thinly.

## Promotion

**Promotion** is how a business informs, persuades and reminds customers about its products.

| Method | Description | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising (TV, radio, press, online, social media) | Paid messages | Reaches many people; builds awareness | Can be expensive; hard to measure |
| Sales promotions (discounts, BOGOF, free samples, coupons) | Short-term incentives | Boosts sales quickly | Can reduce profit and teach customers to wait for deals |
| Personal selling | Sales staff talk to customers | Can answer questions and close sales | Costly; reaches few people |
| Public relations and sponsorship | Positive publicity | Builds image; credible | Less control; takes time |
| Direct marketing (email, post, text) | Messages sent straight to chosen customers | Targeted and measurable | Seen as junk mail if overused |
| Social media and influencers | Content online | Cheap; reaches young customers; interactive | Hard to control the message |

**Worked example 5: cost of an online campaign.** An online campaign costs $9,000 and reaches 150,000 people. 1.5% buy.

```
Customers won = 1.5% x 150,000 = 2,250
Cost per customer = $9,000 / 2,250 = $4.00
```

If each customer earns $12 profit, the campaign gains 2,250 x $12 - $9,000 = $18,000.

### The promotional mix

The combination of promotional methods used is the **promotional mix**. It depends on:

- **the target market:** where they get information, such as social media for teenagers
- **the marketing budget:** a small business cannot afford national TV advertising
- **the activities of competitors:** a business may match or avoid rivals' promotions
- **the product:** complex products may need personal selling; everyday items need advertising and offers
- **the stage of the life cycle:** heavy advertising when a product is launched.

## Place

**Place** is how products reach customers: where they are sold and how they are distributed. A **channel of distribution** is the route a product takes from producer to consumer.

| Channel | Route | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Producer to consumer (website, own shops, telesales, vending machines, direct selling) | Producer keeps all the profit and controls the brand; must manage the selling and delivery |
| Via retailer | Producer to retailer to consumer | Retailer reaches many customers; takes a share |
| Via wholesaler | Producer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer | Wholesaler buys in bulk and breaks it down; adds another share |

Intermediaries each add a margin, so the final price rises.

**Worked example 6: the chain.** A manufacturer sells to a wholesaler for $10. The wholesaler adds 25%, and the retailer adds 40%.

```
Wholesaler's price = $10 x 1.25  = $12.50
Retailer's price   = $12.50 x 1.40 = $17.50
```

The customer pays $17.50, of which the manufacturer receives $10. Selling direct at $14 would give the manufacturer $4 more per unit and the customer a lower price, but the manufacturer would have to pay for selling and delivery.

**Choosing a channel** depends on the product (perishable goods need short channels), the customers (spread out or concentrated), the cost and the control the business wants.

### E-commerce and mobile commerce

**E-commerce** is buying and selling online, through a computer or mobile phone (m-commerce). It extends a business's reach to national and international markets.

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Large reach, including international markets | Delivery and returns are costly |
| Open all day and night | Strong competition and price comparison |
| Lower premises costs | Customers cannot touch or try the product |
| Customer data for targeted marketing | Security and technical risks |
| Easy ordering by phone | Not all customers have internet access |

## Integration: how the mix works together

The four elements must fit one another and the target market. A luxury watch needs a high price, a premium product, upmarket promotion and exclusive shops. A budget product needs a low price, wide distribution and promotions that stress value. Inconsistent mixes confuse customers, as when a premium product is discounted in a market stall.

The best mix changes over time. In introduction a business may use heavy promotion and a skimming or penetration price; in maturity it may lower the price, add extension strategies and widen distribution. Changes in technology, competition and customers' tastes mean that businesses must keep reviewing the mix.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing skimming (start high) with penetration (start low).
- Treating promotion as the whole of marketing.
- Describing a pricing method without the reason it suits the market.
- Forgetting that intermediaries add to the final price.
- Applying one mix to every product and market.
- Listing the stages of the product life cycle without describing what a business does at each.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** and **state** questions, give the meaning or an example. For **explain** questions, link the choice to the business: "penetration pricing suits a new snack in a crowded market because it quickly wins customers". For **calculate** questions, show the mark-up or price step by step. For **recommend** and **evaluate** questions, use the facts of the case (target market, budget, competitors, stage of the life cycle) and make the four Ps fit together.
