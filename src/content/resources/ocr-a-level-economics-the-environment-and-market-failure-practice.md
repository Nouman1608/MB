## Questions

**1.** A lake in Country E supplies a town with drinking water, hosts a sailing club and takes treated sewage from a works. Identify which function of the environment each use represents. **[3]**

**2.** A quarry in Country A has MPB: P = 100 − 4Q and MPC: P = 16 + 2Q. Dust costs nearby farms £18 for each unit produced.

**(a)** Calculate the market quantity and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find MSC and calculate the socially optimal quantity and price. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the tax per unit that achieves the optimum and the revenue raised. **[2]**

**3.** Households in Country G use diesel generators that pollute their neighbours.

**(a)** Using a diagram described in words, explain how this causes market failure. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why a landowner who plants woodland creates a different market failure. **[2]**

**4.** In a model of Country H, degradation D = 8 + 2.4Y − 0.06Y², where Y is income per head in thousands.

**(a)** Calculate D when Y = 10, and the income at the turning point. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why degradation may fall beyond the turning point. **[2]**
**(c)** State one limit of the environmental Kuznets curve as a guide to policy. **[1]**

**5.** Real GDP in Country J grows 2.5% a year.

**(a)** Emissions per unit of GDP fall 3.5%. Calculate the percentage change in emissions. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the fall in emissions per unit of GDP that would hold total emissions constant. **[2]**

**6.** Country K holds 450 million units of ore and extracts 15 million a year.

**(a)** Calculate how many years the ore lasts at a constant rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Extraction instead grows 3% a year. Show in which year the ore runs out. **[3]**
**(c)** Give one reason the actual path may differ. **[1]**

**7.** A fuel in Country L rises in price from £2.00 to £2.50 after a tax. Monthly sales fall from 1,000 to 940 units.

**(a)** Calculate PED from these figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the price rise needed for a 15% fall in quantity, assuming this elasticity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what the result implies for the effectiveness of the tax. **[3]**

**8.** A firm in Country M faces compliance costs of £1,500 for an emissions standard. The chance of inspection is 0.2 and the fine is £4,000.

**(a)** Calculate the expected fine and state whether the firm will comply, assuming it only seeks to minimise cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the minimum fine that would deter it. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one other limit of environmental standards. **[2]**

**9.** Firms P and R must cut pollution by 50 tonnes in total. Marginal abatement cost: P = 0.5q, R = 2q, where q is tonnes cut. Total cost is 0.25q² for P and q² for R.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost if each cuts 25 tonnes. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cuts and permit price under tradable permits. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the total cost saving and each firm's net cost after trading. **[3]**

**10.** For each of information provision, government expenditure and public and private partnerships, give one strength and one limit in reducing environmental degradation. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether economic growth will cause environmental degradation and limit sustainable development. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of a global agreement, compared with national taxes, in cutting emissions of a global pollutant. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Drinking water: provider of resources [1]. Sailing: provider of amenities [1]. Absorbing treated sewage: absorber of waste [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the function and tie it to the use. A use with no function named is hard to credit.

**2. (a)** 100 − 4Q = 16 + 2Q [1]; **Q = 14, P = £44** [1].
**(b)** MSC = 16 + 2Q + 18 = 34 + 2Q [1]; 100 − 4Q = 34 + 2Q gives **Q = 11** [1] and **P = £56** [1].
**(c)** ½ × 18 × (14 − 11) [1] = **£27** [1].
**(d)** Tax = MEC = **£18** [1]; revenue = 18 × 11 = **£198** [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the equations. Written working lets a reader follow your reasoning even if a figure slips, so set each step out.

**3. (a)** Consumers ignore the harm to neighbours, so MSB is below MPB [1]. The market quantity is where MPB = MPC [1], which is above the optimum where MSB = MSC [1]. Resources are over-allocated to generator use, with a welfare loss triangle [1].
**(b)** Woodland gives a positive externality of production, such as cleaner air [1]; MSC is below MPC, so the market output is too low [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A labelled diagram description, with the market and social points named, supports the explanation.

**4. (a)** D = 8 + 24 − 6 = **26** [1]; Y = 2.4 ÷ (2 × 0.06) [1] = **20** [1].
**(b)** Any two: cleaner technology [1]; a shift to services, or stronger regulation funded by higher income [1].
**(c)** It is a pattern, not a law, and damage may be irreversible before the turn [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give reasons for the downward slope, not just its shape.

**5. (a)** 1.025 × 0.965 = 0.9891 [1]; emissions fall **1.09%** [1].
**(b)** 1 − 1 ÷ 1.025 [1] = **2.44%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Multiply the growth factors. Subtracting 3.5 from 2.5 treats percentages as additive.

**6. (a)** 450 ÷ 15 = **30 years** [1].
**(b)** Cumulative use is 15 × (1.03ⁿ − 1) ÷ 0.03 [1]. After 21 years it is 430.1 [1]; after 22 years 458.1, which exceeds 450, so it runs out during **year 22** [1].
**(c)** New discoveries, higher prices cutting demand, or recycling [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Test the years either side of the answer so the conclusion is clear.

**7. (a)** Price +25%, quantity −6% [1]; PED = −6 ÷ 25 = **−0.24** [1].
**(b)** 15 ÷ 0.24 = **62.5%** [1]; so the price must rise by 62.5% [1].
**(c)** A value between 0 and −1 means demand is inelastic [1], so use falls proportionately less than price rises [1]. The tax raises revenue but needs to be large, which has equity effects on poorer households [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The sign matters. State that PED is negative and what an inelastic value means for the policy.

**8. (a)** 0.2 × 4,000 = **£800** [1]; below £1,500, so it will **not comply** [1].
**(b)** 1,500 ÷ 0.2 = **£7,500** [1]; a fine above this deters [1].
**(c)** Monitoring is costly [1], and the standard gives no incentive to cut beyond it [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the numbers to the idea of deterrence makes the point stronger than stating the arithmetic alone.

**9. (a)** P: 0.25 × 625 = 156.25 [1]; R: 625; total **£781.25** [1].
**(b)** Equal marginal cost: 0.5qP = 2qR with qP + qR = 50 [1]; **qP = 40, qR = 10** [1]; price = 0.5 × 40 = **£20** [1].
**(c)** Cost = 0.25 × 1,600 + 100 = 500; saving = **£281.25** [1]. R buys 15 permits (£300): net cost R = 100 + 300 = **£400** [1]; P sells them: net cost P = 400 − 300 = **£100** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the equal marginal cost condition before the answers. A quantity without that condition is hard to justify.

**10.** Information: cheap [1]; slow to change behaviour and hard to measure [1]. Government expenditure: acts where markets will not [1]; has an opportunity cost [1]. Partnership: brings private capital and skill [1]; long contracts may give poor value [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each strength and limit with its own policy. Generic points that fit all three gain less.

**11.** For: more output uses more resources and creates more waste [1]; non-renewable stocks are finite [1]; the extraction path in question 6 shows how fast growth can use up a stock [1]. Against: growth funds clean technology and enforcement [1]; rising prices push substitutes and recycling [1]; question 5 shows emissions can fall while GDP rises if intensity falls fast enough [1]. Sustainable development needs present needs met without harming future ones [1]. The environmental Kuznets curve suggests degradation may fall at higher incomes, but only for some pollutants and with a possible irreversible cost [1]. The outcome depends on technology, policy and the resource [1]. Judgement: growth need not cause harm, but without policy it is likely to for non-renewable resources and global pollutants [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned conclusion that names what the outcome depends on gains more credit than two lists.

**12.** A global pollutant is not confined by borders, so only action covering most emitters addresses the whole problem [1]. National taxes use prices and have legal force [1], but a country acting alone may see firms relocate, leaving world emissions unchanged [1]. A global agreement avoids this leakage [1]. However, countries gain from others cutting while they do not (free riding) [1], and costs fall unevenly between richer and poorer countries [1]. Enforcement is weak [1], and agreement may take years [1]. National taxes can raise revenue and apply quickly [1]. Judgement: an agreement is needed for a global pollutant, but works only with national policies to deliver it [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two policies directly, not one after the other. A final judgement should name the condition on which it depends.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a quantity without the equations that produced it.
- Placing MSC below MPC for a negative externality of production.
- Diagrams without a labelled market point, social point and welfare triangle.
- Adding percentages instead of multiplying growth factors.
- Giving PED without a sign or without saying what it implies.
- Describing a policy without judging how well it works at the level asked.
- Saying growth must harm, or must help, the environment with no weighing of both.
- Forgetting that permits cap the quantity and a tax sets the price.
