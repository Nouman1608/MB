These questions cover section 3.3 Costs and break-even analysis of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. They suit both Paper 1 (small businesses) and Paper 2 (large businesses), as both can draw on the whole content. Money is in pounds; businesses are invented. The standard form is: break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, where contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. Rounding break-even output up to a whole unit is the writer's own advice, and the formula is the standard form; neither is specification text.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term variable costs. **[2]**

**2.** Mehwish runs a small bakery. Identify whether each of these costs is fixed or variable. **[4]**

- Monthly rent of the shop unit
- Flour used in the loaves
- Insurance for the oven
- Paper bags used for each customer

**3.** Mira makes mugs. She sells each mug for £8. Variable cost is £3 a mug. Fixed costs are £2,000 a month.

**(a)** Complete the table for each output: revenue, total costs and profit or loss. **[4]**

| Mugs | Revenue (£) | Total costs (£) | Profit or loss (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | | | |
| 400 | | | |
| 600 | | | |
| 800 | | | |

**(b)** Identify Mira's break-even level of output. **[1]**

**4.** A firm sells a product for £25. Variable cost is £13 a unit. Fixed costs are £18,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even level of output. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit if the firm sells 2,100 units. **[2]**

**5.** A break-even chart for a printing business has these plotted points. Revenue is £0 at 0 sets and £24,000 at 2,000 sets. Total costs are £6,000 at 0 sets and £18,000 at 2,000 sets. Both lines are straight.

**(a)** Identify the break-even level of output. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit at 1,800 sets. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the loss at 600 sets. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way a rise in selling price can lower a firm's break-even output. **[3]**

**7.** Jaspreet runs a food truck. A meal sells for £9. Variable cost is £4 a meal and fixed costs are £3,500 a month. His supplier now raises the variable cost to £4.50 a meal.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even output before the rise. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the break-even output after the rise. **[2]**

**(c)** Analyse how the rise affects Jaspreet's monthly profit if he sells 1,000 meals. **[4]**

**8.** Outline two limitations of break-even charts. **[4]**

**9.** A business's fixed costs rise but its price and variable cost per unit do not change. Describe what happens to the total costs line and the break-even point on its chart. **[3]**

**10.** Orchard Candles sells 1,200 candles a month at £12 each. Variable cost is £7 a candle and fixed costs are £5,400 a month. The owner is choosing between two options.

- Option A: raise the price to £15. She expects sales to fall to 900 candles.
- Option B: keep the price at £12 and buy cheaper wax, so variable cost falls to £6.50. She expects sales to stay at 1,200.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even output for each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Justify which option the owner should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Layla plans to open a cafe. Fixed costs are £9,000 a month. The average customer spends £6 and the variable cost per customer is £2.40. She forecasts 100 customers a day for 26 days each month.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even number of customers a month. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate her forecast monthly profit. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether break-even analysis gives Layla enough information to decide whether to open the cafe. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** Costs that change in proportion to output [1], such as materials or packaging, so that total variable cost rises as more is produced [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition is likely to gain most when it says what the cost depends on (output). An example alone does not define the term.

**2.** Rent: fixed [1]. Flour: variable [1]. Insurance: fixed [1]. Paper bags: variable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Judge each cost separately by asking whether it changes when output changes.

**3. (a)** 200 mugs: revenue 1,600, total costs 2,000 + 600 = 2,600, loss of 1,000 [1]. 400 mugs: revenue 3,200, total costs 3,200, profit 0 [1]. 600 mugs: revenue 4,800, total costs 3,800, profit 1,000 [1]. 800 mugs: revenue 6,400, total costs 4,400, profit 2,000 [1].
**(b)** Break-even is **400 mugs**, where revenue equals total costs [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show how each row was found. Writing a loss as a negative figure or with the word loss makes the sign clear, and a bare number is easy to misread.

**4. (a)** Contribution per unit = 25 − 13 = £12 [1]. Break-even = 18,000 ÷ 12 [1] = **1,500 units** [1].
**(b)** Units above break-even = 2,100 − 1,500 = 600, and 600 × 12 [1] = **£7,200** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing fixed costs by the selling price is the common slip. Setting out the contribution first makes the method clear even if the arithmetic goes wrong.

**5. (a)** The lines meet where revenue equals total costs. Revenue slopes at £12 a set (24,000 ÷ 2,000) and total costs at £6 a set (12,000 ÷ 2,000), so 12x = 6,000 + 6x. Break-even is **1,000 sets** [1].
**(b)** Revenue = 1,800 × 12 = 21,600. Total costs = 6,000 + 6 × 1,800 = 16,800 [1]. Profit = **£4,800** [1].
**(c)** Revenue = 600 × 12 = 7,200. Total costs = 6,000 + 3,600 = 9,600 [1]. Loss = **£2,400** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* When a chart gives two points per line, work out the price and variable cost per set from them and state both; this shows the reasoning.

**6.** A higher price increases the contribution on each unit [1], because variable cost per unit is unchanged [1]. Each unit therefore covers more of the fixed costs, so fewer units are needed to break even [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong Explain answer states the fact and then develops it twice. Saying "it makes more money" without linking to fixed costs gives less to credit.

**7. (a)** Contribution = 9 − 4 = £5 [1]. 3,500 ÷ 5 = **700 meals** [1].
**(b)** Contribution = 9 − 4.50 = £4.50 [1]. 3,500 ÷ 4.50 = 777.8, so **778 meals** [1].
**(c)** Contribution per meal falls from £5 to £4.50 [1]. Profit at 1,000 meals falls from 1,000 × 5 − 3,500 = £1,500 to 1,000 × 4.50 − 3,500 = £1,000 [1], a fall of £500 or one third [1]. He must now sell 78 more meals to break even, so the van is less safe if sales drop, so he could consider raising the price or changing supplier [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers are likely to gain more when the figures are linked to the business's position, for example the extra 78 meals, rather than only restating the calculation.

**8.** First limitation, for example revenue is drawn as a straight line, which assumes the same price at every output [1]. Link: in practice discounts for bulk orders lower average price, so revenue is overstated [1]. Second limitation, for example the chart assumes all output is sold [1]. Link: unsold stock is still paid for, so real profit is lower than shown [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Outline wants two linked points for each limitation. Naming a limitation without saying what it does to the chart or the decision gives only the first point.

**9.** The total costs line starts higher on the vertical axis by the amount of the increase [1] and stays parallel to the old line, since variable cost per unit is unchanged [1]. It meets the revenue line further to the right, so the break-even output increases [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both the line and the break-even point, and give a direction.

**10. (a)** Option A: contribution = 15 − 7 = £8, and 5,400 ÷ 8 = **675 candles** [2]. Option B: contribution = 12 − 6.50 = £5.50 [1], and 5,400 ÷ 5.50 = 981.8, so **982 candles** [1].
**(b)** Profit for A at 900 candles = 900 × 8 − 5,400 = £1,800 [1]. Profit for B at 1,200 candles = 1,200 × 5.50 − 5,400 = £1,200 [1]. Option A has the lower break-even output and the higher profit, and it would also beat today's profit of £600 [1]. However, A relies on the forecast fall being only to 900; if more customers leave, the profit is lower, and B keeps sales steady [1]. B's break-even output of 982 is close to its sales of 1,200, so an 18% sales drop removes profit [1]. Recommend Option A because the lower break-even output and higher profit outweigh the risk, provided she is confident that sales will stay above about 825 candles, the level at which A and B give the same profit [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify asks you to recommend one option. Use figures from both, give one risk and reach a clear choice.

**11. (a)** Contribution per customer = 6 − 2.40 = £3.60 [1]. 9,000 ÷ 3.60 [1] = **2,500 customers** [1].
**(b)** Forecast customers = 100 × 26 = 2,600, which is 100 above break-even [1]. Profit = 100 × 3.60 = **£360** [1], since each of the 100 customers above break-even adds £3.60.
**(c)** Points for: the analysis shows she needs 2,500 customers, or about 96 a day, to cover costs [1]. The forecast is only 100 above that, so the cafe is close to break-even and a small fall in customers would give a loss [1]. It is quick and uses her own figures, so it highlights risk before she spends money [1]. Points against: the forecast is an estimate and may be wrong [1]. The chart does not show whether customers will actually come, nor competition or location [1]. Costs such as rent may change, and a loss in early months would affect her cash [1]. Judgement: break-even analysis is helpful for showing how thin the margin is, but she needs market research and a cash-flow forecast too [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers are likely to gain more when they use the calculated figures from parts (a) and (b) and finish with a supported conclusion rather than a summary.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of by the contribution per unit.
- Giving break-even output with no unit, or leaving a decimal such as 981.8 instead of a whole unit.
- Treating variable cost per unit as the total variable cost.
- Writing a loss as a profit, or leaving out the minus sign or the word loss.
- Starting the total costs line at zero rather than at fixed costs.
- Giving one point in an Explain answer, which needs a statement and two expansion points.
- Listing limitations without saying what they do to the business's decision.
- In Justify and Evaluate answers, calculating well but giving no recommendation or conclusion.
