This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5 Newton's laws of motion** in Section 3.2 Mechanics and materials of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. The specification is brief, "knowledge and application of the three laws of motion" and "use of F = ma in situations where the mass is constant", but application is where marks are won or lost: choosing the system, drawing the forces and writing one equation per object.

## What the specification asks for

- knowledge and application of the three laws of motion
- use of F = ma in situations where the mass is constant.

F in F = ma is the **resultant** force. The momentum form of the second law, F = Δ(mv)/Δt, belongs to 3.2.6 and is needed when mass changes; in this subtopic, mass is always constant.

## The three laws

**First law.** An object remains at rest, or continues to move at constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it. The tendency to keep the same velocity is called **inertia**; an object with more mass has more inertia.

**Second law.** The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force on it and in the same direction: **F = ma**, with F in N, m in kg and a in m s⁻². This also defines the newton: 1 N is the resultant force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m s⁻².

**Third law.** If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size, opposite in direction and of the same type. The two forces act on **different** objects, so they never cancel each other.

### How the laws fit together

- Zero resultant force → no acceleration (first law; equilibrium from 3.2.1).
- Non-zero resultant force → acceleration in its direction, of size F/m (second law).
- Every force is one half of an interaction pair (third law).

## Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows **one** object and **only** the forces acting **on** it, each drawn from the object in the direction it acts. Common forces:

| Force | Direction |
|---|---|
| Weight, W = mg | Vertically down, from the centre of mass |
| Normal contact force | Perpendicular to the surface, away from it |
| Friction | Along the surface, opposing (attempted) sliding |
| Tension | Along a string or rope, away from the object |
| Drag or air resistance | Opposite to the velocity |
| Driving force, thrust | In the direction of the push |

Then: choose a positive direction, find the resultant force along it, and set it equal to ma.

## Worked example 1: two blocks in contact

**Question.** Blocks of mass 2.0 kg and 4.0 kg sit side by side on a smooth floor. A horizontal force of 12 N pushes the 2.0 kg block, which pushes the 4.0 kg block. Find the acceleration and the force between the blocks.

**Method.**

1. Whole system (6.0 kg): the only horizontal external force is 12 N, so a = 12 ÷ 6.0 = **2.0 m s⁻²**.
2. 4.0 kg block alone: the only horizontal force on it is the push P from the 2.0 kg block. P = 4.0 × 2.0 = **8.0 N**.
3. Check on the 2.0 kg block: 12 − 8.0 = 4.0 N = 2.0 × 2.0. Correct.

**Comment.** By the third law, the 4.0 kg block pushes back on the 2.0 kg block with 8.0 N. That is why the 2.0 kg block's resultant is only 4.0 N.

## Worked example 2: a car towing a trailer

**Question.** A car of mass 1200 kg tows a trailer of mass 400 kg along a level road. The driving force is 2400 N. Resistive forces are 300 N on the car and 100 N on the trailer. Find the acceleration and the tension in the tow bar.

**Method.**

1. System: resultant = 2400 − 300 − 100 = 2000 N; a = 2000 ÷ 1600 = **1.25 m s⁻²**.
2. Trailer alone: T − 100 = 400 × 1.25, so T = **600 N**.
3. Check on the car: 2400 − 300 − 600 = 1500 N = 1200 × 1.25. Correct.

**Comment.** Treat the tow bar force as internal when you consider the whole system, and as external when you isolate one vehicle. Choose whichever object has fewest forces for the second step.

## Worked example 3: a pulley system

**Question.** A 3.0 kg block on a smooth horizontal table is joined by a light string over a smooth pulley at the table's edge to a 1.0 kg mass hanging freely. Find the acceleration and the tension. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹.

**Method.** The string keeps both masses moving with the same size of acceleration.

1. Hanging mass: 1.0 × 9.81 − T = 1.0a.
2. Block on table: T = 3.0a.
3. Add the equations: 9.81 = 4.0a, so a = **2.45 m s⁻²**; T = 3.0 × 2.45 = **7.36 N**.

**Comment.** The tension is less than the hanging weight (9.81 N). If T equalled the weight, the hanging mass would have no resultant force and could not accelerate.

## Worked example 4: a slope with friction

**Question.** A 5.0 kg box slides down a slope at 25° to the horizontal. The frictional force on it is 8.0 N. Find its acceleration.

**Method.**

1. Component of weight down the slope = mg sin 25° = 5.0 × 9.81 × 0.423 = 20.7 N.
2. Resultant down the slope = 20.7 − 8.0 = 12.7 N.
3. a = 12.7 ÷ 5.0 = **2.5 m s⁻²** down the slope.

**Comment.** The normal contact force and mg cos 25° act perpendicular to the slope and cancel, so they do not appear in the equation along the slope.

## Identifying third-law pairs

A genuine pair: same type of force, equal size, opposite direction, acting on two different objects.

| Force | Its third-law partner |
|---|---|
| Earth pulls an apple down (gravitational) | Apple pulls the Earth up (gravitational) |
| Swimmer's hands push water backwards (contact) | Water pushes the swimmer forwards (contact) |
| Floor pushes up on a box (contact) | Box pushes down on the floor (contact) |
| Tyre pushes road backwards (friction) | Road pushes tyre forwards (friction) |

**Not a pair:** the weight of a box and the normal force on it. They both act on the box, they are different types, and they are equal only when the box is not accelerating vertically.

## Common mistakes

- Using a single force instead of the resultant force in F = ma.
- Writing "action and reaction cancel": they act on different objects.
- Drawing forces that the object exerts on something else on its free-body diagram.
- Assuming the tension in a pulley string equals the hanging weight when the system accelerates.
- Using mass (kg) where weight (N) is needed: the weight of 5.0 kg is 49 N, not 5.0 N.
- Stating the first law as "an object at rest stays at rest" and leaving out constant velocity.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** a law: use the full wording, including "resultant force" for the first and second laws and "different objects" or "same type" for the third.
- **Draw** or **label** a free-body diagram: one object, arrows from it, each labelled with a named force.
- **Calculate**: write "resultant force = ma" with the forces listed and signed before substituting.
- **Explain** a situation using Newton's laws: name the law you are using and link it to the forces, as in "the resultant force is zero, so by the first law the velocity is constant".
- **Identify** a third-law partner: give the object it acts on and the type of force.
