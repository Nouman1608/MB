This guide goes deeper into one short but heavily used statement of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification: **4.2.1.1 Chemical bonds**, the opening subsection of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the
properties of matter. It is examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier, nothing in it is marked
(HT only), and there is no required practical. Almost every later question on structure and properties
starts from this subsection, because you cannot explain a melting point or a conductivity until you have
named the bond and said what attracts what.

For a fast recap use the [chemical bonds revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-bonds-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [chemical bonds practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-bonds-practice/).
The broader [chemical bonds and ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-bonds-and-ionic-compounds/)
covers this subsection alongside 4.2.1.3. The course hub is [AQA GCSE Chemistry](/boards/aqa/gcse/chemistry/).

## What the specification asks for

The subsection makes three kinds of statement, and each one turns into a different kind of exam question.

| Statement in 4.2.1.1 | What it becomes in a question |
|---|---|
| There are three types of strong chemical bond: ionic, covalent and metallic | "Name the type of bonding in…" |
| The particles: oppositely charged ions; atoms sharing pairs of electrons; atoms sharing delocalised electrons | "Describe the particles in…" or "What is meant by…" |
| Where each occurs: metal + non-metal compounds; most non-metallic elements and non-metal compounds; metallic elements and alloys | "Explain how you can tell that X is ionic" |
| Explain bonding in terms of electrostatic forces and the transfer or sharing of electrons | "Explain how the atoms are held together" |

The last bullet is the skill that separates a one-mark answer from a full-mark one. Naming the bond is
recall; explaining it means saying **which electrons move or are shared** and **which opposite charges
attract**.

## Step 1: decide the bond type from the elements

Use the position of each element in the periodic table. Metals are to the left of the stepped line;
non-metals are to the right, and hydrogen counts as a non-metal.

1. **Is it a single metallic element or a mixture of metals (an alloy)?** Metallic bonding.
2. **Is it a compound containing a metal and a non-metal?** Ionic bonding.
3. **Is it a non-metal element, or a compound of non-metals only?** Covalent bonding.
4. **Is it a Group 0 element?** The atoms stay separate; there are no strong bonds between them.

Three habits make this reliable:

- **Read every element in the formula.** CaCO₃ contains a metal (calcium), so the compound is classed as
  ionic, even though it also contains two non-metals.
- **Count element types, not atoms.** Cl₂ has two atoms of one element; it is still a non-metal element, so
  the bonding is covalent.
- **Do not let the word "compound" pull you towards ionic.** Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, is a compound, but both
  elements are non-metals, so it is covalent.

### Worked example 1: a classification table

*Name the type of bonding in each substance and give your reason: lithium oxide, ammonia, nickel,
an alloy of copper and nickel, phosphorus trichloride, neon.*

| Substance | Elements present | Bonding | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lithium oxide, Li₂O | Li (metal), O (non-metal) | Ionic | Compound of a metal with a non-metal |
| Ammonia, NH₃ | N, H (both non-metals) | Covalent | Compound of non-metals |
| Nickel, Ni | Ni (metal) | Metallic | Metallic element |
| Copper–nickel alloy | Cu, Ni (both metals) | Metallic | Alloys have metallic bonding |
| Phosphorus trichloride, PCl₃ | P, Cl (both non-metals) | Covalent | Compound of non-metals |
| Neon, Ne | One noble gas | No strong bonds | Atoms already have a stable arrangement of electrons |

The reason column is what earns credit in an "explain how you know" question. "Because it is ionic" is
circular; "because it is a compound of a metal and a non-metal" is a reason.

## Step 2: explain the bond with electrostatic forces

An electrostatic force is the attraction between opposite charges (or repulsion between like charges).
All three strong bonds are attractions of this kind. What differs is **where the positive and negative
charges come from** and **how far the attraction reaches**.

| | Ionic | Covalent | Metallic |
|---|---|---|---|
| What happens to electrons | Transferred from metal atoms to non-metal atoms | A pair is shared between two atoms | Outer electrons become delocalised |
| Positive charge | Positive metal ions | The nuclei of the two bonded atoms | Positive metal ions (atoms that have released outer electrons) |
| Negative charge | Negative non-metal ions | The shared pair of electrons | The delocalised electrons |
| Reach of the attraction | Through the whole lattice, in every direction | Between one pair of atoms only | Through the whole structure |

The last row is worth learning. A covalent bond joins **two particular atoms**, which is why covalent
substances can form separate molecules. Ionic and metallic bonding have no particular partner for each
particle, which is why they always give giant structures.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how the particles are held together in solid magnesium chloride and in a chlorine
molecule.*

> **Magnesium chloride:** electrons are **transferred** from magnesium atoms to chlorine atoms *(the
> transfer, and its direction)*. Magnesium atoms become positive ions and chlorine atoms become negative
> ions *(both ions named with their sign)*. The oppositely charged ions are held by **strong electrostatic
> attraction** *(the force, named)*.
>
> **Chlorine molecule:** the two chlorine atoms **share a pair of electrons** *(sharing, and "pair")*. The
> negative shared pair is attracted to the **positive nuclei of both atoms** *(what attracts what)*, and this
> strong attraction holds the two atoms together.

Notice what the answer does not say. It does not call the ionic bond "a shared electron", and it does not
stop at "chlorine atoms share electrons". Each half names the electron change and then the attraction.

### Worked example 2: correcting a weak answer

*A student writes: "In copper the atoms are bonded because they share electrons with each other." Improve
the answer.*

```
Problem 1: "share electrons" sounds covalent; metallic sharing is
           of DELOCALISED electrons, free to move through the structure
Problem 2: no electrostatic force is named
Improved:  The outer-shell electrons of the copper atoms are delocalised,
           so they move through the whole structure. There is a strong
           electrostatic attraction between these negative electrons and
           the positive metal ions, which holds the structure together.
```

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Name / Give / State:** one word or phrase is enough: "ionic", "covalent", "metallic". Do not add
  explanation you were not asked for, but do not use vague words such as "chemical bond".
- **Describe:** say what the particles are and what happens to the electrons, without needing "why".
- **Explain:** link a cause to an effect. For bonding, the cause is the transfer or sharing of electrons
  and the effect is an electrostatic attraction between named opposite charges.
- **Compare:** give a similarity **and** differences, each one stated for both substances. "Both involve
  electrostatic attraction" is a similarity; "ionic involves transfer but covalent involves sharing" is a
  difference stated for both.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a bond between two non-metals ionic because one of them "takes" the electrons. At GCSE, a
  compound of non-metals is covalent.
- Missing the metal in a formula with several non-metals, such as K₂SO₄ or CaCO₃.
- Writing "ions share electrons" or "atoms are attracted by the electrons" with no mention of opposite
  charges.
- Forgetting that the particles in a covalent bond and in metallic bonding are both described as
  **atoms** in the specification, while the particles in ionic bonding are **ions**.
- Saying the noble gases form covalent molecules. Their atoms remain separate.
- Treating an alloy as a compound. An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements; its bonding is
  metallic.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is hydrogen a metal or a non-metal for bonding questions?**
Hydrogen is a non-metal. Compounds such as hydrogen chloride and water are therefore covalent.

**Why does the specification say "most" non-metallic elements are covalent?**
Because the Group 0 elements are non-metals that do not form bonds between their atoms: they exist as
single atoms.

**Do I need to know how strong each type of bond is?**
You need to know that all three are strong chemical bonds. How bond strength links to melting points is
taught later in topic 4.2.
