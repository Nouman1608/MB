## Two questions this section answers

**3.2.1.1 Globalisation** — what is it and what drives it? **3.2.1.2 Global systems** — what does it produce, and who benefits or loses?

## Dimensions and drivers of globalisation (3.2.1.1)

**Flows**: capital, labour, products, services, information. **Drivers**: technology, financial/transport/security/communications/management/information systems, trade agreements. Practise naming a **real example** for each flow type — a specific FDI flow, a specific migration pattern, a specific supply chain — rather than listing categories abstractly.

## Interdependence — a double-edged phenomenon (3.2.1.2)

Unequal flows of people, money, ideas and technology can **promote stability, growth and development** — but also cause **inequalities, conflicts and injustices**. Unequal power relations let some states drive global systems to their own advantage; others can only respond or resist in a more constrained way. Avoid one-sided answers — show **both** outcomes for the same flow or system.

## Worked example: unequal power relations

```
Claim:      states with greater economic/political power can drive
            global systems (trade rules, financial institutions) to
            their own advantage
Example:    a developed economy negotiating trade terms favouring its
            own industries, or setting terms within an international
            financial institution
Consequence: less powerful states are more constrained, often limited
            to responding rather than shaping decisions
Balance:    acknowledge genuine opportunities global systems create for
            less powerful states too (investment, market access), even
            as terms remain shaped by more powerful actors
```

This **claim–example–consequence–balance** structure is the evaluative depth this sub-topic is assessed against.

## Worked example: two-part exam question

A typical exam question first asks candidates to explain a dimension or factor of globalisation, then follow up asking them to assess its consequences.

```
Part (a): Explain how developments in communications technology have
          driven globalisation. [describe the dimension/driver --
          faster, cheaper global communication enables coordinated
          international production, real-time financial transactions,
          and instant information flow across borders]

Part (b): Assess the consequences of this for global interdependence.
          [use the same example to show BOTH outcomes -- it can
          promote growth by enabling businesses to coordinate global
          supply chains efficiently, but it can also deepen
          inequality if the infrastructure and skills needed to
          benefit are concentrated in already-wealthy regions]
```

Preparing both halves together, with the **same running example** carried from part (a) into part (b), is the efficient revision structure this section's typical exam format rewards, since re-using one well-understood example across both dimension and consequence saves significant recall time under exam pressure.

## Global marketing and patterns of production

Beyond the five named flows, this sub-topic also covers global marketing and patterns of production, distribution and consumption -- how businesses standardise or adapt products for global markets, and how global supply chains distribute production across countries to exploit cost, skill or resource advantages. Connect this content to a real transnational corporation example where possible, since naming a specific company's global production pattern demonstrates the applied understanding this sub-topic tests more convincingly than describing the pattern abstractly.

## Key terms

**Globalisation** — increasing economic, political, social and environmental interconnectedness, driven by flows of capital, labour, products, services and information. **Interdependence** — a state where decisions in one place affect others elsewhere. **Global system** — a network of interconnected processes operating across borders (trade, finance, governance). **Geopolitics** — how geography, power and international relations interact to shape political decisions.

## Vocabulary precision

Distinguishing "globalisation" (the process and its drivers) from "global systems" (the structures and networks that result) avoids one of the more common conceptual blurs in this section, since exam questions sometimes hinge on which of the two is actually being asked about -- read the command word and the noun it attaches to carefully before deciding which sub-topic's content to draw on.

## Common mistakes

- Listing globalisation's dimensions without a concrete example for any.
- Presenting globalisation/interdependence as uniformly positive or negative.
- Describing unequal power relations abstractly, without naming a specific mechanism.
- Confusing globalisation's dimensions (3.2.1.1, what is flowing) with interdependence's consequences (3.2.1.2, what results).

## Quick self-test

2. Give one way interdependence can promote development, and one way it can cause inequality.
3. Name one specific mechanism through which powerful states can shape global systems.
4. What distinguishes 3.2.1.1 from 3.2.1.2?
5. Why should an answer on interdependence avoid being one-sided?
6. What content does the section following this one (International Trade and Access to Markets) cover?

**Answers:** 1. Capital, labour, products, services, information. 2. Promote: investment/technology transfer can drive growth. Cause inequality: unequal flows can concentrate benefits with already-powerful actors, worsening disparities elsewhere. 3. Any valid example, e.g. negotiating trade agreements or setting terms within an international financial institution. 4. 3.2.1.1 covers what globalisation is and what drives it (the dimensions and factors); 3.2.1.2 covers what results from it (interdependence, its benefits and costs). 5. Because the specification frames interdependence as genuinely double-edged — capable of promoting stability and growth, but also inequality and conflict — so a strong answer shows both sides for the same example. 6. The role of transnational corporations and how trade agreements shape access to markets.
