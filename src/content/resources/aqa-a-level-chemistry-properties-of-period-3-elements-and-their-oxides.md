## Reactions of sodium and magnesium with water

**Sodium** reacts vigorously with cold water. It floats, melts into a ball, fizzes and moves about. The solution is strongly alkaline (pH about 13 to 14).

`2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)`

**Magnesium** reacts very slowly with cold water, giving a few bubbles of hydrogen. Magnesium hydroxide
is only slightly soluble, so the solution is weakly alkaline (pH about 9 to 10).

`Mg(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)`

Magnesium reacts much faster with **steam**. It burns with a bright white flame and forms magnesium oxide,
not the hydroxide.

`Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)`

**Why sodium is more reactive.** In both reactions the metal atoms lose their outer electrons to form
positive ions. A sodium atom loses one electron. A magnesium atom must lose two, and the second is removed
from a positive ion, so much more energy is needed in total. Magnesium therefore reacts more slowly.

## Reactions of the elements with oxygen

All six elements Na to S burn when heated in oxygen. Each forms its highest oxide, except sulfur, which
first forms SO₂.

| Element | Observation on burning | Equation | Oxidation state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | yellow-orange flame, white solid | `4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O` | +1 |
| Mg | bright white flame, white solid | `2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO` | +2 |
| Al | powder burns with a bright white flame, white solid | `4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃` | +3 |
| Si | needs strong heating, white solid | `Si + O₂ → SiO₂` | +4 |
| P | burns with a bright flame, dense white fumes | `P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀` | +5 |
| S | blue flame, colourless choking gas | `S + O₂ → SO₂` | +4 |

Two points to know:

- A piece of aluminium seems unreactive because a thin, tough layer of Al₂O₃ covers its surface and stops
  further reaction. Powdered aluminium has a large surface area and burns well.
- SO₂ is only converted to SO₃ by further oxidation, which needs a catalyst (vanadium(V) oxide) and heat:
  `2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃`. In SO₃ sulfur is +6.

The maximum oxidation state rises by one each time from Na (+1) to S (+6), because it equals the number of
outer electrons that the atom can use in bonding.

### Worked example 1

**What mass of sodium oxide forms when 2.30 g of sodium burns completely in oxygen? (Ar: Na = 23.0,
O = 16.0)**

```
moles of Na  = 2.30 / 23.0 = 0.100 mol
4Na → 2Na₂O, so moles of Na₂O = 0.100 / 2 = 0.0500 mol
Mr of Na₂O   = 2(23.0) + 16.0 = 62.0
mass of Na₂O = 0.0500 × 62.0 = 3.10 g
```

## Melting points of the highest oxides

The highest oxides are Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀ and SO₃. Their melting points rise from Na₂O to
MgO, stay high for Al₂O₃ and SiO₂, then fall sharply for P₄O₁₀ and SO₃. MgO has the highest melting point
of the set. In order: MgO > Al₂O₃ > SiO₂ > Na₂O > P₄O₁₀ > SO₃.

| Oxide | Structure | Bonding broken on melting |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂O, MgO | giant ionic lattice | strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions |
| Al₂O₃ | giant ionic lattice with some covalent character | strong ionic attraction |
| SiO₂ | macromolecular (giant covalent) | many strong covalent bonds |
| P₄O₁₀, SO₃ (and SO₂) | simple molecular | weak intermolecular forces only |

**Na₂O and MgO.** Both are giant ionic lattices. Mg²⁺ has a higher charge than Na⁺ and is smaller, so
the attraction between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions is stronger. More energy is needed to overcome it, so MgO melts
at a higher temperature.

**Al₂O₃.** Al³⁺ is small and highly charged. It distorts (polarises) the electron cloud of nearby O²⁻ ions,
so the bonding has some covalent character. Its melting point is high but lower than that of MgO.

**SiO₂.** Each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four oxygen atoms and each oxygen to two silicon atoms
in a giant structure. Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds, so the melting point is high.

**P₄O₁₀, SO₃ and SO₂.** These are simple molecules. Melting overcomes only the weak forces between
molecules, not the covalent bonds inside them, so melting points are low. P₄O₁₀ molecules are much larger,
with more electrons (140 per molecule against 40 in SO₃), so the van der Waals forces between them are
stronger and P₄O₁₀ melts at a higher temperature than SO₃. SO₂ melts lower still.

### Worked example 2

**Explain why MgO has a much higher melting point than P₄O₁₀.**

1. MgO is a giant ionic lattice. Melting it overcomes strong attractions between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions,
   which needs a lot of energy.
2. P₄O₁₀ is simple molecular. Melting overcomes only weak van der Waals forces between molecules; no
   covalent bonds break.
3. Much less energy is needed, so P₄O₁₀ melts at a much lower temperature.

## Reactions of the oxides with water

| Oxide | Reaction with water | pH of solution (approx.) | Nature of oxide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | `Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH` | 13 to 14 | basic |
| MgO | `MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂` | 9 to 10 | basic |
| Al₂O₃ | insoluble, no reaction | 7 | amphoteric |
| SiO₂ | insoluble, no reaction | 7 | acidic |
| P₄O₁₀ | `P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄` | 0 to 1 | acidic |
| SO₂ | `SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃` | 2 to 3 | acidic |
| SO₃ | `SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄` | 0 to 1 | acidic |

**Explaining the trend by bonding.**

- **Ionic oxides (Na₂O, MgO)** contain oxide ions. The oxide ion takes a proton from water to form
  hydroxide ions: `O²⁻ + H₂O → 2OH⁻`. The solution is alkaline. Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble, so
  fewer OH⁻ ions are in solution and the pH is lower than for NaOH.
- **Al₂O₃** (ionic with covalent character) and **SiO₂** (macromolecular) have very strong bonding in
  giant structures and do not dissolve, so the water stays at pH 7.
- **Simple molecular covalent oxides (P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃)** react with water to form acids, which release
  H⁺ ions. H₂SO₃ is a weak acid that only partly dissociates (`H₂SO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HSO₃⁻`), so its pH is higher
  than that of H₂SO₄.

So across Period 3 the oxides change from basic (ionic) to amphoteric (Al₂O₃) to acidic (covalent).

## Structures of the acids and anions

In the anions the negative charge is shown on single-bonded oxygen atoms.

```
Phosphoric(V) acid, H₃PO₄         Phosphate ion, PO₄³⁻
          O                                 O
          ‖                                 ‖
   H–O – P – O–H                     ⁻O – P – O⁻
          |                                 |
          O–H                               O⁻

Sulfuric(IV) acid, H₂SO₃          Sulfate(IV) ion, SO₃²⁻
          O                                 O
          ‖                                 ‖
   H–O – S – O–H   (lone pair on S)   ⁻O – S – O⁻   (lone pair on S)

Sulfuric(VI) acid, H₂SO₄          Sulfate(VI) ion, SO₄²⁻
          O                                 O
          ‖                                 ‖
   H–O – S – O–H                     ⁻O – S – O⁻
          ‖                                 ‖
          O                                 O
```

Partly neutralised ions keep some O–H groups: H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ from phosphoric(V) acid, HSO₃⁻ from
sulfuric(IV) acid and HSO₄⁻ from sulfuric(VI) acid. Phosphorus and sulfur(VI) have four bonding regions,
so H₃PO₄, PO₄³⁻, H₂SO₄ and SO₄²⁻ are tetrahedral around the central atom. In H₂SO₃ and SO₃²⁻ sulfur has
three bonding regions and a lone pair, so the shape is pyramidal.

Sulfuric(IV) acid is also called sulfurous acid, and the sulfate(IV) ion is the sulfite ion.

## Reactions of the oxides with acids and bases

**Basic oxides react with acids** to form a salt and water.

```
Na₂O + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O          MgO + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂O
ionic: O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O
```

**Amphoteric Al₂O₃ reacts with both acids and bases.**

```
with acid:  Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
            Al₂O₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂O
with base:  Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄
ionic:      Al₂O₃ + 2OH⁻ + 3H₂O → 2Al(OH)₄⁻
```

**Acidic oxides react with bases.** SiO₂ does not react with water, but it reacts with hot, concentrated
sodium hydroxide and with basic oxides such as CaO.

```
SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + H₂O        SiO₂ + CaO → CaSiO₃
P₄O₁₀ + 12NaOH → 4Na₃PO₄ + 6H₂O
SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O          SO₂ + CaO → CaSO₃
SO₃ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O          SO₃ + MgO → MgSO₄
```

### Worked example 3

**Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide that reacts exactly with 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric
acid. (Ar: Mg = 24.3, O = 16.0)**

```
MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O
moles of HCl = 25.0 / 1000 × 2.00 = 0.0500 mol
moles of MgO = 0.0500 / 2 = 0.0250 mol
Mr of MgO    = 24.3 + 16.0 = 40.3
mass of MgO  = 0.0250 × 40.3 = 1.01 g (3 s.f.)
```

## Practical: burning elements and testing the oxides

A typical method:

1. Heat a small piece of the element on a combustion spoon and lower it into a gas jar of oxygen.
2. When burning stops, add a little distilled water and shake.
3. Measure the pH with universal indicator (compare with a pH chart) or a pH meter (AT c).
4. Record the results in a table of element, observation, oxide formed and pH (PS 2.2).

Handle corrosive and toxic substances with care (AT k). Burn phosphorus and sulfur in a fume cupboard,
because their oxides are toxic and irritant.

## Common errors

- Writing `Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂` for cold water. That equation is for steam; cold water gives Mg(OH)₂.
- Giving pH 14 for MgO. Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble, so the pH is about 9 to 10.
- Saying Al₂O₃ or SiO₂ "dissolve to form a neutral solution". They are insoluble; the water stays neutral.
- Saying covalent bonds break when P₄O₁₀ or SO₃ melts. Only intermolecular forces are overcome.
- Giving SiO₂ "strong intermolecular forces"; it has covalent bonds throughout.
- Writing `SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄`. Sulfur stays +4, so the product is H₂SO₃.
- Forgetting that Al₂O₃ reacts with bases as well as acids.
