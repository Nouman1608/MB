Quick recall for section 3.2 Cash flow forecasting of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1), Issue 2, February 2026, points 3.2.1 and 3.2.2. Worked forecasts with credit sales and a cash-and-profit reconciliation are in the [cash flow forecasting study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-cash-flow-forecasting/); questions are in the [cash flow forecasting practice set](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-cash-flow-forecasting-practice/). Figures are invented, in dollars.

## 1. The specification list

- **3.2.1 Importance of cash:** to pay suppliers, overheads and employees; to prevent business failure (insolvency); the difference between cash and profit.
- **3.2.2 Cash-flow forecasts:** cash inflows, cash outflows, net cash flow, opening and closing balances.

## 2. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash | Money held by the business in notes, coins and bank accounts |
| Cash flow | The movement of money into and out of a business |
| Cash inflow | Money received, such as sales receipts, a loan or owner's capital |
| Cash outflow | Money paid out, such as wages, rent, materials or loan repayments |
| Net cash flow | Total inflows minus total outflows for a period |
| Opening balance | Cash held at the start of a period; the previous closing balance |
| Closing balance | Opening balance plus net cash flow |
| Cash-flow forecast | A prediction of future inflows and outflows, usually month by month |
| Insolvency | Being unable to pay debts when they fall due |
| Overheads | Running costs that are not tied to output, such as rent and insurance |
| Credit sale | A sale where the customer pays at a later date |
| Profit | Revenue minus costs for a period |

Definitions are standard teaching; the specification lists the terms without defining them.

## 3. The two rules (and their rearrangements)

- **Net cash flow = total inflows − total outflows**
  - so total outflows = total inflows − net cash flow
  - and total inflows = total outflows + net cash flow
- **Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**
  - so opening balance = closing balance − net cash flow
  - and the next month's opening balance = this month's closing balance

Brackets show negatives: (2,000) means −2,000.

## 4. Cash versus profit

| | Cash | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Money actually received and paid | Revenue earned minus costs incurred |
| Timing | When the money moves | When the sale is made and the cost belongs |
| Includes loans and owner's capital? | Yes, as inflows | No |
| Includes the full price of a long-lasting asset when bought? | Yes, as an outflow | No, the cost is spread over its life |

Reasons they differ: credit sales not yet paid; supplier credit; loans received or repaid; buying equipment; holding unsold inventory.

**Consequence:** a profitable business can still become insolvent, and a loss-making business can have cash for a while (for example after a loan or selling an asset).

## 5. Worked reminders

**Two-month forecast.** Opening balance 1,200. Month 1: inflows 5,600, outflows 6,900, so net = (1,300) and closing = 1,200 − 1,300 = **(100)**. Month 2: inflows 7,200, outflows 5,100, so net = 2,100 and closing = −100 + 2,100 = **2,000**. The business needs a small overdraft for Month 1 only.

**Credit sales.** A shop receives 70% of sales in the month of sale and 30% the next month. Sales are 8,000 in Month 1 and 9,000 in Month 2. Receipts in Month 2 = 0.7 × 9,000 + 0.3 × 8,000 = 6,300 + 2,400 = **8,700**.

## 6. Improving a forecast cash position

| Problem shown | Matching response | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Short gap of one or two months | Overdraft | Interest; bank can ask for repayment |
| Large one-off purchase | Delay it, or fund it with a loan | Delay may cost sales; loan adds repayments |
| Customers pay slowly | Shorter credit terms or a discount for early payment | Customers may go elsewhere; discount cuts revenue |
| Supplier bills come early | Ask for longer credit (trade payables) | Supplier may refuse or lose goodwill |
| Persistent negative net cash flow | Cut costs or raise sales | Harder and slower; may affect quality |

## 7. Exam traps

- Adding the opening balance into total inflows.
- Treating forecast sales as cash when customers pay later.
- Copying the wrong closing balance into the next opening balance.
- Calling a loan received "profit".
- Recommending a long-term loan for a one-month problem.
- Saying a forecast is "accurate". It is only as reliable as its assumptions.
- Losing a minus sign when finding a missing outflow from a negative net cash flow.

## 8. Answer frames

- **Explain** why cash matters: statement (cash pays suppliers) → expansion (without it supplies stop) → expansion (production halts and orders are lost).
- **Analyse** a forecast: name the month → state the cause from the table → explain the consequence → suggest a matched response.
- **Evaluate** a response: benefit in figures → drawback in context → judgement with the condition that would change it.

## Quick self-test

1. State two reasons why a business needs cash.
2. Define insolvency.
3. Inflows are 12,400 and outflows 13,100. Calculate net cash flow.
4. Opening balance is 2,750 and net cash flow is (900). Calculate the closing balance.
5. Closing balance is 4,200 and net cash flow is 1,700. Calculate the opening balance.
6. Net cash flow is (600) and inflows are 7,400. Calculate outflows.
7. Give two reasons why profit and cash can differ.
8. Customers pay one month after sale. Sales are 5,000 in May and 6,500 in June. What are receipts in June?
9. Is a bank loan received an inflow, an outflow or neither?
10. Give one limitation of a cash-flow forecast.
11. Suggest one way to deal with a negative closing balance caused by a one-off equipment purchase.
12. Why can a fast-growing business run short of cash?

### Answers

1. Any two of: to pay suppliers, to pay overheads, to pay employees, to prevent insolvency.
2. Being unable to pay debts as they fall due.
3. 12,400 − 13,100 = (700).
4. 2,750 − 900 = 1,850.
5. 4,200 − 1,700 = 2,500.
6. 7,400 − (−600) = 8,000.
7. Any two of: credit sales not yet paid; supplier credit; loans received or repaid; spending on long-lasting assets; unsold inventory.
8. 5,000 (May's sales, paid in June).
9. An inflow.
10. It is based on predictions of sales and costs, which may be wrong; unexpected events are not included.
11. Delay the purchase to a month with a surplus, or arrange a short-term overdraft or a loan for the equipment.
12. It pays for extra materials and wages before customers pay for the extra sales, so outflows rise before inflows.
