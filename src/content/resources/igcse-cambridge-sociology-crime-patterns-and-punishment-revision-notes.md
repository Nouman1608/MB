These notes cover key questions 6.2 and 6.3 from the Crime, deviance and social control topic of
Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) Paper 2. Recorded crime and victimisation vary by age, social class,
gender and ethnicity; sociologists explain these patterns through factors such as labelling,
deprivation, status frustration and socialisation, and through five theoretical perspectives; and
societies deal with crime through punishments and policing strategies whose effectiveness is
debated.

For the whole Paper 2, see the [family, education and crime study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-family-education-crime/).

## Syllabus coverage

0495 syllabus 2025–2027:

- **6.2 What are the patterns of and explanations for crime and deviance?** — 6.2.1 patterns of
  crime and victimisation by age, social class, gender, ethnicity; 6.2.2–6.2.5 explanations based on
  age, ethnicity, gender and social class; 6.2.6 theories (postmodernism; interactionism, including
  labelling and moral panic theory; functionalism, including the New Right, subculturalism and
  strain theory; Marxism; feminism); 6.2.7 strengths and limitations.
- **6.3 How is crime dealt with in different societies?** — 6.3.1 aims of punishment; 6.3.2
  effectiveness of types of punishment; 6.3.3 effectiveness of policing and law enforcement.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Victimisation** | Experience of being a victim of crime |
| **Status frustration** | Frustration at failing to gain status by legitimate means (A. Cohen, 1955) |
| **Relative deprivation** | Feeling deprived compared with others |
| **Social exclusion** | Being shut out from mainstream society and opportunities |
| **Chivalry thesis** | Idea that the justice system treats women more leniently (Pollak, 1950) |
| **Institutional racism** | Racism built into the practices of organisations (Macpherson Report, 1999) |
| **Self-fulfilling prophecy** | A label leads people to act as predicted |
| **Moral panic** | Exaggerated public reaction to a group seen as a threat (S. Cohen, 1972) |
| **Strain** | Gap between cultural goals and legitimate means (Merton, 1938) |
| **Deterrence** | Punishing to discourage future crime |
| **Retribution** | Punishment as deserved payback |
| **Incapacitation** | Removing the ability to offend, e.g. prison |
| **Rehabilitation** | Changing the offender so they no longer offend |

## 6.2.1 Patterns (as shown in official statistics)

- **Age:** recorded offending peaks in the teenage years and early twenties; young people are also
  frequently victims.
- **Class:** working-class people are over-represented in recorded crime and prisons; middle-class
  white-collar crime is under-recorded.
- **Gender:** men commit most recorded crime, especially violent crime; women's recorded crime is
  lower and more often property offences.
- **Ethnicity:** in some societies certain minority groups are over-represented in stop and search
  and prison figures — sociologists debate whether this reflects offending or policing.

## Explanations by social characteristic

| Group | Explanations named in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| **Age** (6.2.2) | Police targeting / stop and search; self-fulfilling prophecy; stereotyping; gang culture; socialisation and social control; lack of opportunity; status frustration |
| **Ethnicity** (6.2.3) | Institutional racism; prejudice and discrimination; police targeting; material and relative deprivation; stereotyping; gang culture; status frustration |
| **Gender** (6.2.4) | Chivalry thesis; culture of masculinity; socialisation and social control (Heidensohn, 1985: women more controlled); misogyny; stereotyping; gang culture; lack of opportunity |
| **Social class** (6.2.5) | Social exclusion; material and relative deprivation; inadequate socialisation; lack of opportunity; status frustration; power inequalities; police targeting |

## 6.2.6 Theories

| Theory | Explanation of crime | Example sociologist |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionalism** | Some crime is functional (boundary-setting); too much shows anomie | Durkheim |
| — strain theory | Goals–means gap leads to innovation (crime) | Merton (1938) |
| — subculturalism | Working-class boys form delinquent subcultures | A. Cohen (1955) |
| — New Right | Underclass with inadequate socialisation | Murray (1984) |
| **Interactionism** | Deviance is a label; labelling can create a deviant career; moral panics | Becker (1963); S. Cohen (1972) |
| **Marxism** | Laws and policing serve the ruling class; crimes of the powerful ignored | — |
| **Feminism** | Patriarchal control explains low female crime; women as victims | Heidensohn (1985); Carlen (1988) |
| **Postmodernism** | Crime is diverse and individualised; consumer culture creates new desires and crimes | — |

## 6.2.7 Strengths and limitations (quick evaluation)

- Statistics-based explanations may mistake **policing patterns** for **offending patterns**.
- Strain and subcultural theories explain working-class crime but not white-collar crime.
- Labelling shows how agencies create crime but neglects victims and real harm.
- Marxism highlights power but can overlook working-class victims.
- Feminism explains gender differences but may generalise about all women.

## 6.3 Dealing with crime

**Aims of punishment:** protection of citizens, deterrence, retribution, incapacitation,
rehabilitation.

| Punishment | Main aim | Effectiveness debate |
|---|---|---|
| Community sentencing | Rehabilitation, reparation | Cheaper; may be seen as soft |
| Financial penalties | Deterrence | Unequal impact on rich and poor |
| Prison | Incapacitation, retribution | High reoffending; "schools of crime" |
| Rehabilitation programmes | Rehabilitation | Can reduce reoffending if funded |
| Capital punishment | Retribution, deterrence | Irreversible; limited evidence of deterrence |
| Corporal punishment | Deterrence, retribution | Seen as a human rights violation in many states |
| Exile / ostracism | Protection, shaming | Removes rather than reforms |
| Non-custodial (tagging, curfews, restraining orders), probation, cautions | Control in the community | Depend on monitoring and compliance |
| Social media and vigilante groups | Informal shaming | Risk of wrongful targeting and harm |

**Policing:** targeting and stop and search (may detect crime but can damage trust and reflect
bias); surveillance including digital surveillance (deterrence, privacy concerns); crime prevention;
community policing (trust and local knowledge) versus military-style policing (force; risk of
alienating communities). "Broken windows" (Wilson and Kelling, 1982) underpinned zero-tolerance
approaches.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Treating official statistics as the true picture of who commits crime.
- Confusing Albert Cohen (status frustration, 1955) with Stanley Cohen (moral panics, 1972).
- Listing punishments without assessing **effectiveness**, which is what 6.3.2 asks for.
- Forgetting victimisation in 6.2.1.
- Using only one theory in an evaluation answer.

## Quick self-test

1. Give two explanations for higher recorded crime among young people.
2. What is the chivalry thesis?
3. Explain Merton's idea of innovation.
4. Name the five aims of punishment.
5. Give one strength and one limitation of community policing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are men more criminal than women?** Explanations include a culture of masculinity, greater
social control of women (Heidensohn) and more opportunity; the chivalry thesis suggests part of the
gap is lenient treatment of women.

**Is prison effective?** It protects the public while offenders are inside, but high reoffending
rates suggest it often fails to rehabilitate.

**Which theory explains white-collar crime best?** Marxism, because it focuses on crimes of the
powerful and why they are rarely punished.
