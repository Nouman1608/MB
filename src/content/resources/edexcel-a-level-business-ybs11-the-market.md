This guide covers section **1.3.2 The market** in Unit 1 (Marketing and people) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business, Issue 1. It is the most quantitative part of the unit: you must explain what shifts demand and supply, draw and interpret diagrams, and calculate and apply price and income elasticity of demand. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the whole unit, see the [Marketing and People study guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-business-marketing-and-people/). Condensed notes are in [The Market revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-the-market-revision-notes/) and questions in [The Market practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-the-market-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Demand:** factors leading to a change in demand: prices of substitutes and complementary goods, consumer incomes, fashions, tastes and preferences, marketing, advertising and branding, demographics, external shocks and seasonality.
2. **Supply:** factors leading to a change in supply: costs of production, new technology, indirect taxes, government subsidies and external shocks.
3. **Markets:** the interaction of demand and supply, and drawing and interpreting diagrams that show the causes and consequences of changes.
4. **PED:** calculation, interpretation of values, influencing factors, significance for pricing, and the relationship with total revenue.
5. **YED:** calculation, normal and inferior goods, interpretation of values, influencing factors and significance to businesses.

## Movement along a curve or a shift?

This distinction underpins every diagram question. A change in the product's **own price** causes a movement **along** the demand curve (a change in quantity demanded) and along the supply curve. A change in **any other factor** shifts the whole curve. If you write "demand rises because the price fell", you have described a movement, not a shift, and the examiner will expect the word "extension".

## Shifts in practice: telling the story of a market

Strong answers explain the mechanism, not just the direction.

- **Substitutes and complements.** If the price of coffee pods rises, demand for loose ground coffee (a substitute) shifts right. If the price of pod machines falls, demand for pods (a complement) shifts right, because more households own the machine the pods need.
- **Demographics.** An ageing population shifts demand right for mobility aids and left for products bought mainly by young adults, over years rather than months.
- **External shocks.** A sudden event, such as a storm closing a port or a health scare, can shift demand or supply abruptly. Shocks are often temporary, so a firm must judge whether to change prices or wait.
- **Seasonality.** Demand for ice cream, school uniforms or heating oil shifts predictably through the year; businesses plan staffing and inventory around it.
- **Indirect taxes and subsidies.** A tax on each unit raises firms' costs, so supply shifts left (upwards by the amount of the tax). A subsidy per unit lowers costs, so supply shifts right.

When both curves shift together, the effect on one variable is clear and the other depends on the relative size of the shifts. For example, if demand rises and new technology raises supply at the same time, quantity certainly rises, but price may rise, fall or stay the same.

## Worked example 1: an indirect tax

Fenwick Juices sells in a local market where demand is Qd = 900 − 30P and supply is Qs = 100 + 50P (P in $, Q in cartons a day).

```
Before the tax:   900 - 30P = 100 + 50P
                  800 = 80P   ->  P = $10.00,  Q = 600

Tax of $1.60 a carton: firms need $1.60 more at every output,
so supply becomes Qs = 100 + 50(P - 1.60) = 20 + 50P

After the tax:    900 - 30P = 20 + 50P
                  880 = 80P   ->  P = $11.00,  Q = 570
```

Price rises by $1.00, which is **less than the $1.60 tax**, because some customers buy less as the price rises, so firms cannot pass on the whole tax. Each firm now keeps $11.00 − $1.60 = $9.40 a carton instead of $10.00, and sells fewer cartons. On a diagram you would show the supply curve shifting left (upwards), a new equilibrium at a higher price and lower quantity, and label both equilibria.

## Drawing a demand and supply diagram

The specification includes the command word **construct**, which means drawing an accurately labelled diagram. A complete diagram has: axes labelled "Price" and "Quantity"; a downward-sloping demand curve labelled D and an upward-sloping supply curve labelled S; the original equilibrium marked with dotted lines to both axes (P1, Q1); the shifted curve labelled D1 or S1 with an arrow showing direction; and the new equilibrium (P2, Q2). Marks are lost for unlabelled axes or an arrow that points the wrong way.

## Price elasticity of demand: beyond the formula

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price
```

Interpreting the number:

| Value (ignore the minus sign) | Description | What a price rise does to total revenue |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Rises in proportion to price |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Rises |
| 1 | Unitary | Stays the same |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Falls |

**Factors influencing PED:** the number and closeness of substitutes; whether the product is a necessity; the proportion of income it takes; brand loyalty and habit; and time, since customers find alternatives given longer.

**Significance for pricing.** PED tells a business what will happen to revenue, but a pricing decision is about profit. If demand is inelastic, a price rise raises revenue **and** lowers costs, because fewer units are made, so profit rises on both counts. If demand is elastic, a price cut raises revenue, but extra units bring extra variable costs, so profit may not rise.

A price rise raises revenue when demand is perfectly inelastic or inelastic, leaves it unchanged at 1 and reduces it when demand is elastic. ## Worked example 2: choosing between price rises

Brockley Gyms charges $40 a month and has 10,000 members, so revenue is $400,000 a month. It is considering a 10% rise to $44. Research gives two estimates of PED.

```
If PED = -0.4:  members fall 4%   -> 9,600 x $44 = $422,400  (revenue +$22,400)
If PED = -1.8:  members fall 18%  -> 8,200 x $44 = $360,800  (revenue -$39,200)
```

The decision depends entirely on which estimate is reliable. A gym with long contracts and few rivals nearby is likely to be closer to −0.4; one surrounded by budget gyms is likely to be closer to −1.8. That reasoning, using the business's context to judge the estimate, is what earns evaluation marks.

## Income elasticity of demand

```
YED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income
```

Here **the sign matters**. A positive YED shows a **normal good**; above +1 it is income elastic, often a luxury, and between 0 and +1 it is income inelastic, typical of necessities. A negative YED shows an **inferior good**, bought less as incomes rise because customers trade up.

**Factors influencing YED:** whether the product is a necessity or a luxury; the income level of the customers (a car is a luxury in a low-income market and closer to a necessity in a high-income one); and the existence of better alternatives that customers switch to as they become richer.

**Worked example 3.** Average incomes rise by 5%. Demand for Brockley's premium personal-training package rises by 9%, giving YED = 9 ÷ 5 = **+1.8**. Demand for its off-peak budget membership falls by 2%, giving YED = −2 ÷ 5 = **−0.4**. In a period of rising incomes Brockley should expect growth in personal training and plan staff for it; in a recession the pattern reverses, and the budget membership protects revenue.

**Significance.** YED helps businesses forecast sales as the economy changes, plan capacity and staff, and balance a product portfolio so that not every product depends on rising incomes.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a price change a "shift" in demand.
- Showing a tax as a shift of the demand curve rather than supply.
- Dropping the sign of YED, which hides whether a good is inferior.
- Concluding on revenue when the question asks about profit.
- Treating a PED estimate as certain; it comes from research or past data and may not hold for a larger change or a later year.
- Calculating percentage changes from the new figure instead of the original.

## Exam technique

**Calculate** questions (4 marks) reward the formula, the substitution and the answer, so show every step. **Analyse** questions on demand and supply need a chain: the factor, the shift, the new price and quantity, and the consequence for the named business. **Assess** questions about pricing should use PED to predict revenue, then question the estimate and consider costs, rivals and brand before judging. Use figures from the source in every paragraph.
