Condensed recall for **3.2.2 Section B: Resource security** (OxfordAQA International AS and A-level
Geography 9635, Unit 2). There is no separate study guide for this section yet, so read it alongside
the [9635 subject guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-subject-guide/) and the
[Global Systems and Governance study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-geography-global-systems-and-governance/),
whose flows and power relations recur here. These notes work from the key concepts; check the exact
content list against the specification.

The specification's sub-headings are **resource development, natural resource issues, water
security, energy security, resource futures** and two **case studies**. It centres on **water and
energy**; you may concentrate on one of them in your case studies.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resource security | Reliable access to enough of a resource (such as water, energy or minerals) at an affordable price and acceptable quality for a population or country |
| Renewable | Replenished naturally on human timescales (solar, wind, water) |
| Non-renewable | Finite on those timescales (fossil fuels, minerals) |
| Energy mix | The combination of energy sources a country uses |
| Sustainable management | Using resources so supplies last for future generations |
| Stock resource | A finite store that is used up as it is exploited (coal, oil, gas, uranium, fossil groundwater) |
| Flow resource | A resource renewed continuously by natural flows (sunlight, wind, river flow, tides) |
| Water stress | Demand for water approaches or exceeds the renewable supply available |
| Primary / secondary energy | Energy in its natural form (coal, crude oil, wind) / energy converted into a usable carrier (electricity, petrol) |
| Virtual water | The water used to produce a good, traded invisibly when the good is traded |

## Resource development

**Stock resource evaluation** -- from most to least certain:

1. **Measured reserves** -- quantity known with confidence and economic to extract.
2. **Indicated reserves** -- estimated from more widely spaced sampling.
3. **Inferred resources** -- suggested by limited evidence, quantity uncertain.
4. **Possible resources** -- may exist from geological reasoning but are unproven.

Reserves are not fixed: higher prices and new technology turn resources into reserves.

**Development over time:** exploration → exploitation → development. The **resource frontier** is the
edge of current exploitation, pushed into new places (deep water, the Arctic, oil sands) as
technology and prices allow. **Resource peak** is the point at which output of a finite resource
reaches its maximum before declining, as in the idea of "peak oil".

**Environmental impact assessment (EIA)** evaluates the likely effects of a project -- on
ecosystems, water, air, communities -- before approval, and proposes mitigation.

## Why supply and demand differ between places

Resources are **unevenly distributed physically**, while demand varies with **population, income and
development**. This mismatch drives trade, dependence and geopolitical tension.

**Global patterns:** much oil and gas is produced in the Middle East, Russia and North America, while
large consumers include China, the USA, the EU, India and Japan, so energy moves through pipelines and
shipping routes. Water availability is high in humid tropical and temperate regions and low in arid
regions such as the Middle East and North Africa, where demand from irrigation and growing cities
causes stress.

## Water, energy, minerals

- **Water:** *physical* scarcity means not enough water to meet demand; *economic* scarcity means
  water exists but people lack the investment or infrastructure to use it.
- **Energy:** a diverse **energy mix** reduces dependence on one fuel or supplier and so improves
  energy security.
- **Minerals:** finite and unevenly distributed, so supply can depend on a few producers.

## Water security

- **Sources:** surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers), desalinated water
  and recycled water.
- **Components of demand:** agriculture (usually the largest user worldwide, mainly irrigation),
  industry and domestic use.
- **Physical controls on supply:** **climate** (rainfall amount and reliability, evaporation),
  **geology** (permeable rocks store groundwater; impermeable rocks favour surface reservoirs) and
  **drainage** (river networks and basins determine where water collects).

**Increasing supply:** catchment protection, river **diversion**, **storage** in reservoirs, **water
transfers** between basins and **desalination**.

**Example of a major scheme -- the Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze, China:** the world's largest power
station by installed capacity and a major flood-control and storage scheme. Environmental impacts:
flooding of the valley and displacement of over a million people, trapped sediment, changes to river
ecology and fisheries, and slope instability around the reservoir.

**Managing consumption:** metering and pricing, efficient irrigation (drip systems), fixing leaks,
water-saving appliances, public campaigns.

**Sustainability issues:** **virtual water trade** (water-scarce countries import water-intensive
food instead of growing it), conservation, recycling, **grey water** reuse (household wastewater from
sinks and showers reused for toilets or gardens), and **groundwater management** to stop aquifers
being drawn down faster than they recharge.

**Water conflict at different scales:** local (farmers versus cities over irrigation water),
national (upstream and downstream states within a country) and international -- for example the
**Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam** on the Blue Nile, which has caused long-running tension between
Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt over how quickly the reservoir is filled.

## Energy security

- **Energy mixes differ:** France relies heavily on nuclear power for electricity; Norway on
  hydro-electric power; many Gulf states on oil and gas; China has depended largely on coal while
  expanding renewables rapidly.
- **Physical controls:** **geology** decides where fossil fuels and uranium occur; **climate**
  affects solar and wind potential and heating demand; **drainage** and relief decide hydro-electric
  potential.
- **Globalising world:** competing national interests and **transnational corporations** (oil majors
  and state energy companies) control production, refining and distribution. Supply can be used as
  political leverage: Russia's cuts to gas supplies to Europe after its 2022 invasion of Ukraine
  forced European states to find new suppliers, including liquefied natural gas by ship.
- **Environmental impacts of a major development:** oil, coal or gas fields cause land disturbance,
  habitat loss, water pollution and spills along pipelines and shipping routes -- the **Deepwater
  Horizon** blowout in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010 is a well-documented example.
- **Increasing supply:** oil and gas exploration, nuclear power, renewable sources.
- **Managing consumption:** insulation, efficient appliances and vehicles, public transport,
  pricing and taxes.
- **Sustainability issues:** acid rain, the enhanced greenhouse effect, nuclear waste, energy
  conservation.

## Insecurity and conflict

Competition for scarce or shared resources, dependence on imports and control by a few producers can
create tension between and within states. Evaluate with **named examples**; do not assume scarcity
always causes conflict, since cooperation and trade can also follow.

## Managing resources sustainably

- **Efficiency**, **recycling**, **substitution** and **demand management**, alongside increased
  supply.
- **Trade-offs:** extraction and supply bring economic benefits and jobs, but may carry environmental
  damage and social costs. Weigh costs against benefits and reach a judgement.

## Resource futures and the water-energy link

Alternative futures depend on **technology** (cheaper renewables, storage, efficient desalination),
**economics** (prices, investment), **environment** (climate change altering rainfall and demand)
and **politics** (agreements, conflict). **Desalination** shows the link between energy and water:
it supplies water in arid coastal states such as Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Israel, but needs large
amounts of energy, so its cost and carbon footprint depend on the energy source -- which is why
solar-powered desalination is being developed.

## Answer structure

Claim → named example → consequence → balance (for whom, in the short and long term). Show both
outcomes where the evidence allows.

## Exam traps

- Treating "renewable" as "unlimited" or "always sustainable".
- Confusing physical scarcity with economic scarcity.
- Assuming resource-rich countries are always secure.
- Generic points about "energy" with no named place.
- Describing costs and benefits without a final judgement.
- Treating reserves as fixed: they change with price and technology.

## Self-test

1. Define resource security.
2. Distinguish renewable from non-renewable resources.
3. What is the difference between physical and economic water scarcity?
4. How does a diverse energy mix improve energy security?
5. Name two ways to manage resources sustainably.
6. Distinguish a stock resource from a flow resource.
7. Why can measured reserves increase without any new discovery?
8. What is the resource frontier?
9. What is virtual water, and how can trade in it help a water-scarce country?
10. Give two environmental impacts of a major dam scheme.
11. Why is desalination an example of the link between energy and water security?
12. Give one example of a resource being used as political leverage.

**Answers:** 1. Reliable access to enough of a resource at an affordable price and acceptable
quality. 2. Renewable resources are replenished on human timescales; non-renewable are finite on
those timescales. 3. Physical: not enough water; economic: water exists but cannot be used for lack
of investment or infrastructure. 4. It reduces dependence on one fuel or supplier. 5. For example
efficiency and recycling. 6. A stock resource is finite and used up; a flow resource is renewed
continuously by natural flows. 7. Higher prices or new technology make known resources economic to
extract. 8. The edge of current exploitation, extended into new areas as technology and prices
allow. 9. The water used to produce goods; importing water-intensive food saves domestic water. 10.
For example flooding of land and displacement of people, and trapped sediment. 11. It provides water
but needs large amounts of energy, so its cost depends on energy supply. 12. For example Russia
