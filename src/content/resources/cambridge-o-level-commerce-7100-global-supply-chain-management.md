A **supply chain** is the whole sequence of businesses and activities that move a product from the supplier of the raw materials to the final consumer. A **global supply chain** is one that crosses national borders: the cotton may be grown in one country, the cloth woven in a second, the shirt sewn in a third and sold in a fourth. Sub-topic 3.2 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) has two parts: **global supply chains** (3.2.1) and **global supply chain risks** (3.2.2).

## 3.2.1 Global supply chains

### Types of global supply chain

| Type | Links | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** supply chain | Supplier → manufacturer → consumer | Few links; the manufacturer may sell directly to the consumer, for example online |
| **Complex** supply chain | Supplier → agent → wholesaler → retailer → consumer | Many links; used when goods must reach many customers in many places |

In a complex supply chain the agent arranges deals between the supplier and the wholesaler, the wholesaler buys in bulk and sells in smaller amounts, and the retailer sells to consumers. Each extra link adds a cost, so the final price is higher, but each link also does a job, such as breaking bulk or finding customers.

### Features of global supply chain management

Supply chain management is the planning and control of all the activities in the chain so that goods arrive at the right place, at the right time, in the right condition, at the right cost. The syllabus describes three parts.

| Part | Meaning | Example for a mobile phone company |
|---|---|---|
| **Upstream** | The suppliers and activities before the business: sourcing raw materials and components | Mines supplying metals; factories making screens and batteries |
| **The enterprise** | The business itself, which turns inputs into the finished product | The company that designs, assembles and tests the phones |
| **Downstream** | The activities after the business: distribution, wholesaling, retailing and customer service | Warehouses, shops, online delivery and repairs |

### Importance of suppliers

A business depends on its suppliers because they provide the materials, parts and services it needs. Good suppliers:

- deliver on time, so production does not stop;
- supply goods of the right quality, so fewer products are faulty;
- offer fair prices and credit terms, which help the business's costs and cash flow;
- are reliable and keep to agreements;
- can supply more when demand rises.

If a key supplier fails, the whole chain can stop. For this reason a firm may use more than one supplier.

### Benefits of global supply chain management

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Lower cost of production | Materials and parts can be bought from where they are cheapest |
| Lower labour costs | Making goods in countries with lower wages reduces costs |
| Lower cost of purchasing | Buying in large amounts from many suppliers may allow bulk discounts |
| Advancement in logistics | Better transport, containerisation and tracking systems make long journeys faster and safer |
| Advancement in communication | The internet, email and video calls allow quick contact and ordering |

### Worked example: comparing the cost of supply

A company needs 10,000 units of a part each year. Home production costs $8.50 per unit. An overseas supplier charges $6.20 per unit, and shipping and customs add $0.90 per unit.

1. Overseas cost per unit = 6.20 + 0.90 = $7.10.
2. Saving per unit = 8.50 − 7.10 = $1.40.
3. Saving per year = 1.40 × 10,000 = $14,000.

The overseas supplier is cheaper on these figures. The business must still weigh the saving against risks such as delay and supplier failure, which is the subject of 3.2.2.

## 3.2.2 Global supply chain risks

A global supply chain is longer and has more links than a local one, so there are more things that can go wrong.

| Risk | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Environmental** | Harm to the environment caused by the supply chain, or environmental problems that disrupt it | Pollution from a factory; deforestation to clear land for farming; a company's reputation suffers |
| **Natural disasters** | Events such as floods, earthquakes and storms | A flood closes a port or destroys a factory, so deliveries stop |
| **Financial** | Money problems | A supplier goes bankrupt; exchange rates move; payment is not made |
| **Ethical** | Suppliers behave in ways that are unfair or harmful | Child labour; unsafe working conditions; very low pay |
| **Technological** | Failure or misuse of technology | A computer system fails; hackers attack the order system |
| **Political** | Decisions or events in government | War; new tariffs or embargoes; unstable governments; changes in law |

### Effects of risks on a business

- Production stops because parts do not arrive.
- Costs rise because goods must be bought from other suppliers or sent by faster, dearer transport.
- Customers are let down by late deliveries, and the business loses sales and reputation.
- The business may be criticised in the media for unethical suppliers.

### The importance of risk assessment

**Risk assessment** is identifying the risks, judging how likely each one is and how serious its effect would be, and deciding what to do about it. It is important because:

- it helps a business prepare **before** a problem occurs, instead of reacting afterwards;
- it lets the business decide where to spend money on protection;
- it protects the business's reputation and its customers;
- it can lower costs by avoiding disruption.

Actions that follow from a risk assessment include using more than one supplier, holding extra stock, buying insurance, choosing suppliers in stable countries, checking suppliers' working conditions and keeping back-up computer systems.

### Worked example: a simple risk table

*Invented example.* A furniture company imports wood from an overseas supplier.

| Risk | Likelihood | Effect | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floods close the port (natural disaster) | Low | High: no wood for weeks | Hold extra stock; use a second supplier |
| Supplier cuts down forests illegally (environmental, ethical) | Medium | High: harms reputation | Buy only from certified suppliers |
| New tariff on wood (political) | Medium | Medium: costs rise | Look for a supplier in a trading bloc partner country |
| Exchange rate moves (financial) | High | Medium | Agree fixed prices in advance |

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain **two** benefits to a business of using a global supply chain. [4]

> One benefit is lower labour costs [1]. The business can make goods in a country where wages are lower, so each unit costs less to produce [1]. A second benefit is advancement in logistics [1]. Improved transport and tracking mean goods can be moved long distances quickly and arrive safely, so the business can supply customers anywhere [1].

**Why it scores 4:** each benefit is stated and then developed with the effect on the business. A list of benefits with no explanation would earn only half the marks.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the retailer in a simple supply chain. The simple chain is supplier, manufacturer, consumer.
- Muddling upstream and downstream. Upstream comes before the business, downstream after it.
- Naming a risk without saying how it affects the business.
- Describing risk assessment as "solving the problem". It is identifying and judging risks beforehand.
- Forgetting that lower costs must be weighed against risks and transport costs.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a chain by listing the links in order.
- **Explain** a benefit or risk with a reason and effect.
- **Match** risks to the six types in the syllabus, using their names.
- **Calculate** per-unit cost first, then multiply for the total.
