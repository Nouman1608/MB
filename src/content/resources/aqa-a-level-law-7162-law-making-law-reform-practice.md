## Section A

**1.** State the year in which the Law Commission was established and the Act that created it. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the composition of the Law Commission. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the Act known as the Sentencing Code, and the function of the Law Commission it illustrates. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by repeal in the work of the Law Commission. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Outline the stages of a typical Law Commission project, from selection of the work to the final report. **[5]**

**6.** Explain the difference between codification and consolidation, with an example of each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** examples of Acts of Parliament resulting from Law Commission reform reports. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** measures introduced since 2009 to improve the implementation of Law Commission reports. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the Law Commission is better placed than judges to reform a whole area of law. **[4]**

**10.** A government minister says: "The Law Commission's report on offences against the person is excellent, but there is no room in this year's parliamentary timetable." Explain what this illustrates about reform through the Law Commission, referring to evidence. **[5]**

**11.** Explain **two** advantages of the Law Commission's independence and expertise. **[4]**

**12.** "Reform through the Law Commission has been largely unsuccessful." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** 1965 [1]; the Law Commissions Act 1965 [1].

**2.** A chair who is a High Court or Court of Appeal judge [1], and four other commissioners drawn from judges, practising lawyers and legal academics, supported by research lawyers and draftsmen [1].

**3.** The Sentencing Act 2020 [1]; consolidation [1].

**4.** Removing obsolete and unnecessary laws from the statute book, usually through Statute Law (Repeals) Bills [1].

**5.** The project is selected from a programme of law reform approved by the Lord Chancellor, or referred by a government department [1]. Research lawyers study the existing law and its problems [1]. A consultation paper sets out provisional proposals and invites responses [1]. The Commission considers the responses [1] and publishes a final report with recommendations, usually including a draft Bill [1].

**6.** Codification brings all the law on a topic, both statute and case law, into a single code [1], for example the draft Criminal Code of 1989, which was never enacted [1]. Consolidation combines several Acts on one subject into one Act without changing the substance of the law [1], for example the Sentencing Act 2020 [1].

**7.** Any two, two marks each: the Fraud Act 2006 replaced a number of complex deception offences with a general offence of fraud [1] [1]; the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 allowed a third party to enforce a contract term made for their benefit [1] [1]. (Also accept the Computer Misuse Act 1990, the Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023, the Automated Vehicles Act 2024, or the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 with explanation.)

**8.** Any two, two marks each: the Law Commission Act 2009 requires the Lord Chancellor to report to Parliament each year on implementation [1], which puts public pressure on government to act [1]; the 2010 protocol requires a department to support a project before it starts and ministers to respond to the report [1], reducing the risk that completed work is ignored [1]. (Also accept the House of Lords special procedure for uncontroversial Commission Bills, saving debating time.)

**9.** Judges can change the law only when a suitable case comes before them [1], and only on the point argued in that case [1]. The Commission can review a whole area at once, consult widely and propose a coherent scheme [1], and its proposals can be enacted prospectively, avoiding the retrospective effect of judicial change [1].

**10.** It illustrates that the Commission has no power to implement its own proposals [1]; reform depends on government accepting a report and finding parliamentary time [1]. Evidence: the Commission's reports on offences against the person in 1993 and 2015 proposed replacing the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 but have not been implemented [1]. Similarly, the draft Criminal Code of 1989 was never enacted [1]. This shows that political priorities, not legal merit, determine whether reform happens [1].

**11.** Independence: the Commission is not controlled by a political party, so it can recommend changes on their legal merits [1] and propose reform in areas that are unattractive to politicians but legally important [1]. Expertise: commissioners and staff are experienced judges, lawyers and academics [1], so reports are thoroughly researched and draft Bills are technically sound [1].

**12.** For the statement: the original aim of codifying major areas has failed, as shown by the draft Criminal Code [1]; major criminal law reports such as those on offences against the person have not been implemented [1]; partial implementation, as in the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, can leave gaps [1]; the Commission depends on government support and parliamentary time [1]. Against: many reports have been implemented, such as the Fraud Act 2006 and the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 [1]; recent technical reforms, such as the Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023, show continuing success [1]; consolidation and repeal have made the statute book clearer, for example through the Sentencing Act 2020 [1]; reforms since 2009 have improved implementation [1]. Conclusion: the Commission has been successful in technical and consolidation work but less so in politically sensitive criminal law reform, so "largely unsuccessful" overstates the case [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing codification with consolidation.
- Naming an Act without explaining what the reform achieved.
- Treating the Commission as able to make law.
- In essays, using only failures or only successes.
