## Poles and forces

Every magnet has two poles, a **north-seeking (N) pole** and a **south-seeking (S) pole**, where its magnetic effect is strongest. Between two magnets:

- **like poles repel** (N and N, or S and S);
- **unlike poles attract** (N and S).

A **magnetic material** is one that is attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. A **non-magnetic material** is not attracted and cannot be magnetised: copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass. Note that aluminium and copper are metals but are non-magnetic; "all metals are magnetic" is a common wrong answer.

An unmagnetised piece of magnetic material is attracted by **either** pole of a magnet. A magnet is attracted to one pole of another magnet and repelled by the other. So:

> **Attraction** happens between a magnet and a magnet (unlike poles) **or** between a magnet and an unmagnetised magnetic material. Only **repulsion** proves that both objects are magnets.

### Annotated model answer: identifying three bars

*Three identical-looking bars, X, Y and Z, are a magnet, an unmagnetised iron bar and a brass bar. Describe how to identify each, using only the bars. [4]*

> "Bring each bar near both ends of each other bar. The bar that is not attracted to either of the others is the **brass** bar, because brass is non-magnetic. [1] The remaining two bars attract each other whichever ends are brought together, so attraction alone cannot tell them apart: a magnet attracts unmagnetised iron at either pole. [1] Now hold bar X so that one end touches the **middle** of bar Y. If they attract strongly, X is the magnet: its pole induces magnetism in the iron. [1] If there is little or no attraction, Y is the magnet, because the middle of a magnet has almost no magnetic effect. [1]"

**Why this scores:** it uses the idea that attraction is not proof correctly, and the end-to-middle test really does separate the magnet from the iron. Repulsion is not available as a test here, because there is only one magnet.

## Induced magnetism

When a piece of unmagnetised magnetic material is placed near (or touching) a magnet, it **becomes a magnet itself**: this is **induced magnetism**. The end nearest the magnet becomes the **opposite** pole, so the two attract. That is why a magnet attracts iron from either pole: an N pole induces an S pole in the near end of the iron, and an S pole induces an N pole.

Induced magnetism explains a chain of iron nails hanging from one pole of a magnet: each nail is magnetised by the one above it and attracts the next.

## Temporary and permanent magnets

| Property | Soft iron | Steel |
|---|---|---|
| How easily it is magnetised | Easily | Less easily |
| What happens when the magnetising field is removed | Loses almost all its magnetism | Keeps its magnetism |
| Type of magnet | **Temporary** | **Permanent** |
| Typical use | Core of an electromagnet, relay or transformer | Compass needle, magnets in loudspeakers and motors |

A clear demonstration: hang a chain of soft-iron nails from one magnet and a chain of steel pins from another. Remove the magnets carefully. The soft-iron chain falls apart at once; the steel pins stay partly stuck together because they remain magnetised.

**Choosing a material** in an exam question comes down to one test: does the magnetism need to **switch off** (soft iron) or **stay** (steel)?

## Magnetic fields

A **magnetic field** is a region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force. The **direction** of the field at a point is the direction of the force on an **N pole** placed there. That is why field lines are drawn leaving N poles and entering S poles, and why a small plotting compass lines up along the field: its own N pole is pushed along the field direction and its S pole the opposite way.

Rules for drawing field lines:

- Start on N and end on S; put an arrow on every line, pointing from N to S outside the magnet.
- Lines never cross (at any point the field has only one direction).
- Lines are closest together where the field is strongest, at the poles.
- Draw them smooth and symmetrical; do not let them touch except at the poles.

**Plotting compass or iron filings?** Iron filings sprinkled on card over a magnet show the whole pattern quickly, but every filing is magnetised by induction and lines up along the field without indicating which way it points, so filings give **no direction**. A plotting compass is slower, but its needle shows the **direction** at each point; mark its tip, move it on, and join the dots.

## Interacting fields (Extended only)

The forces between magnets are due to **interactions between their magnetic fields**. Placing two magnets close together produces a combined field:

- **Unlike poles facing (N to S):** lines run straight across the gap from N to S, closely spaced. The magnets attract.
- **Like poles facing (N to N):** lines from each pole curve away from the gap. Midway between the poles is a **neutral point** where the two fields cancel and a compass would not settle in any direction. The magnets repel.
- **Between the flat, wide poles of a U-shaped (horseshoe) magnet:** lines are parallel, evenly spaced and run from N to S: a **uniform** field, the same strength everywhere in that region.

The **relative strength** of a field is shown by the spacing of the lines: closer lines, stronger field. So the field of a bar magnet is strongest at its poles and gets weaker with distance.

## Uses of permanent magnets and electromagnets

An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire, usually wound on a soft-iron core, that is magnetised when a current flows and loses its magnetism when the current is switched off. Its strength can be changed by changing the current.

| Permanent magnets | Electromagnets |
|---|---|
| Loudspeakers and headphones | Cranes that lift scrap iron and drop it when switched off |
| Electric motors | Relays (a small current switches a larger one) |
| Compass needles | Electric bells and door locks |
| Magnetic catches on doors and fridge seals | Separating iron and steel from other waste |

In an exam, justify the choice: an electromagnet is chosen when the magnetism must be turned on and off or varied; a permanent magnet when a steady field is needed with no power supply.

## Common mistakes

- Using attraction as proof that an object is a magnet.
- Saying all metals are magnetic.
- Drawing field lines that cross, or without arrows, or with arrows from S to N outside the magnet.
- Swapping soft iron and steel in a temporary-or-permanent question.
- Saying iron filings show the direction of the field.
- Describing induced magnetism without saying which pole is induced at the near end.

## Exam technique

- **State:** a short fact, such as "like poles repel".
- **Describe** an experiment: name the apparatus, what you do, what you see and what it shows.
- **Explain** (Extended): bring in the idea that the fields of the two magnets interact, and refer to line spacing for strength.
- **Draw:** at least four lines on each side of a bar magnet, symmetrical, with arrows; use a pencil and keep lines smooth.
- **Suggest** a material or a type of magnet: link your choice to whether the magnetism must be temporary or permanent.
