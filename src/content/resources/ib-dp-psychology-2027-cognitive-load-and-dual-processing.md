Two terms in the cognitive approach list of the 2027 IB Psychology guide need a closer look: **cognitive load theory** and **dual processing theory**. The guide's learning objectives are "the role of cognitive load theory in understanding human cognition" and "the role of the dual processing theory in understanding decision-making". Both are cognitive models, so they can be applied in any context, and both link to the concepts of bias, measurement and change. This guide explains each theory, shows how they connect, and teaches you to evaluate them. The guide names no required studies, so the studies here are illustrations; swap in your own if you prefer.

## Why these two theories sit together

Both theories start from the same fact: the mind has limited capacity for conscious, effortful thought. Cognitive load theory asks what happens to learning when that capacity is overloaded. Dual processing theory asks what the mind does when conscious effort is unavailable or not used: it relies on fast, automatic processing, which is efficient but can produce systematic errors. Putting them together gives a powerful explanation of why people learn badly from badly designed material and why they make biased decisions under pressure.

## 1. Cognitive load theory

**Cognitive load theory (CLT)**, developed by John Sweller, proposes that learning is limited by the capacity of **working memory**. Working memory holds and manipulates a small amount of information for a short time. Classic estimates suggest that people can hold about seven (plus or minus two) items briefly, and recent work suggests about four chunks when rehearsal is blocked. By contrast, **long-term memory** has no known limit and stores knowledge as **schemas** (organised frameworks). When knowledge is stored in a schema, a whole structure can be handled as a single chunk, so experts can process complex material with little load.

### Three kinds of load

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic load | Mental effort set by the complexity of the material and the learner's prior knowledge | Solving an equation with three unknown steps is harder than a one-step equation |
| Extraneous load | Effort caused by how the material is presented, which does not help learning | A diagram and its labels on opposite pages, so the learner has to search |
| Germane load | Effort that goes into building and automating schemas | Comparing worked examples to spot the pattern |

The design aim is to **reduce extraneous load**, **manage intrinsic load** (for example by splitting a task into steps) and **leave capacity for germane load**.

### Key findings and effects

- **Worked-example effect.** Sweller and Cooper (1985) reported that learners who studied worked algebra examples before practising performed better, with less time, than learners who solved all problems themselves. For novices, studying a solved example reduces the load of searching for a method and frees capacity to build a schema.
- **Split-attention effect.** When learners must integrate two sources that are separated (such as a diagram and text), working memory is used to hold and link them. Chandler and Sweller (1991) found that learning improved when text was placed within the diagram. The fix is to integrate sources.
- **Redundancy effect.** Repeating the same information in several forms (a diagram plus a spoken description plus the same text) wastes capacity.
- **Expertise reversal effect.** Support that helps novices (such as worked examples) can become extraneous load for experts, who already hold the schema. Good teaching adjusts as expertise grows.

### Worked example (redesigning a lesson)

A teacher shows a complex map of the water cycle on one slide with a long paragraph of explanation at the bottom, and reads the paragraph aloud. Students struggle. CLT analysis:
1. *Split attention:* students must look from map to paragraph to find which label matches.
2. *Redundancy:* the spoken text duplicates the written paragraph.
3. *Intrinsic load:* all stages of the cycle appear at once.

Redesign: label the stages on the map itself, speak the explanation while leaving out the paragraph, and reveal the stages one at a time. A strong answer says why each change reduces load and what could be measured (test scores, time to learn, ratings of mental effort).

### Measuring cognitive load

Researchers use self-report mental-effort ratings, performance on a **secondary task** (a dual-task method: slower response to a tone suggests heavier primary load), and physiological measures such as pupil dilation. Each is indirect, so cognitive load is a construct that must be **operationalised**, which links to the concept of measurement.

### Evaluating CLT

- **Strengths:** supported by many controlled experiments, practical advice for teaching and interface design, and consistent with what is known about working memory.
- **Limitations:** the three types of load are hard to separate in practice, so the theory can explain any result after the event; self-ratings are subjective; many studies use short lab tasks with students, so results for long-term learning in classrooms may differ.
- **Concepts:** measurement, causality (experiments isolate design effects), change (redesign improves learning), bias (sampling).

## 2. Dual processing theory

**Dual processing theory** proposes that thinking uses two modes. Kahneman popularised the labels System 1 and System 2.

| Feature | System 1 (Type 1) | System 2 (Type 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Effort | Automatic, low effort | Controlled, effortful |
| Awareness | Mostly unconscious | Conscious |
| Basis | Habits, associations, heuristics | Rules, analysis |
| Strength | Efficient in familiar situations | Can check and correct |
| Weakness | Prone to bias | Limited capacity, slow, and can be lazy |

The model explains **cognitive biases**. When System 1 uses a mental shortcut (a **heuristic**) in an unfamiliar or tricky situation, it can produce a systematic error. System 2 can override the answer, but only if it is engaged and has capacity.

### Evidence

- **The bat and ball problem.** A bat and a ball cost 1.10 together, and the bat costs 1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball? The intuitive answer is 10 cents, but the correct answer is 5 cents (the bat is then 1.05, and 0.05 plus 1.05 is 1.10). Frederick (2005) reported that many university students gave the intuitive wrong answer, which suggests they relied on fast thinking. The problem illustrates what happens when System 2 does not check System 1.
- **Anchoring and availability.** Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that judgements are pulled towards an irrelevant starting number (anchoring) and are influenced by how easily examples come to mind (availability). These are examples of System 1 heuristics.
- **Cognitive load and food choice.** Shiv and Fedorikhin (1999) asked participants to remember either a two-digit or a seven-digit number and then offered a choice between fruit salad and chocolate cake. Participants who held the longer number were more likely to choose the cake. The study suggests that when System 2 capacity is used up, impulsive System 1 choices increase.

### Worked example (applying the theory)

A tired driver at the end of a long shift sees a pedestrian step out and brakes instantly, which is System 1 and is helpful. Later, the same driver is asked to calculate how much a fine will cost with a 15 per cent discount and quickly guesses a round figure, which is a System 1 shortcut and is inaccurate. The theory predicts that fatigue and time pressure increase reliance on System 1. A strong answer explains both outcomes and says that System 1 is not simply bad: it is efficient and often accurate.

### Evaluating dual processing

- **Strengths:** a simple model that explains a range of biases, supported by experiments such as the load and food choice study, and widely used in applied settings such as health choices and economics.
- **Limitations:** the two systems are descriptive labels, not separate brain systems; the model is hard to falsify because a result can be explained as System 1 or System 2 after the event; some tasks involve a blend; and findings come from lab tasks with Western student samples.
- **Concepts:** bias (cognitive biases), perspective (cognitive model versus other approaches), causality (load experiments), change (nudges and debiasing strategies).

## 3. Connecting the two theories

| Link | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Load limits System 2 | System 2 depends on working memory, so high load reduces its capacity and increases System 1 errors |
| Schemas make System 1 effective | Experts handle familiar tasks quickly because well-built schemas allow automatic, accurate responses |
| Design can support System 2 | Reducing extraneous load leaves capacity for analysis |
| Both are cognitive models | They explain behaviour by internal information processing, which sociocultural and biological approaches may challenge |

**Application to learning and health.** In learning, reduce extraneous load and give worked examples to novices. In health decisions, a stressed or distracted person is more likely to rely on habit, so making the healthy choice the default can help (a nudge).

## Using the theories in an exam answer

- *Section A:* explain the theory in two or three precise points and give a clear, original example.
- *Section B:* apply the theory to details in the scenario. Say which load type or system is operating, why, and what would change the outcome.
- *Section C:* use the theories as part of an argument about concepts such as bias, change or measurement, and evaluate the evidence.

## Common mistakes

- Treating cognitive load as the same thing as being "tired" or "stressed". It is about working memory demand.
- Saying that System 1 is always wrong or that System 2 is always correct.
- Describing a study without saying what it shows about the theory.
- Forgetting to mention measurement problems for cognitive load.
- Presenting the two systems as separate parts of the brain.
