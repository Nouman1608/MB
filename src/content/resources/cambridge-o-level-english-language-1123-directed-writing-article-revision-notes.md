These notes cover the **article**, one of the five forms the syllabus names for Paper 2 Section A: candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article". The general method is in the [directed writing revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/); the full explanation and a model article are in the [writing an article study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-article/).

## Task facts

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Marks | 25: writing 15 (W1–W5), reading 10 (R3, R5) |
| Length | about 250–350 words |
| Form objectives | W2 organisation for effect; W4 register for the publication's readers |
| Purposes | discursive, argumentative or persuasive |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Headline | the title, designed to inform and attract |
| Subheading | a short line under the headline giving the angle |
| Hook | the opening paragraph that draws readers in |
| Viewpoint | the writer's judgement on the issue |
| Attribution | saying who said something: "the head teacher said..." |
| Topic sentence | the first sentence of a paragraph, moving the argument on |
| Circular ending | an ending that returns to the opening scene or question |
| Publication | where the article appears: school magazine, local newspaper, website |

## Article versus speech versus report

| | Speech | Report | Article |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | greeting | title + purpose | headline + hook |
| Audience | listeners | one decision-maker | readers of a publication |
| Register | spoken | impersonal | set by publication |
| End | call to action | recommendations | memorable line |

## Register by publication

| Publication | Register |
|---|---|
| school magazine | lively, inclusive, standard English |
| local newspaper | clear, measured, more formal |
| club newsletter | friendly, practical |
| website | engaging, concise, accurate |

## Headline techniques

- Question: "Should volunteering be compulsory?"
- Wordplay or alliteration: "Forced to care?"
- Striking claim: "Two hours a week that could change a town"
- Keep it short and clear.

## Hook types

| Hook | When it works |
|---|---|
| Scene | when the sources suggest a real situation or person |
| Question | when the issue is genuinely open |
| Surprising fact | when a source contains a striking figure or change |
| Short quotation | when a source has a memorable phrase |

## Paragraph recipe

**Topic sentence** → **source point** (own words or brief attributed quotation) → **development** (example, consequence, link to readers) → **evaluation** (how convincing, whose interest).

## Mini example: one body paragraph (original)

> The council insists that the new sports hall will be "open to everyone". It is a welcome promise,
> but the opening hours tell a different story: the hall closes at six, just as most people finish
> work. If the council wants the whole town to use it, the doors need to stay open in the evenings.

- **Topic sentence:** introduces the council's claim.
- **Source point:** brief, attributed quotation.
- **Evaluation:** the opening hours contradict the promise.
- **Development:** a practical change, linked to readers' lives.

## Endings

- Return to the opening scene or question.
- One-sentence judgement.
- A challenge to readers.
- Not "In conclusion"; not a repeat of the headline.

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Headline: "My article" | "Forced to care? The debate over compulsory volunteering" |
| "Hello readers, today I will talk about..." | "It is 7.30 on a Saturday morning, and Bilal is already sorting tins." |
| "Text 2 says it turns kindness into homework." | "One student put it bluntly: compulsory volunteering 'turns kindness into homework'." |
| "In conclusion, there are good and bad points." | "You can't force someone to care. But you can give them the chance." |

## Exam traps

- No headline.
- A greeting, as if it were a speech.
- Essay signposts and "In conclusion".
- Long copied quotations.
- No viewpoint.
- Register that does not suit the publication.
- Invented facts that contradict the sources.
- Paragraphs that follow the order of the texts instead of the line of argument.

## Self-test

1. Who is an article written for?
2. What two jobs does a headline do?
3. Name three types of hook.
4. How does the register of a school magazine article differ from that of a local newspaper article?
5. Why should quotations from the sources be short?
6. What is attribution?
7. Why does an article still need a viewpoint if it is balanced?
8. Give two good ways to end an article.
9. Why is "Hello readers" unsuitable?
10. What is a subheading for?
11. Write a headline for an article on whether the town should ban cars from the centre on Sundays.
12. How long should the article be?

**Answers**

1. The readers of a publication, such as a school magazine or local newspaper.
2. It tells readers what the article is about and makes them want to read it.
3. Any three: scene; question; surprising fact; short quotation.
4. A school magazine article can be livelier and more inclusive ("we", "you"); a newspaper article is more measured and formal.
5. Marks come from using and evaluating material in your own words, not from copying it.
6. Saying who made a claim or said something.
7. Readers expect a judgement; a list of points with no conclusion lacks direction.
8. Any two: return to the opening; a one-sentence judgement; a challenge to readers.
9. A greeting belongs to a speech; an article opens with a headline and hook.
10. To add the angle or summarise the issue in a line under the headline.
11. For example: "Car-free Sundays: breath of fresh air or a ghost town?"
12. About 250–350 words.
