## What place is

**Place** is the part of the marketing mix that decides how a product reaches the customer: where it is sold and how it gets there. It covers the channel (who handles it), the outlets (shops, websites, vending machines), and the physical movement and storage of goods. A product that customers cannot find or receive when they want it does not sell, however good it is.

## Channels of distribution

A **channel of distribution** is the route a product takes from producer to final customer.

| Channel | Route | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Producer to consumer | Producer keeps the whole margin and owns the customer relationship; but must handle selling and delivery |
| Via a retailer | Producer, retailer, consumer | Retailer provides outlets, display and local reach; takes a margin |
| Via a wholesaler | Producer, wholesaler, retailer, consumer | Wholesaler buys in bulk and breaks it into smaller loads for retailers; useful for reaching many small shops; takes a further margin |
| Via an agent | Agent sells for commission without owning stock | Useful in export markets |

Longer channels reach more customers but give the producer less of the final price and less control. Shorter channels give more margin and control but need more work and capital.

### Worked example 1: what the producer earns in each channel

Hollis Housewares makes a cast-iron pan. It costs **£12** to make. The final price to the consumer is **£30** in every channel.

**Direct (own website).** Delivery and packing cost £6 per order. The website and marketing cost £25,000 a year. Forecast sales are 5,000 pans.

1. Contribution per pan = £30 − £12 − £6 = **£12**.
2. Total contribution = 5,000 × £12 = £60,000.
3. After website costs: £60,000 − £25,000 = **£35,000**.

**Via retailers.** Hollis sells to retailers at £18. Retailers add £12 (a 40% share of the £30 price: 12 ÷ 30). Forecast sales are 20,000 pans.

1. Contribution per pan = £18 − £12 = **£6**.
2. Total contribution = 20,000 × £6 = **£120,000**.

**Via wholesalers.** Hollis sells to a wholesaler at £15, who sells to retailers at £18 (a 20% mark-up on £15). Forecast sales are 50,000 pans.

1. Contribution per pan = £15 − £12 = **£3**.
2. Total contribution = 50,000 × £3 = **£150,000**.

**Comparison.**

| Channel | Contribution per pan | Volume | Total contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct | £12 | 5,000 | £35,000 (after fixed costs) |
| Retailer | £6 | 20,000 | £120,000 |
| Wholesaler | £3 | 50,000 | £150,000 |

**Interpretation.** The direct channel earns the most per pan (£12) but the least in total, because Hollis cannot reach as many customers. The wholesaler earns the least per pan but the most overall. Choosing a channel therefore depends on the objective: profit and volume point to wholesalers and retailers; brand control and customer data point to direct sales. Many firms use **several channels** (multi-channel) to combine reach and margin. The risk is channel conflict, if retailers resent Hollis selling directly at the same price.

## Logistics: online, digital and physical distribution

**Logistics** is the planning and control of the flow of goods from the producer to the customer. It includes warehousing, stock holding, order handling, packaging, transport and returns.

- **Physical distribution:** moving goods by road, rail, sea or air to warehouses and shops. Costs depend on distance, speed and load size. Retailers require reliable, on-time deliveries.
- **Online distribution:** customers order on a website, and the business must pick, pack and deliver items, often in small orders to many addresses. Delivery cost per item is high and customers expect speed and free returns.
- **Digital distribution:** products delivered electronically (software, music, e-books, streaming) have almost no delivery cost and reach customers worldwide instantly, though piracy and platform fees may be an issue.
- **Click and collect and omnichannel:** customers order online and collect from a store, or switch between channels. This uses existing shops as collection points and can raise store footfall.

Good logistics offers shorter delivery times, fewer stock-outs, lower costs and better customer service. Poor logistics causes late deliveries, damaged goods, lost sales and bad reviews. Evaluating a strategy means comparing cost, speed, reliability and customer expectation. A same-day delivery service may attract customers but raise cost per order so much that small orders make a loss.

## Patterns of distribution

The **pattern of distribution** describes how many outlets sell the product.

| Pattern | Meaning | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensive** | As many outlets as possible | Convenience goods bought often with little effort (snacks, newspapers) |
| **Selective** | A limited number of chosen outlets | Goods where customers compare (clothing, electronics) |
| **Exclusive** | One or very few outlets in an area | Luxury or specialist goods (high-end watches, supercars) |

### Worked example 2: comparing patterns

A producer of premium chocolate considers three patterns. Outlets keep part of the retail price as their margin.

| | Intensive | Selective | Exclusive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outlets | 8,000 | 900 | 60 |
| Average retail sales per outlet | £1,800 | £9,000 | £60,000 |
| Retail sales | 8,000 × £1,800 = **£14.4 million** | 900 × £9,000 = **£8.1 million** | 60 × £60,000 = **£3.6 million** |
| Outlet margin | 25% | 30% | 40% |
| Producer receives | 75% × £14.4m = **£10.8m** | 70% × £8.1m = **£5.67m** | 60% × £3.6m = **£2.16m** |

**Interpretation.** Intensive distribution brings the highest sales and receipts but puts the chocolate beside cheaper brands, which can reduce its premium image and force price promotion. Exclusive distribution protects image and gives the best service, but the producer receives only £2.16 million and has little reach. Selective distribution is a middle course. The right choice depends on the product's image and the price the customer will pay. A premium brand could choose selective distribution to protect its image and keep reach, accepting lower sales than the intensive pattern.

## Importance of place

Place affects sales, because availability and convenience drive purchases, and it affects cost, because distribution is a large part of the price of many products. It shapes the brand image: where a product is sold signals what it is. **Stakeholders:** customers benefit from convenience; retailers and wholesalers depend on stock; employees in warehousing and delivery rely on volumes; suppliers must meet delivery timetables; local communities experience traffic and pollution from distribution.

## Choosing a place mix

To **recommend and justify** a place mix:

1. Start with the target customer: where do they shop, and how fast do they expect delivery?
2. Match the pattern to the image and price (premium goods favour selective or exclusive).
3. Compare channels by contribution and control, as in worked example 1.
4. Check logistics: capacity, cost, returns and technology.
5. Consider the risks: channel conflict, dependence on a single retailer, supply failures.

## Common mistakes

- Equating place with shops only. Online and digital distribution are part of it.
- Saying shorter channels are always more profitable. They earn more per unit but may sell far fewer.
- Describing patterns of distribution without linking them to the product's image.
- Ignoring the cost of delivery and returns in an online strategy.
- Recommending a mix without comparing options.

## Exam technique

Define the term, apply it to the product, use numbers where given, and judge the options against the objective. Always say why the pattern or channel fits the image and customer.
