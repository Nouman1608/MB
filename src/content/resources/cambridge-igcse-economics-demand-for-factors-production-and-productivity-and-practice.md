## Questions

**1.** Define derived demand and give one example. **[2]**

**2.** A print shop in Town E sells 4,000 printed pages a week. Demand then rises to 5,000 pages a week. Each operator produces 500 pages a week, and this does not change.

**(a)** Calculate the number of operators needed before and after the rise in demand. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in the number of operators. **[1]**

**3.** A furniture workshop made 2,400 chairs with 16 workers in year 1. In year 2 it made 2,730 chairs with 21 workers.

**(a)** Calculate labour productivity in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one possible reason why productivity changed as it did. **[2]**

**4.** Firm R can make 400 units a day by Method 1 (9 workers and 1 machine) or Method 2 (3 workers and 4 machines). A worker costs 70 a day and a machine costs 120 a day.

**(a)** Calculate the daily cost of each method. **[2]**
**(b)** State which method a cost-minimising firm would choose. **[1]**
**(c)** The wage falls to 40 a day. Calculate the new cost of each method and state the effect on the choice. **[3]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why a firm might choose capital-intensive production. **[4]**

**6.**

**(a)** Distinguish between production and productivity. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two influences on productivity. **[4]**

**7.** Town C has forty street-food stalls selling similar meals. Explain how a high number of firms is likely to affect price, quality, choice and profit. **[6]**

**8.** Firm Z sold 6,300 units in a market where 9,000 units were sold in total. It raised its price from 8.00 to 10.00.

**(a)** Calculate Firm Z's market share. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in its price. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two disadvantages of monopoly for consumers. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two advantages of monopoly. **[4]**

**10.** Method L costs 1,500 fixed plus 4 per unit. Method C costs 6,000 fixed plus 1.5 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of each method for 3,000 units. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the output at which the two methods cost the same. **[2]**
**(c)** Advise a firm that expects to make 800 units a month, using figures. **[2]**

**11.** On an island, four firms once supplied bottled water. Together they sold 60,000 bottles a month at 1.50 each, with a cost of 1.40 per bottle. One firm now supplies the island. It sells 50,000 bottles a month at 1.80, with a cost of 1.10 per bottle.

**(a)** Calculate the total monthly profit before and after the change. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in price. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether island consumers benefit from the single supplier. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Demand for a factor of production that comes from the demand for the product it helps to make [1]. Example: demand for bakers comes from demand for bread [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition gains credit when it says "derived from" the product market. An example alone does not show that you know why the demand arises.

**2. (a)** Before: 4,000 ÷ 500 = 8 operators [1]. After: 5,000 ÷ 500 = 10 operators [1].
**(b)** (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = **25%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show each division. Clear working lets the reader see your method, and it helps you spot a slip before you move to the next part.

**3. (a)** Year 1: 2,400 ÷ 16 = 150 chairs per worker [1]. Year 2: 2,730 ÷ 21 = 130 chairs per worker [1].
**(b)** (130 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 [1] = **−13.3%** (a fall of 13.3%) [1].
**(c)** The workforce rose faster than output (workers up 31.25%, chairs up 13.75%) [1], so each worker made fewer chairs. For example, new workers may be inexperienced or may share the same equipment [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the direction of change in the answer. A bare "13.3" without "fall" or a minus sign is incomplete.

**4. (a)** Method 1: 9 × 70 + 1 × 120 = 630 + 120 = 750 [1]. Method 2: 3 × 70 + 4 × 120 = 210 + 480 = 690 [1].
**(b)** **Method 2**, because it is cheaper for the same output [1].
**(c)** Method 1: 9 × 40 + 120 = 480 [1]. Method 2: 3 × 40 + 480 = 600 [1]. Method 1 is now cheaper, so the firm switches towards labour [1].
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion about the switch is needed in part (c). Two correct costs with no comment on the firm's choice leave the question half answered.

**5.** Any two reasons, each with development, for example:
- Labour is expensive compared with machinery [1], so replacing workers lowers cost per unit [1].
- Demand is large and steady [1], so the machines are used fully and high fixed costs are spread over many units [1].
(Other developed reasons are possible, for example consistent quality that machines repeat exactly.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason becomes a full point when it is followed by "so" or "because" and a consequence. A list of four short reasons usually earns less than two developed ones.

**6. (a)** Production is the total output [1]; productivity is output per unit of input, for example per worker [1].
**(b)** Training raises skills [1], so each worker makes more per hour [1]. Better machinery or technology [1] lets each worker produce more in the same time [1]. (Other valid influences: organisation, motivation, health.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the influence and then say what it does to output per worker. "Training" alone names a factor but does not explain.

**7.** Price: lower [1] because stalls compete for customers, who can switch easily [1]. Quality: stalls try to keep it high or improve it to attract buyers [1]. Choice: wide, with many sellers and varieties [1]. Profit: lower, because competition holds prices down [1] and high profit would attract new stalls, which pushes profit back [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names four items. A response that covers only price and profit leaves out available credit, so cover each in turn and link it to the town.

**8. (a)** 6,300 ÷ 9,000 × 100 [1] = **70%** [1].
**(b)** (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 [1] = **25%** [1].
**(c)** Higher prices: with few alternatives the firm can raise price, so consumers pay more [1] and may buy less [1]. Less choice: barriers keep rivals out, so consumers cannot switch to other varieties [1], and the firm has less pressure to improve quality [1]. (Other valid disadvantages are possible.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two disadvantages need two separate points. Repeating "high prices" in different words is one point.

**9.** Economies of scale: producing on a large scale spreads costs [1], so cost per unit can fall and this may allow lower prices [1]. Research funding: high profit can pay for research and development [1], which can bring new or better products [1]. (Other valid: competing abroad, natural monopoly.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advantages of monopoly are often missed. Say that the benefit is possible and depends on whether the firm passes savings on.

**10. (a)** L: 1,500 + 4 × 3,000 = 13,500 [1]. C: 6,000 + 1.5 × 3,000 = 10,500 [1].
**(b)** 1,500 + 4q = 6,000 + 1.5q, so 2.5q = 4,500 [1], q = **1,800 units** [1].
**(c)** At 800 units: L costs 1,500 + 3,200 = 4,700 and C costs 6,000 + 1,200 = 7,200 [1]. Method L is cheaper, because 800 is below 1,800 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Advice must use figures from the question. A claim that "labour is cheaper for small output" without a calculation is weaker.

**11. (a)** Profit = (price − cost) × bottles [1]. Before: 0.10 × 60,000 = 6,000 [1]. After: 0.70 × 50,000 = 35,000 [1].
**(b)** (1.80 − 1.50) ÷ 1.50 × 100 = **20%** rise [1].
**(c)** Against: price is up 20% [1] and quantity is down 16.7%, so fewer bottles are bought [1]. Cost per bottle fell 21.4%, which suggests economies of scale [1], but the saving was not passed on: profit rose from 6,000 to 35,000 [1]. There is also less choice and no rivals [1]. It depends on whether regulation or new entrants would force prices down. On this evidence consumers are probably worse off, because the price rose despite the lower cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit for a supported judgement that depends on conditions. Repeating the figures without stating whether consumers gain or lose does not reach a judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the sign or direction in a percentage change.
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original when finding a percentage change.
- Treating a rise in total output as proof that productivity rose.
- Giving a factor price effect without saying which factor becomes cheaper relative to the other.
- Listing reasons with no consequence attached.
- Writing "monopoly is bad" in an evaluation with no advantages and no judgement.
- Mixing up the effect of competition on profit.
- Using real-world claims that the question does not support, instead of the data given.
