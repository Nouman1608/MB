## Questions

**1.** Explain the meaning of each term.

**(a)** Homozygous **[1]**
**(b)** Heterozygous **[1]**

**2.** In a species of mouse, black fur is controlled by a dominant allele (B) and brown fur by a recessive allele (b). Two black mice, both with genotype Bb, are crossed.

**(a)** Complete a Punnett square for this cross. **[2]**
**(b)** State the ratio of black to brown offspring. **[1]**
**(c)** State the probability that an offspring is brown. **[1]**

**3.** A student says: "In a heterozygous mouse the recessive allele disappears because the dominant allele dominates it." Explain why this statement is wrong. **[3]**

**4.** A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse (bb).

**(a)** Explain how a brown pup in the litter would show that the black parent has genotype Bb. **[2]**
**(b)** The black parent has genotype Bb. Calculate the probability that a litter of six pups are all black. **[2]**
**(c)** A litter of six pups is all black. Suggest what you can conclude about the black parent. **[1]**

**5.** Phenylketonuria is a disorder caused by a recessive allele (p). The normal allele is P. Two parents who do not have the disorder have a child who does.

**(a)** State the genotypes of the parents and explain how you know. **[2]**
**(b)** Draw a Punnett square for the parents' next child. **[2]**
**(c)** Give the probability that the next child is unaffected and carries the recessive allele. **[1]**
**(d)** Give the probability that the next child has the disorder. **[1]**

**6.** A disorder is caused by a dominant allele (D). Both parents have the disorder and both are heterozygous.

**(a)** Show, using a Punnett square or working, the probability that a child does not have the disorder. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the probability that two children both do not have the disorder. **[2]**

**7.** Describe how the sex of a human baby is determined. Use a genetic cross. **[3]**

**8.** A couple have four daughters.

**(a)** A relative says the fifth child is more likely to be a boy. Evaluate this statement. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the probability that all five of their children are girls. **[2]**

**9.** A plant breeder crosses two heterozygous tall plants (Tt). Tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). The table shows the offspring from two separate crosses.

| Cross | Tall offspring | Dwarf offspring | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 10 | 6 | 16 |
| B | 296 | 104 | 400 |

**(a)** Calculate the numbers of tall and dwarf offspring expected in each cross. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the results of cross A are further from the expected ratio than the results of cross B. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the ratio of tall to dwarf plants in cross B, in the form x : 1, to 1 decimal place. **[1]**

**10.** Human height varies across a wide range, from short to tall, with many values in between.

**(a)** Explain why a single-gene Punnett square cannot predict the height of a child. **[2]**
**(b)** Give one other human feature that is controlled by many genes. **[1]**
**(c)** Name one non-genetic factor that affects height. **[1]**

**11.** Gregor Mendel studied inheritance by breeding pea plants in the 1860s.

**(a)** Give two reasons why pea plants were a good choice for his investigations. **[2]**
**(b)** His work was not widely accepted until around 1900. Suggest two reasons. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe two later developments that helped to explain what Mendel's "units" are. **[2]**

**12.** A pure-breeding tall plant (TT) is crossed with a pure-breeding dwarf plant (tt). Tall is dominant.

**(a)** State the genotype and phenotype of the first generation. **[2]**
**(b)** Two of the first generation are crossed. Draw a Punnett square for this cross. **[2]**
**(c)** The cross produces 128 second-generation plants. Calculate how many are expected to be tall and how many dwarf. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate how many of the tall plants are expected to be heterozygous. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Having two identical alleles of a gene (for example BB or bb) [1] **[1]**
**(b)** Having two different alleles of a gene (for example Bb) [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Give an example genotype as well as the definition; it shows the idea is understood and protects the answer if the wording is loose.

**2. (a)** Gametes from each parent B and b shown on the edges [1]; boxes BB, Bb, Bb, bb [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 3 black : 1 brown [1] **[1]**
**(c)** 1/4 (or 25%, or 0.25) [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A ratio and a probability are different forms of the answer. Check which one the question asks for, and do not give 3 : 1 when 1/4 is wanted.

**3.** The recessive allele is still present in the heterozygous mouse, so it has not disappeared [1]. Half of the gametes carry it, so it can be passed on to offspring [1]. It is shown in the phenotype only when two copies are present (bb), because one dominant allele is enough to give the dominant feature [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say the alleles do not interact to remove one another: dominance describes which feature shows in the phenotype, not one allele destroying the other.

**4. (a)** A brown pup is bb, so it received one b from each parent [1]. The brown parent can only give b, so the black parent must have given b and must be Bb [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Each pup has a 1/2 chance of being black [1]. (1/2)⁶ = 1/64 (or 0.016, or about 1.6%) [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The parent is probably BB, but it could still be Bb, because there is a 1/64 chance of this result from a Bb parent [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Where an answer asks you to "suggest" a conclusion, a cautious statement that mentions the remaining probability is stronger than a flat claim that the genotype is proved.

**5. (a)** Both parents are Pp [1]. The child is pp, so each parent must have passed on p, but the parents are unaffected, so each also has P [1] **[2]**

| | P | p |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | PP | Pp |
| **p** | Pp | pp |

**(b)** Gametes P and p shown for both parents [1]; correct four boxes as in the square above [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Two boxes out of four are Pp: 1/2 (50%) [1] **[1]**
**(d)** One box out of four is pp: 1/4 (25%) [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Reasoning from the child back to the parents is a common test. State which alleles each parent must have passed on, not just the genotypes.

**6. (a)** Hh × Hh gives HH, Hh, Hh, hh [1]. Only hh is unaffected, so the probability is 1/4 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** The events are independent, so multiply: 1/4 × 1/4 [1] = 1/16 (0.0625, or 6.25%) [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication step. A final fraction with no working leaves nothing to give credit for if it is wrong.

**7.** Females have two X chromosomes (XX) and males have X and Y (XY) [1]. All eggs carry X, while half of the sperm carry X and half carry Y [1]. Punnett square: eggs X and X against sperm X and Y gives XX, XX, XY, XY, so the chance of a boy is 1/2 (50%) and a girl 1/2 [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the sperm decides the sex, and label which gametes come from which parent. A square with unlabelled parents is harder to give credit to.

**8. (a)** The statement is wrong [1]. Each birth is an independent event with a 1/2 chance of a boy, because the sperm is equally likely to carry X or Y, so earlier children do not change the odds [1] **[2]**
**(b)** (1/2)⁵ [1] = 1/32 (0.03125, or about 3.1%) [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the word independent and explain it in terms of sperm carrying X or Y; "it is just luck" is too vague to earn the mark.

**9. (a)** Cross A: 3/4 × 16 = 12 tall and 4 dwarf [1]. Cross B: 3/4 × 400 = 300 tall and 100 dwarf [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Fertilisation is random, so any set of offspring can differ from the prediction by chance [1]. A small sample (16) is affected more by chance than a large one (400), so the larger sample is closer to 3 : 1 [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 296 ÷ 104 = 2.846..., so 2.8 : 1 [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the sample sizes in your explanation, using the numbers 16 and 400, so the comparison is clear.

**10. (a)** Height is controlled by many genes, not one [1]. A single-gene square only predicts a few clear categories, but height shows a continuous range [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Skin colour [1] **[1]**
**(c)** Diet (or nutrition, or illness in childhood) [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Genes" alone is not an explanation here; say that many genes act together and say what a single-gene square would predict instead.

**11. (a)** Any two from: they grow quickly; they produce many offspring so large numbers could be counted; they have clear either-or features; he could control which plants were crossed [1] [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Any two from: chromosomes and genes had not been discovered, so there was no physical explanation for his units; his work was not widely read; other scientists had not repeated or checked his results [1] [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Any two from: microscopes showed chromosomes and later work suggested the units are on them; the structure of DNA was worked out in 1953, showing how information can be stored and copied; the human genome was sequenced [1] [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* History answers need the idea and its effect: do not just name an event, say what it showed or what new evidence it provided.

**12. (a)** Tt [1]; tall [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Gametes T and t for both parents [1]; boxes TT, Tt, Tt, tt [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Tall: 128 × 3/4 = 96 [1]; dwarf: 128 × 1/4 = 32 [1] **[2]**
**(d)** Two of the three tall boxes are Tt, so 2/3 × 96 = 64 [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (d), use the tall plants only as your total (96), not all 128, and show the fraction you applied.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing letters in a Punnett square without saying what they represent.
- Putting both alleles of a parent into one gamete.
- Giving a genotype ratio when asked for phenotypes.
- Answering a probability question with a ratio such as 3 : 1.
- Saying the recessive allele "disappears" or the dominant allele "wins".
- Treating a past result as changing the next birth's odds.
- Using total offspring when the question asks only about one phenotype.
