This guide covers sub-topic **6(b) Magnetism** of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4 specification: statements
6.2 to 6.7. None of these statements carries a "P" reference, so all of
this content can be assessed on Paper 1P as well as Paper 2P. For the
whole of Topic 6 in one place, use the
[Magnetism and Electromagnetism study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-magnetism-and-electromagnetism/).

## What the specification asks for

- **6.2** know that magnets repel and attract other magnets and attract magnetic substances
- **6.3** describe the properties of magnetically hard and soft materials
- **6.4** understand the term "magnetic field line"
- **6.5** know that magnetism is induced in some materials when they are placed in a magnetic field
- **6.6** practical: investigate the magnetic field pattern for a permanent bar magnet and between two bar magnets
- **6.7** describe how to use two permanent magnets to produce a uniform magnetic field pattern

## Forces between magnets and magnetic substances

Every magnet has two poles, a north-seeking (N) pole and a south-seeking
(S) pole. The forces between poles follow one rule: **like poles repel,
unlike poles attract**. The force is strongest close to the poles and
falls off quickly as the magnets are moved apart.

A **magnetic substance** is a material that is attracted to a magnet but
is not itself a permanent magnet unless it has been magnetised. The
common magnetic metals are **iron, steel, nickel and cobalt**. Most other
materials (copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass) are
non-magnetic: a magnet has no effect on them.

This gives a standard test. Attraction cannot tell you whether a metal
bar is a magnet, because a magnet attracts both a magnet's unlike pole and
an unmagnetised piece of iron. **Repulsion is the only proof that an
object is a magnet.** Bring one end of the bar up to each pole of a known
magnet in turn: if either end is repelled, the bar is magnetised.

## Magnetically hard and soft materials

| Property | Magnetically hard (e.g. steel) | Magnetically soft (e.g. iron) |
|---|---|---|
| Ease of magnetising | Harder to magnetise | Magnetised easily |
| Behaviour when the field is removed | Keeps its magnetism | Loses its magnetism almost at once |
| Typical use | Permanent magnets, compass needles | Electromagnet cores, transformer cores |

The words "hard" and "soft" describe magnetic behaviour, not how the
material feels. In a description question, a full answer gives both
halves of the property (how easily it is magnetised **and** whether it
keeps the magnetism) and links the property to the use. Iron is chosen for
an electromagnet core because the magnetism must switch off when the
current is switched off; steel would stay magnetised and keep holding the
load.

## Magnetic field lines

A **magnetic field** is the region around a magnet where a magnetic
substance or another magnet experiences a force. A **magnetic field
line** shows the direction of the force that would act on a north pole
placed at that point. The rules for drawing them are:

- Lines run from the **N pole to the S pole** outside the magnet, with arrows to show this.
- Lines **never cross**: the field at any point has only one direction.
- Lines that are **closer together** show a **stronger** field, so lines are most crowded at the poles.
- Lines should meet the end of the magnet, and should be smooth curves, not straight segments joined at corners.

A plotting compass placed in the field lines up along the field line,
with its N end pointing in the direction of the arrow. This is why
Earth's magnetic field makes a compass point north: the compass is lining
up with Earth's field lines.

## Induced magnetism

When a piece of magnetic material is placed in a magnetic field, it
becomes a magnet itself. This is **induced magnetism**. The end of an
iron nail nearest the N pole of a bar magnet becomes an induced S pole,
and the far end becomes an induced N pole. Because the nearest poles are
unlike, **induced magnetism always causes attraction**. This is the reason
a magnet attracts unmagnetised iron at all.

Induced magnetism also explains a chain of paper clips hanging from a
magnet: each clip becomes magnetised by the field and holds the next one.
If the clips are iron (magnetically soft), the chain falls apart when the
magnet is removed. If they are steel, some magnetism stays and the clips
may still cling together weakly.

## Required practical: field patterns (6.6)

The specification asks you to investigate the field pattern for one bar
magnet and between two bar magnets. Two methods are used.

**Plotting compass method**

1. Place the bar magnet in the centre of a sheet of paper and draw round it.
2. Place a small plotting compass near one pole. Mark a dot at the end the needle points to.
3. Move the compass so that its tail is over that dot, and mark the new position of the needle's point.
4. Repeat until you reach the other pole, then join the dots with a smooth curve and add an arrow from N to S.
5. Start again from a different point near the pole to draw further lines.

**Iron filings method**

Place a sheet of paper or card over the magnet, sprinkle iron filings
thinly and tap the card gently. Each filing becomes a tiny induced magnet
and lines up with the field. This shows the shape of the whole pattern
quickly, but it does **not** show the direction of the field; you need a
compass for the arrows.

**Two bar magnets**

- **Unlike poles facing (N to S):** field lines pass straight across the gap from one magnet to the other. The magnets attract.
- **Like poles facing (N to N):** field lines from each pole curve away sideways. Midway between the poles there is a **neutral point**, where the two fields cancel and a compass shows no preferred direction. The magnets repel.

Work on a flat surface away from steel objects and other magnets, since
either distorts the pattern.

## Producing a uniform field (6.7)

A **uniform** magnetic field has the same strength and direction
everywhere in a region. On a diagram it is shown by **straight, parallel,
equally spaced** field lines, all with arrows the same way.

To produce one with two permanent magnets:

- Use two flat (slab-shaped) magnets with large pole faces.
- Place them **parallel** to each other with **opposite (unlike) poles facing** across a small gap.
- In the gap, away from the edges, the field lines run straight across from N to S, equally spaced.

Near the edges of the gap the lines bulge outwards, so the field is only
uniform in the central region. A smaller gap and wider pole faces make
the uniform region a larger fraction of the space between the magnets.
This arrangement is the field used later in the motor effect, which is why
diagrams of motors and loudspeakers show parallel lines between the
poles.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (describe).** *Describe how a student could use a plotting
compass to find the direction of the field at a point near a bar
magnet.*

Model answer: place the compass at the point [1]; the needle lines up
with the field [1]; the direction the **N end** of the needle points is
the direction of the field at that point [1].

**Example 2 (explain).** *A steel bar is attracted to both ends of a bar
magnet. A student concludes that the steel bar is not a magnet. Explain
whether the conclusion is justified.*

Model answer: the conclusion is justified [1]. If the steel bar were a
magnet, one of its ends would be **repelled** by one pole of the bar
magnet, because like poles repel [1]. Attraction to both poles is what an
unmagnetised magnetic material shows, because the magnet **induces** an
unlike pole in the nearest end of the bar [1].

## Common mistakes

- Using attraction as evidence that an object is a magnet.
- Calling a material "soft" because it is easy to bend, or describing only one half of the property.
- Drawing field lines that cross, start in mid-air, or have arrows from S to N outside the magnet.
- Drawing curved lines between opposite poles when the question asks for a uniform field.
- Saying iron filings show the direction of the field.
- Forgetting the neutral point when sketching the field between two like poles.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (for example, the field-pattern practical): give the steps in order, with enough detail that someone else could follow them. Name the equipment.
- **Draw**: use a sharp pencil, smooth curves, at least three lines each side of the magnet, and arrows on every line.
- **Explain** (for example, why an iron nail is attracted): include a reason, here that magnetism is induced with an unlike pole nearest the magnet.
- **State/Give**: short recall, such as naming a magnetic metal. One word can be enough.
