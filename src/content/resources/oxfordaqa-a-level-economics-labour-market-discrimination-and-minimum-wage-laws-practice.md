These questions test sections 3.3.4.5 (discrimination in the labour market) and 3.3.4.6 (minimum wage laws) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification, version 1.2. Section 3.3 is extra International A2 content. All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

**1.** State two conditions necessary for an employer to pay equally productive groups of workers different wages. **[2]**

**2.** A firm in Country C pays these hourly wages. Men: 22, 24, 26, 30, 48. Women: 18, 20, 22, 24, 26.

**(a)** Calculate the mean pay gap. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the median pay gap. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the two gaps differ and why neither proves discrimination. **[2]**

**3.** A workshop hires two equally productive groups, P and Q. Marginal revenue product (MRP) is 36 an hour for both. Supply to the firm: group P, w = 18 + 0.15L; group Q, w = 12 + 0.3L, where w is the hourly wage and L the number of workers.

**(a)** Find the wage and employment for each group when the firm hires where marginal cost of labour equals MRP. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the firm's surplus (MRP minus wages, summed over all workers). **[2]**
**(c)** A law requires both groups to be paid 27. Find employment of group Q and the new surplus. **[3]**

**4.** In Country D, 50 workers in group A and 150 in group B have equal skills. Occupation X has labour demand w = 40 − 0.2L, and occupation Y has w = 36 − 0.1L. Group B is barred from X. Output value is the area under demand: 40L − 0.1L² in X and 36L − 0.05L² in Y. Without the barrier, 80 workers would be in X and 120 in Y at a wage of 24.

**(a)** Calculate each occupation's wage and the pay gap. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in output value caused by the barrier. **[4]**

**5.** Explain two disadvantages of wage discrimination for the economy as a whole. **[4]**

**6.** In a competitive labour market, demand is L = 1,500 − 100w and supply is L = 50w + 300.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** A minimum wage of 9.50 is introduced. Calculate unemployment. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the elasticity of labour demand over this wage rise. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the change in the wage bill and explain it. **[2]**

**7.** One employer hires all workers in a town. Its MRP is w = 20 − 0.01L and labour supply is w = 2 + 0.01L.

**(a)** Find the wage and employment that maximise profit. **[3]**
**(b)** A minimum wage of 10 is introduced. Find employment. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the highest minimum wage at which employment is still at least the level in part (a). **[2]**

**8.** The national minimum wage is 9.00 in Year 1 (price index 100) and 9.45 in Year 2 (price index 106). Median pay in Year 2 is 15.

**(a)** Calculate the Year 2 minimum wage in Year 1 prices. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the real minimum wage. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the ratio of the Year 2 minimum to median pay. **[1]**

**9.** A rise in the wage from 10 to 10.50 cuts employment from 8,000 to 7,840. Calculate the elasticity of demand for labour and the percentage change in the wage bill. **[3]**

**10.** One country has a single minimum wage of 10. Region R has median pay of 14 and region S median pay of 25. Explain why the effects differ. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a national minimum wage benefits low-paid workers and the economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of the following, [1] + [1]: the employer has power over wages (monopsony or immobile workers); the groups can be identified and kept apart; the groups differ in supply elasticity; workers have poor information or limited mobility. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State each condition precisely. "Discrimination happens" is a restatement, not a condition.

**2. (a)** Men: 150/5 = 30; women: 110/5 = 22 [1]. Gap = 8/30 = **26.7%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Medians 26 and 22 [1]. Gap = 4/26 = **15.4%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The 48 pulls up the male mean more than the median [1]. Job type, hours and experience may explain part of either gap, so only the unexplained part indicates discrimination [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the higher average, state which average you used, and avoid claiming proof from raw data.

**3. (a)** Group P: MC = 18 + 0.3L; 18 + 0.3L = 36 gives L = **60** [1]; w = 18 + 9 = **27** [1]. Group Q: MC = 12 + 0.6L; L = **40** [1]; w = 12 + 12 = **24** [1] **[4]**
**(b)** P: (36 − 27) × 60 = 540; Q: (36 − 24) × 40 = 480 [1]. Surplus = **1,020** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Q supplies (27 − 12)/0.3 = **50** workers [1]. P still supplies 60, so total 110 [1]. Surplus = (36 − 27) × 110 = **990** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Derive marginal cost from the supply curve for each group before equating it with MRP. Using the supply curve itself as MC gives wrong values.

**4. (a)** X: 40 − 0.2 × 50 = **30** [1]. Y: 36 − 0.1 × 150 = **21** [1]. Gap = 9/30 = **30%** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** With the barrier: X = 2,000 − 250 = 1,750 and Y = 5,400 − 1,125 = 4,275, total **6,025** [1]. Without: X = 3,200 − 640 = 2,560 and Y = 4,320 − 720 = 3,600, total **6,160** [1]. Fall = 135 [1], which is 135/6,160 = **2.2%** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State the base for the percentage. A fall measured against the output with the barrier gives a different figure and does not answer the question.

**5.** Misallocation of labour: workers are in jobs where their marginal product is lower, so output is below its potential [1]; this lowers GDP and living standards [1]. Wider inequality: lower-paid groups have less income, so lower consumption and tax receipts, and may need more welfare spending [1]; this raises the burden on public finances [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each disadvantage needs a developed link to the whole economy. Naming "unfairness" alone is a one-sided point about the group.

**6. (a)** 1,500 − 100w = 50w + 300 gives w = **8** [1], L = **700** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Demand = 1,500 − 950 = 550 and supply = 475 + 300 = 775 [1]. Unemployment = **225** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Employment change −150/700 = −21.4% and wage change 1.5/8 = 18.75% [1]. Elasticity = **−1.14** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** 8 × 700 = 5,600 and 9.50 × 550 = 5,225, a fall of **6.7%** [1]. Demand is elastic, so the percentage fall in employment is larger than the rise in the wage [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the original values as the base, keep the sign of the elasticity and interpret it. Add a conclusion to the number.

**7. (a)** MC = 2 + 0.02L [1]. Set equal to 20 − 0.01L: L = **600** [1]; w = 2 + 6 = **8** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** At 10, supply = 800 and demand = (20 − 10)/0.01 = 1,000 [1]. The firm cannot hire more than workers offer, so employment is **800** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Demand must be at least 600: 20 − w ≥ 6 [1], so w = **14** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the quantity supplied with the quantity demanded at the floor before naming employment. A bare "800" with no comparison leaves the reasoning unshown.

**8. (a)** 9.45/1.06 [1] = **8.92** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** (8.92 − 9.00)/9.00 = **−0.9%** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** 9.45/15 = **63%** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Deflate by the index divided by 100, not by subtracting it. State the base year of the real figure.

**9.** Employment −160/8,000 = −2% and wage +0.50/10 = +5%, so elasticity = **−0.4** [1]. Wage bill 80,000 to 82,320 [1], a rise of **2.9%** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate the wage bill before and after and compare. Inferring the direction from the elasticity alone leaves the wage bill change unshown.

**10.** Ratio to median is 10/14 = 71% in R and 10/25 = 40% in S [1]. The floor affects far more workers and raises wage costs more in R [1]. If demand for labour is elastic, R loses more jobs [1]. In S the floor is below what most firms pay, so it has little effect on poverty or jobs [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two regions explicitly, using the ratio, rather than describing one region only.

**11.** Credit for any eight, developed: pay rises for those in work cut poverty [1]; in a competitive market a floor above equilibrium causes unemployment [1]; if demand for labour is inelastic the wage bill rises, if elastic it falls [1]; under monopsony a floor can raise employment [1]; higher pay can raise productivity and retention [1]; firms may raise prices, cut hours or substitute capital [1]; the effect depends on the level relative to median and in real terms [1]; a reasoned judgement, such as that a moderate floor helps most [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the points against each other and reach a judgement rather than listing effects.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Treating every pay difference as discrimination.
* Using the supply curve instead of marginal cost when finding the monopsony or discriminating wage.
* Dividing a percentage change by the new value.
* Dropping the sign of an elasticity or not interpreting it.
* Drawing a floor below equilibrium and still showing unemployment.
* Ignoring the wage bill and gains to workers who stay in jobs.
* Ignoring monopsony when judging a minimum wage.
* Comparing wages across years without deflating.
