## Answers

**1.** Propanenitrile [1].

**2.** KCN (potassium cyanide) [1]; dissolved in ethanol, heat (under reflux) [1].

**3.** 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH₃CH(OH)CN [1].

**4.** Catalyst: it supplies CN⁻ ions, which are regenerated [1].

**5.** Hydrolysis [1].

**6.** The C=O bond is polar, Cᵟ⁺=Oᵟ⁻, and a curly arrow goes from the lone pair on the carbon of CN⁻ to
the δ+ carbonyl carbon [1]. A curly arrow from the C=O π bond to the oxygen gives an alkoxide
intermediate, (CH₃)₂C(O⁻)CN [1]. The O⁻ removes H⁺ from HCN, regenerating CN⁻ [1]. The product is
2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN [1].

**7. (a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CN [1]; pentanenitrile [1].

**(b)** Nucleophilic substitution [1].

**(c)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CN + NaOH + H₂O → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COONa + NH₃ [1];
CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COONa + HCl → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COOH + NaCl [1].

**8.** Step 1: oxidise ethanol to ethanal with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, distilling the product off as it forms
[1]. Step 2: heat ethanal with HCN and a KCN catalyst to give 2-hydroxypropanenitrile [1]. Step 3: heat
with dilute acid (or dilute alkali then acidify) to hydrolyse the nitrile to 2-hydroxypropanoic acid [1].

**9. (a)** The hydroxynitrile from ethanal, CH₃CH(OH)CN [1], and from butanone, CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)(OH)CN [1].
(Methanal gives HOCH₂CN, whose carbon carries two H atoms.)

**(b)** The carbon carrying OH and CN is also bonded to two identical CH₃ groups, so it does not have four
different groups [1].

**10.** Mr(CH₃CHO) = 44.0, so n = 4.40 / 44.0 = 0.100 mol [1]. Mr(CH₃CH(OH)CN) = 3(12.0) + 5(1.0) + 14.0
+ 16.0 = 71.0, so n = 5.68 / 71.0 = 0.0800 mol [1]. Yield = 0.0800 / 0.100 × 100 = 80.0% [1].

**11.** In bromoethane, CN⁻ attacks an sp³ carbon bonded to a good leaving group, Br, which leaves as Br⁻,
so the result is substitution [1]. In ethanal, CN⁻ attacks the sp² carbonyl carbon, which has no leaving
group; the C=O π bond breaks instead and nothing is lost, so the result is addition [1].

**12. (a)** Propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN (the absorption is C≡N) [1].

**(b)** Bromoethane [1].

**13. (a)** CH₃CH(CN)CH₃ [1].

**(b)** CH₃CH(COOH)CH₃, that is (CH₃)₂CHCOOH [1].

**14. (a)** (CH₃CH₂)₂C(OH)CN [1].

**(b)** No: the carbon bonded to OH and CN also carries two identical ethyl groups [1].

**(c)** (CH₃CH₂)₂C(OH)COOH: the C≡N is hydrolysed to COOH and the OH group is unchanged [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "aqueous KCN" or leaving out "ethanol" for the halogenoalkane reaction.
- Calling KCN a reagent rather than a catalyst in hydroxynitrile formation.
- Drawing the curly arrow from the nitrogen of CN⁻.
- Forgetting the alkoxide intermediate or the protonation step.
- Miscounting carbons when naming nitriles: the C≡N carbon is included.
- Distilling instead of refluxing when the target is a carboxylic acid; distilling is right only when the
  aldehyde is wanted, as in question 8.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does hydrolysis affect the OH group of a hydroxynitrile?**
No. Only the C≡N group is hydrolysed, to COOH, so a hydroxynitrile gives a hydroxy carboxylic acid with
the OH still on the carbon next to the COOH.

**How do I know when a synthesis needs a cyanide step?**
Compare carbon counts. If the product has one more carbon than the starting material, use KCN on a
halogenoalkane, or HCN with a KCN catalyst on an aldehyde or ketone.

**Why does the hydroxynitrile from ethanal have a chiral centre?**
Its C2 carbon is bonded to four different groups: H, OH, CH₃ and CN. Any aldehyde except methanal gives a
hydroxynitrile with a chiral centre.
