## 3.3.9.1 Carboxylic acids and esters

### Structures and names

A **carboxylic acid** contains the –COOH group: a C=O and an O–H on the same carbon. The carbon of
–COOH counts as carbon 1 in the name, and the suffix is **-oic acid**.

- HCOOH: methanoic acid
- CH₃CH₂COOH: propanoic acid
- CH₃CH(CH₃)COOH: 2-methylpropanoic acid

An **ester** contains –COO– joining two carbon chains. It forms from an acid and an alcohol. The name
has two words: the **alcohol part first** (as an alkyl group, such as methyl or ethyl) and the **acid
part second** (ending -oate).

### Worked example 1: naming esters

Name CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ and HCOOCH₃.

1. Find the C=O. The chain that contains the C=O carbon is the acid part.
2. In CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ the acid part is CH₃CH₂CO– (3 carbons, propanoate). The part on the other O
   is –CH₂CH₃ (ethyl).
3. Name: **ethyl propanoate**.
4. In HCOOCH₃ the acid part is HCO– (1 carbon, methanoate); the other part is methyl. Name:
   **methyl methanoate**.

### Carboxylic acids are weak acids

A carboxylic acid only **partially dissociates** in water, so it is a weak acid:

`CH₃CH₂COOH ⇌ CH₃CH₂COO⁻ + H⁺`

Even so, it is strong enough to release carbon dioxide from carbonates. You see effervescence, and the
gas turns limewater cloudy.

### Worked example 2: an acid with a carbonate

Write an equation for ethanoic acid reacting with calcium carbonate.

1. Each CO₃²⁻ needs two H⁺, so you need two acid molecules.
2. The salt is calcium ethanoate, (CH₃COO)₂Ca.

`2CH₃COOH + CaCO₃ → (CH₃COO)₂Ca + CO₂ + H₂O`

### Making esters

A carboxylic acid and an alcohol react, **in the presence of an acid catalyst**, to give an ester and
water. Concentrated sulfuric acid is the usual catalyst. The reaction is reversible, so the yield is
never 100%.

`CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O`

### Worked example 3: esterification yield

7.40 g of propanoic acid is warmed with excess ethanol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid.
6.63 g of ethyl propanoate is collected. Use Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0. Calculate the
percentage yield.

1. Equation: CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (1 : 1).
2. Mr of propanoic acid (C₃H₆O₂) = 74.0. Moles = 7.40 ÷ 74.0 = 0.100 mol.
3. Mr of ethyl propanoate (C₅H₁₀O₂) = 102.0. Theoretical mass = 0.100 × 102.0 = 10.2 g.
4. Percentage yield = 6.63 ÷ 10.2 × 100 = **65.0%**.

### Uses of esters

The specification lists common uses: **solvents**, **plasticisers**, **perfumes** and **food
flavourings**. Many small esters have sweet, fruity smells, which is why they appear in perfumes and
flavourings.

### Fats and oils

Vegetable oils and animal fats are **esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol)**, HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH.
Each of the three –OH groups forms an ester link with a long-chain carboxylic acid, so one fat
molecule contains three ester groups. These are often called triglycerides.

### Hydrolysis of esters

Esters can be hydrolysed in two ways.

| Condition | Products | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Acid: heat under reflux with dilute acid | Carboxylic acid + alcohol | Reversible, so incomplete |
| Alkaline: heat under reflux with NaOH(aq) | Salt of the carboxylic acid + alcohol | Goes to completion |

For ethyl propanoate:

`CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂OH` (acid catalyst)

`CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + CH₃CH₂OH`

Alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion because the carboxylate ion formed does not react with the
alcohol to re-form the ester. To get the free acid afterwards, add a strong acid.

### Soap and biodiesel

**Soap.** Hydrolysing a vegetable oil or animal fat in **alkaline** conditions gives soap (salts of
long-chain carboxylic acids) and glycerol.

**Biodiesel** is a mixture of **methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids**. It is made by reacting
vegetable oils with **methanol** in the presence of a catalyst. Glycerol forms as well.

### Worked example 4: soap and biodiesel from one fat

Glyceryl tridodecanoate, (C₁₁H₂₃COO)₃C₃H₅, has Mr 638.0. A 31.9 g sample is treated in two separate
ways. Calculate the maximum mass of (a) sodium dodecanoate (Mr 222.0) from hydrolysis with NaOH, and
(b) methyl dodecanoate (Mr 214.0) from reaction with methanol.

1. Moles of fat = 31.9 ÷ 638.0 = 0.0500 mol.
2. Soap equation: (C₁₁H₂₃COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₁H₂₃COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃.
3. Moles of soap = 3 × 0.0500 = 0.150 mol. Mass = 0.150 × 222.0 = **33.3 g**.
4. Biodiesel equation: (C₁₁H₂₃COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3CH₃OH → 3C₁₁H₂₃COOCH₃ + C₃H₅(OH)₃.
5. Moles of methyl ester = 0.150 mol. Mass = 0.150 × 214.0 = **32.1 g**.

The 1 : 3 ratio is the step to watch. One fat molecule holds three acid chains.

## 3.3.9.2 Acylation

### Structures

| Type | Group | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acyl chloride | –COCl | CH₃COCl, ethanoyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | –CO–O–CO– | (CH₃CO)₂O, ethanoic anhydride |
| Amide | –CONH₂ (or –CONHR) | CH₃CONH₂, ethanamide |

An amide made from a primary amine has the alkyl group on nitrogen, for example CH₃CONHCH₃,
N-methylethanamide.

### The four reactions

Acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides both react with **water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines**. In
each case the acyl group (CH₃CO–) replaces an H on the nucleophile. The acyl chloride gives HCl as the
by-product; the anhydride gives ethanoic acid.

| Nucleophile | Product | Ethanoyl chloride | Ethanoic anhydride |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | Carboxylic acid | CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl | (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O → 2CH₃COOH |
| Alcohol | Ester | CH₃COCl + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + HCl | (CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + CH₃COOH |
| Ammonia | Amide | CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl | (CH₃CO)₂O + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + CH₃COONH₄ |
| Primary amine | N-substituted amide | CH₃COCl + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + CH₃NH₃Cl | (CH₃CO)₂O + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + CH₃COONH₃CH₃ |

With ammonia and amines you need **two** molecules: one acts as the nucleophile, and the second
reacts with the acid by-product to form a salt.

The specification suggests recording observations of these reactions. Ethanoyl chloride reacts
vigorously with water, and the hydrogen chloride produced appears as misty fumes.

### Mechanism: nucleophilic addition–elimination

You must be able to outline this mechanism for **acyl chlorides** with water, alcohols, ammonia and
primary amines. The carbonyl carbon is δ+ because both O and Cl pull electron density away from it.

### Worked example 5: ethanoyl chloride and water

```
Step 1 (addition)
  H2O:  →  C(δ+) of CH3–C(=O)–Cl     lone pair on O attacks the carbonyl C
  C=O  →  O                          the π electrons move onto O

  Intermediate:  CH3–C(O⁻)(Cl)–O⁺H2

Step 2 (elimination)
  O⁻ lone pair → C–O                  C=O re-forms
  C–Cl → Cl                           Cl⁻ leaves
  Cl⁻ removes H⁺ from O⁺H2            HCl forms

  Product: CH3COOH + HCl
```

In the intermediate, show the negative charge on O and the positive charge on the oxygen that came
from water. Every arrow starts at a lone pair or a bond.

With an alcohol the attacking atom is the alcohol O, and the product is an ester. With ammonia or a
primary amine the attacking atom is N, and the intermediate carries N⁺. For ammonia and amines, the
H⁺ is removed by a second NH₃ or amine molecule.

### Aspirin: ethanoic anhydride versus ethanoyl chloride

Aspirin is made by acylating 2-hydroxybenzoic acid. Industry uses **ethanoic anhydride** rather than
ethanoyl chloride:

`HOC₆H₄COOH + (CH₃CO)₂O → CH₃COOC₆H₄COOH + CH₃COOH`

Advantages you can explain from the chemistry:

- The by-product is ethanoic acid, not hydrogen chloride. Hydrogen chloride is a corrosive, toxic gas.
- Ethanoic anhydride reacts less vigorously than ethanoyl chloride, so the reaction is easier to
  control.
- Ethanoic anhydride is hydrolysed less readily by water, including moisture in the air, so it is
  easier to store and handle.

## Required practical 10

**Preparation of a pure organic solid and test of its purity.** A typical example is aspirin.

1. React, then collect the crude solid by filtration under reduced pressure.
2. Recrystallise: dissolve the solid in the **minimum volume of hot solvent**, so the product
   crystallises on cooling.
3. Filter the hot solution if insoluble impurities are present.
4. Cool so crystals form. Soluble impurities stay in solution.
5. Filter under reduced pressure, wash with a little cold solvent, and dry.
6. Measure the melting point. A pure solid melts sharply at the value given in a data source.
   An impure solid melts lower and over a wider range.

**Preparation of a pure organic liquid.** A typical example is an ester.

1. Heat the acid, alcohol and acid catalyst under reflux.
2. Distil off the crude ester.
3. Shake with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a separating funnel to remove acid. Release
   the pressure often, because CO₂ forms. Keep the organic layer.
4. Dry with an anhydrous drying agent, such as anhydrous sodium sulfate, then decant or filter.
5. Redistil, collecting the fraction that boils at the ester's boiling point.

## Common errors

- Naming esters backwards. CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ is ethyl ethanoate; CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ is methyl propanoate.
- Calling a carboxylic acid "strong" because it fizzes with carbonates. It is weak: partial dissociation.
- Forgetting that acid hydrolysis is reversible and alkaline hydrolysis gives a salt, not the acid.
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio for fats. A fat gives **three** soap or biodiesel molecules.
- Writing CH₃COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + HCl. In excess ammonia the HCl becomes NH₄Cl, so use 2NH₃.
- Calling the mechanism "nucleophilic substitution". The AQA name is **nucleophilic
  addition–elimination**.
- Missing the charges on the intermediate, or showing Cl⁻ leaving before the C=O re-forms.
- Using a large volume of hot solvent in recrystallisation, which loses product.
