## Questions

**1.** For each statement about Country D, state whether it is positive, normative or both. **(a)** Average rents rose by 6% last year. **(b)** Rents are too high. **(c)** A cut in income tax will increase hours worked. **(d)** The government should fund more training because youth unemployment is 9%. **[4]**

**2.** Explain one way in which economics is similar to, and one way in which it differs from, the natural sciences in its methods. **[4]**

**3.** A laundry cleans 800 garments a day. Method P uses 8 workers and 1 machine. Method R uses 3 workers and 3 machines. A worker costs 80 a day and a machine 160 a day.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per garment of each method. **[2]**
**(b)** The wage rises to 120. Calculate the new cost per garment of each method. **[2]**
**(c)** State the other two basic economic decisions beside "how to produce", and explain one of them. **[2]**

**4.** A small coastal fishery uses a boat, the stock of fish in the sea, a crew and an owner who takes the risk of starting the business. Classify each into a factor of production. **[4]**

**5.** A farmer has 15 hectares. A hectare earns net revenue of 1,200 from tomatoes, 950 from peppers and 800 from herbs.

**(a)** State the opportunity cost of one hectare of tomatoes. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total net revenue and the total opportunity cost if all 15 hectares grow tomatoes. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why herbs are not part of this opportunity cost. **[1]**

**6.** Country B produces manufactured goods (M) and food (F), in thousands of units. Its boundary passes through these points: (0, 130), (50, 120), (78, 104), (104, 78), (120, 50) and (130, 0), written (M, F).

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one extra unit of M when moving from (78, 104) to (104, 78), and from (104, 78) to (120, 50). **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what happens to the opportunity cost as M rises, and why. **[4]**

**7.** Country B's boundary satisfies M² + F² = 16,900. The economy is at M = 60, F = 78.

**(a)** Show that it is inside the boundary. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the largest increase in M possible with F held at 78. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain two possible reasons why resources might be unemployed. **[2]**

**8.** Improved irrigation raises Country B's maximum food output from 130 to 156 while its maximum M stays at 130. The new boundary satisfies (M ÷ 130)² + (F ÷ 156)² = 1.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in maximum food output. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how the boundary changes. **[2]**
**(c)** At M = 104, calculate the extra F now possible compared with the old boundary. **[2]**

**9.** A minister says that the point (104, 78) is better than (78, 104) because it has more manufactured goods. **(a)** Explain why both points are productively efficient. **[2]** **(b)** Explain why the diagram cannot show which is allocatively efficient. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that disagreements between economists about policy arise mainly from differences in value judgements. **[9]**

**11.** Country C is considering draining a wetland to create farmland. Evaluate the decision, using the ideas of scarcity, opportunity cost and a production possibility diagram. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Positive: it can be checked against data [1]. (b) Normative: "too high" is a value judgement [1]. (c) Positive: testable, even if it proves false [1]. (d) Both: "should" is normative and the unemployment rate is a testable claim [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Both" is only creditworthy if you name the testable element and the judgement element; giving the label alone is weak.

**2.** Similarity: both build simplified models with assumptions and test hypotheses against evidence [1]. Economists, for example, use ceteris paribus to isolate one cause, as a physicist holds other variables constant [1]. Difference: economics studies people, who respond to incentives and to the theories themselves [1], and controlled experiments are rarely possible, so cause and effect are harder to separate and conclusions are tendencies [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs development, not just a label such as "models" or "experiments"; a list of three one-word similarities earns little.

**3.** (a) P: 8 × 80 + 160 = 800, and 800 ÷ 800 = 1.00 [1]. R: 3 × 80 + 3 × 160 = 720, and 720 ÷ 800 = 0.90 [1].
(b) P: 8 × 120 + 160 = 1,120, giving 1.40 [1]. R: 3 × 120 + 480 = 840, giving 1.05 [1].
(c) What to produce, and for whom [1]. Either explained, for example "for whom" is how output is shared among households by income [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show total cost before dividing, so that a slip in the final division can still be seen and credited.

**4.** The boat is capital [1]. The fish stock is land, the natural resource (or environment) [1]. The crew is labour [1]. The owner's risk-taking is enterprise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Capital is man-made production goods, not money; answers that call the owner's funds "capital" lose the point.

**5.** (a) Peppers: 950, the next best use [1].
(b) Revenue 15 × 1,200 = 18,000 [1]. Opportunity cost 15 × 950 = 14,250 [1].
(c) Opportunity cost is only the next best alternative given up, not the sum of all alternatives [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding 950 and 800 is the most common error; state "next best" explicitly.

**6.** (a) First move: M rises by 26 and F falls by 26, so 26 ÷ 26 = 1 unit of F [1]. Second move: M rises by 16 and F falls by 28, so 28 ÷ 16 = 1.75 [1].
(b) The opportunity cost rises as M rises [1]. Earlier, the move from (0, 130) to (50, 120) cost only 0.2 units of F per unit of M [1]. This is because resources are not equally suited to both uses [1]. The first resources moved are best at making M, and later ones that are better at food must be moved, so more F is lost per extra M [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cite numbers from the table, and give a reason as well as the trend; the reason is that resources suit the two goods unequally.

**7.** (a) 60² + 78² = 3,600 + 6,084 = 9,684, which is below 16,900 [1].
(b) At F = 78, the boundary has M = 104, so the increase is 104 − 60 = 44 [1].
(c) One reason, such as a fall in demand during a recession leaving workers and machines idle [1]. A second reason, such as a mismatch of workers' skills with the jobs available, or resources used inefficiently [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Reasons must explain why resources are idle; "the economy is poor" gives no development.

**8.** (a) (156 − 130) ÷ 130 × 100 = 20% [1].
(b) The food intercept rises from 130 to 156 [1]. The manufacturing intercept stays at 130, so the boundary shifts outward unevenly, mainly along the food axis [1].
(c) New F at M = 104 is 156 × √(1 − 0.64) = 156 × 0.6 = 93.6 [1]. The old boundary gave 78, so the gain is 15.6 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Describing the shift requires naming what changed on each axis; "it shifts out" alone is incomplete.

**9.** (a) Both points are on the boundary, so all resources are used fully [1]. More of one good is possible only by producing less of the other [1].
(b) Allocative efficiency depends on what society values and where benefit equals cost [1]. The boundary shows only what is possible, not preferences, so choosing between points involves value judgements [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not say that boundary points are "equally good"; say they are equally efficient in production.

**10.** Disagreements can arise because economists use different models or read ambiguous evidence differently [1]. For example, whether a tax cut raises hours worked is testable but data are hard to separate from other influences [1]. Experiments are rarely possible, so evidence is open to interpretation [1]. Value judgements matter too: economists give different weight to equality and efficiency [1]. Two economists can agree on the same predicted outcome and still disagree over whether it is desirable [1]. Values also influence which objectives and questions are studied [1]. The two interact, since a preference may be presented as a positive finding [1]. Evaluation: where evidence is strong, disagreement is more likely to be about values, and where evidence is weak, positive disputes matter more [1]. Judgement: values are often decisive on policy choices, but positive disagreements are not trivial [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation earns credit for weighing the two explanations and reaching a justified conclusion, not for adding a third list of points.

**11.** Scarcity: land and the environment are limited, wants are unlimited, so choice is needed [1]. The wetland is a scarce environmental resource, providing flood protection and habitat, and draining may be hard to reverse [1]. Draining has an opportunity cost: the wetland services forgone [1]. Not draining also has one: the farm output forgone [1]. On a PPD with food on one axis and environmental services on the other, moving along the boundary shows the trade-off [1]. Opportunity cost likely rises, since the last hectares drained may be the most valuable ecologically [1]. Short-run output gains may reduce long-run capacity if the service is lost, so the boundary could shift inward later [1]. If workers are idle, the farm uses unemployed resources, reducing labour's opportunity cost [1]. Positive consequences (output, jobs, flood risk) can be estimated [1]. Moral and political judgements, such as duties to future generations and property rights, differ between people [1]. Evaluation: the answer depends on how reversible the damage is, how well the wetland services can be valued and whether already cleared land could be used [1]. Judgement: drain only if the measured gain clearly exceeds the full opportunity cost, including the environment [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the three named ideas as a chain, linking each to the wetland; listing definitions without applying them to the context earns little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a mixed statement purely positive or purely normative.
- Defining opportunity cost as a sum of all rejected options, or as only money.
- Forgetting working in cost per unit; give total cost first.
- Dividing the wrong way round when finding opportunity cost.
- Stating that cost rises without explaining that resources are not equally suited to both uses.
- Describing a shift of the boundary without naming each intercept.
- Treating all boundary points as allocatively efficient.
- Writing evaluation as a second list rather than weighing and concluding.
- Making general points about the environment without tying them to the case.
