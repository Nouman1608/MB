> Geography (2217) assessment objectives. They are not taken from any past paper
> and are not endorsed by Cambridge International.

Use these questions alongside the
[Population Structure study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-population-structure/)
and [Population Structure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-population-structure-revision-notes/).
Pyramid data are given as tables. All data are illustrative unless a real place is named.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term dependency ratio. **[2]**

**2.** State what a wide base on a population pyramid shows about a country. **[1]**

**3.** A country has a population of 30 million: 9 million aged 0–14, 18 million aged 15–64 and
3 million aged 65 and over. Calculate its dependency ratio. Show your working. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** reasons why there are usually more women than men in the oldest age groups.
**[2]**

**5.** Explain why the dependency ratio does not give an exact measure of how many people depend on
others. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows the population structure of two countries, as percentages of each country's
total population.

| Age group | Country X males | Country X females | Country Y males | Country Y females |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–14 | 19 | 18 | 7 | 7 |
| 15–64 | 29 | 29 | 31 | 30 |
| 65 and over | 2 | 3 | 11 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio for each country. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the old-age dependency for Country Y. **[1]**
**(c)** Compare the population structures of Country X and Country Y. **[4]**
**(d)** Suggest which country is at a higher level of economic development. Give **two** reasons
from the data. **[3]**

**7.** In a country that recruits many foreign workers, males aged 25–44 make up 38% of the
population and females aged 25–44 make up 12%.

**(a)** Explain this imbalance. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** effects of this structure on the country. **[2]**

**8.** Explain the problems a country may face if a large proportion of its population is aged
under 15. **[5]**

**9.** For a named country with a high dependent population, describe its population structure and
explain how the government has responded. **[7]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The number of dependants (people aged 0–14 and 65 and over) [1] for every 100 people of
working age (15–64) [1].

**2.** A high birth rate, so a large proportion of young people (children) [1].

**3.** (9 + 3) ÷ 18 × 100 [1] = **66.7** [1].

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: women have a longer life expectancy; men are more likely to have
worked in dangerous or physically demanding jobs; men have historically had higher rates of
smoking and drinking; more men died in past wars.

**5.** Many people aged 15–64 are **not in paid work**, such as students, unemployed people or
carers [1]; and many people aged 65 and over, or teenagers under 15, **do work** and are not
dependent [1].

**6. (a)** X: (37 + 5) ÷ 58 × 100 = **72.4** [1]. Y: (14 + 25) ÷ 61 × 100 = **63.9** [1].
**(b)** 25 ÷ 61 × 100 = **41.0** [1].
**(c)** Up to 4 marks; comparative statements with figures:
- X has a much larger proportion of children: 37% aged 0–14 compared with 14% in Y [1].
- Y has a much larger proportion of elderly people: 25% aged 65+ compared with 5% in X [1].
- Both have a similar working-age share (58% and 61%) [1].
- X's dependants are mainly young; Y's are mainly old, so X's base is wide and Y's top is wide [1].
- Y has more elderly women (14%) than men (11%), a larger gap than in X [1].
**(d)** Country Y [1]. Any two reasons, 1 mark each: a low proportion of children shows a low birth
rate; a high proportion aged 65+ shows long life expectancy; more elderly women than men reflects
long female life expectancy typical of high-income countries.

**7. (a)** The country attracts large numbers of **labour migrants** for jobs in construction,
oil and services [1]; most of these migrants are **young adult men**, who often come without their
families [1].
**(b)** Any two, 1 mark each: a low dependency ratio; a large workforce for development
projects; a very unbalanced sex ratio; demand for basic worker housing; a large outflow of
remittances; social issues for workers separated from families. Credit reasonable suggestions.

**8.** Up to 5 marks for developed points, for example:
- High demand for **schools and teachers** [1], which may be unaffordable so classes are large or
  children leave school early [1].
- Demand for **maternal and child health care and vaccination** [1].
- **High youth dependency** means a small working population supports many children through
  taxation [1].
- When the cohort reaches working age, there may not be enough **jobs or housing** [1], leading to
  unemployment, informal work or emigration [1].
- **Population momentum**: many young people will become parents, so growth continues [1].

**9.** Mark in levels.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** general points about an ageing or youthful population; no named country or
  little detail.
- **Level 2 (4–5):** named country with some description of structure and at least one response.
- **Level 3 (6–7):** detailed description of structure with specific responses, clearly linked to
  the problems they address.

Indicative content (Japan): narrow base from a low birth rate; very wide top, with more than a
quarter of people aged 65 and over; more elderly women than men; population shrinking. Problems:
rising pension and health costs, labour shortages, rural depopulation. Responses: raising the age
at which pensions are paid and encouraging older people to keep working; encouraging more women
into work; childcare support to raise the birth rate; a new visa category for skilled foreign
workers in 2019; investment in robotics and technology for care. Equivalent credit for a youthful
country such as Niger, with responses such as family planning and investment in education.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparisons that describe one country, then the other, with no comparative words.
- Dividing by 100 instead of by the 15–64 group.
- Case studies with no detail of the government's actual measures.
