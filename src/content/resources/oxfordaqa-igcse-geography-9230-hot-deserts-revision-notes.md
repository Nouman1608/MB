These notes cover section 3.1.2.3, Hot deserts, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. Key ideas: "Hot desert ecosystems have a range of distinctive characteristics"; "Areas on the fringe of hot deserts are at risk of desertification." For full explanations, read the [hot deserts study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-hot-deserts/). For the whole unit, see the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/).

## Specification checklist

- **Physical characteristics**; **interdependence** of climate, water, soils, plants, animals, people.
- **Adaptations**; **biodiversity** issues.
- **Examples:** causes of desertification (climate change, population growth, fuel wood removal, overgrazing, over-cultivation, soil erosion).
- **Examples:** strategies (water and soil management, tree planting, appropriate technology).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hot desert | Region with usually under 250 mm rain a year and very hot days |
| Desertification | Degradation of dry fringe land so that it becomes desert-like |
| Fringe | Edge of a desert, semi-arid and farmed |
| Overgrazing | Too many animals for the vegetation |
| Over-cultivation | Cropping so intensively that soil is exhausted |
| Fuel wood | Wood cut for cooking and heating |
| Appropriate technology | Simple, low-cost, locally maintainable methods |
| Oasis | Fertile area where groundwater reaches the surface |

## Characteristics at a glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Location | About 15-30° north and south: Sahara, Arabian, Thar, Namib, Kalahari, Australian deserts |
| Cause of location | Air sinks at about 30°, giving high pressure and few clouds |
| Rainfall | Under about 250 mm a year; unreliable; short heavy storms |
| Temperature | Over 40°C by day in summer; large fall at night (clear skies) |
| Soils | Thin, sandy or stony, low humus, may be salty |
| Water | Rare; seasonal rivers, oases, groundwater |
| Vegetation | Sparse, widely spaced |

## Interdependence

- Dry climate: little plant growth, so little humus.
- Poor soil: holds little water, runoff is high.
- Few plants: limited food and shelter, so few animals.
- Plant roots bind soil; without plants, erosion by wind and flash floods.
- People depend on scarce water; nomads move herds to allow recovery.

## Adaptations

| Organism | Adaptation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cactus | Stem water store; waxy skin; spines | Keep and conserve water |
| Cactus | Shallow wide roots | Catch rare rain fast |
| Acacia | Long tap root | Reach groundwater |
| Camel | Hump fat; wide feet; long eyelashes | Energy store; walk on sand; protect eyes |
| Fennec fox | Large ears; nocturnal | Lose heat; avoid midday sun |
| Kangaroo rat | Water from seeds; concentrated urine; burrows | Needs little drinking water |
| Reptiles | Burrows; active at dawn and dusk | Avoid heat |

## Biodiversity

- Fewer species than rainforests, but highly specialised.
- Vulnerable: extreme conditions; slow recovery; small water sources; human pressures (mining, off-road vehicles, hunting, farming); climate change.

## Desertification: causes

| Cause | Chain of reasoning |
|---|---|
| Climate change | Less or more erratic rain and higher temperatures lead to plant death and dry soil |
| Population growth | More demand for food, fuel and land |
| Fuel wood removal | Trees cut, so soil loses cover and roots |
| Overgrazing | Plants eaten faster than they regrow, so soil is bare and trampled |
| Over-cultivation | No rest for soil; nutrients lost; soil dusty |
| Soil erosion | Wind and storms remove topsoil, so land is less productive |

Causes link up: more people, so more grazing and wood cutting, so more erosion, so lower yields, so more land is farmed. It is a downward cycle.

## Desertification: strategies

| Strategy | Examples | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Water and soil management | Stone lines, bunds, planting pits (zaï), drip irrigation | Trap water and soil; use water efficiently |
| Tree planting | Shelterbelts; protecting regrowing trees; Great Green Wall | Reduces wind, binds soil, provides fuel, fodder and shade |
| Appropriate technology | Fuel-efficient stoves; solar cookers; hand-dug wells; rotating grazing | Cheap, local, easy to maintain; reduces pressure on land |

## Example: the Sahel (check against your own notes)

- Semi-arid belt south of the Sahara.
- Severe droughts in the 1970s and 1980s.
- Stone lines, planting pits and protection of naturally regrowing trees in parts of Burkina Faso and Niger: low-cost, improved yields and tree cover.
- Great Green Wall (launched 2007): now a patchwork of land restoration; progress slower than planned.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how plants and animals are adapted to life in a hot desert. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "Cacti store water in their stems and have waxy skins and spines instead of leaves, which cuts water loss. They have wide shallow roots to catch rain quickly. Acacia trees have very long tap roots to reach groundwater. Animals such as the fennec fox are nocturnal and have large ears to lose heat. The kangaroo rat gets water from seeds and shelters in a burrow, so it rarely needs to drink."

How it scores: each adaptation earns a mark only when it is linked to its benefit, such as "stores water" or "loses heat".

## Quick recall: five ideas to remember

- Hot deserts are at about 15 to 30 degrees north and south, where air sinks.
- Rainfall is under about 250 mm and unreliable; nights can be cold.
- Desertification is land degradation on the desert fringe, not natural desert growth.
- Causes combine in a downward cycle: more people, more grazing, more erosion.
- Cheap local methods (stone lines, planting pits, tree protection) can work well.

## Exam traps

- Desertification is **land degradation**, not natural desert growth.
- Hot deserts have **low, unreliable rainfall**, not no rain.
- Each cause needs a mechanism: how does it lead to bare, eroded soil?
- Each strategy needs a reason: what does it do?
- Don't mix up rainforest and desert adaptations.
- Use specific examples, not general statements.

## Self-test

1. Between which latitudes are most hot deserts found?
2. Why is the night colder than the day in a hot desert?
3. Explain why hot deserts lie in the 15-30° belt.
4. Describe **two** adaptations of cacti.
5. Why do desert soils have little humus?
6. Define desertification.
7. Explain how overgrazing and removal of fuel wood each lead to soil erosion.
8. Suggest why population growth increases the risk of desertification.
9. Describe how stone lines help to reduce desertification.
10. Why is appropriate technology often better than large-scale schemes for poor farmers?

**Answers**

1. About 15° to 30° north and south of the equator.
2. The skies are clear, so heat escapes quickly at night.
3. Air that rose at the equator sinks around 30°, forming high pressure with few clouds and little rain.
4. Any two: water storage in the stem; waxy skin; spines; shallow wide roots.
5. There is little vegetation, so few dead leaves are added to the soil.
6. The degradation of dry fringe land so that it becomes desert-like and cannot support plants, animals and people.
7. Overgrazing removes plants and compacts the soil; fuel wood removal cuts trees and shrubs; in both cases the soil is bare and loose, so wind and rain remove the topsoil.
8. More people need food, fuel and water, so more land is grazed, cultivated and stripped of wood.
9. They slow the flow of runoff, trapping water and soil behind them, so more water soaks in and plants can grow.
10. It is cheap, uses local skills and materials, and can be maintained by the people who use it.
