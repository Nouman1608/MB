Section 11.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Religion and society*, opens the
Religion section of Paper 4 under heading 11, *Religion and social order*. The syllabus lists four
points: ways of defining religion; difficulties in measuring religious belief; social groups and
religiosity, including class, gender, ethnicity and age; and religion and other belief systems.
Heading 11 asks candidates to contrast structural and interactionist theories, and 11.1 is where
that contrast starts, because how you define and measure religion decides what you find.

## Ways of defining religion

Definitions fall into three families: substantive (what religion *is*), functional (what it
*does*) and social constructionist (what people *say* it is). Beyond the overview, three points
deepen an answer.

### Inclusive and exclusive definitions

- **Exclusive** definitions, usually substantive, draw a tight boundary around religion, typically
  belief in the supernatural. They make comparison easier and make it possible to measure decline.
- **Inclusive** definitions, usually functional, include anything that performs religious
  functions. Nationalism, sport or political ideologies may then count, which makes religion seem
  universal and secularisation hard to prove.

### Durkheim's and Geertz's definitions

- Émile Durkheim (1912) defined religion as a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to
  sacred things, which unite those who adhere to them into a single moral community. The
  definition combines substance (the sacred) with function (moral community).
- Clifford Geertz ("Religion as a Cultural System", 1966) defined religion as a system of symbols
  that establishes powerful, pervasive and long-lasting moods and motivations by formulating
  conceptions of a general order of existence. His approach focuses on meaning, which makes it
  attractive to interpretivists.

### Why the choice matters

A researcher using a substantive definition may conclude that religion is declining because fewer
people believe in God; a researcher using a functional definition may find that "religion" has
moved into new forms. When you evaluate any claim in Section C, ask which definition it relies on.

## Difficulties in measuring religious belief

### Three dimensions that do not always line up

| Dimension | Measured by | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Belief** | Survey questions on God, afterlife, prayer | Wording changes answers; belief can be vague |
| **Belonging** | Membership, census identity | People may identify culturally without believing |
| **Practice** | Attendance counts, prayer, ritual | Practice may reflect social pressure, not belief |

Grace Davie's *Religion in Britain since 1945: Believing without Belonging* (1994) showed that
these dimensions can separate: many people in Britain held some religious belief but did not belong
to or attend a church. Measuring only attendance would understate belief; measuring only census
identity might overstate it.

### Specific measurement problems

- **Census questions.** The census in England and Wales first asked a voluntary question on religion
  in 2001. Because the question asked about religious *identity*, it measured belonging rather than
  belief or practice.
- **Over-reporting.** C. Kirk Hadaway, Penny Long Marler and Mark Chaves (1993) compared
  Americans' self-reported church attendance with counts of people actually present in churches and
  found that self-reports substantially exaggerated attendance. Social desirability shapes answers
  in societies where religion is valued.
- **Under-reporting** may occur where religion is stigmatised or politically risky.
- **Attendance counts.** Head counts, such as the English Church Census conducted by Peter
  Brierley, are reliable for a given day but miss irregular attenders and those who worship at home.
- **Cross-cultural comparison.** Congregational attendance is central to some faiths but not to
  others; in many Hindu households worship takes place at a home shrine, so attendance measures
  based on Christian practice can be misleading.
- **Operationalisation.** Glock and Stark's dimensions (belief, practice, experience, knowledge,
  consequences) show that "religiosity" has several parts, each needing its own indicator.

**Methodological link:** positivists prefer large surveys and counts for reliability; interpretivists
argue that the meaning of belief can only be understood through in-depth interviews or observation.

## Social groups and religiosity

### Gender

- **Socialisation and roles:** women's greater involvement in caring and child-rearing may connect
  them with religious organisations and rites of passage.
- **Risk aversion:** Alan Miller and John Hoffmann (1995) argued that women are more risk-averse
  than men and that not being religious can be seen as a risk.
- **Structural location:** women's greater presence in part-time work or at home has given more
  time and opportunity for religious involvement in some societies.
- **Evaluation:** patterns differ between faiths and societies; in some religions men's
  participation in public worship is higher because of religious rules.

### Age

- **Ageing effect:** people may become more religious as they approach death.
- **Generational effect:** David Voas and Alasdair Crockett (2005) analysed British survey data and
  argued that each generation has been less religious than the one before, so decline is driven by
  generational replacement rather than individuals losing faith.
- **Exceptions:** young people may be drawn to new religious movements or to forms of spirituality
  outside churches.

### Ethnicity

- Minority ethnic and migrant groups often show higher religiosity than majority populations in
  Western societies.
- **Cultural defence and transition** (Bruce) explain this through identity and support in a new
  society.
- **Community:** religious organisations may provide social networks, welfare and language support.
- **Evaluation:** "ethnic minority" covers groups with very different religious traditions, and
  second and third generations may differ from their parents.

### Class

- Rodney Stark and William Sims Bainbridge (*A Theory of Religion*, 1987) argued that religion
  offers **compensators**, promises of future rewards, to those who lack rewards now, which helps
  explain the appeal of some sects to deprived groups.
- Middle-class groups may be drawn to world-affirming movements and, in some societies, have higher
  rates of formal membership and attendance.
- **Evaluation:** class patterns vary greatly by country and denomination.

## Religion and other belief systems

The syllabus asks how religion compares with other ways of understanding the world, such as
science and political ideologies.

- **Open and closed belief systems.** Karl Popper argued that science is an open belief system: its
  claims are open to falsification. Religious belief systems are often described as closed, because
  they claim absolute truth and can explain away contradictions.
- **Self-sustaining beliefs.** E.E. Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the
  Azande* (1937) showed how the Azande explained failures of their poison oracle without abandoning
  belief in it, for example by blaming incorrect procedure. Michael Polanyi argued that all belief
  systems, science included, have similar protective devices.
- **Comparing traditional thought and science.** Robin Horton (1967) argued that African
  traditional religious thought and Western science both try to explain and predict events, but
  science is more open to alternative theories.
- **Ideologies as functional alternatives.** Political ideologies such as Marxism or nationalism can
  provide meaning, ritual and community in ways similar to religion, which supports functional
  definitions.

**Evaluation:** sociologists of science, following Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions* (1962), argue that scientists also work within paradigms that resist challenge, so
the open/closed distinction may be one of degree.

## Common mistakes

- Giving one definition of religion without saying how definitions affect findings.
- Treating belief, belonging and practice as the same thing.
- Explaining gender or age differences without evaluating them for particular faiths or societies.
- Leaving out "religion and other belief systems", which the syllabus names.
- Quoting attendance or census figures you cannot source.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: give the definition, measurement problem or explanation, develop it with
  a study, and apply it to an example.
- **Evaluate** questions: contrast structural explanations (socialisation, structural location,
  deprivation) with interactionist ones (meaning, identity), as heading 11 asks.
- Link forward to 12.1: every secularisation argument depends on how religion is defined and
  measured.
