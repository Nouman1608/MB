## Section A

**1.** Identify the statute that refers to "the existing constitutional principle of the rule of law". **[1]**

**2.** State **two** of Lord Bingham's eight principles of the rule of law. **[2]**

**3.** Name the article of the European Convention on Human Rights that prohibits punishment without law. **[1]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a retrospective law. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain the difference between Raz's formal account of the rule of law and Bingham's substantive account. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how *M v Home Office* (1993) illustrates equality before the law. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how *R (Laporte) v Chief Constable of Gloucestershire* (2006) illustrates the principle that no person shall be sanctioned except in accordance with the law. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which the process of law making may strain the rule of law. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the rule of law applies to substantive criminal law, using **one** example that supports it and **one** that strains it. **[5]**

**10.** A new Act gives a minister power to issue certificates overruling any tribunal decision the minister disagrees with, and states that no court may question a certificate. Explain how this Act might be criticised using the rule of law, referring to decided cases. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how *R (Miller) v Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General* (2019) applied the rule of law to the exercise of government power. **[4]**

**12.** "The rule of law is well protected in England and Wales." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (section 1) [1].

**2.** Any two: the law must be accessible, clear and predictable; legal rights should be decided by law not discretion; the law should apply equally to all unless objective differences justify otherwise; officials must act in good faith and within their powers; the law must protect fundamental human rights; civil disputes must be resolved without prohibitive cost or inordinate delay; procedures must be fair; the state must comply with international law [1] [1].

**3.** Article 7 [1].

**4.** A law that changes the legal consequences of conduct that took place before the law was made [1], for example making past conduct criminal or reversing a court decision already given [1].

**5.** Raz's account is formal: the rule of law requires that law is capable of guiding conduct, so it must be prospective, open, clear, stable and applied by independent courts [1] [1]. On this view a law could satisfy the rule of law while being unjust in content [1]. Bingham's account is substantive: it also requires adequate protection of fundamental human rights, so an unjust law that violated rights would breach the rule of law [1].

**6.** Equality before the law requires that everyone, including the government, is subject to the law and the courts [1]. In *M v Home Office* the Home Secretary disobeyed a court order to return a deported asylum seeker [1], and was held in contempt of court, showing that ministers cannot ignore court orders [1].

**7.** The principle requires state action against a person to have legal authority [1]. In *Laporte* police stopped coaches carrying protesters and escorted them back to London without arresting anyone [1]. The House of Lords held the action unlawful because there was no imminent breach of the peace [1], so the police had no legal power to restrict the passengers' liberty in this way [1].

**8.** Any two developed points, two marks each: retrospective legislation, such as the War Damage Act 1965, changes the effect of past conduct and undermines clarity and fairness [1] [1]; Henry VIII powers let ministers amend Acts by delegated legislation with limited scrutiny, weakening the requirement that law is made through the proper democratic process [1] [1]. (Also accept: ouster clauses attempting to exclude judicial review; legislation passed quickly with little debate.)

**9.** The rule of law requires offences to be clear and defined in advance [1]. Supporting example: the presumption of mens rea in *Sweet v Parsley* (1969), or Article 7, which protects people from conviction for conduct that was not an offence when committed [1] [1]. Straining example: vague common law offences such as conspiracy to corrupt public morals in *Shaw v DPP* (1962), where people cannot know in advance exactly what is forbidden (also accept strict liability or *R v R*, 1991) [1] [1].

**10.** The power allows a minister to overrule a judicial decision because he disagrees with it, which undermines the independence of the courts and fairness [1]. In *R (Evans) v Attorney General* (2015) the Supreme Court held that a ministerial override of a tribunal decision was invalid for this reason [1] [1]. The clause preventing courts from questioning certificates is an ouster clause, conflicting with the principle that official power must be subject to legal limits [1]. In *Anisminic* (1969) and *Privacy International* (2019) the courts read such clauses narrowly so that judicial review was not excluded [1] [1].

**11.** The case concerned the Prime Minister's advice to the Queen to prorogue Parliament for five weeks [1]. The Supreme Court held that it could decide the limits of the prerogative power to prorogue [1], and that the advice was unlawful because it frustrated Parliament's constitutional functions without reasonable justification [1]. It shows that even the highest government power is subject to legal limits enforced by independent courts [1].

**12.** For the statement: independent courts hold government to account, as in *M v Home Office* (1993) and *Miller; Cherry* (2019) [1]; the courts resist ouster clauses and ministerial overrides, as in *Evans* (2015) [1]; the Human Rights Act 1998 gives effect to Article 7 and requires compatibility statements on new Bills [1]; section 1 of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 and the Lord Chancellor's oath give the doctrine statutory recognition [1]. Against: parliamentary supremacy allows Parliament to pass retrospective or rights-restricting laws, as with the War Damage Act 1965 [1]; Henry VIII powers and large volumes of delegated legislation weaken scrutiny [1]; cost and reductions in legal aid limit access to the courts, despite *UNISON* (2017) [1]; equality has exceptions such as diplomatic immunity [1]. Conclusion: the courts protect the rule of law strongly when cases reach them, but its protection depends on political restraint and practical access to justice [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing Dicey's version when the question asks for the specification's elements.
- Naming a case without explaining which element it illustrates.
- In scenario questions, criticising the law generally instead of linking each feature to a principle and a case.
- In essays, forgetting that parliamentary supremacy is itself a limit on judicial protection of the rule of law.
