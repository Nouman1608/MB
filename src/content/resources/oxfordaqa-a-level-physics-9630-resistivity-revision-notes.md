## The equation

ρ = RA/L, so R = ρL/A

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| ρ | resistivity | Ω m |
| R | resistance | Ω |
| A | cross-sectional area (perpendicular to current) | m² |
| L | length along the current | m |

For a wire, A = πd²/4. For a rectangular strip, A = width × thickness.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resistivity | Resistance of a sample of unit length and unit cross-sectional area; a material property |
| Ntc thermistor | Semiconductor component whose resistance falls as temperature rises |
| Temperature sensor | Device whose output changes with temperature, such as a thermistor in a potential divider |
| Superconductor | Material with zero resistivity at and below its critical temperature |
| Critical temperature | Temperature at and below which a material is superconducting; different for each material |

## Scaling rules (same material and temperature)

| Change | Effect on R |
|---|---|
| Double L | × 2 |
| Double d | × 1/4 |
| Halve d | × 4 |
| Stretch to n times the length at constant volume | × n² |
| Material with twice ρ | × 2 |

Stretching: L × n and A ÷ n, so R × n².

## Measuring resistivity

1. Micrometer: diameter at several points and orientations; mean d; A = πd²/4.
2. Several lengths of wire; R = V/I for each (small current to avoid heating).
3. Graph of R against L: gradient = ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A.
4. A non-zero intercept means constant contact resistance; it does not affect the gradient.

## Temperature effects

| Component | As temperature rises | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Metal | R increases | Ions vibrate more; more electron-ion collisions; number of free electrons about constant |
| Ntc thermistor | R decreases, often steeply | More charge carriers released; this outweighs extra vibration |

An ntc thermistor's R-T graph is a falling curve, steepest at low temperature in a typical range. Used with a potential divider, it gives an output pd that changes with temperature.

## Superconductivity

- Resistivity exactly zero at and below the critical temperature.
- Critical temperature depends on the material.
- Applications: strong magnetic fields (large currents in coils with no heating, for example in MRI scanners and particle accelerators); reduced energy loss in transmitting electrical power (I²R loss is zero).
- Critical field is not assessed.

## Worked reminders

**Stretched wire.** A wire of resistance 4.0 Ω is drawn out to three times its length at constant volume. New R = 4.0 × 3² = 36 Ω.

**Strip on its side.** A strip 40 mm long, 5.0 mm wide and 0.50 mm thick has resistivity 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m. Along its length: A = 5.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.50 × 10⁻³ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m², so R = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.040 / 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.016 Ω.

## Exam traps

- Diameter used as radius, or not squared.
- Area left in mm².
- Unit written as Ω m⁻¹.
- Stretching problems that change only the length.
- Ntc thermistor explanation with no mention of charge carriers.
- "Superconductors have very low resistance": it is zero.
- Superconductivity described as happening at "low temperatures" without the critical temperature.

## Quick self-test

1. State the equation that defines resistivity.
2. Show that the unit of resistivity is Ω m.
3. Find the resistance of a 1.5 m wire of diameter 0.50 mm and resistivity 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
4. A sample 0.50 m long with cross-sectional area 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² has resistance 2.0 Ω. Find ρ.
5. Wire Y has the same length and material as wire X but half the diameter. Compare their resistances.
6. A wire is stretched uniformly to twice its length. By what factor does its resistance change?
7. Find the resistance of 100 m of copper wire of diameter 2.0 mm (ρ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m).
8. An R-L graph for a wire of diameter 0.50 mm has gradient 2.5 Ω m⁻¹. Find ρ.
9. Explain how the resistance of a metal wire changes as it is heated.
10. Describe and explain how the resistance of an ntc thermistor changes as it is heated.
11. Define superconductivity.
12. Give two applications of superconductors named in the specification.

### Answers

1. ρ = RA/L.
2. Ω × m² / m = Ω m.
3. A = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²; R = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.5 / 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.7 Ω.
4. ρ = 2.0 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ / 0.50 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
5. Y has a quarter of the area, so four times the resistance.
6. Length × 2 and area ÷ 2, so resistance × 4.
7. A = π × (1.0 × 10⁻³)² = 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ m²; R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 100 / 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.54 Ω.
8. A = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²; ρ = 2.5 × 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
9. It increases: the lattice ions vibrate more, so free electrons collide with them more often.
10. It decreases: heating releases more charge carriers, which outweighs the effect of greater vibration.
11. A material is superconducting when its resistivity is zero, which happens at and below its critical temperature.
12. Producing strong magnetic fields; reducing energy loss in the transmission of electric power.
