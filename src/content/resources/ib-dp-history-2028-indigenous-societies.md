**HL only.** This study is part of the Regional study for the Americas, which is examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument about the region and to reach your own judgement. This guide covers the first Americas study: Indigenous societies in the Americas, circa 750 to 1500. Every Paper 3 question on this study will make you refer to **one Indigenous society**. This guide uses the **Aztec (Mexica) Triple Alliance** and the **Inca (Tawantinsuyu)** as its main case studies, with short comparisons to the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) and the Maya. Choose one society, and know it in depth.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide gives six inquiry topics for this study. They are: how political authority was established and maintained; how economic structures worked; how social organisation worked; the impact of warfare; the role of culture; and the challenges societies faced. The four concepts help here. Questions usually ask about **cause and consequence** (why did a state grow?), **continuity and change** (how far did a system last?) or **significance** (how important was religion or warfare?). See the [guide to the four concepts](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) for how to use them.

## Key chronology

- About 1325: the Mexica found Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco.
- 1428: Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan form the Triple Alliance and defeat Azcapotzalco.
- About 1438 onward: Pachacuti begins the rapid expansion of the Inca state from Cusco.
- 1440 to 1469: reign of Moctezuma I, who extends Aztec conquests.
- 1502: Moctezuma II becomes tlatoani. The Aztec state is at its largest by 1519.
- Haudenosaunee Confederacy: formed before European contact. The date is debated, with estimates ranging from the 12th to the 15th century.

## Establishment and maintenance of political authority

**Aztec.** The Mexica were late arrivals in the Valley of Mexico. They rose by serving stronger neighbours as soldiers, then by joining the Triple Alliance. The ruler was the **tlatoani**, "speaker". He was chosen by a council of nobles from within the royal family, so succession was elective inside a ruling lineage. Conquered cities were usually left with their own rulers. They paid tribute and supplied warriors. Historians such as Ross Hassig call this a **hegemonic** empire: it controlled by threat and tribute rather than by garrisons and direct administration. The cost was fragility, because tributary cities could rebel when central power seemed weak.

**Inca.** The **Sapa Inca** was ruler, military leader and the child of the sun god Inti. Authority was **centralised** to an unusual degree. The empire was divided into four quarters (hence Tawantinsuyu, "land of four parts"), run by governors from Cusco. Local lords (**kurakas**) kept their positions if loyal, and were tied to the state by gifts and marriage. The Inca moved loyal and troublesome populations (**mitmaq**) to secure frontiers and break up local resistance. A network of roads, rest houses (**tambos**) and runners (**chaskis**) carried orders and information. Records were kept on knotted cords called **quipu**.

**Religion and warfare as legitimacy.** Both states made rulers sacred. The Aztec used the cult of Huitzilopochtli, sacrifice and large public ceremonies at the Templo Mayor to show that the state fed the gods and so kept the universe in motion. The Inca preserved the mummies of dead rulers, who were consulted and given estates and servants. Victory in war was proof of divine favour. A common error is to treat religion and politics as separate. For both societies they were one system.

**Law and codes of conduct.** Aztec law was written in pictographic form and enforced by judges, with harsh penalties for theft, drunkenness among commoners and adultery. Inca law relied on local officials and state inspectors, and on the idea that work and obedience were duties owed to the state. In both cases, rules supported a hierarchical order.

**Alliances.** The Aztec Triple Alliance shared conquered tribute among the three partners, with Tenochtitlan taking the largest share. The Haudenosaunee offer a contrast: five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca) joined a confederacy under the Great Law of Peace, and delegates met in a Grand Council. Power was shared and decisions depended on consensus. Clan mothers had the right to name and remove leaders.

## Economic structures

**Sedentary organisation, with limits.** Both the Aztec and the Inca depended on farming, but in different environments. The Aztec practised intensive agriculture in the lake basin, with **chinampas** (raised fields built in shallow water) giving several harvests a year, and maize, beans, squash and chilli as staples. The Inca farmed steep slopes using stone **terraces**, irrigation channels and a wide range of crops, including potatoes, maize and quinoa, and herded llamas and alpacas at altitude. Neither society used draught animals or the wheel for transport. Hunter-gatherer and mixed peoples lived around the edges of both empires, so the extent of sedentary life varied.

**Landholding and irrigation.** Among the Aztec, land was held by kin-based neighbourhood groups called **calpulli**, by nobles, and by temples. Among the Inca, land was divided in theory into fields for the state, for the religious cult and for the community, with **ayllu** kin groups working the community share. The state controlled major irrigation works and storehouses (**qollqa**) that held food and goods for soldiers and for times of poor harvest.

**Tribute and labour.** Aztec tribute came in cloth, maize, cacao, feathers, warrior costumes and other goods, and it fed the capital and its elite. Inca tribute was largely labour, called **mit'a**, rather than goods. People worked on roads, terraces, buildings and in the army for a set period each year. The anthropologist John Murra argued that this was based on **reciprocity**: the state gave food, drink and gifts in return for labour, and this made demands feel like traditional obligations. Earlier writers, such as Louis Baudin, had described the Inca state as a kind of "socialist empire". Most later historians reject that label because of the strict hierarchy and compulsion.

**Trade and markets.** The Aztec had large markets, notably at Tlatelolco, and professional long-distance merchants (**pochteca**) who also acted as spies. Cacao beans and cloth worked as a form of currency. The Inca had little in the way of markets or money, and relied more on state-managed redistribution. The Haudenosaunee traded furs, shell and goods along rivers and portage paths, and their villages combined farming with hunting.

## Social organisation

**Hierarchies and gender.** Aztec society was ranked: the ruler and nobles (**pipiltin**), commoners (**macehualtin**), dependent workers, and enslaved people (often debtors or criminals, and not an inherited status). Inca society had the Sapa Inca and royal kin at the top, then provincial lords, then ordinary households grouped in the ayllu. Women's work in both was vital, especially weaving, which produced the cloth used for tribute. Gender roles were complementary but unequal. Aztec girls and boys were trained for different roles, and women could be priestesses, midwives and traders, but not rulers. Among the Haudenosaunee, descent was **matrilineal**, and women controlled the longhouse and much of the farming. This made them unusually influential.

## The impact of warfare

Warfare shaped these societies. **Expansion** brought tribute, land, captives and prestige. For the Aztec, warriors rose in rank by taking captives, and ceremonial "flower wars" with rivals such as Tlaxcala are linked to the need for sacrificial victims and to training. For the Inca, conquest provided land and labour, and soldiers were organised and supplied through the road and storehouse system. Looting was less central to the Inca than the integration of conquered peoples. **Subjugation of other Indigenous peoples** was a basic fact of both empires, and this matters for the next study: many Indigenous peoples later allied with Europeans against the Aztec and Inca because they had been conquered and taxed. Be careful not to present Indigenous societies as one united group before 1500. They were rivals as well as neighbours.

## The role of culture

**Language** held communities together. Nahuatl and Quechua were spread by empire. **Religion** explained the order of the universe and the place of the ruler. The Aztec believed in a cycle of created worlds, and in the need to give the gods blood and hearts. The Inca worshipped Inti, Viracocha the creator, the earth mother Pachamama and sacred places called **huacas**. **The arts** included Aztec featherwork, sculpture, poetry and codices, and Inca textiles, stone masonry and metalwork. **The natural world** was part of religion for all of them: mountains, water and maize were sacred. Haudenosaunee ceremonies marked the farming year and honoured the "Three Sisters" crops of maize, beans and squash.

## Challenges faced by Indigenous societies

The guide asks you to consider four types of challenge. **Threats from other Indigenous societies**: the Aztec faced hostile neighbours such as Tlaxcala and the Tarascans; the Inca had to fight on distant frontiers. **Environmental factors**: droughts and frosts caused famine in the Valley of Mexico in the 1450s, and the Maya lowlands suffered long droughts around the 9th century. **Social factors, including overpopulation**: some historians argue that population pressure and over-farming stressed the Maya and the Valley of Mexico. This is debated and cannot be proved for every case. **Political weaknesses**: reliance on tribute and elite rulers meant that disputes over succession (as in the Inca after about 1527, when civil war broke out between two sons of Huayna Capac) or tributary revolts could weaken a state before any outside attack.

## Using arguments in Paper 3

Paper 3 rewards evaluation of perspectives. Useful arguments on this study include:

- **Terror or religion?** Was Aztec sacrifice mainly a religious duty, or also a political tool to frighten enemies? Most historians say both, and reject the older claim that it was driven by a shortage of protein.
- **Hegemony or direct rule?** Was the Aztec state a loose tribute network, or more organised than that?
- **Reciprocity or compulsion?** Was the Inca system based on shared obligation, or on state coercion?
- **Why did the Maya cities decline?** Drought, warfare, overpopulation or political failure. A strong answer weighs these rather than naming one.
- **The source problem.** Much of what we know comes from Spanish writers after the conquest, such as Bernardino de Sahagún and Garcilaso de la Vega, or from Indigenous writers working under Spanish rule, such as Guaman Poma. Their purposes shape their accounts. Archaeology, codices and oral tradition offer other evidence.

## Worked example: planning an essay

Question: "To what extent was warfare the most important factor in the maintenance of political authority in one Indigenous society?"

1. Choose your society (for example, the Aztec Triple Alliance).
2. State your line: warfare was central but not sufficient, because tribute, religion and alliances turned conquest into lasting authority.
3. Paragraph one: warfare. Conquest created tribute and prestige. Rulers were warriors.
4. Paragraph two: tribute and economic structures. Tribute fed Tenochtitlan and made cities dependent.
5. Paragraph three: religion and legitimacy. Sacrifice and ritual presented the ruler as essential to the cosmos.
6. Paragraph four: counter-argument. Heavy reliance on war created resentment, and rebellions show the limits of hegemony.
7. Evaluate perspectives (for example, hegemonic empire and terror versus religious explanations), then give a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about "the Aztecs and the Incas" together. The exam asks for one society, so go deep on one.
- Describing the Spanish conquest. The dates stop at 1500, and the question will be about the society before contact.
- Treating all Indigenous peoples as the same.
- Presenting sacrifice or tribute as a list of facts without saying what it shows about authority.
- Using a historian's name without saying what the argument is.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to learn all of the suggested societies?**
No. Choose one in depth. A second can help with comparison, but it is not required.

**Is the Spanish conquest part of this study?**
Not as a topic. It belongs to the next study. Use it only to explain what the society was like before it.
