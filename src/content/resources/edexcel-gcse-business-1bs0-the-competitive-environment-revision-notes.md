## The specification in brief

Understanding the competitive environment:
- strengths and weaknesses of competitors based on price, quality, location, product range and customer service;
- the impact of competition on business decision making.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Competitor | A business selling similar products to the same customers |
| Competitive environment | The number, strength and behaviour of the rivals a business faces |
| Strength | Something a competitor does better than others, or that customers value |
| Weakness | Something a competitor does poorly, giving others a chance to win its customers |
| Product range | The variety of products or services a business offers |
| Customer service | How a business treats customers before, during and after a sale |
| Competitor grid | A table scoring rivals on each factor to compare them |
| Market share | A business's sales as a percentage of total sales in the market (standard formula: business sales ÷ total market sales × 100) |

## Condensed recall

### The five factors: quick questions to ask
- **Price:** cheaper or dearer than others? Justified by anything extra?
- **Quality:** what do reviews and complaints say?
- **Location:** easy for target customers to reach? Sells online?
- **Product range:** enough choice for target customers?
- **Customer service:** helpful, quick, reliable after the sale?

### Patterns to expect
- Low price often comes with less range or service.
- High quality often comes with high price.
- A rival's weakness is another business's opportunity.

### Reading a competitor grid
1. Compare each factor across rivals, not just the totals.
2. Remember totals treat all factors as equal; customers may not.
3. Check market share: a rival with a low total but a large share shows what customers really value.

### How competition affects decisions
| Decision | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Enter the market? | Strong rivals raise the risk |
| Price | Match, undercut or sit above, depending on what else is offered |
| Quality and service | Improve where rivals are weak; raises costs |
| Location | Avoid rivals or locate near them where customers compare |
| Range | Fill gaps in rivals' ranges or specialise |
| Promotion | Stress how the business is different |
| Profit | Often squeezed by lower prices or higher costs |

### Market share mini example
A town's cafés sell 5,000 coffees a week. One café sells 900. 900 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 18%.

## Answer frame for competition questions

These are suggested sentence starters, not specification wording.

- "Rival X is strong on ... because ..., but weak on ..."
- "This weakness gives the business a chance to ..."
- "To compete, the business could ..., which would ... sales but raise costs because ..."
- "Rival X may respond by ..., so the business should ..."

## Exam traps

- Describing a factor without comparing rivals.
- Treating the highest-scoring rival as the market leader without checking market share.
- Assuming the answer to competition is always a price cut.
- Forgetting that competing on quality or service raises costs.
- Ignoring how rivals might react to a new entrant.
- Writing about competition in general when the case names specific rivals.

## Self-test

1. List the five factors the specification uses to judge competitors.
2. Define competitor.
3. Give one sign that a competitor is weak on customer service.
4. Why can a competitor be strong on price but weak on quality?
5. A market has total sales of £80,000 a month. A shop has sales of £12,000. Calculate its market share.
6. Why should a business not rely only on the totals in a competitor grid?
7. Give two decisions that competition can affect.
8. Why might a new business choose to locate near its competitors?
9. Explain one way strong competition can reduce a business's profit.
10. A rival has poor reviews but a 40% market share. What might this suggest?
11. How could a business find out about a rival's customer service?
12. Explain one way an established rival might respond to a new business.

### Answers

1. Price, quality, location, product range, customer service.
2. A business selling similar products to the same customers.
3. Any one: slow replies, complaints about rude staff, difficult returns, poor reviews about service.
4. Keeping prices low often means cheaper materials or fewer staff, which lowers quality.
5. £12,000 ÷ £80,000 × 100 = 15%.
6. The totals assume every factor matters equally, but customers may care much more about one, such as price.
7. Any two: price, quality, location, product range, customer service, promotion, whether to enter the market.
8. Customers come to the area to compare, so the business is seen by many potential buyers.
9. The business may have to cut its price to match rivals, which lowers the profit earned on each sale.
10. Customers probably choose on another factor, such as low price or a convenient location.
11. Any one: read online reviews; visit as a customer (mystery shopping); read social media comments.
12. It might cut its prices, increase promotion or improve its service to keep its customers.
