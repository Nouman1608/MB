The syllabus lists "factors (physical, social, economic, political) affecting agricultural land use and practices on farms", naming "the roles of irrigation, land tenure, the nature of demand and distance from markets, and agricultural technology"; "the concept of an agricultural system with inputs, throughputs, subsystems and output: one arable system and one pastoral system"; "intensive and extensive production and agricultural productivity"; and "issues in the intensification of agriculture and the extension of cultivation". ## What 11.1 asks you to know

| Syllabus wording | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Factors affecting land use and practices on farms | Why a farmer grows what they grow, and how they grow it |
| Irrigation, land tenure, demand and distance, technology | Four named factors you must be able to explain with examples |
| An agricultural system: inputs, throughputs, subsystems, output | Treating a farm as a system, for one arable and one pastoral example |
| Intensive and extensive production; productivity | Inputs per hectare, and output per unit of each input |
| Issues in intensification and extension of cultivation | The costs of getting more food from existing land or from new land |

"Land use" is what land is used for (rice, cattle); "practices" are the methods (hand weeding, terracing). Keep them distinct.

## Physical factors: setting the limits

Physical factors decide what is possible. Human factors then decide which possibility is chosen.

- **Temperature and growing season.** Grass and most temperate crops grow little below about 6°C, so the length of the season above this threshold limits cropping at high latitudes and altitudes. Temperature falls with height, so upland farms have shorter seasons than lowland farms nearby.
- **Precipitation.** What matters is effective rainfall after evaporation, and its reliability and timing.
- **Relief.** Steep slopes resist machinery and lose soil quickly, so they are more often grazed or terraced. Sun-facing slopes warm earlier.
- **Soils.** Depth, texture, drainage and acidity decide which crops thrive; thin upland soils suit grazing more than ploughing.

Physical limits can be stretched at a cost (irrigation, greenhouses, lime, terraces); whether a farmer can afford to do so depends on the human factors below.

## The four named factors in more depth

### Irrigation

| Method | How it works | Typical use | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flood or furrow | Water flows over or between crop rows by gravity | Rice, cotton, sugar cane | Much water lost to evaporation and seepage |
| Sprinkler or centre pivot | Pumped water sprayed from rotating arms | Large grain and fodder farms | High energy and capital costs |
| Drip (trickle) | Pipes release water at each plant's roots | Fruit, vegetables, high-value crops | Expensive to install; pipes clog |

Irrigation changes land use (water-demanding crops replace dry-land crops or grazing) and practices (two or three harvests a year, more fertiliser and labour).

### Land tenure

- **Owner-occupation** gives security to invest in drainage, terraces or orchards that take years to pay back.
- **Cash tenancy** fixes rent, so the tenant keeps any extra output, but short leases discourage long-term improvement.
- **Sharecropping** takes rent as a share of the harvest, so the tenant gains only part of any extra yield.
- **Communal tenure** gives a group shared rights, often to grazing; it suits mobile herding but makes individual investment hard.
- **State or collective farms** let the state set land use and targets.
- **Plantations** are large company-owned estates growing one export crop with hired labour.

Insecure tenure also limits credit, because land cannot be offered as security; this falls heavily on women who farm land without holding title.

### The nature of demand and distance from markets

**Subsistence** farmers grow mainly for their own household, so land use follows the family diet. **Commercial** farmers grow for sale, so land use follows prices. Most smallholders are in between, selling a surplus.

As incomes rise, diets usually shift from staple grains towards meat, dairy, fruit and vegetables, raising demand for animal feed and for perishable produce grown near cities or moved through a refrigerated cold chain.

**Distance from markets** matters through transport cost and time. Bulky, perishable or fragile goods lose value fast, so they are grown close to buyers unless technology shortens the journey. A remote farm is pushed towards products that are light, durable or able to walk to market, such as livestock.

### Agricultural technology

Technology includes machinery, high-yielding and drought-tolerant seed, agrochemicals, pumps, greenhouses, mobile phones that give price information, and GPS-guided precision tools. It raises output but needs capital, skills, fuel and spare parts, so it can widen gaps between farmers who can afford it and those who cannot.

## Agricultural systems: two more examples

A system answer must show flows, not a list: inputs pass through throughputs to outputs, and some outputs feed back.

**Arable: smallholder rain-fed maize and bean farming in East Africa**

- *Inputs:* seasonal rainfall, family labour, saved or bought seed, hand hoes or hired ox-ploughs, small amounts of fertiliser or manure.
- *Throughputs:* land preparation before the rains, planting maize with beans between the rows, weeding, harvesting, drying and storage.
- *Subsystems:* a crop subsystem and a small livestock subsystem; crop residues feed a cow or goats, whose manure returns to the plots.
- *Outputs:* maize and beans for the household, a surplus for the local market, residues, and income.
- *Feedback:* a good harvest pays for fertiliser next season; a drought forces livestock sales, weakening next season's inputs.

**Pastoral: nomadic pastoralism in the drylands of Kenya and Tanzania**

- *Inputs:* natural grassland, seasonal water sources, herds of cattle, goats and sheep, family labour and inherited knowledge of grazing routes.
- *Throughputs:* moving herds between wet-season and dry-season pastures, watering, protecting herds, milking.
- *Subsystems:* herd subsystem and grassland subsystem; movement allows grass to recover.
- *Outputs:* milk, meat, hides, animals sold or exchanged, and social status, since herds also act as savings.
- *System stress:* when grazing land is fenced or farmed, herds are confined, grass cannot recover and the system loses flexibility.

## Intensive, extensive and productivity

**Intensity** describes inputs per hectare. **Productivity** describes output per unit of an input. Three measures are useful:

- **Land productivity (yield):** output per hectare.
- **Labour productivity:** output per worker or per hour worked.
- **Energy ratio:** energy in the food produced divided by the energy put in (fuel, fertiliser, machinery, labour). Low-input traditional systems often have a higher energy ratio than highly mechanised ones, even though their yield is lower.

### Worked example

*Two fictional farms grow rice. Farm P is a 2 ha hand-cultivated holding with 5 workers producing 9 tonnes. Farm Q is a 400 ha mechanised farm with 4 workers producing 2 400 tonnes.*

- Yield: P = 9 ÷ 2 = **4.5 t per ha**; Q = 2 400 ÷ 400 = **6.0 t per ha**.
- Labour productivity: P = 9 ÷ 5 = **1.8 t per worker**; Q = 2 400 ÷ 4 = **600 t per worker**.

Q leads on both measures, so the generalisation that smallholders win per hectare does not always hold: irrigated, fertilised commercial rice can out-yield poorly supplied plots. Read the data first.

## Issues in intensification and the extension of cultivation

**Intensification** (more output from the same land):

- soil compaction from heavy machinery, and loss of soil organic matter under continuous cropping;
- monoculture, which raises pest and disease risk and drives pesticide resistance;
- groundwater depletion and salinisation where irrigation outpaces recharge or drainage;
- eutrophication of rivers and lakes from fertiliser and manure;
- antibiotic use and welfare concerns in livestock units;
- debt for farmers who borrow to buy inputs.

**Extension of cultivation** (more land brought into farming):

- clearance of forest, wetland or grassland, releasing carbon and destroying habitat;
- ploughing of marginal semi-arid land, which risks soil erosion and land degradation in dry years;
- loss of dry-season grazing for pastoralists, causing conflict between herders and farmers;
- large land acquisitions by investors that displace local users with weak tenure.

The two are linked: if intensification fails on degraded land, farmers move on to new land.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how land tenure can influence farming practices. [5]*

> Land tenure is the way land is held, such as ownership or tenancy (**definition**). An owner-occupier has secure rights, so can invest in terraces or drainage that pay back over many years (**security leads to investment**). A sharecropper gives part of the harvest as rent, so gains only part of any extra yield and has less reason to buy fertiliser (**effect on inputs**). Where inheritance divides land, holdings fragment into small plots that are hard to mechanise (**fragmentation**). Tenants without title often cannot borrow, because land cannot be used as security (**credit**).

## Common mistakes

- Listing physical factors without saying what each one limits.
- Treating land use and farming practices as the same thing.
- Writing a "system" with no feedback or subsystem link.
- Calling a farm intensive because it is small.
- Quoting yield without units, or comparing yield on one farm with output per worker on another.
- Treating intensification only as a success; the syllabus asks for its issues.

## Exam technique for Paper 4

- **Describe** a resource: quote figures, give the pattern and an anomaly.
- **Explain**: link each factor to its effect on land use or practice ("because…, so…").
- **Suggest**: apply 11.1 ideas to an unfamiliar farm; every reason must fit the resource.
- **Assess**, **Evaluate**, **How far**: weigh factors, vary the judgement by scale or type of farm, and conclude. Evaluation carries a large share of Paper 4 marks, so judge throughout.
