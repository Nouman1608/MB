Quick recall for section 1.3, Classification of businesses, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Worked examples are in the [classification of businesses study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-classification-of-businesses/); exam-style questions are in the [classification of businesses practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-classification-of-businesses-practice/). All figures are invented.

## The specification's definitions (1.3.1)

| Sector | Specification wording | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting raw materials from the earth | Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil drilling |
| Secondary | Converting raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods | Manufacturing, food processing, construction, refining |
| Tertiary | Provision of a wide variety of services | Retail, transport, banking, insurance, tourism, education, repairs |

Learn the wording: "define" questions reward it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Raw material | A natural resource in its basic state, such as crude oil, timber or wheat |
| Semi-finished good | Processed material that becomes an input for another business, such as planks or steel sheet |
| Finished good | A product ready for the final user |
| Service | An activity done for a customer, with nothing new made |
| Chain of production | The stages a product passes through from primary to tertiary |

## Classify the activity, not the product

- Wheat farm: primary. Flour mill: secondary. Bakery: secondary. Bread shop: tertiary.
- Fish farm: primary (harvesting a natural resource).
- House builder: secondary.
- Car repair garage: tertiary.
- Online retailer: tertiary.
- Restaurant: prepares food (secondary) and serves it (tertiary); usually treated as mainly tertiary.

## The chain of production

Primary → secondary → tertiary → customer, with tertiary services (transport, banking, insurance, advertising) supporting every stage.

Example: cotton farm (primary) → spinning mill and garment factory (secondary) → shipping firm and clothes shop (tertiary).

A problem at one stage spreads along the chain: a poor cotton harvest raises the mill's costs, which raises the factory's costs, which raises shop prices or squeezes margins.

## What each sector needs and risks

| Sector | Key needs | Key risks |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Land or natural resource; equipment | Weather, disease, resource running out, price swings |
| Secondary | Machines, materials, space, labour | Material and energy costs, breakdowns, cheaper rivals |
| Tertiary | Customers, skilled staff, access | Changing tastes, competition, reputation |

## Working with sector data

- Share (%) = sector figure ÷ total × 100.
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
- Check that shares add to 100%.

Reminder: revenues of 30, 90 and 80 (millions) give a total of 200 and shares of 15%, 45% and 40%.

## Applying it in answers

- Outline: sector, then one consequence for this business.
- Analyse: follow a change along the chain to the business named.
- Evaluate: weigh the benefit of operating in several sectors (control of supply and outlets) against the cost and risk of running very different activities.

## Businesses in more than one sector

- Name each activity and its sector, then say which is the main one, using revenue or what the case says the business does most.
- Reminder: a wine estate grows grapes (primary), makes wine (secondary) and runs tours and a shop (tertiary).
- Benefits of covering several sectors: control over supplies and their quality; profit kept from each stage; direct contact with customers.
- Drawbacks: very different skills and equipment are needed in each sector; more money is tied up; a problem in one part can affect the whole business.

## How sector shapes a decision

| Decision | Primary example | Secondary example | Tertiary example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location | Where the resource is | Near materials or transport links | Near customers, or online |
| Main costs | Equipment, land | Machinery, materials, energy | Staff, premises |
| Main risk | Weather and prices | Breakdowns and input costs | Changing tastes and competition |

## Exam traps

- Calling a shop that sells a product "secondary" because the product is manufactured.
- Putting construction in the tertiary sector.
- Forgetting semi-finished goods are secondary output.
- Classifying a multi-sector business as only one sector without explanation.
- Shares that do not add to 100%, or a percentage change worked from the new figure.
- Naming a sector with no link to the business's decision.

## Self-test

1. Define the secondary sector using the specification's wording.
2. Classify a quarry.
3. Classify an insurance company.
4. Classify a business that builds bridges.
5. Give one example of a semi-finished good.
6. Name the sector of a business that grows tomatoes and the sector of the business that makes them into sauce.
7. Give one risk especially faced by primary businesses.
8. A firm's revenues are primary 50, secondary 150, tertiary 300 (millions). Calculate the secondary share.
9. Jobs in the secondary sector fell from 40,000 to 34,000. Calculate the percentage change.
10. Explain how a strike at a car factory could affect a tertiary business.
11. Why might a chocolate maker own cocoa farms?
12. What does "identify" ask you to do, according to the specification?

### Answers

1. Converting raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods.
2. Primary.
3. Tertiary.
4. Secondary.
5. Planks, steel sheet, flour or yarn (any valid example).
6. Primary; secondary.
7. Bad weather, disease or the resource running out (any one).
8. 150 ÷ 500 × 100 = 30%.
9. (34,000 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = −15%.
10. Fewer cars are made, so car showrooms (tertiary) have fewer cars to sell, and their sales and revenue fall.
11. To control the supply and quality of its main raw material.
12. Select the correct answer from a graph or table of data.
