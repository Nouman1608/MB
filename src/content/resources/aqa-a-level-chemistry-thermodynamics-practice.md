All enthalpy values are in kJ mol⁻¹ and all absolute entropies are in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Take Mr(H₂O) = 18.0.

## Questions

**1.** Define each term.

**(a)** Enthalpy of atomisation **[2]**
**(b)** Enthalpy of hydration **[2]**

**2.** Write an equation, with state symbols, for each of these enthalpy changes.

**(a)** The first electron affinity of oxygen **[1]**
**(b)** The second ionisation energy of strontium **[1]**
**(c)** The lattice enthalpy of formation of potassium oxide, K₂O **[1]**

**3.** Use these data for sodium oxide, Na₂O.
ΔfH⦵(Na₂O) –414; ΔatH⦵(Na) +107; first IE(Na) +496; O=O bond enthalpy +498; first EA(O) –141;
second EA(O) +798.

**(a)** Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation of Na₂O. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the second electron affinity of oxygen is endothermic. **[2]**

**4.** Use the data to calculate the second ionisation energy of calcium.
ΔfH⦵(CaO) –635; ΔatH⦵(Ca) +178; first IE(Ca) +590; ½ × O=O bond enthalpy +249; first EA(O) –141;
second EA(O) +798; lattice enthalpy of formation of CaO –3454. **[3]**

**5.** The table gives lattice enthalpies of formation for two ionic compounds, X and Y.

| Compound | From a Born–Haber cycle | From the perfect ionic model |
|---|---|---|
| X | –688 | –684 |
| Y | –916 | –834 |

**(a)** State two assumptions of the perfect ionic model. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce which compound has more covalent character. Explain your answer. **[3]**

**6.** This question is about dissolving Group 2 chlorides.

**(a)** The lattice enthalpy of dissociation of MgCl₂ is +2524. ΔhydH(Mg²⁺) = –1921 and
ΔhydH(Cl⁻) = –364. Calculate the enthalpy of solution of MgCl₂. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the hydration enthalpy of Mg²⁺ is more exothermic than that of Na⁺. **[2]**
**(c)** ΔsolH(CaCl₂) = –81 and its lattice enthalpy of dissociation is +2258. Use ΔhydH(Cl⁻) from
part (a) to calculate the hydration enthalpy of Ca²⁺. **[2]**

**7.** For sodium bromide, ΔsolH = –1, the lattice enthalpy of formation is –747 and
ΔhydH(Na⁺) = –406. Calculate the enthalpy of hydration of the bromide ion. **[3]**

**8.** State whether ΔS is positive or negative for each change. Give a reason in each case.

**(a)** H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) **[1]**
**(b)** N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) **[1]**
**(c)** NH₄Cl(s) → NH₃(g) + HCl(g) **[1]**

**9.** Ethene reacts with hydrogen: C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), ΔH⦵ = –137.
Absolute entropies: C₂H₄(g) 220; H₂(g) 131; C₂H₆(g) 230.

**(a)** Calculate ΔS for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔG at 298 K. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the temperature above which the reaction is no longer feasible. **[2]**

**10.** Zinc oxide can be reduced by carbon: ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g), ΔH⦵ = +238.
Absolute entropies: ZnO(s) 44; C(s) 6; Zn(s) 42; CO(g) 198.

**(a)** Explain, without calculation, why ΔS is positive. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔS for the reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the lowest temperature at which the reaction is feasible. **[3]**
**(d)** Calculate ΔG at 1500 K and state what it shows. **[2]**

**11.** A straight-line graph of ΔG (kJ mol⁻¹) against T (K) for a reaction passes through
(400, –12) and (700, –36).

**(a)** Use the graph to find ΔS, in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Find ΔH. **[2]**
**(c)** State the temperature range over which the reaction is feasible. **[1]**

**12.** A student finds the entropy change for vaporising water. A 2.20 kW kettle keeps water boiling at
373 K. In 90 s, the mass of water falls by 82.0 g.

**(a)** Explain why ΔG = 0 for H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) at 373 K. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔH for the vaporisation of water in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate ΔS for the vaporisation of water. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain whether your values from (b) and (c) are likely to be too high or too low. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous atoms is formed [1] from the element in its
standard state [1].
**(b)** The enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous ions [1] dissolves in water to form 1 mole of
aqueous ions [1].
*Examiner insight:* "1 mole of gaseous atoms" is the key idea in (a); a definition based on breaking
one mole of bonds describes bond enthalpy instead.

**2. (a)** O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g) [1]
**(b)** Sr⁺(g) → Sr²⁺(g) + e⁻ [1]
**(c)** 2K⁺(g) + O²⁻(g) → K₂O(s) [1]
*Examiner insight:* the second ionisation energy starts from the 1+ ion, not the atom; writing
Sr(g) → Sr²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ describes the sum of two ionisation energies.

**3. (a)** Steps to gaseous ions: 2 × 107 = 214 and 2 × 496 = 992 [1]
½ × 498 = 249 for one O atom; sum = 214 + 992 + 249 – 141 + 798 = +2112 [1]
Lattice enthalpy of formation = ΔfH – sum = –414 – 2112 [1]
= **–2526 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The electron is being added to an O⁻ ion, which is already negative [1]. Energy is needed to
overcome the repulsion between the ion and the incoming electron [1].
*Examiner insight:* doubling only one of the sodium steps is the usual slip; label each term with its
multiplier so the working can be followed.

**4.** Hess's law: –635 = 178 + 590 + IE₂ + 249 – 141 + 798 – 3454 [1]
IE₂ = –635 – 178 – 590 – 249 + 141 – 798 + 3454 [1]
= **+1145 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* write the full Hess's law equation before rearranging; a sign error made while
moving terms is then easy to spot.

**5. (a)** Ions are perfect spheres with evenly spread charge [1]. Bonding is purely electrostatic,
with no covalent character [1].
**(b)** **Y** has more covalent character [1]. Its Born–Haber value is 82 kJ mol⁻¹ more exothermic
than the model value, compared with only 4 kJ mol⁻¹ for X [1]. The cation in Y polarises the anion,
distorting its electron cloud and adding attraction the model ignores [1].
*Examiner insight:* the comparison needs the direction, not just the size of the gap: the experimental
value is *more exothermic*, so extra bonding is present.

**6. (a)** ΔsolH = lattice dissociation + ΔhydH(Mg²⁺) + 2 × ΔhydH(Cl⁻) [1]
= 2524 – 1921 – 728 [1]
= **–125 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and a smaller radius than Na⁺ [1], so it attracts the δ– oxygen
atoms of water molecules more strongly [1].
**(c)** –81 = 2258 + ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) + 2(–364), so ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) = –81 – 2258 + 728 [1]
= **–1611 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* using ΔhydH(Cl⁻) once instead of twice gives +239 in (a), with the wrong sign as
well as the wrong size.

**7.** Lattice enthalpy of dissociation = +747 [1]
–1 = 747 – 406 + ΔhydH(Br⁻) [1]
ΔhydH(Br⁻) = **–342 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the data give lattice *formation*, so reverse the sign before you build the
solution cycle.

**8. (a)** Positive: a liquid becomes a gas, so disorder increases [1].
**(b)** Negative: 4 moles of gas become 2 moles of gas [1].
**(c)** Positive: a solid forms 2 moles of gas [1].
*Examiner insight:* a sign with no reason is incomplete; refer to states or to moles of gas.

**9. (a)** ΔS = 230 – (220 + 131) [1] = **–121 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔG = –137 – 298 × (–0.121) [1] = **–101 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** T = ΔH/ΔS = –137 / –0.121 [1] = **1132 K** [1]
*Examiner insight:* with ΔH and ΔS both negative, the reaction is feasible *below* 1132 K; reversing
"above" and "below" turns a correct number into a wrong conclusion.

**10. (a)** A gas (CO) forms from solids only [1].
**(b)** ΔS = (42 + 198) – (44 + 6) [1] = **+190 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** At the limit ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH/ΔS [1]
= 238 / 0.190 [1]
= **1253 K** [1]
**(d)** ΔG = 238 – 1500 × 0.190 = **–47 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. ΔG is negative, so the reaction is feasible
at 1500 K [1].
*Examiner insight:* dividing 238 by 190 gives 1.25 K; check that a "feasible above" temperature for
a reduction like this is a high temperature in kelvin.

**11. (a)** Gradient = (–36 – (–12)) / (700 – 400) = –0.080 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ [1]
ΔS = –gradient = **+80 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔH = ΔG + TΔS = –12 + 400 × 0.080 [1] = **+20 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Feasible at **250 K and above** (ΔG ≤ 0 when T ≥ 20/0.080) [1]
*Examiner insight:* the gradient is –ΔS, so a line sloping downwards means ΔS is positive; quoting
–80 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ is a common sign error.

**12. (a)** At the boiling point, liquid and vapour are in equilibrium [1].
**(b)** Energy = 2200 × 90 = 198 000 J = 198 kJ [1]
n(H₂O) = 82.0 / 18.0 = 4.56 mol [1]
ΔH = 198 / 4.56 = **+43.5 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** ΔS = ΔH/T = 43 500 / 373 = **+117 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** Too high [1]: some of the energy heats the kettle and surroundings instead of vaporising water,
so the energy per mole vaporised is overestimated [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (c) keep ΔH in J mol⁻¹ to get ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; dividing 43.5 by 373 gives
0.117, which needs kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ as its unit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining atomisation per mole of bonds broken instead of per mole of gaseous atoms formed.
- Writing a second ionisation energy from the atom rather than from the 1+ ion.
- Doubling only some of the steps when the formula contains two of a particle (Na₂O, MgCl₂).
- Using lattice enthalpy of formation in a solution cycle without changing its sign.
- Giving the sign of ΔS with no reason.
- Mixing J and kJ in ΔG = ΔH – TΔS.
- Stating the temperature but not whether the reaction is feasible above or below it.
- Reading the gradient of a ΔG–T graph as +ΔS.
- Missing units on ΔS (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹) and signs on enthalpy values.
