## The task in one box

| Feature | Section A |
|---|---|
| Text | One poem you have not studied, printed on the paper |
| Task | One essay on the poem |
| Marks | 20 (about 22% of Paper 1) |
| Assessment objective | AO2 only: language, form and structure, and their effects |
| Recommended time | 35 minutes |
| What you need to know beforehand | Nothing about the poem: only the method |

## The five-step method

1. **Sense:** who speaks, to whom, about what; mood at start and end; read the title.
2. **Turn:** find where the poem shifts (a "but", a tense change, a short final line).
3. **Annotate:** six to eight tiny details, each with its effect written beside it.
4. **Overview:** one sentence answering the question and naming the poem's movement.
5. **Plan and write:** three or four paragraphs in the poem's order, each mixing language, form and
   structure; leave two or three minutes to reread.

## Key terms

| Term | What it is | What it can do |
|---|---|---|
| Enjambment | A sentence running over a line or stanza break | Creates momentum, or isolates the word left at the line's end |
| Caesura | A pause inside a line, usually punctuation | Halts the reader; marks hesitation or a sudden realisation |
| End-stopping | A line that finishes with punctuation | Feels controlled, certain or final |
| Volta | A turn in thought or tone | Signals a change of mind; often the key to the argument |
| Sibilance | Repeated "s" sounds | Hushed, secretive or sinister effects |
| Assonance | Repeated vowel sounds | Slows the pace; mournful or musical tone |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting ideas side by side | Sharpens a contrast the poem is exploring |
| Paradox | A statement that seems to contradict itself | Captures a complicated feeling |
| Refrain | A repeated line or phrase | Emphasis, obsession, or a sense of return |
| Persona | A speaker who is not the poet | Lets the poem show a viewpoint the reader can judge |

## What an effective sentence contains

**Method + short quotation + effect + link to the question.** For example: "The single-line final
stanza leaves the speaker's decision hanging, so the reader feels her uncertainty."

## Traps

- Paraphrasing the poem rather than analysing it.
- Quoting whole lines instead of the word that matters.
- Naming a rhyme scheme or metre without saying what it does.
- Guessing about the poet's life or period: AO2 credits analysis of the text in front of you.
- Running out of time before the ending, which often changes the meaning.
- Overrunning 35 minutes and squeezing Section C, which is worth twice as much.

## How Section A helps the rest of Paper 1

The same AO2 skills earn marks in Section B, where the anthology poems are compared, so regular unseen

## Self-test

1. Which assessment objective is Section A marked on, and what three areas does it cover?
2. How many marks is Section A worth and how long does the paper recommend?
3. What is the difference between enjambment and caesura?
4. Why is "the poet uses alliteration" a weak sentence on its own?
5. Where in a poem should you look first for the "turn"?
6. Why is speculating about the poet's life unhelpful here?

**Answers:** 1. AO2: language, form and structure, and the meanings and effects they create.
2. 20 marks; 35 minutes. 3. Enjambment runs a sentence over a line break; caesura is a pause within
a line. 4. It names a feature without quoting precisely or explaining the effect on meaning or the
reader. 5. At connectives such as "but" or "yet", changes of tense or pronoun, stanza changes and the
final lines. 6. The poem is unseen and marks are for analysing the text on the page, so guesswork adds
nothing and can mislead you.
