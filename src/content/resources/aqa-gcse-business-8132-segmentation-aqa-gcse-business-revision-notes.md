## Core recall

**Segmentation** = dividing a market into groups of customers who share a characteristic and have similar needs. The business picks a **target market** (one or more segments) and builds a marketing mix for it.

The specification names **four** criteria: **gender, age, location, income**. Memory hook: **G**ood **A**dverts **L**ook **I**nformed.

### The four criteria

| Criterion | Example segments | How a business might use it |
|---|---|---|
| Gender | Men, women | Separate clothing or toiletry ranges; different packaging and advertising placement |
| Age | Children, teenagers, 25 to 44, retired | Product versions by life stage; social media for younger buyers, printed press for some older readers |
| Location | Country, region, town, urban or rural | Stock suited to local needs; choice of where to open or deliver |
| Income | Low, middle, high | Basic, standard and premium versions at different prices |

Criteria can be combined, such as "city-dwelling 25 to 44 year olds on high incomes", to target more precisely.

### Three steps

1. Divide the market.
2. Choose the target segment(s): size, spending, growth, competition.
3. Design the marketing mix for each.

### Why segment

| Benefit | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Mix fits the group's needs | More of the group buy |
| Promotion aimed at likely buyers | Less wasted spending |
| Unmet needs in a segment become visible | Market opportunities to fill |
| Customers feel the product is for them | Higher sales and loyalty |
| Price set for each segment | Premium prices where incomes allow; value prices where needed |

### Drawbacks

- Separate products and promotion for each segment raise costs.
- A narrow segment can limit total sales.
- Members of a segment do not all behave alike, so assumptions can be wrong.
- Segments change, so research must be repeated.

## Working with segment data

| What you are asked | Method |
|---|---|
| Total customers | Add the segments |
| Share of customers | Segment ÷ total × 100 |
| Revenue from a segment | Customers × average spend |
| Share of revenue | Segment revenue ÷ total revenue × 100 |
| Change in a segment | New − old; percentage change = change ÷ old × 100 |

Quick example: a café has 1,500 customers aged under 25 spending £4 each and 900 aged 25 and over spending £7 each. Revenue: 1,500 × £4 = £6,000 and 900 × £7 = £6,300. The smaller segment earns more.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Market | All the potential customers for a product |
| Segmentation | Dividing a market into groups with shared characteristics |
| Market segment | One group within a market whose members share a characteristic |
| Target market | The segment or segments a business aims its product at |
| Gender segmentation | Grouping customers as men or women |
| Age segmentation | Grouping customers by age band |
| Location segmentation | Grouping customers by where they live |
| Income segmentation | Grouping customers by how much they earn |
| Market opportunity | A gap where customer needs are not being met |

## Exam traps

- Segmentation is the dividing; the target market is the segment chosen.
- Use the four criteria the specification names.
- Explain how the criterion changes the marketing mix; naming it scores little.
- Do not stereotype: products can appeal across genders and ages.
- In data questions, the biggest segment by customers may not be the biggest by revenue.
- Always show working for shares and percentages.

## Self-test

1. Define segmentation.
2. State the four criteria for segmentation named in the specification.
3. What is a target market?
4. Give an example of a product segmented by age.
5. Explain how a supermarket might use income segmentation.
6. How could a sportswear shop use location segmentation?
7. State two benefits of segmentation.
8. Explain one drawback of segmentation.
9. A cinema has 4,000 customers: 1,400 are under 18. What percentage is under 18?
10. 600 customers spend £12 each and 250 spend £30 each. Which group earns more revenue, and by how much?
11. Why might a business combine criteria when describing its target market?
12. Why does segmentation need repeating over time?

### Answers

1. Dividing a market into groups of customers who share a characteristic and have similar needs.
2. Gender, age, location and income.
3. The segment or segments a business chooses to aim its product at.
4. For example, a games console with a children's version, or toothpaste for children and adults.
5. It could offer a value own-brand range for lower-income customers and a premium range for higher-income customers, priced to suit each.
6. For example, stocking surfing and swimwear in coastal towns and hiking gear in towns near hills.
7. Any two of: a better-targeted mix, less wasted promotion, spotting market opportunities, higher sales and loyalty, suitable pricing.
8. For example, making separate versions and campaigns for each segment raises costs, which may cut profit.
9. 1,400 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **35%**.
10. 600 × £12 = £7,200; 250 × £30 = £7,500. The second group earns **£300** more.
11. A combination describes the customers more precisely, so promotion and product can be aimed accurately.
12. Segments change in size and needs, for example as incomes or tastes change.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know lifestyle segmentation for AQA GCSE Business (8132)?**
The specification names gender, age, location and income. Focus your answers on these four.

**How do I use segmentation in a marketing mix answer?**
State the target segment from the case, then justify each of price, product, promotion and place with a feature of that segment.
