## Definition and the ILO measure

**Unemployment** means people who are able and willing to work, and are looking for work, but cannot find a job. Two parts matter: the person wants work, and no work is available to them. A student in full-time study or a retired person is not unemployed.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) measure turns this into a survey test. A person counts as unemployed if all three of these are true:

1. They have no paid work in the reference week.
2. They are available to start work within about two weeks.
3. They have actively looked for work in the last four weeks or so.

Everyone of working age who is not employed and does not pass the test is **economically inactive** (for example, full-time students, carers and the long-term sick). The ILO measure uses the same test in every country, so rates can be compared.

**Labour force** = employed + unemployed.

**Unemployment rate** = (unemployed ÷ labour force) × 100.

### Worked example 1: the rate

Country A (illustrative), in thousands of people:

| Group | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Employed | 18,600 | 18,250 |
| Unemployed (ILO) | 1,400 | 1,750 |
| Economically inactive | 6,000 | 6,000 |

Year 1 labour force = 18,600 + 1,400 = 20,000 thousand.
Year 1 rate = 1,400 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **7%**.

Year 2 labour force = 18,250 + 1,750 = 20,000 thousand.
Year 2 rate = 1,750 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **8.75%**.

The number of unemployed rose by 1,750 − 1,400 = 350 thousand. As a percentage change: 350 ÷ 1,400 × 100 = **25%**. The rate rose by 8.75 − 7 = **1.75 percentage points**. Keep these two apart: 25% describes the change in the number, and 1.75 points describes the change in the rate.

Dividing by the wrong base is a common slip. Using the whole working-age group of 26,000 thousand gives 1,400 ÷ 26,000 × 100 = 5.4%, which is wrong because the inactive are not in the labour force.

## The five types

| Type | What causes it | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Fall in total demand in a downturn or recession | Spread across many industries; rises and falls with the cycle |
| Structural | Decline of an industry or a skills mismatch, for example from new technology or overseas competition | Concentrated in certain industries or regions; can last years |
| Seasonal | Firms need fewer staff in the quiet months of the year | Predictable; affects tourism, farming, construction |
| Voluntary | A person chooses not to accept jobs at the wages on offer | Worker is able to work but turns down work |
| Frictional | Time taken to move between jobs | Short; people are between jobs or just entered the labour force |

**Cyclical chain:** households and firms cut spending, so firms sell less, so they need fewer workers, so they make redundancies. Falling incomes then cut spending again.

**Structural chain:** a factory closes because cheaper imports win its customers. The sacked workers have skills for that industry only. Vacancies exist elsewhere, but in other skills or other towns, so the unemployment lasts. This is the hardest type to solve because workers must retrain or move.

**Frictional chain:** a worker leaves a job to look for a better one. A graduate starts to apply for first jobs. Vacancies and job seekers exist at the same time, but matching takes time. Some frictional unemployment exists in every economy.

### Worked example 2: seasonal pattern

A seaside resort (illustrative) has a labour force of 4,000.

| Month | Unemployed | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| January | 520 | 520 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 13% |
| April | 240 | 6% |
| July | 80 | 2% |
| October | 200 | 5% |

The gap between January and July is 13 − 2 = 11 percentage points. The four-month average is (520 + 240 + 80 + 200) ÷ 4 = 260 people, or 6.5%. The pattern repeats each year, so the cause is seasonal. A fall from January to July is not evidence that the town's economy is improving.

### Worked example 3: classify the case

- A bank clerk sacked after the bank closes branches and moves services online, with no digital skills: structural.
- A farm picker out of work between harvests: seasonal.
- An accountant who turns down a job because the pay is lower than she wants, and waits for a better offer: voluntary (and partly frictional).
- A shop assistant made redundant when national spending drops in a recession: cyclical.
- A graduate looking for a first job: frictional.

## The consequences

### Output and scarce resources

Unemployed workers are an unused factor of production. Resources are scarce, so leaving labour idle wastes them: the economy produces less than it could, so actual output is below productive potential. In PPF terms, the economy sits inside the frontier. Long-term unemployment makes it worse, because skills fade and firms may prefer recent workers, so labour is wasted even when jobs return. Machines and factories may also sit idle if firms cut production.

**Worked example 4:** Country A's extra 350 thousand unemployed each would have produced an average of 24,000 currency units a year. Lost output = 350,000 × 24,000 = 8,400,000,000 units, or 8,400 million. This assumes they would have matched average output, which is a simplification.

### Poverty

Wages are the main income for most households. Unemployment benefit is usually lower than the wage, so household income falls. A household with two earners on 1,800 and 1,200 a month has 3,000. If the second earner loses the job and receives a benefit of 500, income becomes 1,800 + 500 = 2,300. The fall is 700 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = **23.3%**. Where savings are small, that gap pushes families towards poverty, and the risk grows the longer the spell lasts.

### Government spending on benefits and tax revenue

The government pays more benefits and collects less tax at the same time. Take a worker on a gross wage of 20,000 a year in Country A.

| Item | Employed | Unemployed |
|---|---|---|
| Income tax (15% of wage) | 3,000 | 0 |
| Income after tax / benefit | 17,000 | 6,000 (benefit) |
| Spending | 80% of 17,000 = 13,600 | 6,000 |
| Sales tax (10% of spending) | 1,360 | 600 |

Cost to the government of one more unemployed person = benefit 6,000 + lost income tax 3,000 + lost sales tax (1,360 − 600 = 760) = **9,760**.

For 50,000 more unemployed: 50,000 × 9,760 = **488 million**. Of this, benefits are 300 million and lost tax is 188 million. Households also spend (13,600 − 6,000) × 50,000 = 380 million less. That money is not available for schools, hospitals or other public services, or must be found by borrowing or higher taxes.

### Consumer and business confidence

Households fear for their own jobs, so they save more and spend less. Falling spending cuts sales. Firms then expect weak demand, postpone investment and may hold back hiring. This reduces demand for workers and can worsen unemployment, so cyclical unemployment can feed on itself.

### Society

Long spells can bring stress, ill health and loss of self-esteem. Unemployment may be concentrated in some regions or groups, such as young people, which widens inequality. Pressure on community services can grow, and in some areas crime may rise. These effects are harder to measure than output or tax.

## Evaluating the impact

A good evaluation weighs the consequences. Consider:

- **Type.** Frictional and seasonal spells are short and cost little. Structural and long-term unemployment cost much more.
- **Size and length.** A rise of 1.75 points hurts more if it lasts several years.
- **Who is affected.** One region or age group carrying most of the burden is a bigger problem for society.
- **Time frame.** Lost output is immediate, while lost skills damage the future.
- **Other conditions.** Generous benefits soften poverty but raise the government's bill.

### A model evaluation chain

Question: assess the impact of a rise in unemployment on a country's government. A strong answer runs: benefit spending rises (cause), income tax and sales tax fall (cause), so the budget moves towards deficit (effect), so the government must borrow, cut other spending or raise taxes (consequence). Then weigh it: the cost is largest if the rise is long-lasting and structural, and smaller if it is short and seasonal. Finish with a supported judgement, such as "the effect on the budget is serious mainly because the unemployed are many and out of work for long".

## Common errors

- Giving a one-line list with no chain. State the cause, the effect and the next effect.
- Treating all unemployment as bad. Some frictional unemployment is normal.
- Mixing up the percentage change in the number with the change in the rate in points.
- Including the economically inactive in the labour force.
- Saying the unemployed "do not want to work" for every type.
