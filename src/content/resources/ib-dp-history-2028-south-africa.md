**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 6, "Developments in South Africa (1867 to 2020)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The skill is evaluating arguments: you must understand a claim, weigh perspectives and reach a judgement. The six inquiry topics are: the Mineral Revolution; the South African War; apartheid; resistance to apartheid; international opposition; and the end of apartheid. The subject involves racial violence and injustice, so write with precision and respect.

## 1. Impact of the Mineral Revolution (from 1867)

The discovery of diamonds near Kimberley (from 1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) transformed a farming region into an industrial economy. Cecil Rhodes consolidated the diamond fields in De Beers, and Johannesburg grew from a camp into a city.

- **Social and economic effects of mining.** Foreign capital, railways and towns grew. The Transvaal government under Paul Kruger gained revenue but feared foreign influence.
- **Migration of white miners and growth of Boer nationalism.** Thousands of foreign miners (Uitlanders) arrived in the Transvaal. Kruger's government refused them easy voting rights, and many Boers saw British capital as a threat to independence.
- **Migration of Africans to mines and cities.** Men left rural areas for work, often on contracts, in compounds. Taxes (such as the Cape's Glen Grey Act of 1894) pushed people into wage work.
- **Exploitation of African labour and the beginnings of segregation.** Pass controls, the compound system, low wages and job colour bars reserved skilled work for whites. The Natives Land Act of 1913 limited African land ownership to reserves, about 7 per cent of the land at first.

**Argument to weigh:** "The Mineral Revolution caused segregation." Support: labour demand created pass laws and compounds. Challenge: racial inequality in law and land already existed before the discoveries.

## 2. Outbreak and impact of the South African War (1899 to 1902)

- **Causes.** Economic: the gold of the Transvaal. Political: the status of the Uitlanders, the Jameson Raid (1895 to 1896), a failed attempt to overthrow Kruger, and British imperial aims under Alfred Milner. Historians still debate whether the war was driven by gold and capitalists or by British determination to be the supreme power in southern Africa.
- **Military impact.** Boer forces won early battles (Black Week, December 1899). British forces under Roberts and Kitchener took the republics' capitals by 1900. The Boers then fought as guerrilla commandos. Britain responded with the burning of farms and the use of concentration camps, in which tens of thousands died, including about 26,000 Boer women and children, and many Africans in separate camps. Emily Hobhouse exposed conditions.
- **Political and economic impact.** The Treaty of Vereeniging (May 1902) ended the war. The Union of South Africa (1910) joined the Cape, Natal, Transvaal and Orange Free State. Black Africans were largely excluded from political rights.
- **Impact on society and Boer identity.** The camps became a symbol of suffering for Afrikaner nationalism. Language and cultural movements grew, and the Broederbond was founded in 1918.

## 3. Emergence and development of apartheid

- **Segregation before 1948.** The Natives Land Act (1913), the Urban Areas Act (1923), the Mines and Works Act (1911 and later amendments) and the removal of Africans from the Cape common voters' roll (1936) built a system of racial separation under governments led by Botha, Smuts and Hertzog.
- **National Party victory in 1948.** D. F. Malan's party won a narrow majority of seats, supported by Afrikaner voters concerned about African urbanisation and by the party's promise of "apartheid" (apartness).
- **Petty apartheid:** laws on daily life, such as the Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949), the Immorality Amendment Act (1950) and the Reservation of Separate Amenities Act (1953).
- **Grand apartheid:** the Population Registration Act (1950), the Group Areas Act (1950), the Bantu Authorities Act (1951), Bantu Education Act (1953), and the creation of "homelands" (Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act, 1959). Africans were made citizens of homelands, and forced removals such as Sophiatown (1955) and District Six (from 1966) displaced many thousands.
- **Subjugation and violence.** Security laws such as the Suppression of Communism Act (1950), detention without trial, and police violence. Hendrik Verwoerd, prime minister from 1958, is seen as the leading theorist of the system.

## 4. Growth and development of resistance to apartheid

- **ANC and Mandela.** The ANC (founded 1912) was joined by a Youth League in 1944 whose members included Nelson Mandela, Walter Sisulu and Oliver Tambo. The Defiance Campaign (1952) and Freedom Charter (1955) used mass non-violent methods. The Treason Trial (1956 to 1961) tried 156 leaders.
- **Changing methods.** After Sharpeville on 21 March 1960, when police killed 69 people during a pass-law protest, the ANC and the rival Pan Africanist Congress were banned. The ANC formed *Umkhonto we Sizwe* in 1961 for sabotage. Mandela and others were jailed at the Rivonia Trial (1963 to 1964).
- **Black Consciousness.** Steve Biko and the South African Students' Organisation (1968) argued that Black people needed psychological liberation and self-reliance. Biko died in police custody in September 1977.
- **Soweto and township violence.** The Soweto uprising of 16 June 1976 began as a student protest against Afrikaans as a language of instruction and spread across the country. In the 1980s the United Democratic Front (1983), trade unions (COSATU, 1985) and township revolts challenged the state, which declared states of emergency in 1985 and 1986.

**Argument to weigh:** "Armed struggle, not non-violence, brought change." Support: it kept pressure alive after 1960. Challenge: its military impact was small; mass protest, strikes and international pressure mattered more.

## 5. Impact of international opposition and changing attitudes

- **Global opposition.** South Africa was excluded from the Olympic Games from the 1960s, and sporting tours such as the 1981 Springbok tour of New Zealand caused major protests. Cultural boycotts and the Anti-Apartheid Movement kept the issue visible.
- **Economic sanctions, boycotts and divestment.** The UN arms embargo (1977) and later economic measures, including the US Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act (1986) passed over President Reagan's veto, and decisions by banks in 1985 to stop rolling over loans, reduced confidence.
- **End of the Cold War.** South Africa's government had presented itself as an anti-communist ally. After the end of the Cold War, and with a settlement in Angola and Namibia in 1988 to 1990, that argument weakened.
- **Role of other African states.** The Frontline States hosted exiles and supported the ANC, and the Organisation of African Unity campaigned for sanctions. South Africa responded with raids and destabilisation of neighbours.

## 6. Successes and failures in the end of apartheid

- **Mandela and de Klerk.** F. W. de Klerk, president from 1989, unbanned the ANC and released Mandela on 11 February 1990. Both men shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993. Historians debate whether de Klerk acted from vision or from necessity (economic strain, unrest, and the end of the Cold War), and how much credit goes to Mandela's leadership.
- **CODESA and the 1994 elections.** The Convention for a Democratic South Africa began in December 1991. Violence, including the Boipatong massacre (1992) and the assassination of Chris Hani (1993), threatened talks. An interim constitution led to elections on 27 April 1994, in which the ANC won about 62 per cent and Mandela became president.
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission.** Established in 1995 under Archbishop Desmond Tutu, it offered amnesty in return for full disclosure of politically motivated crimes. Supporters credit it with uncovering truth and avoiding violence. Critics argue that it gave too little justice to victims.
- **Ongoing challenges.** Persistent inequality, unemployment, the HIV/AIDS epidemic, violence, corruption and power shortages, along with continuing debates about land.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "International pressure was the main reason for the end of apartheid." Evaluate this claim.

1. State the claim and factors to compare: international pressure, internal resistance, economic strain, and the political choices of de Klerk.
2. For: sanctions and divestment reduced growth, isolation affected morale, and the Cold War's end removed a defence.
3. Against: township revolts, strikes and the UDF made the country ungovernable; business leaders pressed for reform; the elite chose to negotiate.
4. Perspectives: some historians stress external pressure, others the internal struggle, others the interaction of the two.
5. Judgement: pressure mattered, but it worked through internal resistance and economic crisis, so "main" is too strong.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the story of Mandela without evaluating other causes.
- Confusing petty and grand apartheid.
- Treating 1990 or 1994 as the end of all inequality.
- Forgetting the Mineral Revolution and the war as long-term background.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which people can be named in questions?** Those in the guide: Nelson Mandela, Steve Biko and Frederik Willem de Klerk, plus events like Sharpeville, Soweto, CODESA and the TRC.

**Do I need exact vote shares?** No. Say "about" if you give a figure.
