The specification content tested here is: "Goebbels, the use of propaganda and censorship; Nazi culture; repression and the police state and the roles of Himmler, the SS and Gestapo; opposition and resistance, including White Rose group, Swing Youth, Edelweiss Pirates and July 1944 bomb plot."

## Section A: knowledge and description

**1.** Name two methods Goebbels used to spread Nazi propaganda. **[2]**

**2.** Describe two features of the Nazi police state. **[4]**

**3.** Describe two features of the White Rose group. **[4]**

**4.** Describe two features of Nazi culture. **[4]**

**5.** Describe two features of the July 1944 bomb plot. **[4]**

## Section B: change, interpretations and judgement

**6.** In what ways were the lives of Germans affected by Nazi censorship? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**7.** In what ways were the lives of young Germans who rejected the Hitler Youth affected by the Nazi regime? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**Interpretation A** *(invented)* -- From a history of everyday life in Nazi Germany written in 1992 by a German historian:
"Most Germans were not terrorised into obedience. They read the newspapers, listened to the radio, cheered at rallies and believed much of what they heard. The Gestapo was small; it did not need to be large, because neighbours did its work for it."

**Interpretation B** *(invented)* -- From the memoir of a Social Democrat imprisoned in Dachau, published in 1947:
"Do not let anyone tell you Germans loved the Nazis. We kept quiet because we knew what happened to those who spoke. I saw men return from the camp who would not say a word about it. That silence was the Nazis' greatest weapon."

**8.** How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about why Germans obeyed the Nazi regime? Explain your answer using Interpretations A and B. **[4]**

**9.** Which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about why Germans obeyed the Nazi regime? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what Interpretations A and B say. **[8]**

**10.** Which of the following was the more important reason why the Nazis were able to control Germany in the years 1933-39: propaganda and censorship; the police state? Explain your answer with reference to both reasons. **[12]**

**11.** Which of the following was the more important reason why opposition to the Nazis was limited: fear of repression; the popularity of the regime? Explain your answer with reference to both reasons. **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** "Describe" questions: each of two features identified [1] and developed with detail [1]. "In what ways" questions: each of two ways identified [1], supported with evidence [1] and explained as a change [2]. Interpretation questions follow the pattern in the [interpretation questions practice](/resources/aqa-gcse-world-history-8145-interpretation-questions-practice/). 12-mark essays: first bullet explained with evidence [4], second bullet explained with evidence [4], a linked judgement on which mattered more [4].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: radio, including cheap People's Receiver sets and loudspeakers; control of newspapers; rallies such as Nuremberg; films and newsreels; posters; the 1936 Olympics.

**2.** Feature one [2]: the Gestapo, the secret state police [1], which could arrest people without trial and send them to concentration camps [1]. Feature two [2]: the SS, led by Himmler [1], which ran the concentration camps and became the regime's main instrument of terror [1]. Other creditable features: informers and block wardens; the People's Court for political crimes; Himmler's control of all German police from 1936.

**3.** Feature one [2]: it was a group of students at Munich University [1], including Hans and Sophie Scholl [1]. Feature two [2]: it produced and distributed leaflets in 1942-43 [1] condemning Nazi crimes, including the murder of Jews, and urging passive resistance [1]. Also creditable: the leaders were caught distributing leaflets at the university in February 1943 and executed within days.

**4.** Feature one [2]: art was meant to promote Nazi ideas [1], showing heroic workers, soldiers and peasant families, while modern art was mocked in the 1937 Degenerate Art exhibition [1]. Feature two [2]: culture was controlled through the Reich Chamber of Culture [1], so only approved writers, artists and musicians could work [1]. Also creditable: jazz condemned as un-German; monumental architecture under Speer; Strength through Joy concerts and theatre trips.

**5.** Feature one [2]: it was planned by army officers and conservatives [1] who believed Hitler was leading Germany to disaster as the war was being lost [1]. Feature two [2]: on 20 July 1944 Claus von Stauffenberg left a bomb in a briefcase at Hitler's headquarters in East Prussia [1]; Hitler survived, Stauffenberg was shot that night and thousands were arrested [1].

**6.** Way one [4]: what Germans could read was restricted [1]; books by Jewish, socialist and other banned authors were burned on 10 May 1933 and opposition newspapers were closed [1]; so Germans now received only news and ideas approved by the regime, and their view of events was shaped by Goebbels's ministry [2]. Way two [4]: people working in culture lost their freedom or their jobs [1]; the Reich Chamber of Culture decided who could work as a writer, musician, artist or journalist [1]; so those who were Jewish, left-wing or modern in style were excluded from their professions, and many left Germany [2]. Also creditable: listening to foreign radio became a crime in wartime, so even private listening became dangerous.

**7.** Way one [4]: they faced pressure and punishment for nonconformity [1]; the Swing Youth in Hamburg were targeted, and in 1942 Himmler ordered leaders sent to concentration camps [1]; so young people's choice of music, dress and dancing became a political matter that could lead to imprisonment [2]. Way two [4]: some were drawn into active resistance and its dangers [1]; Edelweiss Pirates in Cologne fought Hitler Youth patrols, and in wartime some helped deserters and escaped prisoners; a group linked to them was publicly hanged in November 1944 [1]; so for these young people rejecting the regime changed from avoiding its organisations to risking their lives [2].

**8.** Difference [2]: A argues that most Germans obeyed because they believed the regime's propaganda and co-operated with it, while B argues that they obeyed out of fear. Support from A [1]: "Most Germans were not terrorised into obedience"; "believed much of what they heard". Support from B [1]: "We kept quiet because we knew what happened to those who spoke."

**9.** A tested [3]: propaganda reached millions through radio, rallies and the press; many Germans welcomed falling unemployment and foreign-policy successes such as the Rhineland (1936); the Gestapo had relatively few officers and relied on denunciations from the public. But A underplays terror: thousands of political opponents were imprisoned in camps such as Dachau from 1933. B tested [3]: the author's experience of Dachau is supported by the use of camps against socialists and communists; fear of arrest explains why few spoke out. But B's own position as a Social Democrat prisoner may lead him to generalise from opponents like himself to all Germans. Judgement [2]: for example, A is more convincing for the majority of Germans in the 1930s, while B is more convincing for the regime's political opponents; a strong answer decides which matters more for "why Germans obeyed" and explains why.

**10.** Propaganda and censorship [4]: Goebbels's ministry controlled radio, the press and film; the People's Receiver brought Hitler's speeches into homes; rallies and the Olympics created a sense of national unity; censorship removed alternative ideas. This kept many Germans loyal and made control easier because they co-operated willingly. The police state [4]: Himmler's SS and the Gestapo arrested opponents without trial; Dachau opened in March 1933; informers and block wardens made people careful about what they said; the People's Court tried political crimes. This removed organised opposition and frightened others into silence. Linked judgement [4]: for example, propaganda was more important for most Germans because the Gestapo was small and depended on co-operation, which propaganda helped to create; or the police state was more important because without the destruction of opponents in 1933-34 propaganda would have faced open challenge. The judgement must explain *why* one mattered more, ideally showing how they worked together.

**11.** Fear of repression [4]: the Gestapo and informers made dissent dangerous; camps held political prisoners; the White Rose leaders were executed within days in 1943, and those linked to the Edelweiss Pirates were hanged in 1944, showing the cost of resistance. Popularity [4]: jobs created by public works and rearmament; Strength through Joy leisure; foreign-policy successes in the 1930s; propaganda presenting Hitler as Germany's saviour. Many Germans saw no reason to oppose. Linked judgement [4]: for example, popularity was more important in the 1930s, when most Germans felt their lives had improved, while fear became more important in wartime, when support fell but resistance was crushed; or fear was more important because even the army officers who opposed Hitler in 1944 had waited years before acting. Credit any well-reasoned judgement with evidence.
