## What the specification lists

- Nature and purpose of Ansoff's matrix.
- Distinguish between the quadrants.
- Identify examples of strategies for each quadrant.
- Evaluate the level of risk for a business and its stakeholders in each quadrant.
- Recommend and justify a strategy using the matrix.
- Assess the usefulness of the matrix in making a business decision.

## Core recall

### The matrix

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration | Product development |
| **New markets** | Market development | Diversification |

- Nature: a growth-planning model on two dimensions, product and market.
- Purpose: set out growth options and show that risk rises with distance from current knowledge.

### Classification tests

1. Market = customer group, not place or channel. Same customers through a new channel is the existing market.
2. Product = new only if it needs new skills, equipment or suppliers. A new size or pack is existing.
3. Borderline? Say so and justify.

### Strategies

| Quadrant | Examples |
|---|---|
| Penetration | Promotions, advertising, loyalty schemes, wider distribution, taking over a rival |
| Market development | New regions or countries, new segments, new uses |
| Product development | New models, improved versions, range extensions, complementary services |
| Diversification | Related (shares skills or customers; includes backward and forward vertical) or unrelated |

- Backward vertical: moving into the supplier's stage.
- Forward vertical: moving into the customer's stage.
- Related diversification is less risky than unrelated.

### Source of risk in each quadrant

| Quadrant | Knowledge gap | Reduce risk by |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration | Little gap, but limited room to grow; retaliation | Check share and saturation; compete on service |
| Market development | Customers, rivals, laws, culture | Research; local agent; regional trial |
| Product development | Technical skills; whether customers want it | Prototypes; test with loyal customers |
| Diversification | Both at once; management stretched | Related moves; acquisition; joint venture |

Penetration can be low risk but also low reward, and is risky in a saturated market with strong rivals.

### Stakeholder risk

- Penetration: little change for staff and suppliers; price wars hit margins and dividends.
- Market development: relocation or travel for staff; lenders fund unfamiliar sites.
- Product development: new skills for staff; existing customers may be disappointed.
- Diversification: widest exposure for shareholders, employees and lenders.

### Recommending a strategy

Choice → link to objective and resources → use the case figures (cost, profit, payback) → compare with an alternative → main risk → condition.

### Usefulness

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Simple, quick | No figures: cannot show profit or affordability |
| Shows the full range of options | New and existing are matters of degree |
| Links options to sources of risk | Ignores competitors and market change |
| Good first step | Risk ranking is a generalisation |

Most useful at the start of a decision; least useful for the final choice.

## Quick worked reminder

Halden Ice Cream (invented), £400,000 to invest, target £150,000 extra profit: A penetration £250,000 for £60,000 (4.2 years); B market development £180,000 for £75,000 (2.4 years); C product development £220,000 for £80,000 (2.75 years); D related diversification £400,000 for £110,000 (3.6 years). B and C together cost £400,000 and add £155,000, spreading risk across two different gaps.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market penetration | Selling more existing products to existing customers |
| Market development | Selling existing products to new customer groups |
| Product development | Selling new products to existing customers |
| Diversification | Selling new products to new customers |
| Related diversification | A new activity that shares skills, technology or customers with the old |
| Unrelated diversification | A new activity with no link to the existing business |
| Vertical integration | Moving into a supplier's or customer's stage of production |
| Market saturation | When most potential customers already buy, leaving little room to grow |

## Exam traps

- A new channel to the same customers is not a new market.
- A minor product change is not product development.
- Penetration is not automatically safe.
- Use the figures; the matrix alone does not decide.
- State who bears the risk, not just how big it is.

## Self-test

1. What are the two dimensions of Ansoff's matrix?
2. A bakery starts selling its bread through a delivery app to its existing customers. Classify it.
3. A bakery starts supplying bread to school canteens. Classify it.
4. A brewery buys a chain of pubs. What kind of diversification is this?
5. Why is related diversification usually less risky than unrelated?
6. Give the main source of risk in market development.
7. Give one way to reduce the risk of product development.
8. Why might market penetration be risky for a business with a 45% share of a saturated market?
9. Which stakeholders face the widest risk in diversification?
10. An option costs £150,000 and adds £40,000 profit a year. Find the payback.
11. Give two limitations of Ansoff's matrix.
12. When is the matrix most useful?

### Answers

1. Products (existing or new) and markets (existing or new).
2. **Market penetration**: same product, same customers, new channel.
3. **Market development**: same product, new customer group.
4. **Related diversification (forward vertical)**: moving into its customers' stage.
5. Some skills, technology or customer knowledge transfer to the new activity.
6. Not knowing the new customers, rivals, laws or culture.
7. Any one: prototypes; testing with loyal customers; launching to a sample first.
8. Growth must come from rivals' customers, which may provoke a price war that cuts margins.
9. Shareholders, employees and lenders, because both product and market are unfamiliar.
10. 150,000 ÷ 40,000 = **3.75 years**.
11. Any two: no figures; new and existing are matters of degree; ignores competitors and market change; risk ranking is a generalisation.
12. At the start of a strategic decision, to identify and classify options and the risks to investigate.
