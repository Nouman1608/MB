## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *variable costs* and give **one** example. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** examples of fixed costs for a hair salon. **[2]**

**3.** Fernhill Frames sells picture frames at £18 each. The variable cost of each frame is £7 and fixed costs are £3,300 a month. In May it sells 450 frames.

**(a)** Calculate total costs for May. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit for May. **[2]**

**4.** A business has a break-even output of 640 units and sells 900 units. Calculate the margin of safety. **[1]**

**5.** On a break-even chart, which **one** line starts at zero on the vertical axis? A: fixed costs. B: total costs. C: revenue. D: variable costs plus fixed costs. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why a rise in the selling price lowers a business's break-even output. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Kestle Kites has the following figures for one month.

| Output (kites) | 0 | 200 | 400 | 600 | 800 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (£) | 0 | 5,000 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 20,000 |
| Total costs (£) | 6,000 | 8,000 | 10,000 | 12,000 | 14,000 |

**(a)** Identify the break-even output. **[1]**
**(b)** Kestle Kites sells 700 kites at £25 each. Variable cost is £10 a kite. Calculate the profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the margin of safety at 700 kites. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain the effect on break-even output if Kestle Kites' rent rises. **[2]**

**8.** Morvane Removals is considering a new van costing £32,000. It expects returns of £10,000 a year for 4 years.

**(a)** Calculate the average rate of return. **[4]**
**(b)** The business would borrow the money at 8% interest. Explain whether the investment is worthwhile on these figures. **[2]**

**9.** Ruthern Bakery is comparing two ovens.

Oven A costs £18,000 and is expected to return £8,000 a year for 3 years.
Oven B costs £24,000 and is expected to return £9,000, £9,000, £10,000 and £10,000 over 4 years.

**(a)** Calculate the ARR for Oven A. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the ARR for Oven B. **[3]**

**10.** A break-even chart for Tavlow Tents shows that the revenue line and the total cost line cross at an output of 1,500 tents and a value of £45,000. The fixed cost line is at £15,000.

**(a)** State the break-even output. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the selling price of each tent. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the variable cost of each tent. **[2]**

**11.** Lowenna is opening a café and has used break-even analysis to plan her first year. Analyse **two** limitations of break-even analysis for her café. **[6]**

**12.** Ruthern Bakery can afford only one oven. Oven A has an ARR of about 11.1% and costs £18,000. Oven B has an ARR of about 14.6% and costs £24,000. The bakery would borrow the money at 9% interest. Oven B is larger and would need the bakery to rearrange its kitchen, closing for two days. Evaluate which oven Ruthern Bakery should buy. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Costs that change directly with the level of output [1]. Example: raw materials, packaging or ingredients [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: rent, insurance, business rates, salaries of salaried staff, loan interest [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Total variable costs 450 × £7 = £3,150 [1]; total costs £3,150 + £3,300 = **£6,450** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Revenue 450 × £18 = £8,100 [1]; profit £8,100 − £6,450 = **£1,650** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 900 − 640 = **260 units** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Each unit sold brings in more revenue while its variable cost stays the same [1], so fewer units are needed for revenue to cover total costs [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** **400 kites**, where revenue and total costs both equal £10,000 [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Revenue 700 × £25 = £17,500; total costs £6,000 + 700 × £10 = £13,000 [1]; profit **£4,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 700 − 400 = **300 kites** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Rent is a fixed cost, so the total cost line shifts upwards [1], meaning more kites must be sold to cover costs, so break-even output rises [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Total return 4 × £10,000 = £40,000 [1]. Total profit £40,000 − £32,000 = £8,000 [1]. Average annual profit £8,000 ÷ 4 = £2,000 [1]. ARR £2,000 ÷ £32,000 × 100 = **6.25%** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Not worthwhile on these figures [1], because the ARR of 6.25% is below the 8% interest rate, so the van earns less than the cost of borrowing for it [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** Total return 3 × £8,000 = £24,000; total profit £24,000 − £18,000 = £6,000 [1]. Average annual profit £6,000 ÷ 3 = £2,000 [1]. ARR £2,000 ÷ £18,000 × 100 = **11.1%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Total return £38,000; total profit £38,000 − £24,000 = £14,000 [1]. Average annual profit £14,000 ÷ 4 = £3,500 [1]. ARR £3,500 ÷ £24,000 × 100 = **14.6%** [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** **1,500 tents** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** At break-even, revenue is £45,000 from 1,500 tents [1], so price = £45,000 ÷ 1,500 = **£30** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Total variable costs at 1,500 tents = £45,000 − £15,000 = £30,000 [1]; variable cost per tent = £30,000 ÷ 1,500 = **£20** [1]. **[2]**

**11.** First, break-even assumes everything produced is sold [1], but a café's food can go unsold and be thrown away [1], so her real costs per sale may be higher and she may need to sell more than the analysis shows [1]. Second, it relies on forecasts of prices and costs [1]; as a new business, Lowenna has no past figures, so her estimates of customer numbers or ingredient costs may be wrong [1], meaning her break-even point could be inaccurate and she could make a loss for longer than planned [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Knowledge: ARR is average annual profit as a percentage of the cost of an investment [1].
Application: Oven B's ARR of 14.6% is higher than Oven A's 11.1% [1]; both are above the 9% interest rate [1].
Analysis: Oven B gives a greater return on each pound invested, so it adds more to profit after paying interest [1]; it lasts four years against three, giving returns for longer [1]; Oven A costs £6,000 less, so the bakery borrows less and pays less interest [1].
Evaluation: Oven B closes the bakery for two days, losing sales, which the ARR does not include [1]; the returns are forecasts and a larger oven only pays if the bakery can sell the extra output [1]. Judgement: Oven B, because its ARR is well above the 9% interest rate and above Oven A's, which should outweigh two days of lost sales, provided demand is strong enough [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing each ARR with the interest rate, and mentioning a factor the ARR leaves out, lifts an answer into evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding fixed costs to the variable cost per unit instead of to total variable costs.
- Dividing average annual profit by the total return instead of the cost of the investment.
- Forgetting to subtract the investment cost before finding the average.
- Reading break-even from the fixed cost line.
- Giving margin of safety with no units, or in pounds when output is in units.
- Recommending an investment on ARR alone without comparing it with the interest rate or mentioning risks.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I get marks if my final answer is wrong?**
Often yes, if your working is clear. Write each step, such as total return, total profit and average annual profit, so method marks can be given.

**How do I find the selling price from a break-even chart?**
At break-even, divide the revenue at that point by the break-even output.
