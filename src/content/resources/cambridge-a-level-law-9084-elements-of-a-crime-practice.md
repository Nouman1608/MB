## Section A -- short answers and scenarios

**1.** Define actus reus and mens rea. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the conduct element of a crime must normally be voluntary, using a case. **[2]**

**3.** State the "but for" test and give the case that illustrates it. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **three** situations in which the law imposes a duty to act. **[3]**

**5.** Erin is employed as a lifeguard at a public pool. She sees a child struggling in the water but carries on reading her phone, and the child is badly hurt. Explain whether Erin's failure to act can form the actus reus of an offence. **[4]**

**6.** Bilal pushes Clara, who falls and breaks her leg. At hospital she is given an overdose of painkillers by mistake but recovers from the overdose; her leg heals more slowly than expected. Explain whether Bilal caused Clara's injury. **[5]**

**7.** Dev sets fire to his own shed to claim on his insurance. He knows that his neighbour's fence is touching the shed but thinks, "It probably won't catch." The fence burns down. Explain whether Dev was reckless as to the damage to the fence. **[5]**

**8.** Freya places a firework in a postbox to destroy letters for a prank, knowing that a postal worker is about to open it. The postal worker is injured. Explain whether Freya intended the injury. **[5]**

## Section B -- short answer and essay

**9.** Distinguish direct intention from oblique intention. **[4]**

**10.** Explain the effect of the thin skull rule, with reference to a case. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how the courts decide whether an act of a third party or of the victim breaks the chain of causation. **[8]**

**12.** "The law on recklessness is now fair to defendants." Evaluate this statement with reference to the development of the law. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Actus reus is the physical or external element of a crime – the conduct, result and circumstances [1]. Mens rea is the mental element that the definition of the offence requires, such as intention or recklessness [1].

**2.** Criminal liability is based on fault, so a person should not be punished for movements they did not choose [1]. In *Hill v Baxter* (1958) the court gave the example of a driver attacked by a swarm of bees, whose actions would not be voluntary [1].

**3.** The result would not have happened "but for" the defendant's conduct [1]. In *R v White* (1910) the victim died of a heart attack before the poison took effect, so the test was not satisfied [1].

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: a statutory duty [1]; a contractual duty, as in *R v Pittwood* (1902) [1]; a duty arising from a public office, as in *R v Dytham* (1979) [1]; voluntary assumption of care; a special relationship such as parent and child; creating a dangerous situation, as in *R v Miller* (1983).

**5.** The general rule is that a failure to act is not an actus reus [1]. An exception exists where the defendant is under a contractual duty to act [1]. In *R v Pittwood* (1902) a level-crossing keeper who failed to close the gate under his contract was liable [1]. Erin's contract as a lifeguard requires her to supervise and rescue swimmers, so her omission is capable of forming the actus reus [1].

**6.** **Factual causation**: but for Bilal's push, Clara would not have fallen and broken her leg, so the test is satisfied [1]. **Legal causation**: the push must be an operating and substantial cause, not necessarily the only cause (*R v Smith* (1959)) [1]. **Medical treatment**: negligent treatment breaks the chain only where it is so independent and so potent that the original injury becomes insignificant (*R v Cheshire* (1991)) [1]. The overdose did not cause the broken leg, and the fracture is still the operating cause of her injury [1]. The chain is not broken, so Bilal caused Clara's injury [1].

**7.** Since *R v G* (2003) recklessness is subjective: the defendant must be aware of a risk [1] and it must be unreasonable, in the circumstances known to him, to take it [1]. Dev knew the fence touched the shed and his thought "it probably won't catch" shows he considered the risk and was aware of it [1]. Burning a shed for an insurance claim gives no social value that could justify the risk, so taking it was unreasonable [1]. Dev was therefore reckless as to the damage to the fence [1].

**8.** Freya's aim was to destroy letters, not to injure, so she had no direct intention as to the injury (*R v Mohan* (1975)) [1]. Oblique intention applies the *Woollin* (1998) test: the jury may find intention only if injury was a virtual certainty as a result of her act, barring unforeseen intervention [1], and she appreciated that this was so [1]. A firework exploding in a postbox that is about to be opened makes injury highly likely, and the jury could find it virtually certain [1]. If so, and Freya knew this, the jury is entitled but not obliged to find intention (*R v Matthews and Alleyne* (2003)) [1].

**9.** Direct intention is where the consequence is the defendant's aim or purpose [1], as defined in *R v Mohan* (1975) [1]. Oblique intention covers a consequence that is not the aim but is a virtual certainty of the defendant's act, which the defendant appreciates [1], under the test in *R v Woollin* (1998) [1].

**10.** The defendant must take the victim as they find them, so a victim's unusual physical or other characteristics do not break the chain of causation [1]. In *R v Blaue* (1975) the victim, a Jehovah's Witness, refused a blood transfusion after being stabbed and died [1]; the refusal did not break the chain, and the defendant was liable for her death [1].

**11.** An intervening act (*novus actus interveniens*) breaks the chain only if it is free, deliberate and informed, or so unforeseeable that it becomes the real cause [1]. **Third parties**: a reasonable and foreseeable reaction to the defendant's conduct does not break the chain [1]; in *R v Pagett* (1983) police firing back at the defendant, who used a girl as a shield, did not break it [1]. **Medical treatment** will rarely break the chain (*R v Cheshire* (1991)) [1] unless it is "palpably wrong" and the original wound has nearly healed, as in *R v Jordan* (1956) [1]. **The victim**: an escape attempt does not break the chain unless "so daft" as to be unforeseeable, as in *R v Roberts* (1971) [1]. A victim's free, deliberate and informed choice does break it, as in *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007), where the victim injected himself [1]. The thin skull rule means a victim's characteristics never break the chain (*R v Blaue* (1975)) [1].

**12.** **Development**: *R v Cunningham* (1957) set a subjective test – the defendant must foresee the risk [1]. *MPC v Caldwell* (1982) added an objective test covering risks obvious to a reasonable person [1], which led to convictions of defendants incapable of seeing the risk [1]. *R v G* (2003) overruled *Caldwell* and returned to a subjective test of awareness of a risk which it was unreasonable to take [1]. **Fair**: liability now matches personal fault, so children and those with limited capacity are not convicted for risks they could not foresee [1]; it is consistent with the general principle that mens rea is subjective [1]. **Unfair or problematic**: defendants who give no thought to an obvious risk, through indifference, may escape liability [1]; proof depends on what the defendant says they foresaw, which can be difficult for juries [1]; the "unreasonable to take the risk" limb is partly objective and its application can vary [1]. **Conclusion**: the law is now broadly fair to defendants because it requires real foresight, though it may sometimes be too generous to the thoughtless [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a duty situation without linking it to the facts.
- Analysing factual causation but not legal causation.
- Treating every medical error as breaking the chain.
- Saying the jury must find intention once virtual certainty is shown.
- Evaluating recklessness without tracing *Cunningham*, *Caldwell* and *R v G*.
