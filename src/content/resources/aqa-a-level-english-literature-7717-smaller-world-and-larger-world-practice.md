## Section B: an unseen poem and essay skills

**Unseen extract (original poem).** The speaker remembers her mother's kitchen in the year a factory
closed.

> The radio said the word *rationalise*
> the morning Mum began to save the string.
> She'd never saved it, not in all the years
> my father brought his wages home in brown
> envelopes she opened with a knife.
> Now there was string, and paper bags, and jars
> washed clean for nothing we would ever fill.
> The man in London said the town was *lean*.
> We had a word for lean. It wasn't that.
> She kept the knife. She put it in the drawer
> beside the string, as if one day the wages
> might come back, and need to be let out.

**6.** Analyse how the poem connects the smaller world of the kitchen to the larger world. **[8]**

**7.** Explain the contrast between the voices of the radio and "the man in London" and the voice of
the family. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the significance of the knife in the final sentence. **[3]**

**9.** Explain one connection between the poem and a set text in the way a national decision reaches
a household. **[3]**

**10.** A student writes: "*The Kite Runner* is set in Afghanistan during a war." Explain two ways to
turn this into an analytical point about smaller and larger worlds. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer for the question: "Protest writing is most powerful when it stays in
the small world." List four elements of your plan, naming two texts not used in your Section B
answer. **[4]**

**12.** Explain how the idea of a microcosm could be used to meet AO4 in a Section C answer. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Domestic, workplace, and local and national government [1]. Tracing more than one level shows
how power at one scale reaches another [1], which is where AQA locates the protest: in "the connection
of the smaller world to the larger world" [1].

**2.** The tavern alternates with the court and the rebels, so each world comments on the others [1];
in Act 2 Scene 4 Falstaff and Hal play the king and the prince, rehearsing the larger world in the
smaller [1]; Hal's final reply in the game anticipates his rejection of Falstaff, a political decision
made in the tavern [1].

**3.** They move the story from one woman's room to an academic symposium in a distant future [1], so
Offred's account becomes a historical document, and the reader is invited to question how large-scale
history treats individual suffering [1].

**4.** Gradgrind's classroom drills children in facts [1]; that philosophy shapes the minds that run
Coketown's factories [1]; and Gradgrind becomes a Member of Parliament, so the same idea governs a
lesson, a workplace and the state [1].

**5.** Torvald's new post as bank manager gives him power over Krogstad's job [1], so Krogstad's threat
to Nora is an attempt to keep his place at work, and the household's safety depends on the world of
money outside it [1].

**6.** Any eight, [1] each:

- The radio brings the language of the larger world into the kitchen.
- The italicised *rationalise* marks it as an official, borrowed word.
- Saving string shows a national decision becoming a domestic habit of thrift.
- "She'd never saved it" uses contrast to show the change in the household.
- The brown wage envelopes are a small object standing for the factory's place in family life.
- The list of string, paper bags and jars builds an image of anxious hoarding.
- "Washed clean for nothing we would ever fill" shows loss of hope through an image of emptiness.
- "The man in London" is anonymous and distant, the national government reduced to a voice.
- The italicised *lean* is an economic euphemism.
- "We had a word for lean. It wasn't that" sets the family's language against official language.
- The short, plain sentences of the final lines show a quiet refusal to accept the official account.
- The knife kept "beside the string" joins past security and present thrift in one drawer.

**7.** The radio and the man in London use abstract economic terms, *rationalise* and *lean* [1],
which disguise the human cost [1]; the family's voice is plain and concrete, and "We had a word for
lean" implies a harsher word, such as hungry, that the official voice avoids [1].

**8.** The knife once opened wage envelopes, so it is a symbol of the family's former security [1];
keeping it shows hope or refusal to accept the closure [1]; the final image of wages that "need to be
let out" turns the knife into an image of release that will not come, ending the poem in quiet grief
[1].

**9.** A connection identified [1], supported by reference to a set text [1], with an explanation [1]:
for example, like *Harvest*, where enclosure arrives as a local catastrophe, the poem shows an
economic decision made elsewhere changing daily life; or, like *Hard Times*, it shows how official
language reduces workers to economic units.

**10.** Two developments, [2] each for a developed point or [1] for a partial one: analyse how the
friendship of Amir and Hassan mirrors the national hierarchy of Pashtun and Hazara; analyse how
national events (the end of the monarchy, the Soviet invasion, the Taliban) break apart the household;
analyse Amir's return to Kabul as a connection between the private story of guilt and the national
story of destruction; link to the structure of the novel moving between Kabul and California.

**11.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis on "most powerful"; two named texts different from the Section
B text; a paragraph comparing how each text uses a small world; a paragraph on how each opens to the
larger world; contexts for each text; an alternative interpretation; a comparative judgement.

**12.** The idea gives a shared method to compare across texts [1], for example comparing the
Commander's household in *The Handmaid's Tale* with the Helmers' home in *A Doll's House* as small
worlds that reflect larger systems of power [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing settings without analysing how they connect.
- Treating domestic power as unpolitical.
- Unseen answers that list techniques without an argument about power.
- In Section C, reusing the Section B text.

For full practice, write a timed Section A answer of about an hour on the unseen poem above.
