These questions cover sub-topic 7.3, Managing human population size, of the 2027-2029 Cambridge O Level
Environmental Management (5014) syllabus: the five named strategies and their benefits and limitations.
The contexts are a rural district with limited services, an ageing high-income country and a country
reviewing its migration rules.

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by an *anti-natalist* policy. **[1]**

**2.** Name **two** strategies for managing population size that aim mainly to reduce the birth rate. **[2]**

**3.** State **one** measure a government could use to control migration. **[1]**

**4.** State the approximate number of children per woman needed to replace a population without
migration. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** A rural district has 600 000 people and is growing at 3.0% a year. Few girls attend secondary school
and the nearest family-planning clinic is 80 km away.
(a) Calculate the population increase in one year. **[1]**
(b) A programme reduces growth to 2.2% a year. Calculate how many fewer people are added in the first
year. **[2]**
(c) Explain how improved access to contraception could lower the district's birth rate. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why building secondary schools for girls in the district could reduce population growth.
**[3]**

**7.** Explain why improving access to healthcare in the district could increase population growth at first
but reduce it later. **[3]**

**8.** Describe **three** measures a pro-natalist policy might include. **[3]**

**9.** An ageing high-income country has 1.4 children per woman.
(a) Explain why the government is concerned about this. **[2]**
(b) Suggest why a pro-natalist policy may have only a small effect. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how control of migration could be used to increase the working population of an ageing
country. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **two** limitations of a policy that legally limits the number of children a family may have.
**[4]**

**12.** Explain how emigration of skilled young workers can affect the country they leave. **[2]**

## Section C

**13.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of access to contraception and access to education as
strategies for reducing population growth in the rural district in Question 5. **[6]**

**14.** An ageing high-income country is choosing between a pro-natalist policy and encouraging
immigration. Discuss the benefits and limitations of each and suggest which would work faster. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A government policy that aims to reduce the birth rate. **[1]**

**2.** Any two: access to contraception **[1]**; anti-natalist population policies **[1]**; access to education
**[1]**. **[max 2]**

**3.** Any one: visas; work permits; quotas; border controls; points-based entry systems. **[1]**

**4.** About 2.1. **[1]**

**5.** (a) 600 000 x 0.03 = **18 000**. **[1]**
(b) 600 000 x 0.022 = 13 200 **[1]**; 18 000 - 13 200 = **4 800** fewer people **[1]**.
(c) Couples could choose how many children to have and when **[1]**, so unplanned births fall and the
number of children per woman decreases **[1]**.

**6.** Educated girls tend to marry and have children later **[1]**; they are more likely to find paid work
and to know about family planning **[1]**, so they tend to have fewer children, lowering the birth rate over
time **[1]**.

**7.** Better healthcare lowers death rates, especially of children, so more people survive and growth rises
at first **[1]**. As parents become confident their children will survive, they choose to have fewer **[1]**,
and clinics also provide contraception, so the birth rate falls and growth slows later **[1]**.

**8.** Any three: payments to parents for each child **[1]**; paid parental leave **[1]**; subsidised childcare
**[1]**; tax relief for families **[1]**; help with housing **[1]**. **[max 3]**

**9.** (a) 1.4 is below replacement level, so the population will age and eventually decline **[1]**; fewer
workers will pay taxes to support pensions and healthcare for the elderly **[1]**.
(b) Family size also depends on housing costs, careers and lifestyle **[1]**, so payments may only change the
timing of births or affect few families; any effect takes a generation to reach the workforce **[1]**.

**10.** The government could issue more visas or work permits, or raise quotas **[1]**, to attract young
working-age immigrants who fill labour shortages and pay taxes **[1]**.

**11.** Two explained limitations, two marks each. It restricts personal freedom and family choice **[1]**,
raising human-rights concerns and making the policy unpopular and hard to enforce **[1]**. It can cause rapid
ageing later **[1]**, because small generations of workers must support large elderly generations (accept:
gender imbalance where sons are preferred) **[1]**.

**12.** The country loses workers it has trained, such as doctors and engineers, so services and the
economy suffer (brain drain) **[1]**; the remaining population ages because young adults leave **[1]**.

**13.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six. Contraception: lets couples choose family size and lowers
unplanned births **[1]**; relatively cheap, for example through mobile clinics **[1]**; but works only if
people want smaller families **[1]** and supply is hard to maintain 80 km from a clinic **[1]**. Education:
lasting effect, as educated girls marry later and have fewer children **[1]**; but it is slow and needs
buildings and teachers **[1]**. Conclusion: the two work best together, as education increases the use of
contraception **[1]**. **[max 6]**

**14.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six. Pro-natalist policy: supports families and may raise children
per woman **[1]**; but it is expensive **[1]**, its effect is often small **[1]**, and new children take about
15 years or more to join the workforce **[1]**. Immigration: adds working-age people immediately and fills
labour shortages **[1]**; but it can cause tension if housing and services do not expand, and migrants may
later leave **[1]**. Conclusion: encouraging immigration works faster; a pro-natalist policy is a slower,
longer-term measure **[1]**. **[max 6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a pro-natalist policy a way to reduce growth.
- Explaining healthcare's effect without both stages.
- Giving a strategy's benefit and limitation as the same point reversed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name real population policies?**
The 2027-2029 syllabus does not name case studies, but a short accurate example, such as China's
one-child policy, can strengthen a discussion.

**Is control of migration only about stopping people entering?**
No. A government can use it to encourage immigration, to limit it, or to reduce the emigration of skilled
workers.
