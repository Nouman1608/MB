## Core meanings

**Lean production** is an approach to operations that aims to use fewer resources to make the same or more output, by cutting waste at every stage while keeping quality. "Waste" means anything that uses time, money, space or effort without adding value that a customer would pay for.

Lean production started in Japanese car manufacturing and is now used in services, hospitals and offices as well as factories. The specification does not list the kinds of waste, but a common classification helps you to explain how a business applies lean ideas:

- **overproduction:** making more than customers have ordered;
- **waiting:** idle workers or machines waiting for materials or instructions;
- **transport:** moving materials further than necessary;
- **over-processing:** doing more work on a product than the customer needs;
- **excess stock:** materials and finished goods that tie up cash and space;
- **unnecessary movement:** workers walking or reaching more than necessary;
- **defects:** faulty products that must be reworked or scrapped.

Lean production is therefore about **flow** and **value**. Each step is checked against one question: does this add value for the customer?

## Methods of lean production

### Just in time (JIT)

Materials and components arrive just as they are needed, and finished goods leave soon after they are made. This cuts excess stock, storage space and the risk of stock going out of date. It needs reliable suppliers, accurate forecasting and little defective material, because there is no buffer to absorb problems. Kanban cards are often used to signal when more material is needed.

### Kaizen (continuous improvement)

Kaizen means continuous improvement through small changes suggested by employees. Staff who do the job know where time is lost, so a team might meet each week to suggest improvements. Many small changes can add up to large savings. Kaizen needs training, management who act on suggestions and a culture where staff are not blamed for raising problems. It helps motivation because workers have a say, but it can be slow to deliver large gains and suggestions may dry up if nothing is done about them.

### Cell production

Workers are grouped into small teams (cells) that each complete a whole part of the product or process. Machines are arranged close together in the order of the process, which reduces movement, waiting and stock between stages. Team members are multi-skilled, so they can cover for each other. Cells give workers more variety and responsibility but need training and a redesign of the layout.

### Time-based management

The business treats time as a source of competitive advantage and sets out to cut the time from order to delivery, and from idea to launch. It reduces set-up times, speeds up decisions and removes delays between stages. Faster response to customers can win orders and reduce the stock that has to be held. Speed must not be allowed to damage quality.

### Related ideas

Some businesses add **simultaneous engineering**, where design, production and marketing work in parallel rather than one after another, and **quality circles** in which groups of workers solve quality problems. These are not named in the specification, so use them only as examples if they fit a case.

## Why businesses use lean production

- To cut costs by reducing waste, stock and rework.
- To improve quality, because problems are found and fixed at once.
- To respond quickly to changes in customer demand.
- To free cash from stock and use it elsewhere.
- To compete with rivals on price or delivery time.
- To involve and motivate employees through kaizen and teamwork.

## Worked example: costing a lean change

Greaves Joinery (invented) makes 20,000 window units a year. Material costs £12 a unit. Before the change, 6% of units are scrapped because of cutting errors. Stock of timber and finished frames is worth £90,000, and holding stock costs 20% of its value each year. Workers spend about 3 minutes a unit walking between stages and waiting for materials; labour costs £15 an hour.

The directors introduce kaizen groups, cell production and a kanban system. They expect: scrap to fall to 3%; stock to fall to £55,000; and the 3 minutes of walking and waiting to be removed.

| Saving | Working | Annual saving |
|---|---|---|
| Less scrap | (6% − 3%) × 20,000 units × £12 = 600 × £12 | £7,200 |
| Lower stock | (£90,000 − £55,000) × 20% | £7,000 |
| Less walking and waiting | 3 min × 20,000 = 60,000 min = 1,000 hours × £15 | £15,000 |
| **Total** | | **£29,200** |

One-off costs: training £18,000, kanban boards and software £4,000, rearranging the workshop £13,000, a total of **£35,000**.

Payback = £35,000 ÷ £29,200 = 1.2 years, or about 14 months.

Some points to evaluate:

- The savings are estimates. If scrap falls only to 4.5%, the scrap saving is halved, to £3,600, and the payback lengthens.
- The saved labour time is only a real saving if hours are cut or the same staff make more units. If workers are paid the same hours, the benefit is extra output, which is only valuable if there is demand for it.
- The £35,000 is paid at the start, while savings build up over time.
- Non-financial effects matter: shorter delivery times may win orders, staff may be more motivated by kaizen groups, but they may fear that "lean" means job cuts.

**Judgement.** A payback of about 14 months is attractive for a small firm, provided that savings are real and the staff co-operate. The case is stronger if the business also gains new orders from faster delivery. It would be weaker if the timber supplier were unreliable, because the lower stock would then risk stoppages.

## Impact on stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible positive impact | Possible negative impact |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Lower costs, higher profit, cash freed | Cost of change; risk of stoppages |
| Employees | Multi-skilled, more say, job satisfaction | Fear of redundancy; pressure to improve every year |
| Customers | Better quality, faster delivery, possibly lower prices | Less choice if stock is low |
| Suppliers | Long-term partnership with reliable customers | Pressure for frequent small deliveries and tighter quality |
| Community | Less waste and fewer lorry journeys | Job losses if the workforce is reduced |

## Recommending methods

Choose methods to fit the business and its problem.

- **Large stocks and slow cash flow:** JIT or kanban, if suppliers are reliable and close.
- **Many defects or rework:** kaizen groups and a focus on quality at each stage.
- **A long, scattered process with a lot of waiting and transport:** cell production.
- **Slow response to customers:** time-based management.
- **Unreliable suppliers or highly variable demand:** be cautious about JIT; start with kaizen.

State what the method costs, how long it will take and how the business will know if it works, such as scrap rate, stock value and delivery time.

## Evaluating

Weigh up the following.

1. **Cost savings against the cost of change.** Use payback if figures are given.
2. **Risk.** JIT has no buffer; a supply failure stops production.
3. **People.** Lean depends on the active involvement of staff, so management attitude matters.
4. **Fit.** A firm that makes one-off items or has unpredictable demand may find it harder to apply some lean methods.
5. **Time.** Benefits build up over months or years; staff may become tired of constant improvement.
6. **Judgement.** Which methods, in what order and why.

## Common mistakes

- Equating lean production with redundancy. It aims to remove waste, which may or may not involve cutting jobs.
- Writing "JIT" as the only method of lean production.
- Describing a method without saying how it reduces waste.
- Ignoring supplier reliability when recommending JIT.
- Counting a saving in labour time as cash saved when no hours are actually cut.

## Exam technique

"Explain how and why" needs the method, the mechanism and the benefit. "Recommend and justify" needs a choice, reasons based on the case and a comment on risk. Use figures from the case to show the scale of the saving, and compare it with the cost of the change.
