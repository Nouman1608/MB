These questions cover section **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Forces between molecules study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-forces-between-molecules/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-forces-between-molecules-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Name the type of intermolecular force present between molecules of each substance. Give all that apply.

**(a)** Argon **[1]**

**(b)** Hydrogen chloride, HCl **[1]**

**(c)** Water **[1]**

**2.** Explain how London forces arise between molecules of a non-polar substance such as chlorine. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows the boiling points of the halogens.

| Halogen | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Fluorine | −188 |
| Chlorine | −34 |
| Bromine | 59 |
| Iodine | 184 |

**(a)** State the trend. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the trend. **[3]**

**4.** The two isomers pentane and 2,2-dimethylpropane both have the formula C₅H₁₂. Pentane boils at 36 °C and 2,2-dimethylpropane boils at 9 °C. Explain the difference. **[3]**

**5.** Butane, C₄H₁₀, and propanone, CH₃COCH₃, have the same relative molecular mass (58). Butane boils at −1 °C and propanone at 56 °C. Explain the difference. **[3]**

**6.** State the conditions needed for a hydrogen bond to form between two molecules. **[2]**

**7.** Draw a diagram to show hydrogen bonding between two molecules of water. Show lone pairs, partial charges and the bond angle between the hydrogen bond and the covalent bond. **[3]**

**8.** Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, boils at 78 °C and methoxymethane, CH₃OCH₃, which has the same Mᵣ, boils at −24 °C. Explain the difference. **[3]**

**9.** The boiling points of the Group 16 hydrides are H₂O 100 °C, H₂S −60 °C, H₂Se −41 °C and H₂Te −2 °C.

**(a)** Explain the trend from H₂S to H₂Te. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why water has a much higher boiling point than predicted by this trend. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why ice is less dense than liquid water. **[3]**

**11.** State what happens to the covalent bonds and to the intermolecular forces when water boils. **[2]**

**12.** Hydrogen fluoride boils at 20 °C but hydrogen chloride boils at −85 °C. A student says "this is because HF has stronger covalent bonds". Evaluate this explanation. **[3]**

**13.** Ammonia dissolves readily in water. Explain this in terms of the forces between the molecules. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** London forces only [1]

**(b)** London forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]

**(c)** London forces, permanent dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding (accept hydrogen bonding together with London forces) [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Electrons move constantly, so at any instant the distribution in a molecule is uneven, giving a temporary dipole [1]; this temporary dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule [1]; the oppositely charged ends of the two dipoles attract each other [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** The boiling point increases down the group [1]

**(b)** The molecules are non-polar, so only London forces act between them [1]; the number of electrons increases down the group, so the temporary dipoles are larger [1]; the attractions between molecules are stronger, so more energy is needed to separate them [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Both have only London forces and the same number of electrons [1]; pentane is a straight chain, so its molecules have a larger area of contact and stronger London forces [1]; 2,2-dimethylpropane is compact and almost spherical, so it has weaker forces and needs less energy to boil [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The molecules have the same Mᵣ, so London forces are similar [1]; propanone is polar because of its C=O bond, so it also has permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]; these extra forces need more energy to overcome, so propanone boils at a higher temperature [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine in one molecule [1]; and a lone pair on a nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine atom in a neighbouring molecule [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Two water molecules drawn with O δ− and H δ+ labelled [1]; a dotted line from the H of one molecule to a lone pair on the O of the other, with the lone pair shown [1]; the O–H···O hydrogen bond drawn in a straight line (180°) with the covalent bond [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Ethanol has an O–H bond, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds [1]; methoxymethane has no hydrogen bonded to oxygen, so it has only permanent dipole–dipole forces and London forces [1]; hydrogen bonds are stronger, so more energy is needed to boil ethanol [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** The molecules are held by London forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]; the number of electrons increases from H₂S to H₂Te, so London forces get stronger and the boiling point rises [1]

**(b)** Water molecules form hydrogen bonds [1]; because oxygen is small and highly electronegative and has lone pairs [1]; the hydrogen bonds are stronger than the forces in the other hydrides, so much more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1]. **[5]**

**10.** In ice, each water molecule is hydrogen bonded to four others [1]; this forms an open lattice with spaces in it [1]; the molecules are further apart than in liquid water, so ice has a lower density [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The covalent bonds (O–H) stay intact [1]; the intermolecular forces, which are mainly hydrogen bonds, are overcome [1]. **[2]**

**12.** The explanation is wrong [1]. Boiling does not break the covalent bonds in the molecules; it overcomes intermolecular forces [1]. HF has hydrogen bonds, which are stronger than the forces in HCl, and this explains its higher boiling point [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Ammonia and water molecules both form hydrogen bonds [1]; ammonia molecules can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules (N lone pair with H of water, and N–H with O lone pair), so the molecules mix [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that covalent bonds break when a molecule boils.
- Omitting the lone pair in a hydrogen bond diagram.
- Leaving out London forces in polar molecules.
- Naming the force but not saying why it is stronger.
- Comparing atoms rather than molecules.
