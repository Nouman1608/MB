**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 10, "Independence movements in Algeria, Angola, Ghana, Guinea, Namibia and Tanganyika (circa 1900 to 2000)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The guide says every examination question will require you to refer to **one of the prescribed independence movements** and recommends teaching a variety. The six inquiry topics are: domestic reasons for the emergence of independence movements; external reasons; the growth of pro-independence parties; the importance of non-violence; the importance of armed struggle; and the impact of independence movements.

## The six movements at a glance

| Country | Colonial power | Independence | Main route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ghana (Gold Coast) | Britain | 1957 | Mass politics and negotiation (CPP, Nkrumah) |
| Guinea | France | 1958 | Referendum rejection of the French Community (Sékou Touré) |
| Tanganyika | Britain (UN trust territory) | 1961 | Peaceful nationalism (TANU, Nyerere) |
| Algeria | France | 1962 | Armed struggle (FLN) |
| Angola | Portugal | 1975 | Armed struggle by three movements |
| Namibia | South Africa | 1990 | Armed struggle plus UN diplomacy (SWAPO) |

## 1. Domestic reasons for the emergence of independence movements

- **Social factors, including racial discrimination and oppression.** In Algeria, about one million European settlers (*pieds-noirs*) held the best land and political power, while the Muslim majority lived under legal inequality. In Angola, the Portuguese system divided people into "assimilados" and a majority subject to forced labour. In Namibia, South Africa extended apartheid policies. In Tanganyika and Ghana, racial barriers in government and education bred resentment.
- **Economic conditions.** Ghanaian cocoa farmers resented low prices set by European buying firms and the government's order to cut trees infected with swollen shoot disease. Angolan peasants resented forced cotton growing and contract labour. Inflation and unemployment after the Second World War affected townspeople and ex-servicemen. The 1948 Accra riots followed a protest by ex-servicemen.
- **Colonial administration, including the involvement of Africans.** Indirect rule through chiefs created a group of educated Africans excluded from the higher positions, which pushed them toward nationalism. In Tanganyika the land alienation of the Meru people (1951) became a political cause.
- **Actions of colonial settlers.** In Algeria, settlers blocked reforms that would have given Muslims more votes. In Angola, white settlement increased in the 1950s. In Namibia, settlers and the South African administration controlled land and labour.

**Argument to weigh:** "Economic grievances were the main domestic cause of independence movements." Support: cocoa in Ghana, forced cotton in Angola. Challenge: in Algeria and Namibia, political exclusion and settler domination were more important.

## 2. External reasons for the emergence of independence movements

- **Role of ideas.** Pan-Africanism (the 1945 Manchester Pan-African Congress, with Kwame Nkrumah), the principle of self-determination in the Atlantic Charter (1941) and the UN Charter, and Gandhi's example in India (1947). In Algeria, ideas of Arab and Islamic identity were important.
- **Success of movements in other countries.** Ghana's independence in 1957 encouraged other leaders. The Algerian war influenced Francophone Africa and the Bandung Conference of 1955 gave a platform to Asian and African leaders.
- **Post-war economic and political weaknesses of the colonial power.** Britain and France had been weakened by war. France faced defeat at Dien Bien Phu in Indochina in 1954, and the Suez crisis of 1956 exposed the limits of British and French power. Portugal, a poor dictatorship, held on longest.
- **Impact of the Cold War.** The United States and the Soviet Union each criticised old empires while competing for influence. In Angola, the MPLA received Soviet and Cuban help, while the US, China and South Africa backed rivals. In Namibia, the Cold War shaped South Africa's decision to resist SWAPO.

## 3. Emergence and growth of pro-independence political parties

- **Leaders and charisma.** Nkrumah in Ghana; Sékou Touré in Guinea; Julius Nyerere in Tanganyika; Ahmed Ben Bella and other FLN leaders in Algeria; Agostinho Neto, Holden Roberto and Jonas Savimbi in Angola; Sam Nujoma in Namibia.
- **Political organisation.** Nkrumah broke from the older UGCC to form the Convention People's Party (CPP) in 1949, built a mass party with youth, women, traders and workers, and won the 1951 election while in prison. TANU, formed in 1954, united Tanganyika across ethnic and religious lines using Swahili. Touré built his party in Guinea from the trade union movement.
- **Strategy and ideology.** Nkrumah's "Positive Action" slogan, "Seek ye first the political kingdom"; African socialism and Pan-Africanism; the FLN's blend of nationalism and Islam; Marxism in the MPLA.
- **Divisions.** In Ghana, the UGCC leaders were more conservative. In Algeria, the FLN fought a violent struggle against Messali Hadj's rival movement, the MNA. In Angola, the MPLA, FNLA and UNITA were divided by ideology, region and ethnicity, and eventually fought each other. In Namibia, SWAPO had rivals but became the leading movement.

## 4. Importance of non-violence in achieving independence

- **Campaigning and building a popular support base.** The CPP's rallies and newspapers; TANU's branches across the territory.
- **Protests and strikes.** Nkrumah's Positive Action in January 1950; the strikes of 1953 in Guinea; boycotts of European goods.
- **Negotiations with the colonial power.** In Tanganyika, TANU won elections in 1958 to 1960 under a multiracial voting scheme, and the British governor Richard Turnbull cooperated, leading to independence on 9 December 1961. In Ghana, Governor Arden-Clarke worked with Nkrumah after 1951, and independence came on 6 March 1957.
- **Colonial responses.** Imprisonment and bans, then constitutional changes and elections. In Guinea, de Gaulle's 1958 referendum on the French Community was rejected by about 95 per cent of voters, and France withdrew rapidly. In Namibia, diplomacy at the UN included the 1966 revocation of South Africa's mandate and Security Council Resolution 435 (1978).

**Argument to weigh:** "Non-violence was enough to win independence." Support: Ghana, Guinea, Tanganyika. Challenge: Britain and France were already willing to leave tropical Africa, and the contrast with Algeria, Angola and Namibia shows that settler and strategic interests changed outcomes.

## 5. Importance of armed struggle in achieving independence

- **Conventional and guerrilla warfare.** The FLN began its war on 1 November 1954, with attacks across Algeria. Angolan risings in early 1961 led to a thirteen-year guerrilla war against Portugal. SWAPO's armed wing began operations in 1966 from bases in Angola and Zambia.
- **Response of the colonial power.** France sent hundreds of thousands of troops. The Battle of Algiers (1956 to 1957) was won by the French army using torture, which damaged France's reputation. Portugal used large forces and forced resettlement. South Africa used heavy military force and militia in Namibia.
- **Support of other countries.** Algeria received help from Egypt, Morocco and Tunisia and later from Cold War states. Cuba and the USSR supported the MPLA and SWAPO. Frontline states hosted SWAPO.
- **Role of military leaders.** The FLN's military leaders, Neto's role as a guerrilla leader, and SWAPO's commanders. Cuban troops in Angola helped defeat South Africa in the late 1980s, including at Cuito Cuanavale (1987 to 1988), which led to the 1988 agreements and Namibian independence on 21 March 1990.

Results: the French decision to leave Algeria was made after the political crisis of 1958 that brought de Gaulle back to power, and talks led to the Evian Accords (March 1962) and independence on 5 July 1962. Estimates of Algerian deaths vary widely, from several hundred thousand to more than a million, and they should be given as estimates. In Angola, the coup against Portugal's dictatorship in April 1974 brought independence in November 1975, but it was followed by a long civil war.

**Argument to weigh:** "Armed struggle was decisive in Algeria and Angola." Support: the war's costs forced political change. Challenge: in Angola, the Portuguese revolution, not military defeat, ended the colonial war.

## 6. Impact of independence movements

- **Political impact, including new Indigenous leaders.** Nkrumah, Touré, Nyerere, Ben Bella, Neto and Nujoma became heads of state. Many moved towards one-party rule within a few years.
- **Social impacts and new forms of identity.** National identity in place of colonial districts; Pan-Africanism; in Tanzania, Swahili language and the policy of *ujamaa*; in Algeria, Arab and Islamic identity.
- **Women.** Women in Algeria joined the FLN as fighters, couriers and bombers (for example Djamila Bouhired); in Ghana, market women supported the CPP; in Namibia, women served in SWAPO. After independence, many found their political role reduced.
- **Marginalised groups.** About 800,000 Europeans left Algeria in 1962. The *harkis*, Algerians who served the French, suffered reprisals. In Angola, civil war devastated rural people. In Namibia, groups such as the Herero and Nama sought recognition for earlier atrocities.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "The importance of charismatic leadership has been overstated in the history of independence movements." Evaluate this claim with reference to one movement.

1. Choose Ghana. Leadership: Nkrumah's skill in forming the CPP, Positive Action, and imprisonment as a symbol.
2. For: the CPP's growth after 1949 depended on his message and organisation.
3. Against: the 1948 riots and economic grievances created a mass base; ex-servicemen, market women and cocoa farmers were crucial; British willingness to negotiate and the external situation.
4. Perspectives: biographers and supporters stress his role; critics stress the role of structures and others such as Danquah.
5. Judgement: leadership turned grievances into a mass party, but the conditions made it possible.

## Common mistakes

- Treating independence as a single event; show the process.
- Describing Algeria without evaluating how important armed struggle was.
- Assuming non-violent movements faced no repression.
- Overlooking rivalries among nationalist movements.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to study all six?** No. Study at least two, ideally one non-violent and one armed, so you can compare.

**Which dates are essential?** Independence dates and the starting points of key campaigns, such as 1949 (CPP), 1954 (TANU and the FLN), 1961 (Angola) and 1966 (SWAPO).
