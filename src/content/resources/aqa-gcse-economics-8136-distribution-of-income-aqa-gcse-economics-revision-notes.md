These notes cover **section 3.2.2.6 Distribution of income** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "income and wealth inequality", its causes and consequences, and "government policies to redistribute income and wealth and their consequences", including "how redistribution of income and wealth can be achieved through taxation and government spending". ## Condensed recall

- **Income** is a flow over time; **wealth** is a stock at a point in time. Wealth earns income; saved income becomes wealth.
- The ONS ranks households and splits them into **quintiles** (fifths) or **deciles** (tenths).
- Income stages: **original** → add cash benefits → **gross** → take off direct taxes → **disposable** → take off indirect taxes → **post-tax** → add benefits in kind → **final**.
- Original income is the most unequal stage; the gap narrows through the later stages.
- Wealth is more unequal than income in the UK.
- Tax type depends on the **percentage** of income paid: progressive (rising %), proportional (same %), regressive (falling %).
- Indirect taxes such as VAT tend to be regressive because poorer households spend a larger share of their income.
- Redistribution uses **both** taxation and government spending.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quintile | One fifth of households, ranked by income |
| Original income | Income before any government taxes or benefits |
| Disposable income | Income after direct taxes and cash benefits |
| Final income | Income after all taxes, plus the value of services such as the NHS and state schools |
| Progressive tax | Takes a higher percentage of income as income rises |
| Proportional tax | Takes the same percentage of income at every level |
| Regressive tax | Takes a higher percentage of low incomes than of high incomes |
| Means-tested benefit | Paid only to people whose income or savings are below a threshold, e.g. Universal Credit |
| Universal benefit | Paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income |
| Benefit in kind | A service provided free or below cost rather than cash |

## Causes: income versus wealth

| Income inequality | Wealth inequality |
|---|---|
| Different skills, qualifications and productivity | Inheritance passes wealth down generations |
| Part-time, temporary or insecure work | High earners can save more every year |
| Unemployment and economic inactivity | Rising house and share prices benefit existing owners |
| Discrimination | Returns on wealth (rent, dividends, interest) add to the wealth of those who already have it |
| Regional pay differences | Home ownership varies with age and region |

## Consequences: both sides

- **Costs:** poverty, worse health, fewer opportunities for children in poorer homes, social tension, higher benefit spending, lower spending by poorer households.
- **Benefits:** incentives to train, work and start businesses; rewards for risk-taking; savings that fund investment.
- How much inequality is acceptable is a **value judgement**.

## Redistribution tools and their consequences

| Tool | How it narrows gaps | Consequence or drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive income tax | Higher percentage from higher incomes | May reduce incentives; avoidance |
| Inheritance tax, capital gains tax | Tax wealth and its growth | Can be avoided through planning; unpopular |
| Means-tested benefits | Target money at the poorest | Withdrawal as earnings rise can weaken the reward for working more; some eligible people do not claim |
| Universal benefits | Simple, no stigma, high take-up | Costly; some money goes to people who do not need it |
| Benefits in kind (NHS, state schools) | Worth more relative to income for poorer households | Funded by taxes |
| Spending on education and training | Raises future earnings of low-income groups | Slow to take effect |

## Worked calculations

**1. Checking a tax type.** A tax takes £1,800 from a household on £12,000 (15%) and £4,320 from a household on £48,000 (9%). The percentage falls as income rises, so the tax is **regressive**, even though the richer household pays more in pounds.

**2. A proportional tax and the gap.** A flat 15% tax on incomes of £20,000 and £100,000 leaves £17,000 and £85,000. Before tax the ratio is 100,000 ÷ 20,000 = 5; after tax it is 85,000 ÷ 17,000 = **5**. A proportional tax does not change the ratio between incomes.

**3. A tax-free allowance makes a tax progressive.** No tax on the first £12,000, then 20%. On £15,000: 0.2 × 3,000 = £600, or **4%**. On £30,000: 0.2 × 18,000 = £3,600, or **12%**. The allowance makes the percentage rise with income.

**4. Quintile ratio.** Quintile shares of income are 6%, 11%, 16%, 23% and 44%. The top fifth receives 44 ÷ 6 = about **7.3 times** the share of the bottom fifth.

**5. Disposable income.** Original income £14,000, cash benefits £5,000, direct taxes £1,200. Disposable income = 14,000 + 5,000 − 1,200 = **£17,800**.

## Exam traps

- "The rich pay more tax" does not make a tax progressive; compare percentages.
- Disposable income is after **direct** taxes; indirect taxes come off at the next stage.
- Universal Credit is means-tested, despite its name.
- Inequality and poverty are different: everyone's income can rise while the gap widens.
- Redistribution happens through spending as well as taxation; give an example of each when asked how.

## Self-test

1. State one difference between income and wealth.
2. Name the ONS income stage reached by subtracting direct taxes from gross income.
3. What is a benefit in kind? Give an example.
4. Give two causes of wealth inequality.
5. A tax takes £2,000 from an income of £10,000 and £6,000 from an income of £60,000. Is it progressive, proportional or regressive?
6. Explain why a flat-rate income tax does not change the ratio of high to low incomes.
7. Distinguish between a means-tested benefit and a universal benefit.
8. Give one drawback of means-tested benefits.
9. Why does spending on state schools reduce inequality over time?
10. Give one possible benefit of some income inequality.
11. Original income £22,000, cash benefits £1,500, direct taxes £3,100. Find disposable income.
12. Why is VAT usually regressive?

### Answers

1. Income is a flow received over a period; wealth is a stock of assets owned at a point in time.
2. Disposable income.
3. A service provided free or below cost instead of cash, e.g. NHS treatment or state education.
4. Any two: inheritance, higher saving by high earners, rising house or share prices, returns on existing wealth.
5. 20% and 10%: the percentage falls as income rises, so it is regressive.
6. Everyone pays the same percentage, so after-tax incomes keep the same proportions to each other.
7. A means-tested benefit is paid only to those below an income or savings threshold; a universal benefit is paid to everyone in the group whatever their income.
8. Any one: withdrawal as earnings rise can weaken the incentive to work more; some eligible people do not claim; it is costly to check eligibility.
9. It raises the skills and qualifications of children from low-income families, so they can earn more as adults.
10. The chance of higher pay gives people an incentive to train, work hard and start businesses.
11. 22,000 + 1,500 − 3,100 = £20,400.
12. Low-income households spend a larger share of their income on goods and services that carry VAT, so it takes a higher percentage of their income.
