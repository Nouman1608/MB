## Section A

**1.** Define the term *quaternary sector*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **one** business in the public sector and **one** business in the private sector, and state the main difference in their ownership. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between unlimited liability and limited liability. **[3]**

**4.** Brightwell Joiners is a partnership of three equal partners with unlimited liability. It closes owing $70 000 and its assets sell for $40 000.

**(a)** Calculate the shortfall. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain what this means for each partner. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a group of fruit growers might form a co-operative. **[2]**

**6.** The founders of Tarrow Kitchens Ltd own 3 000 000 shares. The company plans to become a public limited company.

**(a)** Calculate the founders' percentage holding if 2 000 000 new shares are issued. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate it if 4 000 000 new shares are issued, and explain the significance of the difference. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows the share of employment by sector in the fictional country of Teluva.

| Sector | 20 years ago | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 30% | 12% |
| Secondary | 25% | 24% |
| Tertiary | 40% | 54% |
| Quaternary | 5% | 10% |

**(a)** Identify the sector whose relative importance has fallen the most, and by how many percentage points. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** likely reasons for the change in the tertiary sector. **[4]**

**(c)** Analyse **two** consequences of these changes for a clothing manufacturer based in Teluva. **[6]**

**8.** Ana Ruiz plans to open a sandwich shop. She can open independently or as a franchisee.

| | Independent | Franchise |
|---|---|---|
| Expected annual revenue | $210 000 | $250 000 |
| Annual costs before any franchise fees | $180 000 | $205 000 |
| Royalty | none | 5% of revenue |
| Initial fee | none | $20 000, spread over 4 years |

**(a)** Calculate the annual profit under each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend which option Ana should choose. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why a social enterprise might find it harder to raise finance than a private limited company. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate whether a successful private limited company should convert to a public limited company. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The sector of the economy concerned with knowledge-based activity [1], such as research and development, IT services and data analysis [1].

**2.** Public sector: for example a state-run school or a national rail operator [1]. Private sector: for example a supermarket chain or a local hairdresser [1]. Public-sector organisations are owned and controlled by the state; private-sector businesses are owned by individuals or shareholders [1].

**3.** With unlimited liability the owners are personally responsible for all the business's debts [1], so personal assets such as a house can be taken to pay creditors [1]. With limited liability owners can lose only the amount they invested in shares, so personal assets are protected [1].

**4. (a)** $70 000 - $40 000 = **$30 000** [1].

**(b)** Creditors can claim the $30 000 from the partners' personal assets [1]; if shared equally each partner pays $10 000, but a partner may have to pay more if another cannot pay [1].

**5.** By selling together, growers gain bargaining power with large buyers and can obtain better prices [1], and they can share the cost of expensive equipment such as cold storage, which benefits all members [1].

**6. (a)** 3 000 000 ÷ 5 000 000 × 100 = **60%** [1].

**(b)** 3 000 000 ÷ 7 000 000 × 100 ≈ **42.9%** [1]. With 60% the founders keep majority control [1]; with 42.9% they lose it, so other shareholders could outvote them and the company becomes open to takeover [1].

**7. (a)** Primary sector [1], down 30 - 12 = **18 percentage points** [1].

**(b)** Rising incomes increase demand for services such as leisure, finance and health [1], so service firms expand and hire more workers [1]. Mechanisation in farming and factories releases workers [1], who move into service jobs [1].

**(c)** Fewer local textile suppliers may survive as the secondary sector shrinks [1], so the manufacturer may need to import materials [1], raising costs and exposing it to exchange rate changes [1]. Workers with manufacturing skills may become scarcer as young people choose service careers [1], so the firm may have to raise wages or fund training [1], increasing costs and reducing competitiveness against imports [1].

**8. (a)** Independent: $210 000 - $180 000 = **$30 000** [1]. Franchise: royalty = 5% × $250 000 = $12 500 [1]; annual share of initial fee = $20 000 ÷ 4 = $5 000 [1]; profit = $250 000 - $205 000 - $12 500 - $5 000 = **$27 500** [1].

**(b)** On these forecasts the independent shop earns $2 500 more a year [1]. However, the franchise revenue forecast is backed by a known brand and tested system, so it may be more reliable than the independent forecast [1], and the franchisor provides training and supplies, which helps a first-time owner [1]. Against the franchise: royalties continue as long as she operates, and she cannot change the menu or suppliers freely [1]. Recommendation: if Ana is experienced and confident in her independent forecast, she should open independently for higher profit and control [1]; if she is new to the industry, the lower risk of the franchise may justify a slightly lower expected profit [1].

**9.** Its aims put social purpose before profit, so investors expecting a high financial return may not be attracted [1], and lenders may see lower profits as a sign of higher risk [1]. Some social enterprises cannot sell shares to raise capital, or choose not to in order to protect their mission [1], so they rely on grants and trading surpluses, which may be uncertain [1].

**10.** For conversion: selling shares to the public can raise very large sums for expansion without interest payments [1]; shares become easy to buy and sell, so founders can turn some of their stake into cash [1]; plc status may raise the company's profile with suppliers and lenders [1]. Against conversion: the founders' stake is diluted, so they may lose control [1]; the company becomes open to hostile takeover bids [1]; flotation is expensive, and annual reporting and disclosure requirements increase [1]; shareholders may press for short-term profits and dividends, conflicting with long-term plans [1]. Alternatives such as loans or private investors may provide enough finance while keeping control [1]. The decision depends on how much finance is needed, how quickly [1], and how much the founders value control [1]. Judgement: conversion is appropriate if the company needs large-scale capital that private sources cannot provide [1]; if the expansion can be financed by retained profit and borrowing, staying private is better [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a public limited company part of the public sector.
- Ignoring the initial franchise fee when comparing profits.
- Explaining reasons for sector change when the question asks for consequences.
- Reaching no recommendation in a "recommend" or "evaluate" question.
