**This guide covers a requirement that applies only to the non-exam assessment of AQA A-level
English Literature B (7717).** The specification says that "in non-exam assessment only, discussion
of different interpretations must include, on at least one text in a conventional response,
consideration of different interpretations of the text(s) over time". It also says that texts chosen
for the NEA "must allow access to a range of critical views and interpretations, including over
time". The [Theory and Independence study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-b-theory-and-independence/)
explains the two essays, the Critical anthology and the text-selection rules; this page explains
what "over time" means, how to choose texts that allow it, and how to write it into a conventional
essay.

## What the requirement asks

Read the sentence carefully, because each part matters.

- **"In non-exam assessment only"**: the exam papers reward different interpretations (AO5), but the
  "over time" condition is specific to the NEA.
- **"On at least one text in a conventional response"**: you must do it in a conventional essay. If
  your second piece is re-creative, the conventional essay is where it must happen. If you write two
  conventional essays, at least one must include it.
- **"Different interpretations of the text(s) over time"**: not simply two critics, but readings that
  differ because they come from different moments in the text's reception.

The wider AO5 statement explains the purpose: interpretation "is not a fixed process but a dynamic
one". An essay that shows how a text has been read differently across decades demonstrates that
dynamic directly.

## Different critics or different times?

Two interpretations can differ for several reasons, and only some of them meet the requirement.

| Kind of difference | Example | Meets "over time"? |
|---|---|---|
| Two modern critics who disagree | Two recent essays on a novel's ending | Not on its own |
| A reading from the text's first reception and a later one | An early review set against a later theoretical reading | Yes |
| Readings shaped by a critical movement that emerged later | A post-colonial reading of a novel written before the movement existed | Yes, if you show the change |
| A rewriting that responds to the original | A later novel that retells the story from another viewpoint | Yes, as evidence of changing interpretation |

The safest approach is to identify at least two **moments** in a text's reception and explain why
readers at each moment saw the text differently.

## Moments of reception

A useful frame for most texts written before the late twentieth century:

1. **First reception**: how the text was read when it appeared. Reviews, letters and the author's
   own statements can show this, but report them only if your teacher or a reliable edition gives
   you the source.
2. **Later critical traditions**: how readers in a later period, with different values, re-read it.
3. **The theoretical turn**: the arrival of feminist, Marxist, post-colonial and eco-critical
   readings, which correspond to sections of the Critical anthology.
4. **Present-day reading**: your own reading as a twenty-first-century reader, which is itself an
   interpretation shaped by its time.

The anthology section on **literary value and the canon** is especially useful here, because it is
about how a text's reputation rises or falls over time.

## Choosing texts that allow it

Because the requirement is part of text selection, check it before you commit to a text. Ask:

- Has the text been read in noticeably different ways since it appeared?
- Can you find reliable critical material from more than one period, through your teacher, an
  annotated edition or a library?
- Does the text's history connect to one of the anthology sections you plan to use?
- Is the text absent from the A-level exam set-text lists, which the NEA prohibits?

A text published very recently may have too short a reception history to show change. A text with
a long, contested history makes the requirement much easier to meet.

## Two illustrations

These examples show the kind of history to look for. Neither appears on the 7717 exam set-text lists,
but always confirm your choice with your teacher.

**Jane Eyre (prose).** Charlotte Brontë's novel of 1847 has been read in strikingly different ways.
In 1966 Jean Rhys published *Wide Sargasso Sea*, which gives a voice and a history to Bertha Mason,
Rochester's Creole wife. In 1979 Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar's *The Madwoman in the Attic* read
Bertha as Jane's dark double, an expression of the anger a Victorian woman could not show. In 1985
Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak argued, in a post-colonial essay, that the novel's feminist individualism
depends on Bertha's destruction. The same character moves from obstacle to double to colonised
victim, so the novel's reception history is itself an argument about gender and empire.

**Heart of Darkness (prose).** Joseph Conrad's novella was long read as a critique of imperial greed
and a psychological journey. In a lecture given in 1975, Chinua Achebe argued that the novella
dehumanises Africans, presenting them as a backdrop for a European's crisis, and called Conrad a
racist. Achebe's reading changed the terms of debate: later critics have had to answer whether a text
can critique imperialism while reproducing its assumptions.

## Worked example: a conventional-essay paragraph

**Task shape (original):** "*Jane Eyre* is a novel about a woman's freedom." Using ideas from the
Critical anthology, to what extent do you agree?

> For many readers the novel's account of Jane's journey to independence has made it a founding text
> of feminist criticism. Gilbert and Gubar's reading in 1979 developed this view by treating Bertha,
> confined in the attic, as Jane's double: the anger Jane learns to master is enacted by the woman she
> never meets. On this reading, Bertha's violence belongs to Jane's freedom, because it expresses what
> a Victorian heroine had to suppress. Yet later post-colonial readings, such as Spivak's in 1985,
> ask what this interpretation leaves out. Bertha is a Creole woman from the Caribbean, and Jane's
> happy ending requires her death; the freedom the novel celebrates is available to an English woman
> at the cost of a colonial one. Rhys had already dramatised this objection in *Wide Sargasso Sea*,
> where Bertha becomes Antoinette and her madness is shown to have causes. Read across these moments,
> the novel is about a woman's freedom, but the question of *which* woman has changed with each
> generation of readers.

**Why it works.** It names readings from different decades and explains why they differ, links them
to two anthology sections (feminist and post-colonial theory), and uses the change itself as
evidence for a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting two recent critics** and calling it "over time".
- **Leaving the requirement to the re-creative piece**, where it does not count.
- **Inventing reception.** Never invent what early readers thought; use sources you can cite.
- **Listing critics** without explaining why readings changed.
- **Treating later readings as simply correct.** Explain what each moment saw and missed.
- **Choosing a text with no reception history** to work with.

## Planning technique

In your plan, mark the
paragraph where interpretations over time appear, name the two or more moments, and link each to an
anthology section. Keep quotations from critics short, since AQA's NEA guidance excludes quotations
from the word count, and your own argument must carry the essay.
