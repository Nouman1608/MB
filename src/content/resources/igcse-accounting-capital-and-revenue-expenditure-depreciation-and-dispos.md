## Capital and revenue expenditure

**Capital expenditure** is spending on buying a non-current asset, on improving it, or on getting it ready for use, such as delivery and installation. **Revenue expenditure** is spending that keeps the business running from day to day and keeps assets in their present condition, such as repairs, servicing and decoration.

Ask of every payment: does it add to what the business owns, or keep it running? Thornlea Joinery makes kitchen cabinets. These are its payments this year.

| Payment | Type | Debited to |
|---|---|---|
| Band saw 9,600 | Capital | Machinery |
| Delivery of the saw 400 | Capital (getting it ready for use) | Machinery |
| Wiring to power the saw 750 | Capital (getting it ready for use) | Machinery |
| Replacement saw blades 460 | Revenue | Repairs |
| Annual service of machines 380 | Revenue | Repairs |
| Extension to the workshop 18,000 | Capital (improvement) | Premises |
| Repainting workshop walls 1,150 | Revenue (maintenance) | Repairs |
| Legal fees on buying the workshop 2,200 | Capital (cost of acquiring) | Premises |

**How to account for them.** Capital expenditure is debited to the non-current asset account and appears in the statement of financial position, reaching the statement of profit or loss only through depreciation. Revenue expenditure is debited to an expense account and is moved into the statement of profit or loss when the year closes. If the saw is bought on credit from Norwood Machinery Supplies, the journal entry is debit Machinery 9,600, credit Norwood Machinery Supplies 9,600.

## Capital and revenue receipts

A **capital receipt** is money received that is not income from trading: the owner paying in capital, a loan, or the proceeds of selling a non-current asset. A **revenue receipt** is income earned from running the business, such as sales, rent received and commission received.

| Receipt | Type | Credited to |
|---|---|---|
| Old lathe sold for 2,100 | Capital | Disposal account |
| Owner pays in 5,000 | Capital | Capital account |
| Bank loan of 20,000 | Capital | Loan account (a liability) |
| Sales of cabinets | Revenue | Sales |
| Rent received or commission received | Revenue | Rent received or commission received |

Revenue receipts are credited to income accounts and are transferred to the statement of profit or loss. Capital receipts are not income. A loan and the owner's capital stay in the statement of financial position; when a non-current asset is sold, only the resulting profit or loss on disposal is taken to the statement of profit or loss.

## Effect of incorrect treatment

Thornlea's draft profit was 41,500. It depreciates machinery at 10% a year on cost, straight-line, charging a whole year in the year an asset is bought. Four items were misposted.

| Item | Posted wrongly | Effect on profit | Effect on non-current assets |
|---|---|---|---|
| 750 wiring for a new machine | Debited to wages | Charged 750 instead of 75 depreciation, so understated by 675 | Machinery understated by 675 |
| 460 replacement blades | Debited to machinery, then depreciated | Charged 46 instead of 460, so overstated by 414 | Machinery overstated by 414 |
| 5,000 paid in by the owner | Credited to sales | Overstated by 5,000 | None: bank was debited correctly |
| 1,200 rent received for sub-letting | Credited to the capital account | Understated by 1,200 | None |

```
Corrected profit
Draft profit                        41,500
Add: wiring capitalised, net of depreciation   675
Less: blades expensed, net of depreciation     414
Less: capital wrongly counted as sales       5,000
Add: rent received counted as capital        1,200
Corrected profit                    37,961
```

Net effect on non-current assets: 675 less 414 gives an increase of 261. Treating revenue expenditure as capital overstates profit and assets, but by less than the full amount because some depreciation has been charged. Treating capital as revenue does the opposite. A receipt put in the wrong class changes profit but leaves asset values alone, unless an asset sold is left in the books.

## What depreciation is

**Depreciation** is the share of a non-current asset's cost consumed during an accounting period by wear and tear, the passage of time or obsolescence. No cash leaves the business when it is charged. It is not money set aside to replace the asset.

It must be accounted for because the asset helps earn revenue over several years, so its cost should be matched against those years, not charged all in the year of purchase. Without it, profit would be overstated and the statement of financial position would show assets at more than a realistic value.

## Three methods

**Straight-line** gives the same charge every year. A printing press costs 48,000, is expected to last seven years and to be worth 6,000 at the end: (48,000 − 6,000) ÷ 7 = 6,000 a year.

**Reducing balance** charges a fixed percentage of net book value, so the charge falls each year. A computer network costs 15,000 and is depreciated at 40%.

```
Year   Net book value at start   Depreciation   Net book value at end
1               15,000              6,000              9,000
2                9,000              3,600              5,400
3                5,400              2,160              3,240
```

**Revaluation** suits collections of small items. Hand tools are valued at 3,150 at the start of the year, 820 of tools are bought, and they are valued at 2,990 at the end. Depreciation = 3,150 + 820 − 2,990 = 980.

## Choosing a method

| Asset | Suitable method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Premises, machinery, fixtures and fittings | Straight-line | Benefit is spread fairly evenly |
| Motor vehicles, computer equipment | Reducing balance | Heavier loss of value and out-of-date risk early on |
| Loose tools and similar small items | Revaluation | Items are too many and too cheap to track one by one |

Follow any method, rate or policy a question states. Once chosen, a method is applied consistently each year.

## Recording depreciation, purchase and sale

**Depreciation:** debit statement of profit or loss, credit provision for depreciation. The asset account stays at cost, so net book value is cost less the provision. Under revaluation the tools account is usually credited until it equals the closing valuation; follow the layout the question gives.

**Purchase:** debit the non-current asset account, credit bank, or the supplier if bought on credit.

**Sale:** use the disposal account. Debit it with the asset's cost and credit it with the asset's accumulated depreciation and the proceeds; whatever is needed to balance it is the profit or loss. Profit on disposal equals proceeds minus net book value.

Wexford Print Works has a year end of 31 December 2028. It depreciates machinery at 10% a year on cost, straight-line, for each complete month owned. At 1 January 2028 the machinery account held three machines: A, bought 1 January 2025 for 30,000; B, bought 1 July 2026 for 24,000; and C, bought 1 January 2027 for 18,000. The provision for depreciation was 14,400 (A 9,000, B 3,600, C 1,800).

- 1 April: machine D bought on credit from Norwood Machinery Supplies for 27,600.
- 1 October: machine A sold for 20,000 by bank transfer.

Machine A was owned for nine months of 2028, so its 2028 charge is 30,000 × 10% × 9/12 = 2,250, and its accumulated depreciation is 9,000 + 2,250 = 11,250. Net book value is 30,000 − 11,250 = 18,750, so **profit on disposal is 20,000 − 18,750 = 1,250**. The year's charge is A 2,250 + B 2,400 + C 1,800 + D 2,070 (27,600 × 10% × 9/12) = 8,520.

## Common errors

- Putting delivery and installation of a new asset into expenses, or putting repairs into the asset account.
- Crediting the proceeds of selling an asset to sales, or a loan to income.
- Applying the reducing balance percentage to original cost every year.
- Crediting depreciation to the asset account (when a provision account is used) or leaving the sold asset's depreciation in the provision.
- Comparing proceeds with cost instead of net book value.
