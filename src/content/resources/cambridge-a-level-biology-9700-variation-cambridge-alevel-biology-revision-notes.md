## Core recall

### Causes of variation

- **Genetic**: different alleles (from mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation). Example: ABO blood group.
- **Environmental**: conditions during life. Example: a scar; language spoken.
- **Both**: genes set the potential, environment decides the outcome. Example: height; mass of a plant grown with different nutrients.
- Genetically identical individuals (identical twins, clones, cuttings) differ only through environment.

### Two types of variation

| | Discontinuous | Continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | Distinct categories | Range between extremes |
| Data | Counted (qualitative) | Measured (quantitative) |
| Graph | Bar chart | Histogram, often normal distribution |
| Genes | One or few | Many (polygenes) |
| Allele effect | Large | Small, additive |
| Environment | Little effect | Large effect |
| Example | ABO blood group | Height, leaf length |

### Polygenes in one paragraph

Many genes at different loci each contribute a small additive amount to the phenotype. The more contributing alleles, the larger the value. With n genes there are 2n + 1 genotype classes; environmental variation within each class merges them into a continuous range.

### Additive model from AaBbCc × AaBbCc

| Contributing alleles | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proportion (out of 64) | 1 | 6 | 15 | 20 | 15 | 6 | 1 |

### The t-test

```
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ÷ √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )
degrees of freedom = n₁ + n₂ − 2
```

- Use for: comparing **two means** of **continuous**, roughly **normally distributed** data.
- Null hypothesis: no significant difference between the means.
- t > critical value (p = 0.05) → significant → reject the null hypothesis.
- t < critical value → not significant → accept the null hypothesis.
- Critical values are given in the exam; the formula is given; degrees of freedom are **not**.

### Method checklist

1. Calculate both means.
2. Calculate both standard deviations; square them.
3. Divide each s² by its n; add; square root.
4. Divide the difference in means by this value.
5. Work out degrees of freedom.
6. Compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
7. State the conclusion about the null hypothesis in context.

### Chi-squared or t-test?

| Situation | Test |
|---|---|
| Comparing two means of measured data | t-test |
| Comparing observed and expected counts in categories | Chi-squared |

### Worked reminder: a short t-test

Means 12.0 and 10.4; s₁² = 1.8 and s₂² = 2.2; n = 10 in each sample.

```
√(1.8/10 + 2.2/10) = √0.40 = 0.632
t = (12.0 − 10.4) ÷ 0.632 = 2.53
degrees of freedom = 10 + 10 − 2 = 18; critical value 2.10
2.53 > 2.10, so the difference is significant
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Phenotypic variation | Differences in observable characteristics between individuals |
| Discontinuous variation | Variation with distinct categories and no intermediates |
| Continuous variation | Variation with a range of values between extremes |
| Polygenes | Many genes at different loci that together affect one characteristic |
| Additive effect | Each contributing allele adds a similar small amount to the phenotype |
| Normal distribution | Symmetrical, bell-shaped spread around the mean |
| Null hypothesis | The statement that there is no significant difference |
| Degrees of freedom | For the t-test, n₁ + n₂ − 2 |

## Exam traps

- Height is **genetic and environmental**, not genetic only.
- Continuous variation is about **many genes**, not "many alleles".
- t-test degrees of freedom: **n₁ + n₂ − 2** (chi-squared uses classes − 1).
- Use **s²** in the formula, not s.
- "Significant" means unlikely to be due to chance (p < 0.05); it does not identify the cause.
- Do not use a t-test on counts in categories.
- Give an example for each cause when the question says "with examples".
- Keep unrounded values until the final step.

## Self-test

1. Give an example of variation caused only by genes.
2. Give an example of variation caused by both genes and environment.
3. How could you show that variation in a plant's height is partly environmental?
4. Which type of variation gives a normal distribution?
5. Explain the genetic basis of discontinuous variation.
6. Explain the genetic basis of continuous variation.
7. How many height classes are possible with four additive genes?
8. State the null hypothesis for a t-test.
9. Two samples have n = 12 and n = 15. How many degrees of freedom?
10. t = 1.65 and the critical value is 2.06. What do you conclude?
11. Means are 18.0 and 15.0; s₁²/n₁ = 0.80 and s₂²/n₂ = 1.45. Calculate t.
12. Which test would compare numbers of offspring in four phenotype classes with a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio?

### Answers

1. ABO blood group.
2. Human height (or plant mass).
3. Grow genetically identical plants (cuttings or a clone) in different conditions; any differences are environmental.
4. Continuous variation.
5. One or a few genes with alleles of large effect give distinct phenotypes; the environment has little effect.
6. Many genes (polygenes), each with small additive effects, combined with environmental effects, give a range of values.
7. 2 × 4 + 1 = **9**.
8. There is no significant difference between the means of the two samples.
9. 12 + 15 − 2 = **25**.
10. Not significant; accept the null hypothesis: the difference could be due to chance.
11. √(0.80 + 1.45) = √2.25 = 1.5; t = 3.0 ÷ 1.5 = **2.0**.
12. The chi-squared test.
