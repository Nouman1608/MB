Quick-recall notes for the Shakespeare question. For the full method with a worked paragraph, use the
[Shakespeare study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-8702-section-a-shakespeare/).

## The task in one view

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Text | One play from the specification's list of six, studied as a whole |
| Question | One question on your play; an extract is printed |
| What you do | Write in detail about the extract, then about the play as a whole |
| Marks | 30, plus 4 for AO4 (34 of Paper 1's 64) |
| Split in AQA mark schemes | AO1 12, AO2 12, AO3 6, then AO4 4 |
| Book | Closed: quotations from the wider play come from memory |

## Core recall

- **"Presents"** means focus on Shakespeare's choices. Name the dramatist as the agent of every
  effect.
- **"Explore"** rewards more than one possible reading and a developing argument.
- **The extract** shows close analysis; **the whole play** shows development. Both bullets must be
  answered.
- **Drama, not novel.** Think about what an audience sees and hears: who is on stage, who speaks, who
  stays silent, who exits.
- **Metre carries meaning.** Iambic pentameter is a ten-syllable da-DUM line. A broken, shared or
  irregular line often marks tension, interruption or loss of control.
- **Mode switches matter.** Verse for formal or high-status speech, prose for comedy, low status,
  letters or madness, couplets to close a scene. A switch is often the most analysable moment in an
  extract.
- **Context belongs inside analysis.** Attach a contextual point to a specific line, and only if it
  explains why that line matters.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| Soliloquy | Speech by a character alone on stage | Private motive revealed to the audience only |
| Aside | Remark unheard by other characters | Audience becomes a confidant; creates irony |
| Dramatic irony | Audience knows more than a character | Lines gain a second meaning |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter | Control, status, seriousness |
| Caesura | Pause in the middle of a line | Hesitation, shock, a turn in thought |
| Enjambment | Sense runs over the line ending | Urgency, overflowing feeling |
| Stichomythia | Rapid exchange of single lines | Conflict, wit, rising tension |
| Hamartia | A fatal flaw or error in a tragic hero | Responsibility and downfall |
| Foil | Character who contrasts with another | Highlights the other's qualities |
| Foreshadowing | Hint of later events | Structure; tension; inevitability |
| Motif | Repeated image or idea | Tracks a theme across the whole play |
| Embedded stage direction | Action implied in dialogue | Shows awareness of performance |

## Planning in five minutes

1. Circle the focus word in the question.
2. Annotate four or five details in the extract linked to it.
3. Write a one-line argument covering the whole play.
4. List two or three other moments, ideally one earlier and one later, each with a short quotation or
   precise event.
5. Choose one context point that fits a specific moment.

## Paragraph checklist

- Topic sentence that answers the question.
- Short embedded quotation, or a precise reference to an event.
- Analysis of a method: a word, an image, the metre, the scene's structure, a stage action.
- Effect on the audience, with a second reading if one exists.
- Link to the play as a whole or to context, where relevant.

## Exam traps

- **Writing about the extract alone.** The question has two bullets, and an answer that
  covers only one cannot show the whole-text knowledge the task asks for.
- **Writing about the play alone.** Starting outside the extract ignores the first bullet.
- **Character as a real person.** "She feels guilty" is weaker than "Shakespeare presents her guilt
  through...".
- **Naming techniques without effects.** "Shakespeare uses a metaphor" earns little on its own.
- **Long quotations.** Copying several lines wastes time and shows less analysis than two well-chosen
  words.
- **Generic history.** A paragraph about the period that does not explain the line in front of you.
- **Forgetting AO4.** Spelling, punctuation and sentence variety earn 4 marks here and nowhere else on
  Paper 1.
- **Overrunning.** This question carries just over half the paper's marks; leave enough time for the
  novel in Section B.

## Context prompts

Use whichever fits your play and question:

- The original audience: mixed classes standing and seated, used to listening closely to language
  because the stage had little scenery.
- Female roles played by boys or young men, since women did not act on the public stage in
  Shakespeare's lifetime.
- Beliefs about order: monarch and subject, parent and child, husband and wife.
- Religion and the supernatural, where the play uses them.
- Genre expectations: a tragedy moves towards death and loss; a comedy towards marriage and
  reconciliation, often with darker undertones.

## Self-test

1. What two things does the Shakespeare question ask you to write about?
2. How many marks are available, including AO4?
3. What is the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?
4. What is iambic pentameter?
5. Give two reasons a character might speak in prose.
6. What might a rhyming couplet at the end of a scene suggest?
7. What does a caesura in the middle of a line often show?
8. Why should you write "Shakespeare presents..." rather than describe a character directly?
9. Name two kinds of context that could be relevant to a Shakespeare play.
10. How should you divide your time between the extract and the rest of the play?
11. Which assessment objective covers spelling and punctuation, and is it assessed in Section B?
12. What is dramatic irony and why is it useful in an answer?

## Answers

1. The extract in detail, and the play as a whole.
2. 30 marks plus 4 for AO4, making 34.
3. A soliloquy is spoken by a character alone on stage; an aside is a brief remark that other
   characters present on stage do not hear.
4. A line of verse with ten syllables in five unstressed-stressed pairs (da-DUM).
5. Any two of: lower social status, comedy, letters or documents, a breakdown in the character's
   mind, informal or intimate conversation.
6. Closure, a decisive or proverbial conclusion, or a neatness that the audience may distrust.
7. Hesitation, shock, a change of direction in thought, or emotion interrupting speech.
8. Because the marks are for analysing the writer's choices, not for treating characters as real
   people.
9. Any two of: the theatre and its audience, all-male casting, beliefs about hierarchy, gender,
   religion or the supernatural, genre expectations.
10. Give both real weight; roughly balanced, with enough on the extract to analyse closely and enough
    on the wider play to show development.
11. AO4. No: on Paper 1 it is assessed only in Section A, the Shakespeare question.
12. The audience knows something a character does not, so lines carry a second meaning; it lets you
    discuss audience response and structure together.
