## Section A

**1.** State **two** sources of short-term finance. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** sources of long-term finance. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by working capital. **[1]**

**4.** State the difference between a cash discount and a trade discount. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate the revenue from selling 850 units at $14 each. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A shop sells 2,000 units at $18 each. Cost of sales is $21,600 and expenses are $9,000.

**(a)** Calculate revenue. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate gross profit. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the gross profit margin as a percentage. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate net profit. **[1]**

**7.** A trader's current assets are: stock $6,200, debtors $2,300, cash $1,000. Current liabilities are: creditors $4,100 and overdraft $1,400.

**(a)** Calculate working capital. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain what the answer shows about the business. **[1]**

**8.** A retailer buys a coat for $48 and sells it for $72. Calculate the mark-up percentage. **[3]**

**9.** A wholesaler wants a mark-up of 30% on goods that cost $70. Calculate the selling price. **[2]**

**10.** A business is owed $30,000 in invoices. A factor pays 85% now and charges a 2% fee on the invoice total.

**(a)** Calculate the cash paid at once. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the fee. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why the business may use debt factoring. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **two** differences between ordinary shares and debentures as sources of long-term finance. **[4]**

**12.** A bakery needs $4,000 to cover wages for the next two months, until customers settle their accounts. Recommend a suitable source of finance and justify it. **[3]**

**13.** A shop's net profit is falling. Explain **two** ways in which the owner could improve it, and one risk of each. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: trade credit; debt factoring; hire purchase; leasing; sale and leaseback; overdraft; selling assets; owner's savings; retained profit [1 each].

**2.** Any two from: ordinary shares; preference shares; loans; debentures; venture capital; crowdfunding [1 each].

**3.** Current assets minus current liabilities, the money available for day-to-day running [1].

**4.** A cash discount is given for paying promptly [1]. A trade discount is given by a seller to another business regardless of when it pays [1].

**5.** 850 × 14 = $11,900 [1].

**6.** **(a)** 2,000 × 18 = $36,000 [1].

**(b)** 36,000 − 21,600 = $14,400 [1].

**(c)** 14,400 ÷ 36,000 × 100 [1] = 40% [1].

**(d)** 14,400 − 9,000 = $5,400 [1].

**7.** **(a)** Current assets = 6,200 + 2,300 + 1,000 = $9,500 [1]. Current liabilities = 4,100 + 1,400 = $5,500 [1]. Working capital = 9,500 − 5,500 = $4,000 [1].

**(b)** The business has enough short-term assets to pay its short-term debts [1].

**8.** Profit on each coat = 72 − 48 = $24 [1]. Mark-up = 24 ÷ 48 × 100 [1] = 50% [1].

**9.** Mark-up = 30% × 70 = $21 [1]. Selling price = 70 + 21 = $91 [1].

**10.** **(a)** 85% × 30,000 = $25,500 [1].

**(b)** 2% × 30,000 = $600 [1].

**(c)** The business gets most of the money at once, which helps cash flow [1], and does not have to chase customers for payment [1].

**11.** Ordinary shares give shareholders ownership and a vote [1], whereas debentures are loans and holders are creditors with no vote [1]. Ordinary shares pay a dividend that varies with profit and need not be paid [1], while debentures pay fixed interest that must be paid whether or not there is a profit [1].

**12.** An overdraft [1], because it is short-term and flexible [1], and interest is only charged on the amount used until customers have paid [1]. Accept debt factoring or trade credit with justification.

**13.** Any two ways, each with a risk, 3 marks each: reduce expenses, for example by cutting electricity use or cheaper rent [1], so net profit rises [1]; risk: cutting too much may lower service quality [1]. Raise prices [1], so each sale brings more profit [1]; risk: customers may buy less or go to competitors [1]. Improve advertising or sales promotion [1] to raise sales [1]; risk: the cost of advertising may be more than the extra profit [1]. Find cheaper suppliers [1] to lower cost of sales [1]; risk: lower quality [1]. Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 6(c): dividing by cost of sales instead of revenue.
- Question 7: including the overdraft as a current asset.
- Question 8: dividing by the selling price (that gives margin, not mark-up).
- Question 9: giving the mark-up ($21) rather than the selling price.
- Question 10: taking 85% of the wrong figure, or forgetting the fee is a cost.
- Question 13: no risk given.

## Quick reference for checking your answers

| Item | Key point |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Net profit | Gross profit − expenses |
| Margin | ÷ revenue |
| Mark-up | ÷ cost |
| Working capital | Current assets − current liabilities |
