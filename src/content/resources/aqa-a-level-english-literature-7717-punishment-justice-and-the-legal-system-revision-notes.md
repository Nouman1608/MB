## Three kinds of justice

1. **Legal**: courts, magistrates, police, prisons, the gallows.
2. **Retributive or private**: revenge, or a detective acting outside the courts.
3. **Moral or divine**: God, fate, conscience, or the narrative's own punishments.

Most texts set two of these against each other. Name which kind a question is really about.

## Text grid

| Text | Main kind of justice | Key moment | Argument to make |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Oliver Twist* | Legal, satirised | Oliver before Mr Fang; the Dodger's trial; Fagin's condemned cell | The law threatens the innocent and turns punishment into spectacle |
| *Atonement* | Legal, mistaken | Robbie convicted on Briony's word | Class prejudice makes injustice easy; the guilty man prospers |
| "The Ballad of Reading Gaol" | Legal, from inside prison | The condemned soldier's last weeks; quicklime burial | Punishment degrades all; "each man kills the thing he loves" widens guilt |
| *Hamlet* | Private retribution | The Ghost's command; Laertes as foil | The criminal is the king, so the law cannot act; revenge has a cost |
| "The Laboratory" | Private, jealous | Buying the poison | No court at all; the reader judges |
| *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* | Detective outside the courts | Poirot's offer to Dr Sheppard | Order restored privately, bypassing a trial |
| *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* | Supernatural | Death and Life-in-Death dicing for the crew | Punishment far beyond human proportion |
| *Brighton Rock* | Secular against religious | Ida's "right and wrong" against "good and evil" | Two moral systems compete; the police barely matter |
| "Peter Grimes" | Community and conscience | Called to account; forbidden another boy | Shunning and haunting replace a trial |

## Links to related elements

- "How far there is a moral purpose and restoration of order": does justice restore order, or only
  end the story?
- "The sense that there will be a resolution and the criminal will be punished": which texts fulfil
  this expectation, and which frustrate it?
- "The victims of crime and the inclusion of suffering": justice for whom?

## Paragraph frame for a justice essay

1. **Define** the kind of justice the question means.
2. **Locate** the key scene where that justice acts or fails.
3. **Analyse** the methods: legal register, perspective, setting, structure.
4. **Weigh** a second kind of justice that the text sets against the first.
5. **Judge** whether order is restored, and for whom.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Retribution | Punishment given in return for a wrong |
| Revenge tragedy | Drama driven by a quest for vengeance, often ending in multiple deaths |
| Miscarriage of justice | Conviction of an innocent person |
| Accusation | A charge of wrongdoing, true or false |
| Magistrate | An official who hears minor cases and commits others for trial |
| Condemned cell | Where a prisoner awaits execution |
| Poetic justice | Punishment or reward delivered by the narrative itself |
| Vigilante justice | Justice taken into private hands |
| Spectacle | Punishment staged publicly, for an audience |
| Foil | A character whose contrast highlights another's qualities |

## Context to use

- **Victorian criminal justice**: public execution outside Newgate, transportation, and the
  workhouse as institutions of punishment in *Oliver Twist*.
- **Wilde's imprisonment** in Reading Gaol and the poem's publication under his cell designation, C.3.3; Wilde's prisoner's-eye view gives the poem its authority.
- **Class and the law** in the 1930s setting of *Atonement*.
- **Religious frameworks**: damnation and grace in *Brighton Rock*; revenge and the soul in *Hamlet*.

## Unseen checklist

- Who holds power, and through what register (legal titles, commands, formal language)?
- Is the accused guilty, innocent or uncertain?
- Whose perspective controls sympathy?
- Is punishment proportionate, cruel or spectacular?
- Is order being restored, or is justice failing?

## Exam traps

- Assuming crime writing always trusts the law.
- Equating revenge with justice without argument.
- Using "justice" without naming which kind.
- Listing historical facts instead of linking context to a scene.
- Forgetting "injustice" and "accusation", which AQA lists beside punishment.

## Self-test

1. Name the three kinds of justice.
2. Who is the magistrate before whom Oliver appears, and how does Dickens present him?
3. How does the Artful Dodger treat his trial?
4. On whose word is Robbie convicted, and who actually committed the assault?
5. Under what name was "The Ballad of Reading Gaol" published?
6. Why can the law not help Hamlet?
7. How does Laertes function in relation to revenge?
8. What does Poirot offer Dr Sheppard?
9. Who dice for the crew in *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*?
10. What contrast does Greene draw between Ida and Pinkie?
11. How is Peter Grimes punished?
12. Give two related elements from AQA's list that connect to this one.

## Answers

1. Legal; retributive or private; moral or divine.
2. Mr Fang, presented as a bullying, unjust magistrate who nearly convicts the innocent, fainting boy.
3. As a performance, mocking the court and demanding his rights as an Englishman.
4. Briony's; Paul Marshall.
5. Wilde's cell designation in Reading Gaol, C.3.3.
6. Because the murderer is the king, the source of legal justice.
7. As a foil: he seeks revenge at once, exposing Hamlet's scruples, and is manipulated by Claudius.
8. The chance to take his own life rather than face trial, partly to spare his sister.
9. Death and Life-in-Death.
10. Ida thinks in terms of "right and wrong"; Pinkie and Rose think in terms of "good and evil".
11. By the town's judgement and shunning, and by psychological torment as he is haunted on the water.
12. Any two: how far there is a moral purpose and restoration of order; the sense that the criminal
    will be punished; the victims of crime and the inclusion of suffering.
