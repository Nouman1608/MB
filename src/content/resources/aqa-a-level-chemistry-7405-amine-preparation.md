## Amines: names and classes

Amines are derived from ammonia, NH₃, by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms with alkyl or aryl groups. The functional group contains nitrogen with a lone pair.

| Class | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | RNH₂ | Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ |
| Secondary | R₂NH | Dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH |
| Tertiary | R₃N | Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N |
| Aromatic (primary) | ArNH₂ | Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂ |

Names can be written as alkylamine (propylamine) or as alkanamine (propan-1-amine). Phenylamine has the NH₂ group attached directly to the benzene ring. The class (primary, secondary or tertiary) depends on how many carbon groups are bonded to nitrogen, not on the carbon chain.

## Route 1: halogenoalkane with ammonia

This is a nucleophilic substitution, covered in the Halogenoalkanes sub-topic.

```
CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br
```

- **Reagents and conditions**: a large excess of ammonia dissolved in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube (under pressure).
- Two molecules of ammonia are used: one as the nucleophile, one to remove H⁺ from the intermediate.
- The product is a **primary amine**.

### The limitation

The primary amine formed still has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is also a nucleophile. It can attack another halogenoalkane, giving a secondary amine, then a tertiary amine and finally a quaternary ammonium salt (see the Nucleophilic properties sub-topic). The excess of ammonia lowers the chance of this because the ammonia molecules greatly outnumber the amine molecules, but a mixture of products is still formed. The yield of the primary amine is therefore limited.

## Route 2: reduction of a nitrile

A nitrile, R–C≡N, is reduced to a primary amine, R–CH₂NH₂.

```
CH₃CH₂C≡N + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂
```

- **Reagents**: lithium tetrahydridoaluminate (LiAlH₄) in dry ether; or hydrogen gas with a nickel catalyst at high temperature and pressure. The specification states only that the nitrile is reduced, so either is acceptable if you give conditions.
- The product is a pure **primary amine**, with no secondary or tertiary amine, because the nitrogen atom is not attacked by a second reagent molecule.
- The nitrile itself comes from a halogenoalkane and potassium cyanide, so the route has **two steps** and **adds one carbon** to the chain:

```
CH₃CH₂Br  →  CH₃CH₂CN  →  CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂
bromoethane    propanenitrile    propylamine
```

Compared with route 1, this gives a better yield of the primary amine and a longer carbon chain, at the cost of an extra step and the toxicity of cyanide.

## Route 3: reduction of a nitro compound

Aromatic amines are made from nitro compounds, which come from the nitration of benzene rings.

```
C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O
nitrobenzene       phenylamine
```

- **Reagents**: tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, heated under reflux. (Hydrogen with a nickel catalyst is an alternative.)
- In the acid, the product is present as the salt **phenylammonium chloride**, C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻, not the free amine.
- **Aqueous sodium hydroxide** is added at the end to deprotonate the salt and release the free phenylamine:

```
C₆H₅NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → C₆H₅NH₂ + H₂O
```

### Why this matters

Phenylamine is the starting material for **dyes**. The specification states that aromatic amines are used in the manufacture of dyes. Together with the nitration step, the sequence benzene → nitrobenzene → phenylamine is a standard two-step synthesis.

## Choosing a route

| Target | Best route | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A primary aliphatic amine, same number of carbons as the halogenoalkane | Halogenoalkane with excess ammonia | One step, but a mixture is likely |
| A primary aliphatic amine, one more carbon than the halogenoalkane | Halogenoalkane with KCN, then reduce | Pure primary amine, longer chain |
| Phenylamine | Nitrobenzene with Sn and HCl, then NaOH | The only route in the specification for aromatic amines |

## Reagents and what they do: a summary

| Reagent | Role | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Excess NH₃ in ethanol | Nucleophile and proton acceptor | Halogenoalkane to primary amine |
| KCN in aqueous ethanol | Cyanide nucleophile; lengthens the chain | Halogenoalkane to nitrile |
| LiAlH₄ in dry ether | Reducing agent (source of H⁻) | Nitrile to primary amine |
| H₂ and Ni | Catalytic hydrogenation | Nitrile or nitro compound to amine |
| Sn and concentrated HCl | Reducing agent in acid | Nitrobenzene to phenylammonium chloride |
| NaOH(aq) | Base; removes H⁺ from the salt | Phenylammonium ion to phenylamine |

Notice that every preparation uses either a nucleophilic substitution or a reduction. When you see a synthesis question, ask which of these two changes gets from the starting material to the target, and then check how many carbons the target has compared with the start.

## Worked example 1: a two-step synthesis

**Question.** Starting from 1-bromopropane, prepare butylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂.

- Step 1: heat 1-bromopropane under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol and water. CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + Br⁻.
- Step 2: reduce butanenitrile with LiAlH₄ in dry ether (or H₂ and Ni). CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂.

Direct reaction with ammonia would give propylamine (three carbons), so the nitrile step is needed to get four carbons.

## Worked example 2: balancing the reduction

**Question.** Show that 6[H] are needed to reduce nitrobenzene to phenylamine.

```
C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O
O atoms: 2 on the left, 2 in 2H₂O on the right ✓
H atoms: 5 + 6 = 11 on the left; 7 (in C₆H₅NH₂) + 4 (in 2H₂O) = 11 on the right ✓
```

The two oxygen atoms leave as water, each needing two hydrogens, and the nitrogen gains two hydrogens: 4 + 2 = 6.

## Worked example 3: yield of phenylamine

12.3 g of nitrobenzene (Mᵣ = 123.0) is reduced to phenylamine (Mᵣ = 93.0). 6.51 g of purified phenylamine is obtained.

```
n(nitrobenzene)       = 12.3 ÷ 123.0 = 0.100 mol
theoretical mass      = 0.100 × 93.0 = 9.30 g
percentage yield      = 6.51 ÷ 9.30 × 100 = 70.0 %
```

The ratio is 1 : 1 because each nitrobenzene molecule gives one phenylamine molecule.

## Worked example 4: from benzene to a dye intermediate

**Question.** Outline how benzene can be converted into phenylamine.

1. Nitration: concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ at about 50 °C gives nitrobenzene.
2. Reduction: tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, heat under reflux; then add excess aqueous NaOH to release phenylamine.

## Common mistakes

- Using dilute ammonia, or forgetting that excess ammonia in ethanol in a sealed tube is needed.
- Claiming the ammonia route gives a pure primary amine.
- Counting the carbons wrongly in the nitrile route: the nitrile carbon becomes the CH₂ of the amine, so the amine has the same number of carbons as the nitrile.
- Writing phenylamine, not phenylammonium chloride, as the product immediately after reduction with tin and hydrochloric acid, and then forgetting the NaOH step.
- Giving LiAlH₄ without dry ether, or using aqueous conditions.
- Balancing the nitro reduction with the wrong number of [H] or leaving out water.
- Calling an amine primary because it has a long chain; look at the nitrogen.
- Mixing up the two reduction reactions in the specification: nitrile to amine, and nitro compound to amine.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare an amine**: give reagents, solvent and conditions for every step.
- **Compare routes**: refer to purity of product, number of steps and chain length.
- **Equations**: use [H] for reductions and check that atoms balance.
- **Synthesis**: name each intermediate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a sealed tube needed for the ammonia reaction?**
Ammonia is a gas and would escape from an open vessel when heated. A sealed tube also keeps the reaction under pressure.

**Why is sodium hydroxide added after tin and hydrochloric acid?**
The acid converts the amine into its salt. Sodium hydroxide removes the extra proton to give the free amine.

**Can amines be made from alcohols directly?**
Not by a route in this specification. The halogenoalkane route starts from a halogenoalkane.
