## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polymer | Long molecule made of many repeating monomers |
| Nucleotide | Monomer of DNA: sugar + phosphate + one base |
| Double helix | Two DNA strands wound around each other |
| Complementary bases | A with T, G with C |
| Gene | Section of DNA that codes for one protein |
| Triplet | Group of three bases that codes for one amino acid |
| mRNA | Single-stranded copy of a gene; carries the code to the cytoplasm |
| tRNA | Carries amino acids to the ribosome |
| Metabolism | All the chemical reactions in a cell or organism |
| Enzyme | Biological catalyst (a protein) |
| Active site | The part of the enzyme with a specific shape that the substrate fits into |
| Denatured | Enzyme's shape, including the active site, is permanently altered, so it cannot work |

## DNA in five lines (B1.2a to B1.2c)

- DNA is a polymer made of nucleotides.
- Two strands form a double helix.
- Each nucleotide has the same sugar and phosphate group, plus one of four bases: A, T, G or C.
- Sugar and phosphate form the outside of each strand; bases pair in the middle.
- Pairs: **A-T and G-C** only.

### Method: find percentages of bases

1. Find the partner of the base you are given (same percentage).
2. Add the two known percentages.
3. Subtract from 100.
4. Divide by 2 for the other pair.

Example: 35% thymine gives 35% adenine, 70% in total, 30% left, so **15% each of G and C**.

## Protein synthesis (B1.2d) - Higher Tier only

1. DNA unzips around the gene.
2. A copy is made as mRNA in the nucleus (**transcription**).
3. mRNA moves out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm.
4. The mRNA sequence is translated at a ribosome (**translation**); tRNA carries the amino acids.
5. Amino acids join in the order set by the mRNA. The chain folds into the protein.

Mnemonic for the stages: copy in the nucleus, read in the cytoplasm.

### Method: gene to protein

1. Write the template strand in triplets.
2. Swap each base for its partner to give mRNA triplets (RNA uses U instead of T, so A pairs with U).
3. Look up each triplet in the table the question gives you.
4. Write the amino acids in order.

## DNA structure and the protein made (B1.2e) - Higher Tier only

- Sequence of bases in the gene → sequence of triplets → order of amino acids.
- Different amino acid order → different folding → different shape → different function.
- For an enzyme, a changed shape can mean a changed active site, so the substrate no longer fits.
- A base change may alter one amino acid, or none (the new triplet can specify the same amino acid as the old one). A deleted or added base shifts every later triplet.
- Number of bases = number of amino acids × 3 (coding sequence only).

## Enzymes (B1.2g)

| Idea | Key points |
|---|---|
| Role | Speed up metabolic reactions; not used up |
| Active site | Specific shape; substrate binds here |
| Lock and key | Substrate shape matches the active site, so each enzyme is specific |
| Denaturation | Shape of active site lost, permanent; caused by high temperature or extreme pH |

### Effects on rate

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature (up to optimum) | Rate increases | More collisions with more energy |
| Temperature (above optimum) | Rate falls to zero | Denaturation |
| pH | Rate highest at optimum, falls on either side | Active site shape changes |
| Substrate concentration | Rises, then levels off | All active sites occupied |
| Enzyme concentration | Rises in proportion while substrate is plentiful | More active sites |

Optimum conditions differ between enzymes. Not every enzyme has an optimum of 37 °C, and not every optimum pH is 7.

## Experiments and rates (B1.2f)

- Catalase with hydrogen peroxide: volume of oxygen in a set time.
- Amylase with starch: time for iodine to stop turning blue-black.
- Change one variable; control the others; repeat and take a mean.
- Water bath for temperature; buffer solutions for pH.

### Method: calculate a rate

1. Rate = amount of product ÷ time (or amount of substrate used ÷ time).
2. Write the unit as a compound measure: cm³/s, g/min.
3. From a graph, rate = gradient. Use a small interval at the start for the initial rate.
4. If only a time is measured, 1 ÷ time is a measure of rate: a shorter time means a faster reaction.

Percentage change = (new - old) ÷ old × 100.

Worked reminder: 45 cm³ of oxygen in 90 s is 45 ÷ 90 = **0.50 cm³/s**. If the rate falls from 0.50 to 0.20 cm³/s, the change is (0.20 - 0.50) ÷ 0.50 × 100 = **60% decrease**.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Transcription and translation | Transcription copies the gene into mRNA in the nucleus; translation reads the mRNA in the cytoplasm to build the protein |
| DNA and mRNA | DNA is a double-stranded helix that stays in the nucleus; mRNA is a single-stranded copy that carries the code out |
| mRNA and tRNA | mRNA carries the code; tRNA carries amino acids |
| Slowed and denatured | Cold slows an enzyme and the effect can be reversed; heat or extreme pH denatures it permanently |
| Optimum and limiting factor | The optimum is the best value of a variable; a limiting factor is whatever stops the rate rising further |
| Substrate and product | Substrate goes in and fits the active site; product comes out |

## Describing graphs

A good description has three parts: what the line does, a number from the graph to prove it, and a reason.

1. Say what happens, such as "the rate rises then levels off".
2. Quote data with units, such as "from 0.35 to 0.55 cm³/s between 35 and 45 °C".
3. Explain using enzyme ideas: collisions, active sites, denaturation.

Second reminder: a pH investigation gives the times for starch to disappear as 120 s at pH 5, 60 s at pH 6 and 40 s at pH 7. Using 1000 ÷ time, the rates are 8.3, 16.7 and 25.0. Doubling the rate from pH 5 to pH 6 shows that pH 5 is further from the optimum. The data alone do not show where the optimum is; testing more values around pH 7 would.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the monomer of DNA.
2. Write the strand paired with GGATC.
3. A DNA sample is 35% thymine. State the percentage of cytosine.
4. (Higher Tier only) Template CAT TGC. Write the mRNA.
5. (Higher Tier only) How many bases are needed to code for 50 amino acids?
6. (Higher Tier only) Where does translation take place, and what does tRNA carry?
7. Calculate the rate when 45 cm³ of oxygen forms in 90 s.
8. Starch disappears in 125 s. Calculate 1000 ÷ time as a measure of rate.
9. Explain why no product forms at a very high temperature.
10. Explain why a substrate concentration graph levels off.
11. A rate falls from 0.50 to 0.20 cm³/s. Find the percentage decrease.
12. Give two things to control when testing the effect of pH on amylase.

### Answers

1. Nucleotide.
2. CCTAG.
3. 15%.
4. GUA ACG.
5. 150.
6. In the cytoplasm, at a ribosome; tRNA carries amino acids.
7. 0.50 cm³/s.
8. 8.0 (per 1000 s).
9. The enzyme is denatured: its active site has been permanently altered, so the substrate cannot bind.
10. All the active sites are occupied, so extra substrate cannot be processed faster; another factor is limiting.
11. 60% decrease.
12. Any two from: temperature, volume of enzyme, concentration of enzyme, volume and concentration of starch.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "protein" or "sugar" when asked what DNA is made of.
- Pairing A with C, or A with G, in a complementary strand.
- Saying enzymes are "killed" or "die" instead of denatured.
- Saying that cold denatures an enzyme.
- Treating the active site as the whole enzyme. It is one region, and its shape matches the substrate.
- Saying a graph "stays the same" without saying why: say the active sites are saturated or the substrate has run out.
- Missing units on a rate, or dividing the wrong way round.
- Not controlling variables in an experiment plan, or changing two at once.
- Mixing up transcription (making mRNA in the nucleus) with translation (making the protein in the cytoplasm).
- Suggesting every enzyme works best at 37 °C or at pH 7.
