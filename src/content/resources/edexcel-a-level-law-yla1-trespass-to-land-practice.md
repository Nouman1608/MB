These questions cover content area 2.3.9, "Trespass to land": unlawful entry, intentional and direct interference, and continuing trespass and remedies.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define trespass to land. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by saying that trespass to land is actionable per se. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** ways in which a person can commit a trespass other than by walking onto land. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between trespass to land and private nuisance. **[3]**

**5.** State **two** defences to an action for trespass to land. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Owen believes a strip of grass between his house and his neighbour Priti's house belongs to him. He mows it every week and plants flowers there. A survey shows it belongs to Priti.

Advise Priti on whether Owen has committed a trespass. **[4]**

**7.** Hassan invites a plumber, Will, to fix a leaking kitchen tap. While in the house Will goes upstairs and looks through Hassan's bedroom drawers.

Explain whether Will is a trespasser. **[3]**

**8.** A property company builds offices next to Lucia's shop. The arm of its tower crane swings over the shop roof several times a day, about 20 metres up. It causes no damage.

Explain whether Lucia has a claim and which remedy she should seek. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the meaning of a continuing trespass, using **one** decided case, and why it matters for remedies. **[4]**

**10.** Bianca tells a door-to-door salesman, who has walked up her garden path, to leave. He stays on the doorstep arguing for ten minutes, so she pushes him firmly back to the gate.

Explain whether the salesman was a trespasser and whether Bianca acted lawfully. **[5]**

**11.** Explain, using **two** decided cases, how the law of trespass applies to the use of a public highway. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the law of trespass to land gives adequate protection to people in possession of land while respecting the interests of others. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** An **unjustifiable, direct and intentional interference** [1] with **land in the possession of another** [1].

**2.** The claimant can sue **without proving any damage or loss** [1]; the interference itself is the wrong, so at least **nominal damages** are available [1].

**3.** Any three [1] each: placing or throwing objects onto land or against a wall (*Westripp v Baldock*); structures or objects overhanging the airspace (*Kelsen*; *Anchor Brewhouse*; *Laiqat v Majid*); interfering beneath the surface (*Bocardo*); remaining after permission is revoked (*Robson v Hallett*); exceeding the permission given (*The Calgarth*); misusing a highway (*Harrison v Duke of Rutland*); leaving objects on land (continuing trespass).

**4.** Trespass is a **direct** physical interference with land [1] and is **actionable per se** [1]. Nuisance is an **indirect** interference with the use or enjoyment of land, such as noise, smells or roots, and the claimant must show **damage** [1].

**5.** Any two [1] each: **licence**; **lawful authority** (such as police powers under PACE 1984); **statutory access** (Access to Neighbouring Land Act 1992); **necessity** (*Rigby v Chief Constable of Northamptonshire*).

**6.** Owen has **entered** Priti's land directly by mowing and planting [1]. Trespass requires an **intention to enter**, not an intention to trespass [1]; his honest **mistake** about the boundary is **no defence** (*Basely v Clarkson* (1681)) [1]. He has committed trespass, and the flowers left there are a **continuing trespass**; Priti can claim at least nominal damages and an injunction [1].

**7.** Will entered with a **licence** to fix the tap [1]. A visitor who goes beyond the purpose of the permission becomes a **trespasser** [1], as Scrutton LJ explained in *The Calgarth* [1927]; by going upstairs and searching drawers, Will exceeded his licence and was a trespasser in those parts of the house [1].

**8.** The crane arm passes through Lucia's **airspace** at a height needed for the ordinary use and enjoyment of her property [1], so it is a **trespass**, as in *Anchor Brewhouse v Berkley House* [1987] [1]. It is not like an aircraft at a reasonable height (*Bernstein v Skyviews* [1978]) [1]. Trespass is **actionable per se**, so the lack of damage does not matter [1]. Her best remedy is an **injunction** to stop the crane oversailing, though the court might award **damages in lieu** if an injunction would be oppressive (*Jaggard v Sawyer* [1995]) [1].

**9.** A continuing trespass is one that **persists**, such as an object or structure left on the claimant's land [1]. In *Holmes v Wilson* (1839) buttresses left on the land were a continuing trespass, and a second action could be brought after damages had been paid once [1]. It matters because **each day gives a fresh cause of action** [1], and the most useful remedy is a **mandatory injunction** ordering removal [1].

**10.** The salesman entered with an **implied licence** to walk up the path and knock [1]. When Bianca told him to leave, the licence was **revoked**; he then had a **reasonable time** to go (*Robson v Hallett* [1967]) [1]. Staying ten minutes to argue is more than a reasonable time, so he became a **trespasser** [1]. An occupier may use **reasonable force** to remove a trespasser after asking them to leave [1]. Firmly pushing him back to the gate is likely to be reasonable, unlike the excessive force in *Collins v Renison* (1754) [1].

**11.** The public may use a highway to **pass and repass** and for other **reasonable** purposes [1]. Using it for another purpose can be trespass against the owner of the land beneath: in *Harrison v Duke of Rutland* [1893] a man who used the road to disrupt grouse shooting was a trespasser [1]. However, in *DPP v Jones* [1999] the House of Lords held that a **peaceful, non-obstructive assembly** can be a reasonable use of the highway [1], so the law balances landowners' rights with public use (*Hickman v Maisey* [1900] may also be credited) [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: definition; actionable per se; forms of entry; intention and directness; continuing trespass; defences; remedies.
- **Protection for possessors**: no need to prove damage; mistake no defence (*Basely*); airspace and subsoil protected (*Anchor Brewhouse*; *Bocardo*); continuing trespass and injunctions; self-help with reasonable force.
- **Respect for others' interests**: aircraft at a reasonable height (*Bernstein*); reasonable use of the highway (*DPP v Jones*); statutory access for repairs; necessity (*Rigby*); damages in lieu where an injunction would be oppressive (*Jaggard v Sawyer*); the reasonable time to leave (*Robson v Hallett*).
- **Criticisms**: strict liability for honest mistakes can seem harsh; litigation is costly when damages are nominal; the line between trespass and nuisance can be arbitrary; protesters may be restricted.
- **Evaluation**: reach a conclusion, for example that the tort strikes a reasonable balance because its strong protection is tempered by sensible exceptions and flexible remedies.

Top-level answers use cases accurately and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a claimant must prove damage.
- Accepting a boundary mistake as a defence.
- Calling indirect interference trespass.
- Forgetting the reasonable time to leave after a licence is revoked.
- Treating all overflight as trespass.
