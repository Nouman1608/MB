## Section A

**1.** What is genetic modification?

A: breeding the best animals together

B: changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing or inserting genes

C: producing identical offspring from one parent

D: the natural change in a population over time

**[1]**

**2.** Which structure in a bacterium is used to carry a human gene into the cell?

A: the cell wall

B: a flagellum

C: a plasmid

D: a ribosome

**[1]**

**3.** State the hormone that is made by bacteria into which a human gene has been inserted. **[1]**

**4.** State **one** advantage of using bacteria rather than animals to make insulin. **[1]**

**5.** Name **one** feature given to crop plants by genetic modification. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how bacteria can be made to produce human insulin. **[5]**

**7.** A farmer grows maize that has been genetically modified to be resistant to a herbicide.

**(a)** Explain how this helps the farmer. **[3]**

**(b)** The yield of GM maize was 9.0 tonnes per hectare and the yield of non-GM maize was 7.5 tonnes per hectare. Calculate the percentage increase in yield. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** possible risk of growing herbicide-resistant crops. **[1]**

**8.** Maize is genetically modified to make a protein that is poisonous to caterpillars.

**(a)** State **two** benefits to a farmer. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why butterflies that feed on nearby plants could be at risk. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how the caterpillars could become resistant over time. **[3]**

**9.** Rice has been genetically modified to make additional vitamin A.

**(a)** Explain why this might be useful in countries where people eat a lot of rice. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why some people are concerned about growing GM crops. **[2]**

**10.** A scientist inserted a gene for pest resistance into 400 plant cells. Only 24 of the cells grew into plants with the gene.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of cells that grew into plants with the gene. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the scientist treated a large number of cells. **[1]**

**11.** Compare genetic modification with selective breeding, giving **two** differences. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss the advantages and risks of genetic modification of crop plants. **[5]**

---

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Insulin [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any one of: bacteria reproduce rapidly; less ethical concern; large quantities can be made cheaply; the insulin is identical to human insulin [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any one of: herbicide resistance; insect resistance; additional vitamins [1]. **[1]**

**6.** The human insulin gene is cut out of human DNA [1]. A bacterial plasmid is cut open [1] and the gene is inserted into it [1]. The plasmid is put into bacteria [1], which are grown in a fermenter where they reproduce and make insulin [1]. **[5]**

**7.**

**(a)** The farmer can spray the field with herbicide [1]. The weeds are killed but the crop survives [1], so there is less competition for light, water and ions and the yield rises [1].

**(b)** (9.0 - 7.5) divided by 7.5 x 100 [1] = 20 per cent [1].

**(c)** Any one of: genes could spread to wild plants, making weeds resistant; it may encourage over-use of herbicides [1]. **[6]**

**8.**

**(a)** Any two of: less damage to the crop; higher yield; less insecticide needed; lower costs [1 each].

**(b)** Pollen or plant material containing the toxin may land on plants that the butterflies' caterpillars eat [1], so the toxin could harm them as well [1].

**(c)** There is variation, and some caterpillars may have a mutation that gives resistance [1]. These survive and reproduce [1] and pass on the resistance allele, so more of the population is resistant [1]. **[7]**

**9.**

**(a)** The additional vitamin helps to prevent vitamin deficiency [1] in people whose diet is mainly rice [1].

**(b)** Any two of: possible effects on human health; genes may spread to wild plants; effects on other organisms; ethical or religious objections; cost of seeds [1 each]. **[4]**

**10.**

**(a)** 24 divided by 400 x 100 [1] = 6 per cent [1].

**(b)** Only a small percentage are successful, so many cells are needed to get enough plants with the gene [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Any two of: GM changes one or a few individual genes whereas selective breeding changes many genes at once [1]; GM can move genes from a different species whereas selective breeding uses the variation within a species [1]; GM can give the feature in fewer generations [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Advantages: higher yields and more food [1]; reduced use of pesticides [1]; crops with extra nutrients [1]. Risks: genes might spread to wild plants [1]; harm to non-target organisms or unknown effects on health [1]. **[5]**

---

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing selective breeding when asked about genetic modification.
- Forgetting that the gene is inserted into a plasmid.
- Giving a discussion with advantages only.
- Dividing the increase by the new value in a percentage calculation.
- Writing "GM crops are unsafe" with no explanation of the potential risk.
