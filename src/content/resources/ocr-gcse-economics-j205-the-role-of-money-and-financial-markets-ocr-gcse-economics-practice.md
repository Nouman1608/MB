These questions cover section 2.8 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), The role of money and financial markets. All people, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide to money and financial markets](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-money-and-financial-markets-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [money and financial markets revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-money-and-financial-markets-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by money acting as a medium of exchange. **[1]**

**2.** Explain one problem of barter that money solves. **[2]**

**3.** State one service that a bank provides to producers. **[1]**

**4.** Explain one way in which a building society differs from a bank. **[2]**

**5.** An insurer has 4,000 customers who each pay a car insurance premium of £600 a year. In one year 200 customers claim, with an average claim of £9,500.

**(a)** Calculate the total premiums collected. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how much is left after claims are paid. **[2]**

**6.** A saver has £3,600 in an account. The interest rate falls from 4.5% to 3.0%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in interest earned over one year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in interest earned. **[1]**

**7.** A firm has a loan of £14,000. The rate charged rises from 5.4% to 7.2%. Calculate the extra interest the firm pays per month. **[2]**

**8.** Which one of the following is most likely to happen when interest rates fall? **[1]**

- A. Households save a larger share of their income.
- B. Firms invest less in new machinery.
- C. Borrowing to buy cars and homes increases.
- D. Banks pay savers higher rates.

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Harbourside Joinery is considering three projects, each funded by a bank loan.

| Project | Cost | Expected extra profit per year (before interest) |
|---|---|---|
| X: new saw line | £80,000 | £5,600 |
| Y: delivery van | £45,000 | £2,250 |
| Z: dust extraction | £20,000 | £1,300 |

**(a)** Calculate the expected annual return, as a percentage, on each project. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify which projects are worth borrowing for at an interest rate of 6%, and which at 4.5%. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what your answers show about the link between interest rates and investment. **[2]**

**10.** The Okafor household has a variable-rate mortgage of £180,000 and savings of £12,000. Rates rise by one percentage point: the mortgage rate goes from 3.75% to 4.75% and the savings rate from 2.0% to 3.0%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in yearly mortgage interest. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in yearly interest on savings. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the net effect on the household per month. **[1]**
**(d)** Analyse how this change is likely to affect the household's spending. **[2]**

**11.** Lena puts £5,000 into a savings account for three years, with interest added at the end of each year.

**(a)** Calculate her balance after three years at 2.5% and at 4.0%, to the nearest penny. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference between the two balances. **[1]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of the financial sector for the government. **[6]**

**13.** A central bank raises interest rates by two percentage points. Evaluate whether this is likely to reduce consumer spending. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Money is generally accepted in payment for goods and services, and the receiver can spend it again [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Barter needs a double coincidence of wants, where each trader must want exactly what the other offers [1]. With money, a seller can accept payment from anyone and spend it with anyone, so trade is quicker and cheaper [1]. (Credit also: goods are hard to divide; too many exchange rates.) **[2]**

**3.** Any one: business loans or overdrafts; business accounts and payment services; a safe place to hold revenue [1]. **[1]**

**4.** A building society is mutual, owned by its savers and borrowers, while a bank is owned by shareholders [1]. So a building society can return surpluses to members through better rates rather than paying dividends [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 4,000 × £600 = **£2,400,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Claims = 200 × £9,500 = £1,900,000 [1]. Left = 2,400,000 − 1,900,000 = **£500,000** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The £500,000 must still cover the insurer's running costs, so it is not all profit.

**6. (a)** Before: 3,600 × 0.045 = £162; after: 3,600 × 0.03 = £108 [1]. Change = **£54 less** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** −54 ÷ 162 × 100 = **−33.3%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The rate fell 1.5 percentage points, but interest earned fell by a third. Use the right term for each.

**7.** 14,000 × 0.072 = £1,008 and 14,000 × 0.054 = £756, so £252 extra a year [1]. 252 ÷ 12 = **£21 a month** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** **C** [1]. Cheaper borrowing encourages loans for large purchases. A, B and D describe the effects of a rate rise. **[1]**

**9. (a)** X: 5,600 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **7%** [1]. Y: 2,250 ÷ 45,000 × 100 = **5%** [1]. Z: 1,300 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **6.5%** [1].
**(b)** At 6%, only X and Z earn more than the cost of borrowing [1]. At 4.5%, all three are worth doing [1].
**(c)** A lower interest rate means more projects earn a return above the cost of finance [1], so total investment rises from £100,000 to £145,000 as the rate falls from 6% to 4.5% [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures you calculated. "Lower rates increase investment" without the numbers misses the application mark.

**10. (a)** 180,000 × 0.0375 = £6,750; 180,000 × 0.0475 = £8,550 [1]. Change = **+£1,800 a year** [1].
**(b)** 12,000 × 0.02 = £240 and 12,000 × 0.03 = £360, so **+£120 a year** [1].
**(c)** (1,800 − 120) ÷ 12 = **£140 a month worse off** [1].
**(d)** The household is a net borrower, so its disposable income falls by about £140 a month [1]. It is likely to cut spending on non-essentials such as meals out, and the extra reward for saving gives a further reason to spend less [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** At 2.5%: 5,000 × 1.025³ = **£5,384.45** [1]. At 4.0%: 5,000 × 1.04³ = **£5,624.32** [1].
**(b)** 5,624.32 − 5,384.45 = **£239.87** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Interest is added each year, so multiply by the growth factor three times. Simple interest would give £5,375 and £5,600, which are wrong here.

**12.** The government collects taxes and pays benefits and wages through bank accounts, so the payment system lets it run public services efficiently [1]. It borrows from financial institutions to fund a budget deficit, which lets it spend on schools or roads without raising taxes at once [1]. Its interest rate decisions reach households and firms through the rates banks set, so the sector is how monetary policy works [1]. However, if banks fail, the government may have to support them, which costs taxpayers money and has an opportunity cost [1]. Heavy borrowing also brings future interest payments [1]. Judgement: the sector is essential for the government because almost every policy relies on it, but only if institutions are stable, so regulation of banks matters as much as the services they provide [1]. **[6]**

**13.** For a fall: higher rates raise repayments on variable-rate mortgages and loans, cutting disposable income [1], so borrowers spend less on goods and services [1]. Borrowing for new cars, furniture and holidays becomes dearer, so credit-funded spending falls [1]. Saving becomes more rewarding, so households may save a larger share of income [1]. Against: many households have fixed-rate mortgages and will not feel the rise until their deal ends [1]. Savers with no debts receive more interest and may spend more [1]. If confidence is high or wages are rising fast, spending may hold up [1]. Judgement: spending is likely to fall, because a two-point rise is large, but the effect will be delayed and smaller where most debt is fixed and many households are net savers [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers separate borrowers from savers and fixed from variable debt, then decide which effect is bigger and say why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing percentage points with per cent.
- Giving only the new interest figure when "the change" is asked for.
- Using simple interest when interest is added each year.
- Calling a savings account "investment".
- Treating every borrower as hit at once by a rate rise.
- Evaluating with no judgement, or a judgement with no reason.
