This guide covers subtopic **2.1 Concept and use of a classification system** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to understand that organisms can be classified into groups by the features they share, to describe a species as a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring, to describe the binomial system of naming species, and to construct and use dichotomous keys based on identifiable features.

This page goes further on the four skills the examiners test: explaining why classification is useful, defining a species with a worked example, writing scientific names correctly, and building a key that works. ## Why organisms are classified

There are millions of different kinds of living things. Without a system for sorting them, biologists would have no sensible way to study, name or talk about them. A classification system sorts organisms into **groups** according to the **features they share**.

The key idea is that organisms in the same group are more like one another than they are like organisms in other groups. A cat and a lion share many features, such as fur, milk production and a particular tooth arrangement, so they are placed close together. A cat and an oak tree share very few, so they are placed far apart.

Classification is useful because:

- it makes the huge variety of living things easier to study, because you can learn about a group instead of every single organism
- it makes organisms easier to identify, because you only need to match an unknown organism to the features of a group
- it gives scientists everywhere a shared way to refer to the same organism
- it helps biologists to predict features, because a newly found organism placed in a group is likely to share that group's other features

The features used must be ones you can observe and that do not vary much within a group. Body covering, number of legs, how an animal reproduces and whether a plant has flowers are good choices. Body size is a poor choice, because a young animal and an adult of the same species differ in size.

## What a species is

A **species** is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce **fertile offspring**. Both parts of that statement matter. Two organisms that can mate are not necessarily the same species. They are the same species only if their offspring are themselves able to reproduce.

### Worked example: the mule

A horse and a donkey can mate, and the offspring is a mule. Are a horse and a donkey the same species?

1. Check whether they can reproduce. Yes, a mule is born.
2. Check whether the offspring are fertile. Mules are almost always **sterile**: they cannot produce offspring of their own.
3. Conclude. Because the offspring are not fertile, a horse and a donkey are **different species**.

A model answer would say: "A horse and a donkey are different species because although they can breed, their offspring (mules) are sterile and cannot reproduce."

Now compare two dogs of different breeds, such as a poodle and a spaniel. They can breed and their puppies are fertile, so they belong to the same species. Differences in size, coat or colour inside a species do not make new species.

## The binomial system of naming species

Everyday names change from place to place. The bird called a robin in Britain is a different species from the bird called a robin in North America. Scientists therefore use an **internationally agreed** system, the **binomial system**. Every species receives a scientific name made up of **two parts**:

- the first part is the **genus** (a group of closely related species), and it begins with a **capital letter**
- the second part is the **species**, and it begins with a **lower-case letter**

Humans are *Homo sapiens*: genus *Homo*, species *sapiens*. The domestic cat is *Felis catus* and the lion is *Panthera leo*. The cat and the lion have different genus names because they are not as closely related as, say, the lion (*Panthera leo*) and the tiger (*Panthera tigris*), which share a genus.

Rules for writing a scientific name:

1. Genus first, with a capital letter.
2. Species second, with a lower-case letter, even when it comes from a person's name.
3. Print the name in *italics*. In handwriting, **underline** each word separately, because you cannot write italics.

If a question asks you to "write the scientific name" and you write "Homo Sapiens" or "homo sapiens" you lose the mark, even though you named the right organism.

### Why two parts?

The genus tells you which closely related group the organism belongs to, so the name already carries information. The species word then picks out one kind within that genus. The system is agreed internationally, so a biologist in any country who reads *Panthera leo* knows exactly which animal is meant, whatever language they speak.

## Dichotomous keys

A **dichotomous key** is a tool for identifying organisms. "Dichotomous" means "divided into two". At each step the user chooses between two statements about an observable feature, and each choice leads either to another pair of statements or to the name of an organism.

### Using a key

Work through the numbered steps in order. Always read both statements of a pair before you decide. The worked example below uses five animals.

| Step | Statement | Go to / answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Legs present | Step 2 |
| 1b | Legs absent | Step 4 |
| 2a | Six legs | housefly |
| 2b | More than six legs | Step 3 |
| 3a | Eight legs | spider |
| 3b | More than eight legs | centipede |
| 4a | Shell present | snail |
| 4b | No shell | earthworm |

To identify an animal with eight legs: step 1, legs are present, so go to step 2; step 2, not six legs, so 2b and go to step 3; step 3, eight legs, so it is a spider. Write down the path you followed, because examiners often give a mark for correct use of the key and a separate mark for the answer.

### Constructing a key

You may be given a set of organisms, usually shown in a table of features or in drawings, and asked to build a key. Follow this method.

1. **List the features** that differ between the organisms.
2. **Choose one feature that splits the group into two parts**, each part being as nearly equal as you can make it. Write it as a pair of statements, for example "wings present" and "wings absent".
3. **Send each part to its own next step**, and repeat with a new feature until each organism is alone.
4. **Check** by using your key on every organism. Each one must end at its own name, and no organism may fit both statements of a pair.

### Worked example: building a key

*A table shows four plants. Plant A has needle-shaped leaves and cones. Plant B has broad leaves and flowers with petals in threes. Plant C has broad leaves and flowers with petals in fives. Plant D has fronds and no flowers or cones. Construct a key.*

Feature to split first: "flowers present". Plants B and C have flowers; A and D do not. Then split each pair with one further feature.

| Step | Statement | Go to / answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Flowers present | Step 2 |
| 1b | No flowers | Step 3 |
| 2a | Petals in threes | Plant B |
| 2b | Petals in fives | Plant C |
| 3a | Cones present | Plant A |
| 3b | Fronds present | Plant D |

Check: Plant D has no flowers, so 1b, then it has fronds, so 3b. Plant A has no flowers, so 1b, then cones, so 3a. Every plant ends at its own name.

### Rules for a good key

- Use features that can be **seen**, such as "has wings". Avoid opinions such as "looks dangerous".
- Use features that stay **the same** in all individuals. Colour and size are often unreliable.
- Each pair must be **opposites**, so that exactly one statement is true for any organism.
- Use **yes or no** features where possible, because they leave no room for doubt.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a species only as "organisms that can reproduce". The word **fertile** is what earns the mark.
- Writing the scientific name without a capital letter for the genus, or with a capital for the species.
- Forgetting to underline or italicise the name.
- Using size or colour in a key.
- Writing statements in a key that overlap, so that an organism fits both.
- Calling a binomial name a "common name"; it is a scientific name agreed internationally.

## Command words

- **Describe**: state the main features, for example how the binomial system works.
- **Define**: give the precise meaning, for example of a species.
- **Explain**: give reasons, for example why horses and donkeys are different species.
- **Construct**: build a key from the data you are given.
- **Use**: work through a key and state the path you took.
