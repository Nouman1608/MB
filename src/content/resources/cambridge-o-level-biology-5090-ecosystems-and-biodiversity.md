This guide covers subtopic **19.3 Ecosystems and biodiversity** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe a population, a community, an ecosystem and biodiversity; to identify and state the factors affecting the rate of population growth (food supply, competition, predation and disease); and to understand that the growth of the human population is increasing the demand for global resources.

The [Relationships of organisms topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-relationships-of-organisms-with-one-another-and-with-the-env/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on the terms and on population growth, with worked data. Use the [19.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-ecosystems-and-biodiversity-revision-notes/) for recall and the [19.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-ecosystems-and-biodiversity-practice/) to test yourself.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population | A group of organisms of one species, living in the same area, at the same time |
| Community | All of the populations of different species in an ecosystem |
| Ecosystem | A unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together |
| Biodiversity | The number of different species that live in an area |

The terms move from small to large. A population is a single species. A community puts all the populations together. An ecosystem adds the non-living (abiotic) environment, such as soil, water, light and temperature, and the interactions between the living and non-living parts.

### Examples

*A pond.*

- Population: all the frogs in the pond.
- Community: all the frogs, fish, snails, insects, algae and other plants and microorganisms in the pond.
- Ecosystem: the pond community together with the water, light, temperature, mud and dissolved oxygen.

A different example is a woodland, a coral reef or a desert. An ecosystem can be small (a rotting log) or large (a forest).

### Worked example 1: sorting the terms

*Match each description with the term: population, community or ecosystem.*

- "All the oak trees in a wood." **Population** (one species).
- "All the plants, animals and fungi in a wood." **Community** (all the species).
- "The wood, including its soil, climate, plants, animals and fungi, and how they interact." **Ecosystem**.

The key word is "species": a population is one species and a community is many.

## Biodiversity

**Biodiversity is the number of different species that live in an area.** A tropical rainforest has high biodiversity, with a very large number of species. A cultivated field of wheat has low biodiversity because it is dominated by one species. Biodiversity is important because ecosystems with many species tend to be more stable, and because species provide food, medicines and materials. Loss of biodiversity is covered in the effects of humans and conservation subtopics.

### Worked example 2: comparing biodiversity

*Two quadrat surveys of 10 quadrats found the following numbers of species.*

| Habitat | Species found |
|---|---|
| Mown lawn | 4 |
| Uncut meadow | 19 |

The meadow has the higher biodiversity. The difference is 19 - 4 = 15 species, and the meadow has 19 divided by 4 = 4.75 times as many species as the lawn. The mowing prevents most plants from flowering and growing, so fewer species can survive.

(Sampling is done with quadrats placed at random, with a large enough number to give a reliable result.)

## Population growth

A population changes in size because of **births**, **deaths**, **immigration** and **emigration**. The rate of population growth depends on conditions in the environment. The syllabus names four factors.

| Factor | Effect on population growth |
|---|---|
| Food supply | More food means more survive and breed, so the population grows faster; a shortage slows growth or causes numbers to fall |
| Competition | Individuals compete for food, water, space and mates; the more individuals there are, the more intense the competition, which slows growth |
| Predation | Predators kill and eat prey, reducing the prey population (and the predator population then depends on the prey) |
| Disease | Disease kills individuals or reduces their ability to breed, particularly when the population is dense |

### A typical growth curve

When a small number of organisms arrives in a new area with plenty of resources, the pattern of growth is:

1. **Slow start.** There are few individuals to breed.
2. **Rapid growth.** The population increases quickly (each generation is larger than the last), because food is plentiful and there are few predators and diseases.
3. **Levelling off.** The population stops increasing because resources become limited and competition, predation and disease increase. The population stays about constant at the level the environment can support.

### Worked example 3: interpreting population data

*A population of yeast cells was counted every 2 hours in a flask of sugar solution.*

| Time (hours) | Number of cells (thousands) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 2 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 4 | 14 |
| 6 | 40 |
| 8 | 60 |
| 10 | 62 |
| 12 | 61 |

1. Between 4 and 6 hours, the increase is 40 - 14 = 26 thousand, which is 26 divided by 2 = 13 thousand per hour.
2. Between 10 and 12 hours, the number hardly changes (a fall of 1 thousand), so the population has levelled off.
3. Explanation for the levelling: the sugar is running out and waste products such as ethanol build up, so more cells die and fewer divide. Competition for food limits growth.
4. The growth between 6 and 8 hours is 20 thousand, which is 10 thousand per hour, less than between 4 and 6 hours. The growth rate is already slowing.

### Predator and prey

If a population of prey increases, the food supply for predators increases, so the predator population rises later. More predators eat more prey, so the prey population falls. Then the predators have less food and their population falls. The two populations rise and fall in a repeating pattern, with the peak of predators following the peak of prey.

## The human population

The human population has grown rapidly, because of improved food production, medicine, sanitation and healthcare, which have reduced death rates. The world population is more than 8 billion. The syllabus asks you to understand that **the growth of the human population is increasing the demand for global resources**.

Increasing demands include:

- **food and land** for agriculture, which leads to deforestation and reduced biodiversity;
- **water** for drinking, farming and industry;
- **energy**, so more fossil fuels are burned, adding carbon dioxide to the air;
- **materials** such as wood and minerals;
- **space** for housing, which reduces the habitats available to wild species;
- more **waste** and **pollution**.

### Worked example 4: growth of the human population

*In a country, the population was 40 million in 1990 and 52 million in 2020.*

1. Increase = 52 - 40 = 12 million.
2. Percentage increase = 12 divided by 40 x 100 = 30 per cent.
3. Increase per year (average) = 12 divided by 30 = 0.4 million per year.

If food production does not rise by at least the same proportion, there is less food per person.

## Sampling an ecosystem

To find out which organisms live in an area and how many there are, biologists use **sampling**, because it is not possible to count every organism. A **quadrat** is a square frame, often 1 m by 1 m, that is placed on the ground. The plants inside the frame are identified and counted. To get a result that represents the whole area:

1. Use a **random** method to choose where to put the quadrat, for example by using random numbers as coordinates along two tape measures.
2. Use a **large number** of quadrats, to reduce the effect of unusual patches.
3. Calculate the **mean** number of organisms per quadrat.
4. Estimate the total: mean per quadrat x (total area divided by area of one quadrat).

### Worked example 5: estimating a population

*A field has an area of 2 000 m2. Ten quadrats of 1 m2 were placed at random and the numbers of daisies counted were 3, 5, 2, 4, 6, 3, 5, 4, 2 and 6.*

1. Total daisies = 3 + 5 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 6 = 40.
2. Mean per quadrat = 40 divided by 10 = 4 daisies per m2.
3. Estimated population = 4 x 2 000 = 8 000 daisies.

This is an estimate, and it is more reliable if more quadrats are used and they are placed at random.

## Linking the ideas

Ecosystems are connected to the earlier subtopics. Energy enters through producers and flows along food chains (19.1). The nutrients are recycled by decomposers (19.2). The number of each species is controlled by the factors that affect population growth. Biodiversity is the sum of all these species. When a human activity changes one part, for example by removing a predator or clearing a habitat, other parts of the ecosystem are affected, which leads into the next two subtopics on the effects of humans and on conservation.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a population as "all the organisms in an area". It is one species.
- Confusing community with ecosystem. An ecosystem also includes the physical environment.
- Describing biodiversity as the number of organisms. It is the number of different species.
- Listing factors without saying how they change the growth.
- Forgetting immigration and emigration as well as births and deaths.
- Saying that population growth continues forever. It levels off when resources become limiting.
- Writing that human population growth "has no effect" on other species.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the definitions of the terms.
- **Identify**: pick out the factor that is affecting a population from data.
- **Explain**: give the reason why a population levels off.
- **Understand**: show how the increase in the human population increases the demand for resources.
