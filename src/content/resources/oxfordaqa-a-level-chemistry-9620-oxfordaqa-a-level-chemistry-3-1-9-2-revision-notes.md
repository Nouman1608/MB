## Core recall

- Electrochemical cells are a **commercial source of electrical energy**.
- Three types: **non-rechargeable (irreversible)**, **rechargeable**, **fuel cells**.
- Fuel cells generate a current and **do not need to be electrically recharged**: fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously.
- Lithium cell (simplified, discharge): positive Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻; negative Li → Li⁺ + e⁻.
- Alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell: negative H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻; positive O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; overall 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
- You must be able to **deduce** electrode reactions and EMFs from **given** data for unfamiliar cells.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Non-rechargeable (irreversible) cell | Reactions cannot practically be reversed; discarded when a reactant is used up |
| Rechargeable cell | An external current reverses the electrode reactions, restoring the reactants |
| Fuel cell | Fuel and oxidant supplied continuously; generates current without electrical recharging |
| Discharge | The cell supplies current; spontaneous reactions occur |
| Recharge | An external supply drives the reverse reactions |
| Positive electrode | Where reduction occurs during discharge |
| Negative electrode | Where oxidation occurs during discharge |
| Electrolyte | Conducts by movement of ions between electrodes |

## Deducing the reactions: the routine

**Discharge**

1. More positive E⦵ → that half-equation runs **forwards** (reduction, positive electrode).
2. Less positive E⦵ → that half-equation runs **backwards** (oxidation, negative electrode).
3. Match electrons, add, cancel common species (OH⁻, H⁺, H₂O).
4. EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative). Do not multiply E⦵.

**Recharge**: reverse **both** discharge half-reactions.

## Generating a current

- Oxidation at the negative electrode releases electrons.
- Electrons flow through the external circuit to the positive electrode, doing work on the way.
- Reduction at the positive electrode uses the electrons.
- Ions move through the electrolyte to balance charge.

## Cells to recognise

| Cell | Type | EMF from given data / V | Overall discharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead–acid | Rechargeable | 2.05 (6 in series ≈ 12 V) | Pb + PbO₂ + 2H⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O |
| Nickel–cadmium | Rechargeable | 1.40 | Cd + 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + 2Ni(OH)₂ |
| Zinc–silver oxide | Non-rechargeable | 1.60 | Zn + Ag₂O → ZnO + 2Ag |
| Lithium (cobalt oxide) | Rechargeable | 3.60 | Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻ |
| Hydrogen–oxygen | Fuel cell | 1.23 | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O |

**Oxidation-state checks:** Pb 0 and +4 → +2; Ni +3 → +2; Cd 0 → +2; Co +4 → +3.

## Why some cells are rechargeable

- Products stay on the electrodes as solids (for example PbSO₄), so a reverse current can convert them back.
- Lead–acid: during discharge, acid is used up and water formed; the electrolyte becomes more dilute.

## Fuel cells: alkaline and acidic

- Both: overall 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and EMF 1.23 V.
- Alkaline: OH⁻ carries charge; OH⁻ formed at the positive electrode is used at the negative.
- Acidic: H⁺ carries charge; negative H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; positive O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O.

## Benefits and risks

| | Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Rechargeable | Reused, less waste; portable power | Toxic lead and cadmium; lithium fire risk; charging electricity may come from fossil fuels |
| Non-rechargeable | Cheap, convenient | Single use wastes metals; leaks in landfill |
| Fuel cell | Only water at the point of use; no recharging | Hydrogen often made using fossil fuels; flammable and explosive; hard to store |

Recycling cells recovers metals and stops toxic compounds entering the environment.

## Exam traps

- Discharge with the half-equations the wrong way round.
- Recharge with only one half-equation reversed.
- OH⁻ or H₂O left on both sides of the overall equation.
- E⦵ doubled when a half-equation is doubled.
- "A fuel cell needs recharging" or "stores electricity".
- Benefits and risks stated vaguely, without a reason.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three types of cell in the specification.
2. Why does a fuel cell not need recharging?
3. Which half-equation runs forwards during discharge?
4. What happens to the electrode reactions during recharging?
5. Lead–acid data: +1.69 V and −0.36 V. Calculate the EMF of one cell.
6. Why do six lead–acid cells give about 12 V?
7. In the nickel–cadmium cell, what is the change in oxidation state of nickel during discharge?
8. Zinc–air data: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻ +0.40 V; ZnO + H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn + 2OH⁻ −1.26 V. Calculate the EMF.
9. Write the overall discharge equation for the zinc–air cell in question 8.
10. In the lithium cell, what is the oxidation state of cobalt before and after discharge?
11. Write the negative-electrode reaction in an alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.
12. Give one benefit and one risk of hydrogen fuel cells.

### Answers

1. Non-rechargeable (irreversible), rechargeable, fuel cells.
2. Fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously from outside the cell.
3. The one with the **more positive** E⦵ (reduction at the positive electrode).
4. **Both** are reversed by the external supply.
5. 1.69 − (−0.36) = **+2.05 V**.
6. They are connected in series: 6 × 2.05 V ≈ 12.3 V.
7. **+3 → +2** (reduced).
8. 0.40 − (−1.26) = **+1.66 V**.
9. **2Zn + O₂ → 2ZnO** (zinc equation ×2; H₂O and OH⁻ cancel).
10. **+4** in CoO₂, **+3** in Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻.
11. H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻
12. Benefit: only water is produced where it is used. Risk: hydrogen is flammable and explosive in air (or is often made using fossil fuels).
