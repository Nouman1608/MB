This guide covers **section 3.1.2 Perception**, the second of the four sections of Paper 1 (Cognition and Behaviour) in AQA GCSE Psychology (8182). The [Paper 1 Cognition and Behaviour guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-cognition-and-behaviour/) lists the content in outline; this page goes deeper into perception alone, with everyday examples of each cue, a full explanation of each illusion, evaluation of both theories and worked exam answers.

## What section 3.1.2 covers

- **Sensation and perception:** the difference between them.
- **Visual cues and constancies:** monocular depth cues (height in plane, relative size, occlusion, linear perspective) and binocular depth cues (retinal disparity, convergence).
- **Gibson's direct theory** (the influence of nature), including the role of **motion parallax** in everyday perception.
- **Visual illusions:** explanations (ambiguity, misinterpreted depth cues, fiction, size constancy) and examples (Ponzo, Müller-Lyer, Rubin's vase, the Ames Room, the Kanizsa triangle, the Necker cube).
- **Gregory's constructivist theory** (the influence of nurture).
- **Factors affecting perception:** perceptual set, culture, motivation, emotion and expectation; the **Gilchrist and Nesberg** study of motivation and the **Bruner and Minturn** study of perceptual set.

## Sensation and perception

**Sensation** is the detection of physical energy by the sense organs: light falling on the retina, sound waves reaching the ear. **Perception** is the brain's organisation and interpretation of that information so that it has meaning. The image on each retina is flat and upside down, yet we see a three-dimensional, upright world: perception adds what sensation alone does not contain.

## Depth cues and constancy

Depth cues let the brain judge distance from a flat retinal image.

| Cue | Type | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Height in plane | Monocular | Boats near the horizon in a photo of the sea look further away than boats near the bottom of the picture |
| Relative size | Monocular | Of two cars assumed to be the same size, the one that looks smaller seems further away |
| Occlusion | Monocular | A person standing in front of a door hides part of it, so the person is seen as nearer |
| Linear perspective | Monocular | Railway lines appear to meet in the distance |
| Retinal disparity | Binocular | Each eye gets a slightly different view; the difference is larger for near objects, which the brain uses to judge depth |
| Convergence | Binocular | Your eyes turn inwards to focus on a finger brought close to your nose; the muscle strain signals "near" |

**Size constancy** is our tendency to see an object as staying the same size even though its retinal image shrinks as it moves away. A friend walking away does not seem to shrink. Constancy is usually helpful, but it is also one reason illusions work.

## Gibson's direct theory: the influence of nature

Gibson argued that perception is **direct** and **bottom-up**: the light reaching the eye (the **optic array**) contains enough information to perceive the world accurately, with no need for inference from past experience. Because this ability is innate and has helped our species survive, the theory stresses **nature**.

**Motion parallax** is a key source of that information. When you move, objects near you seem to move past quickly and in the opposite direction, while distant objects move slowly and seem to travel with you. From a train window, the fence by the track flashes past while the hills hardly move, and the brain reads the difference in speed directly as distance.

**Evaluation.** Gibson's theory explains why perception is usually fast and accurate in real, moving environments, such as when catching a ball or landing a plane. Its weakness is that it **cannot easily explain visual illusions**: if perception were direct, we should not be fooled. Supporters reply that most illusions are flat, still drawings, which give much less information than the real world.

## Visual illusions explained

| Illusion | What you see | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Rubin's vase | A vase or two faces in profile, switching between them | **Ambiguity**: one image supports two interpretations of figure and background |
| Necker cube | A wire cube that flips between two orientations | **Ambiguity**: the drawing has no cue to say which face is at the front |
| Kanizsa triangle | A bright white triangle with clear edges | **Fiction**: the brain fills in edges that are not drawn |
| Ponzo | Two equal bars between converging lines; the upper bar looks longer | **Misinterpreted depth cues and size constancy**: the lines are read as a receding road, so the upper bar is "further away" and is scaled up |
| Müller-Lyer | Two equal lines; the one with outward fins looks longer | **Misinterpreted depth cues and size constancy**: Gregory suggested the fins resemble the corners of rooms and buildings, so one line is read as further away |
| Ames Room | People in the two back corners look very different in size | **Misinterpreted depth cues**: the room is distorted, but we assume it is rectangular, so one corner is wrongly seen as being as near as the other |

## Gregory's constructivist theory: the influence of nurture

Gregory argued that perception is **indirect** and **top-down**. The sensory information reaching the eye is often incomplete or ambiguous, so the brain constructs a "best guess", using **past experience and knowledge** to make inferences. Because these inferences are learned, the theory stresses **nurture**.

**Evaluation.** The theory explains illusions well: the brain applies rules learned from a three-dimensional world, such as size constancy, to flat drawings where they do not fit. It also explains **perceptual set**, since expectation is a form of top-down influence. However, if perception depended on guesses, it would be **less reliable** than it is, and the theory struggles to explain how perception is so fast and accurate in everyday life. Much of its evidence comes from artificial, still images.

## Factors affecting perception

**Perceptual set** is a readiness to perceive something in a particular way. Several factors create it:

- **Expectation.** In **Bruner and Minturn's study**, participants briefly saw an ambiguous figure that could be read as the letter **B** or the number **13**. Those who saw it among letters tended to report B; those who saw it among numbers tended to report 13. Context created an expectation that shaped what was perceived. A weakness is that this is an artificial task, though it is easy to replicate.
- **Motivation.** In **Gilchrist and Nesberg's study**, participants who had gone without food for longer judged pictures of food to be brighter than participants who had eaten. Hunger made food stand out. The setting was artificial and judging brightness is not an everyday task, so ecological validity is low.
- **Emotion.** How we feel can make us more or less likely to notice something; for example, an anxious person may be quicker to notice possible threats.
- **Culture.** Experience in a particular environment affects how drawings and depth cues are interpreted. Cross-cultural research has suggested that people who grow up surrounded by straight-edged buildings (a "carpentered" environment) may be more strongly affected by the Müller-Lyer illusion, which supports Gregory's emphasis on nurture.

## Worked example 1: explaining an illusion

> *Explain how the Ponzo illusion can be explained by misinterpreted depth cues.* [3 marks]

"The converging lines act as **linear perspective**, so the brain reads them as railway lines going into the distance [1]. The upper bar is therefore perceived as **further away** than the lower bar [1]. Because both bars cast the same-sized image on the retina, **size constancy** leads the brain to scale the 'further' bar up, so it looks longer [1]."

## Worked example 2: comparing the theories

> *Explain one difference between Gibson's and Gregory's theories of perception.* [2 marks]

"Gibson's theory says perception is **bottom-up** and needs no past experience, **whereas** Gregory's says it is **top-down**, using past experience to make inferences [1]. This means Gibson emphasises **nature** and Gregory emphasises **nurture** [1]."

"Whereas" signals the comparison; describing each theory separately with no link often scores only partially.

## Common mistakes

- Calling Gibson's theory top-down, or Gregory's bottom-up.
- Listing retinal disparity or convergence as monocular cues.
- Saying an illusion is caused by "the eyes" rather than by the brain's interpretation.
- Describing Bruner and Minturn without saying what it shows about **expectation**.
- Naming perceptual set without saying which factor (motivation, expectation, culture, emotion) produced it.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Identify / Name:** give the cue, illusion or theory; no explanation needed.
- **Outline / Describe:** set out the key features; for a study, what was done and what was found.
- **Explain:** link cause and effect, for example "because the lines are read as perspective, so...".
- **Evaluate:** give developed strengths and weaknesses and a judgement; for the theories, illusions and real-world accuracy are the classic points.
- **Distinguish / Explain one difference:** use comparative words such as "whereas".

In scenario questions, name the cue or factor and tie it to the details in the stem.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is motion parallax?**
It is the depth cue in which, as you move, near objects seem to move past quickly and distant objects slowly. Gibson argued it gives direct information about distance.

**Which illusions are explained by ambiguity?**
Rubin's vase and the Necker cube, because each image can be interpreted in two ways and the brain switches between them.

**How does Gregory's theory explain the Müller-Lyer illusion?**
Gregory suggested the fins resemble the corners of rooms and buildings, so the brain applies size constancy and perceives the line it reads as further away as longer.
