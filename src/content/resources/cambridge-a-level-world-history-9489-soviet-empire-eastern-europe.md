**The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953-91 is the first topic of the International option on Cambridge 9489 Paper 4.** Its key questions ask how the USSR's relationship with the satellite states developed up to 1968 (7.1), why 1964-85 became a period of political and economic stagnation (7.2), how Gorbachev tried to respond to the crisis (7.3) and how and why Soviet control of Eastern Europe collapsed by 1991 (7.4). Paper 4 sets two 30-mark essays from your option in 1 hour 45 minutes. This guide deepens the [International option study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-depth-study-international-history-study-guide/); for recall use the [Soviet Empire revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-9489-soviet-empire-eastern-europe-revision-notes/) and for essays the [Soviet Empire practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-world-history-9489-soviet-empire-eastern-europe-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

- **7.1** The Warsaw Pact; government in Czechoslovakia, Poland, East Germany and Hungary; repression (censorship, the Stasi, the Church, emigration); social and economic conditions; uprisings in the 1950s, Hungary 1956, Berlin and the Wall, the Prague Spring; Soviet reactions and the Brezhnev Doctrine.
- **7.2** Brezhnev, Andropov and Chernenko; corruption, the nomenklatura and gerontocracy; the economy, the 10th Five Year Plan and defence spending; détente, Afghanistan and the Second Cold War; the satellite states and challenges from the West, activism and the Church.
- **7.3** Gorbachev's rise; the situation in 1985; glasnost, perestroika and demokratizatsiya; the 12th Five Year Plan, Chernobyl; summits with the USA.
- **7.4** Solidarity and Poland; Gorbachev's impact on Eastern Europe; the collapse of the Soviet Union.

## 7.1 Control and challenge to 1968

**The Warsaw Pact (May 1955)** was formed days after West Germany joined NATO. It gave the USSR a legal basis for stationing troops in member states and a unified command under a Soviet officer, and formalised a bloc already bound by COMECON (1949).

**How the satellites were governed.** Each was a one-party state where the Communist Party controlled the government, police, media and economy, and leaders depended on Moscow's approval. Common tools were **censorship**; security police such as East Germany's **Stasi**, which built a vast network of informers; pressure on churches -- Cardinal Wyszyński was interned in Poland from 1953 to 1956 -- and **restrictions on emigration**. Central planning prioritised heavy industry; collectivisation and shortages of consumer goods and housing meant living standards lagged behind the West, although employment, education and basic welfare were guaranteed.

**Threats to stability.**
- **East Germany, June 1953:** higher work norms provoked strikes and demonstrations across the GDR; Soviet tanks crushed them.
- **Poland, 1956:** after Khrushchev's Secret Speech (February 1956) and the Poznań riots (June), Władysław **Gomułka** returned as leader in October. Khrushchev accepted him because Poland stayed communist and in the Warsaw Pact.
- **Hungary, 1956:** student demonstrations on 23 October led to the return of **Imre Nagy**, who allowed other parties and announced withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. Soviet forces invaded on 4 November; thousands died, around 200,000 fled, and János **Kádár** was installed. Nagy was executed in 1958. The West did not intervene, distracted by Suez and unwilling to risk war.
- **Berlin:** millions of East Germans left through Berlin in the 1950s. Khrushchev's ultimatum of 1958 failed, and on 13 August 1961 the GDR sealed the border with what became the **Berlin Wall**.
- **Czechoslovakia, 1968:** Alexander **Dubček**'s "socialism with a human face" -- the Action Programme, the end of censorship and plans for economic reform -- alarmed Moscow and neighbouring leaders. Warsaw Pact forces invaded on 20-21 August 1968 (Romania refused to take part). Dubček was replaced by Gustáv Husák in 1969, and "normalisation" followed.

**The Brezhnev Doctrine (1968)** declared that when socialism was threatened in one country it was the concern of all socialist states -- justifying intervention after the event and warning others.

**Lesson for essays:** Moscow tolerated variation (Gomułka, Kádár's later "goulash communism") but not departure from the party's monopoly or the Pact.

## 7.2 Stagnation, 1964-85

**Leadership.** Brezhnev (1964-82) promised "stability of cadres" after Khrushchev's reorganisations. The **nomenklatura** -- officials appointed from party-approved lists -- gained job security and privileges, and corruption spread, notably in Uzbekistan and the Caucasus. The leadership aged into a **gerontocracy**; Brezhnev's successors **Andropov** (1982-84), who began a discipline and anti-corruption campaign, and **Chernenko** (1984-85) were both ill and died within months of each other.

**Economy.** Growth slowed. The Kosygin reforms of 1965 gave enterprises limited autonomy but were diluted. The **10th Five Year Plan** (1976-80) stressed "quality and efficiency" but missed key targets. Agriculture needed large grain imports; technology lagged, especially in computing; and **defence spending** took a large share of resources to maintain parity with the USA. Soviet oil and gas earnings in the 1970s masked the problems.

**Foreign relations.** Détente brought SALT I (1972) and the **Helsinki Accords (1975)**, whose human rights clauses dissidents used. The **invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979)** became a costly war that damaged morale and international standing, and helped start the **Second Cold War** with Reagan's rearmament.

**The satellite states.** Many borrowed heavily from the West in the 1970s to fund consumption and investment; debt and price rises became political dangers. Challenges came from Western radio and television, dissident groups such as Czechoslovakia's **Charter 77** (1977), and the Church -- strengthened in Poland by the election of the Polish Pope **John Paul II** (1978) and his visit in 1979.

## 7.3 Gorbachev's response

**Rise.** Gorbachev, aged 54, was chosen in March 1985 with Andropov's patronage behind him, as the leadership recognised the need for a younger, energetic leader.

**The situation in 1985:** a stagnant economy; food shortages and rationing in some areas; a growing budget deficit; absenteeism and alcoholism; poor housing and services; pressure from the arms race and from the young and educated who knew about Western living standards.

**New thinking.**
- **Perestroika** (restructuring): the Law on State Enterprise (1987) and the Law on Cooperatives (1988) allowed more autonomy and small private businesses. Partial reform disrupted the planned economy without creating a market.
- **Glasnost** (openness): freedom to criticise and publish; after the **Chernobyl** disaster (April 1986), whose concealment exposed secrecy, glasnost widened. Criticism soon reached the party and the Soviet past.
- **Demokratizatsiya**: contested elections to a new Congress of People's Deputies (1989) and, in 1990, the end of the party's constitutional monopoly.

**Economic results.** The **12th Five Year Plan** (1986-90) aimed at acceleration but failed. Falling world oil prices, the cost of the anti-alcohol campaign to state revenue, declining foreign trade and the **fragmentation** of supply chains as republics and enterprises acted independently deepened shortages.

**Foreign relations.** Summits at **Geneva (1985)**, **Reykjavik (1986)** and **Washington (1987)**, where the INF Treaty was signed, ended the Second Cold War. Withdrawal from Afghanistan was completed in 1989.

## 7.4 Collapse

**Solidarity.** Debt, falling living standards and meat price rises (July 1980) led to strikes; at the **Gdańsk** shipyard in **August 1980** workers led by Lech **Wałęsa** issued **21 Demands**, including free trade unions and the right to strike. The government conceded, and Solidarity grew to around ten million members. Under Soviet pressure, General **Jaruzelski** imposed **martial law** in December 1981 and banned Solidarity, avoiding a Soviet invasion.

**Gorbachev's impact.** He told Eastern European leaders they would no longer be propped up by force -- the **Sinatra Doctrine** ("do it their way"), named in October 1989. **Solidarity was re-legalised** in April 1989 after round-table talks, and won the semi-free election of June 1989. Hungary opened its border with Austria in 1989; East Germans fled west, mass protests followed, and the **Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989**. The Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia and the violent fall of Ceaușescu in Romania followed in December. Within the USSR, riots in Kazakhstan (1986) and independence movements in the **Baltic states** -- Lithuania declared independence in March 1990 -- met a mix of concessions and force, including the killings in Vilnius in January 1991.

**The end of the USSR.** Conservatives opposed Gorbachev's reforms and a new union treaty; reformers led by Boris Yeltsin wanted faster change. The **August 1991 coup** by hardliners collapsed within three days after Yeltsin led resistance in Moscow and troops refused to fire. It discredited the party and destroyed Gorbachev's authority. Republics declared independence; Russia, Ukraine and Belarus founded the Commonwealth of Independent States in December 1991, and Gorbachev resigned on 25 December 1991.

## Worked example: setting up a judgement

*"Economic failure was the main reason for the collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe." Assess this view.* **[30]**

1. **Criterion:** a cause is "main" if the others depended on it.
2. **For:** debt and shortages created Solidarity (1980) and the protests of 1989; Soviet economic weakness made subsidies and military intervention unaffordable.
3. **Against:** Gorbachev's decision not to use force (the Sinatra Doctrine) was a political choice; nationalism and the Church gave movements their identity; Western influence and Helsinki rights gave them a language.
4. **Judgement:** economic failure created the pressure, but without Gorbachev's refusal to intervene, the 1953, 1956 and 1968 pattern of repression could have repeated.

## Common mistakes

- Merging the collapse of the satellites (1989) and of the USSR (1991).
- Describing the Brezhnev Doctrine as announced before the 1968 invasion.
- Treating Gorbachev as aiming to end communism; he aimed to reform it.

## Exam technique

**Assess** questions on "how and why" need both narrative precision and causation. Group causes (economic, political, national, external) and show links between them.
