## Causes: how oil gets out

- **Extraction**: blow-outs and leaks at wells and platforms; spills during loading.
- **Pipelines**: corrosion, storm or anchor damage, weak joints, theft.
- **Shipping**: groundings, collisions, fuel leaks.
- **Tank cleaning at sea**: oily washing water released deliberately.
- **Refineries**: leaks and waste water at coastal plants.

Accidents are dramatic; routine releases add oil every day.

## Fate of spilled oil

Floats → spreads as a slick → lighter parts evaporate → some forms a thick emulsion → droplets disperse → some sinks or washes ashore as sticky lumps. So surface, water column, seabed and shore are all affected.

## Impacts: organism → reason

| Group | Main harm | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Birds | Cold, drowning, poisoning | Matted feathers lose waterproofing, insulation and buoyancy; oil swallowed while preening |
| Marine mammals | Cold, breathing damage, poisoning | Fur loses insulation; fumes inhaled at the surface; oil swallowed |
| Fish | Suffocation, death of eggs, contamination | Gills coated; surface eggs killed; toxins in tissue make fish unsafe |
| Crustaceans | Smothering and poisoning | Sunk or stranded oil covers shore and seabed |
| Seaweeds | Less photosynthesis | Oil blocks light; shore seaweed smothered |
| Coral reefs | Coral death | Smothering, blocked light, toxic compounds |
| Beaches | Loss of life and income | Oil coats sand and rocks; tourism falls; costly clean-up |

Link outwards: damaged seaweed and coral → loss of habitat and food → declines further up the food web.

## Prevention vs minimising impacts

**Prevention: stop oil escaping**
- MARPOL: international rules limiting oily discharges and controlling tank cleaning and waste.
- Double hulls: inner hull keeps oil in if the outer hull is holed.
- Risk assessments: find hazards and plan responses in advance.
- Regular maintenance: find corrosion and worn parts before failure.

**Minimising impacts: reduce damage**
- Improved navigation: fewer groundings and collisions on dangerous routes.
- Booms: floating barriers that contain or divert a slick; sorbents soak up oil.
- Detergent sprays: break oil into droplets that disperse.
- Skimmers: lift oil off the surface into tanks.
- Controlled burning: burn thick, fresh oil at sea.

## Benefit / limitation pairs

- Booms: protect coasts / fail in rough water.
- Sorbents: simple / create oily waste.
- Detergents: fast over large areas / toxic; oil still in water.
- Skimmers: recover oil / slow; need calm seas.
- Burning: removes a lot quickly / smoke; needs calm weather and thick oil.
- MARPOL: global rules / depends on enforcement at sea.
- Double hulls: contain oil after a breach / more expensive tankers.

## Quick calculations

- Oil remaining = spill × (100 − % removed) ÷ 100. A 5,000-tonne spill with 35% removed leaves 5,000 × 0.65 = 3,250 tonnes.
- Boom to circle a slick = π × diameter. A 0.8 km slick needs about 2.5 km of boom.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Slick | A layer of oil floating and spreading on the water surface |
| MARPOL | International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships |
| Double-hulled tanker | A tanker with two hull layers separated by a space |
| Risk assessment | Identifying hazards and planning to reduce them |
| Boom | A floating barrier that contains or diverts oil |
| Sorbent | A material that soaks up oil |
| Detergent spray | A chemical that breaks oil into small droplets |
| Skimmer | A device that removes oil from the surface |
| Controlled burning | Deliberately burning spilled oil on the water |

## Exam traps

- Booms, sorbents, detergents, skimmers and burning are **not** prevention.
- Improved navigation is listed under minimising impacts in the syllabus, even though it reduces accidents.
- Detergents disperse oil; they do not remove it.
- Name the reason for harm (insulation, buoyancy, light, gills), not just "oil kills".
- Crustaceans are crabs, shrimps and similar animals, not all shellfish.

8. Give one limitation of booms.
9. What do detergent sprays do to oil?
10. Give one limitation of controlled burning.
11. A spill of 3,000 tonnes has 45% removed. How much oil remains?
12. Is a skimmer a prevention or a minimising strategy?
