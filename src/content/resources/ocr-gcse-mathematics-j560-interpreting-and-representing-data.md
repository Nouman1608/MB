This guide covers section 12.02, Interpreting and representing data, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560 specification, Version 2.1 (August 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Statement 12.02a is content for **both tiers**. Statement 12.02b, on cumulative frequency graphs and histograms, sits in the column headed "Higher tier learners should additionally be able to", so it is **Higher tier only** and is labelled below.

The [sampling, charts, cumulative frequency and histograms guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-sampling-and-representing-data-with-charts-line-graphs/) introduces 12.02 alongside 12.01. This page goes further on 12.02 alone: matching a chart to the data type, reading a pie chart backwards, comparing pie charts with different totals, completing two-way tables, describing a time series, and building and reading cumulative frequency graphs and unequal-width histograms. ## What the specification asks

| Ref | Statement in the specification | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 12.02a Categorical and numerical data | "Interpret and construct charts appropriate to the data type; including frequency tables, bar charts, pie charts and pictograms for categorical data, vertical line charts for ungrouped discrete numerical data. Interpret multiple and composite bar charts." | Both |
| 12.02a | "Design tables to classify data. Interpret and construct line graphs for time series data, and identify trends (e.g. seasonal variations)." | Both |
| 12.02b Grouped data | "Interpret and construct diagrams for grouped data as appropriate, i.e. cumulative frequency graphs and histograms (with either equal or unequal class intervals)." | Higher tier only |

## Matching the chart to the data

| Data type | Example | Suitable diagrams |
|---|---|---|
| Categorical | Favourite sport, eye colour | Frequency table, bar chart, pie chart, pictogram |
| Ungrouped discrete | Number of goals in a match | Frequency table, vertical line chart |
| Data recorded over time | Monthly sales | Line graph (time series) |
| Grouped continuous | Heights, times, masses | Cumulative frequency graph, histogram (Higher) |

A **vertical line chart** draws a thin line at each value, with height equal to its frequency. It suits discrete numbers because nothing lies between 2 goals and 3 goals. For goals per match with frequencies 0: 4, 1: 7, 2: 5, 3: 3, 4: 1, the tallest line is at 1, so 1 goal is the mode.

## Pie charts in both directions

Constructing: angle = (frequency ÷ total) × 360°.

Interpreting: frequency = (angle ÷ 360°) × total.

**Worked example 1.** A pie chart shows the holiday choices of 240 people. The beach sector has an angle of 105°. How many chose the beach?

```
105/360 × 240 = 70 people
```

**Comparing two pie charts.** School A (300 students) and School B (120 students) each have a 90° sector for cycling. Both show that a quarter of students cycle, but School A has 75 cyclists and School B has 30. A pie chart shows proportions, not frequencies. To compare numbers, you need the totals.

## Pictograms

The key gives the value of one symbol. If one symbol represents 6 cups of coffee, then 27 cups is 27 ÷ 6 = 4.5 symbols: four whole symbols and a half. When reading, work out what fraction of a symbol each part shows: a quarter symbol is 1.5 cups.

## Multiple and composite bar charts

In a **multiple** bar chart, bars for each group stand side by side, and each bar is read from the axis. In a **composite** bar chart, the parts are stacked into one bar per group.

**Worked example 2.** A composite bar for Year 10 lunch choices has sections from 0 to 45 (hot meal), 45 to 80 (sandwich) and 80 to 120 (packed lunch).

```
hot meal = 45 − 0  = 45
sandwich = 80 − 45 = 35
packed   = 120 − 80 = 40
total    = 120 (the top of the bar)
```

The top of the bar is the group total. A composite bar chart makes totals easy to compare; a multiple bar chart makes individual categories easier to compare.

## Designing tables to classify data

A two-way table classifies the same people by two features. Row and column totals let you find missing entries.

**Worked example 3.** 150 students are classified by year group and how they travel. There are 80 Year 10s and 70 Year 11s. 60 travel by bus, 55 walk and 35 come by car. 34 Year 10s take the bus and 22 Year 11s walk. Complete the table.

| | Bus | Walk | Car | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 10 | 34 | 55 − 22 = **33** | 80 − 34 − 33 = **13** | 80 |
| Year 11 | 60 − 34 = **26** | 22 | 35 − 13 = **22** | 70 |
| Total | 60 | 55 | 35 | 150 |

Check the last row you filled: 26 + 22 + 22 = 70.

When you design a data collection table, give categories that do not overlap and that cover every possible response, include an "other" category if needed, and leave columns for tallies and frequencies.

## Time series

A **time series** records the same quantity at equal time intervals. Plot time across the horizontal axis and join the points with straight lines.

**Worked example 4.** Heating oil delivered by a supplier, in thousands of litres:

| Year | Jan–Mar | Apr–Jun | Jul–Sep | Oct–Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 48 | 22 | 10 | 40 |
| Year 2 | 44 | 20 | 9 | 37 |
| Year 3 | 41 | 18 | 8 | 34 |

- **Seasonal variation:** every year, deliveries are highest in January to March and lowest in July to September, matching colder and warmer months.
- **Trend:** each quarter is lower than the same quarter a year earlier, so the overall trend is **downward**.
- **Prediction:** January to March fell by 4 and then by 3, so about 37 or 38 thousand litres is a reasonable estimate for Year 4. Say that it assumes the pattern continues.

Compare like with like. Saying "deliveries fell from 48 to 22" mixes the trend with the seasonal pattern.

## Cumulative frequency graphs (Higher tier only)

Cumulative frequency is a running total. Plot each value at the **upper class boundary**, start at the lower boundary of the first class with a cumulative frequency of 0, and join with a smooth curve.

**Worked example 5.** 80 people time how long they take to finish a puzzle.

| Time t (min) | 0<t≤2 | 2<t≤4 | 4<t≤6 | 6<t≤8 | 8<t≤10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 6 | 15 | 28 | 19 | 12 |
| Cumulative frequency | 6 | 21 | 49 | 68 | 80 |

Plot (0, 0), (2, 6), (4, 21), (6, 49), (8, 68) and (10, 80).

To estimate how many took **more than 7 minutes**, read up from t = 7 to the curve and across: the cumulative frequency is about 59, so about 80 − 59 = **21 people** took longer. The steepest part of the curve (4 to 6 minutes) is where the data are most concentrated.

## Histograms (Higher tier only)

With unequal class widths, frequency is shown by the **area** of each bar, and the vertical axis is frequency density.

```
frequency density = frequency ÷ class width
```

**Worked example 6.** Lengths of 120 fish:

| Length l (cm) | 10<l≤20 | 20<l≤25 | 25<l≤30 | 30<l≤40 | 40<l≤60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 18 | 24 | 30 | 36 | 12 |
| Class width | 10 | 5 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| Frequency density | 1.8 | 4.8 | 6.0 | 3.6 | 0.6 |

Draw bars with no gaps, each as wide as its class and as tall as its frequency density.

- The **tallest bar** is 25 to 30 cm, but the **largest frequency** is 30 to 40 cm (36 fish). Height shows density, not frequency.
- **Estimate the number between 35 cm and 45 cm**, assuming values are spread evenly within each class: 35 to 40 is half of its bar, 5 × 3.6 = 18; 40 to 45 is 5 × 0.6 = 3. Estimate: **21 fish**.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the top of a composite section instead of subtracting its two edges.
- Comparing frequencies from two pie charts without using the totals.
- Drawing bar charts with touching bars for categories, or line charts with wide bars.
- Describing a time series by comparing neighbouring seasons instead of the same season each year.
- Plotting cumulative frequency at the midpoint instead of the upper boundary (Higher).
- Using bar height as frequency in an unequal-width histogram (Higher).

## Exam technique

- **"Construct" or "Draw"**: label both axes, give a key where needed, and show angle or density calculations in a table.
- **"Interpret" or "Describe"**: use the context and quote values from the chart.
- **"Compare"**: write a comparison sentence, such as "School A has more cyclists, although the proportion is the same".
- **"Estimate"** from a graph: draw your reading lines and give the answer as "about".
