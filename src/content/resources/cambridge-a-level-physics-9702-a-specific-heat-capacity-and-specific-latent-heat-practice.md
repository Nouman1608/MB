Unless told otherwise, take: specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; specific latent heat of vaporisation of water = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. Experimental data are invented for practice.

## Section A

**1.** Define specific latent heat of fusion. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between specific latent heat of fusion and specific latent heat of vaporisation. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of a 0.75 kg copper block from 20 °C to 180 °C. The specific heat capacity of copper is 385 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. **[2]**

**4.** Express the unit of specific heat capacity, J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, in SI base units. **[2]**

**5.** Explain, in terms of molecules, why the temperature of a pure substance stays constant while it is melting, even though energy is being supplied. **[2]**

**6.** Explain, in terms of molecules, why the specific latent heat of vaporisation of a substance is much greater than its specific latent heat of fusion. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** 10 g of steam at 100 °C condenses on a person's skin, and the water formed cools to 37 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy released when 10 g of water at 100 °C cools to 37 °C. **[1]**

**(c)** Use your answers to explain why a scald from steam is usually more serious than one from the same mass of boiling water. **[1]**

**8.** A 1.00 kg aluminium block is heated by an electrical heater connected to a 12 V supply. The current is 4.0 A. In 360 s the temperature of the block rises by 18.5 K.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium from these data. **[3]**

**(b)** A data book gives 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Explain why the experimental value is larger. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** improvement to the method that would reduce this difference. **[1]**

**9.** A 0.25 kg sample of a solid is heated by an 80 W heater. Ignore energy losses. The sample warms from 20 °C to 60 °C in 160 s, stays at 60 °C for 250 s while it melts, and then the liquid warms from 60 °C to 90 °C in 75 s.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the solid. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the liquid. **[2]**

**(d)** State how the heating curve shows which state has the larger specific heat capacity. **[1]**

**10.** A kettle of power 2.2 kW contains 0.20 kg of water at 25 °C. It is switched on and left until all the water has boiled away.

**(a)** Calculate the total energy needed. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the minimum time taken. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the actual time is longer. **[1]**

**11.** To measure the specific latent heat of vaporisation of a liquid, a heater keeps the liquid boiling. In two runs, each lasting 400 s, the results are:

| Heater power / W | Mass boiled away / g |
|---|---|
| 50 | 6.0 |
| 30 | 2.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the specific latent heat of vaporisation. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the rate at which energy is lost to the surroundings. **[2]**

**12.** A drink of mass 0.30 kg and specific heat capacity 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ is at 25 °C. Ice at 0 °C is added. The final temperature of the drink and melted ice is 5 °C. Calculate the mass of ice added, assuming no energy exchange with the surroundings. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The energy required per unit mass [1] to change a solid into a liquid without a change in temperature [1].

**2.** Fusion refers to the change between solid and liquid (melting or freezing) [1]; vaporisation refers to the change between liquid and gas (boiling or condensing) [1].

**3.** Q = mcΔθ = 0.75 × 385 × 160 [1] = 4.6 × 10⁴ J [1].

**4.** J = kg m² s⁻² [1]; so J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ = m² s⁻² K⁻¹ [1].

**5.** The energy supplied is used to break the regular arrangement and increase the separation of the molecules, increasing their potential energy [1]; the mean kinetic energy of the molecules does not change, so the temperature stays constant [1].

**6.** On vaporisation, molecules are separated to much larger distances [1], so nearly all the intermolecular attractions must be overcome, whereas on melting the molecules stay close together [1]; also the vapour occupies a much larger volume, so work is done against the atmosphere as it expands [1].

**7. (a)** Condensing: 0.010 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 22 600 J; cooling: 0.010 × 4200 × 63 = 2650 J [1]; total = 2.5 × 10⁴ J [1].

**(b)** 0.010 × 4200 × 63 = 2.6 × 10³ J [1].

**(c)** The steam releases about ten times as much energy to the skin, mostly from its latent heat as it condenses [1].

**8. (a)** Energy = VIt = 12 × 4.0 × 360 = 17 280 J [1]; c = Q/(mΔθ) = 17 280 ÷ (1.00 × 18.5) [1] = 930 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** Some energy from the heater is lost to the surroundings, so the block absorbs less than VIt; the calculation uses all of VIt and so overestimates c [1].

**(c)** Insulate the block (for example with lagging); or use a smaller temperature rise centred near room temperature; or apply a correction for cooling [1].

**9. (a)** c = Pt/(mΔθ) = 80 × 160 ÷ (0.25 × 40) [1] = 1280 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** L_f = Pt/m = 80 × 250 ÷ 0.25 [1] = 8.0 × 10⁴ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** c = 80 × 75 ÷ (0.25 × 30) [1] = 800 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ [1].

**(d)** The solid has the larger c: its section of the graph has the smaller gradient (it rises 40 K in 160 s, compared with 30 K in 75 s for the liquid) [1].

**10. (a)** Heating: 0.20 × 4200 × 75 = 63 000 J [1]; boiling: 0.20 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 452 000 J [1]; total = 5.15 × 10⁵ J [1].

**(b)** t = 5.15 × 10⁵ ÷ 2200 = 234 s [1].

**(c)** Energy is also lost to the surroundings and used to heat the kettle itself [1].

**11. (a)** 50 × 400 = (6.0 × 10⁻³)L + 400h and 30 × 400 = (2.5 × 10⁻³)L + 400h [1]; subtracting: 20 × 400 = (3.5 × 10⁻³)L [1]; L = 2.3 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** 400h = 20 000 – (6.0 × 10⁻³ × 2.29 × 10⁶) = 20 000 – 13 700 = 6300 J [1]; h = 16 W [1].

**12.** Energy lost by drink = 0.30 × 4200 × 20 = 25 200 J [1]. Energy gained by ice = m(3.34 × 10⁵ + 4200 × 5) = 355 000m [1]. m = 25 200 ÷ 355 000 = 0.071 kg [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting "per unit mass" or "without a change in temperature" from definitions.
- Using one specific heat capacity for both the solid and the liquid.
- Forgetting the warming stage of melted ice in mixing questions.
- Leaving masses in grams.
- Saying losses make the measured specific heat capacity too small.
- Writing that no energy is supplied while the temperature is constant.
