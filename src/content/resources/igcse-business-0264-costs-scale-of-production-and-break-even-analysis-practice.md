## Questions

**1.** Define variable costs. **[2]**

**2.** Cobbleton Bakery has four costs: flour, shop rent, cardboard boxes and the owner's salary. Identify whether each is a fixed cost or a variable cost. **[4]**

**3.** Cobbleton Bakery has fixed costs of £5,400 a month. Variable cost is £0.70 per loaf. In one month it makes 6,000 loaves.

**(a)** Calculate the total variable cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the average cost per loaf. **[2]**

**4.** Stainmoor Joinery plans to double the number of chairs it makes each year. Explain two economies of scale it might gain. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two causes of diseconomies of scale. **[4]**

**6.** Dovesdale Cafe uses 1,800 litres of milk a month. Supplier A charges £0.82 per litre with no other charge. Supplier B charges £0.76 per litre plus a fixed delivery fee of £90 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly cost of using each supplier. **[3]**
**(b)** State which supplier is cheaper and by how much. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason, other than cost, why the café might still choose the dearer supplier. **[2]**

**7.** Kettlewick Toys makes three products. Fixed costs of £40,000 a month are shared between them.

| Product | Selling price per unit | Variable cost per unit | Units sold a month |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | £15 | £9 | 3,000 |
| B | £24 | £17 | 1,500 |
| C | £40 | £31 | 2,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per unit of each product. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total contribution Product C earns in a month. **[1]**
**(c)** Fixed costs would stay at £40,000 if Product C were stopped. Explain whether the cost data supports stopping C. **[3]**

**8.** Marshgate Games sells a card game for £24. Variable cost is £14 per game and fixed costs are £35,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per game. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the break-even output. **[2]**
**(c)** Sales are expected to be 4,200 games. Calculate the margin of safety. **[2]**
**(d)** Define margin of safety. **[2]**

**9.** Tollgate Tours sells day tickets at £40 each. Variable cost is £15 per ticket and fixed costs are £12,000 for the season. Part of its break-even table is shown.

| Tickets sold | 0 | 240 | 480 | 720 | 960 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total revenue (£) | 0 | 9,600 | 19,200 | 28,800 | 38,400 |
| Total cost (£) | 12,000 | 15,600 | ? | ? | ? |

**(a)** Calculate the three missing total costs. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how you would draw the total cost line on a break-even chart. **[2]**
**(c)** State the break-even output shown by the table. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the profit when 720 tickets are sold. **[2]**

**10.** Stainmoor Joinery sells a chair for £80. Variable cost is £50 per chair and fixed costs are £45,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even output. **[2]**
**(b)** The owner is thinking of cutting the price to £74. Calculate the new break-even output. **[2]**
**(c)** Instead, the owner finds a cheaper workshop and fixed costs fall to £39,000, with the price at £80. Calculate the new break-even output. **[2]**
**(d)** Variable cost rises to £55 per chair. Explain the effect on the total cost line and on break-even output. **[2]**

**11.** Explain two limitations of break-even analysis for a business that is planning a new range of products. **[4]**

**12.** Pickersgill Printing sells boxes of printed labels at £6 a box. Its one machine can make up to 9,000 boxes a year. Fixed costs are £24,000 a year and variable cost is £2.40 per box. Sales are 8,500 boxes. The owner is thinking of buying a second machine. Fixed costs would rise to £34,800 a year and variable cost would fall to £2.10 per box. The owner forecasts sales of 12,500 boxes, but this depends on one customer who has not yet signed a contract.

**(a)** Calculate the current break-even output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the break-even output with the second machine. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the forecast annual profit with the second machine. **[2]**
**(d)** Justify whether the owner should buy the second machine. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Costs that change [1] directly with the level of output (or number of units produced) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs the idea of change linked to output. "Costs that vary" with no link to output repeats the word.

**2.** Flour: variable [1]. Shop rent: fixed [1]. Cardboard boxes: variable [1]. Owner's salary: fixed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-word reply for each item is enough. Do not explain at length.

**3. (a)** 0.70 × 6,000 = **£4,200** [1]
**(b)** 5,400 + 4,200 = **£9,600** [1]
**(c)** 9,600 ÷ 6,000 [1] = **£1.60** per loaf [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the division in (c): a bare wrong figure gives an examiner nothing to credit. Include the £ sign.
**[4 in total]**

**4.** Any two types, each identified and explained in context, for example:
Purchasing economies [1]: it buys twice as much timber, so the supplier may give a discount, which lowers the timber cost per chair [1].
Technical economies [1]: with double the output, a larger automatic saw becomes affordable and is used fully, which lowers cost per chair [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a type earns little without the link to a lower average cost. Tie each reason to timber, machines or chairs.

**5.** Any two causes, each named and outlined, for example:
Poor communication [1]: messages pass through many layers and arrive late or distorted [1].
Lack of commitment or loyalty from employees [1]: in a large workforce people feel unimportant, so effort falls and more leave [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" asks for the main points, so one sentence on each cause is enough. A cause named but with no outline is a half answer.

**6. (a)** Supplier A: 0.82 × 1,800 = **£1,476** [1]. Supplier B: 0.76 × 1,800 = 1,368 [1], plus 90 = **£1,458** [1].
**(b)** Supplier B is cheaper, by **£18** a month [1].
**(c)** Supplier A may deliver more reliably [1], so the café is less likely to run out of milk on busy days [1]. (Other valid reasons: quality, payment terms.)
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the delivery fee is the usual slip in (a). In (c) a bare "quality" is a label; say what difference it makes.
**[6 in total]**

**7. (a)** A: 15 − 9 = **£6** [1]. B: 24 − 17 = **£7** [1]. C: 40 − 31 = **£9** [1].
**(b)** 9 × 2,000 = **£18,000** [1]
**(c)** The £40,000 of fixed costs continues whether or not C is made [1]. Stopping C would remove £18,000 of contribution [1], so monthly profit would fall by £18,000, and the cost data supports continuing with C unless the resources could be used to earn more [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare contribution, not selling price, and link it to the fixed costs that stay. Claiming "C is most profitable" with no figures does not use the data.
**[7 in total]**

**8. (a)** 24 − 14 = **£10** [1]
**(b)** 35,000 ÷ 10 [1] = **3,500 games** [1]
**(c)** 4,200 − 3,500 [1] = **700 games** [1]
**(d)** The number of units by which actual (or expected) sales are above break-even output [1], showing how far sales could fall before the business makes a loss [1].
*Examiner insight:* Break-even output and margin of safety are in units, so give units, not pounds.
**[7 in total]**

**9. (a)** At 480: 12,000 + 15 × 480 = **£19,200** [1]. At 720: 12,000 + 10,800 = **£22,800** [1]. At 960: 12,000 + 14,400 = **£26,400** [1].
**(b)** Plot points of tickets against total cost, starting at £12,000 on the vertical axis when no tickets are sold [1], then join the points with a straight line [1].
**(c)** **480 tickets**, where total revenue and total cost are both £19,200 [1].
**(d)** 28,800 − 22,800 [1] = **£6,000 profit** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The total cost line begins at the fixed cost level, not the origin.
**[8 in total]**

**10. (a)** Contribution = 80 − 50 = £30 [1]. 45,000 ÷ 30 = **1,500 chairs** [1].
**(b)** Contribution = 74 − 50 = £24 [1]. 45,000 ÷ 24 = **1,875 chairs** [1].
**(c)** 39,000 ÷ 30 [1] = **1,300 chairs** [1].
**(d)** The total cost line becomes steeper [1]. Contribution falls to £25, so break-even output rises to 1,800 chairs (moves right on the chart) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the new contribution before dividing. If a question went on to ask for a judgement on the price cut, you would also need to comment on whether demand will rise.
**[8 in total]**

**11.** Any two limitations, each stated and explained for a new range, for example:
It assumes all output is sold at a constant price [1]. New products may need discounts to attract customers, so revenue per unit would be lower than forecast [1].
It relies on forecasts of costs and sales [1]. For untested products these estimates may be wrong, so the break-even point may be misleading [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A limitation must be linked to the situation. "It is not accurate" with no reason is too general to earn full credit.

**12. (a)** Contribution = 6 − 2.40 = £3.60 [1]. 24,000 ÷ 3.60 = 6,666.7, so **6,667 boxes** [1].
**(b)** Contribution = 6 − 2.10 = £3.90 [1]. 34,800 ÷ 3.90 = 8,923.1, so **8,924 boxes** [1].
**(c)** Contribution = 12,500 × 3.90 = £48,750 [1]. Profit = 48,750 − 34,800 = **£13,950** [1].
**(d)** For: forecast profit of £13,950 is well above the £8,400 the old machine earns at its limit of 9,000 boxes (9,000 × 3.60 − 24,000) [1]. Against: break-even output rises from 6,667 to 8,924 boxes, and if sales stay at 8,500 the business would lose £1,650 (8,500 × 3.90 − 34,800), because the forecast depends on an unsigned customer [1]. Judgement: the owner should buy only once the customer's order is confirmed [1], because that removes the main risk to the large margin of safety (3,576 boxes) the machine would then give [1].
*Examiner insight:* A justify answer needs a decision and figures to support it. Quoting only the profit, and ignoring the higher break-even point and the risk, gives a one-sided case.
**[10 in total]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of contribution per unit.
- Giving break-even output or margin of safety without units.
- Subtracting the wrong way round in the margin of safety.
- Starting the total cost line at the origin.
- Naming an economy or diseconomy of scale with no effect on average cost.
- Deciding on break-even output alone, with no comment on demand or risk, or giving only the points in favour of a "justify" answer.
