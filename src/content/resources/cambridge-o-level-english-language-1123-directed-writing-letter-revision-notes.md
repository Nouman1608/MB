These notes cover the **letter**, one of the five forms the syllabus names for Paper 2 Section A: candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article". The general method is in the [directed writing revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/); the full explanation and a model letter are in the [writing a letter study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-letter/).

## Task facts

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Marks | 25: writing 15 (W1–W5), reading 10 (R3, R5) |
| Length | about 250–350 words |
| Key form objective | W4: use register appropriate to context |
| Also central | W2: organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect |
| Purposes | discursive, argumentative or persuasive |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recipient | the person or organisation the letter is addressed to |
| Salutation | the greeting: "Dear..." |
| Sign-off | the closing formula: "Yours sincerely", "Yours faithfully" |
| Purpose statement | the opening sentence that says why you are writing and what you think |
| Request | what you want the recipient to do |
| Polite firmness | a courteous tone combined with clear, evidence-based demands |
| Letter to the editor | a letter to a newspaper, written for its readers |
| Concession | acknowledging a fair point on the other side before answering it |

## Salutations and sign-offs

| Salutation | Sign-off |
|---|---|
| Dear Mr / Ms / Dr [surname] | Yours sincerely |
| Dear Sir or Madam | Yours faithfully |
| Dear Editor | Yours faithfully |
| Dear [first name] (friend or relative) | Best wishes / With love |

Memory aid: **s**urname, **s**incerely; unnamed, faithfully.

## Register by recipient

| Recipient | Register | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| council, company | formal | courteous, precise |
| head teacher | formal | respectful, constructive |
| newspaper editor | formal | openly persuasive, aware of other readers |
| friend or relative | semi-formal or informal | warm but organised |

## Letter shape (about 300 words)

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Salutation | matches the recipient |
| Opening paragraph | who you are, the issue, your view |
| Body paragraphs (3) | one point each: source detail + development or evaluation + link to the request |
| Counter-point paragraph | the strongest opposing view, conceded fairly and answered |
| Closing paragraph | the specific request, a brief thank you |
| Sign-off | matches the salutation |

## Polite and firm phrases

- "I am writing to express my concern about..."
- "While I understand..., I believe..."
- "It is difficult to see how..."
- "I would urge you to reconsider..."
- "I would be grateful if you could..."
- "I hope you will agree that..."
- "Thank you for considering my views."

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Hi, I'm writing about the buses." | "I am writing as a regular passenger to object to the plan to end the evening service." |
| "Your plan is stupid." | "The plan appears to overlook passengers who have no alternative." |
| "Please do something about it." | "I would ask you to keep at least one late service and to consult passengers before deciding." |
| "Yours sincerely" after "Dear Sir or Madam" | "Yours faithfully" |

## Mini example: one paragraph, three moves (original)

> Your notice says that "few passengers" use the late bus. Few, perhaps, but not unimportant: nurses
> leaving evening shifts and students at the evening college have no other way home. I would ask the
> company to publish its passenger figures before making a final decision.

**Use** (the company's claim) → **evaluate** (vague evidence; who those passengers are) → **request**
(publish figures).

## Letters to the editor: extras

- Refer to the article or event that prompted the letter.
- Speak to readers' shared experience.
- End with a memorable point or a call to the community.
- Stay formal; never attack the journalist.

## Exam traps

- Mismatched salutation and sign-off.
- No purpose statement.
- Rude or sarcastic tone.
- Vague or missing request.
- Wrong recipient or wrong role.
- Retelling the sources in order.
- Informal words in a formal letter.
- Spending time on addresses instead of content.

## Self-test

1. Which writing objective is most affected by the choice of recipient?
2. What sign-off follows "Dear Sir or Madam"?
3. What sign-off follows "Dear Mrs Ali"?
4. What three things should the opening paragraph tell the reader?
5. Rewrite politely and firmly: "You obviously don't care about students."
6. How does a letter to the editor differ from a letter to a council?
7. Why include a paragraph answering the opposing view?
8. What should the closing paragraph contain?
9. Give two useful phrases for a formal request.
10. Why should a letter make a specific request rather than a general one?
11. Rewrite for a formal letter: "There are loads of problems with this idea."
12. How long should the letter be?

**Answers**

1. W4, register appropriate to context.
2. "Yours faithfully".
3. "Yours sincerely".
4. Who you are, what the letter is about, and what you think.
5. For example: "I am concerned that the proposal does not take students' needs into account."
6. It is written for the newspaper's readers as well as the editor, so it can appeal more openly to the community, and it usually refers to an article or event.
7. The recipient will know the opposing view; answering it fairly makes the argument more convincing.
8. A specific request for action and a brief thank you, followed by the sign-off.
9. Any two: "I would be grateful if...", "I would urge you to...", "I would ask you to consider...".
10. A specific request is easier to agree to and shows the writer has thought about solutions.
11. For example: "This proposal raises several serious concerns."
12. About 250–350 words.
