## 1. The structure of the economy

Economists group economic activity into three sectors.

| Sector | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracts or harvests natural resources | Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction |
| Secondary | Turns materials into goods | Manufacturing, construction, energy generation |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Retail, banking, health care, education, tourism |

The structure of an economy is the share of output, or of employment, in each sector. As economies develop, the usual pattern is a shift from primary towards secondary activity (**industrialisation**), and later from secondary towards tertiary activity (**deindustrialisation**). In the UK today, services produce about four-fifths of output.

**Worked example 1 (invented Country Z, employment).**

| | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (m workers) | 6.0 | 2.4 | 3.6 | 12.0 |
| Share | 50% | 20% | 30% | 100% |
| Year 20 (m workers) | 3.0 | 4.5 | 7.5 | 15.0 |
| Share | 20% | 30% | 50% | 100% |

- The primary share falls by 30 percentage points and the number of primary workers halves.
- Tertiary employment rises from 3.6 m to 7.5 m, an increase of 3.9 ÷ 3.6 × 100 = **108.3%**.
- A falling **share** is not always a falling **number**: secondary employment rises from 2.4 m to 4.5 m.

**Why the structure changes.**

- **Productivity in farming rises**, so fewer workers are needed to feed the population and labour is released for factories and offices.
- **Demand changes with income.** As incomes rise, spending on food rises less than in proportion, while many services (health, leisure, finance) are income elastic.
- **Comparative advantage shifts.** Manufacturing may move to countries with lower labour costs, while richer economies specialise in high-skill services.

Moving workers from low-productivity farming to factories and offices raises average output per worker, so structural change is itself a source of growth.

## 2. Sustainable development as a policy objective

**Economic development** is a sustained improvement in living standards and wellbeing. It includes health, education, poverty reduction and freedom, not just income.

**Sustainable development** is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This definition comes from the Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future* (1987). It has three strands:

- **Economic:** rising incomes and secure jobs.
- **Social:** better health and education, less poverty, fairer distribution.
- **Environmental:** keeping the stock of **natural capital** (forests, fish stocks, clean air, a stable climate) so that it can keep supporting output.

**Why governments pursue it.** It is about fairness between generations: using up resources today leaves future generations poorer. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, 17 goals adopted in 2015, run to 2030, and the UK is legally committed to net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050. Policies include carbon and pollution taxes, subsidies for renewable energy, regulation, and investment in education and health.

## 3. Economic growth and sustainable development

Growth and sustainable development can support each other or conflict.

| Growth helps sustainable development | Growth conflicts with sustainable development |
|---|---|
| Higher incomes and tax revenue fund schools, hospitals and clean water, and reduce poverty | Output based on depleting natural resources cannot continue |
| Richer economies can afford cleaner technology and regulation | Pollution and carbon emissions rise with production |
| A shift to services may use fewer resources per unit of output | Gains may go to a few, so social progress lags |

**Worked example 2 (invented Country W, natural capital).** Real GDP rises from 500 bn to 520 bn, growth of 4.0%. Felling forest that will not regrow contributed 5 bn of output in year 1 and 15 bn in year 2. Deducting that lost natural capital gives 495 bn and 505 bn, so the adjusted growth rate is 10 ÷ 495 × 100 = **2.02%**. Half of the measured growth came from running down a stock that future output depends on.

**Decoupling.** If real GDP rises by 20% while emissions rise by 8%, emissions per unit of output fall to 1.08 ÷ 1.20 = 0.90 of their old level, a fall of 10%. This **relative** decoupling still lets total emissions rise; only **absolute** decoupling, with emissions falling as output grows, cuts total damage.

**Evaluation.** Growth makes sustainable development easier to afford but does not guarantee it. The outcome depends on the **type** of growth (resource extraction or skills and technology), **policy** (whether pollution is priced), **distribution**, the **time period** and the **stage of development**: for a very poor country, growth may be the fastest way to improve health and education.

## 4. GDP as a measure

**Strengths.** GDP and GDP per capita are produced regularly, use a common method across countries, and measure the resources available for consumption and public services.

**Weaknesses as a measure of development.** GDP per capita is an average, so it hides **inequality**. It leaves out **unpaid work** and much of the **informal economy**, counts **pollution** and resource depletion as gains, and ignores **leisure** and wellbeing. Comparisons between countries also depend on **exchange rates**, so purchasing-power adjusted figures are better.

## 5. The Human Development Index (HDI)

The HDI, published by the United Nations Development Programme, combines three dimensions into one score between 0 and 1:

| Dimension | Indicator |
|---|---|
| Health | Life expectancy at birth |
| Education | Mean years of schooling and expected years of schooling |
| Standard of living | Gross National Income (GNI) per head, adjusted for purchasing power |

**Worked example 3 (simplified method, invented countries).** This is a simplified method for practice, not the official calculation. Each index = (actual − minimum) ÷ (maximum − minimum), using goalposts of 20 to 85 years for life expectancy, 0 to 18 for expected years of schooling and 0 to 50,000 for income per head. The simplified HDI is the simple average of the three indices.

| | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| Life expectancy | 72 | 59 |
| Expected years of schooling | 13.5 | 9.0 |
| Income per head | 20,000 | 30,000 |
| Health index | 52 ÷ 65 = 0.800 | 39 ÷ 65 = 0.600 |
| Education index | 13.5 ÷ 18 = 0.750 | 9 ÷ 18 = 0.500 |
| Income index | 20,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.400 | 30,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.600 |
| Simplified HDI | 1.950 ÷ 3 = **0.650** | 1.700 ÷ 3 = **0.567** |

Country Y has 50% more income per head but a lower HDI, because its health and education outcomes are much weaker. The official method takes the logarithm of income and uses a geometric mean, so each extra unit of income adds less to the score and a very weak dimension pulls the total down more.

**Evaluation of the HDI.**

- **Strengths:** it is broader than GDP, simple to compare, and shows that income does not automatically become health and education.
- **Weaknesses:** it leaves out inequality (the UNDP also publishes an inequality-adjusted HDI), the environment and political freedom; its equal weights are a choice; years of schooling ignore the quality of education; and data in poorer countries can be out of date.

## 6. Other social and cultural indicators

| Indicator | What it adds to GDP |
|---|---|
| Infant mortality and access to clean water | Basic health and living conditions |
| Literacy rate | Whether education reaches everyone |
| Gini coefficient | How evenly income is shared |
| Life satisfaction surveys (for example the ONS personal well-being questions) | How people feel about their lives |
| Carbon emissions per head | Environmental cost of output |
| Participation in cultural life, trust and community ties | Social and cultural wellbeing that has no market price |

**Evaluation.** These indicators capture what income misses. But some are subjective, what counts as a good life differs between cultures, they are hard to combine, and data can be poor where it matters most. The most useful approach is a **dashboard**: GDP per capita for resources, the HDI for broad human development, and a few social, cultural and environmental indicators for what both leave out.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Evaluate the usefulness of the HDI as a measure of development."

> The HDI combines life expectancy, years of schooling and income per head into a score between 0 and 1 *[knowledge]*. It is more useful than GDP per capita because it shows whether income is turned into health and education; a country can have higher income per head but a lower HDI *[analysis]*. However, it leaves out inequality, so a country where gains go to a few can score well *[evaluation]*, and it ignores environmental damage, so it cannot show whether development is sustainable *[evaluation]*. Overall, the HDI is a better single guide to development than GDP, but it is most useful alongside indicators of inequality and the environment *[judgement]*.

## Common mistakes

- Treating growth and development as the same thing.
- Saying a falling share of a sector means fewer people work in it.
- Describing sustainable development only as protecting the environment, leaving out the economic and social strands.
- Listing the HDI's dimensions without saying what it leaves out.
- Claiming one measure is "best" without saying for what purpose.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** define the term, apply it to the data and link it to living standards.
- **Calculate:** show the formula and substitution. Shares are in per cent; a change in a share is in percentage points.
- **Evaluate:** weigh both sides, then say what the answer depends on: the type of growth, policy, the stage of development or the purpose of the measure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a larger tertiary sector not always mean a more developed economy?**
Some low-income economies have large informal service sectors, such as street trading, with low productivity. The share of services is a guide to development, not proof of it.
