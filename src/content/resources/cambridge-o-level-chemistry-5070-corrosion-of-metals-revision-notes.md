Condensed recall for Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 subtopic **9.5 Corrosion of metals**. Full explanations and model answers are in the [Corrosion of metals study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-corrosion-of-metals/).

## The five outcomes

1. Rusting of iron and steel needs **water and oxygen** and forms **hydrated iron(III) oxide**.
2. Barrier methods work by **excluding oxygen or water**.
3. Named barriers: **painting, greasing, coating with plastic**.
4. Sacrificial protection explained by the **reactivity series** and **electron loss**.
5. **Galvanising** with zinc = barrier **and** sacrificial protection.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Corrosion | Gradual destruction of a metal by reaction with its surroundings |
| Rusting | Corrosion of iron or steel only |
| Rust | Hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O |
| Barrier method | A coating that keeps oxygen and/or water away from the iron |
| Sacrificial protection | A more reactive metal corrodes in preference to iron, protecting it |
| Galvanising | Coating iron or steel with zinc |
| Drying agent | Substance that absorbs water vapour (e.g. anhydrous calcium chloride) |

## Equation

```
iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide
4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O
```

Rusting is oxidation: iron gains oxygen and loses electrons.

## The three-tube investigation

| Tube | Set-up | Conditions | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Tap water, open | Water + oxygen | Rust |
| B | Boiled water + oil layer, stoppered | Water, no oxygen | No rust |
| C | Anhydrous calcium chloride, stoppered | Oxygen, no water | No rust |

Purpose of each step:

- Boiling: removes dissolved oxygen.
- Oil layer: stops oxygen redissolving.
- Calcium chloride: absorbs water vapour.
- Stoppers: keep out fresh air and moisture.

## Barrier methods matched to objects

| Method | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Painting | Car body, bridge | Rusts where scratched |
| Greasing / oiling | Bicycle chain, tools | Suits moving parts; also lubricates |
| Plastic coating | Wire fence, dish rack | Waterproof and tough |

Weakness of all barriers: protection stops where the coating is damaged.

## Sacrificial protection: three-step explanation

1. Zinc (or magnesium) is **more reactive** than iron.
2. It **loses electrons** more readily: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
3. So it corrodes **instead of** the iron; the iron does not lose electrons and does not rust.

The sacrificial metal is used up and must be replaced.

## Galvanising: two jobs

| Situation | How zinc protects |
|---|---|
| Coating intact | Barrier: excludes oxygen and water |
| Coating scratched | Sacrificial: zinc corrodes in preference to iron |

## Paint versus galvanising

| Feature | Painted steel | Galvanised steel |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier | Yes | Yes |
| Protected when scratched | No: iron exposed and rusts | Yes: zinc corrodes instead |
| Coating metal more reactive than iron? | Not a metal | Yes (zinc) |

A coating of a metal **less** reactive than iron gives barrier protection only; once scratched, the iron corrodes.

## Barrier versus sacrificial at a glance

| Feature | Barrier method | Sacrificial protection |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Excludes oxygen and/or water | More reactive metal loses electrons instead of iron |
| Needs the iron covered? | Yes, completely | No: contact with the iron is enough |
| Protects at a scratch? | No | Yes |
| Gets used up? | Wears or chips over time | Sacrificial metal corrodes away |
| Examples | Paint, grease, plastic | Zinc or magnesium blocks; zinc in galvanising |

## Exam traps

- Rust is **hydrated iron(III) oxide**, not just "iron oxide".
- Both **water and oxygen** are needed: "air" alone is not enough.
- Only iron and steel **rust**; other metals **corrode**.
- The sacrificial metal is **more** reactive than iron.
- Galvanising needs both explanations: barrier **and** sacrificial.
- In a scratched-coating question, the barrier idea earns no credit.
- Give the specific purpose of each experimental step, not "for a fair test".
- Copper cannot protect iron sacrificially; it is less reactive.

## Model answers

**"State the conditions needed for iron to rust." [2]** Water [1] and oxygen [1].

**"Describe how painting prevents rusting." [1]** The paint forms a layer that keeps oxygen and water away from the iron.

**"Explain why magnesium blocks attached to a steel pipe stop it rusting." [3]** Magnesium is more reactive than iron [1]. It loses electrons in preference to iron (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻) [1]. So the magnesium corrodes and the iron does not rust [1].

## Self-test

1. Name the product of rusting.
2. State the two conditions needed for rusting.
3. Why is the water boiled in the "no oxygen" tube?
4. What is the purpose of anhydrous calcium chloride in the "no water" tube?
5. Name three barrier methods.
6. Explain how a barrier method prevents rusting.
7. Why is a bicycle chain greased rather than painted?
8. Define sacrificial protection.
9. Write the equation for zinc losing electrons.
10. Describe galvanising.
11. Explain why galvanised iron does not rust when scratched.
12. Suggest why copper cannot be used for sacrificial protection of iron.

**Answers:** 1. Hydrated iron(III) oxide. 2. Water and oxygen. 3. To remove dissolved oxygen (air). 4. It is a drying agent that absorbs water vapour. 5. Painting, greasing, coating with plastic. 6. It excludes oxygen and/or water from the surface. 7. The chain moves, so paint would be scraped off; grease stays on and also lubricates. 8. A more reactive metal in contact with iron corrodes in preference to the iron. 9. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. 10. Coating iron or steel with a layer of zinc. 11. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it loses electrons and corrodes instead of the iron. 12. Copper is less reactive than iron, so iron would lose electrons first.
