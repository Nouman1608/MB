## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a statement of financial position. **[2]**

**2.** A van costs £36,000 and has a residual value of £6,000 and a life of five years. Calculate the annual depreciation using the straight line method. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by depreciation. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a current liability?

A: A five-year bank loan.

B: Retained earnings.

C: Money owed to suppliers.

D: Goodwill. **[1]**

**5.** A company buys a business for £150,000. The fair value of its net assets is £118,000. Calculate the goodwill. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Wexley Printers Ltd has these balances at the year end.

- Non-current assets at cost £420,000; accumulated depreciation £120,000.
- Inventory £40,000; receivables £55,000; cash £5,000.
- Payables £38,000; bank overdraft £12,000.
- Bank loan repayable in four years £100,000.
- Share capital £120,000.

**(a)** Calculate the net book value of non-current assets. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate current assets, current liabilities and working capital. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate net assets. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate retained earnings. **[2]**

**(e)** Analyse the financial position of Wexley Printers Ltd. **[6]**

**7.** Wexley's receivables of £55,000 are shown after a provision of £3,000 for doubtful debts. Its stock includes goods that cost £20,000 but could now be sold for only £14,000.

**(a)** Explain why the receivables are shown after a provision. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the effect on profit and net assets of writing down the stock to its net realisable value. **[3]**

**8.** Wexley buys a new press for £36,000 with a residual value of £6,000 and a life of five years. It considers depreciating it by 25% reducing balance instead of straight line.

**(a)** Calculate the depreciation charge and net book value at the end of years 1, 2 and 3 using reducing balance at 25%. **[4]**

**(b)** Compare the effect on year 1 profit of the two methods. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate which method Wexley should use. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of the statement of financial position to a bank considering a loan to Wexley Printers Ltd. **[8]**

**10.** A company's directors say that the brand name and the skills of their staff are worth more than all the assets in the statement of financial position. Explain why these do not appear and evaluate the effect on the usefulness of the statement. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A statement of financial position shows what a business owns (assets), owes (liabilities) and is worth (equity) [1] at a particular date [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (£36,000 − £6,000) ÷ 5 [1] = **£6,000** a year [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Depreciation is the fall in value of a non-current asset over time because of use, age or obsolescence [1], and the provision for it spreads the cost of the asset over its useful life by charging part of it to the income statement each year [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Goodwill = price paid − fair value of net assets [1] = £150,000 − £118,000 = **£32,000** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** £420,000 − £120,000 = **£300,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Current assets = £40,000 + £55,000 + £5,000 = **£100,000** [1]. Current liabilities = £38,000 + £12,000 = **£50,000** [1]. Working capital = £100,000 − £50,000 = **£50,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Net assets = £300,000 + £50,000 − £100,000 [1] = **£250,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Equity = net assets = £250,000 [1]. Retained earnings = £250,000 − £120,000 = **£130,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** Liquidity: working capital is positive at £50,000 and the current ratio is 100,000 ÷ 50,000 = 2.0 [1], but only £5,000 is cash and there is an overdraft [1]. The acid test ratio (excluding inventory) is 60,000 ÷ 50,000 = 1.2, which is acceptable [1].
Funding: the loan is £100,000 and capital employed is £350,000, so gearing is 28.6% [1], which is moderate [1].
Equity: retained earnings are 52% of equity (130,000 ÷ 250,000), showing that the business has funded itself mainly from retained profits [1].
Judgement: the business appears financially sound, but the figures are a snapshot and the overdraft should be watched [1]. (Maximum 6 marks.) **[6]**

**7. (a)** The provision recognises that some customers are unlikely to pay [1]. Under prudence, assets must not be overstated [1], so receivables are valued at the amount expected to be collected (£58,000 gross less £3,000 = £55,000) [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Write-down = £20,000 − £14,000 = **£6,000** [1]. Profit falls by £6,000 [1] and net assets (inventory) fall by £6,000 [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Year 1: 25% × £36,000 = £9,000; NBV £27,000. Year 2: 25% × £27,000 = £6,750; NBV £20,250. Year 3: 25% × £20,250 = £5,062.50; NBV £15,187.50 [1] [1] [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Straight line charge = £6,000 a year [1]. Reducing balance charge in year 1 = £9,000 [1], so year 1 profit is £3,000 lower with reducing balance [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Reducing balance suits a press that loses value quickly or needs more repairs as it ages, because it charges more when the asset is newest [1]. It matches the cost of use over time and gives a more realistic asset value in the early years [1]. Straight line is simpler and gives a steady profit effect [1], which is easier to forecast and compare [1].
Whichever is chosen, the consistency convention means Wexley must use it every year, and should not switch just to improve profit [1].
Judgement: reducing balance if the press loses value fastest early on; straight line if it gives even service over five years [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Uses: it shows the assets that could be offered as security (non-current assets with an NBV of £300,000) [1]; it shows existing borrowing and gearing (28.6%), so the bank can judge how much more debt the company can carry [1]; it shows liquidity, for example a current ratio of 2.0 [1]; retained earnings show that past profits have been kept in the business [1].
Limits: it is a snapshot, so figures can be changed by timing [1]; assets are at cost less depreciation, not what they would sell for, so security could be worth more or less [1]; it shows nothing about future profit or cash, so the bank would also want a forecast [1].
Judgement: very useful for judging security and risk, but not enough alone [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Brand names and staff skills do not appear because they cannot be reliably measured or owned [1], and the accounting convention of objectivity requires values to be based on evidence, such as a purchase price [1]. A brand developed by the company itself has no purchase cost [1].
The effect is that the statement may understate the real value of the business, so net assets and equity look lower than the business is worth [1]. Users such as investors may need to supplement it with other information on market position and staff [1]. Judgement: the statement remains useful for assets and debts, but is not a measure of a company's full value [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out working capital when finding total assets less current liabilities.
- Applying the reducing balance rate to cost every year.
- Saying depreciation reduces cash.
- Forgetting that net assets equal equity.
- Describing the statement without applying it to the case.
