This guide covers topic 3.4 **Policy conflicts** in Implementing policy, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to evaluate "conflicts and trade-offs between policy objectives". It is a purely evaluative topic: there are no new definitions, but you must use what you know about AD, AS and the three types of policy to show when objectives clash, why, and how governments can choose between them. All countries and data are invented.

Use it with the [policy conflicts revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-policy-conflicts-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/) and the [policy conflicts practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-policy-conflicts-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). The policies involved are in [fiscal policy](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-fiscal-policy-ocr-aslevel-economics/), [monetary policy](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-monetary-policy-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and [supply side policy](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-supply-side-policy-ocr-aslevel-economics/). The A Level extension is in [policy conflicts and the evolution of macroeconomic policy](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-policy-conflicts-and-the-evolution-of-macroeconomic/).

## 1. The objectives, and why they can clash

Component 02 sets out these macroeconomic objectives in Economic policy objectives, topics 2.1 to 2.5:

- **Economic growth**, ideally steady and sustainable.
- **Full employment**, or low unemployment.
- **Low and stable inflation**, in the UK a 2% CPI target.
- **A sustainable balance of payments position**, usually judged by the current account.
- **Sustainable development**, meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

A **conflict** exists when a policy that moves the economy towards one objective moves it away from another. A **trade-off** is the choice that results: how much of one objective to give up to gain more of another.

Conflicts arise because most policies work by shifting AD, and a shift in AD moves output, jobs, prices and imports together.

## 2. Growth and employment versus inflation

When AD rises, firms produce more and hire more, so growth rises and unemployment falls. But as the economy approaches full capacity, bottlenecks appear and firms raise prices. On the rising section of the AS curve, more output and jobs come with more inflation.

The conflict is **weak** when there is much spare capacity: in a deep recession, higher AD raises output with little inflation. It is **strong** near full capacity, where most of any rise in AD becomes inflation.

The reverse applies to anti-inflation policy. Higher interest rates reduce AD, bringing inflation down, but at the cost of slower growth and rising unemployment.

## 3. Growth versus the current account

Rising incomes increase spending on imports. If exports do not grow as fast, the current account deficit widens. Faster growth than trading partners therefore tends to worsen the current account, especially in an economy where consumers buy many imported goods. Inflation adds to the problem: if domestic prices rise faster than foreign prices, exports become less competitive.

Policies that improve the current account by reducing AD will, in turn, reduce growth and raise unemployment.

## 4. Inflation versus the current account, through the exchange rate

Raising interest rates to reduce inflation tends to cause the currency to appreciate. An appreciation lowers import prices, which helps reduce inflation, but makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account tends to worsen. Exporters and firms competing with imports lose out.

## 5. Growth versus sustainable development

Faster growth usually means more use of energy and raw materials, more pollution and more waste. Measured GDP rises, but environmental damage can reduce the ability of future generations to produce and enjoy a good quality of life. The conflict is weaker if growth comes from services, cleaner technology or more efficient use of resources.

## 6. Reducing a budget deficit versus growth and jobs

Cutting government spending or raising taxes (fiscal consolidation) reduces AD in the short run, which slows growth and can raise unemployment. Lower growth then reduces tax revenue and raises benefit spending, so the deficit falls by less than planned. The case for consolidation is about longer-term objectives, such as lower debt interest and confidence in the public finances.

## 7. Supply shocks: the hardest case

A rise in world energy prices shifts SRAS left. Inflation rises **and** output falls at the same time.

- Raising AD to protect output and jobs pushes inflation higher still.
- Reducing AD to bring inflation down deepens the fall in output and raises unemployment.

No demand-side policy can improve both objectives at once. That is why supply shocks create the sharpest trade-offs.

**Worked example 1: reading a conflict from data.**

| Year | Real GDP growth (%) | Unemployment (%) | CPI inflation (%) | Current account (% of GDP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.8 | 7.2 | 1.4 | -1.5 |
| 2 | 2.6 | 6.1 | 2.1 | -2.4 |
| 3 | 3.4 | 4.8 | 3.6 | -3.7 |

Country V cut interest rates in year 1.

- Growth rose from 0.8% to 3.4% and unemployment fell by 2.4 percentage points: two objectives improved together.
- Inflation rose from 1.4% to 3.6%, above a 2% target by year 3.
- The current account deficit widened from 1.5% to 3.7% of GDP, as rising incomes pulled in imports.

**Evaluation.** In year 2 there was little conflict: inflation was near target and spare capacity remained. By year 3 the conflict was sharp. A judgement on the policy depends on how much weight is put on each objective and on whether the deficit is financed sustainably.

## 8. When objectives do not conflict

Do not assume every objective always clashes with every other.

- **In a recession,** raising AD can increase growth and reduce unemployment while inflation stays low.
- **Supply side improvements** shift LRAS right, raising growth, lowering inflationary pressure and, through competitiveness, helping the current account.
- **Low, stable inflation** can support long-run growth by reducing uncertainty for investors.

## 9. Managing conflicts

| Approach | How it helps | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Prioritise** one objective, for example an inflation target | Gives a clear rule and anchors expectations | Other objectives may suffer |
| **Combine policies** (the policy mix) | One tool for each objective, such as tighter monetary policy with supply side measures | Policies can work against each other if not co-ordinated |
| **Use supply side policy** | Raises capacity, easing the growth-inflation trade-off | Long time lags |
| **Take a longer view** | Accept short-run costs for long-run gains | Short-run costs can be politically hard |
| **Target gradually** | Bring inflation down slowly to limit job losses | Inflation stays above target for longer |

**Worked example 2: comparing options.** Country W faces 5% inflation from a cost shock and rising unemployment. Its options:

| Policy | Inflation | Unemployment | Growth | Current account |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raise interest rates | Improves | Worsens | Worsens | Worsens (appreciation) |
| Raise government spending | Worsens | Improves | Improves | Worsens |
| Energy efficiency subsidies and training | Improves slowly | Improves slowly | Improves in the long run | Improves slowly |
| Do nothing and wait for the shock to fade | Improves if temporary | Worsens in the short run | Worsens in the short run | Uncertain |

The best answer usually combines a supply side measure with a cautious demand-side stance, and states which objective is given priority and why.

## Common mistakes

- Listing conflicts without explaining the mechanism through AD or AS.
- Saying growth always causes inflation, ignoring spare capacity.
- Forgetting the exchange rate link between interest rates and the current account.
- Treating all objectives as equally important in every situation.
- Failing to reach a judgement about which objective should take priority.

## Exam technique

- For an **evaluate** question, follow four steps: name the conflict; explain it with a chain through AD or AS; state the conditions under which it is strong or weak; and judge which objective should take priority in the context given.
- Use data in the question to show the conflict: quote changes in percentage points and compare with targets.
- An AD/AS diagram with LRAS helps to show why spare capacity determines the strength of the growth-inflation trade-off.
