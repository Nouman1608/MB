This study guide teaches two subtopics of Topic 1 Number in the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027: **1.9 Estimation** and **1.14 Using a calculator**. The Core statements (C1.9, C1.14) and the Extended statements (E1.9, E1.14) are word-for-word the same, so everything on this page applies to both tiers. There is no Extended-only content in these two subtopics.

The tier affects only which papers you sit. Core candidates take Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates take Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). The syllabus says Papers 1 and 2 test everything **except** Using a calculator (C1.14 and E1.14), so 1.14 is tested only on Papers 3 and 4. Estimation is tested on all four papers.

## 1.9.1 Rounding to a specified accuracy

Every rounding question follows the same three steps.

1. Find the last digit you are allowed to keep.
2. Look at the **next** digit only.
3. If that digit is 5 or more, round the kept digit up. If it is 4 or less, leave it. Then fill any gap before the decimal point with zeros.

**Nearest 10, 100 or 1000.** The kept digit is in the tens, hundreds or thousands column.

**Decimal places (d.p.).** Count digits after the decimal point.

**Significant figures (s.f.).** Count from the **first non-zero digit**. Zeros at the start of a decimal (0.0…) are never significant. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (in 4.07 the 0 counts).

### Worked example 1

Round 46 285 to (a) the nearest 100, (b) the nearest 1000, (c) 2 significant figures, (d) 4 significant figures.

```
(a) keep the hundreds digit 2; next digit 8 -> round up      46 300
(b) keep the thousands digit 6; next digit 2 -> leave        46 000
(c) first two s.f. are 4 and 6; next digit 2 -> leave        46 000
(d) first four s.f. are 4, 6, 2, 8; next digit 5 -> round up 46 290
```

The zeros in 46 000 are placeholders. Writing "46" for (b) changes the size of the number and scores nothing.

### Worked example 2

Round 0.070 49 to (a) 1 s.f., (b) 2 s.f., (c) 3 s.f.

```
first significant figure is the 7 (the 0.0 in front does not count)
(a) 7, next digit 0 -> leave          0.07
(b) 7, 0, next digit 4 -> leave       0.070
(c) 7, 0, 4, next digit 9 -> round up 0.0705
```

In (b) the final zero **must** be written. 0.070 shows two significant figures; 0.07 shows only one.

### Worked example 3

Round 12.9963 to (a) 2 d.p., (b) 3 s.f.

```
(a) keep 12.99; next digit 6 -> round the 9 up
    12.99 + 0.01 = 13.00             answer 13.00
(b) keep 12.9; next digit 9 -> round up
    12.9 + 0.1 = 13.0                answer 13.0
```

When a 9 rounds up it carries into the column to its left. Keep the trailing zeros so the answer still shows the accuracy asked for.

## 1.9.2 Making estimates

An estimate is a quick, rough answer you can find in your head. In an exam the instruction is usually "by writing each number correct to 1 significant figure, estimate the value of …". Follow it exactly:

1. Round **every** number to 1 s.f. and write the rounded calculation down.
2. Work it out with the rounded numbers.
3. Give the estimate. Do not then use a calculator or the original numbers.

Writing the rounded calculation is the method mark. On a non-calculator paper, a bare answer with no rounded values shown may score nothing even if it is right.

### Worked example 4 (non-calculator)

Estimate the value of (62.4 × 0.187) ÷ 3.87.

```
62.4 -> 60      0.187 -> 0.2      3.87 -> 4
(60 x 0.2) / 4 = 12 / 4 = 3
```

The estimate is **3**. (The calculator value is 3.015…, so the estimate is close.)

### Worked example 5 (non-calculator)

Estimate the value of (812 + 197) ÷ 0.48.

```
812 -> 800      197 -> 200      0.48 -> 0.5
(800 + 200) / 0.5 = 1000 / 0.5 = 2000
```

Dividing by 0.5 is the same as multiplying by 2. Dividing a positive number by a number between 0 and 1 makes the answer **bigger**, which is a quick check on your working.

### Worked example 6 (non-calculator)

Estimate √(0.0385 × 412).

```
0.0385 -> 0.04      412 -> 400
0.04 x 400 = 16     sqrt(16) = 4
```

The estimate is **4**.

### Estimating with measurements

The syllabus includes quantities and measurements, not just bare numbers. A rectangular room measures 4.85 m by 6.12 m. Rounding each length to 1 s.f. gives 5 m × 6 m, so the floor area is **about 30 m²**. The exact area is 29.682 m², so the estimate is a good check.

Estimates are also how you check a calculator answer on Papers 3 and 4. If your estimate is 30 and the display shows 296.82, you have mis-keyed a decimal point.

## 1.9.3 Rounding answers to a reasonable accuracy

The final answer must make sense for the question.

- **Calculator papers:** give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures, or angles in degrees to 1 decimal place, unless the question says otherwise. This is the rule written in the syllabus for Papers 3 and 4. Exact answers (such as 2.5 or 120) are given exactly.
- **Money:** to the nearest cent (2 d.p.), so a display of 7.3 dollars is written $7.30.
- **Whole objects:** decide whether to round up or down from the context, not from the digit after the point.

### Worked example 7

(a) 157 students go on a trip. Each minibus holds 12 students. How many minibuses are needed?

(b) Cinema tickets cost $3.85 each. How many tickets can be bought with 50 dollars?

```
(a) 157 / 12 = 13.08...   13 buses carry only 156, so round UP    14 minibuses
(b) 50 / 3.85 = 12.98...  13 tickets would cost $50.05, so DOWN  12 tickets
```

Normal rounding gives 13 in both cases, and 13 is wrong in both. "How many are needed" rounds up; "how many can you afford or fit" rounds down.

## 1.14.1 Using a calculator efficiently

The syllabus example is to know not to round values within a calculation and only round the final answer. The Paper 3 and 4 instructions say the same: avoid rounding until the final answer, and when a later part uses an earlier answer, use the value **before** it was rounded.

Efficient habits:

- Use the **Ans** key (or memory) to carry full values from one step to the next.
- Use **brackets** round any numerator or denominator with more than one term.
- Use the **π key** (or 3.142, which the syllabus allows), not 3.14.
- Use the **×10ˣ** (or EXP) key for standard form, rather than typing "× 10 ^".

### Worked example 8 (calculator)

A circle has circumference 30 cm. Find (a) its radius, (b) its area.

```
(a) r = 30 / (2 x pi) = 4.774 648...      radius = 4.77 cm (3 s.f.)
(b) correct: A = pi x Ans^2 = 71.619...    area = 71.6 cm²
    wrong:   A = pi x 4.77^2 = 71.480...   area = 71.5 cm²
```

Using the rounded radius in (b) gives 71.5, which loses the accuracy mark. Keep 4.774 648… in the calculator.

## 1.14.2 Entering values appropriately

Time is the most common trap because there are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100.

- Minutes to hours: **divide by 60**. 1 hour 50 minutes = 1 + 50/60 = 1.8333… hours.
- Or use the **degrees–minutes–seconds key** (marked ° ′ ″ on most scientific calculators): enter 1° 50′ 0″.

### Worked example 9 (calculator)

A car travels 148 km in 1 hour 50 minutes. Find its average speed.

```
correct:  148 / (1 + 50/60) = 80.727...           80.7 km/h
wrong 1:  148 / 1.50       = 98.666...           98.7 km/h  (typed 1 h 50 min as 1.50)
wrong 2:  148 / 1.83       = 80.874...           80.9 km/h  (rounded the time first)
```

Wrong 2 shows 1.14.1 and 1.14.2 working together: even the correct conversion, rounded too early, moves the 3 s.f. answer.

Other entries to get right:

- **Units:** convert to matching units before entering, e.g. 45 cm as 0.45 m if the other lengths are in metres.
- **Negative numbers:** use brackets when squaring a negative, as the Number revision notes show.
- **Fractions:** use the fraction key or brackets, so 3/(4 × 5) is not entered as 3 ÷ 4 × 5.

## 1.14.3 Interpreting the display

The calculator gives a number. You must decide what it means.

| Display | Context | Write |
|---|---|---|
| 15.3 | money in dollars | $15.30 |
| 0.833 333 3 | cost of one item in dollars | $0.83 |
| 3.7 | time in hours | 3 hours 42 minutes (0.7 × 60 = 42) |
| 0.45 | time in hours | 27 minutes (0.45 × 60 = 27) |
| 4.7 × 10⁻⁰⁴ or 4.7E-04 | any | 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.000 47 |
| 13.083 333 | number of minibuses needed | 14 |

The standard-form display is not an answer in itself. Write it properly as 4.7 × 10⁻⁴, or as an ordinary number if the question asks for one. Never copy "E-04" onto the answer line.

When a question says "write down all the figures on your calculator display", copy every digit shown, then round in the next part.

## Common errors

- Writing 0.07 when 2 s.f. is asked for (0.070), or dropping the zeros in 13.00.
- Counting the zeros in 0.0705 as significant figures.
- Estimating with the original numbers, or not showing the 1 s.f. values.
- Rounding 13.08 minibuses down to 13, or 12.98 tickets up to 13.
- Entering 1 hour 50 minutes as 1.50 or 1.5.
- Reading 3.7 hours as 3 hours 70 minutes.
- Rounding a radius or a time to 3 s.f. and then using it in the next step.
- Copying "4.7E-04" as the answer, or writing $15.3.
