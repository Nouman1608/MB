**These notes condense how to analyse brief and visual sources in Paper 2 Section A of OxfordAQA
International GCSE English Language (9270).** The specification says "some of the texts may be brief
or solely visual". For full explanation and a worked answer, use the
[brief and visual texts study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-brief-and-visual-texts/);
for the section as a whole, see the
[Paper 2 Section A reading revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-reading-revision-notes/).

## The key objective

**R4**: "explain and evaluate how writers use linguistic, grammatical, structural and presentational
features to achieve effects and engage and influence the reader". For visual texts, presentational
features carry most of the meaning.

## Evidence without quotation

Describe precisely: **position, size, colour, gaze, expression**.

- Weak: "a sad person".
- Strong: "an elderly man alone at the far left of the frame, looking down at his hands".

## The five-layer method

| Layer | Ask | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Salience | What do I see first? | The main message |
| 2. Composition | Where are things placed? Foreground, background, empty space? | Isolation, pressure, community |
| 3. Gaze and angle | Does the figure look at me? Is the camera high or low? | Direct appeal; power or vulnerability |
| 4. Colour, light, typography | What stands out? What associations? | Emphasis, mood, tone |
| 5. Image and words | Does the caption fix or change the image? | Anchorage of one reading |

## Charts and infographics

- **Selection**: what is measured, what is missing?
- **Comparison**: what is placed side by side?
- **Emphasis**: which figure is largest or coloured?
- **Labelling**: neutral or loaded headings?
- **Icons**: do pictures make data emotional?

Describe accurately; never add figures the chart does not give.

## Brief texts: compression toolkit

| Feature | What it does |
|---|---|
| Imperative | Commands the reader to act |
| Ellipsis (missing words) | Makes a connection seem obvious |
| Wordplay or pun | Packs two meanings into one phrase |
| Inclusive pronoun ("our", "we") | Builds community |
| Contrast | Before and after, problem and solution |
| Rhythm and repetition | Makes the phrase memorable |
| Hashtag or tag | Signals a campaign; invites sharing |

## Brief fiction

A short story, poem or fictional message can also be brief. Ask: what situation is implied, and what
must the reader infer?

## Paragraph frame for a visual source

```
FEATURE    precise description (position, size, colour, gaze)
EFFECT     what it makes the reader see, feel or think
PURPOSE    how it serves the designer's aim (warn, persuade, inform)
LINK       how words or a second feature reinforce or change it
```

Mini-example (original poster of a lone tree in a car park): "The single tree is placed in the centre
of a wide, grey car park, so it looks isolated and fragile; the caption 'The last one?' turns that
isolation into a warning about losing green space."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Salience | How strongly an element stands out |
| Composition | The arrangement of elements in an image |
| Foreground / background | Front and rear of the image space |
| Gaze | Where a figure in an image is looking |
| Camera angle | The viewpoint from which an image is taken |
| Anchorage | When words fix the meaning of an image |
| Typography | The style, size and arrangement of type |
| Connotation | An association a colour, word or image carries |
| Multimodal | Combining words and images |
| Compression | Saying much in few words |

## Linking to other sources

- Compare **methods**: an image works instantly; an article builds through argument.
- Compare **perspectives**: does the poster agree with the article's view?
- Note ideas you could rework in **Section B**.

## Exam traps

- Describing without analysing effect.
- Vague description of the image.
- Ignoring the words on a visual text, or the image when there are words.
- Treating a chart as neutral.
- Inventing details.
- Writing too little because the text is short.

## Self-test

1. What does the specification say about brief and visual texts in Paper 2?
2. Which reading objective applies most directly, and which word in it matters for visuals?
3. How do you give evidence from an image?
4. Name the five layers of the method.
5. What is salience?
6. What does a figure looking directly at the viewer suggest?
7. What is anchorage? Give an example.
8. Give three questions to ask of a chart.
9. What is ellipsis, and what effect can it have in a slogan?
10. Can a brief text be fiction?
11. How might you compare a poster with an article in a pair question?
12. Why should you not write less about a brief text?

## Answers

1. "Some of the texts may be brief or solely visual"; texts may be fiction or non-fiction.
2. R4; "presentational" (features).
3. By describing the relevant feature precisely: position, size, colour, gaze, expression.
4. Salience; composition; gaze and angle; colour, light and typography; image and words together.
5. How strongly an element stands out and draws attention.
6. A direct appeal to the viewer, creating personal address.
7. Words fixing one meaning of an image, such as a caption turning a beach photograph into a warning
   about overcrowding.
8. Any three: what is measured; what is missing; what is compared; which figure is emphasised; how it
   is labelled; whether icons add emotion.
9. Leaving out words; it can make a link seem obvious, as in "Fewer cars. Cleaner air."
10. Yes; the specification allows fiction, and a short story, poem or fictional message can be brief.
11. Compare methods (instant visual impact against built-up argument) and perspectives on the issue.
12. Because each word or feature is chosen under pressure of space and can support detailed analysis.
