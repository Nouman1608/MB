Section 3.2.4 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, is marked as chemistry-only content. It states that nanoscience refers to structures 1–100 nm in size, "of the order of a few hundred atoms"; that nanoparticles show properties different from those of the same materials in bulk and have a high surface area to volume ratio; and that this may lead to new computers, new catalysts, new coatings, highly selective sensors, stronger and lighter construction materials, and new cosmetics such as suntan creams and deodorants. You should know what nanoscience and nanoparticles are and consider some applications, but you "do not need to know specific examples or properties". Instead, "questions may be set on information that is provided about these materials and relating their use to their structure".

The existing [structure and bonding of carbon and nanoparticles guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-structure-and-bonding-of-carbon-and-nanoparticles/) introduces this section. This guide builds the three skills the questions actually test: working with sizes on the nanometre scale, reasoning with surface area to volume ratio, and using unfamiliar information to explain a use.

## Key definitions

- **Nanoscience** is the study of structures 1–100 nm in size.
- A **nanoparticle** is a particle in that size range; it contains of the order of a few hundred atoms.
- **Bulk material** is the same substance in ordinary-sized pieces or powders.
- **1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m**, one thousand-millionth of a metre. Also 1 µm (micrometre) = 1000 nm.

## Skill 1: deciding whether something is nanoscale

Convert the size to nanometres, then check whether it lies between 1 and 100 nm.

| Size given | Conversion | In nm | Nanoparticle? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ m | ÷ 1 × 10⁻⁹ | 25 | yes |
| 0.08 µm | × 1000 | 80 | yes |
| 4 × 10⁻⁷ m | ÷ 1 × 10⁻⁹ | 400 | no |
| 0.5 nm | — | 0.5 | no: below 1 nm, about the size of a single small molecule |

When you divide by 1 × 10⁻⁹, the power of ten increases by 9: 10⁻⁸ becomes 10¹, so 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ m = 2.5 × 10¹ nm = 25 nm.

## Skill 2: surface area to volume ratio

The specification links the special behaviour of nanoparticles to their **high surface area to volume ratio**. A larger fraction of the atoms in a nanoparticle are at its surface, where they can contact and react with other substances.

For a cube of side L:

```
surface area = 6 × L²
volume       = L³
SA : V       = 6L² ÷ L³ = 6 ÷ L
```

So the ratio depends only on 1 ÷ L: **if the side is divided by 10, the surface area to volume ratio is multiplied by 10.**

### Worked example 1

*Compare the surface area to volume ratio of a cube-shaped nanoparticle of side 10 nm with one of side 100 nm.*

| Side | Surface area | Volume | SA : V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 nm | 6 × 10² = 600 nm² | 10³ = 1000 nm³ | 600 ÷ 1000 = 0.6 per nm |
| 100 nm | 6 × 100² = 60 000 nm² | 100³ = 1 000 000 nm³ | 60 000 ÷ 1 000 000 = 0.06 per nm |

The smaller particle has a side 10 times shorter and a surface area to volume ratio **10 times larger**.

### Worked example 2: same mass, more surface

*A 1 cm cube of a metal oxide is cut into cubes of side 1 mm. How does the total surface area change?*

The 1 cm cube has 6 faces of 1 cm² each: 6 cm². Cutting it into 1 mm cubes gives 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 cubes, each with surface area 6 × (0.1 cm)² = 0.06 cm². Total = 1000 × 0.06 = **60 cm²**, ten times the original, for the same mass. Each further division of the side by 10 multiplies the total surface by another 10, which is why the same mass of a substance as nanoparticles has an enormous surface area.

## Why properties change

Because so much of a nanoparticle is surface, it can behave differently from the same material in bulk:

- **Catalysts**: a reaction on a catalyst happens at its surface. Far more surface per gram means more sites where reacting particles can make contact, so less catalyst is needed.
- **Sensors**: a sensor responds when particles of a substance contact its surface. A large surface makes it more sensitive, and the surface can be designed to respond to one substance only: "highly selective".
- **Coatings and cosmetics**: very small particles can form thin, even layers; the properties of the layer depend on the nanoparticles' structure.
- **Construction materials and computers**: structures built on the nanometre scale can be strong for their mass or can be made extremely small.

You will not be asked to recall examples of nanoparticles or their properties. Questions will give you the information and ask you to explain a use from it.

## Skill 3: answering a question from given information

### Worked example 3

*Information (invented for this example): "Nanoparticles of substance M are 20 nm across. When added to a coating for windows, a layer of M breaks down dirt when light shines on it. The same mass of M as a powder with particles 20 µm across is much less effective."*

*(a) Show that the nanoparticles are within the nanoscale range. (b) Explain why the nanoparticles are more effective than the powder.*

**(a)** 20 nm lies between 1 and 100 nm. [1]

**(b)** The powder particles are 20 µm = 20 000 nm across, 1000 times larger [1]. The nanoparticles therefore have a surface area to volume ratio 1000 times larger [1], so for the same mass much more of M is at the surface, where it can contact the dirt [1].

*Why it scores:* each step uses the information given. The answer quotes the sizes, makes a comparison and links surface area to the job the material does.

### What "relating use to structure" looks like

| Information you might be given | Link to make |
|---|---|
| a nanoparticle catalyst needs less material than the bulk form | larger surface area to volume ratio; more surface for reaction |
| a sensor detects very small amounts of a gas | large surface; many contact points |
| a coating is thin and transparent | particles much smaller than in an ordinary powder |
| a new material is stronger but lighter | nanoscale structure gives strength without extra mass |
| a possible risk to health is being studied | particles small enough to behave differently in the body; information may be incomplete |

## Common mistakes

- **Getting the size range wrong.** It is 1–100 nm, not 1–100 µm.
- **Converting the wrong way.** Metres to nanometres means multiplying by 10⁹ (or dividing by 10⁻⁹).
- **Saying nanoparticles have "a large surface area".** A single nanoparticle has a tiny surface area. The key idea is a large surface area **to volume** ratio.
- **Stating that nanoparticles are a different substance.** They are the same material; the size changes the properties.
- **Recalling examples instead of using the information given.**

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the conversion or ratio with units, as in the tables above.
- **Explain** a use: name the property in the information, link it to the surface area to volume ratio or to particle size, then to the use.
- **Evaluate**: if information gives both a benefit and a concern, discuss both and state which the evidence supports.
