> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. Answers use Italy, England and Germany as model cases; adapt them to your own choices.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 2 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-renaissance-reformation-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-renaissance-reformation/).

## Questions

**1.** Define humanism in the context of the Renaissance. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two reasons why wealth mattered to the emergence of the Renaissance in Italian city-states. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "Now any man may buy a book for the price of a meal, and read in his own tongue what the priests once kept in Latin. The preachers say it is the Devil's art; I say it is God's last gift." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a sixteenth-century townsman.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the effect of printing on religious debate. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of using the extract as evidence for how widely printing changed belief. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why Martin Luther was able to survive after the Diet of Worms in 1521. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the impact of the Renaissance on one country outside Italy. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the role of the Jesuits in the Catholic response to the Reformation. **[6]**

**7.** "The Reformation in Germany brought more political than religious change." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the positions of Erasmus and Luther on reform of the Church. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss why historians disagree about whether the Thirty Years' War was a religious war. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2: a movement that studied classical Latin and Greek writing (1); and focused on human achievement, education and critical reading of texts (1). **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. Trade and banking created rich families and guilds who could pay for art, buildings and scholars, as with the Medici (2). Rival city-states used spending on culture to show prestige and power, so wealth turned into patronage (2). Accept: the Church's wealth in Rome financed major commissions. **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: printing made books cheaper (1); people could read religious texts in their own language rather than Latin (1); it shows disagreement, with preachers condemning print while the author welcomes it, so print became part of the debate (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: the extract is one person's voice and cannot show general change (1); it is constructed in the voice of a townsman, and townspeople were more likely to be literate than peasants (1); many people could not read, so preaching and word of mouth also carried ideas, which the extract ignores (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4: Frederick the Wise of Saxony protected him and had him hidden at the Wartburg (1); German princes had political and financial reasons to support reform (1); Charles V was occupied with wars against France and the Ottomans and relied on the princes' support, so could not enforce the Edict of Worms (1); Luther's message was already spreading in print, so it could not be silenced by removing one man (1). **[4]**

**5.** Markband approach, up to 6. For England: literature, such as Wyatt, Marlowe and Shakespeare drawing on Italian and classical models (1 for a developed example); political ideas, such as More's *Utopia* (1); science, such as Bacon on experiment and Harvey on circulation (1); the arts, such as Holbein and later classical architecture (1); analysis of extent: most change reached elites and towns, while rural people were less affected (1); a supported judgement on overall impact (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: founded by Ignatius of Loyola and approved by Paul III in 1540 (1); ran schools that trained Catholic elites (1); sent missionaries to Europe and beyond (1); advised rulers and acted as confessors, so Catholic rulers supported them (1); disciplined obedience to the pope strengthened Catholic loyalty (1); examination of limits, for example that they worked alongside Trent, the Inquisition and rulers' power, and did not act alone (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, for 15 in all. Understanding of the claim, with religious change in Lutheran states (German services, clergy marriage) and political change (princes' control of churches, the Peasants' War, Augsburg 1555) (3). Evaluation of the religious theme: new beliefs and church life in Lutheran lands, and later Calvinist recognition (3). Evaluation of the political theme: princes took church lands and authority, and the Empire was divided by faith (3). Perspectives: a historian's view, such as the confessionalization idea that religion and state-building went together, or a view that political motives drove princes (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for example that the two cannot be separated, since religious change gave princes their political gains, so the claim is partly right but too simple (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4: similarity, both criticised abuses and wanted scripture central (1); Erasmus wanted reform from inside and stressed free will, while Luther broke with Rome (1); Luther stressed faith alone and the will bound by sin, shown in their debate in the 1520s (1); a judgement on why they differed, for example Erasmus's humanist caution against Luther's radical break (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: the war began in a religious dispute in Bohemia (1); religious identity shaped alliances, such as the Protestant Union and Catholic League (1); the opposing view stresses dynastic and territorial rivalry, shown when Catholic France supported Protestant powers against the Habsburgs (2); explains why historians differ: evidence emphasised, definitions of "religious", and the long timespan (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
