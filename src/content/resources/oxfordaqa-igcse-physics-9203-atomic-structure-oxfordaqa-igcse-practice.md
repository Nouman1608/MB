These questions cover section 3.7.1 Atomic structure, statements a to f, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Take the radius of an atom as about 10⁻¹⁰ m and of a nucleus as about 10⁻¹⁵ m where needed. For help, see the [atomic structure study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-oxfordaqa-igcse/) and the [atomic structure revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-oxfordaqa-igcse-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State which two particles are found in the nucleus. **[1]**

**2.** Which particle has a relative charge of −1 and a very small relative mass?

A: proton

B: neutron

C: electron

D: alpha particle

**[1]**

**3.** State what is meant by the mass number of an atom. **[1]**

**4.** An atom of fluorine is written ¹⁹₉F. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom. **[3]**

**5.** An atom of calcium, ⁴⁰₂₀Ca, loses two electrons. State the charge of the ion formed and the number of electrons it has. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why an atom has no overall charge. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** In an experiment, alpha particles are fired at a thin sheet of gold foil.

**(a)** Describe the three main observations. **[3]**

**(b)** For each observation, state what it shows about the atom. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the observation of alpha particles bouncing back shows where the mass of the atom is concentrated. **[2]**

**8.** Before the alpha scattering experiment, the plum pudding model described the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons spread through it.

**(a)** State what the plum pudding model predicted would happen to alpha particles fired at thin foil. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the results led scientists to replace this model. **[2]**

**9.** Two atoms are written ³⁵₁₇X and ³⁷₁₇Y.

**(a)** Explain why X and Y are the same element. **[1]**

**(b)** State the term used to describe atoms like X and Y. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the number of neutrons in each atom. **[2]**

**(d)** State, with a reason, whether X and Y have the same number of electrons when neutral. **[2]**

**10.** The radius of a nucleus is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and the radius of the atom is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

**(a)** Calculate how many times larger the radius of the atom is than the radius of the nucleus. **[1]**

**(b)** A model nucleus is a bead of radius 3 mm. Calculate the radius of the model atom in metres. **[2]**

**(c)** Use your answer to explain why the atom is described as mostly empty space. **[1]**

**11.** Complete the table for the following particles. **[6]**

| Particle | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¹⁴₇N atom | | | |
| ³²₁₆S²⁻ ion | | | |
| ²³₁₁Na⁺ ion | | | |

**12.** A student writes: "When an atom becomes an ion, the number of protons changes, so it becomes a different element."

**(a)** Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how an atom of oxygen, ¹⁶₈O, can become an ion with a charge of −2. **[2]**

**(c)** State whether an isotope or an ion is formed when an atom gains a neutron. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Protons and neutrons [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Protons 9 [1]; neutrons 19 − 9 = 10 [1]; electrons 9 [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Charge **+2** [1]; electrons 20 − 2 = **18** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** It contains equal numbers of protons and electrons [1]. Their charges are equal and opposite (+1 and −1), so they cancel [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Most alpha particles passed straight through [1]. A few were deflected through small angles [1]. A very small number bounced back [1].

**(b)** Most of the atom is empty space [1]. The positive charge is concentrated in a small region that repels alpha particles passing close to it [1]. The positive charge and almost all the mass are in a tiny central nucleus [1].

**(c)** A fast alpha particle can only be turned back by hitting something much more massive than itself [1], so the mass must be concentrated in a tiny nucleus rather than spread through the atom [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** All the alpha particles would pass through with only very small deflections [1].

**(b)** Some alpha particles were deflected through large angles or bounced back [1], which the evenly spread charge and mass of the plum pudding model could not explain; a small, massive, positive nucleus was needed [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** They have the same number of protons (atomic number 17) [1].

**(b)** Isotopes [1].

**(c)** X: 35 − 17 = **18** [1]; Y: 37 − 17 = **20** [1].

**(d)** Yes [1]; a neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, and both have 17 protons [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ = **1 × 10⁵** (100 000) [1].

**(b)** 3 mm × 100 000 = 300 000 mm [1] = **300 m** [1].

**(c)** The nucleus, which holds nearly all the mass, takes up only a tiny fraction of the space of the atom, so most of the atom contains no matter apart from the electrons [1]. **[4]**

**11.** ¹⁴₇N: 7, 7, 7 [2]. ³²₁₆S²⁻: 16, 16, 18 [2]. ²³₁₁Na⁺: 11, 12, 10 [2]. For each row, two marks if all three numbers are correct, one mark if two are correct. **[6]**

**12. (a)** Forming an ion changes the number of electrons, not protons [1]. The number of protons stays the same, so it is still the same element [1].

**(b)** The atom gains [1] two outer electrons, so it has 8 protons and 10 electrons [1].

**(c)** An isotope [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Ions differ in electrons; isotopes differ in neutrons. Many students swap these when under pressure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the nucleus, rather than the atom, is mostly empty space.
- Listing scattering observations without matching conclusions.
- Using Z instead of A − Z for neutrons.
- Getting the electron count for negative ions the wrong way round.
- Converting millimetres to metres incorrectly in scale questions.
- Confusing ions with isotopes.
