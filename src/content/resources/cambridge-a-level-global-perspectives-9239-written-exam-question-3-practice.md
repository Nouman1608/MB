These questions practise Question 3 of Component 1 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, which "requires a longer, essay response based on the two source documents" focused on "the arguments and perspectives given in the documents" and ending in "a reasoned judgement". For the overview of the paper, see the [Component 1 Written Exam study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-written-exam/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, with two original sources. The city, organisations and details are fictional. The real Question 3 is a single 25-mark essay; Questions 1-10 below build the component skills, and Question 11 is a full-length essay question. Use them alongside the specimen and past papers your teacher provides.

Revise first with the [Question 3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-3/) and the [Question 3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-3-revision-notes/).

## Sources

**Source A** (speech by the mayor of the fictional city of Corvale)

> From next year, private cars will be banned from Corvale's historic centre. In the six months of our trial on three streets, footfall in shops rose and nitrogen dioxide readings fell. Cities from northern Europe to East Asia have pedestrianised their centres, and none has gone back. A few traders complain, as they always do when anything changes, but they will thank us once their streets are full of shoppers. Clean air is a right, not a luxury. Buses will be free in the centre, so no one need be left behind.

**Source B** (statement from a fictional association of disabled residents and small traders)

> We support cleaner air, but a total ban on private cars in Corvale's centre is the wrong way to achieve it. Many disabled residents cannot use buses, however free they are, because the stops are far from their homes and the old streets are steep. The trial lasted six months on three streets, in spring and summer, when people are more likely to walk anyway. Several of our member shops reported lower takings on the trial streets, although others did well. A better plan would allow blue-badge holders and delivery vehicles, phase the ban in over three years, and publish the air-quality data so residents can judge for themselves.

## Section A

**1.** Identify the main conclusion of each source. **[2]**

**2.** Identify one unstated assumption in Source A and assess whether it is reasonable. **[3]**

**3.** Assess the analogy Source A uses about other cities. **[3]**

**4.** Compare how the two sources deal with opposing views. **[4]**

**5.** Compare the use of language in the two sources. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how the context of each writer might shape its perspective. **[4]**

**7.** Identify one consequence of Source B's recommendation that the source does not acknowledge. **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** Write one comparative paragraph on the criterion "logic and structure", ending with a mini-judgement. **[5]**

**9.** A student ends an essay: "Both sources make good points. Source A cares about the environment and Source B cares about disabled people. I think cars should be banned." Explain **two** weaknesses of this conclusion and rewrite it. **[4]**

**10.** Plan an answer to Question 11 by listing **three** comparison criteria, each with a one-sentence mini-judgement. **[3]**

**11.** "Source A is more convincing than Source B." To what extent do you agree? Make an overall judgement. **[25]**

## Answers

**1.** Source A: private cars will be (should be) banned from Corvale's historic centre [1]. Source B: a total ban is the wrong way to achieve cleaner air, and a phased plan with exemptions should be used instead [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any one assumption [1], with assessment [2]:
- Free buses meet everyone's travel needs [1]. Not reasonable [1]: Source B gives a specific reason why some disabled residents cannot use buses, and Source A offers nothing to show otherwise [1].
- Results from three streets over six months will hold for the whole centre all year [1]. Questionable [1]: a small, seasonal trial may not represent winter conditions or streets with different shops [1].
- Traders' complaints come from resistance to change rather than real losses [1]. Not reasonable [1]: Source B reports lower takings for some shops, which is a substantive reason [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The analogy compares Corvale with cities that have pedestrianised [1]. It has some force, because it suggests the policy can be sustained [1], but it is weak: no cities are named, "none has gone back" is a sweeping claim with no support, and the source does not show those cities share Corvale's steep historic streets or bus network [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Source A mentions traders' complaints but dismisses them as habitual ("as they always do") rather than answering them, which is close to attacking the arguer rather than the argument [1] [1]. Source B begins by accepting its opponents' goal of cleaner air and answers the trial evidence directly, noting that some shops did well [1]. So B engages with opposing views more fairly, which strengthens its credibility [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Source A uses confident, emotive phrases such as "Clean air is a right, not a luxury" and "full of shoppers" [1]. Source B uses measured, qualified language, such as "Several of our member shops" and "although others did well" [1]. A's language asserts where B's qualifies, so B's tone is more consistent with its evidence, though A's slogan does express a genuine value [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The mayor has committed to the policy and needs public support, so the speech presents the trial positively and frames the ban as a right, with little room for doubt [1] [1]. The association represents disabled residents and small traders, so its perspective puts access and livelihoods first, which explains its focus on exemptions and phasing rather than on maximising air-quality gains [1] [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Any one: allowing blue-badge holders and delivery vehicles would keep some traffic and pollution in the centre [1], so air quality would improve less than under a total ban [1]. Or: phasing over three years delays the health benefits [1], while residents continue breathing polluted air [1]. Or: exemptions need enforcement, which adds cost and complexity [1] [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Indicative paragraph; credit equivalent points:
- Signposts the criterion [1].
- Source A: moves from a small trial to a citywide ban, a hasty generalisation [1].
- Source A: "no one need be left behind" rests on the unsupported assumption that free buses serve everyone [1].
- Source B: argues from specific limitations of the trial and of buses to a conditional recommendation, so the conclusion follows more closely from the reasons [1].
- Mini-judgement: on logic and structure, Source B is stronger because its recommendation matches the limits of the evidence [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Weakness 1: it gives no judgement on which source is more convincing, so it does not answer the question [1]. Weakness 2: the final sentence is a personal view on the issue that is not reasoned from the comparison [1]. Rewrite, for example: "Source B is more convincing, because its recommendation follows from the limits of the trial evidence and it answers Source A's argument directly, while Source A relies on an unsupported assumption about buses [1]. Source B is weaker in offering no air-quality data of its own, so the verdict would change if a longer trial showed no losses for traders [1]." **[4]**

**10.** Any three criteria with sensible mini-judgements, one mark each, for example: logic (B stronger: conclusion matches evidence) [1]; counter-arguments (B stronger: engages rather than dismisses) [1]; implications (A clearer on air quality but ignores access; B ignores the cost of exemptions, so roughly balanced) [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Real responses are marked holistically by level. Indicative content:

- **Introduction** stating a clear verdict, for example that the claim is mostly wrong, and the main reason [1] [1].
- **Arguments and conclusions** identified accurately for both sources [1] [1].
- **Logic:** A's leap from a three-street, six-month trial to a full ban [1]; B's conditional recommendation follows from its reasons [1].
- **Evidence:** both rely on the same trial; A reports gains without figures [1]; B adds traders' reports but no air-quality data of its own [1].
- **Assumptions:** A assumes free buses serve everyone [1] and that complaints are mere resistance [1]; B assumes exemptions would not undermine air-quality gains [1].
- **Counter-arguments:** A dismisses traders [1]; B accepts the shared goal and answers the trial evidence [1].
- **Language:** A's emotive slogans [1]; B's qualified wording [1].
- **Analogy:** A's unnamed cities and sweeping "none has gone back" [1].
- **Implications:** A does not address access for disabled residents [1]; B does not address reduced air-quality gains or enforcement costs [1].
- **Perspectives and context:** A's perspective puts public health and the mayor's policy first [1]; B's puts access and livelihoods first, reflecting who it represents [1].
- **Judgement** weighing criteria, for example that logic and treatment of opposing views outweigh A's clearer health focus [1] [1].
- **Limit of verdict** acknowledged, for example that longer trial data could change it [1].
- **AO3:** clear structure, signposted comparative paragraphs and precise terminology [1] [1]. **[25]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about car bans in general rather than about how these two sources argue.
- Comparing only in the final paragraph.
- Calling A's treatment of traders "ad hominem" without quoting the phrase or explaining the effect.
- Treating B as stronger simply because it is calmer, without testing its own assumptions.
- Ignoring the implications each source leaves out.
- Ending without an overall judgement, or with one that contradicts the paragraphs before it.
