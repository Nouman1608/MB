## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by economically inactive, giving an example. **[2]**

**2.** Which person is unemployed under the international definition used in the Labour Force Survey?

A: A retired teacher who is not looking for work

B: A full-time student who is not looking for work

C: A person who lost their job, looked for work last week and can start next week

D: A part-time worker who would like more hours **[1]**

**3.** In Country U, 24.3 million people are employed and 2.7 million are unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. **[2]**

**4.** The unemployment rate falls from 6.0% to 4.5%. State the change in percentage points and as a percentage. **[2]**

**5.** State two weaknesses of the claimant count as a measure of unemployment. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the type of unemployment in each case.

**(a)** A miner who loses their job when the local mine closes and whose skills are not needed by other local employers. **[1]**

**(b)** A ski instructor without work in the summer. **[1]**

**(c)** A factory worker laid off when spending falls during a recession. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In invented Country V the labour force is 30 million. In year 1 the LFS measure of unemployment is 1.8 million and the claimant count is 1.2 million. In year 2, after benefit rules are tightened, the LFS measure is still 1.8 million but the claimant count falls to 0.9 million.

**(a)** Calculate the LFS unemployment rate. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the claimant count fell while the LFS measure did not. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain which measure is more useful for comparing unemployment with other countries. **[2]**

**8.** In a recession, cyclical unemployment in invented Country W rises by 0.5 million. Each worker produced £50,000 of output a year, paid £7,000 a year in taxes when employed, and receives £6,000 a year in benefits when unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate the lost output each year. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the yearly cost to the public finances. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain two other consequences of this rise in unemployment. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why expansionary fiscal policy may do little to reduce structural unemployment. **[4]**

**10.** Explain two possible effects on an economy of reaching full employment. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the significance of the consequences of unemployment for an economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Economically inactive people are neither in work nor seeking work, so they are outside the labour force [1], for example a full-time student or a retired person [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A and B are inactive; D is employed (underemployed). **[1]**

**3.** Labour force = 24.3 + 2.7 = 27.0 million [1]. Rate = 2.7 ÷ 27.0 × 100 = **10.0%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A fall of **1.5 percentage points** [1]; as a percentage, −1.5 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **−25%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, one mark each: it misses unemployed people who are not entitled to or do not claim benefits [1]; it changes when benefit rules change, so it is hard to compare over time [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** **Structural** [1].

**(b)** **Seasonal** [1].

**(c)** **Cyclical** (demand-deficient) [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 1.8 ÷ 30 × 100 = **6.0%** [1].

**(b)** The claimant count includes only people receiving benefits [1]. Tighter rules meant some unemployed people lost entitlement or stopped claiming, so they left the count [1], but they were still without work and seeking jobs, so the LFS, which does not depend on benefits, still counted them [1].

**(c)** The LFS [1], because it uses the same international definition in different countries, whereas claimant counts depend on each country's own benefit rules [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 0.5 m × £50,000 = **£25 bn** [1].

**(b)** Cost per worker = £7,000 + £6,000 = £13,000 [1]; total = 0.5 m × £13,000 = **£6.5 bn** [1].

**(c)** Individuals lose income, so their spending falls [1], which reduces demand for other firms and can cause further job losses [1]. The longer people stay unemployed, the more their skills and confidence fall [1], so some cyclical unemployment may become structural and harder to reverse [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Expansionary fiscal policy raises aggregate demand, which creates jobs [1]. But structural unemployment arises from a mismatch of skills or location [1], so the new jobs may need skills the unemployed do not have or be in other regions [1]. Without retraining or help with moving, extra demand may cause shortages of suitable workers and inflation rather than a fall in structural unemployment [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Higher output and incomes: the economy produces close to its potential [1], raising tax revenue and reducing benefit spending [1]. Inflationary pressure: firms compete for scarce workers and bid up wages [1], and higher costs are passed on as higher prices [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Unemployment means lost output: the economy produces inside its PPC [1], so goods and services that could have been produced are lost for ever [1].
- Public finances worsen through lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending [1], which may force cuts elsewhere or higher borrowing [1].
- Individuals suffer lost income, poorer health and lower future earnings [1], and inequality and regional decline can follow [1].
- Significance depends on duration: short frictional spells have small costs, while long-term unemployment erodes skills [1].
- It depends on type: cyclical unemployment may reverse in a recovery, while structural unemployment can last for years [1].
- There can be some benefits to firms, such as easier recruitment and lower wage pressure, but these are small compared with the costs [1].
- Judgement: the consequences are most significant when unemployment is long-term, structural or concentrated in particular regions [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Make the judgement turn on type and duration rather than listing more consequences.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the population instead of the labour force.
- Writing "1.5%" when the answer is 1.5 percentage points.
- Counting students or retired people as unemployed.
- Suggesting more aggregate demand as the cure for structural unemployment.
- Giving consequences without saying who bears them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do the LFS and claimant count give different figures?**
They count different groups. The LFS uses a survey and the international definition, so it includes unemployed people who do not claim benefits. The claimant count includes only those receiving unemployment-related benefits.

**Which type of unemployment is hardest to reduce?**
Structural unemployment is usually the hardest, because workers need new skills or must move to where jobs are, which takes time and money.
