This guide covers the **American Option: The History of the USA,
1820–1941**, an AS Level route through Papers 1 and 2 for Cambridge
International AS & A Level History 9489.

## Where this fits in 9489

At AS Level, candidates study one of three options — European
(Modern Europe, 1750–1921), American, or International — each with
four named topics, assessed via Paper 1 (a document question) and
Paper 2 (an outline study), with the specific AS topics rotating
between the two papers from year to year. A school teaches one option,
not all three, so the American Option is a genuine alternative route
through 9489 rather than additional content alongside the European
Option.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE AS & A LEVEL HISTORY 9489 — AMERICAN OPTION: THE HISTORY
OF THE USA, 1820–1941**

- 2.1 The Origins of the Civil War, 1820–61 — the growing sectional
divide between North and South over slavery, economics and states'
rights in the decades leading up to war
- 2.2 Civil War and Reconstruction, 1861–77 — the course of the Civil
War and the political and social challenges of reintegrating the
South and defining the status of formerly enslaved people afterward
- 2.3 The Gilded Age and Progressive Era, 1870s to 1920 — rapid
industrialisation and its social consequences, and the reform
movements that responded to them
- 2.4 The Great Crash, The Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41
— the causes of the 1929 Wall Street Crash, the resulting economic
depression, and Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal response

## How to approach it

The Origins of the Civil War (2.1) is best understood as several
interlocking causes building over decades rather than a single trigger
— the expansion of slavery into new territories, deep economic
differences between an industrialising North and an agrarian,
slavery-dependent South, and disputes over the constitutional balance
between federal and state power all reinforced each other, and strong
answers weigh these causes against one another rather than presenting
only one. Civil War and Reconstruction (2.2) rewards being able to
discuss the war and its aftermath as connected, not separate: the
military and political course of the war shaped what Reconstruction
then had to resolve, particularly the unresolved question of what
freedom and citizenship would actually mean for formerly enslaved
people in the post-war South.

The Gilded Age and Progressive Era (2.3) spans roughly fifty years of
rapid change, so it helps to organise revision around a clear before-
and-after structure: the social and economic consequences of rapid
industrialisation and urbanisation (the "Gilded Age" problems — 
inequality, corruption, poor urban and working conditions), followed
by the specific reform movements and legislation of the Progressive
Era that responded to those problems. Being able to name and briefly
explain a specific Progressive reform (rather than describing
"progressivism" only in general terms) tends to be worth more marks
than a purely descriptive account of the period's atmosphere.

The Great Crash, Depression and New Deal (2.4) is frequently assessed
on causation and evaluation together: candidates should be able to
explain the causes of the 1929 Crash (speculative excess, structural
weaknesses in banking and agriculture, and the limits of 1920s
prosperity) and then evaluate specific New Deal measures on their own
terms — some addressed relief, some recovery, and some longer-term
reform — rather than treating "the New Deal" as a single undifferentiated
policy. A frequent error across this whole option is describing events
in isolation from their consequences; because Paper 1 and Paper 2 both
test the ability to reach a supported judgement (not just narrate a
sequence of events), practising short, evidence-based arguments on
each of these four topics is more valuable exam preparation than
memorising a timeline alone.

## Worked example: weighing causes of the Civil War

A typical exam question asks candidates to assess which factor was
most important in causing the Civil War. A weak answer lists slavery,
economic differences and states' rights as three separate causes with
no attempt to weigh them. A stronger answer argues, for instance, that
economic and constitutional disputes were themselves substantially
shaped by the underlying question of slavery's expansion — meaning the
three "separate" causes were in practice deeply entangled rather than
independent — and reaches a supported judgement about which factor
provides the most convincing overall explanation, using specific
evidence (a named event, law, or political crisis) to back the
argument rather than a general assertion. This kind of weighed,
evidence-based judgement, rather than a balanced list with no
conclusion, is what both Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward most highly.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Treating the Civil War's causes as a simple checklist rather than
  interlocking factors that reinforced one another over decades.
- Describing Reconstruction only in terms of policy, without engaging
  with the lived reality it created (or failed to create) for formerly
  enslaved people.
- Discussing the Gilded Age's problems without connecting them to the
  specific Progressive Era reforms that followed as a response.
- Explaining the 1929 Crash as a sudden, isolated event rather than
  the product of structural weaknesses building through the 1920s.
- Treating "the New Deal" as one uniform policy rather than a set of
  distinct measures aimed at relief, recovery and reform, each worth
  evaluating on its own terms.
