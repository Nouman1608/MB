This guide covers the **American Option: The History of the USA,
1820–1941**, an AS Level route through Papers 1 and 2 for Cambridge
International AS & A Level History 9489. This 2027-2029 series
syllabus replaces the previous one, which the board states was valid
for the 2026 series only — the former separate "Origins of the Civil
War, 1820–61" topic is now merged into a single topic, "The Civil War
and Reconstruction, 1820–77."

## Where this fits in 9489

At AS Level, candidates study one of three options — [European
(Modern Europe, 1774–1924)](/resources/a-level-world-history-modern-europe-1750-1921/),
American, or [International](/resources/a-level-world-history-international-1870-1945/)
— each with **three** named topics, assessed via Paper 1 (a document
question) and Paper 2 (an outline study). A school teaches one option,
not all three, so the American Option is a genuine alternative route
through 9489 rather than additional content alongside the others.

The rotation is a fixed, published table, not an unpredictable one:
each topic serves as the Paper 1 topic once every three years, and
Paper 1 and Paper 2 always examine disjoint topics in a given year.

| Year | Paper 1 topic | Paper 2 topics |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920 | The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77; The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41 |
| 2028 | The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41 | The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77; The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920 |
| 2029 | The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77 | The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920; The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41 |

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE AS & A LEVEL HISTORY 9489 — AMERICAN OPTION: THE HISTORY
OF THE USA, 1820–1941**

- Section 4: The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77 — the growing
sectional divide between North and South over slavery, economics and
states' rights; the course of the Civil War; and the political and
social challenges of reintegrating the South and defining the status
of formerly enslaved people afterward
- Section 5: The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870s to 1920 —
rapid industrialisation and its social consequences, and the reform
movements that responded to them
- Section 6: The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal,
1920–41 — the causes of the 1929 Wall Street Crash, the resulting
economic depression, and Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal response

## How to approach it

The Civil War and Reconstruction (Section 4) now spans the full run
from the growing sectional divide of the 1820s through to the end of
Reconstruction in 1877, so it is best understood as one connected
narrative rather than two separate stories: the interlocking causes
that built over decades — the expansion of slavery into new
territories, deep economic differences between an industrialising
North and an agrarian, slavery-dependent South, and disputes over the
constitutional balance between federal and state power — fed directly
into the war itself, and the war's military and political course then
shaped what Reconstruction had to resolve, particularly the unresolved
question of what freedom and citizenship would actually mean for
formerly enslaved people in the post-war South. Strong answers weigh
the causes against one another rather than presenting only one, and
treat causation and aftermath as one continuous chain rather than
stopping at 1861 or starting only from 1865.

The Gilded Age and Progressive Era (Section 5) spans roughly fifty
years of rapid change, so it helps to organise revision around a clear
before-and-after structure: the social and economic consequences of
rapid industrialisation and urbanisation (the "Gilded Age" problems —
inequality, corruption, poor urban and working conditions), followed
by the specific reform movements and legislation of the Progressive
Era that responded to those problems. Being able to name and briefly
explain a specific Progressive reform (rather than describing
"progressivism" only in general terms) tends to be worth more marks
than a purely descriptive account of the period's atmosphere.

The Great Crash, Depression and New Deal (Section 6) is frequently
assessed on causation and evaluation together: candidates should be
able to explain the causes of the 1929 Crash (speculative excess,
structural weaknesses in banking and agriculture, and the limits of
1920s prosperity) and then evaluate specific New Deal measures on
their own terms — some addressed relief, some recovery, and some
longer-term reform — rather than treating "the New Deal" as a single
undifferentiated policy. A frequent error across this whole option is
describing events in isolation from their consequences; because Paper
1 and Paper 2 both test the ability to reach a supported judgement
(not just narrate a sequence of events), practising short,
evidence-based arguments on each of these three topics is more
valuable exam preparation than memorising a timeline alone.

## Worked example: weighing causes of the Civil War

A typical exam question asks candidates to assess which factor was
most important in causing the Civil War. A weak answer lists slavery,
economic differences and states' rights as three separate causes with
no attempt to weigh them. A stronger answer argues, for instance, that
economic and constitutional disputes were themselves substantially
shaped by the underlying question of slavery's expansion — meaning the
three "separate" causes were in practice deeply entangled rather than
independent — and reaches a supported judgement about which factor
provides the most convincing overall explanation, using specific
evidence (a named event, law, or political crisis) to back the
argument rather than a general assertion. This kind of weighed,
evidence-based judgement, rather than a balanced list with no
conclusion, is what both Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward most highly.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Treating the Civil War's causes as a simple checklist rather than
  interlocking factors that reinforced one another over decades.
- Describing Reconstruction only in terms of policy, without engaging
  with the lived reality it created (or failed to create) for formerly
  enslaved people.
- Discussing the Gilded Age's problems without connecting them to the
  specific Progressive Era reforms that followed as a response.
- Explaining the 1929 Crash as a sudden, isolated event rather than
  the product of structural weaknesses building through the 1920s.
- Treating "the New Deal" as one uniform policy rather than a set of
  distinct measures aimed at relief, recovery and reform, each worth
  evaluating on its own terms.
