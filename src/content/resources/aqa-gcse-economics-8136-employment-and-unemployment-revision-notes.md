These notes cover **section 3.2.2.3 Employment and unemployment** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "employment and unemployment and how they are measured", the "types, causes and consequences of unemployment" and "government policies to reduce unemployment". They add the employment and inactivity rates, the causes behind each type and a wider set of affected groups to the shorter treatment in the [economic growth and unemployment revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-economic-growth-and-unemployment-revision-notes/). For full explanations see the [employment and unemployment study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-employment-and-unemployment/), then try the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-employment-and-unemployment-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Condensed recall

- The working-age population splits into three groups: **employed**, **unemployed** and **economically inactive**.
- **Labour force** = employed + unemployed. Inactive people are outside it.
- Under the ILO definition used by the Labour Force Survey, one hour of paid work in the survey week makes a person employed, so part-time workers count as employed.
- To be **ILO unemployed** a person must be without work, able to start within two weeks and have looked for work in the past four weeks.
- **Unemployment rate** = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
- **Employment rate** = employed ÷ working-age population × 100.
- **Inactivity rate** = inactive ÷ working-age population × 100.
- Unemployment can rise without anyone losing a job, if inactive people start looking for work.
- The **claimant count** uses benefit records; the **Labour Force Survey** uses a household sample. They count different people, so their totals differ.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economically inactive | Without a job and either not looking for work or unable to start, e.g. students, carers, early retired, long-term sick |
| Labour force | Everyone employed plus everyone unemployed |
| Long-term unemployment | Being unemployed for a long period, often taken as a year or more |
| Occupational immobility | Workers cannot easily move between jobs because they lack the skills or qualifications |
| Geographical immobility | Workers cannot easily move between places, e.g. because of housing costs or family ties |
| Demand-deficient unemployment | Another name for cyclical unemployment, caused by too little total spending |
| Job search | The time and effort spent matching a worker to a suitable vacancy |

## The four types: cause, length, best-fit policy

| Type | Underlying cause | Usual length | Best-fit policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural | Long-term change in demand or production: new technology, cheaper imports, a product going out of fashion; made worse by immobility | Long | Training and retraining, apprenticeships, help to relocate, support for new firms in affected regions |
| Seasonal | Demand for labour rises and falls at the same time each year (farming, seaside tourism, Christmas retail) | Short, but repeats | Year-round attractions, training for off-season work |
| Frictional | Time taken to move between jobs or from education to work; imperfect information about vacancies | Short | Better job-matching information and search support |
| Cyclical | Fall in total spending during a downturn, so firms in many industries cut output and jobs | Can be long in a deep recession | Raise demand: lower interest rates, more government spending, lower taxes |

**Quick classification test.** One industry in permanent decline? Structural. Same months every year? Seasonal. Whole economy in a downturn? Cyclical. Worker simply between jobs? Frictional.

## Consequences for different groups

- **Unemployed people:** income falls; skills and confidence fade, which makes the next job harder to get; health can suffer.
- **Young people:** losing early work experience can hold back their pay for years.
- **Older workers:** may find it harder to retrain and may leave the labour force for good, becoming inactive.
- **Workers still in jobs:** job insecurity; weaker bargaining power, so smaller pay rises.
- **Firms:** fewer sales, especially for firms selling non-essentials; easier recruitment and less wage pressure; discount retailers may gain customers.
- **Government:** less income tax, National Insurance and VAT; more spent on benefits; borrowing may rise.
- **Regions and towns:** a major closure cuts spending in local shops, which can spread job losses.
- **The whole economy:** output that idle workers could have produced is lost for good.

## Worked calculations

**1. Three rates.** Working-age population 25.0 million; employed 18.5 million; unemployed 1.5 million; inactive 5.0 million.
- Labour force = 18.5 + 1.5 = 20.0 million.
- Unemployment rate = 1.5 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = **7.5%**.
- Employment rate = 18.5 ÷ 25.0 × 100 = **74.0%**.
- Inactivity rate = 5.0 ÷ 25.0 × 100 = **20.0%**.

**2. Finding the number unemployed.** A labour force of 12.0 million has an unemployment rate of 4.5%. Unemployed = 0.045 × 12.0 = **0.54 million** (540,000).

**3. People rejoining the search.** In calculation 1, 0.5 million inactive carers start looking for work and none finds a job. Unemployed = 2.0 million; labour force = 20.5 million; rate = 2.0 ÷ 20.5 × 100 = **9.8%** (1 d.p.). Employment is unchanged at 18.5 million.

## Exam traps

- An inactive person is not unemployed, however low their income.
- A part-time worker is employed, even if they want more hours.
- The employment rate and the unemployment rate use **different denominators**: the working-age population and the labour force. They do not add up to 100%.
- A factory closing in a recession is cyclical if the cause is falling demand everywhere; it is structural if the industry is in permanent decline.
- Raising total spending does little for structural unemployment, because the problem is a mismatch of skills or place, not a lack of demand.
- "Bad for firms" is too vague. Say which firms lose, and note that recruitment becomes easier.

## Self-test

1. Name the three groups the working-age population is divided into.
2. State the ILO conditions for a person to count as unemployed.
3. A person works six hours a week and wants full-time work. How are they classified?
4. What is meant by occupational immobility?
5. Labour force 8.0 million, unemployed 0.36 million. Find the unemployment rate.
6. Working-age population 10.0 million, employed 7.3 million. Find the employment rate.
7. A ski instructor in Scotland is out of work every summer. Which type of unemployment is this?
8. Why can the claimant count fall when unemployment has not fallen?
9. Give one reason why unemployment can benefit some firms.
10. Explain why lower interest rates suit cyclical but not structural unemployment.
11. Give one consequence of youth unemployment for the individual in later life.
12. Explain how unemployment affects the government's budget.

### Answers

1. Employed, unemployed and economically inactive.
2. Without a job, able to start within two weeks and has looked for work in the past four weeks.
3. Employed (one hour of paid work in the survey week counts).
4. When workers cannot easily move into other jobs because they lack the necessary skills or qualifications.
5. 0.36 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 4.5%.
6. 7.3 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 73%.
7. Seasonal unemployment.
8. Benefit rules may change so fewer people are eligible to claim, or people may stop claiming while still looking for work.
9. Firms can recruit more easily and face less pressure to raise wages.
10. Cheaper borrowing raises spending, which helps when the cause is too little demand; structural unemployment comes from a mismatch of skills or location, which extra spending does not fix.
11. Missing early work experience can lower their skills and earnings for years afterwards.
12. Tax revenue (income tax, VAT) falls while spending on benefits rises, so the deficit widens and borrowing may increase.
