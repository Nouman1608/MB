Sub-topic 6.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029) is the longest in Topic 6, with ten statements. They move from the physical science (Earth structure, plate movement and the features of earthquakes and eruptions) to the human side (impacts, management before, during and after an event, and the opportunities volcanoes offer). This guide works through each statement in syllabus order. For a summary of all four hazards, see the [Natural hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-natural-hazards-study-guide/).

## Structure of the Earth (6.1.1) and the continents (6.1.2)

The syllabus limits structure to three layers:
- **Crust**: the thin, rigid outer layer. Oceanic crust is thinner and denser; continental crust is thicker and less dense.
- **Mantle**: the thick layer beneath the crust, made of hot rock that moves very slowly.
- **Core**: the very hot, dense centre, mainly iron and nickel. Heat from the core drives movement in the mantle.

You must be able to identify the seven continents on a map: **Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania and South America**. Questions may give a world map of earthquakes and ask you to describe the pattern using continent and ocean names, so practise phrases such as "a chain along the west coast of South America".

## Distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes (6.1.3)

Most earthquakes and volcanoes lie in narrow belts that follow the edges of **tectonic plates**, the large slabs into which the crust is broken. The clearest belt surrounds the Pacific Ocean (often called the Ring of Fire). Another runs down the middle of the Atlantic Ocean.

A few volcanoes are far from any boundary. These form over **hotspots**, where an unusually hot plume of mantle rock rises beneath the middle of a plate and melts through it. The Hawaiian Islands are the standard example: as the Pacific Plate moves over the hotspot, a chain of volcanoes forms, oldest furthest from the hotspot.

When you describe a distribution, give the general pattern (linear belts along plate boundaries), a named location, and the exception (hotspots).

## Convection currents and plate boundaries (6.1.4)

Heat from the core warms mantle rock, which becomes less dense and rises. Near the crust it spreads sideways, cools, becomes denser and sinks again. These circular **convection currents** drag the plates above them, a few centimetres a year.

| Boundary | Plate movement | What happens | Hazards | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Divergent or constructive | Plates move apart | Magma rises into the gap and forms new crust | Volcanic activity and earthquakes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland |
| Convergent or destructive | Plates move together | Denser oceanic plate is subducted beneath the other plate and melts | Earthquakes and a line of volcanoes | Andes, Japan |
| Conservative | Plates slide past each other | Friction locks the plates until stress is suddenly released | Earthquakes only, no volcanoes | San Andreas Fault, California |

The syllabus wording to reuse: at convergent boundaries, **subduction of one plate beneath another causes earthquakes and a line of volcanoes**.

## Features of earthquakes (6.1.5)

- **Release of energy**: stress builds as plates lock together; when the rock suddenly slips, stored energy is released as seismic waves that shake the ground.
- **Focus**: the point underground where the rock slips and the earthquake starts.
- **Epicentre**: the point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus, where shaking is usually strongest.
- **Magnitude**: the energy released, measured on the **Moment Magnitude Scale**. Each increase of one whole number means about 32 times more energy.

A shallow focus usually causes more damage at the surface than a deep one, because the waves lose less energy before they arrive.

## Features of volcanic eruptions (6.1.6)

- **Magma rising to the surface to form lava**: molten rock underground is magma; once it erupts it is called lava.
- **Ash**: fine fragments of rock and glass blasted into the air, which can bury crops, collapse roofs and ground aircraft.
- **Gases**: water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.
- **Acid rain**: sulfur dioxide dissolves in rainwater to form acid, damaging vegetation and water bodies.
- **Volcanic bombs**: large lumps of lava thrown out that cool and harden as they fly.
- **Pyroclastic flows**: fast-moving clouds of hot gas, ash and rock that rush down the volcano's slopes, destroying everything in their path.

## Impacts on people and the environment (6.1.7)

The syllabus lists ten. Learn to explain each:
- **Damage to buildings and infrastructure**: shaking, ash load and lava destroy homes, roads, bridges and power lines.
- **Loss of crops, livestock and habitats**: ash and lava bury land; animals are killed.
- **Evacuation of people and livestock**: people leave homes, sometimes for months.
- **Fire**: broken gas pipes and electrical cables ignite after earthquakes; lava sets vegetation alight.
- **Tsunamis**: an undersea earthquake shifts the seabed and displaces water, sending waves that flood coasts.
- **Landslides**: shaking loosens slopes.
- **Contamination of drinking-water supplies**: burst pipes and sewers mix; ash pollutes reservoirs.
- **Water-related disease**: contaminated water spreads diseases such as cholera.
- **Financial losses**: rebuilding costs, lost businesses and lost tourism.
- **Human health**: injuries, breathing problems from ash and gases, and stress.

## Managing the impacts (6.1.8 and 6.1.9)

Arrange strategies by stage. This matches the syllabus wording "before, during and after".

| Stage | Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | Monitoring and warning | Volcanoes give signs (small earthquakes, ground swelling, gas changes), so warnings allow evacuation; tsunami warnings can be issued after an undersea quake | Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely; equipment is costly |
| Before | Land use zoning | Keeps homes, schools and hospitals off fault lines, unstable slopes and lava routes | Land may be scarce; hard to enforce in informal settlements |
| Before | Structure of buildings | Cross-bracing, deep flexible foundations and shock absorbers reduce collapse | Expensive; hard to fit to existing buildings |
| Before | Disaster preparation | Plans, drills, emergency supplies and rescue teams mean people act quickly | Needs regular funding and practice |
| During | Evacuation | Removes people from danger, saving lives | Roads may be blocked; people may refuse to leave property or livestock |
| During and after | Shelters | Safe space, food and water for those made homeless | Overcrowding can spread disease |
| After | Rebuilding of damaged areas | Restores homes and services; a chance to build more safely | Slow and costly |
| After | International aid | Money, rescue teams, medical care and supplies where local resources are overwhelmed | Can arrive late, miss remote areas or create dependency |

## Opportunities provided by volcanoes (6.1.10)

- **Fertile soils**: weathered ash and lava release minerals, so farms give high yields.
- **Extraction of minerals**: sulfur and some metal ores are deposited near volcanoes.
- **Building materials**: rocks such as basalt are quarried for construction.
- **Geothermal power**: hot rock heats underground water, producing steam to turn turbines; Iceland uses geothermal energy widely.
- **Tourism**: visitors come to see craters, hot springs and geysers, creating jobs.

## Worked example 1: comparing magnitudes

On the Moment Magnitude Scale, each whole-number step means about 32 times more energy released.

1. A magnitude 7.0 earthquake compared with a magnitude 6.0: one step, so about 32 times more energy.
2. A magnitude 8.0 compared with a magnitude 6.0: two steps, so 32 × 32 = 1,024, about 1,000 times more energy.

So a single magnitude 8.0 earthquake releases roughly the energy of a thousand magnitude 6.0 earthquakes. Magnitude does not alone decide the damage; depth of focus, building quality and population density matter too.

## Worked example 2: annotated "discuss" answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of land use zoning for managing the impacts of earthquakes. [4]

**Model answer:** Land use zoning keeps important buildings such as hospitals and schools away from fault lines and unstable slopes, so fewer people are in the most dangerous places [1]. Open spaces can be kept as safe gathering areas after the event [1]. However, in crowded cities there may be no other land to build on, so zoning is hard to apply [1], and in informal settlements people may build without permission, so the rules are not enforced [1].

## Common mistakes

- Calling the epicentre the place the earthquake starts; that is the focus.
- Putting volcanoes at conservative boundaries.
- Forgetting hotspots when describing the distribution.
- Listing acid rain as an earthquake impact rather than an eruption feature.
- Saying earthquakes can be predicted precisely.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** continents or boundary types from a map: use the exact names.
- **Describe** a distribution: pattern, named locations, exceptions such as hotspots.
- **Explain** a boundary: movement, process (subduction, rising magma, friction) and resulting hazard.
- **Discuss** strategies: sort them into before, during and after, give a benefit and limitation for each, and end with a judgement.
