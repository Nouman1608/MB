It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level, and A-level Paper 3 can test the practical. The skills column suggests preparing cyclohexene from cyclohexanol, purifying it with a separating funnel and by distillation.

## Reagent, conditions and equation

Heat the alcohol with **concentrated sulfuric acid** or **concentrated phosphoric acid**. The acid is a catalyst. Water is eliminated: the –OH from one carbon and an H from the next carbon.

```
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
```

Phosphoric acid is often preferred in the laboratory because concentrated sulfuric acid also oxidises some of the alcohol, giving unwanted side products.

## The mechanism: what each arrow must show

Elimination here is acid-catalysed, so the first job is to turn –OH, a poor leaving group, into –OH₂⁺, which leaves as a neutral water molecule.

1. **Protonation.** An arrow from a lone pair on the oxygen of –OH to H⁺. Product: R–OH₂⁺, with the + on oxygen.
2. **Loss of water.** An arrow from the C–O bond to the oxygen. Water leaves and a carbocation forms on the carbon that held the –OH.
3. **Loss of H⁺.** An arrow from a C–H bond on an **adjacent** carbon to the C–C bond between that carbon and C⁺. The C=C forms and H⁺ is released, regenerating the catalyst.

Marking points usually rest on: the start and end of each arrow, the + charges on the oxonium ion and the carbocation, and H⁺ shown leaving at the end. An arrow from the H atom itself, rather than from the C–H bond, is a common lost mark.

## Finding every alkene: a systematic method

1. Find the carbon bonded to –OH (call it Cα).
2. List every carbon bonded to Cα that carries at least one H (the β-carbons).
3. Group β-carbons that are equivalent by symmetry. Each different group gives a different **position** of C=C.
4. For each alkene, check for **E/Z isomerism**: each carbon of the C=C must carry two different groups.
5. Count and name every alkene.

### Worked example 1: pentan-2-ol

CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂CH₂CH₃. Cα is C2. β-carbons: C1 (CH₃) and C3 (CH₂). They are different.

```
H lost from C1  →  pent-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃         no E/Z (C1 has two H)
H lost from C3  →  pent-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃          E/Z: C2 has H and CH₃,
                                                       C3 has H and C₂H₅
total: pent-1-ene, (E)-pent-2-ene, (Z)-pent-2-ene  →  three alkenes
```

### Worked example 2: 3-methylpentan-3-ol

CH₃CH₂–C(OH)(CH₃)–CH₂CH₃. Cα is C3. β-carbons: the methyl carbon, and C2 and C4, which are equivalent.

```
H lost from the methyl  →  2-ethylbut-1-ene, CH₂=C(CH₂CH₃)₂     no E/Z
H lost from C2 or C4    →  3-methylpent-2-ene, CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃
                           E/Z: one carbon has H and CH₃; the other has CH₃ and C₂H₅
total: three alkenes
```

### Worked example 3: 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol

(CH₃)₂C(OH)–CH(CH₃)₂. β-carbons: the two methyls on C2 (equivalent) and C3.

```
H lost from a C2 methyl  →  2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene    no E/Z
H lost from C3           →  2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene    no E/Z (each C of C=C has two CH₃)
total: two alkenes
```

**When elimination cannot happen.** The mechanism needs an H atom on a carbon next to Cα. Methanol has only one carbon, so it cannot form an alkene by this route.

## Elimination and hydration: one reaction, two directions

Hydration of an alkene (3.3.5.1) and elimination of water from an alcohol are the reverse of each other, and both use an acid catalyst and pass through a carbocation. The conditions decide the direction:

| Hydration | Elimination |
|---|---|
| Steam in excess | Concentrated acid, little water present |
| Alcohol is the product | Alkene distilled off or removed as it forms |

This is why the same carbocation-stability ideas apply in both directions.

## Polymers without crude oil

The specification highlights that alkenes made by elimination can be polymerised, so polymers can be made without monomers from crude oil:

```
glucose  →(fermentation)→  ethanol  →(elimination)→  ethene  →(addition polymerisation)→  poly(ethene)
```

The polymer is chemically the same as poly(ethene) from crude oil. The advantages and drawbacks mirror the biofuel discussion: a renewable feedstock against land use, energy for distillation and farming inputs.

## Required practical 5: the reason for each step

Cyclohexanol (boiling point about 161 °C) is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. Cyclohexene (about 83 °C) forms.

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Heat gently and distil the mixture, collecting the crude distillate | Cyclohexene boils far lower than cyclohexanol, so it distils off as it forms, which also helps the reaction go further |
| Shake the distillate with sodium carbonate solution in a separating funnel | Neutralises acid carried over; CO₂ is released, so open the tap often to release pressure |
| Run off and discard the lower aqueous layer | Cyclohexene is less dense than water and does not mix with it, so it forms the upper layer |
| Wash with water | Removes remaining soluble impurities |
| Add anhydrous calcium chloride and swirl until clear | Removes water; cloudiness shows water is still present |
| Decant and redistil, collecting near 83 °C | Separates cyclohexene from any remaining cyclohexanol and other impurities |

**Showing the product is an alkene.** Shake a little with bromine water: the orange colour disappears.

### Worked example 4: yield

10.0 g of cyclohexanol (Mᵣ 100.0) gives 5.74 g of cyclohexene (Mᵣ 82.0).

```
n(cyclohexanol) = 10.0 ÷ 100.0 = 0.100 mol
n(cyclohexene)  = 5.74 ÷ 82.0  = 0.0700 mol
yield           = 0.0700 ÷ 0.100 × 100 = 70.0 %
```

Losses come from incomplete reaction, product left in the flask and on the drying agent, and transfers between containers.

## Common mistakes

- Dilute acid instead of concentrated acid.
- Arrow from H⁺ to the oxygen, rather than from the oxygen lone pair to H⁺.
- Third arrow starting at the H atom instead of the C–H bond.
- Missing one β-carbon, or counting equivalent β-carbons twice.
- Forgetting E/Z isomers, or claiming E/Z where one carbon carries two identical groups.
- Saying the organic layer is the lower one in the separating funnel.
- Drying agent "removes impurities" with no mention of water.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline the mechanism**: three steps, all arrows, both intermediates with charges, H⁺ regenerated.
- **Name all the products**: give positional names and E/Z labels; state the total if asked how many.
- **Explain** a practical step: what it removes or achieves and why it works (density, boiling point, neutralisation).
- **Suggest** why the yield is below 100%: give specific losses, not "human error".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the acid called a catalyst in elimination?**
H⁺ is used in the first step to protonate the –OH group and released in the last step, so it is regenerated.

**How do I know if an alkene from elimination shows E/Z isomerism?**
Check each carbon of the C=C. If both carry two different groups, there are E and Z isomers. If either carbon has two identical groups, there are none.

**Why is cyclohexene the upper layer in the separating funnel?**
It is less dense than water and does not mix with it, so it floats on top of the aqueous layer.
