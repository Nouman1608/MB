This guide covers subtopic **13.2, Characteristic organic reactions**, from Topic 13, An introduction to AS Level organic chemistry, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

Subtopic 13.2 is a vocabulary subtopic: it defines the terms every later organic topic uses. The [Organic Mechanisms: An Introduction](/resources/organic-mechanisms-an-introduction/) guide defines them briefly. This page applies them: each term is tied to a real AS reaction, you practise classifying reactions with more than one label, and you see how [O] and [H] balance organic redox equations. Condensed notes are in the [13.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-characteristic-organic-reactions-revision-notes/), and the [13.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-characteristic-organic-reactions-practice/) test it.

## What 13.2 asks you to do

**Outcome 1**: interpret and use the terminology of types of organic compounds and reactions:

- (a) homologous series; (b) saturated and unsaturated; (c) homolytic and heterolytic fission; (d) free radical, initiation, propagation, termination; (e) nucleophile, electrophile, nucleophilic, electrophilic; (f) addition, substitution, elimination, hydrolysis, condensation; (g) oxidation and reduction, using **[O]** for one oxygen atom from an oxidising agent and **[H]** for one hydrogen atom from a reducing agent.

**Outcome 2**: understand and use the terms for types of mechanism: **free-radical substitution**, **electrophilic addition**, **nucleophilic substitution** and **nucleophilic addition**. Curly arrows are expected in mechanisms, and **each arrow should begin at a bond or a lone pair**.

## Describing compounds

**Homologous series**: a family of compounds with the **same functional group** and the **same general formula**, in which each member differs from the next by **CH₂**. Members have **similar chemical properties** and show a **gradual trend in physical properties**, such as boiling point rising with chain length.

**Saturated**: containing only single bonds between carbon atoms. **Unsaturated**: containing at least one carbon–carbon multiple bond, at AS a C=C double bond. Alkanes are saturated; alkenes are unsaturated. Bromine water is decolourised by an unsaturated compound but not by a saturated one.

## How bonds break

**Homolytic fission**: the bond breaks so that **each atom keeps one electron** of the shared pair. Two **free radicals** form.

Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl•

**Heterolytic fission**: the bond breaks so that **one atom takes both electrons**. A cation and an anion form. The more electronegative atom usually takes the pair:

CH₃CH₂–Br → CH₃CH₂⁺ + Br⁻

A **free radical** is a species with an **unpaired electron**, shown by a dot. Homolytic fission is favoured for non-polar bonds when energy is supplied as ultraviolet light; heterolytic fission is favoured for polar bonds, often in solution.

## Free-radical chain reactions

The three stages, illustrated with the bromination of ethane in UV light:

| Stage | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiation** | Radicals are formed by homolytic fission | Br₂ → 2Br• |
| **Propagation** | A radical reacts to form a product and a new radical; the chain continues | Br• + C₂H₆ → C₂H₅• + HBr; then C₂H₅• + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br + Br• |
| **Termination** | Two radicals combine; radicals are removed | Br• + Br• → Br₂; C₂H₅• + Br• → C₂H₅Br; C₂H₅• + C₂H₅• → C₄H₁₀ |

Check: in each propagation step there is one radical on each side. In termination there are two radicals on the left and none on the right. The formation of butane in termination explains why small amounts of a longer alkane appear among the products.

## Attacking species

**Nucleophile**: an **electron-pair donor**. It has a lone pair (and often a negative charge) and attacks an electron-deficient carbon, usually a δ+ carbon. Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O.

**Electrophile**: an **electron-pair acceptor**. It is attracted to a region of high electron density, such as a C=C double bond. Examples: H⁺, the δ+ end of HBr, and Br₂ when it is polarised as it approaches a double bond.

The adjectives **nucleophilic** and **electrophilic** describe the attacking species in a mechanism. So "nucleophilic substitution" means a substitution in which a nucleophile attacks.

## Types of reaction

| Type | Definition | AS example |
|---|---|---|
| **Addition** | Two molecules join to form a single product | CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| **Substitution** | One atom or group is replaced by another | CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ |
| **Elimination** | A small molecule is removed from one molecule, usually forming a C=C bond | CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O + Br⁻ (ethanolic NaOH, heat) |
| **Hydrolysis** | A molecule is broken down by reaction with water | CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH |
| **Condensation** | Two molecules join with the loss of a small molecule, often water | CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O |

**One reaction, two labels.** Some reactions fit more than one term. Bromoethane reacting with water to give ethanol and HBr is a **hydrolysis** (water breaks the C–Br bond) and also a **nucleophilic substitution** (water acts as the nucleophile and replaces Br). Esterification is a **condensation**, and its reverse is a **hydrolysis**. Give the term the question asks for, and use both if asked to classify fully.

## Oxidation and reduction with [O] and [H]

In organic chemistry, oxidation often means **gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen**, and reduction means **gaining hydrogen or losing oxygen**. Balancing with the full oxidising agent, such as acidified dichromate(VI), is complicated, so the syllabus allows [O] and [H].

**Worked example: balancing with [O].** Write equations for the oxidation of ethanol to ethanal and to ethanoic acid.

Ethanol to ethanal: two hydrogen atoms are removed, and they leave as water with one [O]:

CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O

Ethanol to ethanoic acid: the same step, then one more oxygen is added to the aldehyde:

CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O

Check by counting atoms: left C₂H₆O + 2O = C₂H₆O₃; right C₂H₄O₂ + H₂O = C₂H₆O₃.

**Worked example: balancing with [H].** Write equations for the reduction of propanal and of propanone.

CH₃CH₂CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (a primary alcohol)

CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (a secondary alcohol)

Each C=O becomes CH–OH, which needs two hydrogen atoms.

## The four mechanism types

A mechanism name combines **who attacks** with **what happens overall**:

| Mechanism | Attacking species | Typical substrate | Example in AS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free-radical substitution | A free radical | Alkane | Ethane + Br₂ in UV light |
| Electrophilic addition | An electrophile | Alkene (electron-rich C=C) | Ethene + HBr → bromoethane |
| Nucleophilic substitution | A nucleophile | Halogenoalkane (δ+ carbon) | Bromoethane + OH⁻ → ethanol |
| Nucleophilic addition | A nucleophile | Aldehyde or ketone (δ+ carbonyl carbon) | Propanone + HCN → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile |

**Worked example: classify each reaction.**

| Reaction | Classification |
|---|---|
| CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl, UV light | Substitution; free-radical substitution |
| CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (steam, acid catalyst) | Addition; electrophilic addition |
| CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + Br⁻ | Substitution; nucleophilic substitution |
| CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (heated Al₂O₃) | Elimination (dehydration) |
| CH₃CH₂CHO + HCN → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CN | Addition; nucleophilic addition |
| CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃CH₂COOH + H₂O | Oxidation |

### Curly arrows

A curly arrow shows the movement of **a pair of electrons**. It must **start at a bond or a lone pair** and end where the pair goes: at an atom, to form a new bond, or at an atom that will take the pair as the bond breaks. Never start an arrow at a positive charge or at an atom with no lone pair.

## Common mistakes

- **"A homologous series has the same molecular formula."** It has the same general formula; members differ by CH₂.
- **Saying free radicals are charged.** A radical has an unpaired electron and is usually neutral.
- **Defining a nucleophile as "attracted to a positive charge" without "electron-pair donor".**
- **Propagation steps that use up radicals.** One radical in, one radical out.
- **Unbalanced [O] equations.** Count atoms on both sides, including the water.
- **Naming a mechanism by reaction type only.** "Substitution" is incomplete; say nucleophilic or free-radical.
- **Curly arrows from a hydrogen atom or a positive charge.**

## Exam technique: command words in 13.2

- **Define**: give the textbook meaning, for example "an electron-pair donor".
- **State** (type of reaction): use the syllabus terms exactly.
- **Classify**: give both the reaction type and the mechanism type when both apply.
- **Write an equation** (oxidation or reduction): use [O] or [H] and check atoms balance.
- **Identify** (stage of a chain reaction): use the number of radicals on each side.
