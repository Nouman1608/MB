For explanations and worked examples, use the [consumer and producer surplus study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-consumer-and-producer-surplus-cambridge-alevel-economics/); for exam-style questions, use the [consumer and producer surplus practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-consumer-and-producer-surplus-cambridge-alevel-economics-practice/).

## Meanings (2.5.1 and 2.5.2)

- **Consumer surplus:** the difference between the **maximum price consumers are willing to pay** and the **price they actually pay**, summed over all units bought.
- **Producer surplus:** the difference between the **price producers receive** and the **minimum price they would accept**, summed over all units sold.
- **Total surplus:** consumer surplus + producer surplus.

## Areas on the diagram

| Surplus | Area |
|---|---|
| Consumer | Below the demand curve, above the price line, up to the quantity traded |
| Producer | Above the supply curve, below the price line, up to the quantity traded |

For straight lines, each is a triangle: **one half x base (quantity) x height (price gap)**.

- Consumer surplus height = price-axis intercept of demand - price.
- Producer surplus height = price - price-axis intercept of supply.

**Discrete version:** add (willingness to pay - price) for each buyer who buys, and (price - minimum acceptable price) for each seller who sells.

**Worked recall (invented market).** Demand meets the price axis at 40 cu, supply meets it at 4 cu, and equilibrium is a price of 16 cu with 60 units traded. Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (40 - 16) x 60 = 720 cu. Producer surplus = 1/2 x (16 - 4) x 60 = 360 cu. Total surplus = 1/2 x (40 - 4) x 60 = 1,080 cu, which checks the sum.

## Significance

- Consumer surplus measures consumers' **net benefit**; a change in it shows whether consumers gain or lose from a market change.
- Producer surplus reflects the **reward to producers** above their minimum and so the incentive to supply.
- Total surplus is **maximised at the competitive equilibrium**: every unit up to that quantity is valued above the minimum supply price. Less trade means lost surplus, a sign of **inefficiency**.
- Cautions: willingness to pay depends on income; curves are estimates.

## Causes of changes (2.5.3)

| Change | Price | Quantity | Consumer surplus | Producer surplus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises | Usually rises (buyers value the good more) | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls | Usually falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (costs fall) | Falls | Rises | Rises | Usually rises |
| Supply decreases (costs rise) | Rises | Falls | Falls | Usually falls |

Causes: income, tastes, prices of related goods, population (demand side); input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, number of firms, weather (supply side).

## Role of elasticity (2.5.4)

- **Demand shifts:** the elasticity of **supply** decides the outcome.
  - Inelastic supply: large price change, small quantity change; producers take most of the gain or loss.
  - Elastic supply: small price change, large quantity change; consumers take most of it.
  - Limits: perfectly inelastic supply, consumer surplus unchanged by a parallel demand shift; perfectly elastic supply, producer surplus zero throughout.
- **Supply shifts:** the elasticity of **demand** decides the outcome.
  - Inelastic demand: price changes a lot, quantity little.
  - Elastic demand: quantity changes a lot, price little.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Willingness to pay | The maximum a consumer would pay for a unit |
| Minimum supply price | The lowest price at which a producer would sell a unit |
| Consumer surplus | Willingness to pay minus price paid, over all units |
| Producer surplus | Price received minus minimum supply price, over all units |
| Total (community) surplus | Consumer surplus plus producer surplus |
| Efficiency | Resources allocated so that total surplus cannot be increased |

## Exam traps

- Shading the wrong side of the price line.
- Forgetting the half in the triangle.
- Saying consumer surplus always falls when price rises.
- Keeping the old intercept after a curve has shifted.
- Equating producer surplus with profit.
- Writing "elasticity matters" without saying whose elasticity.

## Self-test

1. Define consumer surplus.
2. Define producer surplus.
3. A buyer would pay up to 45 cu and pays 32 cu. What is her consumer surplus?
4. Demand meets the price axis at 80 cu; price is 50 cu; quantity is 300. Calculate consumer surplus.
5. Supply meets the price axis at 20 cu in the market in Question 4. Calculate producer surplus.
6. Where on a diagram is producer surplus found?
7. Why can consumer surplus rise when price rises?
8. Supply is perfectly inelastic and demand rises. Who gains most surplus?
9. Supply is perfectly elastic. What is producer surplus?
10. Costs fall in a market with very inelastic demand. Does the change appear mainly in price or quantity?
11. Why is total surplus largest at the competitive equilibrium?
12. Give one limitation of using consumer surplus to judge welfare.

**Answers**

1. The difference between the maximum price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay, summed over all units bought.
2. The difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they would accept, summed over all units sold.
3. 45 - 32 = 13 cu.
4. 1/2 x (80 - 50) x 300 = 4,500 cu.
5. 1/2 x (50 - 20) x 300 = 4,500 cu.
6. Above the supply curve and below the price line, up to the quantity traded.
7. If the price rise is caused by an increase in demand, consumers now value the good more, so the gap between willingness to pay and price can widen.
8. Producers: the whole rise in demand shows up as a higher price on an unchanged quantity.
9. Zero, because every unit is sold at the minimum price producers would accept.
10. Mainly in price, because quantity demanded responds little.
11. Every unit up to that quantity is worth more to a buyer than its minimum supply price, so all mutually beneficial trades take place.
12. Willingness to pay depends on income, so the needs of poorer consumers count for less (or: demand curves are estimates).
