Businesses can often make more profit by cutting corners. Ethics asks whether doing so is right. Sub-topic 6.2 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) is about **ethics and ethical issues in commerce**: the meaning of ethics, the role of fair trade, and two ethical issues named in the syllabus, **misleading advertising** and **harmful disposal of products**.

## The meaning of ethics

**Ethics** is the set of moral principles about what is right and wrong. In business, ethical behaviour means making decisions that are honest and fair to everyone affected, not just legal. These people are the business's **stakeholders**: customers, employees, suppliers, owners, the local community and the environment.

An action can be legal but not ethical. For example, a company may be allowed to pay very low wages in a country with no minimum wage, but many people would say this is unfair.

| Ethical behaviour | Unethical behaviour |
|---|---|
| Telling the truth about products | Making false claims |
| Paying suppliers and workers fairly | Paying very low wages |
| Keeping workplaces safe | Forcing people to work in dangerous conditions |
| Disposing of waste safely | Dumping harmful waste |
| Respecting customers' data | Selling customers' data without permission |

### Why should a business be ethical?

- **Reputation and customer trust.** Customers prefer to buy from businesses they trust.
- **Loyal employees and suppliers.** People work harder for fair employers.
- **Avoiding penalties.** Unethical conduct can lead to fines and legal action.
- **Long-term success.** Short-term gains from cutting corners can be lost if bad behaviour is exposed.

### Costs of being ethical

- Fairer prices and wages raise costs.
- Safer disposal needs equipment and procedures.
- Prices may have to rise, so some customers may buy from cheaper rivals.

## The role of fair trade

**Fair trade** is a system that aims to give producers in developing countries a fair deal, so that they receive a fair price for their goods and work in good conditions. Products such as coffee, tea, cocoa, sugar and cotton may carry a fair trade label.

### Features of fair trade

- **A guaranteed minimum price**, which protects farmers when world prices fall.
- **A fair trade premium**, an extra payment that the community spends on projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
- **Safe working conditions** and no child or forced labour.
- **Long-term contracts**, so producers can plan ahead.
- **Environmental standards**, such as limits on harmful chemicals.
- **Payment of a fair wage** and the right to join a union.

### Role in commerce

Fair trade changes the relationship between producers and buyers. It reduces the power of large buyers to force very low prices. For consumers, a label shows that the product was produced fairly. For retailers, selling fair trade goods can attract ethically minded customers.

### Worked example: a fair trade price

A coffee farmer sells 500 kg of coffee.

| | Price per kg | Income |
|---|---|---|
| Market price | $0.80 | 500 × 0.80 = $400 |
| Fair trade minimum price | $1.20 | 500 × 1.20 = $600 |

1. Extra income = 600 − 400 = **$200**.
2. Percentage increase = 200 ÷ 400 × 100 = **50%**.
3. A fair trade premium of $0.15 per kg gives the community 500 × 0.15 = **$75** to spend on a project.

A shop that buys the coffee pays more per kilogram. If it passes on the extra $0.40 per kg plus some costs, the price to customers rises, so some customers may choose cheaper brands. This is the dilemma businesses face.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Better income and living standards for producers | Higher prices for consumers or lower profit for retailers |
| Protects workers from exploitation | Only some producers can join; others miss out |
| Appeals to ethical consumers; improves business reputation | Checking and labelling costs |
| Encourages environmentally friendly farming | Some of the extra money may not reach the farmers |

## Ethical issues in commerce

### 1. Misleading advertising

**Misleading advertising** gives customers a false or unclear impression of a product so that they buy it. Examples:

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| False claims | "Cures all illness" when it does not |
| Exaggeration | "The world's best phone" with no evidence |
| Hidden charges | "From $9.99" when most customers pay $25 after fees |
| Fake or paid reviews presented as genuine | Reviews written by the company |
| Misleading images | A photo showing a bigger product than is sold |
| "Greenwashing" | Claiming a product is eco-friendly when it is not |
| Targeting vulnerable people | Promising miracle results to the sick |

**Effects:** customers lose money and trust; honest competitors lose sales; the business can face fines, refunds and loss of reputation.

*Worked example.* A company advertises a "7-day diet pill" that does not work. 800 customers each pay $25 and later demand a refund. Refund cost = 800 × 25 = $20,000. The regulator fines the company $15,000. Total cost = 20,000 + 15,000 = **$35,000**, plus damage to its name. The original sales were also $20,000, so the company made no profit and suffered a loss.

### 2. Harmful disposal of products

**Harmful disposal** means getting rid of products, packaging or waste in a way that damages people or the environment. Examples:

- Dumping chemicals into rivers or on land.
- Throwing away electronic waste, which contains harmful metals.
- Burning plastic, which releases poisonous fumes.
- Leaving unsold goods, such as clothes, to be sent to landfill or burned.
- Selling products that cannot be recycled or are hard to dispose of safely.

**Effects:** pollution of water and soil; harm to wildlife and human health; clean-up costs; fines; damage to reputation.

**Ethical alternatives:** recycling and take-back schemes, safe treatment of hazardous waste, designing products that can be repaired or recycled, and giving clear instructions on how to dispose of the product.

### Ethics and sustainability

Harmful disposal is also a sustainability issue (6.1), because it harms the environment. Keep the focus different in answers. **Ethics** is about right and wrong behaviour towards people and the environment. **Sustainability** is about meeting present needs without harming future generations.

## Case: a chocolate retailer

*Invented example.* Sweetfield is a chain of sweet shops deciding whether to stock a cheaper chocolate bar or a fair trade one.

1. **Cost:** the fair trade bar costs Sweetfield 15% more to buy. If the cheaper bar costs $2.00, the fair trade bar costs 2.00 + (15% × 2.00) = $2.30.
2. **Customers:** some customers care where chocolate comes from and will pay extra. Others look only at price.
3. **Producers:** the cocoa farmers get a guaranteed minimum price and a premium for community projects.
4. **Reputation:** the chain can advertise that it supports fair trade. This only works if the claim is true. A false claim would be misleading advertising.
5. **Decision:** Sweetfield could stock both bars, so customers can choose, and display the fair trade bar clearly. This lets the business test demand before changing its whole range.

The case shows the main tension in business ethics: ethical choices often cost more now but may build trust and loyalty.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why misleading advertising is an ethical issue. [3]

> Misleading advertising gives customers a false impression of a product, for example by claiming that it does something it cannot do [1]. Customers may waste money or be harmed because they bought the product based on a lie [1]. It is unfair to honest competitors and damages trust, so it is wrong even if it increases sales [1].

**Why it scores 3:** it defines the issue, explains the harm and says why it is wrong.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ethics as the same as legality.
- Saying fair trade means goods are free or cheap. It means fairer pay to producers.
- Writing "advertising" instead of "misleading advertising".
- Forgetting that ethical choices can raise costs.
- Describing only the effect on the business and not on people or the environment.
- Mixing up ethics and sustainability.

## Exam technique

- **Define** the term, then **explain** the harm to a named group.
- **Use an example** from the case in the question.
- **Evaluate** by weighing costs of acting ethically against benefits to reputation and people.
