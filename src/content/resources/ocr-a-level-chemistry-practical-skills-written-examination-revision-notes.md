## Definitions to know word for word

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | The one you deliberately change |
| Dependent variable | The one you measure |
| Controlled variable | Any other variable that could affect the result, kept constant |
| Accurate | Close to the true or accepted value |
| Precise | Repeated values are close to one another |
| Repeatable | Same person, same method and equipment, similar results |
| Reproducible | Different person, method or equipment, similar results |
| Resolution | Smallest change the instrument can show |
| Uncertainty | The range, ± a value, within which the true value lies |
| Random error | Unpredictable scatter either side; reduced by repeats |
| Systematic error | Same direction every time; repeats do not remove it |
| Anomaly | A value that does not fit the pattern of the others |
| Valid conclusion | Supported by the data and answers the question asked |

## 1.1.1 Planning

**Method in steps: writing a plan**

1. State the independent and dependent variables, with units.
2. Choose apparatus for each measurement and say why (pipette for an exact volume, gas syringe for gas volume).
3. Choose quantities: estimate the expected reading so it fits the apparatus range.
4. List controlled variables by name and say how each is controlled.
5. Describe the measurements in order, including repeats.
6. Say how the results are processed to give the answer (a calculation, or a graph and what you read from it).

**1.1.1(c) appropriate method?** Ask: does it measure the right quantity; are the readings within range and large enough for a small percentage uncertainty; is anything lost, gained or side-reacting?

Small reminder: a student plans to collect oxygen from hydrogen peroxide in a 50 cm³ syringe but expects about 80 cm³ of gas. The method is not appropriate as planned; use a 100 cm³ syringe or less peroxide.

## 1.1.2 Implementing

**Techniques (1.1.2(a)).** Expect "why" questions on titration, standard solutions, reflux, distillation, filtration, recrystallisation, separating funnels, melting points, chromatography, electrochemical cells, pH measurement and rate methods. One-line reasons:

- Rinse the burette with the titrant, the pipette with the solution it delivers.
- Fill the volumetric flask to the mark with the bottom of the meniscus on the line at eye level, then invert to mix.
- Use a white tile under the flask to see the end point.
- Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling.
- Reflux stops volatile substances escaping; distillation separates by boiling point.

**Units (1.1.2(b), M0.0).** Use cm³ or dm³, mol dm⁻³, kJ mol⁻¹, J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, s, K or °C. Work out units of Kc and k each time.

Worked reminder: Kc = [C]² ÷ ([A][B]³). Units = (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ (mol dm⁻³)⁴ = (mol dm⁻³)⁻² = **dm⁶ mol⁻²**. For rate = k[A][B], k has units **dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**.

**Presenting data (1.1.2(c)).**

- Column headings: quantity / unit. No units in the body of the table.
- Independent variable in the first column.
- Same number of decimal places down a column, matching resolution (burette: 2 d.p., ending 0 or 5).
- Observations in full: colour before and after, "effervescence", "white precipitate".

## 1.1.3 Analysis

**Significant figures (M1.1).** Give the answer to the least number of significant figures in the data used. Round once, at the end. Keep trailing zeros when converting between ordinary and standard form.

Worked reminder: 1.37 g of NaCl (M = 58.44) made up to 250.0 cm³. c = 1.37 ÷ 58.44 ÷ 0.2500 = 0.09377... The mass has 3 s.f., so **c = 0.0938 mol dm⁻³**.

**Graph rules (1.1.3(d)(i)).**

- x-axis: independent variable. Both axes: quantity / unit.
- Scale: points fill more than half the grid; steps of 1, 2 or 5; no awkward scales such as 3 per square.
- Plot points as small crosses; draw a single thin best-fit line or smooth curve.
- The origin is included only if it helps.

**Gradients and intercepts (1.1.3(d)(ii)).**

| Task | How |
|---|---|
| Gradient of a line | Δy ÷ Δx from two far-apart points on the line |
| Gradient of a curve | Draw a tangent at the point; Δy ÷ Δx of the tangent |
| Intercept, axis from zero | Read where the line crosses the y-axis |
| Intercept, axis not from zero | c = y − mx using a point on the line |
| Units of gradient | y-unit ÷ x-unit |

Worked reminder: a calibration line of absorbance against concentration passes through (0.0040 mol dm⁻³, 0.25) and (0.0240 mol dm⁻³, 1.45). Gradient = 1.20 ÷ 0.0200 = **60 dm³ mol⁻¹**. Intercept = 0.25 − 60 × 0.0040 = **0.01**, close to zero, as expected when the blank reads zero.

**Qualitative conclusions (1.1.3(a)).** Follow the specification's order for anions: carbonate, then sulfate, then halide. Conclude only what the tests show; a negative test rules an ion out, it does not identify another.

## 1.1.4 Evaluation

**Formulae**

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Uncertainty of one reading | Usually ± half the smallest division, unless stated |
| Quantity from two readings | Add the two uncertainties |
| Percentage uncertainty | (uncertainty ÷ value) × 100 |
| Result from × or ÷ | Add the percentage uncertainties |
| Percentage error | (│experimental − accepted│ ÷ accepted) × 100 |

Worked reminders:

- 10.0 cm³ pipette, ±0.04 cm³: 0.04 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = **0.40%**.
- Temperature change of 6.0 °C on a thermometer read to ±0.5 °C: two readings give ±1.0 °C, so 1.0 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **17%**. Fix: a thermometer with finer resolution, or a larger temperature change.
- Gas syringe ±0.5 cm³, 40.0 cm³ collected: **1.3%**.

**Method in steps: an evaluation answer**

1. Identify anomalies and say what you did with them.
2. Calculate the percentage error, if an accepted value is given.
3. Calculate the total percentage uncertainty.
4. Compare: error larger than uncertainty means a systematic limitation.
5. Name the specific limitation and its direction of effect.
6. Give an improvement that removes that limitation.

**Limitation → improvement pairs**

| Limitation | Improvement |
|---|---|
| Heat lost to surroundings | Lid and insulation; extrapolate the temperature–time graph |
| Gas escapes before the bung is fitted | Keep reactants apart inside a sealed flask, then mix |
| Small titre, large percentage uncertainty | Larger sample or more concentrated solution |
| End point hard to see | White tile; a more suitable indicator |
| Timing a colour change by eye | Colorimeter or data logger |
| Solid not fully dehydrated | Heat to constant mass |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Accuracy vs precision:** results can be tightly grouped yet all wrong by the same amount.
- **Random vs systematic error:** only random error falls when you repeat and average.
- **Percentage uncertainty vs percentage error:** the first comes from apparatus; the second compares your answer with an accepted value.
- **Limitation vs improvement:** the improvement must address the limitation you named.
- **Repeatable vs reproducible:** same experimenter and method, or different ones.

## Quick self-test

1. Define precision.
2. A student repeats a titration three times with very similar titres, but the mean is 5% above the accepted value. Are the results accurate, precise, both or neither?
3. Burette readings are 0.40 cm³ and 21.65 cm³, each ±0.05 cm³. Give the titre and its percentage uncertainty.
4. Give the units of Kc = [C]² ÷ ([A][B]³).
5. Write 0.000470 mol in standard form without losing significant figures.
6. Calculate the amount in 22.5 cm³ of 0.0815 mol dm⁻³ solution, to an appropriate number of significant figures.
7. Why can an intercept not be read from a graph whose x-axis starts at 2.9 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹?
8. Titres (cm³): rough 24.35, then 23.90, 24.10, 23.95, 23.85. Which do you use, and what is the mean?
9. Name one systematic error in a calorimetry experiment, and its effect on an exothermic ΔH.
10. An experimental value is 52.3 kJ mol⁻¹ and the accepted value is 57.1 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the percentage error.

### Answers

1. Repeated measurements are close to each other.
2. Precise but not accurate.
3. Titre = **21.25 cm³**; uncertainty ±0.10 cm³; 0.10 ÷ 21.25 × 100 = **0.47%**.
4. **dm⁶ mol⁻²**.
5. **4.70 × 10⁻⁴ mol**.
6. 0.0815 × 0.0225 = 0.00183375, so **1.83 × 10⁻³ mol** (3 s.f.).
7. The y-axis is not at x = 0, so the line's crossing point there is not the intercept; calculate it with c = y − mx.
8. Use 23.90, 23.95 and 23.85 (within 0.10 cm³); mean = **23.90 cm³**.
9. Heat loss to the surroundings; the measured temperature rise is too small, so ΔH is less negative than it should be.
10. (57.1 − 52.3) ÷ 57.1 × 100 = **8.4%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing "amount" or "conditions" as controlled variables without naming the quantity.
- Proposing a quantity that would overflow the apparatus, with no estimate of the expected reading.
- Units missing from table headings, or written in every cell.
- Burette readings recorded to 1 d.p. instead of 2 d.p.
- Using ±0.05 cm³ for a titre instead of ±0.10 cm³.
- Giving a final answer with more significant figures than the weakest measurement supports.
- Taking a gradient from two data points that are not on the best-fit line.
- Reading a "y-intercept" off an axis that does not start at zero.
- Saying "human error" or "use better equipment" instead of naming a limitation and a matching improvement.
- Comparing percentage error with percentage uncertainty but not saying what the comparison shows.
