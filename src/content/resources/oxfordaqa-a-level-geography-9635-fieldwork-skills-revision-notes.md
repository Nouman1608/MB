Condensed recall for **3.5.2 Fieldwork skills** (OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography 9635, Unit 5). For worked examples and annotated answers, read the [fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills/); then try the [fieldwork skills practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills-practice/).

## How fieldwork is assessed

- **Unfamiliar fieldwork questions:** apply your understanding of fieldwork techniques to a new scenario.
- **Familiar fieldwork questions:** answer about your own investigation, its methods and findings.
- Unit 5 has 60 marks; 15 assess your own investigation.
- Every student must carry out a **personal fieldwork investigation**, ideally practical.
- **Alternatives** the specification allows: enquiries in the school grounds; interviews in school or at home; working with raw, unprocessed data from the teacher, selecting samples and considering relevance and reliability.
- Being handed pre-processed data is **not** enough: there must be planning and selection.

## The five aspects of enquiry

| Stage | What the specification names |
|---|---|
| 3.5.2.1 Planning and preparation | Aims, location, a justified hypothesis or research question, appropriate methods; background research from secondary sources first; health and safety (risk assessment) |
| 3.5.2.2 Collection of data | Methods from primary and secondary sources, justified; random, systematic and structured/stratified sampling |
| 3.5.2.3 Presentation of data | Maps, graphs, photographs, field sketches, tables, interview quotations, with justification |
| 3.5.2.4 Analysis of data | Statistical techniques where appropriate, other techniques where not |
| 3.5.2.5 Conclusions and evaluation | Refer to aims; accept or reject the hypothesis; ethics and impacts of representing data; increased understanding of place and ideas; evaluate all stages; improvements; future use |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aim | A statement of what the enquiry sets out to find |
| Hypothesis | A testable prediction based on geographical theory |
| Research question | An open question, used when a prediction is not appropriate |
| Null hypothesis | A statement that there is no relationship or difference, used in statistical testing |
| Primary data | Measurements and responses gathered first-hand during the enquiry |
| Secondary data | Existing information gathered by another person or organisation, such as census statistics |
| Random sampling | Every point or person has an equal chance of selection |
| Systematic sampling | Samples at regular intervals in space or time |
| Stratified sampling | Sample divided so each known sub-group is represented in its true proportion |
| Pilot study | A small trial run to test methods and equipment |
| Accuracy | Closeness of a measurement to the true value |
| Precision | Fineness of the measurement scale |
| Reliability | Whether repeating the method gives similar results |
| Validity | Whether the method measures what the hypothesis is about |
| Risk assessment | Identifying hazards, judging likelihood and severity, setting control measures |
| Informed consent | Participants agree after being told the purpose of the study |

## Sampling at a glance

| Type | Strength | Limitation | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Avoids selection bias | May leave parts of the area unsampled | Large, uniform areas, such as a field of vegetation |
| Systematic | Even coverage; easy to repeat | May miss or repeat a regular pattern | Transects, such as down a beach or along a high street |
| Stratified | Representative of sub-groups | Needs prior knowledge of the groups | Populations with known age or land-use structure |

Each type can be **point**, **line** (transect) or **area** (quadrat) based. Calculate a stratified sample as **total sample x proportion of the group**, rounding so the totals still match.

## Writing a good hypothesis

- Names two variables or two groups.
- Predicts a direction ("decreases", "is higher than").
- Is testable with the data you can collect.
- Is justified by a named theory or concept.

## Presentation and analysis choices

- Values at named sites -> located proportional symbols or bars on a base map.
- Two continuous variables -> scatter graph, Spearman's rank.
- Counts in categories -> bar graph, chi-square.
- Changes along a profile -> cross-section or kite diagram.
- Interview responses -> coded themes, counts, short quotations.

## Ethics and impacts

- Consent and the right to refuse.
- Anonymity and secure storage of personal data.
- Avoid damage to the environment, such as trampling dunes.
- Consider how publishing results might affect a place or its residents.

## Evaluating the enquiry

Evaluate **every stage**: was the hypothesis testable, the sample large and representative enough, the equipment accurate, the timing typical, the presentation clear, the analysis appropriate, and the conclusion supported? Then give **specific** improvements and suggest how the study could be **taken forward**.

## Exam traps

- Writing an aim but no testable hypothesis.
- Naming a sampling type without justifying it for the scenario.
- Risk assessments that list hazards with no control measures.
- Using "reliable" when "accurate" or "valid" is meant.
- Conclusions that never accept or reject the hypothesis.
- Evaluations limited to "more data would be better" with no reason.
- Unfamiliar answers that ignore the details of the scenario.

## Self-test

1. In which two ways does Unit 5 assess fieldwork?
2. How many of the 60 Unit 5 marks assess the student's own investigation?
3. Give one alternative to practical fieldwork that the specification allows.
4. Why must secondary research come before data collection?
5. Distinguish reliability from validity.
6. A sample of 60 must reflect land use split 40% residential, 35% commercial and 25% industrial. How many sites in each?
7. Give one weakness of systematic sampling.
8. What is a pilot study for?
9. State three parts of a risk assessment.
10. Give two ethical issues when interviewing residents.
11. What must a conclusion do with the hypothesis?
12. Suggest one way an enquiry could be taken forward.
