This guide covers topic 3.1 **Fiscal policy** in Implementing policy, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification lists a long set of terms: the government budget; direct, indirect, progressive, proportional and regressive taxation; current and capital government expenditure; budget surplus, deficit and balanced budget; the cyclical and structural budget position; national and government debt; discretionary fiscal policy and automatic stabilisers; crowding out; and the Laffer curve. You must also explain and calculate average and marginal tax rates and evaluate the effectiveness of fiscal policy in achieving the government's macroeconomic objectives. All tax systems and figures below are invented.

## 1. The government budget

**Fiscal policy** is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. The **budget** is the government's plan of revenue and spending for the coming year.

Spending is split into two kinds.

- **Current expenditure** is spending on day-to-day running costs: public sector wages, medicines, school supplies and benefit payments. It is used up within the year.
- **Capital expenditure** is spending on long-lasting assets: roads, hospitals, school buildings and broadband networks. It adds to the economy's productive capacity.

The distinction matters for evaluation. A cut in capital spending reduces AD now and LRAS later, while a cut in current spending mostly affects AD.

## 2. Types of tax

| Classification | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | Levied on income or wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm taxed | Income tax, corporation tax |
| **Indirect** | Levied on spending, collected by sellers and passed to the government | VAT, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco |
| **Progressive** | The average tax rate rises as income rises | Income tax with higher rates on higher bands |
| **Proportional** | The average tax rate is the same at all incomes | A single flat rate on all income |
| **Regressive** | The average tax rate falls as income rises | Most indirect taxes, measured against income |

The three-way classification is always judged against **income** and by the **average** rate, not the marginal rate.

## 3. Average and marginal tax rates

- **Average tax rate (ATR)** = total tax paid / total income x 100.
- **Marginal tax rate (MTR)** = extra tax paid / extra income x 100, the rate on the next unit of income.

**Worked example 1.** An invented income tax has these bands:

| Band of annual income | Rate |
|---|---|
| 0 to 12,000 | 0% |
| 12,001 to 50,000 | 20% |
| Above 50,000 | 40% |

*Person A earns 30,000.* Tax = 20% x (30,000 - 12,000) = 3,600. ATR = 3,600 / 30,000 = **12.0%**. MTR = **20%**.

*Person B earns 80,000.* Tax = 20% x 38,000 + 40% x 30,000 = 7,600 + 12,000 = 19,600. ATR = 19,600 / 80,000 = **24.5%**. MTR = **40%**.

The ATR rises with income, so the tax is progressive. Note that B does not pay 40% on all income, a frequent error. For anyone earning above the tax-free band, the MTR exceeds the ATR, because the first slice of income is taxed at a lower rate.

**Worked example 2: why indirect taxes are regressive.** A 20% sales tax is charged on all spending. Household C earns 20,000 and spends 15,000 before tax, paying 3,000 in tax: **15%** of income. Household D earns 100,000 and spends 40,000, paying 8,000: **8%** of income. The rate on spending is the same, but richer households spend a smaller share of income, so the tax takes a smaller share of their income.

## 4. Budget positions

- **Budget deficit:** spending exceeds revenue in a year.
- **Budget surplus:** revenue exceeds spending.
- **Balanced budget:** revenue equals spending.

The actual budget position can be split into two parts.

- The **cyclical** position is the part caused by the economic cycle. In a recession tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises, so the deficit grows; in a boom the reverse happens. It disappears as the economy returns to normal output.
- The **structural** position is the part that would remain even with output at its normal level. It reflects long-term decisions about spending and tax rates.

**Worked example 3.** Country K runs a deficit of 6% of GDP during a recession. Economists estimate that 2.5% of GDP is cyclical. The structural deficit is 6 - 2.5 = **3.5% of GDP**. When the economy recovers, the deficit will fall towards 3.5% on its own, but closing the rest needs discretionary action: higher taxes or lower spending.

## 5. National and government debt

The **national debt** is the total stock of debt the government owes, built up from past deficits less any repayments. It is usually reported as **government (public sector) debt** and expressed as a percentage of GDP. The **deficit** is a flow (borrowing this year); the **debt** is a stock (the total owed).

**Worked example 4.** Country L has debt of 1,000 billion and nominal GDP of 1,250 billion: a ratio of **80%**. It borrows 50 billion this year while nominal GDP grows by 4% to 1,300 billion. Debt becomes 1,050 billion and the ratio becomes 1,050 / 1,300 = **80.8%**. A deficit raises the stock of debt, but whether the debt ratio rises also depends on how fast GDP grows.

## 6. Automatic stabilisers and discretionary policy

**Automatic stabilisers** change the budget without any new decision. In a downturn, income tax and VAT receipts fall and spending on unemployment-related benefits rises. This cushions the fall in disposable income and AD. In a boom, rising tax receipts and falling benefit spending slow the growth of AD. Progressive income tax strengthens the effect.

**Discretionary fiscal policy** is a deliberate change in tax rates or spending, such as a cut in VAT or a new infrastructure programme.

- **Expansionary** (or reflationary) policy raises G or cuts T, shifting AD right.
- **Contractionary** (or deflationary) policy cuts G or raises T, shifting AD left.

Fiscal policy can also act on the supply side. Lower marginal income tax rates may raise incentives to work, and capital spending on infrastructure raises capacity, shifting LRAS right.

## 7. Crowding out

**Crowding out** is the idea that higher government spending reduces private sector spending, so AD rises by less than expected.

- **Financial crowding out.** To fund a deficit the government borrows by selling bonds. Higher demand for loanable funds can push up interest rates, which reduces private investment and consumption.
- **Resource crowding out.** If the economy is at full capacity, the workers and materials used by the state must come from the private sector.

Crowding out is most likely near full employment and least likely in a deep recession, when private saving is high and resources are idle.

## 8. The Laffer curve

The **Laffer curve** shows the relationship between the tax rate and total tax revenue. Revenue is zero at a 0% rate (nothing is collected) and close to zero at 100% (there is no incentive to earn taxable income). Between the two, revenue reaches a maximum.

- Below the revenue-maximising rate, raising the rate raises revenue.
- Above it, raising the rate **reduces** revenue, because people work less, avoid tax or evade it, so a rate cut could raise revenue.

**Evaluation.** Nobody knows exactly where the peak is, and it differs by tax and by country. Supporters use the curve to argue for lower top rates; critics point out that most tax rates may already be below the peak, so cuts would reduce revenue.

## 9. Evaluating the effectiveness of fiscal policy

When judging whether fiscal policy can achieve growth, low unemployment, low inflation and a sustainable balance of payments, use these points.

- **Time lags.** Recognising a problem, passing a budget and implementing spending all take time; a stimulus may arrive after recovery has begun.
- **Size and timing of the effect.** Spending on infrastructure can take years to start. A temporary tax cut may be largely saved.
- **Spare capacity.** With high unemployment, a stimulus raises output; near full capacity it mainly raises inflation.
- **Crowding out** may reduce the net effect on AD.
- **Debt and confidence.** High debt can limit the scope for further borrowing and may raise interest rates if lenders become worried.
- **Conflicts.** Expansion can worsen the current account as imports rise.
- **Supply-side benefits.** Well-chosen capital spending and tax reform can raise capacity, helping long-run growth without lasting inflation.

## Common mistakes

- Applying the top marginal rate to all income.
- Calling VAT progressive because richer people pay more in total: the test is the share of income.
- Confusing the deficit (flow) with the debt (stock).
- Treating automatic stabilisers as discretionary policy.
- Assuming the Laffer curve proves tax cuts raise revenue.
- Ignoring crowding out and spare capacity when judging effectiveness.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the tax in each band, then the ATR or MTR, with units.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw AD shifting for demand-side changes, or LRAS for supply-side changes, or sketch the Laffer curve with labelled axes (tax rate, tax revenue).
- **Evaluate:** weigh time lags, spare capacity, crowding out and debt, then judge against the specific objective in the question.
