Subtopic 4.2 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 has just two learning outcomes. You must "state that a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity with water as the only chemical product", and "describe the advantages and disadvantages of using hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells in comparison with gasoline / petrol engines in vehicles".

The facts are few, but the second outcome is an extended-answer favourite, and marks go to answers that make precise, chemical comparisons rather than vague claims about being "better for the environment". This guide gives you the chemistry behind each point so you can explain it, not just list it. The wider [Electrolysis and Fuel Cells study guide](/resources/electrolysis-and-fuel-cells/) places the fuel cell in Topic 4 as a whole. For quick recall use the [fuel cells revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-hydrogen-oxygen-fuel-cells-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [fuel cells practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-hydrogen-oxygen-fuel-cells-practice/).

## What a fuel cell does

A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell is a device that turns the chemical energy of a reaction between hydrogen and oxygen **directly into electrical energy**. Hydrogen gas is fed to one electrode and oxygen (usually from the air) to the other. As long as both gases keep being supplied, the cell keeps producing electricity.

The overall reaction is:

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

That is the same overall equation as burning hydrogen, but nothing is burned. There is no flame; the two gases are kept apart and react at separate electrodes. Water is the **only chemical product**, which is the exact wording of the first outcome.

## Where the electricity comes from

The reaction in a fuel cell is a redox reaction, so it connects to subtopic 6.4. Hydrogen loses electrons, so it is **oxidised**; oxygen gains electrons, so it is **reduced**. Because the gases react at different electrodes, the electrons released by hydrogen cannot pass straight to oxygen. They travel through the external circuit, and that flow of electrons is the electric current that drives a motor.

The reaction is also strongly exothermic (subtopic 5.1). In a fuel cell a large share of the energy released is transferred as electrical energy rather than as thermal energy.

## Three devices that are easy to confuse

| Device | Energy change | Reactants supplied? | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrolysis of water (as in dilute sulfuric acid) | Electrical energy in, decomposes a compound | Electrolyte in the cell | Hydrogen and oxygen |
| Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell | Chemical energy out as electrical energy | Hydrogen and oxygen supplied continuously | Water only |
| Petrol engine | Chemical energy released as thermal energy by combustion, then movement | Petrol and air supplied continuously | Carbon dioxide and water, plus pollutants |

A fuel cell is in one sense the reverse of the electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid, which you meet in subtopic 4.1: electrolysis uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen in a 2 : 1 volume ratio, and the fuel cell recombines them in the same 2 : 1 ratio to release electricity.

## What a petrol engine produces

To compare fairly, you need to know what the alternative releases. Petrol (gasoline) is a mixture of hydrocarbons obtained from petroleum. Burned in a car engine, it produces:

- **carbon dioxide** from complete combustion, a greenhouse gas linked to climate change
- **carbon monoxide** from incomplete combustion, a toxic gas
- **oxides of nitrogen**, formed when nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high temperature inside the engine; these contribute to acid rain and photochemical smog
- **unburned hydrocarbons** and **particulates** (soot)

None of these is produced by a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell when it is running.

## Advantages of fuel cells compared with petrol engines

- **Water is the only product.** No carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide or oxides of nitrogen are released by the vehicle.
- **No crude oil is needed as the fuel.** Petrol comes from petroleum, a finite resource; hydrogen can be made from water by electrolysis.
- **More efficient.** Chemical energy is converted directly into electrical energy, with fewer energy-transfer stages than burning fuel to drive pistons, so less energy is wasted as heat.
- **Fewer moving parts and quieter running**, because there are no explosions in cylinders.

## Disadvantages of fuel cells compared with petrol engines

- **Hydrogen is a gas and hard to store.** It must be kept in strong, heavy tanks under high pressure, or liquefied at a very low temperature. Petrol is a liquid at room temperature and is stored easily in a simple tank.
- **Hydrogen is highly flammable** and forms explosive mixtures with air, so leaks from high-pressure tanks are a serious hazard.
- **Making hydrogen has its own costs.** Much hydrogen is made from methane, the same source used for the Haber process in subtopic 6.3, and that releases carbon dioxide. Making it by electrolysis of water needs electricity, which may itself come from burning fossil fuels.
- **Few refuelling stations**, compared with the established network for petrol.
- **Fuel cells and fuel-cell vehicles are expensive** to manufacture.

## Putting numbers on the storage problem

You can use subtopic 3.3 to show why storage matters. Suppose a vehicle uses 1.00 kg of hydrogen.

1. Amount of H₂ = 1000 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 500 mol.
2. Volume at r.t.p. = 500 × 24 = 12 000 dm³, which is 12 m³ of gas.
3. Oxygen needed = 500 ÷ 2 = 250 mol, which the cell takes from the air.
4. Water produced = 500 mol × 18 g/mol = 9000 g = **9.00 kg**.

Twelve cubic metres of gas at room conditions is far too large to carry, which is why hydrogen must be compressed to high pressure. A kilogram of petrol, being a liquid, takes up less than 2 dm³.

## Worked model answer

**Question.** Describe the advantages and disadvantages of using hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells instead of petrol engines in cars. **[6]**

**Model answer.**

*Advantages:* a fuel cell's only chemical product is water, whereas a petrol engine releases carbon dioxide, which contributes to climate change, and carbon monoxide, which is toxic. A fuel cell produces no oxides of nitrogen, which petrol engines form at high temperatures and which cause acid rain. Hydrogen does not have to come from crude oil, which is non-renewable.

*Disadvantages:* hydrogen is a gas, so it is harder to store than liquid petrol and must be kept under high pressure in heavy tanks. Hydrogen is very flammable, so leaks are dangerous. Most hydrogen is made from methane, which releases carbon dioxide, or by electrolysis using electricity that may come from fossil fuels. There are few hydrogen filling stations.

**Why it scores:** each point names a specific substance or property and states the comparison with petrol. There is a balance of advantages and disadvantages, which the question demands.

## Common mistakes

- **"Fuel cells cause no pollution."** The car releases none, but making and compressing the hydrogen can. Say "at the point of use" or name what is not released.
- **"The hydrogen is burned."** It reacts at an electrode without combustion; there is no flame.
- **"Fuel cells produce carbon dioxide."** There is no carbon in either reactant, so carbon dioxide cannot form in the cell.
- **Calling water vapour a pollutant** produced by the cell. The syllabus treats water as the harmless, only product.
- **One-sided answers.** A question asking for advantages and disadvantages will cap the marks if only one side is given.
- **Vague comparisons** such as "it is cheaper" or "it is better". Name the property and the reason.
- **Treating a fuel cell as a rechargeable battery.** It is not recharged; it runs as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied.

## Exam technique

- **State:** a short fact, such as "water is the only chemical product". No explanation needed.
- **Describe** (advantages and disadvantages): write separate points, each one comparing the fuel cell with petrol. Aim for at least as many distinct points as there are marks, split between both sides.
- If the question supplies data, such as the energy released per kilogram of each fuel, use the figures in your comparison rather than general statements.
