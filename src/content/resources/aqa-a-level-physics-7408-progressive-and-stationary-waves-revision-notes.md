## Equations

| Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| c = fλ | Wave speed = frequency × wavelength |
| f = 1/T | Frequency is the reciprocal of the period |
| Δφ = 2πx/λ | Phase difference between points x apart (radians) |
| Δφ = 2πt/T | Phase difference between oscillations t apart in time |
| f = (1/2l)√(T/μ) | First harmonic of a string of length l, tension T, mass per unit length μ |
| fₙ = n f₁ | nth harmonic on a string fixed at both ends |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Progressive wave | Transfers energy without transferring matter |
| Amplitude | Maximum displacement from equilibrium |
| Wavelength | Distance between adjacent points in phase |
| Phase difference | Fraction of a cycle by which one oscillation leads another, in rad or degrees |
| In phase / antiphase | Phase difference 0 (or 2π) / π rad |
| Transverse wave | Oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer |
| Longitudinal wave | Oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer |
| Plane-polarised | Oscillations in one plane only |
| Superposition | Resultant displacement = vector sum of individual displacements |
| Stationary wave | Formed by two waves of the same frequency travelling in opposite directions |
| Node / antinode | Point of zero / maximum amplitude |
| First harmonic | Lowest-frequency stationary wave: one loop, l = λ/2 |

## Phase difference quick table

| Separation | Fraction of cycle | Radians | Degrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| λ/4 | 1/4 | π/2 | 90° |
| λ/2 | 1/2 | π | 180° |
| 3λ/4 | 3/4 | 3π/2 | 270° |
| λ | 1 | 2π | 360° (in phase) |

Worked reminder: f = 680 Hz, c = 340 m s⁻¹, so λ = 0.50 m. Points 0.125 m apart are λ/4 apart: **π/2 rad**.

## Polarisation

- Only transverse waves can be polarised, so polarisation is evidence that light (and all electromagnetic radiation) is transverse.
- Two Polaroids: intensity is a maximum when their axes are parallel and zero when they are at 90°; zero twice per full rotation.
- Applications: Polaroid sunglasses cut glare from reflected light; aerials are aligned with the plane of polarisation of the signal.
- Sound is longitudinal, so it cannot be polarised.

## Stationary waves

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Node to node | λ/2 |
| Node to adjacent antinode | λ/4 |
| Phase between adjacent nodes | All in phase |
| Phase across a node | Antiphase |
| Energy transfer | None |

**String fixed at both ends**: nodes at each end. Harmonic n has n loops, λₙ = 2l/n, fₙ = n f₁.

Worked reminder: l = 0.75 m, μ = 2.4 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹, T = 60 N. √(T/μ) = 158 m s⁻¹, so f₁ = 158 / 1.5 = **105 Hz**.

**Proportionalities**: f₁ ∝ 1/l; f₁ ∝ √T; f₁ ∝ 1/√μ.

| Change | Effect on f₁ |
|---|---|
| Double l | Halves |
| Double T | × √2 (about 1.41) |
| × 4 T | Doubles |
| × 4 μ | Halves |

**Microwaves and sound**: a reflector sets up a stationary wave; a probe or microphone finds minima (nodes) λ/2 apart. Count several spacings and divide to reduce uncertainty.

## Required practical 1

**Method in steps**
1. Set up string, pulley, hanging masses (T = mg) and vibration generator.
2. Adjust the signal generator until one loop (first harmonic) is clear and steady.
3. Record f; measure l between the vibrator and the pulley (the two nodes).
4. Vary l, T or μ, one at a time, keeping the others constant.
5. Plot f against 1/l (gradient ½√(T/μ)) or f against √T (gradient 1/(2l√μ)).

Sources of uncertainty: judging the exact frequency for the largest amplitude; locating the nodes; the value of μ (weigh a long length to reduce it).

## Exam traps

- Taking node spacing as λ instead of λ/2.
- Phase difference with x in cm and λ in m.
- "Stationary waves transfer energy."
- "All points on a stationary wave oscillate with the same amplitude."
- Doubling T and doubling f.
- Using the hanging mass in kg as the tension.
- Describing polarisation of sound.
- Forgetting that points either side of a node are in antiphase.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Progressive vs stationary**: energy transferred vs stored; constant vs varying amplitude; varying phase vs in-phase loops.
- **Transverse vs longitudinal**: perpendicular vs parallel oscillation; crests vs compressions.
- **Wave speed vs particle speed**: the wave moves at c; particles only oscillate.

## Quick self-test

1. A wave has period 2.5 ms. Find its frequency.
2. A wave of frequency 200 Hz has speed 1500 m s⁻¹. Find its wavelength.
3. Two points are 3λ/4 apart. State their phase difference in radians.
4. One point reaches its maximum 4 ms after another. The period is 20 ms. Find the phase difference.
5. Explain why sound cannot be polarised.
6. A second Polaroid is rotated from parallel to the first by 90°. What is the transmitted intensity?
7. State two conditions for a stationary wave to form.
8. Adjacent nodes of a stationary microwave are 1.5 cm apart. Find λ and f.
9. A string's first harmonic is 120 Hz. State the frequency of its third harmonic.
10. The tension in a string is increased from 40 N to 90 N. By what factor does f₁ change?
11. What is the phase difference between two points in the same loop of a stationary wave?
12. In Required practical 1, what graph shows f ∝ 1/l?

### Answers

1. f = 1/T = **400 Hz**.
2. λ = c/f = **7.5 m**.
3. **3π/2 rad**.
4. 2π × 4/20 = **0.4π rad (1.26 rad)**.
5. Its oscillations are parallel to its direction of travel, so they cannot be restricted to one plane.
6. **Zero**.
7. Two waves of the same frequency travelling in opposite directions (usually a wave and its reflection), superposing.
8. λ = **3.0 cm**; f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.030 = **1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz**.
9. **360 Hz**.
10. √(90/40) = **1.5**.
11. **Zero**: they are in phase.
12. **f against 1/l**: a straight line through the origin.

## Frequently asked questions

**How can you tell the first harmonic is set up in Required practical 1?**
The string forms a single loop between the vibrator and the pulley, with nodes only at the two ends and the largest amplitude at the centre.

**Why are minima in a microwave stationary wave not quite zero?**
The reflected wave is a little weaker than the incident wave, so the two do not cancel completely at the nodes.
