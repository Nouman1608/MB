This guide covers **Theme 1 Identity and Culture**, the first of
three themes in OxfordAQA International GCSE Urdu (9264), for
International GCSE exams May/June 2026 onwards. Paper 1 (Reading,
Grammar and Meaning) and Paper 2 (Writing) are each 1 hour 45 minutes,
70 marks and 50% of the GCSE. Content is organised around three themes
examined across both papers, rather than paper-specific topics.

## Where this fits in 9264

Identity and Culture introduces vocabulary and grammatical structures
around personal, family and social life, forming the linguistic
foundation that Theme 2 (Local, National, International and Global
Areas of Interest) and Theme 3 (Current and Future Study and
Employment) build on with more complex, less personal subject matter.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE URDU (9264) — THEME 1 IDENTITY AND
CULTURE**

- 1.1 Me, My Family, Friends and People I Know — describing yourself,

- 1.2 Technology in Everyday Life — vocabulary and structures for
discussing technology use
- 1.3 Free-Time Activities — vocabulary and structures for discussing

## How to approach it

Because this theme is examined across both the reading paper and the
writing paper, build active vocabulary (able to produce, not just
recognise) alongside passive reading vocabulary from the start, since
Paper 2 requires productive use of the same content Paper 1 tests
receptively. Family, friends and free-time vocabulary (1.1, 1.3) is
the most personal and most frequently reused content across GCSE-level
language exams, so prioritise fluency here early, since it also
supports more complex writing tasks later in the course. Practising
short, structured written responses on each sub-topic -- rather than
only vocabulary lists -- builds the sentence-level accuracy that Paper
2 rewards. Technology in Everyday Life (1.2) is a useful sub-topic for
practising more abstract opinion-giving language -- likes, dislikes
and reasons -- which extends naturally into the more complex themes
that follow.

## The theme and how it is assessed

**Identity and Culture** is the first of the themes on which the qualification is built. It covers who you are, the people around you, and the cultural life you take part in — family and relationships, friendships, daily routine, food and health, festivals and celebrations, technology in everyday life, and free time.

The theme provides the **contexts** for assessment. What is actually being tested is your ability to understand and produce Urdu across the four skills, so a strong answer is one that uses the language accurately and appropriately, not one that displays cultural knowledge for its own sake.

## Vocabulary you need in place

Preparation for this theme is mostly lexical. Build reliable, active vocabulary in these fields:

- **Family and relationships** — relatives, describing people's appearance and character, getting on and falling out.
- **Daily routine** — times, frequency adverbs, household activities, school and work routine.
- **Food and health** — meals, ingredients, dietary habits, illness, exercise, healthy living.
- **Festivals and traditions** — Eid, weddings, national days, customs, hospitality.
- **Free time** — sport, music, reading, television, going out.
- **Technology** — mobile phones, social media, the internet, with the vocabulary of advantages and disadvantages.

Active vocabulary is what you can produce, not merely recognise. Recognition alone will carry you through reading but fail you in writing and speaking.

## Structures that raise the grade

Accurate simple language beats ambitious language that collapses — but a narrow range caps your marks. Aim to deploy, correctly:

- Present, past and future tenses, with consistent person and gender agreement.
- **Opinion phrases** with reasons — my view is, in my opinion, because, therefore.
- **Comparatives** — better than, the best, more difficult than.
- **Connectives** — and, but, however, moreover, for this reason.
- **Time markers** — usually, sometimes, never, last week, next year.

Extending an answer by giving a reason and an example is the single most reliable way to move up a band.

## Approaching the tasks

For **reading and listening**, identify whether the question wants a detail, an opinion or an inference. For opinion questions, look for evaluative language rather than facts.

For **writing and speaking**, cover every bullet point in the task — an unaddressed bullet caps the mark regardless of quality elsewhere. Give an opinion **with a reason** wherever the task allows, and vary your tenses deliberately.

## Worked example

Task: *Describe a festival you celebrate and say why it is important to you.*

```
Weak:   lists what happens -- we wear new clothes, we eat sweets
        one tense, no opinion, no reason

Strong: describes the festival (present tense)
        recalls a particular occasion (past tense)
        says what you will do next year (future tense)
        gives an opinion WITH a reason -- it matters because it brings
          the family together, and my grandparents visit
        adds one specific detail that only you would write
```

Three tenses, an opinion, a reason and a personal detail — that is the recipe for the top band.

## Common mistakes

Missing a bullet point in the task. Writing entirely in the present tense. Giving opinions with no reason. Losing gender agreement in adjectives and past-tense verbs. Memorising a passage and forcing it in regardless of the question. Using ambitious vocabulary inaccurately when a simpler correct word would score better.

## Quick revision checklist

- Build active vocabulary across all six sub-topics of the theme.
- Use three tenses accurately and consistently, watching gender agreement.
- Give every opinion a reason, and where possible an example.
- Cover every bullet point in a writing or speaking task.
- Distinguish detail, opinion and inference questions in reading and listening.
- Practise extending short answers with reasons, comparisons and connectives.
