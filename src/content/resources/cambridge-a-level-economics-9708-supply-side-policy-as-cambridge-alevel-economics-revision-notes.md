Condensed notes for **section 5.4 Supply-side policy** (AS Level, Cambridge 9708, 2026–2028). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [supply-side policy study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-supply-side-policy-as-cambridge-alevel-economics/). Figures are invented.

## The four syllabus statements

| Statement | What to recall |
|---|---|
| 5.4.1 Meaning | Policy to raise productive capacity, shown as a rightward shift of LRAS |
| 5.4.2 Objectives | Increase productivity and productive capacity |
| 5.4.3 Tools | For example training, infrastructure development, support for technological improvement |
| 5.4.4 AD/AS | Effect on equilibrium national income, real output, price level and employment |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| LRAS | The output the economy can produce when all resources are fully employed |
| SRAS | Output firms supply at each price level when costs of production are given |
| Productive capacity | The maximum output achievable with existing resources and technology |
| Labour productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked |
| Unit labour cost | Wage cost per unit of output: wage per hour ÷ output per hour |
| Human capital | The skills, knowledge and health of the workforce |
| Infrastructure | Shared capital such as transport, energy and communication networks |
| Spare capacity | The gap between full-capacity output and actual output |

## Formulae

```
Labour productivity  = output / hours worked   (or / workers)
Unit labour cost     = wage per hour / output per hour
% change             = (new - old) / old x 100
```

Example: output 9,000 units from 6,000 hours at a wage of 12 per hour. Productivity = 1.5 units per hour; unit labour cost = 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8. If productivity rises to 1.6, unit labour cost = 7.50, a fall of 6.25%.

## Tools: mechanism in one line each

| Tool | Chain |
|---|---|
| Training and education | Better skills → more output per worker → lower unit costs and higher capacity → SRAS and LRAS right |
| Infrastructure | Faster transport, cheaper energy, better communication → lower costs for all firms → SRAS and LRAS right |
| Support for technological improvement | Research grants, tax allowances → new methods and products → higher efficiency → LRAS right |

Other measures count if you show the link to productivity or capacity, for example better health care that reduces days lost to illness.

## Two drawings of LRAS

- **Vertical:** the whole line moves right, from full-capacity output Yf1 to Yf2.
- **Three sections:** the upward-sloping and vertical sections move right; the economy reaches full capacity at a higher output.

## AD/AS outcomes

| Situation | Real output | Price level | Employment |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRAS and LRAS shift right, AD unchanged | Rises | Falls | Usually rises, by less than output if productivity rose |
| Spending on the measure shifts AD right first | Rises | May rise | Rises |
| Economy in deep recession (highly elastic section) | Little immediate change | Little change | Little immediate change; capacity rises for later |

## Timeline of one measure: a new port

```
Years 1-3  Construction spending (G)    -> AD shifts right; jobs in building
Years 4+   Port opens, shipping cheaper -> unit costs fall; SRAS shifts right
Long run   More trade capacity          -> LRAS shifts right; potential output up
```

Answers that separate the short-run demand effect from the long-run supply effect show clear analysis.

## Evaluation checklist

- **Time lags:** training, research and building take years.
- **Cost and opportunity cost:** spending here means less elsewhere, or more borrowing or taxes.
- **Uncertain results:** skills may not match jobs; research may fail.
- **Demand:** new capacity is wasted if AD is too weak to use it.
- **Employment:** productivity gains can reduce the workers needed if demand does not grow.
- **Strength:** the only policy type that raises output while lowering inflationary pressure in the long run.

## Exam traps

- Shifting AD only, or SRAS only, for a capacity-raising measure.
- Saying supply-side policy works quickly.
- Confusing more output with higher productivity.
- Inverting the unit labour cost formula.
- Listing tools without the factor of production each improves.
- Using the market-based versus interventionist distinction at AS without explaining the tool; that distinction is A Level content.

## Self-test

1. Define supply-side policy in terms of LRAS.
2. State the two objectives of supply-side policy named in the syllabus.
3. Give the formula for unit labour cost.
4. A firm produces 4,800 units in 3,200 hours. Calculate labour productivity.
5. If the wage in question 4 is 18 per hour, calculate unit labour cost.
6. Why might a training scheme shift both SRAS and LRAS?
7. Why does building a new port shift AD before it shifts LRAS?
8. What happens to the price level when LRAS and SRAS shift right and AD is unchanged?
9. Why may supply-side policy have little immediate effect on output in a deep recession?
10. Give one reason why higher productivity might not raise employment.
11. Name one tool not listed in the syllabus that could still count as supply-side policy, and explain the link.
12. State one drawback of support for technological improvement.

**Answers:** 1. Government action to raise productive capacity, shown as a rightward shift of LRAS. 2. Increasing productivity and increasing productive capacity. 3. Wage per hour ÷ output per hour. 4. 4,800 ÷ 3,200 = 1.5 units per hour. 5. 18 ÷ 1.5 = 12 per unit. 6. Higher output per worker lowers costs per unit (SRAS) and raises the maximum the economy can produce (LRAS). 7. Construction spending is government spending, part of AD, and the extra capacity exists only once the port opens. 8. It falls, while real output rises. 9. The economy already has spare capacity, so more capacity does not raise actual output unless AD rises. 10. If demand does not grow as fast as productivity, firms can meet demand with fewer workers. 11. For example better health care: fewer days lost to illness raise output per worker. 12. Results are uncertain, or firms may receive support for research they would have done anyway.
