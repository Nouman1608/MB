These notes condense section 3.2, Households, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: the influences on spending, saving and borrowing, including income, the rate of interest and confidence, between different households and over time. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked examples read the [households study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households-cambridge-igcse-economics/), then practise with the [households practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households-cambridge-igcse-economics-practice/). The combined [money, banking and household spending revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-money-banking-and-household-spending-saving-and-revision-notes/) link this section to 3.1.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Disposable income | Income after direct taxes, plus state benefits; what a household can spend or save |
| Spending (consumption) | Buying goods and services for current use |
| Saving | Disposable income not spent in a period |
| Savings | The total amount saved and built up over time (a stock) |
| Borrowing | Taking a loan so that spending can exceed current income |
| Dissaving | Spending more than income by running down past savings |
| Rate of interest | The reward for saving and the cost of borrowing, as a percentage a year |
| Confidence | How optimistic households are about future jobs, income and the economy |
| Wealth | The value of assets a household owns, such as a house or savings |

## Core relationships

```
Disposable income = spending + saving
Saving ratio (%) = saving / disposable income x 100
```

Worked reminder: disposable income 2,400, spending 2,040.

```
Saving = 2,400 - 2,040 = 360
Saving ratio = 360 / 2,400 x 100 = 15%
```

## The three named influences

| Influence | Rises | Spending | Saving | Borrowing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Up | Up | Up (often a larger share) | Less needed |
| Rate of interest | Up | Down (usually) | Up | Down |
| Confidence | Up | Up | Down | Up |

### Rate of interest: three groups

- **New borrowers:** loans dearer, so fewer purchases on credit.
- **Existing borrowers on variable rates:** repayments rise, so less left to spend.
- **Savers living on interest:** more income, so some may spend more.

Worked reminder: a variable-rate loan of 60,000 (interest only) at 5% costs 3,000 a year; at 7% it costs 4,200, so 1,200 a year (100 a month) less is left for other spending.

### Confidence

- Falls with news of job losses, recession or rising prices.
- Low confidence → precautionary saving, delayed purchases of durable goods, less borrowing.
- Can change spending before income changes.

## Other influences (credit if relevant)

- Wealth, for example rising house values.
- Availability and ease of getting credit.
- Taxes on income.
- Age, size and stage of the household.
- Attitudes, habits and culture.
- Expectations of future prices.

## Between different households

| Feature | Higher spending share / borrowing | Higher saving share |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Low income | High income |
| Age | Young adults; retired (spending savings) | Middle-aged |
| Dependants | Many children | Few or none |
| Debt | Large variable-rate loans (hit by rate rises) | Large deposits (gain from rate rises) |
| Job security | Secure job, high confidence | Insecure job, low confidence |

## Over time

- **Life stages:** borrow when young, save in middle age, dissave in retirement.
- **Boom:** incomes and confidence high, so spending and borrowing rise.
- **Recession:** job losses and low confidence, so spending falls and those who can save more.
- **Interest rate changes:** cheaper loans encourage borrowing; dearer loans discourage it.

Worked reminder: income rises from 2,000 to 2,100 (5%) while spending falls from 1,800 to 1,764 (-2%). Saving rises from 200 (10%) to 336 (16%). A change in confidence or interest rates, not income, explains the fall in spending.

## Exam traps

- **Saving versus savings.** Saving is a flow per period; savings is a stock.
- **Share versus amount.** A poor household may save a smaller amount *and* a smaller share; say which you mean.
- **Interest rates and savers.** Do not claim every household spends less after a rate rise.
- **Confidence needs a reason.** Link it to expected jobs or income.
- **Answer for the household described.** If the question names a retired couple, discuss savings and interest income, not mortgages.
- **Borrowing is not extra income.** It must be repaid with interest, reducing future spending.

## Evaluation prompts

- A rate rise may barely affect spending if confidence is high or most households have fixed-rate loans.
- A pay rise may raise saving more than spending if households fear future job losses.
- A fall in confidence may reduce spending even when incomes are rising.

## Self-test

1. Define disposable income.
2. Distinguish saving from savings.
3. A household has disposable income of 1,800 and saves 216. Calculate its saving ratio.
4. State two effects of a fall in the rate of interest on households.
5. Why might a retired couple spend more when interest rates rise?
6. Give two reasons why a fall in confidence reduces spending.
7. Why do high-income households usually save a larger share of income?
8. State one reason why a young adult may borrow.
9. A household's interest-only loan of 80,000 has its rate rise from 3% to 5%. Calculate the extra yearly cost.
10. Name one influence on spending other than income, interest and confidence.
11. Explain how a recession affects household borrowing.
12. Income rises 5% but spending falls. Suggest one explanation.

### Answers

1. Income after direct taxes, plus any state benefits; the amount available to spend or save.
2. Saving is the part of income not spent in a period; savings is the total accumulated over time.
3. 216 / 1,800 x 100 = 12%.
4. Any two: borrowing cheaper so more loans; repayments on variable-rate debts fall so more to spend; saving less rewarding so less saved.
5. Their income from interest on savings rises, so they have more to spend.
6. Households fear losing jobs so save as a cushion; they delay buying expensive durable goods; they avoid new debt.
7. Necessities take a smaller share of their income, so more is left after essential spending.
8. Any one: current income is low but expected to rise; to pay for education, a home or a car.
9. 80,000 x 0.05 - 80,000 x 0.03 = 4,000 - 2,400 = 1,600.
10. Any one: wealth, availability of credit, taxation, age, household size, attitudes, price expectations.
11. Falling incomes and confidence make households unwilling to take on debt, and banks are more cautious about lending, so borrowing falls.
12. A fall in confidence, a rise in interest rates or a rise in taxes.
