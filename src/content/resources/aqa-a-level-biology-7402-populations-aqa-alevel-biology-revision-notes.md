Condensed notes for AQA A-level Biology (7402) section **3.7.2 Populations**. Derivation of the equation and worked examples on dominant phenotypes, codominance and generation changes are in the [populations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-populations-aqa-alevel-biology/). Test yourself with the [populations practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-populations-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population | A group of organisms of the same species occupying the same space at the same time that can potentially interbreed |
| Gene pool | All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time |
| Allele frequency | The proportion of all copies of a gene in the gene pool that are a particular allele |
| Hardy–Weinberg principle | A mathematical model predicting that allele frequencies will not change from generation to generation |

Species exist as **one or more populations**, each with its own gene pool.

## The two equations

**p + q = 1** (allele frequencies)

**p² + 2pq + q² = 1** (genotype frequencies)

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| p | Frequency of the dominant allele |
| q | Frequency of the recessive allele |
| p² | Frequency of homozygous dominant |
| 2pq | Frequency of heterozygotes |
| q² | Frequency of homozygous recessive (= recessive phenotype) |
| p² + 2pq | Frequency of dominant phenotype |

Origin: random combination of gametes gives (p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q².

## Conditions for the principle to apply

1. Large population (no genetic drift).
2. Random mating.
3. No mutation.
4. No migration in or out.
5. No selection: all genotypes survive and reproduce equally.

Mismatch between observed and predicted → at least one condition not met.

## Calculation routes

**Route A: recessive phenotype given (dominance)**

1. q² = recessive phenotype frequency.
2. q = √q².
3. p = 1 − q.
4. Find p², 2pq or p² + 2pq as needed.
5. Multiply by population size for numbers.

**Route B: genotype counts given (or codominance)**

1. Total alleles = 2 × number of individuals.
2. Count one allele: 2 × homozygotes + heterozygotes.
3. Divide by total alleles to get p; q = 1 − p.
4. Compare expected genotype numbers with observed.

**Dominant phenotype given:** recessive phenotype = 1 − dominant phenotype; then Route A.

## Quick reference values

| q² | q | p | 2pq |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 0.1 | 0.9 | 0.18 |
| 0.04 | 0.2 | 0.8 | 0.32 |
| 0.09 | 0.3 | 0.7 | 0.42 |
| 0.16 | 0.4 | 0.6 | 0.48 |
| 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.50 |

## Interpreting data

| Observation | Possible explanation |
|---|---|
| Allele frequency changes between generations | Selection, drift, mutation or migration |
| Fewer heterozygotes than predicted | Non-random mating, or selection against heterozygotes |
| More of one homozygote than predicted | Selection for that phenotype |
| Large change in a small isolated population | Genetic drift |

Always name the condition and link it to the context.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles of a gene |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles of a gene |
| Dominant allele | Expressed in the phenotype when one copy is present |
| Recessive allele | Expressed only when two copies are present |
| Codominant alleles | Both expressed in the heterozygote |
| Genetic drift | Chance changes in allele frequency, important in small populations |

## Exam traps

- q is √(recessive phenotype), not the recessive phenotype itself.
- Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq, so never use it to get p directly.
- Heterozygotes = 2pq, not pq.
- Count two alleles per individual.
- Convert percentages to decimals before using the equations.
- Round only at the end.
- Population definition needs: same species, same place, same time, can interbreed.
- The principle predicts constant allele frequencies, not a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a population.
2. Define gene pool.
3. What does the Hardy–Weinberg principle predict?
4. List the five conditions under which it applies.
5. In a population, 1% show a recessive condition. Calculate q, p and 2pq.
6. 75% of a population show the dominant phenotype. Calculate the frequency of heterozygotes.
7. A population of 400 has 160 AA, 180 Aa and 60 aa. Calculate p.
8. Why is the heterozygote term 2pq?
9. In a population of 10 000, q = 0.02. How many heterozygotes are expected?
10. Allele frequency of q rises from 0.2 to 0.35 over 15 generations. What does this show?
11. Why can you count alleles directly with codominant alleles?
12. Give one condition likely to be broken on a small island and explain why.

### Answers

1. A group of organisms of the same species occupying the same space at the same time that can potentially interbreed.
2. All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time.
3. Allele frequencies will not change from generation to generation.
4. Large population; random mating; no mutation; no migration; no selection.
5. q² = 0.01, q = 0.1, p = 0.9, 2pq = **0.18**.
6. q² = 0.25, q = 0.5, p = 0.5; 2pq = **0.5** (50%).
7. A alleles = 2 × 160 + 180 = 500; total = 800; p = **0.625**.
8. There are two ways to be heterozygous: dominant allele from the egg and recessive from the sperm, or the reverse.
9. p = 0.98; 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392; × 10 000 = **392**.
10. A condition of the principle is not met, for example selection favouring the recessive allele.
11. Each genotype has a distinct phenotype, so the number of each genotype is known.
12. A large population: a small island population is strongly affected by genetic drift, so allele frequencies change by chance.
