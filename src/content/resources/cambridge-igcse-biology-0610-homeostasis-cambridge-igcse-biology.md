This guide covers sub-topic **14.4 Homeostasis** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Only outcomes 1 and 2 are Core: the definition of homeostasis and the fact that insulin decreases blood glucose concentration. Outcomes 3 to 8 are **Extended only**: negative feedback, blood glucose control, Type 1 diabetes, skin structure and temperature control.

The [coordination and response study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-coordination-and-response/) summarises these in tables. This page goes further: building a negative feedback loop step by step, applying it to blood glucose and to temperature in new situations, and writing the long explanations that Paper 4 rewards. ## 14.4.1 and 14.4.2 Core: the essentials

**Homeostasis** is the **maintenance of a constant internal environment**. "Internal environment" means the conditions inside the body that cells depend on, such as body temperature and the glucose concentration of the blood. Cells work best when these stay close to a steady value, because enzymes and other processes depend on them.

**Insulin decreases blood glucose concentration.** For Core papers, that statement is the whole requirement on insulin. Insulin is secreted by the pancreas, as you learn in [hormones](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-hormones-cambridge-igcse-biology/).

## 14.4.3 Negative feedback (Extended only)

Every controlled factor has a **set point**: the normal value the body maintains, such as about 37 °C for core body temperature. Negative feedback works in a loop:

1. The factor moves **away** from the set point.
2. The change is **detected**.
3. A **response** is triggered that moves the factor **back towards** the set point.
4. When the factor returns to the set point, the response stops.

It is called **negative** feedback because the response **reverses** the change: a rise causes a fall; a fall causes a rise. The factor therefore fluctuates slightly around the set point rather than staying perfectly still.

### Model answer: negative feedback

**Question:** Explain the concept of homeostatic control by negative feedback, with reference to a set point. [3]

**Answer:** The body has a set point, a normal value for a factor such as blood glucose concentration (1). If the factor rises above or falls below the set point, the change is detected (1). A response is triggered that reverses the change, returning the factor to the set point (1).

## 14.4.4 Blood glucose control (Extended only)

The **pancreas** detects blood glucose concentration and secretes hormones. The **liver** is the main target organ that responds.

| | Blood glucose too high (after a meal) | Blood glucose too low (fasting, exercise) |
|---|---|---|
| Pancreas secretes | **insulin** | **glucagon** |
| Liver responds by | converting glucose into **glycogen**, which is stored | converting **glycogen** into glucose, which is released into the blood |
| Result | blood glucose falls back to the set point | blood glucose rises back to the set point |

Spell these carefully. **Glucagon** is the hormone; **glycogen** is the storage carbohydrate in the liver. Mixing them up is the most frequent error in this outcome.

### Worked example 1: a meal of pasta

*A student eats a large plate of pasta. Describe how her blood glucose concentration is returned to normal.*

1. Starch in the pasta is digested to glucose, which is absorbed into the blood; blood glucose rises above the set point.
2. The pancreas detects the rise and secretes **insulin**.
3. Insulin travels in the blood to the **liver**.
4. The liver converts glucose into **glycogen** and stores it.
5. Blood glucose falls back towards the set point, and insulin secretion decreases.

This is negative feedback: the rise caused a response that brought the concentration down.

## 14.4.5 Type 1 diabetes (Extended only)

In **Type 1 diabetes**, the pancreas produces little or no insulin. After a meal, blood glucose rises and is not brought back down, because the liver is not stimulated to convert glucose to glycogen.

The outline of treatment:

- **Insulin injections** (or an insulin pump), timed to match meals.
- **Regular monitoring** of blood glucose concentration, for example with a meter.
- **Controlling the diet**, especially the amount and timing of carbohydrate.
- Taking account of **exercise**, which uses glucose.

### Worked example 2: applying the treatment

*A person with Type 1 diabetes plans a long bike ride. Suggest why they check their blood glucose before and during the ride, and carry a sugary snack.*

- Exercise uses glucose for respiration in muscles, so blood glucose can fall too low.
- Checking shows whether the concentration is falling.
- Eating a snack raises blood glucose if it falls below the safe range.

## 14.4.6 The skin (Extended only)

You must identify seven structures in skin diagrams:

| Structure | Where it is | Role in temperature control |
|---|---|---|
| Hairs | grow out of the surface | can be raised or lowered |
| Hair erector muscles | small muscles at the base of each hair | contract to raise hairs |
| Sweat glands | coiled tubes deep in the skin, with ducts to the surface | secrete sweat |
| Receptors | nerve endings near the surface | detect temperature changes |
| Sensory neurones | lead from receptors to the CNS | carry impulses to the brain |
| Blood vessels | including arterioles and surface capillaries | change blood flow near the surface |
| Fatty tissue | layer beneath the skin | insulation |

## 14.4.7 and 14.4.8 Temperature control (Extended only)

### The role of the brain

The **brain** contains a temperature control centre. It monitors the temperature of the **blood** flowing through it and receives impulses from **temperature receptors in the skin** via sensory neurones. It compares these with the set point and coordinates the responses below. This is negative feedback again.

### Responses

| Mechanism | Too hot | Too cold |
|---|---|---|
| Sweating | sweat glands secrete more sweat; water evaporates from the skin surface, transferring heat away | little or no sweat |
| Arterioles to skin surface capillaries | **vasodilation**: arterioles widen, more blood flows through surface capillaries, more heat is lost from the skin | **vasoconstriction**: arterioles narrow, less blood flows through surface capillaries, less heat is lost |
| Shivering | none | muscles contract rapidly; respiration in the muscles releases heat |
| Hairs | hair erector muscles relax; hairs lie flat | hair erector muscles contract; hairs stand up and trap a layer of air, which insulates |
| Fatty tissue | – | insulates, reducing heat loss |

### Worked example 3: a cold swim

*A swimmer stays in cold sea water for 20 minutes. Explain how her body reduces heat loss and generates heat.*

- Temperature receptors in her skin detect cold; impulses travel along sensory neurones to the brain, which also detects a fall in blood temperature.
- **Vasoconstriction:** arterioles supplying her skin surface capillaries narrow, so less blood flows near the surface and less heat is lost.
- **Shivering:** her muscles contract rapidly, and the extra respiration releases heat.
- **Sweating** stops.
- Her **fatty tissue** insulates her body.

### The arteriole trap

The syllabus says vasodilation and vasoconstriction happen in the **arterioles supplying skin surface capillaries**. Capillaries do not widen, narrow or move towards the surface. Write "arterioles widen" or "arterioles narrow", then describe the effect on blood flow in the surface capillaries.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **glucagon** (hormone) and **glycogen** (store).
- Saying insulin "turns glucose into energy".
- Saying the pancreas stores glycogen. The liver does.
- Treatment of Type 1 diabetes given as "take sugar" or "take medicine" with no mention of insulin.
- Saying capillaries dilate, constrict or move.
- Saying sweat "cools you because it is cold"; evaporation of water transfers heat away.
- Saying shivering "warms the muscles by friction"; the heat comes from respiration.
- Leaving the brain out of temperature control.

## Exam technique for 14.4 command words

- **Describe** homeostasis: maintenance of a constant internal environment.
- **State** (Core): insulin decreases blood glucose concentration.
- **Explain** negative feedback (Extended): set point, detection, reversal.
- **Describe** glucose control (Extended): stimulus, hormone, liver response, result.
- **Outline** Type 1 treatment (Extended): insulin, monitoring, diet and exercise.
- **Describe** temperature control (Extended): name the mechanism and its effect on heat loss or heat gain.
