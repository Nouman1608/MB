Condensed recall for **3.1 The characteristics of extreme environments** (IB DP Geography, Option C: Extreme environments). Fuller coverage is in the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/).

## What counts as extreme?

Conditions at or beyond the usual limits for human settlement or ecosystem productivity. Extremeness may come from temperature, aridity, altitude, ice cover or a combination. It is **relative**: what is extreme for one species or society may be home for another.

The option works with three broad groups, and you should be able to say which one an example belongs to:

| Group | Main limiting factor | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hot arid | Lack of water, high daytime temperatures | Subtropical deserts such as the Sahara and the Arabian Desert |
| Cold (polar and periglacial) | Low temperatures, frozen ground, long winter darkness | Antarctica, Greenland, the Arctic tundra of Canada and Siberia |
| High altitude | Cold, thin air, steep slopes, intense radiation | The Himalaya, the Andes, the Alps |

Some places combine factors. Antarctica is cold and also very dry, so it counts as a polar desert. The Atacama in northern Chile is a coastal desert where the cold offshore current and the Andes together keep the climate exceptionally arid.

## Typical characteristics

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Climate | Very low or very high temperatures; large diurnal ranges in deserts; low, unreliable precipitation; strong winds; long snow cover in cold areas |
| Vegetation and soils | Sparse cover, slow decomposition, thin, immature or saline soils; adaptations such as deep roots, waxy leaves, cushion growth forms |
| Ecology | Low net primary productivity and biodiversity, though specialised species can be abundant |
| Hazards | Drought, flash floods, avalanches, frost, glacial outburst floods, altitude sickness |

### Why the climate behaves this way

- **Large diurnal range in deserts:** clear skies and dry air let strong sunshine heat the ground by day, then let heat radiate away quickly at night because there is little cloud or water vapour to trap it.
- **Unreliable rainfall:** where totals are low, a single storm can supply a large share of a year's rain, so the annual average is a poor guide to any one year.
- **Cold at high latitudes:** the sun is low in the sky, so its energy is spread over a larger area; snow and ice have a high **albedo** and reflect much of what arrives.
- **Cold at altitude:** thinner air holds and absorbs less heat, and air pressure falls with height, which is also why oxygen is harder to obtain and altitude sickness occurs.

### Soils and plant adaptations

- In deserts, evaporation draws water upwards through the soil by capillary action and leaves salts behind, producing saline crusts. Organic matter is scarce because little grows.
- In cold areas, decomposition is slow and the ground above **permafrost** is often waterlogged in summer because meltwater cannot drain downward.
- Mountain soils are thin and stony on steep slopes because weathered material is removed before deep soil can form.
- **Xerophytes** survive drought with water storage, spines or waxy surfaces; **ephemerals** complete their life cycle quickly after rare rain. Tundra and alpine plants are low-growing, often in cushions, which keeps them out of the wind and traps warmth near the ground.

## Two calculations

- **Aridity index = precipitation / potential evapotranspiration.** Lower values mean drier. Example: 180 / 1,500 = 0.12. Quote the classification you use, as thresholds differ between sources.
- **Environmental lapse rate:** about 6.5 degrees Celsius of cooling per 1,000 m gained (an average; local conditions vary). Example: 4,000 m higher gives 6.5 x 4 = 26 degrees colder.

One widely used classification (the UNEP drylands scheme) treats values below 0.05 as hyper-arid, 0.05 to 0.20 as arid, 0.20 to 0.50 as semi-arid and 0.50 to 0.65 as dry sub-humid. On that scheme 0.12 is arid. If an exam source gives different thresholds, use the source's.

**Worked check (lapse rate in reverse):** a weather station at 1,200 m records 18 degrees Celsius. A camp is at 5,200 m. Height difference = 4,000 m, so cooling is about 26 degrees and the camp is roughly 18 - 26 = -8 degrees Celsius. State that this is an estimate.

## Global distribution and why

- Hot deserts cluster in the subtropical belts of descending air.
- Cold environments dominate high latitudes.
- Mountain ranges rise at many latitudes.

Explain the cause of each pattern; naming locations alone earns little. A fuller explanation of the hot desert pattern uses four causes:

1. **Subtropical high pressure:** air rising at the equator descends at roughly 30 degrees north and south (the descending limb of the Hadley cell), warms as it sinks and becomes stable, so clouds rarely form.
2. **Continentality:** places far from the sea, such as the interior deserts of central Asia, receive little moisture.
3. **Rain shadow:** air loses its moisture on the windward side of a mountain range and descends dry on the lee side.
4. **Cold ocean currents:** along coasts such as the Atacama and the Namib, cold water cools the air above it, which stabilises it and suppresses rain, though fog is common.

Mountains are explained by tectonics (for example plate collision in the Himalaya and subduction along the Andes), not by climate, so their distribution follows plate boundaries rather than latitude.

## People in extreme environments

Extreme environments usually have **low population density** and are **remote and inaccessible**: long distances, poor transport links and seasonal isolation raise the cost of services and supplies. Many are home to indigenous peoples with long experience of the conditions, such as the Inuit in the Arctic, the Sami in northern Scandinavia, the Tuareg in the Sahara and highland communities in the Andes and on the Tibetan Plateau. Some highland populations show physiological adaptation to low oxygen. Avoid describing these places as empty: they are lightly populated, not uninhabited.

## Model paragraph

*Explain why hot deserts are found mainly between about 15 and 30 degrees of latitude.* "Most hot deserts lie under the descending limb of the Hadley cell. Air that rose at the equator sinks in the subtropics, warming by compression and becoming stable, which suppresses cloud formation and rainfall. The resulting clear skies give intense daytime heating and rapid night-time cooling. Locally the pattern is strengthened by cold offshore currents, as in the Atacama, and by rain shadows, so the driest places combine more than one cause."

Notice the structure: process, consequence, then a located refinement.

## Exam traps

- Using "extreme" as if it meant the same for everyone.
- Treating all deserts as hot and sandy: many are rocky, and some are cold.
- Quoting the lapse rate as exact rather than an average.
- Citing aridity values without saying which classification applies.
- Explaining mountain distribution with climate rather than plate tectonics.
- Describing extreme environments as uninhabited, ignoring indigenous communities.
- Mixing up the environmental lapse rate with the adiabatic rates used for rising air parcels.

## Self-test

1. Why is "extreme" a relative term?
2. Give three characteristics of vegetation or soils in extreme environments.
3. Define the aridity index.
4. Calculate the index for P = 90 mm and PET = 1,800 mm.
5. By how much does temperature fall over 2,000 m using the average lapse rate?
6. Why do hot deserts have large diurnal temperature ranges?
7. Explain how the Hadley cell helps produce hot deserts.
8. How does a cold ocean current make a coastal desert drier?
9. Why are soils above permafrost often waterlogged in summer?
10. What is an ephemeral plant?
11. A station at 800 m records 12 degrees Celsius. Estimate the temperature at 3,800 m.
12. Why is Antarctica described as a desert?

**Answers:** 1. What is extreme for one species or society may be normal for another. 2. For example sparse cover, thin or saline soils, deep roots. 3. Precipitation divided by potential evapotranspiration. 4. 90 / 1,800 = 0.05. 5. 6.5 x 2 = 13 degrees Celsius. 6. Clear skies and dry air allow strong heating by day and rapid loss of heat by radiation at night. 7. Air descends in the subtropics, warms and becomes stable, so clouds and rain rarely form. 8. It cools and stabilises the air above, suppressing the uplift needed for rain. 9. The frozen ground beneath blocks drainage, so meltwater stays near the surface. 10. A plant that completes its life cycle quickly after rare rain. 11. 3,000 m difference gives about 19.5 degrees of cooling, so about 12 - 19.5 = -7.5 degrees Celsius. 12. It receives very little precipitation, so it is dry as well as cold.
