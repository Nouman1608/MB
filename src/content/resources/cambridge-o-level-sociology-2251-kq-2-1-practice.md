These questions practise key question 2.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *How do we learn our
identity?* Section A follows the six-part (a)–(f) shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 2.
Section B gives extra practice on concepts, agencies and theories.

## Section A — Identity question

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *customs* **[2]**
- (ii) *stereotype* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** processes used by agencies of socialisation. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways the media socialises young people. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** reasons why the family may be the most effective agency of socialisation.
**[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the evidence that human behaviour is learned rather than inborn. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the Marxist view that socialisation reproduces social class. **[14]**

## Section B — Concepts, agencies and theories

**(g)** Explain how roles and status can influence a person's identity. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain the difference between consensus and conflict views of socialisation. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways religion acts as an agency of socialisation. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways the workplace socialises adults. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** criticisms of the functionalist view of socialisation. **[4]**

## Answers

**(a)(i) [2]** Traditional practices or norms that have been followed in a society for a long time (1),
such as wedding rituals or ways of celebrating a religious festival (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A fixed, oversimplified and often negative belief about a whole group of people (1),
such as the belief that older people cannot use technology (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two (1 each): canalisation; hidden curriculum; media representations; peer pressure;
religious teachings; role modelling; workplace training; manipulation.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Media representations** show what is "normal" for different groups, such as images of ideal
  bodies or gender roles, which young people may copy (2).
- **Role models:** celebrities and influencers present lifestyles and values that young people admire
  and imitate (2).
- **Approval and disapproval online:** likes and comments reward some behaviour and discourage other
  behaviour, teaching norms (2).
- **Advertising** teaches consumer values, linking identity and status to what people buy (2).

**(d) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each:
- It is the first agency, so it shapes language, basic norms and values before any other influence
  (2).
- Emotional bonds mean children want parental approval, so rewards and sanctions are powerful (2).
- Children spend most of their early years in the family, giving constant opportunities for role
  modelling (2).
- Processes such as canalisation and manipulation (Oakley) shape gender identity from birth (2).

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed points with evidence and some evaluation; 4–6 some development;
1–3 basic points.
- **Isolated children:** Genie, discovered in Los Angeles in 1970 aged 13 after extreme isolation,
  never fully acquired language, suggesting that language depends on social contact at the right
  stage.
- **Relativity of culture:** norms of politeness, dress, food and family life vary between
  societies; if behaviour were inborn it would be similar everywhere.
- **Gender:** Mead (1935) described very different male and female temperaments among the Arapesh,
  Mundugumor and Tchambuli, suggesting gender is cultural.
- **Change over time:** norms such as attitudes to women's paid work change within a generation,
  faster than biology could change.
- **Evaluation:** isolation cases are rare and may involve abuse or disability; Mead's findings were
  questioned; biology may set some limits, so the debate is about how much is learned.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a supported conclusion; 7–10 developed
but less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the Marxist view:*
- Agencies spread capitalist ideology, such as the belief that success depends only on effort, so
  inequality seems fair.
- Althusser described education as an ideological state apparatus that transmits ruling-class ideas.
- The hidden curriculum teaches obedience and acceptance of hierarchy, preparing working-class pupils
  for low-status jobs.
- Families pass on different resources and expectations, so children tend to end up in a class
  similar to their parents'.

*Against or alternatives:*
- Functionalists argue socialisation passes on shared values that benefit everyone and create
  social cohesion, not only ruling-class interests.
- Feminists argue the Marxist view neglects gender: socialisation reproduces patriarchy as well as
  class.
- People are not passive: many recognise inequality, and some working-class young people resist
  school or achieve upward mobility.
- Other identities, such as ethnicity and religion, are also reproduced through socialisation.

*Conclusion:* socialisation does help reproduce class inequality, but the Marxist view overstates how
passive people are and ignores other inequalities.

**(g) [4]** Roles set out the behaviour expected of a position, such as a prefect or an eldest child,
so people learn to act and think in line with them (2). Status gives a social position and level of
respect; how others treat a person because of an ascribed or achieved status shapes how that person
sees themselves (2).

**(h) [4]** Consensus views, such as functionalism, see socialisation as passing on shared values
that most people agree with, creating social cohesion (2). Conflict views, such as Marxism and
feminism, see socialisation as serving the interests of powerful groups, such as the ruling class or
men, and teaching others to accept their position (2).

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- **Religious teachings** set out moral rules, such as honesty or modesty, which believers follow (2).
- **Rituals and festivals** bring people together and strengthen a shared identity and sense of
  belonging (2).
- **Religious rewards and sanctions**, such as respect in the community or disapproval, encourage
  conformity (2).

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- **Workplace training and induction** teach the skills, rules and routines of the job (2).
- **Dress codes and codes of conduct** teach norms of appearance and behaviour (2).
- **Colleagues** act as role models and use informal approval or teasing, shaping occupational
  identity (2).

**(k) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each:
- It assumes everyone shares the same values, ignoring conflict between classes, genders and
  ethnic groups (2).
- It sees people as passive receivers of culture, ignoring how individuals interpret and reject
  what they are taught (2).
- Marxists and feminists argue it ignores whose values are being taught and who benefits (2).

## Frequently asked questions

**How many examples do I need in part (c)?** One per point. Each of the three points should name a
process and give an example of it.

**Should part (f) include feminism?** Yes, as an alternative view. Using a second perspective to
challenge the first is a clear way to show evaluation.
