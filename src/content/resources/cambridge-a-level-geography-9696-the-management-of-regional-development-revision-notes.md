These notes condense section 14.4 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), for examination in 2025 and 2026: the case study of "one country's regional development policy, its regional disparities, some of the difficulties faced in trying to overcome these disparities" and an evaluation of "the attempted solutions". The full case study and an essay plan are in the [14.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development/); exam-style questions are in the [14.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development-practice/). The China case study is summarised in the [Topic 14 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-economic-transition-revision-notes/).

## Case study checklist

For your chosen country, have ready:

- The core and the periphery, named and located.
- At least two indicators of disparity with figures and dates.
- The policy name, the agency or programme, and the dates.
- Three or more specific measures, with places.
- Difficulties in at least three categories (physical, economic, political, social, environmental).
- Evidence of outcomes and a final judgement.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Regional policy | Government action to reduce disparities between regions |
| Assisted area | A region eligible for grants or incentives |
| Growth pole | A planned concentration of investment intended to start cumulative growth in the periphery |
| Leading industry | The large firm or sector at the centre of a growth pole |
| Cathedral in the desert | A large plant in a peripheral region with few local linkages or jobs |
| Cohesion funds | European Union money to reduce regional disparities between and within member states |
| Effectiveness | Whether the policy achieved its aim |
| Efficiency | Value for money, such as cost per job created |
| Equity | Fairness of who benefited |
| Policy sustainability | Whether growth continues after subsidies end |

## Policy tools linked to theory

| Tool | Theory link |
|---|---|
| Infrastructure | Reduces access disadvantage; aims to increase spread effects |
| Grants and tax breaks | Offset higher peripheral costs |
| Growth poles | Try to start cumulative causation in the periphery (Perroux, 1950s) |
| Special economic or free trade zones | Attract investment with tax and customs advantages |
| Relocating government or a new capital | Moves spending and jobs out of the core |
| Controls on the core | Limit further agglomeration |
| Education and health | Raise human capital, reducing brain drain pressure |

## Worked case study summary: Italy and the Mezzogiorno

- **Disparity:** industrial North-west (Milan, Turin, Genoa) versus the South and islands; lower GDP per capita, higher unemployment; mass migration northwards in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **1950:** Cassa per il Mezzogiorno created for land reform, reclamation, irrigation, roads and water.
- **Late 1950s onwards:** growth poles; state-controlled firms required to invest in the South; steelworks at Taranto (1960s); petrochemicals in Sicily and Sardinia; incentives for private firms.
- **1984:** Cassa wound up after criticism of waste and political interference.
- **Since then:** European Union cohesion funds for infrastructure, business and training.
- **Difficulties:** capital-intensive plants with few jobs and weak local linkages ("cathedrals in the desert"); patronage and organised crime in contracts; mountainous interior and distance from markets; continued brain drain; pollution, with a 2012 court order seizing parts of the Taranto plant.
- **Judgement:** living standards and infrastructure improved; relative gap with the North persisted; growth poles did not create self-sustaining growth.

## Comparing two policies

| | Italy (Mezzogiorno) | China (Western Development, see Topic 14 notes) |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 1950 | Proposed 1999 |
| Main tools | Infrastructure, growth poles, incentives | Infrastructure (railways, pipelines, power), incentives, environmental programmes |
| Key difficulty | Weak linkages, political interference | Distance, terrain, coastal pull on migrants |
| Common lesson | Infrastructure alone can strengthen flows to the core | Same |

## Evaluation framework

1. Aim of the policy. 2. Measures used (relative and absolute; economic and social). 3. Time scale. 4. Which groups and places benefited. 5. Unintended effects. 6. Theory: were spread effects created? 7. Conclusion on how far it succeeded.

## Exam traps

- Describing measures with no evidence of outcomes.
- Difficulties listed without their effect on success.
- Judging by money spent rather than results.
- Forgetting unintended environmental or social effects.
- Not naming one country; generic regional policy scores poorly.
- A conclusion that does not answer the question set.

## Self-test

1. Quote the four parts of the 14.4 case study requirement.
2. Define a growth pole and name the economist linked with the idea.
3. What was the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno and when was it created?
4. Name one growth-pole plant in southern Italy.
5. Why were some southern plants called "cathedrals in the desert"?
6. Give one political difficulty faced by the Mezzogiorno policy.
7. Give one environmental cost of the growth-pole strategy.
8. Distinguish effectiveness from efficiency in evaluating a policy.
9. Why can new infrastructure sometimes strengthen the core?
10. What has funded regional development in southern Italy since the Cassa closed?
11. Give one success of Italy's policy.
12. What common lesson do the Italian and Chinese cases suggest?

### Answers

1. One country's regional development policy; its regional disparities; some difficulties in overcoming them; evaluation of the attempted solutions.
2. A planned concentration of investment around a leading industry intended to start cumulative growth; François Perroux.
3. A state fund for the South set up in 1950 to finance land reform, reclamation, irrigation and infrastructure.
4. The state-owned steelworks at Taranto (or the petrochemical complexes in Sicily or Sardinia).
5. They were large and capital-intensive but created few jobs and few local linkages.
6. Patronage or organised crime involvement in public contracts.
7. Pollution, for example at Taranto, where a court ordered parts of the plant seized in 2012.
8. Effectiveness asks whether the aim was achieved; efficiency asks whether it was achieved at reasonable cost.
9. Better links let core firms sell into the periphery and let workers leave it more easily, increasing backwash.
10. European Union regional (cohesion) funds.
11. Major improvements in infrastructure, irrigation and living standards since the 1950s.
12. Infrastructure alone does not close gaps; success depends on local linkages, firms and skills.
