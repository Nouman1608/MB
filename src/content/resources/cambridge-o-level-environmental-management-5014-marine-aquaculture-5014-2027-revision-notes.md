Condensed notes for sub-topic 3.4 Marine aquaculture in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The [marine aquaculture study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-marine-aquaculture-5014-2027/) gives full explanations and worked examples; the [marine aquaculture practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-marine-aquaculture-5014-2027-practice/) test them.

## Map of the sub-topic

- **3.4.1** Exploitation: overfishing and overharvesting; target and bycatch species; food chains.
- **3.4.2** Definition: farming of marine species in captivity (fish, crustaceans, seaweeds).
- **3.4.3** Eight impacts of aquaculture.
- **3.4.4** Nine strategies for managing **harvesting** of wild species.
- **3.4.5** Discuss those strategies.

## Exploitation in chains

**Overfishing**
Demand + bigger boats, nets and fish-finding technology → fish caught faster than they breed → fewer adults → fewer young → stock declines → catches fall despite more effort → possible collapse.

**Target species**
Population falls; average size falls; fewer breeding adults.

**Bycatch**
Unintended species (young fish, turtles, dolphins, sharks, seabirds) caught and often discarded dead → declines in species that are not even sold.

**Food chains**
- Remove a predator → prey increase → prey overgraze their food.
- Remove small prey fish → seabirds, seals and larger fish lose food.
- Trawling damages seabed habitats.

## Aquaculture impacts: two positive, six negative

| + / − | Impact | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| + | Reduced exploitation of natural fisheries | Farmed supply replaces some wild catch |
| + | Increased food supply | Reliable protein; jobs; exports |
| − | Risk of escape | Escapees compete or interbreed with wild stock |
| − | Risk of disease | Crowding spreads disease and parasites to wild animals |
| − | Local food webs | Predators attracted; habitats such as mangroves cleared |
| − | Nutrient enrichment from waste | Feed and faeces → algal growth → oxygen loss |
| − | Energy usage | Pumps, aeration, feed production, transport, refrigeration |
| − | Source of food for farmed species | Feed often made from wild-caught fish |

Seaweed farms need no feed and absorb nutrients, so their impacts are smaller.

## Harvesting controls: nine strategies

| Group | Strategy | How it protects the stock |
|---|---|---|
| Gear | Limits on boat size and overall net size | Smaller catch per trip |
| Gear | Increased mesh size | Young fish escape and breed |
| Gear | Pole and line | One fish at a time; very little bycatch |
| Amount and time | Quotas | Cap on total catch |
| Amount and time | Closed seasons | No fishing during breeding |
| Amount and time | Limited fishing days | Less time at sea |
| Place and law | Protected areas | Populations recover and spill over |
| Place and law | Conservation laws | Protect species; minimum landing sizes |
| Place and law | International agreements | Cover stocks that cross borders; need implementation and monitoring |

## Benefit / limitation pairs for "discuss"

- Gear limits: easy to inspect at port / rules broken at sea.
- Pole and line: selective / low catch per trip, higher price.
- Quotas: cap total catch / discards of over-quota fish; quotas set too high.
- Closed seasons and limited days: protect breeding / lost income.
- Protected areas: whole ecosystem recovers / patrols needed; effort moves elsewhere.
- International agreements: manage shared stocks / not all countries sign; monitoring is costly.

## Calculations to recognise

- Wild fish used in feed = feed mass × proportion from wild fish. 2,000 tonnes × 0.25 = 500 tonnes.
- Quota = stock × permitted proportion. 250,000 tonnes × 0.12 = 30,000 tonnes.
- Percentage decline = (old − new) ÷ old × 100.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exploitation | Using a natural resource, here by catching or harvesting marine species |
| Overfishing | Catching fish faster than the population can replace itself |
| Target species | The species fishers intend to catch |
| Bycatch | Species caught unintentionally |
| Marine aquaculture | Farming of marine species in captivity |
| Crustaceans | Animals such as prawns, shrimps and crabs |
| Quota | Maximum amount of a species that may be caught in a period |
| Closed season | A time when fishing is banned, often during breeding |
| Mesh size | The size of the holes in a net |
| Protected area | Part of the sea where fishing is banned or restricted |

## Exam traps

- Aquaculture is **farming**, not fishing.
- Do not assume aquaculture always helps wild stocks: carnivorous farmed fish eat feed made from wild fish.
- Bycatch is not the target species.
- The 3.4.4 strategies manage wild harvesting; do not answer with fish-farm design.
- International agreements need "implementation and monitoring" to work; say how.
- Seaweed farming is a good counter-example in a discussion of impacts.

## Self-test

1. Define marine aquaculture.
2. Name the three groups of marine species the syllabus includes in aquaculture.
3. What is bycatch?
4. Explain how overfishing a small prey fish can affect seabirds.
5. Give two positive impacts of marine aquaculture.
6. Explain how waste from fish cages can reduce oxygen in the water below.
7. Why can aquaculture still put pressure on wild fish stocks?
8. How does increasing the mesh size of nets protect a fish stock?
9. Why does pole and line fishing produce little bycatch?
10. Give one limitation of quotas.
11. Why are international agreements needed to manage some fish stocks?
12. Calculate a quota for a stock of 320,000 tonnes if 10% may be caught.

## Answers

1. The farming of marine species in captivity.
2. Fish, crustaceans and seaweeds.
3. Species that are caught unintentionally while fishing for a target species.
4. Seabirds lose their food supply, so their breeding success and numbers fall.
5. Reduced exploitation of natural fisheries; increased food supply for humans.
6. Uneaten feed and faeces add nutrients, causing algal growth; bacteria decomposing dead algae and waste use up oxygen.
7. Many farmed species are fed on meal made from wild-caught fish.
8. Young, small fish pass through the net and survive to breed.
9. Fish are caught one at a time, so unwanted animals can be released alive.
10. Fish caught over the quota may be thrown back dead, or the quota may be set too high to protect the stock.
11. Fish move across national boundaries, so one country cannot protect a shared stock alone.
12. 320,000 × 0.10 = 32,000 tonnes.
