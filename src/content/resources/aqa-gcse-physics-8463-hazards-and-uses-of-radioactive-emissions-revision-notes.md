## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Background radiation | Radiation that is around us all of the time, from natural and man-made sources |
| Cosmic rays | Radiation reaching the Earth from space; a natural source of background radiation |
| Fallout | Radioactive material spread into the environment by nuclear weapons testing or a nuclear accident |
| Radiation dose | A measure of the risk of harm from exposure of the body to radiation |
| Sievert (Sv) | Unit of radiation dose; 1000 mSv = 1 Sv (you will not be asked to recall it) |
| Half-life | Time for the number of unstable nuclei in a sample, or its count-rate, to halve |
| Tracer | A radioactive isotope put into the body so that a detector outside can follow where it goes |
| Perceived risk | How dangerous people believe something is, which may differ from the risk shown by data |

## 4.4.3.1 Background radiation: recall list

**Natural sources**

- Rocks, including the radon gas some rocks release.
- Cosmic rays from space.

**Man-made sources**

- Fallout from nuclear weapons testing.
- Fallout from nuclear accidents.

**What changes the level or dose**

| Factor | Example | Why it changes the dose |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Living in an area built on granite | More radon released from the ground |
| Location | Living or working at high altitude | Less air above to absorb cosmic rays |
| Occupation | Airline pilot or cabin crew | Many hours at altitude, so more cosmic rays |
| Occupation | Radiographer, nuclear power worker | Works near radioactive sources |
| Occupation | Miner | Works underground, surrounded by rock |

### Dose conversions

```
mSv → Sv   divide by 1000      5.2 mSv  = 0.0052 Sv  = 5.2 × 10⁻³ Sv
Sv → mSv   multiply by 1000    0.018 Sv = 18 mSv
compare two doses: divide one by the other   9.0 mSv ÷ 1.5 mSv = 6 times larger
```

Always put both doses in the same unit before comparing.

### Correcting for background

```
Step 1  measure the count with no source; divide by the time to get background count-rate
Step 2  measure with the source; divide by the time
Step 3  corrected count-rate = measured − background
```

Use corrected values whenever you read a half-life from data.

## 4.4.3.2 Half-life and hazard

- Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years.
- **Short half-life**: high activity at first; the hazard falls quickly.
- **Long half-life**: lower activity; the hazard lasts a very long time, so waste must be stored securely
  for a long period.
- With standard form, convert to the same unit and the same power of ten before comparing.

```
3.6 × 10³ s  = 3600 s = 1 hour
8.6 × 10⁴ s  ≈ 1 day
2.4 × 10⁴ years is 24 000 years
```

**Method: "Explain why the hazard differs"**

1. Say which isotope has the shorter half-life.
2. Link short half-life to high initial activity that soon falls.
3. Link long half-life to activity that continues for a long time.
4. Apply it to the situation: how long people or the environment are exposed.

## 4.4.3.3 Uses in medicine

| Use | Radiation | Half-life | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tracer to explore an organ | Gamma | A few hours | Gamma leaves the body and is detected outside; weakly ionising; activity soon falls |
| External beam to destroy a tumour | Gamma | Long-lived source outside the body | Penetrates to deep tumours; beams from several directions meet at the tumour |
| Source placed in or near unwanted tissue | Often beta | Matched to the length of treatment | Short range in tissue, so the dose stays close to the target |

Example: the thyroid absorbs iodine, so iodine-131 (a beta emitter) is used to treat some thyroid
conditions.

### Choosing a tracer from a table

```
Reject alpha:  cannot leave the body; strongly ionising inside it
Reject beta:   more ionising than gamma; may not all reach the detector
Reject very short half-life:  decays before the scan is finished
Reject very long half-life:   stays active in the body for months or years
Choose:        gamma, half-life of hours
```

### Evaluating risk: the four moves

1. **Benefit**: what the procedure achieves (diagnosis, destroying a tumour).
2. **Risk**: ionising radiation can damage healthy cells and can cause cancer.
3. **Data**: compare the dose with something familiar, such as yearly background dose.
4. **Judgement**: state whether the benefit outweighs the risk, and why.

Perceived risk can be larger than the measured risk for medical scans and smaller for familiar sources
such as radon at home. Use the numbers given to show which.

## Exam traps

- "Background radiation comes from nuclear power stations." The specification's man-made sources are
  fallout from weapons testing and nuclear accidents.
- Converting mSv to Sv by dividing by 100.
- Saying an isotope with a long half-life is "more radioactive". It is less active but hazardous for longer.
- Choosing a tracer on half-life alone and ignoring the radiation type, or the other way round.
- "Radiation is bad" with no named harm. Say **ionising**, **damages cells**, **can cause cancer**.
- An evaluation with no conclusion. Finish with a judgement that uses the data.
- Reading a half-life from counts that still include background.

## Quick self-test

1. Name one natural and one man-made source of background radiation.
2. Explain why a person living in an area built on granite may receive a higher background dose.
3. Convert 0.45 mSv into sieverts.
4. Convert 0.030 Sv into millisieverts.
5. A CT scan gives 6.0 mSv and a chest examination gives 0.10 mSv. How many times larger is the CT dose?
6. A detector records 36 counts in 2 minutes with no source. Find the background count-rate in counts/s.
7. Isotope A has a half-life of 5 minutes and isotope B has a half-life of 5000 years. Which needs storing
   securely for longer, and why?
8. Write 7.2 × 10³ s in hours.
9. Why is a gamma emitter, not an alpha emitter, used as a tracer?
10. Why should a tracer's half-life not be several years?
11. Give the two medical uses of nuclear radiation named in the specification.
12. A tracer gives a dose of 0.8 mSv. Yearly background is 2.0 mSv. What fraction of a year's background
    is this, and what does it suggest about the risk?

### Answers

1. Natural: rocks or cosmic rays. Man-made: fallout from nuclear weapons testing or a nuclear accident.
2. Granite releases radon gas, a radioactive gas that adds to the background radiation in homes there.
3. 0.45 ÷ 1000 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ Sv** (0.00045 Sv).
4. 0.030 × 1000 = **30 mSv**.
5. 6.0 ÷ 0.10 = **60 times**.
6. 36 ÷ 120 = **0.30 counts/s**.
7. **B**: its long half-life means it stays radioactive, and so hazardous, for thousands of years.
8. 7200 ÷ 3600 = **2 hours**.
9. Gamma passes out of the body to the detector and is weakly ionising; alpha cannot leave the body and
   strongly ionises the tissue around it.
10. The patient's body would stay radioactive for years, receiving a dose long after the test is over.
11. Exploring internal organs; controlling or destroying unwanted tissue.
12. 0.8 ÷ 2.0 = **0.4** of a year's background (about five months). The extra risk is small compared with
    the risk everyone already accepts, so the benefit of the diagnosis is likely to outweigh it.
