Consolidate with the [3.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-1-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-1-practice/).

All countries and figures are invented. The main example is Rendova, whose currency is the rend.

## What 3.1 covers

- The **circular flow of income** as the basis of national income measurement.
- **GDP** and **GNI**: what they measure, and the **output, income and expenditure** approaches.
- **Nominal** and **real** values, **per capita** values and **purchasing power parity (PPP)**, with calculations.
- The **business cycle**: short-term fluctuations around a long-term growth trend.
- How useful GDP and GNI are as measures of **well-being**, and alternative measures.

## Why three approaches give one answer

The circular flow shows that every unit of spending on output becomes someone's income. So the value of output, the incomes generated and the spending on that output measure the same flow:

- **Output approach:** add the value added at each stage of production, to avoid counting intermediate goods twice.
- **Income approach:** add wages, rent, interest and profit earned in production.
- **Expenditure approach:** add spending on final goods and services: GDP = C + I + G + (X - M).

In practice the three totals differ slightly because of measurement errors, but in principle they are equal.

### Avoiding double counting

A Rendova farm sells wheat to a mill for 40 rends; the mill sells flour to a bakery for 70; the bakery sells bread to households for 120. Value added is 40 + (70 - 40) + (120 - 70) = **120 rends**, equal to the final sale. Adding all sales (40 + 70 + 120 = 230) would count the wheat three times.

## A full data set, start to finish

Rendova's figures, in billions of rends:

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Consumption (C) | 320 | |
| Investment (I) | 90 | |
| Government spending (G) | 110 | |
| Exports (X) | 150 | |
| Imports (M) | 170 | |
| Net income from abroad | -12 | |
| Nominal GDP | | 560 |
| GDP deflator (Year 1 = 100) | 100 | 108 |
| Population (millions) | 20.0 | 20.5 |

**Step 1: nominal GDP in Year 1.** 320 + 90 + 110 + (150 - 170) = **500 billion rends**. Net exports are negative, so they reduce GDP.

**Step 2: GNI in Year 1.** GDP + net income from abroad = 500 + (-12) = **488 billion rends**. GNI is lower because foreign owners of Rendova's assets earn more than Rendova's residents earn abroad.

**Step 3: real GDP in Year 2.** Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ deflator × 100 = 560 ÷ 108 × 100 = **518.5 billion rends** (Year 1 prices).

**Step 4: growth rates.**

```
Nominal growth = (560 - 500) / 500 x 100 = 12.0%
Real growth    = (518.5 - 500) / 500 x 100 = 3.7%
```

Most of the nominal rise was inflation; output itself grew by 3.7%.

**Step 5: real GDP per capita.**

```
Year 1: 500 bn / 20.0 m = 25,000 rends
Year 2: 518.5 bn / 20.5 m = about 25,294 rends
Growth = (25,294 - 25,000) / 25,000 x 100 = about 1.2%
```

Output per person grew far more slowly than total output, because population grew by 2.5%. For living standards, real per capita growth is the figure that matters.

## Comparing countries: why PPP

Converting GDP per capita at market exchange rates ignores differences in price levels. A haircut, a bus ride or rent is usually much cheaper in a low-income country. **PPP** adjusts for this by using an exchange rate at which the same basket of goods costs the same in both countries.

**Example.** GDP per capita in the invented country of Losk is 3,000 US dollars at market exchange rates. A basket that costs 100 dollars in the United States costs the equivalent of 40 dollars in Losk at market rates. Each dollar of income in Losk therefore buys 100 / 40 = 2.5 times as much, so GDP per capita at PPP is about 3,000 × 2.5 = **7,500 dollars**. Comparisons of living standards should use the PPP figure.

## Reading the business cycle from data

The **business cycle** is the pattern of short-term fluctuations in real GDP around its **long-term growth trend**, which represents growth in potential output.

Rendova's quarterly real GDP (billions of rends, Year 1 prices):

| Quarter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | 200 | 204 | 207 | 205 | 202 | 203 | 208 |
| Change on previous quarter | | +2.0% | +1.5% | -1.0% | -1.5% | +0.5% | +2.5% |

- **Expansion:** quarters 1 to 3, real GDP rising.
- **Peak:** quarter 3, the highest point before output falls.
- **Contraction:** quarters 4 and 5, real GDP falling. Two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP is a widely used definition of a **recession**.
- **Trough:** quarter 5, the lowest point.
- **Recovery and expansion:** quarters 6 and 7.

When actual output is above the trend, there is a positive output gap and pressure on inflation; below it, there is a negative output gap and spare capacity, usually with rising unemployment. Notice that a fall in the growth rate (quarter 3) is **not** a recession: output was still rising.

## GDP and GNI as measures of well-being

GDP measures the value of market output. Well-being depends on much more. An evaluation should weigh these limitations against the strengths of a measure that is regularly collected and broadly comparable.

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Inaccurate or incomplete data | Statistics rely on surveys and estimates, and are revised later |
| Informal and unrecorded activity | Cash work and home production (cooking, caring) are left out, so GDP understates output, especially where informal work is large |
| Quality of life | GDP ignores leisure, health, safety and stress; longer working hours can raise GDP while lowering well-being |
| Composition of output | Spending on prisons or on clearing up a disaster adds to GDP but does not raise well-being |
| Environmental costs | Pollution and resource depletion are not subtracted, so growth can look better than it is |
| Distribution of income | An average says nothing about who receives the income; per capita GDP can rise while most incomes stagnate |

### Alternative measures

- The **OECD Better Life Index** compares countries across many dimensions of well-being, such as housing, health, education, environment and work-life balance, and lets users weight them.
- **Happiness measures**, such as the World Happiness Report's life evaluations, use survey answers about how satisfied people are with their lives.
- The **Happy Planet Index** combines well-being and life expectancy with the ecological footprint, rewarding countries that achieve good lives with low resource use.

Each still depends on choices about what to include and how to weight it, and survey answers can reflect cultural differences in how people respond. Composite measures are therefore best used alongside GDP, not instead of it.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing nominal figures across years without adjusting for inflation.
- Dividing by the deflator without multiplying by 100.
- Using total GDP to compare living standards between countries of very different populations.
- Calling a slowdown in growth a recession.
- Listing GDP limitations without explaining why each one distorts well-being.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: write the formula, substitute, give units and the base year ("518.5 billion rends at Year 1 prices").
- **Explain using a diagram** for the business cycle: real GDP on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis, a rising trend line, and fluctuations labelled with the four phases.
- **Distinguish** GDP from GNI: location of production versus ownership of factors.
- **Evaluate** GDP as a measure of well-being: give strengths, at least three limitations with reasons, an alternative measure and a judgement that depends on the purpose of the comparison.
