This guide covers section 5.2, Depreciation, in Topic 5 (Accounting for end of period adjustments) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. The four outcomes (a to d) match Topic 2: explain the causes of depreciation, distinguish straight line from reducing balance, calculate and record depreciation in the books of account, and calculate and record profit or loss on disposal. The setting differs. Topic 5 belongs to Paper 2, which the specification says assesses your "ability to produce and make appropriate adjustments to entries at the end of an accounting period". So this page works from trial balances and year-end notes, the way depreciation reaches you in that paper.

All businesses and figures are invented. Amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign. For the first teaching of the two methods and the disposal account, see the [Topic 2 capital and revenue expenditure and depreciation guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-and-depreciation/) and the [end-of-period adjustments overview](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-end-of-period-adjustments/). ## 1. Reading depreciation in a trial balance

A trial balance shows each class of non-current asset **at cost** as a debit, and its **provision for depreciation** as a credit. The provision figure is the depreciation built up by the **start** of the year. This year's charge has not been made yet, and the note tells you how to make it.

The method in four steps:

1. Read the note: method, rate and any policy on part years.
2. Work out the base. Straight line uses cost (or cost less residual value). Reducing balance uses **cost less the provision in the trial balance**.
3. Charge the result as an expense in the income statement.
4. Add the charge to the provision. The statement of financial position shows cost, the new provision, and carrying value.

### Worked example 1: two asset classes

Kestrel Print has a year end of 31 December 2027. Its trial balance includes:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Machinery at cost | 60,000 | |
| Provision for depreciation of machinery | | 21,600 |
| Vehicles at cost | 45,000 | |
| Provision for depreciation of vehicles | | 18,000 |

Notes: machinery is depreciated at 20% a year by the reducing balance method; vehicles at 20% a year on cost (straight line).

- Machinery: 20% × (60,000 − 21,600) = 20% × 38,400 = **7,680**.
- Vehicles: 20% × 45,000 = **9,000**.
- Total charged in the income statement: 7,680 + 9,000 = **16,680**.

| Non-current assets | Cost | Depreciation to date | Carrying value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Machinery | 60,000 | 29,280 | 30,720 |
| Vehicles | 45,000 | 27,000 | 18,000 |
| Total | 105,000 | 56,280 | 48,720 |

Check each row: 21,600 + 7,680 = 29,280, and 18,000 + 9,000 = 27,000. The commonest Paper 2 slip is to take 20% of 60,000 for the machinery. That gives 12,000, which is the right answer only in the first year of a reducing balance asset.

## 2. Explaining a cause for a named asset (5.2 a)

A question may ask why one asset in the scenario loses value. Pick the cause that fits the asset and say what happens to it.

- A delivery van covering long distances: **wear and tear**, because use damages its engine and body.
- Computers in a design studio: **obsolescence**, because faster machines appear and the old ones fall behind even while they work.
- A ten-year lease on a shop: **passage of time**, because its value runs out as the years of the lease are used up.

Model answer for "Explain one cause of depreciation of Kestrel Print's printing machines": *The machines are used every day, so their parts wear out (wear and tear). As they wear, they become less reliable and are worth less, so part of their cost is charged as an expense each year they are used.* That is a cause, why it applies here, and its link to the charge.

## 3. Advising on a method (5.2 b)

The two methods charge the same total over an asset's life when the reducing balance rate brings the asset to its residual value. What changes is the timing, and so the profit of each year.

### Worked example 2: same total, different pattern

A design studio buys computers for 12,000. It expects to use them for three years and sell them for 1,500. The choice is straight line or 50% reducing balance.

| Year | Straight line | 50% reducing balance | Profit is lower under |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 3,500 | 6,000 | Reducing balance, by 2,500 |
| 2 | 3,500 | 3,000 | Straight line, by 500 |
| 3 | 3,500 | 1,500 | Straight line, by 2,000 |
| Total | 10,500 | 10,500 | |

Straight line: (12,000 − 1,500) ÷ 3 = 3,500. Reducing balance: 6,000, then 50% × 6,000 = 3,000, then 50% × 3,000 = 1,500. Both leave a carrying value of 1,500.

Reducing balance suits the computers, because they lose most of their usefulness early through obsolescence. Whatever is chosen must then be kept: changing method to raise one year's profit breaks the consistency concept in section 5.1.

## 4. Part-year policies

Assets are bought and sold during the year, so the question will state a policy. Follow it exactly. The usual ones are:

- a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal
- a charge calculated month by month for the time the asset is owned
- a charge on the assets held at the year end only.

### Worked example 3: purchase and disposal with monthly charges

Greyfriars Joinery has a year end of 31 December 2027. On 1 January 2027 equipment at cost was 36,000 and the provision for depreciation was 14,400. Equipment is depreciated at 10% a year on cost, calculated monthly.

- On 1 April 2027 a new saw was bought for 8,400, paid by bank.
- On 30 September 2027 a sander was sold for 3,200 by bank. It had cost 6,000 on 1 January 2024.

**Depreciation for 2027**

| Asset | Working | Charge |
|---|---|---:|
| Sander (January to September) | 6,000 × 10% × 9/12 | 450 |
| Saw (April to December) | 8,400 × 10% × 9/12 | 630 |
| Rest of the equipment (full year) | (36,000 − 6,000) × 10% | 3,000 |
| Total | | 4,080 |

**Sander on disposal**: depreciation for 2024 to 2026 is 3 × 600 = 1,800, plus 450 for 2027, so 2,250 in total. Carrying value = 6,000 − 2,250 = 3,750. Proceeds 3,200, so a **loss of 550**.

**Equipment at cost**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 Jan Balance b/d | 36,000 | 30 Sep Disposal | 6,000 |
| 1 Apr Bank | 8,400 | 31 Dec Balance c/d | 38,400 |
| | 44,400 | | 44,400 |

**Provision for depreciation of equipment**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 30 Sep Disposal | 2,250 | 1 Jan Balance b/d | 14,400 |
| 31 Dec Balance c/d | 16,230 | 31 Dec Income statement | 4,080 |
| | 18,480 | | 18,480 |

**Disposal of equipment**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 30 Sep Equipment at cost | 6,000 | 30 Sep Provision for depreciation | 2,250 |
| | | 30 Sep Bank | 3,200 |
| | | 31 Dec Income statement (loss) | 550 |
| | 6,000 | | 6,000 |

The income statement shows depreciation of 4,080 and a loss on disposal of 550 as expenses. The statement of financial position shows equipment at 38,400 less 16,230 = **22,170**.

Two points students miss. The year-of-sale charge (450) goes into the provision **before** the transfer to disposal, so the transfer is 2,250, not 1,800. And the full-year charge applies only to equipment held all year, so the sander's cost comes out of the 36,000 first. If asked for journal entries instead, the same figures appear: debit income statement and credit provision 4,080; debit disposal and credit equipment 6,000; debit provision and credit disposal 2,250, each with a narrative. The 3,200 receipt goes through the cash book.

## 5. Effects on profit and errors

Depreciation is an expense with no cash payment, so it lowers profit and the carrying value of assets but does not change the bank balance. That lets you trace an error through the statements:

| Error | Effect on profit | Effect on the statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation charge omitted | Overstated by the charge | Carrying value overstated by the same amount |
| Reducing balance applied to cost, not carrying value | Understated by the excess charge | Carrying value understated |
| Loss on disposal shown as a profit | Overstated by twice the loss | No effect on the carrying value of assets still held |

At Kestrel Print, charging 20% of cost on the machinery gives 12,000 instead of 7,680. Depreciation is overstated by 4,320, so profit is understated by 4,320 and machinery is shown at 26,400 instead of 30,720.

## Common mistakes

- Charging reducing balance on cost after the first year because the trial balance shows the asset at cost.
- Giving a full year's charge to an asset bought or sold during the year when the note says monthly.
- Transferring only the opening depreciation of a sold asset to the disposal account.
- Crediting depreciation to the asset account when the books use a provision account.

## Exam technique

"Calculate" needs a working line before each figure. "Record" needs ledger accounts or journal entries with dates, the other account's name and totals. "Explain" needs a reason linked to the asset named. In financial statement questions, show the depreciation working beside the statement so one slip does not hide correct method.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the provision for depreciation in a trial balance this year's figure?**
No. It is the depreciation built up by the start of the year. Add this year's charge to it before showing the statement of financial position.

**What base is used for reducing balance depreciation from a trial balance?**
Cost less the provision for depreciation shown in the trial balance, which is the carrying value at the start of the year.
