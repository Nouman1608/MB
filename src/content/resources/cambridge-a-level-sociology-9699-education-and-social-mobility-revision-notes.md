These notes condense section 5.2, *Education and social mobility*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. The four syllabus strands are: equal opportunity and meritocracy; how far education systems are meritocratic today; education and life chances, including the consequences of under-achievement for individuals and society; and evidence and arguments linking education and social mobility.

Full explanations and worked answers: [education and social mobility study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-education-and-social-mobility/).

## The argument in brief

Education is the main official route to better life chances, and expansion has gone with large **absolute** upward mobility. Yet **relative** chances between classes have changed far less, because advantaged families convert resources into credentials (Bourdieu, Brown), because pupils from different classes make different choices at equal attainment (Boudon; Breen and Goldthorpe), and because credentials lose value as more people gain them (Hirsch, Collins, Dore).

## Kinds of equality

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equality of access | Same schools and exams open to all |
| Equality of opportunity | Genuinely equal chance of success; hidden barriers removed |
| Equality of outcome | Broadly equal results or rewards between groups |
| Meritocracy | Rewards allocated by ability plus effort (Young, 1958) |

## Is the system meritocratic? Evidence grid

| Supports meritocracy | Challenges meritocracy |
|---|---|
| Saunders, *Unequal but Fair?* (1996): ability and effort predicted outcomes better than class in a British cohort | Halsey, Heath and Ridge, *Origins and Destinations* (1980): service-class boys far more likely to reach university |
| Mass schooling and wider higher education | Brown (1990): **parentocracy** — wealth plus parental wishes |
| Anonymous marking and anti-discrimination law | Collins, *The Credential Society* (1979): credentials filter for status culture |
| Functionalist role allocation | Bourdieu: schools reward cultural capital as if it were ability |

**Counter to Saunders:** childhood "ability" is shaped by class, so controlling for it can hide class effects.

## Explaining the link between background and education

- **Boudon (1974):**
  - *primary effects* — class differences in attainment;
  - *secondary effects* — class differences in choices among pupils with equal attainment.
- **Breen and Goldthorpe (1997): relative risk aversion** — families mainly aim to avoid downward mobility. Middle-class children need university to stay level; working-class children can maintain position without it, so the risks weigh more.
- **Turner (1960):** *contest* mobility (open race, late selection; associated with the USA) versus *sponsored* mobility (early selection of an elite; associated with England's selective system of the time).

## Life chances and positional goods

- Life chances: opportunities for income, health, secure work, housing and long life.
- **Hirsch (1976): positional goods** — their value depends on how many others hold them; degrees lose market value as they spread.
- **Dore (1976): diploma disease** — rising entry requirements.
- **Collins (1979): credentialism** — jobs demand credentials beyond what the work requires.

## Consequences of under-achievement

| Individual | Society |
|---|---|
| Lower earnings, fewer job options | Wasted talent, lower productivity |
| Higher risk of unemployment | Welfare costs |
| Less confidence and civic participation | Weaker cohesion; groups feel excluded |
| Fewer resources for own children | Inequality passed across generations |

**Perspective split:** functionalists and social democrats see waste to be fixed; Marxists see under-achievement as useful for capitalism's need for low-paid labour.

## Three positions on education and mobility

1. **Engine:** schooling lets talent rise (functionalism).
2. **Reproducer:** schooling turns advantage into credentials (Marxism, Bourdieu).
3. **Necessary but not sufficient:** mobility also depends on the occupational structure; without new good jobs, expansion produces credential inflation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Social mobility | Movement between social strata |
| Intergenerational mobility | Comparing a person's position with their parents' |
| Intragenerational mobility | Movement within one person's working life |
| Absolute mobility | Total amount of movement |
| Relative mobility | Chances of people from different origins reaching the same position |
| Primary / secondary effects | Class differences in attainment / in choices at equal attainment |
| Relative risk aversion | Families' priority of avoiding downward mobility |
| Parentocracy | Success shaped by parental wealth and wishes |
| Credentialism | Rising demand for qualifications beyond job needs |
| Positional good | Good whose value falls as more people hold it |
| Contest / sponsored mobility | Open race with late selection / early selection of an elite |

## Exam traps

- Assuming more qualifications means more mobility; relative chances may be unchanged.
- Writing about meritocracy without defining merit (ability plus effort).
- Treating Saunders as a functionalist; he is often grouped with the New Right, and his argument is empirical.
- Forgetting the **society** half of "consequences of under-achievement".
- Applying Turner's categories as if they describe today's systems exactly; use them as ideal types.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish equality of opportunity from equality of outcome.
2. What is the difference between Boudon's primary and secondary effects?
3. Explain relative risk aversion in one sentence.
4. What did Halsey, Heath and Ridge find about university entry?
5. What does parentocracy mean?
6. Give Saunders's main conclusion and one criticism.
7. What is a positional good?
8. Define contest mobility.
9. Give two consequences of under-achievement for society.
10. Why can absolute mobility rise while relative mobility stays stable?
11. What did Collins argue about credentials?

### Answers

1. Opportunity means an equal chance to compete and succeed; outcome means broadly equal results or rewards between groups.
2. Primary effects are class differences in attainment; secondary effects are class differences in choices among pupils who perform equally.
3. Families aim above all to avoid their children falling below the parents' class, so they judge education's costs and risks differently.
4. Boys from service-class backgrounds were far more likely than working-class boys to reach university.
5. A system in which a child's education depends on the wealth and wishes of parents rather than ability and effort (Brown, 1990).
6. Ability and effort explain outcomes better than class, so Britain is broadly meritocratic; critics say measured ability is itself shaped by class.
7. A good whose value depends on how many others have it, such as a degree in a crowded job market.
8. An open race for success with late, reversible selection, which Turner linked to the United States.
9. Any two: wasted talent, lower productivity, higher welfare costs, weaker cohesion, inequality reproduced across generations.
10. Expansion of professional jobs moves many people up, but advantaged families keep their head start, so each class's relative chances change little.
11. Employers use qualifications to filter for status culture, so demand for credentials rises beyond what jobs require.
