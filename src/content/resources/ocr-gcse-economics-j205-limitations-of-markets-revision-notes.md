Recall notes for section 3.8 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. The [limitations of markets study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-limitations-of-markets/) explains every idea with worked examples; the [limitations of markets practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-limitations-of-markets-practice/) test them. All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain **positive and negative externalities**.
- Explain policies to correct them: **taxation and subsidies, state provision, legislation and regulation, information provision**.
- **Evaluate** the use and impact of these policies.
- **Evaluate** their costs, including **opportunity cost**, and benefits.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Third party | Someone outside a transaction who is affected by it |
| Externality | A cost or benefit to a third party not reflected in the market price |
| Negative externality | An external cost, such as pollution |
| Positive externality | An external benefit, such as reduced spread of disease |
| Private cost | Cost paid by the producer or consumer |
| External cost | Cost imposed on third parties |
| Social cost | Private cost + external cost |
| Social benefit | Private benefit + external benefit |
| State provision | Government supplies the good directly, often free |
| Regulation | Rules that limit or require certain actions |
| Information provision | Campaigns, labels and education to change behaviour |

## The core idea in three lines

- Markets ignore external costs and benefits.
- Negative externalities → **overproduction**; positive externalities → **underproduction**.
- Government policy tries to move output towards the level best for society.

## Production or consumption?

| | Negative | Positive |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Factory fumes, quarry noise | Research others benefit from; firm training workers who later move |
| Consumption | Smoking near others, car exhaust, litter | Vaccination, education, well-kept gardens |

## Policies at a glance

| Policy | Used for | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Negative | Raises cost and price, so less is produced and consumed |
| Subsidy | Positive | Lowers cost and price, so more is produced and consumed |
| State provision | Positive | Supplies the good, often free, so use rises |
| Legislation and regulation | Mainly negative | Bans or limits harmful activity; can require beneficial activity |
| Information provision | Both | Informs people of harms or benefits so they choose differently |

## Methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Social cost per unit | Private cost + external cost |
| Total external cost | Output × external cost per unit |
| Tax revenue | Tax per unit × new quantity |
| Subsidy cost | Subsidy per unit × new quantity |
| Harm avoided | Fall in output × external cost per unit |

## Worked reminders

**Tax.** External cost £9 per unit; a £9 tax cuts output from 60,000 to 50,000 units. Revenue = 50,000 × £9 = **£450,000**; harm avoided = 10,000 × £9 = **£90,000**.

**Subsidy.** A £30 subsidy per training course; 8,000 courses taken. Cost = **£240,000**. If 6,500 would have been taken anyway, only 1,500 are extra.

## Evaluation grid

| Policy | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Revenue; reduces quantity; firms choose how to adapt | External cost hard to measure; weak if demand inelastic; can be regressive |
| Subsidy | More of a good thing; cheaper for users | Opportunity cost; pays for some who would buy anyway |
| State provision | Access for all | High cost; possible overuse or inefficiency |
| Regulation | Certain; good for serious harm | Enforcement costs; raises firms' costs; evasion |
| Information | Cheap; keeps choice | Slow; may be ignored |

**Evaluate by asking:** size of the externality, effectiveness, who pays, opportunity cost, side effects, and whether a mix works better.

## Exam traps

- Always **name the third party**.
- A tax **reduces**, it does not eliminate, the harm.
- Subsidies and state provision have an **opportunity cost**.
- Match the policy to the externality and say why.
- External cost is **not** the firm's own cost.

## Self-test

1. Define an externality.
2. Give an example of a negative externality from consumption.
3. State the formula for social cost.
4. A good has a private cost of £45 per unit and an external cost of £9. What is its social cost per unit?
5. Is a good with a positive externality overproduced or underproduced by the market?
6. A £9 per unit tax is placed on a good and 50,000 units are then sold. Calculate tax revenue.
7. A £30 subsidy is paid on each of 8,000 training courses. What does it cost?
8. Which policy is most suited to toxic waste dumping? Why?
9. Give one weakness of information provision.
10. Explain why a subsidy has an opportunity cost.
11. Why might a tax reduce output by very little?
12. Name two policies that can correct a positive externality.

### Answers

1. A **cost or benefit to a third party** that is not reflected in the market price.
2. For example, **smoking** harming people nearby, or **litter** from takeaway food.
3. Social cost = **private cost + external cost**.
4. 45 + 9 = **£54**.
5. **Underproduced.**
6. 50,000 × £9 = **£450,000**.
7. 8,000 × £30 = **£240,000**.
8. **Legislation and regulation**, because the harm is serious and a ban gives certainty.
9. Any one: **slow**, uncertain results; people may **ignore** it; firms' advertising may outweigh it.
10. The money paid in subsidy **could have been spent on something else**, such as schools, or used to cut taxes.
11. If demand is **price inelastic**, a higher price cuts quantity only a little.
12. Any two: **subsidy, state provision, information provision**, regulation requiring the activity.
