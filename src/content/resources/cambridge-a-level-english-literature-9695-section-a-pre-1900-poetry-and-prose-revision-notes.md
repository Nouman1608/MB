Condensed notes for the pre-1900 half of Paper 4. For the full method and an annotated AO5 paragraph,
use the [Paper 4 Section A Pre-1900 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-english-literature-9695-section-a-pre-1900-poetry-and-prose/).

## Section A in five lines

- One essay on one pre-1900 set text, worth **25 marks**.
- **(a)** whole-text essay or **(b)** essay on a printed **passage or poem**.
- Assessed on **AO1 to AO5**, each 20%.
- Closed book; no dictionaries.
- **Form rule**: across Paper 4, one poetry essay and one prose essay.

## Choosing your pairing

| If your pre-1900 text is… | Your post-1900 text must be… |
|---|---|
| A novel (Austen, Eliot, Hardy) | Poetry |
| Poetry (Chaucer, Shakespeare's sonnets, Whitman; Byron from 2027) | Prose |

## Key terms: prose

| Term | Meaning | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Omniscient narrator | Knows all characters' thoughts | Discussing judgement and overview |
| Intrusive narrator | Comments directly on events, may address the reader | Eliot's reflective passages |
| Free indirect style | Character's thought in narrator's voice | Austen's irony; whose view is this? |
| Periodic sentence | Main clause delayed to the end | Suspense, balance, formality |
| Marriage plot | Narrative directed towards marriage | Austen; social and economic pressure |
| Parallel plots | Several storylines mirroring each other | *Middlemarch*'s marriages |
| Determinism | Lives shaped by forces beyond control | Hardy's institutions, chance, coincidence |

## Key terms: poetry

| Term | Meaning | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Heroic couplet | Rhymed iambic pentameter pairs | Chaucer's rhythm; pointed endings |
| Frame narrative | A tale set within a teller's story | Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale |
| Romance | Medieval tale of knights, quests, marvels | The Wife's Tale |
| Shakespearean sonnet | Three quatrains and a couplet | Tracking argument through form |
| Volta | Turn in argument | Where and whether the sonnet turns |
| Sonnet sequence | Linked sonnets read as a series | Poems answering one another |
| Free verse | No fixed metre or rhyme | Whitman; pre-1900 but formally radical |
| Catalogue | Extended list | Whitman's inclusiveness and energy |
| Anaphora | Repeated opening words | Whitman's rhetorical momentum |

## Text reminders

- **Austen**: irony through free indirect style; marriage and money as structural pressures.
- **Eliot, *Middlemarch***: intrusive, reflective narrator; parallel marriages; a provincial town as a
  social web.
- **Hardy, *Jude the Obscure***: aspiration against institutions of class, education, marriage and
  church; tragic structure.
- **Chaucer**: Middle English couplets; Prologue sets experience against written authority; the Tale's
  quest to learn what women most desire.
- **Shakespeare's sonnets**: argument moving through quatrains; the couplet as resolution or twist.
- **Whitman**: free verse, long lines, catalogues, an all-embracing "I".

## Reading Chaucer's Middle English quickly

- **Read aloud**: the rhythm and couplet rhymes become clear, and many words are recognisable by sound.
- **Final -e** is often pronounced, which helps the metre.
- **Common words** to know on sight:

| Middle English | Modern sense |
|---|---|
| eek | also |
| swich | such |
| wight | person, creature |
| sith | since |
| ywis | certainly |
| auctoritee | authority |
| housbonde | husband |
| wo | woe, sorrow |

- In a (b) answer, **quote the original** and analyse it; do not translate whole lines into modern
  English, which wastes time and loses the effect of the language.

## Context: what to use and how

- Marriage, property and inheritance; class and education; religion and doubt; gender roles.
- Literary conventions of the period: the moralising narrator, courtly romance, the sonnet tradition.
- **Rule**: one sentence of context tied to one choice in the text.

## AO5 debates to prepare

- Wife of Bath: anti-feminist caricature or challenge to male authority?
- The marriage plot: conservative reward or ironic critique?
- Tragic novels: blame on individuals, society or fate?
- Sonnets: sincere feeling or rhetorical performance?
- Whitman: inclusive democratic vision or a self that absorbs others?

**AO5 pattern**: reading → test against the text → judgement.

## Answer shapes

**(b) passage or poem**: locate → thesis → close analysis (voice, syntax, form, imagery) → link outward
→ evaluated AO5.

**(a) whole text**: positioned thesis → moments across the whole text, including the ending → method in
every paragraph → AO5 woven through.

## Exam traps

- Criticising older conventions instead of analysing them.
- Judging by modern norms with no sense of period.
- Context paragraphs detached from the text.
- Paraphrasing older language rather than analysing it.
- Breaking the poetry-and-prose form rule.
- Discussing only famous scenes from a long novel.

## Self-test

1. What is the form rule for Paper 4?
2. If your Section A text is *Middlemarch*, what form must your Section B text be?
3. What is printed for a (b) question in this section?
4. Which assessment objectives are tested, and at what weighting?
5. Define free indirect style.
6. What is an intrusive narrator?
7. In which language is *The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale* studied?
8. What does the Wife set against written authority in her Prologue?
9. Describe the structure of a Shakespearean sonnet.
10. Name two features of Whitman's verse.
11. Give one AO5 debate about a pre-1900 set text.
12. What is the rule for using context?

**Answers:** 1. One poetry essay and one prose essay, one from each section. 2. Poetry. 3. A passage or a
poem from the set text. 4. AO1 to AO5, 20% each. 5. A character's thoughts presented in the narrator's
voice, blending the two. 6. A narrator who comments directly on events or addresses the reader. 7. Middle
English. 8. Her own experience of marriage. 9. Three quatrains and a closing couplet. 10. Any two: free
verse, long lines, catalogues, anaphora, an all-embracing "I". 11. For example whether the Wife of Bath is
a caricature or a challenge to male authority. 12. Tie a brief contextual point to a specific choice in
the text.
