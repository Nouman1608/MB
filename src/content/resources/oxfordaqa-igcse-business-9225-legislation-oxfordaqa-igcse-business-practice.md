All businesses and figures are invented. Section B questions refer to the case study about Ferncroft Joinery.

> **Case study: Ferncroft Joinery**
>
> Ferncroft Joinery makes wooden windows and doors for homeowners. It
> employs 18 workshop staff. Ten of them are paid the national minimum
> wage and each works 40 hours a week for 50 weeks a year. The minimum
> wage is rising from $6.50 to $7.10 an hour. After a worker was injured
> by a saw, a health and safety inspector recommended a dust extraction
> system costing $14,000 and safety training for all 18 workshop staff at
> $150 each. Ferncroft has also received complaints that 25 windows it
> fitted last year leak; under consumer law it must repair them, at a cost
> of $220 each. Ferncroft's annual profit is $65,000.

## Section A

**1.** Which **one** area of law requires a business to provide protective clothing for workers who use dangerous machinery? **[1]**
- A. Consumer law
- B. Health and safety law
- C. Competition law
- D. Tax law

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a possible consequence for a business of failing to follow consumer law? **[1]**
- A. A rise in the national minimum wage
- B. A product recall
- C. A fall in interest rates
- D. Movement of labour

**3.** Define the term "national minimum wage". **[2]**

**4.** State **two** ways in which employment law can affect how a business recruits staff. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** benefit to a business of following health and safety law. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Calculate the extra yearly cost to Ferncroft of the rise in the minimum wage. **[2]**

**7.** Calculate the total cost of the inspector's recommendations. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the cost of repairing the leaking windows. **[1]**

**9.** Explain **two** possible consequences for Ferncroft if it ignores the inspector's recommendations. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which consumer protection laws affect Ferncroft Joinery. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse the benefits and drawbacks to Ferncroft of the rise in the national minimum wage. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate whether legislation is more of a cost or a benefit to Ferncroft Joinery. Use your answers to Questions 6, 7 and 8. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The lowest hourly rate of pay [1] that an employer is legally allowed to pay its workers [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: job adverts and interviews must not discriminate [1]; successful applicants must be given proper contracts [1]; the business may need to check applicants are allowed to work; pay offered must be at least the minimum wage. **[2]**

**5.** Following the law reduces the number of accidents [1], so fewer staff are absent and less output is lost, and the business avoids fines or compensation claims [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Hours a year = 10 × 40 × 50 = 20,000 hours [1]. Extra cost = 20,000 × ($7.10 − $6.50) = 20,000 × $0.60 = **$12,000** a year [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Training = 18 × $150 = $2,700 [1]. Total = $14,000 + $2,700 = **$16,700** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** 25 × $220 = **$5,500** [1]. **[1]**

**9.** **Penalties:** the inspector could fine Ferncroft or order it to stop using the saws until they are made safe [1], which would halt production and delay customers' orders [1].
**Further accidents:** without training and dust extraction, another worker could be injured or made ill [1], leading to compensation claims, absence and difficulty recruiting joiners who hear about the poor safety record [1]. **[4]**

**10.** **Repairs:** consumer law requires goods to be fit for purpose, so Ferncroft must repair the 25 leaking windows [1], costing $5,500 and taking joiners away from new orders [1].
**Quality control:** to avoid future claims, Ferncroft may need to test windows more carefully before fitting [1], which raises costs but protects its reputation with homeowners [1]. **[4]**

**11.** **Drawbacks:** the wage bill rises by $12,000 a year [1], cutting profit from $65,000 to $53,000 if nothing else changes [1]. Ferncroft may need to raise its prices, which could lose some customers [1]. **Benefits:** better-paid workshop staff may be more motivated and less likely to leave [1], which saves the cost of recruiting and training new joiners [1]. Rival joiners must pay the same minimum, so Ferncroft is not placed at a disadvantage and they may raise prices too [1]. **[6]**

**12.** **Cost:** together, the three items cost $34,200 in the first year ($12,000 + $16,700 + $5,500) [1], over half of the $65,000 profit [1]; the minimum wage rise is a cost every year, not just once [1].
**Benefit:** the safety measures should prevent further injuries, avoiding compensation, absence and bad publicity [1]; repairing the windows keeps homeowners' trust, and a satisfied customer may recommend Ferncroft [1]; all rivals face the same laws, so the costs are partly shared across the market [1].
**Judgement:** in the short term legislation is mainly a cost, because $34,200 is a large share of profit [1]. In the longer term it is closer to a benefit, because the safety and quality costs are mostly one-off and protect Ferncroft from much larger losses [1]. The answer depends on whether Ferncroft can pass on the wage rise in its prices without losing customers [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the full new wage rather than the increase of $0.60 when calculating the extra cost.
- Forgetting to multiply training by all 18 staff.
- Treating legislation only as a cost and missing the benefits.
- Writing about "the law" in general instead of using Ferncroft's saws, windows and wages.
- Adding the one-off costs to the yearly wage rise without noting that they are different kinds of cost.
