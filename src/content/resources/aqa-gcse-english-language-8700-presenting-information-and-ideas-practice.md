## Section A — Short questions

**1.** State **three** requirements the specification sets for the spoken language presentation. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what "competency basis" means for the way the presentation is graded. **[2]**

**3.** Turn each flat subject into a topic with enough depth for challenging ideas: (a) *video games*; (b) *the school canteen*. **[2]**

**4.** A student plans to present to their teacher alone. Explain what the specification requires, and suggest a suitable form. **[2]**

**5.** Give **three** spoken signposting phrases and say where in a talk each would be used. **[3]**

**6.** Rewrite this written sentence so that it works better when spoken aloud: *"The proposal, which was first suggested by the council in a report that few residents read, would, if adopted, reduce the number of buses serving outlying villages."* **[3]**

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why one vivid example can be more effective in a talk than several statistics. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** Plan a presentation on the topic *"Should every secondary school have a four-day week?"* for an audience of parents. Give an opening hook, at least three main sections and a conclusion, and explain how the plan suits the audience. **[6]**

**9.** Read this original opening of a student's talk.

> *"Right, so, my talk is about recycling. Recycling is when you recycle things. There are lots of things you can
> recycle like paper and plastic and glass and stuff. Recycling is good. So yeah, that's my introduction."*

Identify **three** weaknesses in relation to the criteria and rewrite the opening so that it could meet the Merit criteria. **[6]**

**10.** Compare how you would adapt a talk on *"Why young people should volunteer"* for (a) an audience of Year 7 students and (b) the school's governors. Give **two** differences of content or style for each audience. **[4]**

**11.** A student says: *"I'll put my whole talk on the slides so I don't forget anything."* Explain **two** problems with this approach and describe a better use of notes or slides. **[3]**

**12.** Read this original summary of a student's presentation.

> *Jamal speaks clearly and audibly in formal English. His topic, "Is it fair that young people pay more for car
> insurance?", weighs the interests of drivers, insurers and road users. He signposts each section, opens with a short
> true story about his brother, uses a contrast between two drivers, and ends by returning to his brother. The
> audience of classmates follows easily, and his vocabulary is varied and precise. He answers questions in detail but
> does not develop his answers beyond what he was asked.*

Which features of his **presenting** suggest the Distinction criteria for that strand, and what would stop the overall grade being a Distinction? **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three of the following, one mark each (3 × [1]): a prepared presentation on a specific topic; the topic identified in advance and agreed with the teacher; as a guide, no more than ten minutes; formal in register; planned and organised; listening and responding to questions and feedback; if the audience is the teacher alone, designed for a potentially wider audience.

**2.** The presentation is graded as a whole rather than marked [1], and a student must meet every criterion for a grade to be awarded that grade [1].

**3.** One mark each for a topic that is specific and has more than one side. For example: (a) "Should video games with loot boxes be restricted for under-16s?" [1]; (b) "Should the school canteen stop selling sugary drinks?" [1].

**4.** The presentation must be designed so that it could have a wider audience than one person [1]; a suitable form would replicate, for example, a television or radio interview, or a pitch to a panel [1].

**5.** Three phrases, one mark each with a sensible placing (3 × [1]). For example: "Today I want to look at three questions" (opening, previewing the structure); "That brings me to my second point" (transition between sections); "So, to sum up" (before the conclusion).

**6.** The long sentence is broken into shorter sentences, each with one idea [1]; the embedded clauses are removed or turned into separate statements [1]; the main point is made clear and early [1]. For example: "The council wants to cut buses to the villages. It first suggested this in a report. Not many of us read that report. But if the plan goes ahead, the villages will have fewer buses."

**7.** Listeners hear information only once and cannot reread it, so a vivid example is easier to remember [1]; an example connects with feelings and experience, which helps achieve a persuasive purpose, while a run of figures can blur together [1].

**8.** Award 6 marks:
- an opening hook suited to parents, for example a typical Friday from a parent's point of view [1];
- a first section that weighs benefits, such as time for family or independent study [1];
- a second section that treats concerns seriously, such as childcare and lost teaching time [1];
- a third section offering a conclusion or compromise [1];
- a conclusion that returns to the hook and states a clear view [1];
- an explanation of how the plan suits parents: practical concerns, a measured tone and respect for different family circumstances [1].

**9.** Three weaknesses, one mark each: informal fillers ("Right, so", "stuff", "So yeah") do not suit the formal setting [1]; the ideas are straightforward and circular ("recycling is when you recycle things") [1]; there is no hook, no signposting and no sense of the audience [1]. A rewrite earns up to 3 more marks: a hook that engages the audience [1], a clear, challenging focus [1] and a preview of the structure in formal register [1]. For example: "Last week, our school threw away four bags of plastic at lunch alone. Where does it go? Today I want to ask whether recycling is really working, look at what happens to our waste, and suggest one change we could make by the end of term."

**10.** Two differences for each audience, one mark each (4 × [1]). Year 7: simpler vocabulary and short explanations; concrete, relatable examples such as a school litter-pick; an invitation to join a specific activity; a lively, encouraging tone. Governors: evidence of benefit to students and the community; a measured, formal tone; practical points about organisation and safety; a clear proposal or request.

**11.** The speaker is likely to read from the slides, reducing eye contact and natural delivery [1]; audiences read the text instead of listening, so the slides compete with the speaker [1]. Better: cue cards with keywords and slides with a single image, a key figure or a few words that support the point being made [1].

**12.** Distinction features of presenting: the topic carries sophisticated ideas because it weighs competing interests [1]; vocabulary is varied and precise, suggesting a sophisticated repertoire [1]; an effective range of strategies engages the audience: an anecdote, a contrast and a returning ending [1]; the purpose is achieved and the audience follows easily [1]. What stops a Distinction overall: on a competency basis every criterion must be met, and his answers to questions, though detailed, are not developed with further ideas, which matches the Merit description for responding rather than the Distinction one ("responds perceptively and if appropriate elaborates with further ideas and information") [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a topic too flat for challenging ideas.
- Writing an essay and reading it aloud.
- Forgetting spoken signposting.
- Planning content without thinking about the audience.
- Ignoring the questions and feedback that follow the talk.
