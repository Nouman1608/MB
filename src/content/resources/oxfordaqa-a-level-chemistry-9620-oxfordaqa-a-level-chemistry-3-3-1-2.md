## What the specification asks for

- Reactions of organic compounds can be explained using mechanisms.
- Free-radical mechanisms: the unpaired electron in a radical is shown by a dot; curly arrows are not required. Write balanced equations for the steps.
- Other mechanisms: the formation of a covalent bond is shown by a curly arrow that starts from a lone electron pair or from another covalent bond; the breaking of a covalent bond is shown by a curly arrow starting from the bond.
- Outline mechanisms by drawing the structures of the species involved and curly arrows to represent the movement of electron pairs.

## Two ways a bond can break

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. When it breaks, the pair can be split in two ways.

| Fission | What happens to the pair | Particles formed | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Homolytic** | One electron goes to each atom | Two **radicals** (each with an unpaired electron) | Free-radical substitution (3.3.2.4), initiated by UV light |
| **Heterolytic** | Both electrons go to one atom | A positive ion and a negative ion | Nucleophilic substitution, elimination, electrophilic addition |

Heterolytic fission of a polar bond normally gives the electron pair to the more electronegative atom. In C–Br, the bromine takes the pair and leaves as Br⁻.

## Free-radical mechanisms

### Notation

- A **radical** is a species with an unpaired electron. Show it with a dot on the atom that carries the unpaired electron: Cl•, •CH₃, CH₃CH₂•.
- Do not draw curly arrows for radical steps.
- Write each step as a **separate balanced equation**.

### The three kinds of step

| Step | Radicals on the left | Radicals on the right | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiation | 0 | 2 | Radicals are made, usually by UV light breaking a weak bond such as Cl–Cl |
| Propagation | 1 | 1 | A radical reacts with a molecule to give a product and a new radical; the chain continues |
| Termination | 2 | 0 | Two radicals pair up; the chain stops |

### Worked example 1: radical steps for ethane and chlorine

Write the steps that form chloroethane when ethane reacts with chlorine in UV light.

```
Initiation:    Cl₂ → 2Cl•
Propagation:   CH₃CH₃ + Cl• → CH₃CH₂• + HCl
               CH₃CH₂• + Cl₂ → CH₃CH₂Cl + Cl•
Termination:   CH₃CH₂• + Cl• → CH₃CH₂Cl
               2CH₃CH₂• → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃
               2Cl• → Cl₂
```

Checks to make on every line:

- Atoms balance (count C, H and Cl).
- The dot is on the right atom: the ethyl radical has its unpaired electron on carbon.
- The first propagation step makes **HCl**, not H•. Chlorine radicals remove a hydrogen atom.
- The two propagation steps add up to the overall equation: CH₃CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃CH₂Cl + HCl.

The butane formed in termination is evidence that ethyl radicals are present, because it can only form when two of them join.

## Curly-arrow mechanisms

### What a curly arrow means

A curly arrow shows the movement of **a pair of electrons**. Every arrow has a definite start and end.

**Where an arrow can start:**

- a **lone pair** (draw the pair as two dots on the atom), or
- a **covalent bond** (start at the middle of the bond line).

**Where an arrow ends:**

- at an **atom**, when the pair forms a new bond to it or leaves on it as a lone pair, or
- at the space **between two atoms**, when the pair forms a new bond between them.

An arrow never starts at a positive charge, at an atom with no lone pair, or at a hydrogen atom.

### Keeping track of charge

Total charge is the same before and after every step. If a neutral molecule gains an electron pair from another species, check where the charges end up:

- the atom that **donates** a lone pair to make a new bond becomes more positive by one
- the atom that **receives** a bonding pair as a lone pair, when a bond breaks, becomes more negative by one.

### Partial charges

Show δ+ and δ− on polar bonds where they explain the attack. A nucleophile (an electron pair donor) attacks the δ+ carbon of C–X. An electrophile (an electron pair acceptor) is attracted to the high electron density of a C=C bond.

## Worked example 2: outlining nucleophilic substitution

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromobutane with hydroxide ions.*

```
            δ+    δ−
HO⁻ :  →  CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂–Br     →   CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂–OH  +  :Br⁻
```

Arrows, in words:

1. From a **lone pair on the oxygen** of OH⁻ to the **δ+ carbon** bonded to Br. This forms the new C–O bond.
2. From the **C–Br bond** to the **Br** atom. The bond breaks heterolytically and bromine leaves as Br⁻.

For full marks: show the lone pair and the negative charge on the hydroxide oxygen, the δ+ and δ− on C–Br, both arrows starting in the right places, and Br⁻ as a product. The detailed chemistry of this reaction is in the [halogenoalkanes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes/).

## Worked example 3: outlining electrophilic addition

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of but-2-ene with hydrogen bromide.*

Step 1:

- Arrow from the **C=C double bond** to the **H** of H–Br (δ+ on H, δ− on Br).
- Arrow from the **H–Br bond** to **Br**.
- Products of step 1: the carbocation CH₃C⁺HCH₂CH₃ and Br⁻.

Step 2:

- Arrow from a **lone pair on Br⁻** to the **positive carbon**.
- Product: 2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃.

Charge check: step 1 starts neutral and ends with +1 and −1; step 2 ends neutral. But-2-ene is symmetrical, so only one product forms; unsymmetrical alkenes, and the reasons for major and minor products, are in the [alkenes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-alkenes/).

## Worked example 4: an annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why curly arrows are drawn in the mechanism of Worked example 2 but not in the reaction of methane with chlorine. (2 marks)*

> In the reaction of 1-bromobutane with hydroxide, bonds form and break by the movement of electron pairs (heterolytic fission), which curly arrows represent [1]. In the reaction of methane with chlorine, bonds break homolytically and radicals react using single unpaired electrons; the specification shows these with dots and balanced equations, so curly arrows are not required [1].

## Common mistakes

- Drawing an arrow from H⁺, from a positive charge, or from a bare atom with no lone pair shown.
- Arrows that point the wrong way (from the electrophile to the C=C, or from Br to the C–Br bond).
- Leaving out the lone pair on a nucleophile, or the negative charge.
- Writing H• instead of HCl in the first propagation step.
- Missing the dot on a radical, or putting it on the wrong atom (CH₃• written with the dot on a hydrogen).
- Drawing curly arrows on radical equations.
- A charge that does not balance between the two sides of a step.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Outline the mechanism**: structures of every species, all lone pairs that are used, partial and full charges, and every curly arrow. No words are needed.
- **Write equations for the steps**: one balanced equation per step, dots on radicals, labelled initiation, propagation or termination if the question asks.
- **Explain** (step type): state the number of radicals before and after.
- **Name the mechanism**: two words that say what attacks and what happens, for example "nucleophilic substitution" or "free-radical substitution".
