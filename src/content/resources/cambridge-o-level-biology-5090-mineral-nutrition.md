This guide covers subtopic **6.3 Mineral nutrition** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to explain the importance of nitrate ions for making amino acids, which are required for the production of proteins, and to explain the importance of magnesium ions for making chlorophyll.

The [Plant nutrition guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-plant-nutrition/) covers the whole topic. This subtopic is short, so this page builds depth in the places where marks are won: linking each ion to what it is used for, predicting the effect of a shortage, interpreting experimental data and writing the two-step explanations that examiners expect. Use the [6.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-mineral-nutrition-revision-notes/) for recall and the [6.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-mineral-nutrition-practice/) to test yourself.

## What mineral ions are, and what they are not

Plants make their own food (carbohydrates) by photosynthesis, using carbon dioxide, water and light. A plant needs more than this to survive. It also needs small amounts of **mineral ions** from the soil. Mineral ions do not supply energy and they are not "plant food". They are **raw materials** for making particular substances that carbon, hydrogen and oxygen alone cannot form.

Two ions matter for this syllabus.

| Ion | Used to make | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrate | Amino acids, which are joined to make proteins | Growth, enzymes, cytoplasm and membranes |
| Magnesium | Chlorophyll | Absorbing light for photosynthesis |

## Nitrate ions and proteins

### The chemical reason

Carbohydrates contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. **Amino acids and proteins also contain nitrogen.** A plant cannot make amino acids from the products of photosynthesis alone. It must take nitrogen from the soil, mostly in the form of **nitrate ions**.

In the cell, the nitrogen from nitrate is combined with carbohydrate (made in photosynthesis) to make **amino acids**. The cell then joins amino acids in long chains to make **proteins**.

### Why proteins matter

Proteins have many roles in a plant cell.

- **Enzymes** are proteins, and all the reactions in the plant depend on them.
- Proteins are part of **cytoplasm** and **cell membranes**.
- New cells need new protein, so **growth** depends on a supply of nitrate.

A full explanation therefore runs in three steps: nitrate contains nitrogen, nitrogen is needed to make amino acids, amino acids make proteins that are needed for growth and for enzymes.

### Effects of nitrate deficiency

If a plant lacks nitrate, it cannot make enough protein.

- Growth is **stunted**, because new cells cannot be built.
- Leaves are **yellow**, particularly the older, lower leaves, because the plant moves the little nitrogen available to the growing young leaves.
- Roots and shoots are poorly developed.

## Magnesium ions and chlorophyll

**Chlorophyll** is the green pigment that absorbs light energy in the chloroplasts. Each chlorophyll molecule contains a **magnesium** atom at its centre. A plant without magnesium cannot make chlorophyll.

### Effects of magnesium deficiency

- Leaves turn **yellow** (called chlorosis), often first the older leaves and often between the veins, which stay green.
- Less chlorophyll means less light is absorbed, so the rate of **photosynthesis falls**.
- Less glucose is made, so less is available for respiration, growth and storage, and the plant is small and weak.

A strong answer on magnesium has two links: magnesium makes chlorophyll, and chlorophyll absorbs light for photosynthesis.

## How the ions reach the plant

Both ions are dissolved in the **soil water**, at low concentration. Root hair cells absorb them by **active transport**, against a concentration gradient and using energy from respiration (see subtopic 3.2). The ions then travel in the **xylem** with water to the leaves and growing regions.

Soil can run short of these ions where crops are removed each year, so farmers add **fertilisers** containing nitrate and other ions. Magnesium is also added where soils are poor in it.

## Investigating mineral deficiency

A common method is the **water culture** experiment. It is not named in the syllabus statements, but it shows the same ideas.

1. Prepare three sets of identical seedlings.
2. Grow set A in a **complete** solution (all ions).
3. Grow set B in a solution **without nitrate**.
4. Grow set C in a solution **without magnesium**.
5. Keep light, temperature and water volume the same. Air bubbled through the solution supplies oxygen for respiration in the roots.
6. After several weeks, compare height, leaf colour and mass.

Set A is the **control**, because it shows what normal growth looks like.

### Worked example: reading the data

*After four weeks, the mean heights of seedlings were: complete solution 24.0 cm; without nitrate 9.0 cm; without magnesium 17.0 cm. Calculate the percentage reduction in height for each deficient set, and explain the results.*

1. Without nitrate: reduction = 24.0 - 9.0 = 15.0 cm. Percentage = 15.0 divided by 24.0 x 100 = **62.5 per cent**.
2. Without magnesium: reduction = 24.0 - 17.0 = 7.0 cm. Percentage = 7.0 divided by 24.0 x 100 = **29.2 per cent**.
3. **Explanation of nitrate**: with no nitrate, the seedlings cannot make amino acids and so cannot make proteins. They cannot build new cells, so growth is stunted.
4. **Explanation of magnesium**: with no magnesium, the seedlings cannot make chlorophyll. Their leaves are yellow and they absorb less light, so they photosynthesise less and make less glucose for growth.
5. **Comparison**: in this experiment, the lack of nitrate reduced growth more than the lack of magnesium. In both cases the complete solution is the control that shows what normal growth looks like.

Note that the data show an association. They do not show how big the effect would be for other species or in soil.

## Writing a two-step explanation

Examiners reward a chain of reasoning. For any mineral ion question, write three short links: the **ion**, the **substance it is used to make**, and the **effect of a shortage**. For example: "Magnesium is needed to make chlorophyll. A plant without magnesium has less chlorophyll, so it absorbs less light and photosynthesises more slowly." If you stop after the first sentence you earn only part of the marks. If you skip the first sentence and write only the effect, the examiner cannot see that you know why the ion is needed.

## Why the same plant cannot just use another ion

Each ion has a specific job, and one ion cannot replace another. Sodium, for example, cannot take the place of magnesium at the centre of a chlorophyll molecule. That is why a plant lacking one ion shows a particular pattern of symptoms even when everything else is plentiful.

## Deficiency symptoms side by side

| Feature | Nitrate deficiency | Magnesium deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Strongly stunted | Reduced |
| Leaf colour | Pale green to yellow overall, older leaves first | Yellow between the veins, older leaves first |
| Substance that cannot be made | Amino acids and proteins | Chlorophyll |
| Process mainly affected | Growth and enzyme production | Photosynthesis |

When a question gives a picture or description, decide first whether the key problem is growth or leaf colour. Stunted growth with yellow leaves points to nitrate. Yellow leaves with green veins point to magnesium. Give the reason as well as the name, because "magnesium" alone earns no explanation mark.

## Fertilisers and farming

In a natural ecosystem, dead plants and animals decay and return nitrate to the soil. On a farm the crop is harvested and taken away, so the minerals are not returned. Over time, the soil becomes short of nitrate and the crop yield falls. Farmers add fertilisers that contain nitrate to replace what has been removed.

Fertilisers raise yield because the plants can make more protein and so grow larger. However, too much fertiliser can be washed into streams and lakes. This can cause problems for water life, which is covered in the later topic on relationships of organisms with the environment. For this subtopic, the key idea is simply that the added ions are used by the plant to make amino acids, proteins and chlorophyll.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that nitrate or magnesium is "plant food" or gives the plant energy.
- Saying that magnesium "makes plants green" without naming chlorophyll.
- Stating that nitrate is needed "for protein" without the middle step: amino acids.
- Writing that plants absorb nitrate by osmosis. Water enters by osmosis; the ions enter by active transport (or diffusion if their concentration is higher outside).
- Forgetting that nitrates contain nitrogen.
- Mixing up the two ions' effects: stunted growth suggests nitrate, yellow leaves point to magnesium (although nitrate shortage also causes yellowing).

## Command words

- **Explain**: give the link between the ion, the substance made and the effect of a shortage.
- **State**: name the ion or substance.
- **Predict**: say what you expect to see in a plant grown without an ion.
- **Calculate**: work out a percentage reduction, showing working.
