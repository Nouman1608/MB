## Population change (4.1–4.4)

**Natural increase rate (NIR)** = birth rate − death rate, usually expressed per 1,000 population per year.

| Rate | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Birth rate** | Live births per 1,000 population per year |
| **Death rate** | Deaths per 1,000 population per year |
| **Fertility rate** | Average births per woman of childbearing age |
| **Infant mortality rate** | Deaths under age 1 per 1,000 live births |

Fertility and mortality are driven by **social** (education, status of women), **economic** (cost of children, female employment), **environmental** (disease, sanitation, climate) and **political** (pro-/anti-natalist policy) factors — always link a named factor to the specific rate it changes rather than describing factors in the abstract.

**Age/sex (population) pyramids** — a wide base signals high birth rate; a narrowing top signals high death rate at older ages; bulges/dents mark migration or historical shocks (war, baby boom). **Dependency ratio** = (population under 15 + population over 64) ÷ population 15–64 × 100. A youthful population has a high dependency ratio dominated by the young; an ageing population has a high ratio dominated by the elderly — the policy response to each is opposite (schools/jobs vs. pensions/healthcare).

**Demographic Transition Model (DTM), Stages 1–5:** Stage 1 — high, fluctuating birth and death rates, low natural increase. Stage 2 — death rate falls sharply (sanitation, medicine), birth rate stays high, rapid natural increase. Stage 3 — birth rate falls (contraception, female education, urbanisation), natural increase slows. Stage 4 — both rates low and stable, near-zero natural increase. Stage 5 (where used) — birth rate falls below death rate, natural decrease. Link each stage transition to a *cause*, not just a description of the line moving.

**Population–resource relationships:** food security depends on production, access and stability of supply; shortages arise from war, climatic hazards (drought, flood) and land degradation; technology (irrigation, high-yield varieties) raises **carrying capacity**. Be able to evaluate — not just define — **overpopulation** (resources cannot sustainably support the population at the current standard of living), **underpopulation**, and **optimum population** (the population level that maximises resource use per capita).

**4.4 Management of natural increase** needs a **named, locatable case study** of a real country's population policy (for example, an anti-natalist or pro-natalist programme), covering what the policy did *and* how effectively it worked — description alone answers AO1, not the AO4 evaluation the question is usually testing.

## Migration (5.1–5.4)

**Migration** here excludes moves under one year's duration. **Push factors** drive people away (conflict, poverty, environmental hazard); **pull factors** attract them (jobs, safety, services). **Chain migration** — later migrants follow an established social network from earlier migrants to the same destination. Migration is shaped by **distance** (distance decay — fewer migrants the further the destination) and **age** (young adults migrate most), and constrained by **barriers** — cost, visas, national borders, family ties.

**Internal migration:** rural–urban movement is driven by the urban pull of jobs and services against rural push of limited opportunity; urban–rural (**counterurbanisation**) reflects the reverse for some HIC groups. **Stepped migration** moves people up the settlement hierarchy (village → town → city) rather than directly to the largest centre. Urban–urban and intra-urban movements reshuffle population within the existing urban system.

**International migration** is either **voluntary** (economic migration, seeking better opportunity) or **forced/involuntary** (conflict, persecution — refugee flows). Both types have impacts on **source areas** (remittances but loss of workforce — "brain drain") and **destination areas** (labour supply, cultural change, pressure on services).

**5.4 Management of international migration** needs a **named case study of one migration stream** — its cause, character, scale, pattern and impact on both ends — again assessed for how well any management response actually worked.

## Settlement (6.1–6.3) and its management (6.4)

**Rural settlement change:** depopulation and declining service provision are common in LICs, MICs and HICs alike, driven by out-migration (often to cities) and the loss of a viable customer base for local shops, schools and transport. A required **case study of one rural settlement or area** should show cause and consequence together, not just describe decline.

**Urbanisation** = the increasing proportion of a population living in urban areas. Distinguish **urban growth** (the increase in absolute urban population, from natural increase and/or migration) from **urbanisation** (the shift in the rural/urban *balance*). Related processes: **counterurbanisation** (movement out of cities to smaller settlements/rural areas), **re-urbanisation** (renewed movement back into inner-city areas, often after regeneration), and **urban renewal** (redeveloping declining urban areas). A **world city** sits at the top of the global urban hierarchy through its concentration of finance, business headquarters and connectivity, not simply its population size.

**Urban structure:** the **Central Business District (CBD)** sits at the point of maximum accessibility, so competes hardest for space — this drives **bid rent theory**: land value (and therefore rent a use can afford to pay) falls with distance from the CBD, producing **functional zonation** (CBD → industrial/transitional zone → residential zones of varying density). **Residential segregation** by income or ethnicity arises from ability to pay, discrimination, and the pull of established communities.

**6.4 Management of urban settlements** requires **two** named case studies: a **shanty town/squatter settlement** in an LIC or MIC (its causes and a management response — upgrading, relocation, sites-and-services), and the **provision of infrastructure** (power or transport) for a city. Keep these as two separate, specific, locatable examples — conflating them, or substituting a generic description for a real place, loses marks.

## Assessment objectives to keep in view

Paper 2 splits 30% AO1 (knowledge), 30% AO2 (application/understanding of process), 28% AO3 (data interpretation) and **12% AO4 (evaluation)**. Every "management" sub-topic (4.4, 5.4, 6.4) is where AO4 is concentrated — a revision note or answer that only narrates what a policy or scheme did, without judging how well it worked or whose interests it served, has answered the wrong assessment objective for that content.

## Quick self-test

- Define natural increase rate and state the formula for the dependency ratio.
- Explain, with a cause, why the death rate falls sharply between DTM Stage 1 and Stage 2.
- Name one push and one pull factor for a real internal migration flow you have studied.
- Distinguish urban growth from urbanisation in one sentence.
- State the two named case studies required for 6.4 and what each must show.
