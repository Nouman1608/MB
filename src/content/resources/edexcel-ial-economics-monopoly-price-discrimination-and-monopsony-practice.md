## Questions

**1.** State two assumptions of monopoly. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two barriers to entry that could protect a monopolist. **[4]**

**3.** A sole ferry operator on an island (invented) faces demand P = 150 − 5Q, where Q is thousands of trips and P is in currency units (cu). Its marginal cost is constant at 30 cu and its total fixed cost is 200 (thousand cu).

**(a)** Derive the marginal revenue function. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit-maximising output and price. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the deadweight loss compared with the output where P = MC. **[3]**

**4.** A water network (invented) has total cost TC = 600 + 5Q and faces demand P = 65 − Q.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising output and price. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the output and price if a regulator imposes P = average cost. Give answers to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why a regulator may not set P = MC. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the conditions necessary for a firm to practise third-degree price discrimination. **[4]**

**6.** A cinema (invented) has constant marginal cost of 4 cu per ticket. Adult demand is P = 40 − 2Q and child demand is P = 24 − 2Q (Q in hundreds of tickets).

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising price and quantity in each market. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate total profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate profit if the cinema must charge one price to everyone, and state the gain from discrimination. **[3]**

**7.** Using your answers to question 6, evaluate whether third-degree price discrimination benefits consumers. **[6]**

**8.** Evaluate whether monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. **[8]**

**9.** Explain two assumptions and two conditions needed for a monopsony to operate. **[4]**

**10.** A mining company is the only employer in a remote town (invented). Labour supply is W = 4 + 0.25L and marginal revenue product is MRP = 40 − 0.25L, where W is the hourly wage in cu and L is the number of workers.

**(a)** Derive the marginal cost of labour. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the monopsony employment and wage. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate employment and wage in a competitive labour market. **[2]**
**(d)** A minimum wage of 20 cu is introduced. Calculate employment. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of the monopsony in question 10 for the firm, consumers and employees. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** One seller in the market [1]. High barriers to entry (or no close substitutes, or price maker) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare "it is big" gains nothing. Answers tend to gain credit for precise features such as "single seller" and "barriers to entry".

**2.** Economies of scale: a rival entering small would face higher unit costs [1] [1]. Patents or licences: the law stops rivals selling the product [1] [1]. (Other barriers with a reason, for example branding or sunk costs, are equally creditworthy.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a barrier earns less than explaining why it blocks entry; add the "so" link each time.

**3. (a)** TR = 150Q − 5Q² [1]. MR = **150 − 10Q** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MR = MC: 150 − 10Q = 30 [1]. Q = **12** (thousand) [1]. P = 150 − 60 = **90 cu** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** TR = 90 × 12 = 1,080, and TC = 200 + 30 × 12 = 560 [1]. Profit = **520 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** P = MC: 150 − 5Q = 30 gives Q = 24 [1]. DWL = ½ × (24 − 12) × (90 − 30) [1] = **360 (thousand cu)** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is reading the price off MC. Showing "P from demand" in the working makes the logic visible.

**4. (a)** MR = 65 − 2Q [1]. 65 − 2Q = 5 gives Q = **30** [1]. P = 65 − 30 = **35** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 65 − Q = 600/Q + 5 [1]. Q² − 60Q + 600 = 0 [1]. Q = 30 + √300 = **47.3**, P = **17.7** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** At P = MC = 5, output is 60 [1], and the firm makes a loss equal to its fixed cost of 600, so it would need a subsidy [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the quadratic has two roots. Choosing the larger and saying why (more output at a lower price) tends to gain credit.

**5.** The firm needs market power, so it can set a price [1]. It must be able to separate the markets, for example by age or time [1]. Resale must be prevented, otherwise buyers in the cheap market resell to the dear one [1]. The groups must have different price elasticities of demand, otherwise the profit-maximising price is the same [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers tie each condition to a reason it matters. Listing four items with no explanation tends to stay at the lower end.

**6. (a)** Adults: MR = 40 − 4Q = 4, so Q = **9** [1], P = **22** [1]. Children: 24 − 4Q = 4, so Q = **5** [1], P = **14** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** 9 × 18 + 5 × 10 = 162 + 50 = **212** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Combined demand Q = (40 − P)/2 + (24 − P)/2 = 32 − P, so P = 32 − Q [1]. MR = 32 − 2Q = 4 gives Q = 14, P = 18 [1]. Profit = 14 × 14 = 196, so the gain is **16** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers earn most when MR = MC is solved separately in each market. Applying one price to both and calling it discrimination gains little.

**7.** Children pay 14 instead of 18 and buy 5 hundred instead of 3 [1]. Adults pay 22 instead of 18 and buy 9 hundred instead of 11 [1]. Children's surplus rises and adults' falls [1]. Total consumer surplus falls from 130 to 106 [1]. Profit rises by 16, so total surplus falls by 8 [1]. Judgement: consumers as a group are worse off here, though child consumers gain [1]. In other markets the answer could change if discrimination opens a market that a single price would exclude. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Judgement tends to gain credit when it is tied to a stated reason, for example "worse off in total because the loss to adults is larger".

**8.** Against: higher price and lower output than under competition [1]. Price exceeds MC, so allocative inefficiency and a deadweight loss [1]. Lack of rivals may mean X-inefficiency and productive inefficiency [1]. For: scale economies could cut costs enough to lower the price [1]. Supernormal profit can fund research, aiding dynamic efficiency [1]. But protection from rivals may remove pressure to innovate [1]. Evaluation: it depends on the size of the cost savings, on regulation and on how high the barriers are [1]. Judgement: monopoly is not always against consumers, but it is likely to be where barriers are legal rather than cost-based and regulation is weak [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Always" in the question invites a counter-example. Answers tend to gain credit when they give one specific condition under which consumers gain.

**9.** Assumptions: a single or dominant buyer [1]; the buyer is a price or wage maker facing an upward-sloping supply curve [1]. Conditions: workers (or suppliers) are immobile, so they cannot easily move [1]; few alternative employers or weak unions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep assumptions (what the model says) separate from conditions (what keeps it in place); mixing them tends to lose a mark.

**10. (a)** Total labour cost = WL = 4L + 0.25L² [1]. MCL = **4 + 0.5L** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MCL = MRP: 4 + 0.5L = 40 − 0.25L [1]. L = **48** [1]. Wage from supply = 4 + 12 = **16 cu** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** S = MRP: 4 + 0.25L = 40 − 0.25L [1]. L = **72**, W = **22 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At 20, labour supplied = 64 and labour demanded = 80 [1]. Employment = **64**, up from 48 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Taking the wage from MCL (28) rather than the supply curve is the usual error; say "read down to supply".

**11.** Firm: lower wage (16 against 22) cuts costs and raises profit [1]. But low pay may cause turnover and low effort [1]. Consumers: lower costs may mean lower prices, if the firm faces competition in the product market [1]. If the firm is also a monopolist, there may be no pass-through [1]. Employees: wage 16 against 22 and 48 jobs against 72 [1] [1]. Offset: without the mine there may be no jobs at all, and it may train staff [1]. Judgement: costs fall mainly on workers, and the benefits depend on pass-through and on whether the employer is essential to the town [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming all three groups is expected. Answers tend to gain credit when each group gets a cost and a benefit supported by the figures.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the price from the MC or MR curve instead of going up to demand.
- Using the wrong MR for a linear demand curve.
- Forgetting that fixed costs must be subtracted to get profit.
- Describing barriers to entry without explaining why they deter rivals.
- Applying one price to both markets in a price discrimination question.
- Reading the monopsony wage from the MCL curve.
- Calling monopoly "bad" or "good" with no condition or reason.
- Giving evaluation that is only a restatement of the points above.
