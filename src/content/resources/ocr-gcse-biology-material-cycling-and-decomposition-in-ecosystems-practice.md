None of these statements is printed in bold, so all questions suit both tiers. ## Questions

**1.** Name two materials that cycle between the abiotic and biotic parts of an ecosystem. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the role of microorganisms in the decomposition of a dead tree. **[4]**

**3.** A fox eats a rabbit that has eaten grass. Trace the path of one carbon atom from the air into the fox, and describe how it could later get back to the air. **[5]**

**4.** The water cycle involves several processes.

**(a)** Name the process by which plants lose water vapour from their leaves. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why precipitation is important to organisms living on land. **[3]**

**5.** Suggest what would happen to the growth of producers in a woodland if all the decomposers died. Explain your answer. **[4]**

**6.** A bag of straw has a mass of 320 g. After 8 weeks the mass is 224 g.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage loss of mass. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of decay in grams per week. **[2]**
**(c)** Give the fraction of the original mass that remains, in its simplest form. **[1]**

**7.** A student wants to find how temperature changes how fast leaves decay. Describe how the student could carry out the investigation. **[6]**

**8.** Samples of 100 g of fallen leaves were kept moist with plenty of air for 10 days at different temperatures. The table shows the mass lost.

| Temperature (°C) | 5 | 15 | 25 | 35 | 45 | 55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass lost (g) | 3 | 11 | 26 | 41 | 19 | 2 |

**(a)** Describe how you would plot a graph of these results. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the pattern shown by the results. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how many times faster the rate of decay is at 35 °C than at 15 °C. Round your answer to two significant figures. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain the result at 55 °C. **[2]**

**9.** A gardener places 500 g of the same plant waste in each of five trays with different water contents. The table shows the mass lost after 4 weeks.

| Water content (%) | 10 | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass lost (g) | 20 | 96 | 165 | 110 | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the starting mass lost in the tray with 50 % water. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of decay in the tray with 30 % water, in grams per week. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the mass lost is low at both 10 % and 90 % water content. **[2]**

**10.** In a peat bog, dead plants build up and decay very slowly.

**(a)** State what the term anaerobic means. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the plant remains in the bog decay so slowly. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why turning a compost heap with a fork speeds up decay. **[2]**

**11.** A compost heap started with a mass of 600 g (this is a small model heap). Its mass was measured every 10 days.

| Day | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass (g) | 600 | 510 | 459 | 441 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of decay, in grams per day, for each 10-day interval. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage loss of mass over the 30 days. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why the rate of decay falls as time passes. **[2]**
**(d)** State what happened to most of the carbon in the mass lost. **[1]**

**12.** Explain how the water cycle helps to maintain a pond as a habitat. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Carbon [1]. Water or nitrogen [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the materials themselves; answers such as "gases" or "nutrients" are too vague to be credited.

**2.** The microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) secrete enzymes onto the dead wood [1]. The enzymes break large insoluble molecules into small soluble molecules [1]. The microorganisms absorb the products and respire, releasing carbon dioxide into the air [1]. Mineral ions that are not used are returned to the soil, where plants can take them up [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer says what is released and where it goes; "they break it down" alone does not explain the role.

**3.** Grass takes in carbon dioxide and photosynthesis makes carbon compounds such as glucose [1]. The rabbit eats the grass, so the carbon passes to the rabbit [1]. The fox eats the rabbit, so the carbon passes to the fox [1]. Carbon dioxide returns to the air when the organisms respire [1], and when dead organisms and waste are decomposed by microorganisms, which respire [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Name each process (photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, decomposition) and say what form the carbon is in; a diagram of arrows without labels is hard to credit.

**4. (a)** Transpiration [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Precipitation supplies fresh water to the land [1]. Plants take it up through roots, and it dissolves and carries mineral ions into the plant [1]. Animals drink it, and it keeps rivers, lakes and soil moist for habitats [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the process to an effect on organisms is what turns "it rains" into an explanation; name more than one benefit when three marks are available.

**5.** Dead organisms and waste would not be broken down [1]. Mineral ions would not be returned to the soil [1]. The soil would run short of mineral ions, so producers would grow poorly [1]. Carbon would stay locked in dead material rather than returning to the air as carbon dioxide for photosynthesis [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "suggest" question rewards a clear chain of cause and effect; the prediction alone is not enough.

**6. (a)** Mass lost = 320 - 224 = 96 g [1]. Percentage = 96 / 320 x 100 = **30 %** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Rate = 96 / 8 [1] = **12 g per week** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 224 / 320 = **7/10** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction before dividing, and include units on the rate; a correct bare number may gain less credit than a worked one.

**7.** Weigh equal masses of the same type of leaf and place each in a mesh bag or container [1]. Use the same size of leaf pieces [1]. Keep the bags at different temperatures, such as 5, 15, 25, 35 and 45 °C [1]. Keep moisture and air supply the same for every temperature [1]. Reweigh each bag at regular intervals and record the mass lost [1]. Repeat each temperature and calculate a mean; wash hands after handling decaying material [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which variable is changed, which is measured and which are controlled; listing "keep everything the same" without naming variables is weak.

**8. (a)** Put temperature on the x-axis and mass lost on the y-axis, both labelled with units [1]. Pick scales so the points spread across most of the grid, such as 0 to 60 °C and 0 to 45 g, and plot the points accurately [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Mass lost rises from 3 g at 5 °C to a maximum of 41 g at 35 °C [1], then falls to 19 g at 45 °C and 2 g at 55 °C [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Rate at 35 °C = 41 / 10 = 4.1 g per day and at 15 °C = 11 / 10 = 1.1 g per day [1]. 4.1 / 1.1 = **3.7 times** faster [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At 55 °C the enzymes of the decomposers are denatured [1], and many microorganisms are killed, so little decay occurs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In a "describe" answer, quote figures from the table for the peak and the end values; do not explain until the question asks for it.

**9. (a)** 165 / 500 x 100 = **33 %** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 96 / 4 [1] = **24 g per week** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At 10 % water the microorganisms have too little water for enzyme action and growth [1]. At 90 % the air spaces are filled with water, so there is little oxygen and aerobic decay is slow [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a different reason for each end of the range; repeating "not the right amount of water" does not distinguish them.

**10. (a)** Without oxygen [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The bog is waterlogged, so there is very little oxygen [1]. The decomposers cannot respire aerobically, and decay is by anaerobic microorganisms, which are slow [1]. So the plant remains are not broken down and build up as peat [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Turning mixes air into the heap [1], so more oxygen is available for aerobic respiration and decomposers break down material faster [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name oxygen as the limiting factor rather than writing that the bog is "wet" or "cold".

**11. (a)** Days 0 to 10: (600 - 510) / 10 = **9.0 g per day** [1]. Days 10 to 20: (510 - 459) / 10 = **5.1 g per day** [1]. Days 20 to 30: (459 - 441) / 10 = **1.8 g per day** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Loss = 600 - 441 = 159 g [1]. 159 / 600 x 100 = **26.5 %** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The easily broken-down material is used first, leaving material that is harder to decompose [1]. Conditions may change, for example the heap may dry out, cool or run short of oxygen [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Most of the carbon was released as carbon dioxide by the respiration of the decomposers [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* When asked for rates over several intervals, work each interval separately; using the whole 30 days misses the change in rate.

**12.** Evaporation and transpiration remove water from the pond [1]. Precipitation and run-off replace it [1]. This keeps a supply of fresh water in the pond, so the organisms that live there can survive [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both loss and replacement of water; naming only rain misses why the cycle keeps the habitat steady.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "decomposers eat dead things" without secreted enzymes or absorbed products.
- Omitting that mineral ions are returned to the soil.
- Forgetting that decomposers respire and release carbon dioxide.
- Describing a carbon cycle without naming processes.
- Saying that decay increases with temperature without an optimum or denaturing.
- Giving "too wet" as a reason for slow decay without mentioning oxygen.
- Giving a rate without time units, or using the wrong time interval.
- Calculating a percentage of the final mass instead of the starting mass.
- Mixing up aerobic and anaerobic.
