## What biodegradable means

A material is **biodegradable** if it can be broken down by living organisms, mainly microorganisms, into simpler substances. This happens when enzymes or other chemical processes can attack the bonds in the material. The substances formed, such as water, carbon dioxide and small organic molecules, return to the environment.

## Why polyalkenes are not biodegradable

A polyalkene such as poly(ethene) has a backbone made entirely of C–C bonds, with C–H bonds on the side. These bonds are:

- **strong**, so they need much energy to break,
- **non-polar**, so there is no δ+ atom for a nucleophile such as water to attack,
- present in a **saturated** chain with no C=C bond or functional group.

Microorganisms cannot attack such bonds with their enzymes, and water, acids and alkalis do not react with the chain. Polyalkenes are therefore **chemically inert and non-biodegradable**. They can last for a very long time in the environment.

## Why polyesters and polyamides can be broken down

The links in condensation polymers are polar. In an ester link, –CO–O–, and in an amide link, –CO–NH–, the carbon of the C=O group is bonded to electronegative atoms and carries a partial positive charge (δ+). A nucleophile, such as the oxygen of a water molecule or a hydroxide ion, can attack this carbon, and the link breaks. This is **hydrolysis**.

```
–CO–O–  + H₂O  →  –COOH  +  HO–          (polyester hydrolysis)
–CO–NH– + H₂O  →  –COOH  +  H₂N–         (polyamide hydrolysis)
```

Hydrolysis is speeded up by acid or alkali, and in nature by enzymes made by microorganisms, so these polymers are **biodegradable**. The monomers are released (or their salts, depending on the conditions).

| Link | Products of hydrolysis in acid | Products in alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Ester | Dicarboxylic acid and diol | Salt of the dicarboxylic acid and diol |
| Amide | Dicarboxylic acid and ammonium salt of the diamine | Salt of the dicarboxylic acid and the diamine |

### The explanation for exam answers

Polyesters and polyamides have **polar** ester or amide links whose carbonyl carbon is δ+, so it can be attacked by nucleophiles such as water. Polyalkenes have only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is nothing for a nucleophile to attack. This is the reason that they cannot be hydrolysed.

## Methods of disposal

### 1. Landfill

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cheap and simple | Takes up land and the space available is limited |
| Needs little sorting | Non-biodegradable polymers last for a very long time |
| | Toxic additives, such as plasticisers, may leach into the soil and water |
| | Valuable raw material is wasted |

### 2. Incineration (burning to release energy)

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Reduces the volume of waste | Produces carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas |
| Energy released can heat buildings or generate electricity | Some polymers give toxic gases when burned, for example hydrogen chloride from PVC |
| Needs little sorting | Needs gas scrubbers and careful control, which cost money |

### 3. Recycling

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Conserves crude oil, which is the raw material for most polymers | Polymers must be sorted by type, which is costly |
| Reduces waste to landfill | Recycled material can be of lower quality |
| Uses less energy than making new polymer from raw materials | Collection and transport use energy |

Two kinds of recycling are used. In **mechanical recycling**, thermoplastic polymers are melted and remoulded. In **feedstock recycling**, a polymer is broken down chemically (for example by heating to give monomers or small hydrocarbons) which are reused to make new polymers. This is more energy-demanding but can handle mixed or contaminated plastic.

### 4. Biodegradable polymers

Some polymers are designed to be biodegradable, such as those with ester links. They break down in composting or landfill conditions. Their uses are limited because they may be weaker or more expensive, and they may need particular conditions to degrade.

## Writing an evaluation

For an extended answer, structure it like this.

1. Name the polymer and what it is made of (bonds, links).
2. State the effect on biodegradability, with the chemical reason.
3. Give at least one advantage and one disadvantage for each method of disposal.
4. Make a judgement, supported by the points you have made (for example "recycling is best when the polymer can be sorted easily, otherwise incineration with energy recovery").

## Worked example 1: explaining the difference

**Question.** Explain why nylon 6,6 is biodegradable but poly(ethene) is not.

Nylon 6,6 contains amide links, –CO–NH–. The carbon of the C=O group is δ+, so it can be attacked by nucleophiles such as water, and the links can be hydrolysed. Poly(ethene) contains only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, which are strong and have no δ+ atom for a nucleophile to attack, so it cannot be hydrolysed and microorganisms cannot break it down.

## Worked example 2: hydrolysis products

**Question.** A section of Terylene is refluxed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Name the products.

Alkaline hydrolysis of the ester links gives the sodium salt of benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (disodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate) and **ethane-1,2-diol**.

## Worked example 3: mass of products

A sample of 1.92 kg of poly(ethylene terephthalate) (repeating unit Mᵣ = 192.0) is completely hydrolysed by water under acid conditions. Calculate the masses of benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (Mᵣ = 166.0) and ethane-1,2-diol (Mᵣ = 62.0) formed.

```
n(repeating units)  = 1920 ÷ 192.0 = 10.0 mol
n(acid) = n(diol)   = 10.0 mol
mass of acid        = 10.0 × 166.0 = 1660 g = 1.66 kg
mass of diol        = 10.0 × 62.0  = 620 g  = 0.620 kg
check: water needed = 2 × 10.0 × 18.0 = 360 g; 1.92 + 0.36 = 2.28 kg = 1.66 + 0.62 ✓
```

## Worked example 4: what burning releases

1.00 kg of PVC (repeating unit C₂H₃Cl, Mᵣ = 62.5) burns completely, and all the chlorine forms hydrogen chloride (Mᵣ = 36.5).

```
n(C₂H₃Cl units) = 1000 ÷ 62.5 = 16.0 mol
n(HCl)          = 16.0 mol
mass of HCl     = 16.0 × 36.5 = 584 g
```

This shows why incineration of PVC needs gas-cleaning equipment: HCl is toxic and corrosive. The same burning of 1.00 kg of poly(ethene) (CH₂ unit, Mᵣ = 14.0) gives 1000 ÷ 14.0 = 71.4 mol of CO₂, which is 71.4 × 44.0 = 3140 g.

## Common mistakes

- Saying polyalkenes are non-biodegradable "because they are plastic". Give the reason: strong non-polar bonds with no site for attack.
- Saying that polyesters are biodegradable because they are made from natural substances.
- Writing "all polymers can be recycled" with no reference to sorting and quality.
- Claiming incineration produces no pollution, or that burning poly(ethene) produces HCl.
- Listing only advantages or only disadvantages of a method.
- Giving the products of amide hydrolysis in acid as a free diamine, rather than its ammonium salt.
- Confusing hydrolysis with condensation (they are reverse reactions).
- Saying a polymer is "broken down by enzymes" without saying the bond in the link is attacked by a nucleophile.

## Exam technique

- **Explain hydrolysis**: name the link, say the carbon is δ+, name the nucleophile, say the link breaks.
- **Contrast**: always mention that polyalkenes have non-polar bonds.
- **Evaluate disposal**: balance the points and give a reasoned conclusion.
- **Use specific details**: PVC gives HCl on burning; landfill takes up space; recycling needs sorting.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can polyalkenes burn?**
Yes. They are hydrocarbons and release energy and carbon dioxide on burning. They are inert to water and mild reagents, not to combustion.

**Why is hydrolysis faster with acid or alkali?**
Acid makes the carbonyl carbon more δ+, and hydroxide is a stronger nucleophile than water.

**Is every condensation polymer easily degraded?**
No. Aromatic polymers such as Kevlar are very resistant because of the benzene rings and strong hydrogen bonding. The specification statement refers to polyesters and polyamides in general.
