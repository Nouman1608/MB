These notes condense section **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Forces between molecules study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-forces-between-molecules/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-forces-between-molecules-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Forces between molecules: **permanent dipole–dipole**, **induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals, dispersion, London)** and **hydrogen bonding**.
- **Melting and boiling points** of molecular substances are influenced by the strength of these forces.
- The importance of hydrogen bonding in the **low density of ice** and the **anomalous boiling points** of compounds.
- **Explain** these forces in familiar and unfamiliar molecules.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intermolecular force | An attraction between molecules (not a covalent bond) |
| Temporary (instantaneous) dipole | A short-lived uneven charge distribution in a molecule |
| Induced dipole | A dipole created in one molecule by the dipole of a neighbour |
| London (dispersion) force | Attraction between temporary and induced dipoles |
| Permanent dipole–dipole force | Attraction between the δ+ and δ− ends of polar molecules |
| Hydrogen bond | Strong dipole–dipole attraction between H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule |
| Anomalous | Not following the general trend |

## The three forces

| Force | Between | Cause | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| London | All molecules | Temporary dipoles induce dipoles in neighbours | Increases with number of electrons; weakest for small molecules |
| Permanent dipole–dipole | Polar molecules | δ+ attracts δ− | Stronger than London for similar size |
| Hydrogen bonding | H on N, O or F with a lone pair on N, O or F | Large δ+ on H and lone pair on a small atom | Strongest of the three |

All three are far weaker than covalent bonds.

## London forces in detail

1. Electrons move, so the charge distribution is uneven at any instant, giving a temporary dipole.
2. This induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule.
3. The dipoles attract.

- More electrons (larger Mᵣ) gives bigger dipoles and stronger forces.
- A straight-chain molecule has more surface contact than a branched molecule, so its forces are stronger.

## Hydrogen bonding in detail

- Conditions: hydrogen bonded to N, O or F; a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule.
- Draw: δ+ H, δ− N, O or F, lone pair, dotted line, with the three atoms in a straight line.
- Water: up to **four** hydrogen bonds per molecule (two from the hydrogens, two from the lone pairs).
- Examples: NH₃, H₂O, HF, alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines.

## Boiling points: the logic

Boiling a molecular substance overcomes **intermolecular forces only**. The covalent bonds in each molecule stay intact. Stronger forces mean more energy is needed and a higher boiling point.

| Comparison | Reason |
|---|---|
| Cl₂ < Br₂ < I₂ | More electrons, stronger London forces |
| Pentane > 2,2-dimethylpropane | Straight chain, larger contact area, stronger London forces |
| Propanone > butane (similar Mᵣ) | Propanone is polar and has permanent dipole–dipole forces as well |
| Ethanol > methoxymethane (same Mᵣ) | Ethanol has hydrogen bonds; the ether does not |
| HF > HCl | Hydrogen bonding in HF |
| H₂O > H₂S | Hydrogen bonding in water |

Boiling points in °C: Cl₂ −34, Br₂ 59, I₂ 184; pentane 36, 2,2-dimethylpropane 9; butane −1, propanone 56; ethanol 78, methoxymethane −24; HF 20, HCl −85; H₂O 100, H₂S −60.

## Anomalous hydrides

- Group 14 hydrides (CH₄ to SnH₄) rise smoothly with the number of electrons: London forces only.
- NH₃, H₂O and HF boil far higher than the trend predicts because of hydrogen bonding.
- The rest of each group rises with the number of electrons.

## Ice

- Each molecule is hydrogen bonded to four others in an open tetrahedral lattice.
- This holds molecules further apart than in liquid water, so ice is **less dense** and floats.
- When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and molecules move closer together.

## Worked reminders

**Why does iodine have a higher boiling point than chlorine?** Both are non-polar, so only London forces act. Iodine has many more electrons, so larger temporary dipoles and stronger attractions. More energy is needed to separate iodine molecules.

**Why does ethanol dissolve in water?** Ethanol and water both form hydrogen bonds, so ethanol molecules can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.

**Why is a hydrogen bond not a covalent bond?** It is an attraction between molecules and is far weaker.

**Why do some alkanes of the same Mᵣ have different boiling points?** Shape changes the area of contact and so the London forces.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that covalent bonds break on boiling.
- Omitting the lone pair when describing hydrogen bonding.
- Saying hydrogen bonds exist between any molecule containing hydrogen.
- Ignoring London forces in polar molecules.
- Using the term "intermolecular bonds" for forces that are not bonds in the AQA sense; "forces" is the safer word.
- Failing to link a higher boiling point to more energy needed to overcome the forces.

## Self-test

1. Name the three types of force between molecules.
2. Explain how a London force arises between two neon atoms.
3. Why do London forces increase with the number of electrons?
4. State the conditions needed for hydrogen bonding.
5. Draw a hydrogen bond between two ammonia molecules, showing the lone pair.
6. Explain why HF has a higher boiling point than HCl.
7. Explain why the boiling point of butane (−1 °C) is lower than that of propanone (56 °C).
8. Explain why straight-chain pentane boils at a higher temperature than 2,2-dimethylpropane.
9. How many hydrogen bonds can each water molecule form?
10. Explain why ice floats on water.
11. State what is overcome when water boils.
12. Explain why the boiling points of Group 14 hydrides increase from CH₄ to SnH₄.

### Answers

1. Induced dipole–dipole (London), permanent dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding.
2. Electron movement produces a temporary dipole in one atom, which induces a dipole in the other. The two dipoles attract.
3. More electrons give a larger cloud, which is more easily distorted, so the temporary and induced dipoles are larger and the attraction is stronger.
4. H bonded to N, O or F, and a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule.
5. N–H···N with the lone pair on the second nitrogen, δ+ on H and δ− on N, and a straight dotted line.
6. HF molecules form hydrogen bonds. HCl molecules do not, so they have weaker forces.
7. Both have the same Mᵣ, but propanone is polar and has permanent dipole–dipole forces as well as London forces. Butane has only London forces.
8. Pentane is a straight chain with a larger area of contact, so its London forces are stronger; 2,2-dimethylpropane is compact.
9. Up to four.
10. Hydrogen bonds hold ice in an open lattice with molecules further apart than in liquid water, so ice is less dense.
11. The hydrogen bonds (intermolecular forces) between molecules, not the O–H covalent bonds.
12. The molecules are non-polar. The number of electrons increases, so London forces get stronger.
