## Section A: concepts

**1.** State what the Unit 2 Section A task requires and give one consequence of the exam being open
book. **[2]**

**2.** The specification says places are significant to the texts' "narrative structures". Explain
what this means, with an example from one of the six Section A novels. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between treating setting as a backdrop and treating it as a source of
meaning. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how a first-person or frame narrator affects the way a place is represented. **[3]**

**5.** The specification refers to the natural world and "possibly its despoliation". Explain what
despoliation means and give an example of how one Section A novel presents a damaged landscape.
**[3]**

## Section B: an original passage and essay skills

**Passage (original).**

> When Amara came back to the street after eleven years, the mango tree had gone. In its place
> stood a wall of grey blocks, freshly painted with the name of a bank. She stood where the tree's
> shadow used to fall and found that she was standing in full sun. Her mother's house was still
> there, smaller than she remembered, its blue door now a careful, expensive green. A boy on a
> motorbike asked if she was lost. She told him no, in the accent she had spent a decade learning,
> and he answered her in English, as if she were a visitor. It was the English that hurt. Further
> down, the kiosk where she had bought sweets was selling phone credit, and the woman inside had her
> old schoolfriend's face, only older, and did not look up.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents change in place in the passage. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the passage connects language to place and identity. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the passage presents home as both a physical place and a set of relationships.
**[4]**

**9.** Explain how the passage could be read through the specification's angle of "place as a
political space". **[3]**

**10.** In a practice essay, a student copies a quotation of about ninety words from the novel and
follows it with one sentence of comment. Explain two problems with this and describe a better
approach. **[3]**

**11.** Plan an essay on your own set novel for the question: "Explore the significance of
contrasting places in the novel." List five elements your plan should contain. **[5]**

**12.** Read this student paragraph: "In *The Great Gatsby* there are lots of places. West Egg is
where Gatsby lives and East Egg is where Tom and Daisy live. The valley of ashes is between them.
New York is where they go to have fun." Identify two weaknesses and explain how each could be
fixed. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** One essay question on the prose set text, worth 25 marks [1]. Consequence, any one [1]:
precise and accurate quotation is expected; time can be lost searching or copying, so the text must
be known well enough to find material quickly.

**2.** The order of places, and movement between them, organises how the story unfolds [1], so the
arrangement of places is a method that shapes meaning [1]. Example [1], any one: Marlow's journey
upriver structures *Heart of Darkness* as a movement deeper into the colonial enterprise; Stevens's
motoring trip in *Remains of the Day* triggers memories that structure the novel's return to the
1930s; *Small Island* alternates between "1948" and earlier times and places.

**3.** As a backdrop, setting is only where events happen and could be swapped without changing
meaning [1]. As a source of meaning, setting carries ideas about class, power, identity or feeling,
so analysing it explains the novel's concerns [1].

**4.** The place is filtered through one character's perception, knowledge and prejudice [1], so the
reader must ask what the narrator notices, misreads or leaves out [1]. A frame narrator adds
distance, since the place is also shaped by memory and by the act of telling it to listeners [1].

**5.** Despoliation means the spoiling, plundering or destruction of a place [1]. Example [2] for a
precise, explained example, [1] for a vague one: Fitzgerald's valley of ashes, a waste ground of
industrial dust between the Eggs and New York, shows the human and environmental cost of wealth;
or Conrad's abandoned, rusting machinery at the Company's station shows colonial waste and
exploitation.

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The opening "after eleven years" sets the passage up as a return, so every detail is measured
  against memory.
- The mango tree, a natural, shared landmark, is replaced by "a wall of grey blocks", contrasting
  life and colour with blankness.
- The bank's name on the wall shows commercial power taking over a community space.
- Standing "in full sun" where the shadow fell turns loss into a physical sensation: shelter has
  gone.
- The house "smaller than she remembered" shows that change is also in Amara's perception.
- The door's change from blue to "a careful, expensive green" shows the street becoming wealthier
  and more cautious; "careful" suggests something has been lost along with the old colour.
- The kiosk now selling phone credit replaces childhood pleasures with modern commerce.
- The woman who "did not look up" ends on non-recognition: the place no longer knows her.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: Amara's learned accent marks her years away, so her voice carries the
place she has been; the boy answers in English "as if she were a visitor", so language decides who
belongs; "It was the English that hurt" isolates the moment as the passage's emotional climax, a
short sentence after longer ones; identity is shown as something others assign by hearing her, not
something she controls; the passage implies a different home language without naming it, making
its absence felt.

**8.** Physical place, any two [1] each: the street, tree, house and kiosk are mapped precisely as
landmarks; changes to buildings are noted in physical detail. Relationships, any two [1] each: the
house is "her mother's house", defined by family; the schoolfriend's face links the kiosk to
friendship; the boy's question "if she was lost" shows home depends on being recognised by others;
the woman not looking up suggests a relationship lost along with the place.

**9.** Any three, [1] each: the bank's name painted on the wall shows economic power claiming public
space; changes to the street suggest who can afford to stay and who is displaced; English as the
language offered to an outsider points to global or historical power over language; the passage
raises the question of who has the right to call the street home, the one who left or those who
remained.

**10.** Two problems, [1] each: a long quotation uses time without showing analytical skill; one
sentence of comment cannot analyse the language of so much text, so most of the quotation is
unused. Better approach [1]: select a short phrase or a few words, embed it in a sentence, and
analyse its specific language and effect.

**11.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence argument about what the contrast reveals; the two or more
places to be contrasted, named precisely; the values each place represents (class, power, freedom,
home); the methods that create the contrast (description, symbolism, narrative viewpoint,
structure); evidence from the opening, middle and end; one relevant context; one alternative
interpretation; a conclusion on the significance of the contrast for the novel as a whole.

**12.** Two weaknesses, each identified [1] and fixed [1]:

- It only lists where characters live [1]; it should argue what the geography means, for example
  that old money in East Egg and new money in West Egg are separated by more than water [1].
- It analyses no language [1]; it should quote and analyse a short phrase describing a place, such
  as the dust and greyness of the valley of ashes, and explain its effect [1].
- Credit also, as an alternative [1] + [1]: no narrator is considered; it could ask how Nick, an
  outsider, shapes the reader's view of these places.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing locations instead of explaining their significance.
- Ignoring how the narrator shapes place.
- Using the open book to copy rather than to analyse precisely.

For full practice, write a 25-mark essay on your own novel from the plan in Question 11, using the
book, for about an hour.
