## 4.6.1 What economic growth is

Economic growth means a country produces more goods and services than before. In practice we measure it as the percentage rise in real GDP. Two points matter:

- Growth is about output, not prices. If only prices rise, output has not grown.
- Growth over a short period (a good year after a bad one) is different from a lasting rise in the economy's capacity to produce. The first moves the economy toward its PPC. The second moves the PPC itself.

## 4.6.2 Measuring growth

**GDP** (gross domestic product) adds up the market value of all final output a country makes in a period, usually a year. **Nominal GDP** is valued at current prices, so it rises when prices rise. **Real GDP** is valued at constant prices, so a rise in it can only come from more output.

| Quantity | How to find it |
|---|---|
| Real GDP | nominal GDP x 100 / price index |
| Growth rate (%) | change in real GDP / starting real GDP x 100 |
| GDP per head | real GDP / population |

### Worked example 1: real GDP and GDP per head

Country A is invented. Year 1 is the base year, so its price index is 100. Units are billions of currency units, and population is in millions.

| Year | Nominal GDP | Price index | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 600 | 100 | 30.0 |
| 2 | 650 | 104 | 30.5 |
| 3 | 702 | 108 | 31.0 |
| 4 | 780 | 120 | 31.5 |

Step 1, real GDP. Year 2: 650 / 104 x 100 = 625. Year 3: 702 / 108 x 100 = 650. Year 4: 780 / 120 x 100 = 650. Year 1 is already real: 600.

Step 2, growth. Year 2: (625 - 600) / 600 x 100 = 4.17%. Year 3: (650 - 625) / 625 x 100 = 4.0%. Year 4: (650 - 650) / 650 x 100 = 0%.

Step 3, compare with nominal GDP. Nominal GDP grew by 11.1% in year 4, yet real GDP did not grow at all. The whole rise was inflation.

Step 4, GDP per head. Year 1: 600 / 30.0 = 20.00. Year 3: 650 / 31.0 = 20.97. Year 4: 650 / 31.5 = 20.63, a fall of 1.6% from year 3. Real GDP was flat while the population grew, so the average person had less output to share.

### Why GDP per head is also used

Total real GDP can rise while the average person gains nothing, because population grew as fast or faster. GDP per head is a better guide to average living standards.

### Worked example 2: two countries, same GDP growth

Country D's real GDP grows 3.0% and its population grows 3.4%. GDP per head changes by 1.030 / 1.034 - 1 = -0.39%. Country E's real GDP grows 1.8% and its population 0.3%, so GDP per head changes by 1.018 / 1.003 - 1 = +1.50%. Country D has faster growth but a falling average income. Country E is the one improving on average.

### Limits of the measures

A rise in GDP per head is an average. It does not show how income is shared, the output of the informal economy, unpaid work, leisure, pollution or health. Mention one or two of these when asked how far GDP per head shows living standards.

## 4.6.3 Recession

A recession is a fall in real GDP. A common rule of thumb is that an economy is in recession after two quarters in a row of falling real GDP, though this is a convention, not a law. Output, income and employment fall together.

### Causes

A recession usually starts with a fall in total demand (aggregate demand, the total spending in the economy):

- Consumers cut spending because they fear losing their jobs or because interest rates have risen.
- Firms cut investment when they expect weak sales or when borrowing becomes expensive.
- Exports fall because trading partners are in recession or the exchange rate has risen.
- Government cuts spending or raises taxes sharply.
- A banking crisis cuts lending, so households and firms cannot borrow to spend.
- A supply shock, such as a sharp rise in the price of an important input, raises costs and cuts output.

These effects reinforce each other. Falling spending cuts firms' sales, firms cut jobs, and workers who lose jobs spend less.

### Worked example 3: a recession in numbers

Country B is invented. Real GDP per quarter, in billions: quarter 1, 250; quarter 2, 248; quarter 3, 244; quarter 4, 245. Its output when all resources are fully used (potential output) is 260.

- Quarter 2: (248 - 250) / 250 x 100 = -0.8%. Quarter 3: (244 - 248) / 248 x 100 = -1.61%. Two consecutive falls meets the rule of thumb, so the economy counts as being in recession.
- From peak to trough real GDP fell (244 - 250) / 250 x 100 = -2.4%.
- Actual output as a share of potential: quarter 1, 96.15%; quarter 3, 93.85%. The gap grew from 10 to 16.
- If the average tax rate is 20% of output, tax revenue fell from 50.0 to 48.8, a drop of 1.2 per quarter, while spending on unemployment benefit rose.

### Consequences

Unemployment rises, incomes fall and firms close. Government tax revenue falls and spending on benefits rises, so the budget deficit grows. Inflation usually slows because demand is weak. Investment falls, which can reduce future capacity. A recession in one country can reduce the exports of its trading partners.

### Recession and the PPC

In a recession the economy is inside its PPC because some resources are unemployed. Recession moves the economy further from the curve. The PPC itself has not shifted unless factories are scrapped or skills are lost.

## 4.6.4 Causes of economic growth

There are two different causes. Name which one you are describing.

### Using idle resources: a movement toward the PPC

When total demand rises, firms hire the unemployed and use spare machines, so real GDP rises. The economy moves from a point inside the PPC toward the curve. This growth is limited because once the curve is reached, resources are fully used.

### Worked example 4: PPC for Country C

Country C is invented. It makes capital goods and consumer goods.

| Point | Capital goods | Consumer goods | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 30 | 60 | Inside the PPC |
| Q | 40 | 70 | On the PPC |
| R | 40 | 80 | On the new PPC |

At P, resources are idle. Higher demand takes the economy to Q, where output of both goods rises by 10. Moving from Q to R needs the curve itself to shift outward.

### Shifting the PPC to the right

The PPC shifts right when the economy's capacity grows, through:

- Investment: more machines, factories and roads (capital) raise capacity and productivity.
- Technology: new methods let the same inputs produce more.
- Quantity of the factors of production: more workers (through population growth or immigration), more land and natural resources discovered, more capital.
- Quality of the factors of production: better educated and healthier workers, and better machines. Education and training raise labour productivity.

### Worked example 5: where growth comes from

Real GDP = number of workers x output per worker. If employment rises 1.5% and output per worker rises 2.0%, real GDP rises by 1.015 x 1.02 - 1 = 3.53%. If population rises 1.0%, GDP per head rises by 1.0353 / 1.01 - 1 = 2.50%. Growth driven by output per worker is the more lasting source, because adding workers alone does not raise GDP per head.

## 4.6.5 Consequences of economic growth

**Benefits.** Higher output raises average incomes and can reduce poverty. More jobs are created. Tax revenue rises, so the government can spend more on health, education and infrastructure without raising tax rates. Firms' profits rise, which funds further investment.

### Worked example 6: tax gains

Country A's real GDP is 650 and grows 4%, to 676. Extra output is 26. If 25% goes to the government in tax, tax revenue rises by 6.5.

**Costs.** Faster output can bring pollution, resource depletion and congestion. The gains may go mostly to richer households, so inequality can widen. Rapid demand growth may cause inflation or a rise in imports, worsening the trade balance. Growth that relies on fossil fuels uses up resources future generations need. People may work longer hours or move away from rural communities.

### Different economies, different weights

- A low-income economy, where many lack food, clean water and schooling, usually gains most from growth. Higher output can lift people out of poverty, though extraction of resources may harm the environment.
- A high-income economy has already met basic needs. Extra growth gives smaller gains, and the environmental costs weigh more heavily.
- An economy dependent on one export may find growth unstable, because it depends on that commodity's world price.

## 4.6.6 Policies to promote growth and how effective they are

| Policy | How it promotes growth | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal: more government spending or lower taxes | Raises total demand and uses idle resources | Works best when there is spare capacity. Can cause inflation if the economy is near full use, and raises the deficit |
| Monetary: lower interest rates | Cheaper borrowing raises consumer spending and investment | Takes time. Weak if households and firms are too nervous to borrow |
| Education and training | Raises labour quality and productivity | Slow to pay off, but lasting |
| Infrastructure investment | Lowers firms' costs and raises capacity | Expensive, long to build |
| Incentives for investment and enterprise (tax breaks, lower red tape) | Encourages firms to invest and innovate | Depends on business confidence |
| Trade policy and encouraging foreign investment | Brings in capital and technology and widens markets | Home firms may lose out to competition |

To judge effectiveness, ask four things. Is there spare capacity? How long will the effect take? Who bears the cost? Does it clash with another aim, such as stable prices or a healthy balance of payments?

## Common errors

- Using nominal GDP to claim growth. Always deflate by the price index first.
- Treating a recession as a PPC shift inward. It is a movement to a point further inside the PPC.
- Confusing a rise in demand (movement toward the curve) with a shift of the PPC.
- Describing costs and benefits of growth with no reference to the type of economy.
- Giving policies without evaluating how effective they are.
