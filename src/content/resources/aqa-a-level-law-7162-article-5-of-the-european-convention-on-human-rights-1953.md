This guide covers **Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights 1953**, part of section 3.5
Human Rights in AQA A-level Law (7162). The specification lists three things to know: "Article 5.1:
right to liberty and security of person", "Article 5.1a–5.1c: justified deprivation of liberty – lawful
arrest or detention" and "Article 5.2–5.5: additional requirements to justify deprivation of liberty in
cases of lawful arrest or detention". For the whole human rights option in outline, start with the
[Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## Where Article 5 fits

Article 5 is a **limited** right. It is not qualified like Articles 8, 10 and 11, so there is no general
"necessary in a democratic society" balancing test. Instead the Article itself lists the only grounds on
which a person may be deprived of liberty, and every deprivation must also follow "a procedure prescribed
by law". In a Paper 3 scenario, Article 5 usually appears alongside police powers: an arrest, a period in
custody, a containment of protesters or a detention under terrorism or mental health law.

## Step 1: is there a deprivation of liberty at all?

Article 5 protects physical liberty. It does not cover every restriction on movement; lesser
restrictions fall under Article 2 of Protocol 4, which the United Kingdom has not ratified. So the first
question is always whether the facts amount to a **deprivation** rather than a mere restriction.

- **Guzzardi v Italy (1980)**: the difference between deprivation and restriction is "one of degree or
  intensity, and not one of nature or substance". The Court looks at the type, duration, effects and
  manner of implementation of the measure. Confining a suspect to a small area of an island, under close
  supervision, was a deprivation of liberty.
- **HL v United Kingdom (2004)**: an autistic man who lacked capacity was kept in a psychiatric hospital
  as an informal patient. Staff had complete control over his care and movements, so he was deprived of
  liberty, and the lack of any legal procedure or review breached Article 5.1 and 5.4.
- **P v Cheshire West and Chester Council (2014)**: the Supreme Court set the "acid test": a person is
  deprived of liberty if under **continuous supervision and control and not free to leave**, even if the
  arrangement is kind and the person does not object.
- **Secretary of State for the Home Department v JJ (2007)**: control orders imposing an 18-hour daily
  curfew were a deprivation of liberty.
- **Austin v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis (2009)**: the House of Lords held that containing
  a crowd at Oxford Circus for about seven hours was not a deprivation of liberty, because the measure was
  taken in good faith, was proportionate to a real risk of serious disorder and lasted no longer than
  necessary. The European Court reached the same result in **Austin v United Kingdom (2012)**, stressing
  that the context of crowd control matters.

## Step 2: does a ground in Article 5.1 apply?

The specification names Article 5.1(a) to (c). Each has its own conditions.

| Ground | What it allows | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 5.1(a) | Lawful detention after conviction by a competent court | There must be a causal link between the conviction and the detention |
| 5.1(b) | Lawful arrest or detention for non-compliance with a lawful court order, or to secure fulfilment of an obligation prescribed by law | The obligation must be specific and concrete, not a general duty to obey the law |
| 5.1(c) | Lawful arrest or detention to bring a person before the competent legal authority on reasonable suspicion of an offence, or when reasonably considered necessary to prevent an offence or flight after one | The most common ground in police scenarios |

The other grounds, 5.1(d) to (f), cover minors, people of unsound mind or with infectious diseases, and
immigration control. They are not named in the specification, so mention them only in passing.

### Reasonable suspicion under 5.1(c)

In **Fox, Campbell and Hartley v United Kingdom (1990)** the European Court held that reasonable
suspicion "presupposes the existence of facts or information which would satisfy an objective observer
that the person concerned may have committed the offence". An honest belief alone is not enough. The
government relied on the applicants' previous terrorism convictions and undisclosed intelligence; this
was not sufficient to show reasonable suspicion, and Article 5.1 was breached.

English law applies a similar two-part test. In **O'Hara v Chief Constable of the Royal Ulster
Constabulary (1997)** the House of Lords held that the arresting officer must actually suspect (a
subjective element) and that there must be reasonable grounds for that suspicion (an objective
element). The grounds may come from a briefing by a superior officer, but a bare instruction to arrest
is not enough.

## Step 3: have the safeguards in Articles 5.2 to 5.5 been met?

Even a lawful arrest breaches Article 5 if the procedural safeguards fail.

- **Article 5.2**: the person must be told promptly, in a language they understand, the reasons for the
  arrest and any charge. In Fox, Campbell and Hartley the reasons emerged during questioning within a
  few hours, which the Court accepted as prompt.
- **Article 5.3**: a person held under 5.1(c) must be brought **promptly** before a judge or other
  judicial officer and is entitled to trial within a reasonable time or release pending trial. In
  **Brogan v United Kingdom (1988)** detention under prevention of terrorism legislation for four days
  and six hours without judicial control breached 5.3, even allowing for the terrorist context.
- **Article 5.4**: anyone detained may take proceedings in which a court decides **speedily** whether
  the detention is lawful and orders release if it is not. In England the classic route is habeas corpus.
- **Article 5.5**: a victim of arrest or detention in breach of Article 5 has an enforceable right to
  compensation. Domestically this is met by damages under section 8 of the Human Rights Act 1998 and by
  the tort of false imprisonment.

## Derogation and Article 5

Because Article 5 is not absolute, a state may derogate from it under Article 15 in time of war or
public emergency threatening the life of the nation, but only to the extent strictly required. In
**A v Secretary of State for the Home Department (2004)** the House of Lords accepted that there was a
public emergency after the attacks of 11 September 2001, but held that indefinite detention of foreign
nationals only, under the Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001, was disproportionate and
discriminatory. It quashed the derogation order and declared the detention power incompatible with
Articles 5 and 14. Parliament replaced it with control orders under the Prevention of Terrorism Act
2005, which were themselves later replaced by Terrorism Prevention and Investigation Measures.

## Worked example

*Facts: Priya is stopped outside a shop after a store detective says a woman "of her description" took
a bracelet. PC Lane arrests her without saying why. She is told the reason three hours later at the
police station and is released without charge after 20 hours.*

```
Deprivation?   Arrest and custody are a clear deprivation of liberty (Article 5.1).
Ground?        5.1(c): reasonable suspicion of theft. A specific report from
               a store detective plus a matching description is information
               that could satisfy an objective observer (Fox; O'Hara).
Article 5.2?   Reasons must be given promptly. Three hours with no reason at
               all is hard to justify; English law requires reasons at the
               time of arrest or as soon as practicable.
Article 5.3?   Not reached: she was released without charge inside the
               normal time limits, so there was no need to bring her before
               a judge.
Remedy?        If the arrest was unlawful for lack of reasons, a claim for
               false imprisonment and section 8 damages (Article 5.5).
```

## Common mistakes

- Treating Article 5 as a qualified right and applying a proportionality test to the grounds in 5.1.
  The list is exhaustive; proportionality is relevant to whether there is a deprivation (Austin) and to
  derogation (A v Secretary of State), not to whether a ground exists.
- Jumping to the grounds without first deciding whether there is a deprivation of liberty.
- Confusing Article 5.3 (prompt appearance before a judge after arrest) with Article 5.4 (speedy review
  of any detention by a court).
- Stating that Brogan fixed a precise time limit. The Court said four days and six hours was too long;
  it did not lay down an exact number of hours.
- Forgetting that a breach of 5.2 can occur even when the arrest itself is justified.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: set out the structure of Article 5 (right, grounds, safeguards) and support each
  part with one case.
- **Suggest** and **Advise** questions: work through the three steps above in order, apply each to the
  facts and give a conclusion on whether Article 5 has been breached and what remedy follows.
- **Consider** questions: develop the competing arguments, for example whether a containment is a
  deprivation under Austin or a short, necessary crowd-control measure, and reach a reasoned conclusion
  or range of conclusions.
- **Discuss** questions: evaluate how well Article 5 and English law protect liberty, for example the
  tension between the Brogan line and pre-charge detention in terrorism cases.
