**What do these questions practise?** Section 3 of Paper 1, Key Questions 11 to 16. Section B of the
paper uses a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation and a 14-mark evaluation; they are grouped
here by type. Allow about 5, 9 and 16 minutes for each.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960. **[4]**

**2.** Describe Sheikh Mujib-ur-Rahman's Six Points of 1966. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the One Unit scheme. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the Eighth Amendment of 1985. **[4]**

**5.** Describe Pakistan's nuclear tests of 1998. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why East Pakistan sought independence from West Pakistan. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why Zulfikar Ali Bhutto was overthrown in 1977. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why Benazir Bhutto's governments were dismissed in 1990 and 1996. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why Pakistan joined SEATO and CENTO in the 1950s. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**10.** "Jinnah's leadership was the main reason why Pakistan survived its first year, 1947–48." How
far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**11.** "Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's domestic reforms (1971–77) were successful." How far do you agree?
Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "Pakistan's relations with China have been more successful than its relations with the USA
since 1947." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge parts: 1 mark per relevant point, with a further mark for supporting detail. The 7- and
14-mark parts are marked by levels (tables after the answers). Credit other accurate points.

**1.** It settled the canal water dispute that began when India cut off water to Pakistan's canals
in 1948 [1]. It was signed by Ayub Khan and Nehru, with the World Bank as mediator [1]. India was given
the eastern rivers, the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej, and Pakistan the western rivers, the Indus, Jhelum and
Chenab [1]. Pakistan built dams, such as Mangla and Tarbela, and link canals to replace water from
the eastern rivers, with international funding [1]. **[4]**

**2.** They were demands for autonomy for East Pakistan put forward by the Awami League leader in
1966 [1]. Pakistan would be a federation with a parliamentary government, and the centre would control
only defence and foreign affairs [1]. There would be separate currencies, or measures to stop money
moving from East to West, and the provinces would control taxation and foreign trade [1]. East Pakistan
would have its own militia [1]. **[4]**

**3.** It merged the provinces and states of West Pakistan into a single province [1]. It came into
force in 1955 [1]. It was meant to balance East Pakistan, which had a larger population, and to
simplify administration [1]. It was resented by the smaller provinces, which felt dominated by
Punjab, and was ended by Yahya Khan in 1970 [1]. **[4]**

**4.** It was passed in 1985, under Zia-ul-Haq [1], as part of the deal for ending martial law [1]. It
gave the President the power to dissolve the National Assembly and dismiss the Prime Minister, under
Article 58(2)(b) [1]. The power was used to dismiss governments in 1988, 1990, 1993 and 1996, until
it was removed by the Thirteenth Amendment in 1997 [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Pakistan carried out nuclear tests in May 1998 [1], in the Chagai hills of Balochistan [1].
They followed India's nuclear tests earlier that month [1]. They made Pakistan the first Muslim
country with tested nuclear weapons, but led to economic sanctions from the USA and others [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The two wings were separated by about 1,600 km of India, making unity difficult.
- The decision to make Urdu the only national language led to the language movement and the
  shootings in Dhaka in 1952.
- East Pakistan felt economically exploited: jute earned foreign exchange, but more development
  spending went to the West.
- Bengalis were under-represented in the army and the civil service.
- The Six Points were rejected, and after the Awami League won the 1970 elections power was not
  transferred; military action in 1971 turned demands for autonomy into a demand for
  independence. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The opposition parties, united in the Pakistan National Alliance, accused the government of
  rigging the March 1977 elections.
- Mass protests and strikes followed, and talks between Bhutto and the opposition dragged on.
- Bhutto's use of the Federal Security Force and treatment of opponents made him unpopular with
  many.
- Nationalisation had alienated business owners and some of the middle class.
- General Zia-ul-Haq used the unrest to justify taking power on 5 July 1977. **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- *1990:* conflict with Nawaz Sharif's Punjab government; poor relations with President Ghulam
  Ishaq Khan, including a dispute over appointments; the no-confidence motion of 1989; accusations of
  corruption; the President used the Eighth Amendment to dismiss her.
- *1996:* accusations of corruption and economic mismanagement; inflation and unemployment; IMF
  concerns after devaluation; violence in Karachi; the death of her brother Murtaza; President
  Farooq Leghari dismissed her. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Pakistan felt threatened by India, which was much larger, especially over Kashmir.
- Pakistan needed military equipment and economic aid, which the USA could provide.
- The alliances placed Pakistan on the Western side in the Cold War, and the USA wanted allies near
  the Soviet Union and China.
- Membership was expected to give Pakistan international support. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* Jinnah gave the new state authority and unity; he set up the government in Karachi; he
  appealed for refugee relief through the Quaid's Relief Fund; he founded the State Bank in 1948; he
  toured the provinces to build confidence; his status made other countries take Pakistan seriously.
- *Disagree:* Liaquat Ali Khan and the civil servants who built the administration did much of the
  work; the people, including refugees, showed great determination; help came from abroad; and many
  problems, such as Kashmir, canal water and assets, were not solved in this period.
- *Judgement:* for example, his leadership was crucial to Pakistan's survival, but survival also
  depended on the efforts of many others. **[14]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Successful:* the 1973 Constitution, accepted by all the main parties; land reforms of 1972 and
  1977 lowered ownership ceilings; health and education reforms expanded schools and clinics;
  labour reforms improved workers' rights; the Simla Agreement brought back prisoners of war.
- *Unsuccessful:* nationalisation discouraged investment and lowered efficiency; land reforms had
  loopholes, and much land stayed with large owners; the Federal Security Force and the treatment of
  opponents damaged democracy; disputed elections in 1977 led to his overthrow.
- *Judgement:* for example, his constitutional reform lasted, but his economic reforms were less
  successful. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *China:* a border agreement in 1963; support during the wars with India; the Karakoram Highway;
  military co-operation and trade; Pakistan helped arrange US–China contacts in the early 1970s;
  relations stayed close throughout.
- *USA:* SEATO and CENTO brought aid and weapons; but the USA stopped arms supplies in 1965 and did
  not prevent the loss of East Pakistan in 1971; relations improved after the Soviet invasion of
  Afghanistan in 1979, with large aid; the nuclear programme led to sanctions in the 1990s.
- *Judgement:* for example, relations with China were more consistent, while relations with the USA
  brought large benefits only when US and Pakistani interests coincided. **[14]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating Question 8 as one dismissal: it asks about two governments.
- Giving the date of the Indus Waters Treaty as 1948, when the dispute began.
- Writing about Bhutto's foreign policy in a question on domestic reforms.
- Describing Six Points without saying why West Pakistan rejected them, when the question asks why.
