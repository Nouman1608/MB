These questions cover 3.1.1 Economic methodology and the economic problem in AQA A-level Economics (7136).
Every country and figure is invented. Read the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-methodology-and-the-economic-problem/)

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State whether each statement is positive or normative.

**(a)** "Country T's unemployment rate rose from 4.1% to 4.6% last year." **[1]**

**(b)** "Country T's government should give priority to reducing unemployment." **[1]**

**(c)** "If the goal is to reduce waste quickly, a charge on plastic bags works better than an advertising
campaign." **[1]**

**2.** Explain why a positive statement need not be true. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** reasons why economists cannot easily test their theories in the way that a chemist can.
**[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between capital as a factor of production and money. **[2]**

**5.** A teacher gives up a weekend tutoring job paying 300 to attend an unpaid course. She could otherwise
have spent the weekend decorating, which she values at 150. State the opportunity cost of attending the course
and explain your answer. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by the statement that "the environment is a scarce resource". **[3]**

### Section B

**7.** Country K's production possibilities are shown below.

| Point | Consumer goods (billion) | Capital goods (billion) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 0 |
| B | 90 | 20 |
| C | 75 | 40 |
| D | 50 | 60 |
| E | 0 | 80 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost, in consumer goods, of each extra billion of capital goods between B and
C, and between D and E. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the opportunity cost changes as more capital goods are produced. **[2]**

**(c)** Country K produces 60 billion consumer goods and 30 billion capital goods. Explain what this shows and
what the opportunity cost would be of moving to point C. **[2]**

**8.** Using a production possibility diagram, explain the effect on an economy of a new technology that raises
productivity in agriculture but not in manufacturing. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why every point on a production possibility boundary is productively efficient, but not every
point is allocatively efficient. **[4]**

**10.** Country V's government is choosing between using 2 billion to build a high-speed railway and using it
to fund more nursery places. Explain how both positive economic analysis and value judgements would influence
the decision. **[6]**

**11.** "Economic models are of little use because they rely on unrealistic assumptions." Assess this view.
**[9]**

**12.** Country W's economy is in recession, with high unemployment. A minister claims that "increasing
public spending on roads now has no opportunity cost". Evaluate this claim. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Positive**: it can be checked against labour market data [1].

**(b)** **Normative**: "should give priority" ranks objectives by value [1].

**(c)** **Positive**: the value judgement is in the stated goal; the speed of each policy's effect could be
compared with evidence [1].

**2.** A positive statement is one that can be **tested against evidence** [1]; the test may show that it is
**false**, but it remains positive because evidence can decide it [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two [2]: controlled experiments on whole economies are rarely possible; many variables change at the
same time in real data, so the effect of one cause is hard to isolate; people change their behaviour in
response to incentives, expectations and even published forecasts; ethical limits prevent deliberately harming
groups to test a theory.

**4.** Capital is a **produced good used to make other goods**, such as machinery or a delivery fleet [1]. Money is
a means of payment that buys resources but does not itself produce output [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The opportunity cost is the **next best alternative forgone**: the tutoring job worth **300** [1]. Decorating
was a lower-valued alternative, and opportunity cost is not the sum of all alternatives, so it is not 450 [1].
**[2]**

**6.** Environmental resources such as clean air, water, soil and fish stocks are **limited** relative to the
wants they serve [1]. Some are **non-renewable** and are depleted by use, and renewable ones can be exhausted if
used faster than they regenerate [1]. Using them for one purpose, for example as a place to dump waste, reduces
their availability for others, so there is an opportunity cost [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** B to C: consumer goods fall by 15 for 20 more capital goods = **0.75 billion consumer goods per billion**
[1]. D to E: they fall by 50 for 20 more = **2.5 billion per billion** [1].

**(b)** Resources are **not equally suited** to producing both goods [1]. The first resources moved into capital
goods are those best at making them; later ones are much better at consumer goods, so more consumer goods must be
given up for each extra unit [1].

**(c)** The point lies **inside the boundary**, so resources are unemployed or used inefficiently [1]. Moving to
C raises both outputs (75 consumer and 40 capital goods), so there is **no opportunity cost** in terms of other
output [1].

**8.** Diagram with agricultural goods on one axis and manufactured goods on the other [1]. The boundary
**pivots outward along the agriculture axis**; the manufacturing intercept is unchanged [1]. The economy can now
produce more agricultural goods for any given output of manufactures [1], and because fewer resources are needed
for a given farm output, some can move to manufacturing, so more of **both** goods may be produced [1]. **[4]**

**9.** On the boundary all resources are fully employed and used efficiently [1], so output of one good can rise
only if output of the other falls, which is the meaning of productive efficiency [1]. Allocative efficiency
requires producing the **combination that best matches consumers' preferences** [1]. Only one point, or a narrow
range, has that mix; an economy producing only capital goods could be on the boundary but would fail to meet
consumers' wants [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Positive analysis: estimates of the costs, time savings and extra output from the railway [1]; estimates of
how many parents could return to work and the effect on child development from nursery places [1]; who gains
from each and when [1]. Value judgements: how much weight to give to future benefits against present ones [1];
whether helping low-income families, who may gain most from nursery places, matters more than faster journeys
[1]. Two people accepting the same estimates may disagree because they weigh these values differently, so the
decision cannot be settled by evidence alone [1]. **[6]**

**11.** *For the view:* assumptions such as perfect information or rational behaviour are often false [1];
predictions based on them may be wrong, as behavioural economics shows [1]; ceteris paribus rarely holds, so
real outcomes differ from the model [1]. *Against the view:* every model simplifies; the test is whether it
predicts well enough to guide decisions [1]; demand and supply correctly predicts the direction of many price
changes [1]; models make reasoning clear and show which assumption to relax when results differ [1]; they can
be adapted, for example by adding imperfect information [1]. *Judgement:* models are useful when their key
assumptions roughly hold in the case examined, so the view is too strong; the right approach is to use models
while checking their assumptions against the evidence [2]. **[9]**

**12.** *Support:* in a recession, resources are unemployed, so the economy is inside its production possibility
boundary [1]; using idle workers and machines to build roads need not reduce output of other goods [1], so in
terms of forgone output the opportunity cost may be close to zero [1]. *Against:* the 2 billion could fund
other projects, such as hospitals or tax cuts, so there is still an opportunity cost in terms of the **next best
use of the spending** [1]; some resources used, such as skilled engineers or materials, may not be idle [1];
the borrowing may have to be repaid with higher taxes later [1]; if the recession ends before construction
starts, the project uses resources that are no longer unemployed [1]. *Judgement:* the claim is partly
correct about forgone output while resources are idle, but wrong to say there is no opportunity cost at all,
because other uses of the funds and of fully employed resources are given up [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a statement normative just because it mentions government policy.
- Adding all alternatives together when stating opportunity cost.
- Dividing the wrong way round when calculating opportunity cost from a table.
- Drawing a shift of the whole boundary when only one good's technology has improved.
- Saying a point inside the boundary is "inefficient" without explaining that resources are unemployed.
- Evaluation that lists arguments but reaches no judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a statement about a policy be positive?**
Yes. "The tax reduced sales by 8%" is positive because evidence can test it. It becomes normative only when it
says whether the policy is good, fair or desirable.

**How do I calculate opportunity cost from a production possibility table?**
Divide the fall in output of one good by the rise in output of the other between two points. State the units,
for example "0.75 billion consumer goods per billion capital goods".
