## 3.16 Electron-pair repulsion theory

The theory rests on four ideas.

1. The electron pairs in the outer shell of the central atom repel each other, because they are all negative.
2. The pairs move as far apart as they can, so repulsion is at a minimum. That arrangement fixes the shape.
3. A lone pair is held closer to the central nucleus than a bonding pair, so it spreads out more and repels more
   strongly. Two lone pairs repel each other most, a lone pair and a bonding pair less, two bonding pairs least.
4. A double bond holds both of its pairs in the same region between the same two atoms. For shape purposes it
   counts as one region of electron density, just like a single bond.

The **electron-pair arrangement** includes lone pairs; the **shape** describes only where the atoms are. NH₃
has a tetrahedral arrangement of four pairs but a pyramidal shape.

### A counting method that works every time

1. Write down the number of outer-shell electrons on the central atom (for example 3 for boron, 5 for
   nitrogen and phosphorus, 6 for oxygen and sulfur).
2. Subtract the central atom's own electrons used in bonding: one per single bond, two per double bond, three
   per triple bond.
3. For a negative ion add one electron per negative charge. For a positive ion subtract one per positive charge.
4. Divide what is left by 2. That gives the number of lone pairs.
5. Lone pairs plus bonded atoms gives the number of regions and the arrangement; the atom positions give the
   shape.

**Worked example 1 (3.16, 3.18).** Compare NH₃ with NH₄⁺.

- NH₃: nitrogen has 5 outer electrons. Three N-H bonds use 3. That leaves 2 electrons, which is **1 lone pair**.
  Four regions in total (3 bonding, 1 lone).
- NH₄⁺: nitrogen has 5 outer electrons. Four N-H bonds use 4. The positive charge removes 1 more. That leaves 0,
  so there are **no lone pairs**. Four regions, all bonding.
- Both have four regions, so both have a tetrahedral arrangement.
- NH₃ is **pyramidal**, H-N-H about **107°**. The lone pair repels the three bonding pairs more strongly than they
  repel each other, squeezing them together.
- NH₄⁺ is **tetrahedral**, H-N-H **109.5°**. When NH₃ accepts H⁺, its lone pair becomes the dative bond. All four
  pairs are now bonding pairs and repel equally, so the angle opens out by about 2.5°.

## 3.17 Bond length and bond angle

**Bond length** is the internuclear distance for two atoms joined by a covalent bond. It is the separation at
which the attraction of both nuclei for the shared electrons balances the repulsion between the two nuclei.

**Bond angle** is the angle between two bonds that meet at the same atom. It is written with three atoms, the
middle one being the atom at the vertex, for example the H-O-H angle in water.

More shared electrons between the same two atoms pull the nuclei closer together. Using typical values of
0.154 nm for C-C, 0.134 nm for C=C and 0.120 nm for C≡C, the double bond is about 13% shorter than the single bond
and the triple bond about 22% shorter.

## 3.18 The ten named shapes

| Species | Bonding regions | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BeCl₂ (gas) | 2 | 0 | linear | 180° |
| CO₂ | 2 (both C=O) | 0 | linear | 180° |
| BCl₃ | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| C₂H₄ (each carbon) | 3 (one is C=C) | 0 | trigonal planar at each C; whole molecule planar | about 120° |
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| NH₄⁺ | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 | pyramidal | about 107° |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 | bent (V-shaped, non-linear) | about 104.5° |
| PCl₅ (gas) | 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90° (axial) and 120° (equatorial) |
| SF₆ | 6 | 0 | octahedral | 90° |

Points the explanations need:

- **BeCl₂ and BCl₃**: beryllium has only 2 outer electrons and boron 3, so there are two or three bonding pairs,
  no lone pairs, and fewer than eight electrons round the central atom.
- **CO₂** has two double bonds. Each counts as one region, so two regions point opposite each other.
- **C₂H₄**: each carbon has three regions (two C-H and one C=C), so each carbon is trigonal planar. All six atoms
  lie in one plane. The double bond region repels a little more than a single bond, so the measured H-C-H angle is
  slightly below 120°; quote "about 120°" unless a question gives data.
- **H₂O** has two lone pairs. Lone pair-lone pair repulsion is the strongest of all, so the angle is squeezed
  further than in NH₃. The trend 109.5° → 107° → 104.5° for CH₄, NH₃, H₂O is the key comparison.
- **PCl₅** is named as gaseous because the solid has a different, ionic structure. In the gas, three chlorine
  atoms lie in a triangle round the phosphorus (equatorial, 120° apart) and two sit above and below (axial, 90° to
  the triangle). All five outer electrons of phosphorus are used in bonds.
- **SF₆**: sulfur uses all six outer electrons, giving six bonding pairs; adjacent F-S-F angles are 90°.

### Drawing shapes

Use the standard 3D convention:

- an ordinary line shows a bond lying flat on the page;
- a solid wedge shows a bond pointing out towards you;
- a dashed (hashed) wedge shows a bond pointing back, away from you.

Show lone pairs on the central atom and label the bond angle. A tetrahedral species is two ordinary lines, one
wedge and one dashed wedge.

## 3.19 Predicting analogous shapes

An analogous species has the same number of bonding regions and lone pairs as one of the named ten, so it has
the same shape. Count first; never guess from the formula.

**Worked example 2.** Predict the shape of the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺.

- Oxygen outer electrons: 6.
- Used in three O-H bonds: 3. Positive charge: take away 1.
- Left over: 6 - 3 - 1 = 2, which is 1 lone pair.
- Regions: 3 bonding + 1 lone = 4.

Four regions with one lone pair, like NH₃. H₃O⁺ is **pyramidal** with H-O-H about **107°**.

**Worked example 3.** Predict the shape of the hexafluorosilicate ion, SiF₆²⁻.

- Silicon outer electrons: 4.
- Used in six Si-F bonds: 6. The 2- charge: add 2.
- Left over: 4 - 6 + 2 = 0, so no lone pairs.
- Regions: 6, all bonding.

Six bonding pairs and no lone pairs, like SF₆. SiF₆²⁻ is **octahedral**, F-Si-F **90°**.

**Worked example 4.** Predict the shapes of carbon disulfide, CS₂, and tetrafluoroethene, C₂F₄.

- CS₂ is S=C=S. Carbon uses all 4 outer electrons in two double bonds, so it has no lone pairs and two regions.
  Like CO₂, it is **linear**, 180°.
- C₂F₄ is F₂C=CF₂. Each carbon has one C=C and two C-F bonds: three regions and no lone pairs. Like C₂H₄, each
  carbon is **trigonal planar**, F-C-F about **120°**, and the molecule is planar.

To "explain" a shape, give the numbers of bonding pairs and lone pairs, say the pairs repel to be as far apart
as possible (lone pairs more strongly), then state the shape and angle.

## 3D Metallic bonding

### 3.20 The model

Every metal consists of a **giant lattice of positive ions** surrounded by a **sea of delocalised electrons**. Each atom gives its
outer electrons to the whole structure. Those electrons do not belong to any one ion; they can move through the
lattice. Sodium gives one electron per atom and forms Na⁺ ions; magnesium gives two and forms Mg²⁺ ions;
aluminium gives three and forms Al³⁺ ions.

### 3.21 The bond

**Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the metal ions and the delocalised electrons.**
It acts in all directions throughout the lattice. Do not describe it as atoms sharing electrons.

### 3.22 Interpreting properties

**Electrical conductivity.** The delocalised electrons are free to move. When a potential difference is applied,
they drift through the lattice towards the positive terminal, carrying charge. Metals conduct as solids and when
molten, because the delocalised electrons are present in both states.

**High melting temperature.** To melt a metal, the ions must gain enough energy to move past one another. That
means overcoming much of the strong attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons, which acts
throughout a giant lattice, so a lot of energy is needed.

The strength of metallic bonding increases when:

- the ions carry a **larger charge**;
- there are **more delocalised electrons per atom**;
- the ions are **smaller**, so the delocalised electrons are closer to the centre of positive charge.

**Worked example 5.** Sodium melts at about 98 °C and magnesium at about 650 °C. Explain the difference.

- Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ ions; sodium forms Na⁺ ions. The larger charge on the ion attracts the electron sea more
  strongly.
- Magnesium releases two delocalised electrons per atom; sodium releases one. The electron sea is denser.
- The Mg²⁺ ion is smaller than the Na⁺ ion, so the charges are closer together.
- Overall, magnesium ions attract their electron sea more strongly, so melting magnesium takes more energy.
  Its melting temperature is about 552 °C higher.

**Other simple properties.** Metals are malleable: when a force is applied, rows of ions shift to new positions and the
electron sea still holds them, because the bonding is not fixed between particular atoms.

## Common errors

- Counting only bonded atoms and forgetting lone pairs. Water has two bonded atoms but four regions.
- Treating a double bond as two regions. CO₂ would come out as tetrahedral, which is wrong.
- Giving "tetrahedral" for NH₃. That is the electron-pair arrangement; the shape is pyramidal.
- Writing 90° only for PCl₅. The equatorial angle of 120° is needed as well.
- Leaving out the charge when counting electrons for an ion.
- Saying metals conduct because "ions move". In a solid metal the ions stay in the lattice; the delocalised
  electrons carry the charge.
