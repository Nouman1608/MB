This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. Korea (1840 to 1945) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers the weakness of the late Joseon monarchy, the opening of Korea, rivalry between China, Japan and Russia, reform, Japanese annexation and rule, and the liberation of 1945.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-korea-1840-1945-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-korea-1840-1945-practice/).

## Mid-century challenges to the monarchy

**Sedo politics.** From 1800, a few powerful in-law families (especially the Andong Kim) ruled in the name of child kings. This is called *Sedo* politics. It weakened royal authority, encouraged corrupt office-selling, and put heavy taxes and grain-loan burdens on peasants. Rebellions broke out, notably in 1862.

**Christianity.** Catholic ideas entered Korea in the late eighteenth century through Korean scholars who read Chinese books. The monarchy saw Christianity as a threat because it rejected ancestral rites. There were persecutions in 1801 and 1866, when French missionaries and thousands of Korean Catholics were killed.

**Donghak.** "Eastern Learning" was founded by Choe Je-u in 1860 as a native teaching that blended Confucian, Buddhist and folk ideas and taught that every person has Heaven within. It spread among peasants. Choe was executed in 1864, but the movement survived and grew.

**The Daewongun.** When the child King Gojong took the throne in 1863, his father, Yi Ha-ung (the **Heungseon Daewongun**), became regent. He cut the power of the in-law families, closed most private Confucian academies that avoided tax, rebuilt the Gyeongbokgung palace and reformed taxation. He also enforced a policy of isolation: his government persecuted Catholics and repelled a French force on Ganghwa Island (1866) and an American one (1871).

## "Opening up" of Korea and regional responses

**The Treaty of Ganghwa (1876).** Japan, itself opened by Western powers and modernising since 1868, used a naval incident (1875) to force an unequal treaty on Korea. It opened ports (Busan, then Incheon and Wonsan), gave Japanese extraterritorial rights, and called Korea an independent state, which weakened China's claim to suzerainty over it.

**Gojong and Queen Min.** Gojong took personal rule in 1873, and Queen Min and her relatives became powerful. The court signed treaties with the United States (1882) and other states. Reformers sent missions to Japan and China, but taxes and military costs rose.

**The Imo Incident (1882).** Soldiers who had not been paid mutinied, and a crowd attacked the Japanese legation. The Daewongun briefly regained power, but Qing troops arrested him and took him to China. Japan won compensation.

**The Gapsin Coup (1884) and the Chinese decade.** A group of reformers led by Kim Ok-gyun seized the palace with Japanese support. Chinese troops under Yuan Shikai crushed the coup within three days. China and Japan agreed to withdraw their troops (the Tianjin Convention of 1885), but for the next decade (1885 to 1894) China dominated Korean affairs, with Yuan Shikai acting as resident in Seoul.

## Impact of regional rivalries

**The Donghak Peasant Revolution (1894).** An uprising began in Jeolla province against a corrupt magistrate and spread under Jeon Bong-jun, demanding reform, relief from taxes and the removal of foreign influence. The government asked China for help, and Japan then sent troops in turn. The peasants' war was overtaken by foreign war.

**The Gabo reforms (1894 to 1896).** Under Japanese pressure, Korea abolished the class system, ended slavery and the old civil service examination, allowed widows to remarry and banned child marriage. These were radical, but many Koreans saw them as foreign-imposed. In October 1895 Queen Min was killed by Japanese agents, and in February 1896 Gojong took refuge in the Russian legation.

**The First Sino-Japanese War (1894 to 1895).** Japan's victory led to the **Treaty of Shimonoseki**, which recognised Korea's independence from China and ended the Chinese decade. After the **Triple Intervention** by Russia, France and Germany forced Japan to give back the Liaodong peninsula, Russia's influence in Korea grew.

## Impact of reforms under the Korean Empire

**The Gwangmu reforms (1897 to 1904).** In 1897 Gojong proclaimed the Korean Empire and took the title of emperor, to show equality with China and Japan. The reforms followed the motto "old foundation, new participation". They included a land survey, a new currency, railways, telegraph and electric tram lines in Seoul, and new schools. They strengthened the emperor's power but cost money, and the state remained short of funds.

**The Independence Club (1896 to 1898).** Founded by Seo Jae-pil and others, it published a newspaper in the Korean script (*Tongnip Sinmun*), built the Independence Gate, held public debates, and called for an elected assembly. The government, fearing a loss of imperial power, ended the club in 1898.

**Japan's advance and the Eulsa Treaty.** After Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904 to 1905), it made Korea a protectorate in the **Eulsa Treaty (November 1905)**, which gave Japan control of foreign affairs. Some officials and scholars protested; a few took their own lives. The emperor's secret appeal to the Hague Peace Conference in 1907 failed, and he was forced to abdicate. "Righteous armies" fought on, and in 1909 An Jung-geun killed the Resident-General, Ito Hirobumi.

## Impact of Japanese rule

**Annexation and government (1910).** Japan formally annexed Korea in August 1910. A Governor-General, always a senior Japanese military officer, ruled with wide powers. In the early period, the police and military closely controlled society and banned political groups. A land survey (1910 to 1918) gave legal title to those who could prove ownership. Many peasants who did not register lost land, and Japanese owners and companies gained.

**The March First Movement (1919).** Inspired partly by the death of the former emperor Gojong and by ideas of self-determination, a declaration of independence was read in Seoul on 1 March, and peaceful demonstrations spread across the country. The Japanese suppressed it with force, and thousands died. Students, women (such as Yu Gwan-sun) and religious groups played a major role. The movement led to the formation of a Korean Provisional Government in Shanghai.

**"Cultural rule" and assimilation.** After 1919 the Japanese eased some restrictions, allowing Korean-language newspapers and associations, while expanding the police. From the 1930s, as Japan moved to war, assimilation became harsh: shrine visits were required, Korean was removed from schools and public life, and in 1940 Koreans were pressed to take Japanese names. Education taught loyalty to the emperor.

**Economic developments.** The colonial state built railways, ports and banks, and Japanese firms developed mining, hydroelectric power and chemical works, especially in the north in the 1930s. Rice output was raised and much was shipped to Japan. Korean landlords and some businessmen benefited, but most farmers became tenants, and industrial profits mostly went to Japanese owners. Historians debate how far this was "colonial modernisation" or exploitation.

## Impact of the Second Sino-Japanese War

**Social impact.** After 1937 Korea became a supply base. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans were sent as forced labour to Japan, and many more were mobilised within Korea. Conscription began in 1944. Many Korean women were forced into the Japanese military's sexual slavery system, known as the "comfort women". This remains a major issue in Korea–Japan relations.

**Political developments.** Nationalists worked abroad, in the Provisional Government in China and in exile groups. Communists organised underground or fought with Chinese forces in Manchuria, and some units, including that of Kim Il Sung, fought Japanese troops. Inside Korea, most leaders were forced to collaborate or fall silent.

**Economic impact.** Resources and industry were taken for the war effort. Shortages, forced requisitioning of rice and metal, and rationing hurt living standards.

## Liberation and division

Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945. Korea was divided at the 38th parallel into Soviet and American occupation zones. In the north the Soviets supported Kim Il Sung. In the south the American military government worked with conservatives, including the nationalist Syngman Rhee, who became president of the Republic of Korea in 1948. Kim Il Sung led the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, proclaimed in September 1948.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Korean nationalist histories stress exploitation and resistance. Some Japanese and foreign scholars have emphasised the infrastructure and growth the colonial period created, an idea known as "colonial modernisation".
- Scholars such as Shin Gi-wook and Michael Robinson argue for "colonial modernity", where change was real but experienced under foreign control.
- Bruce Cumings and Carter Eckert have looked at how colonial industrial growth shaped later Korea.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent was Japan responsible for the loss of Korean independence?"** Argue that Japan's military and diplomatic actions (1876, 1894 to 1895, 1905 and 1910) were the immediate cause, but internal weakness (Sedo politics, financial limits, factional politics) and the rivalry among China, Russia and Japan made Korea vulnerable. Support with the Eulsa Treaty and the Gwangmu reforms' limits, and reach a judgement that ranks external force above internal weakness while explaining how the two connected.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Korea as only a victim with no agency.
- Mixing the Gapsin Coup (1884) with the Gabo reforms (1894 to 1896).
- Forgetting the Korean Empire's reforms between 1897 and 1904.
- Giving exact death tolls for the March First Movement as if they were agreed.
