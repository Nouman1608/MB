## The structure of cisplatin

Cisplatin is a complex of platinum(II), [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. The central Pt²⁺ ion has four ligands: two ammonia molecules and two chloride ions. The complex is **neutral** overall, since the 2+ charge of Pt²⁺ is cancelled by two Cl⁻. It is **square planar**, with bond angles of 90° between neighbouring ligands.

Two geometric isomers exist:

```
cis (cisplatin)                   trans
   Cl      NH₃                    Cl      NH₃
     \    /                         \    /
      Pt                             Pt
     /    \                         /    \
   Cl     NH₃                    NH₃      Cl
 (like ligands on the same side)  (like ligands on opposite sides)
```

In **cisplatin** (the cis isomer) the two chloride ligands are next to each other, as are the two ammonia ligands. Only the cis isomer is the effective anticancer drug.

## Ligand replacement with DNA

Ligand replacement (substitution) means that one ligand in a complex is replaced by another. In the body, the chloride ligands of cisplatin can be replaced by other species that have a lone pair of electrons.

In DNA the bases contain nitrogen atoms with lone pairs. **Guanine** has a nitrogen atom that is well placed to bond to the platinum. The nitrogen donates its lone pair to Pt²⁺, forming a **dative covalent bond**. Each platinum can replace both chloride ligands:

```
[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] + 2 N(guanine) → [Pt(NH₃)₂(N-guanine)₂]²⁺ + 2Cl⁻
```

(N-guanine here means a guanine nitrogen atom acting as a ligand.) Because the two Cl⁻ ligands are next to each other (cis), the platinum can bond to two nitrogen atoms that are close together, such as two guanine bases next to one another on the same DNA strand. The ammonia ligands stay in place.

The result is that cisplatin forms a **cross-link** in DNA: the platinum is attached to two nitrogen atoms of guanine bases, linking them. The trans isomer has its chloride ligands on opposite sides of the platinum, so it cannot bond to two nearby bases in the same way.

## Why DNA replication is prevented

Before a cell divides, its DNA must be copied. For copying, the two strands of the double helix have to separate and each acts as a template. When cisplatin is bonded to guanine bases:

- The platinum cross-link holds parts of DNA in a distorted shape.
- The strands cannot unwind and separate properly.
- The enzymes that copy the DNA cannot move along it.
- DNA replication is blocked, so the cell cannot divide, and it may be directed to die.

Cancer cells divide rapidly and uncontrollably, so they are particularly affected by a drug that stops DNA replication. This is why cisplatin works against some cancers.

## Adverse effects

Cisplatin is **not selective**: it does not distinguish a cancer cell from a healthy cell. It reacts with the DNA of any cell it reaches. Cells that also divide rapidly are the most affected, for example:

- cells in the lining of the gut (nausea and vomiting),
- hair follicle cells (hair loss),
- bone marrow cells (reduced production of blood cells, which increases the risk of infection).

The drug and its products can also damage organs such as the kidneys. These are the adverse effects (side effects) that arise because DNA replication is stopped in healthy cells as well as cancer cells.

## The balance of benefits and harms

The specification asks you to appreciate that society needs to **assess the balance** between benefits and adverse effects. The question is not simply "does it work?" but "do the benefits outweigh the harms for this patient and this cancer?"

| Benefits | Adverse effects |
|---|---|
| Can shrink or remove some cancers and save lives | Unpleasant, sometimes serious, side effects |
| Improves survival time and quality of life for some patients | Damage to healthy cells in the body |
| Allows treatment where surgery is not possible | Costs and the need for careful monitoring |

Doctors, patients, researchers and regulators weigh up the evidence. The more serious the illness, the more likely that a drug with serious side effects will be accepted. Chemists continue to work on drugs that are more selective, have fewer side effects, or can be delivered directly to the tumour.

## Worked example 1: what is cisplatin?

**Question.** State the formula, oxidation state of platinum, shape and charge of cisplatin.

Formula [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. Platinum is in oxidation state +2. The shape is square planar. The complex is neutral, because Pt²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ gives a net charge of zero (the NH₃ ligands are neutral).

## Worked example 2: ligand replacement

**Question.** Write an equation for the reaction in which cisplatin bonds to two guanine nitrogen atoms.

```
[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] + 2 N(guanine) → [Pt(NH₃)₂(N-guanine)₂]²⁺ + 2Cl⁻
```

Charge check: the left side is neutral. On the right, the complex has charge 2+ and two chloride ions give 2−, so the total is zero.

## Worked example 3: a calculation

A dose contains 50.0 mg of cisplatin (Mᵣ = 300.1). Aᵣ values: Pt = 195.1, N = 14.0, H = 1.0, Cl = 35.5. Calculate the amount of cisplatin and the mass of platinum in the dose.

```
Mr of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] = 195.1 + 2(14.0 + 3.0) + 2(35.5) = 195.1 + 34.0 + 71.0 = 300.1
n = 0.0500 g ÷ 300.1 = 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ mol
mass of Pt = 50.0 × 195.1 ÷ 300.1 = 32.5 mg
```

Each platinum atom can bond to two nitrogen atoms, so 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ mol could bond to up to 3.33 × 10⁻⁴ mol of guanine nitrogen atoms.

## Worked example 4: explaining an adverse effect

**Question.** Explain why a patient taking cisplatin may lose hair.

Cisplatin stops DNA replication in cells that are dividing quickly. Hair follicle cells divide rapidly, so their DNA replication is also blocked by the drug, and hair growth is affected. The drug cannot tell the difference between cancer cells and these healthy cells.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that cisplatin "kills cancer cells" without saying how: it bonds to guanine and stops DNA replication.
- Saying Pt bonds to the phosphate or sugar. The bond is to a nitrogen atom on guanine.
- Describing the bond as ionic or hydrogen bonding. It is a dative covalent bond.
- Saying the NH₃ ligands are replaced. The chloride ligands are replaced.
- Describing cisplatin as tetrahedral. It is square planar.
- Stating that cisplatin selects cancer cells. It does not.
- Giving only benefits or only harms when asked to assess the balance.
- Forgetting that the chlorine atoms leave as chloride ions.

## Exam technique

- **Describe the action**: ligand replacement, Cl⁻ replaced, Pt bonds to N of guanine, dative covalent bond.
- **Explain the effect**: DNA cannot be replicated, so the cell cannot divide.
- **Explain adverse effects**: the drug acts on any rapidly dividing cell, so healthy cells are affected.
- **Assess**: give benefits and harms, then a reasoned judgement for a given situation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is cisplatin called "cis"?**
The two chloride ligands (and the two ammonia ligands) are on the same side of the square planar complex.

**Is the Pt–N bond covalent?**
Yes. It is a dative (coordinate) covalent bond, in which both electrons come from the nitrogen lone pair.

**Why does the drug affect cancer cells more than healthy cells?**
Cancer cells divide more rapidly and more often, so they are more affected by a drug that blocks replication. Healthy cells that also divide quickly are affected too.
