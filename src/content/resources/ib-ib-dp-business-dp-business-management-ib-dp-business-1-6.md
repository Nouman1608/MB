This guide teaches section 1.6 (Multinational companies (MNCs)) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. The guide's content point is the impact of MNCs on the host countries, at AO3. That means a list of benefits and costs is only the starting point: you must weigh them for a particular country and company and reach a judgement.

The definition of an MNC and the basic benefit-cost table are in the [1.5 and 1.6 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-growth-and-evolution-and-multinational-companies-mncs/). This page gives you the tools to evaluate: impacts sorted by stakeholder, how spillovers and crowding out work, how to follow the money, why the time horizon changes the answer, and what makes the impact larger or smaller. Recall the content with the [1.6 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-6-revision-notes/) and practise with the [1.6 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-6-practice/). All companies, countries and figures are invented; money is in dollars. Definitions are standard textbook forms.

## Who is affected: impacts by stakeholder

The same MNC can help one group in the host country and harm another. Sorting impacts by stakeholder makes an answer balanced and specific.

| Stakeholder in the host country | Possible gains | Possible losses |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Jobs, often higher wages than local firms, training | Poor conditions in some cases; jobs lost if the MNC leaves |
| Local suppliers | New, large orders; higher standards that win other customers | Dependence on one buyer that can switch supplier |
| Local competitors | Pressure to improve; staff trained by the MNC may join them | Loss of customers; closure if they cannot match the MNC's prices |
| Consumers | Wider choice, lower prices, better quality | Less choice later if local rivals close |
| Government | Tax revenue, export earnings, investment | Cost of incentives offered; pressure from a powerful company |
| Local community and environment | Infrastructure such as roads; community projects | Pollution, use of land and water, pressure on local culture |

## Spillovers: the long-run gains

A spillover is a benefit that spreads beyond the MNC itself. Three types matter most.

1. **Technology transfer.** Local suppliers learn new production methods to meet the MNC's standards.
2. **Skills.** Workers trained by the MNC may later work for local firms or start their own.
3. **Supplier linkages.** If the MNC buys inputs locally, its spending creates further jobs in supplying firms.

Spillovers are largest when the MNC buys a high share of its inputs locally and employs local people in skilled and managerial roles. They are smallest when it imports most inputs and uses local staff only for low-skilled work. Look for these details in a case.

## Crowding out: when local firms lose

Crowding out happens when an MNC's lower costs, stronger brand or easier access to finance take customers away from local firms, which then shrink or close.

**Worked example 1: a supermarket in Torvaig.** Brightmart, an MNC, opens a large supermarket in the town of Torvaig.

| Effect | Figure |
|---|---|
| Jobs created at Brightmart | 250 |
| Small local shops that close within two years | 12 |
| Jobs lost at those shops | 60 |
| Typical fall in grocery prices | 8% |

Net jobs: 250 − 60 = **+190**. Consumers gain from lower prices.

But the evaluation should go further. Shop owners lose businesses they owned, not just jobs, and profits from the shops stayed in the town, while some of Brightmart's profit leaves the country. If Brightmart later becomes the only grocer in town, it may raise prices again. The impact is positive in numbers in the short run; whether it stays positive depends on whether competition survives.

## Following the money

Financial flows show how much of an MNC's activity stays in the host country.

**Worked example 2: Kelso Motors in Marendia.** Kelso Motors builds an assembly plant in Marendia. Each year it:

- employs 2,000 local workers at an average wage of 9,000;
- buys 30m of parts from Marendian suppliers;
- makes a profit of 20m, taxed at 15%;
- sends 80% of its after-tax profit to its home country.

The Marendian government paid 10m, once, to build a road to the site.

```
Wages:                 2,000 × 9,000 = 18.0m
Tax:                   20m × 15% = 3.0m
After-tax profit:      20m − 3.0m = 17.0m
Sent abroad:           17.0m × 80% = 13.6m
Reinvested locally:    17.0m − 13.6m = 3.4m
Staying in Marendia:   18.0 + 30.0 + 3.0 + 3.4 = 54.4m a year
```

Each year about 54.4m is spent or kept in Marendia, against 13.6m that leaves. The one-off 10m road cost is recovered by tax alone in just over three years (10 ÷ 3.0 = 3.3), and the road may also serve other users.

What the numbers leave out: whether the 15% tax rate is lower than local firms pay, whether the jobs are skilled, and the environmental cost of the plant. These belong in the evaluation alongside the figures.

## The time horizon changes the answer

| Short run | Long run |
|---|---|
| Construction jobs and spending | Permanent jobs, if the MNC stays |
| Incentives cost the government money | Tax revenue builds up once incentives end |
| Local firms face sudden competition | Local firms either improve or close |
| Training costs for the MNC | Skills spread through the economy |

MNCs can also be **footloose**: they may relocate when costs rise or incentives end. A plant that leaves after five years may leave unemployment and unused buildings behind. In a case, look for the length of any contract, tax holiday or lease.

## What makes the impact larger or smaller

- **The host country's rules.** Strong labour and environmental laws, enforced, limit harm; weak ones allow it.
- **Local content.** The share of inputs bought locally drives supplier linkages.
- **Type of activity.** Research and design roles bring more skills than simple assembly.
- **Bargaining power.** A large host market gives the government more power to set conditions; a small, poor country may accept weaker terms to attract investment.
- **Profit retention.** The more profit reinvested locally, the more stays in the host economy. MNCs can also arrange their internal prices so that profit is recorded in lower-tax countries, which reduces tax paid in the host country.

## A method for evaluation questions

1. **Identify** the main impacts in the case, positive and negative.
2. **Sort** them by stakeholder: who gains and who loses?
3. **Measure** where you can: jobs, wages, tax, money sent abroad.
4. **Consider** time: is the gain lasting, or only while incentives apply?
5. **Judge** whether the overall impact is positive for this host country, and state what it depends on (for example, enforcement of environmental rules, or whether the MNC stays).

STEEPLE (social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal, ethical) is a useful checklist at step 1 so that you do not miss a category.

## Common mistakes

- Listing benefits and costs with no judgement.
- Treating all jobs as equal, ignoring skill, pay and security.
- Ignoring jobs lost in local firms when counting jobs created.
- Assuming money sent abroad means the MNC brings no benefit.
- Forgetting the time horizon and the risk that the MNC leaves.
- Writing about the MNC's home country when the guide asks about the host country.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** and **analyse** (AO2): link each impact to a stakeholder and to evidence in the case.
- **Discuss**, **evaluate**, **to what extent** (AO3): weigh gains against losses using figures where given, then judge, with a condition.
- **Calculate** (AO4): set out money flows step by step, as in the Kelso Motors example.
