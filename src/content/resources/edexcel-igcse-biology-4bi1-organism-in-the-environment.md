## 4.1 Four key terms

Ecology uses precise words. A mark is lost when two of them are swapped.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | All the organisms of **one species** living in one area at one time | All the dandelions on a school field |
| **Community** | All the populations of **different species** living in one area | The dandelions, grasses, insects, birds and soil organisms on the field |
| **Habitat** | The **place** where an organism lives | The field, or a pond |
| **Ecosystem** | A community together with the non-living (abiotic) environment, interacting | The field community plus its soil, light, temperature and water |

A simple way to remember the order of size is: population (one species), community (all species), ecosystem (all species and the physical surroundings).

## 4.2 Estimating population size with quadrats

A **quadrat** is a square frame, often 0.5 m by 0.5 m (area 0.25 m²) or 1 m by 1 m (area 1 m²). It is used for organisms that do not move or move slowly, such as plants, limpets or snails. You cannot count every individual in a large area, so you count a sample and scale up.

### Method for one area

1. Mark out the area with two tape measures laid at right angles.
2. Use random numbers as coordinates. Place the quadrat at each point. Random placement avoids choosing places that suit your expectations.
3. Count the number of the target species in each quadrat. Use a rule for plants on the edge, such as counting only those touching the top and left edges.
4. Repeat for a suitable number of quadrats. More quadrats give a better estimate.
5. Calculate the **mean** number per quadrat, then per m², then multiply by the total area.

### Worked example 1: one area

*A field measures 40 m by 25 m. A student places eight 0.5 m by 0.5 m quadrats at random and counts daisies: 4, 7, 2, 5, 6, 3, 8, 5. Estimate the population of daisies in the field.*

```
total = 4 + 7 + 2 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 8 + 5 = 40
mean per quadrat = 40 ÷ 8 = 5
quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m²
mean per m² = 5 ÷ 0.25 = 20
field area = 40 × 25 = 1000 m²
population = 20 × 1000 = 20 000 daisies
```

The result is an **estimate**. It assumes the quadrats are representative of the whole field.

### Worked example 2: comparing two areas

*A student compares buttercups in a shaded area and an open area. Ten 1 m² quadrats are used in each area. Shaded counts: 1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 1, 3, 0, 2, 0. Open counts: 6, 4, 5, 7, 3, 5, 6, 4, 5, 5. Compare the two areas.*

```
Shaded: total 10, mean = 10 ÷ 10 = 1.0 per m²
Open:   total 50, mean = 50 ÷ 10 = 5.0 per m²
5.0 ÷ 1.0 = 5
```

The open area has five times as many buttercups per m². To make the comparison fair, the student used the **same quadrat size**, the **same number of quadrats**, the same counting rule and random placement in both areas. Only the area changed.

Suggested explanation (4.5): buttercups need light for photosynthesis, so they grow better in the open area where light intensity is higher.

### Improving reliability

- Use more quadrats. A larger sample reduces the effect of an unusual quadrat.
- Ignore anomalies only if you have a reason, and say so.
- Repeat the whole survey on another day if conditions change.

## 4.3B Biodiversity (Paper 2 only)

**Biodiversity** is the variety of living organisms in an area. At IGCSE level, a high biodiversity means there are **many different species**, and they are present in **similar numbers**. Two areas can have the same total number of organisms but different biodiversity.

### Worked example 3

*Site X and site Y each have 50 plants in the sampled area. Site X has five species with 10, 9, 11, 10 and 10 plants. Site Y has three species with 45, 3 and 2 plants. Which site has the higher biodiversity?*

1. Site X has more species (five against three).
2. Site X has an even spread of numbers. Site Y is dominated by one species (45 out of 50).
3. Site X has the higher biodiversity.

A site with one dominant species, such as a field of one crop, usually has low biodiversity.

## 4.4B Distribution and biodiversity by quadrats (Paper 2 only)

### Investigating distribution along a transect

To investigate how organisms are **distributed** across a change in conditions, such as a path from deep shade under trees to an open field, you can lay a tape measure as a **transect** and place quadrats at regular intervals (for example every 2 m). Regular placement is deliberate here, because you want to follow the gradient.

At each point record:

- which species are present and their number or **percentage cover**
- an abiotic factor, such as light intensity measured with a light meter, or soil moisture.

Then compare how the species change along the line. For example, moss may decrease and grass may increase as light intensity rises.

### Percentage cover

For plants that overlap or are difficult to count individually, estimate the percentage of the quadrat covered by each species. A 100-square quadrat grid helps: if grass covers 46 of the 100 squares, the cover is 46%.

### Measuring biodiversity

1. Choose two or more areas and use the same method in each.
2. Place quadrats at random.
3. In each quadrat, identify and record every species and its number or cover.
4. Compare the number of species and how evenly the numbers are spread.

The area with more species, more evenly distributed, has the higher biodiversity.

## 4.5 Abiotic and biotic factors

**Abiotic factors** are non-living. **Biotic factors** are living.

| Abiotic | Biotic |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Food supply |
| Light intensity | Predators |
| Water availability | Competition (for food, light, space, mates) |
| pH | Disease and parasites |
| Mineral ions in soil | |
| Oxygen concentration in water | |
| Wind speed | |

To score full marks, link a named factor to a process and then to the population:

- **Light intensity** is low in a wood in summer, so there is less photosynthesis, so fewer plants grow on the woodland floor.
- **Predators** eat prey, so deaths rise and the prey population falls. When prey becomes scarce, predator numbers fall as well.
- **Competition** for food or space between members of one species limits the number the habitat can support.
- **Disease** spreads easily where organisms are crowded, so the population falls.

### Worked example 4

*Seaweed grows on rocks along a sea shore. At the top of the shore the rocks are out of water for long periods. At the bottom they are always covered. Explain why the species of seaweed change from the top to the bottom.*

1. Water availability and exposure vary along the shore, as do temperature and light.
2. Species that can survive drying out live at the top (abiotic factor).
3. Species that need to be covered by water live at the bottom.
4. Competition and grazing by animals may also limit where each species grows (biotic factors).

## Common mistakes

- Using "habitat" to mean "community", or "population" for all the organisms in an area. A population is one species.
- Placing quadrats where there are many plants. Quadrats must be placed at random for a population estimate.
- Counting with different quadrat sizes in the two areas.
- Forgetting to scale up from the mean per quadrat to the whole area.
- Dividing the mean per quadrat by the area of the whole field instead of the area of the quadrat.
- Defining biodiversity as "the number of organisms". It is the variety of species.
- Giving "temperature" as a biotic factor.
- Writing "it is too cold" without saying which process or population is affected.

## Self-check

1. State the difference between a population and a community.
2. Explain why the quadrats are placed at random in a population survey.
3. A mean of 3 plants per 0.25 m² quadrat is found. How many plants per m²?
4. Name two abiotic and two biotic factors that affect a population.

**Answers:** (1) A population is one species in an area; a community is all the species. (2) To avoid bias in choosing the sites, so the sample is representative. (3) 3 ÷ 0.25 = 12 per m². (4) Abiotic: for example temperature and light intensity. Biotic: for example predators and competition.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a habitat and an ecosystem?**
A habitat is the place where an organism lives. An ecosystem is the community of living things together with the non-living environment.

**Why use a mean in a quadrat survey?**
The count varies from quadrat to quadrat. The mean gives a typical value that can be scaled up to the whole area.

**Is biodiversity the same as the number of organisms?**
No. It is the variety of species, and how evenly the numbers are spread between them.
