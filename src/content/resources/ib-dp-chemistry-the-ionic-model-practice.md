## Questions

**1.** Magnesium has atomic number 12.

**(a)** State what is meant by the term cation. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the formula of the ion formed by magnesium and the number of electrons it contains. **[2]**

**2.** The electron configurations of three atoms, J, L and M, are shown. J, L and M are not their chemical symbols.

J: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵
L: [Ar] 4s²
M: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p³

**(a)** Deduce the charge on the ion formed by each atom. **[3]**
**(b)** Deduce the formula of the compound formed between L and M, using the letters given. **[1]**

**3.** Iron and copper are transition elements.

**(a)** Explain why the name of an iron compound includes a Roman numeral but the name of a sodium compound does not. **[1]**
**(b)** Name Fe(OH)₂ and FePO₄. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the formula of copper(I) sulfide. **[1]**

**4.** Write the formula of each compound.

**(a)** ammonium sulfate **[1]**
**(b)** scandium nitrate **[1]**
**(c)** potassium hydrogencarbonate **[1]**
**(d)** iron(II) phosphate **[1]**

**5.** Name each compound.

**(a)** Cs₂S **[1]**
**(b)** Ca(OH)₂ **[1]**
**(c)** Cr₂(CO₃)₃ **[1]**

**6.** A technician makes up a solution containing 0.150 mol of gallium sulfate, Ga₂(SO₄)₃. Take the Avogadro constant as 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** State the number of ions in one formula unit of gallium sulfate. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total number of ions present in the solution. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the number of sulfate ions present. **[1]**

**7.** The formula of solid potassium iodide is written as KI.

**(a)** Describe the structure of solid potassium iodide. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why KI is described as an empirical formula. **[1]**

**8.** A student tests lithium chloride for electrical conductivity in three states: as a solid, as a liquid at high temperature, and as a solution in water.

**(a)** Predict, for each state, whether lithium chloride conducts. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your predictions in terms of the particles present. **[3]**

**9.** A sample of rubidium bromide dissolves in water but not in hexane. Explain this difference. **[3]**

**10.** Use these values for the lattice enthalpies: NaF 926 kJ mol⁻¹, MgO 3791 kJ mol⁻¹. Use these ionic radii (pm): Na⁺ 102, Mg²⁺ 72, F⁻ 133, O²⁻ 140.

**(a)** Write an equation, including state symbols, for the process whose enthalpy change is the lattice enthalpy of magnesium oxide. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the lattice enthalpy of MgO is roughly four times that of NaF. **[3]**
**(c)** Predict which of the two compounds has the higher melting point, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**11.** Use these ionic radii (pm): Li⁺ 76, Cs⁺ 167, Ca²⁺ 100, Ba²⁺ 135, Cl⁻ 181, O²⁻ 140.

**(a)** Rank caesium chloride, lithium chloride, barium oxide and calcium oxide in order of decreasing lattice enthalpy. Explain your order. **[4]**
**(b)** Lithium chloride is described as having low volatility. Explain this property. **[2]**
**(c)** Calcium oxide forms when calcium burns in oxygen. Use electron configurations to explain the charge on each ion formed. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A positive ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons [1]
**(b)** **Mg²⁺** [1]; 12 − 2 = **10 electrons** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The charge must be written with both number and sign, as 2+; "Mg2" or "Mg⁺²" is not the correct way to write the ion.

**2. (a)** J has 7 outer electrons and gains 1: **J⁻** [1]. L has 2 outer electrons and loses them: **L²⁺** [1]. M has 5 outer electrons (4s² 4p³) and gains 3: **M³⁻** [1]
**(b)** LCM of 2 and 3 is 6, so 3 L²⁺ and 2 M³⁻: **L₃M₂** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For M, count only the n = 4 electrons; the full 3d¹⁰ sublevel is not part of the outer level, and counting it leads to a wrong charge.

**3. (a)** Iron forms ions of more than one charge (Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺), so the numeral shows which ion is present; sodium only forms Na⁺ [1]
**(b)** **iron(II) hydroxide** [1]; **iron(III) phosphate** [1]
**(c)** Cu⁺ and S²⁻ in a 2 : 1 ratio: **Cu₂S** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), the numeral comes from the anion: PO₄³⁻ carries 3−, so the single iron ion must be 3+. Show that link if a question asks you to explain the name.

**4. (a)** **(NH₄)₂SO₄** [1]
**(b)** **Sc(NO₃)₃** [1]
**(c)** **KHCO₃** [1]
**(d)** Fe²⁺ and PO₄³⁻, LCM 6: **Fe₃(PO₄)₂** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Brackets are part of the formula. Without them, a formula such as Fe3PO42 is ambiguous, so write the brackets every time a polyatomic ion appears more than once.

**5. (a)** **caesium sulfide** [1]
**(b)** **calcium hydroxide** [1]
**(c)** Three CO₃²⁻ give 6−, shared by two chromium ions: **chromium(III) carbonate** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check the ending of the anion name: "sulfate" for Cs₂S or "carbide" for CO₃²⁻ names a different species altogether.

**6. (a)** 2 Ga³⁺ + 3 SO₄²⁻ = **5 ions** [1]
**(b)** moles of ions = 5 × 0.150 = 0.750 mol [1]; number = 0.750 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **4.52 × 10²³** [1]
**(c)** 3 × 0.150 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **2.71 × 10²³** sulfate ions [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the ion count per formula unit in your working, so your method stays clear to the reader even if the arithmetic slips.

**7. (a)** A giant three-dimensional lattice of K⁺ and I⁻ ions in a regular repeating arrangement [1]; each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge, held by electrostatic attraction in all directions [1]
**(b)** There are no discrete molecules; the formula shows only the simplest ratio of ions, 1 : 1 [1]
*Examiner insight:* Words such as "molecule" or "intermolecular forces" in a description of an ionic solid contradict the model, so avoid them even in passing.

**8. (a)** Solid: does not conduct; liquid (molten): conducts; aqueous: conducts [1]
**(b)** Lithium chloride contains Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions [1]. In the solid, every ion is locked into its place in the lattice, so no charged particle can travel through it [1]. Once the solid melts or dissolves, the Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions can drift through the liquid and carry charge to the electrodes [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name ions as the charge carriers; an answer that mentions moving electrons for any of the three states describes metallic conduction instead.

**9.** Water is polar; its δ− oxygen is attracted to Rb⁺ and its δ+ hydrogen atoms are attracted to Br⁻ [1]. These attractions are strong enough to separate the ions from the lattice and surround them [1]. Hexane is non-polar, so it cannot attract the ions strongly enough to overcome the attractions in the lattice [1]
*Examiner insight:* Get the orientation right: say which end of the water molecule faces which ion, because reversing them is a factual error.

**10. (a)** MgO(s) → Mg²⁺(g) + O²⁻(g): correct species and direction [1]; correct state symbols [1]
**(b)** Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ carry charges of 2+ and 2−, against 1+ and 1− in NaF, so the charge product is four times larger [1]. The ions in MgO are also slightly closer (72 + 140 = 212 pm against 102 + 133 = 235 pm) [1]. A higher charge product and a shorter distance give a stronger electrostatic attraction, so far more energy is needed to separate the ions; the charge factor accounts for most of the difference (3791/926 ≈ 4.1) [1]
**(c)** **MgO** has the higher melting point [1]; its ions attract each other more strongly, so more energy is needed to break down the lattice [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), the lattice enthalpy refers to separating the solid into gaseous ions; writing atoms, or a reaction forming the solid from elements, describes a different enthalpy change.

**11. (a)** **CaO > BaO > LiCl > CsCl** [1]. CaO and BaO contain 2+ and 2− ions, so they have stronger attractions than the singly charged chlorides [1]. Ca²⁺ is smaller than Ba²⁺ (distances 240 pm and 275 pm), so CaO is larger than BaO [1]. Li⁺ is smaller than Cs⁺ (distances 257 pm and 348 pm), so LiCl is larger than CsCl [1]
**(b)** Each ion is strongly attracted to the many oppositely charged ions around it, right across the lattice [1]; overcoming all of these attractions takes a lot of energy, which gives lithium chloride high melting and boiling points [1]
**(c)** Calcium ([Ar] 4s²) loses its two 4s electrons to form Ca²⁺, with the configuration of argon [1]. Oxygen (1s² 2s² 2p⁴) gains two electrons to fill 2p, forming O²⁻ with the configuration of neon [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), back up each placing with a reason that names charge or radius, and when the charges differ, make the charge comparison first.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing charges with the sign first (Mg⁺²) or leaving out the number (Ca⁺ for calcium).
- Counting the full 3d¹⁰ sublevel as outer electrons for elements such as gallium, arsenic or selenium.
- Dropping brackets round polyatomic ions, or changing the formula of the polyatomic ion itself.
- Giving the Roman numeral without checking the charge carried by the anion.
- Multiplying by the number of formula units instead of the number of ions per formula unit in mole questions.
- Describing an ionic lattice using "molecules" or "intermolecular forces".
- Stating that ionic compounds conduct because electrons move, or that the solid conducts at all.
- Describing water's attraction to ions with the dipole the wrong way round.
- Ranking lattice enthalpies by radius alone when the ions carry different charges.
- Writing a lattice enthalpy equation with atoms, or with the solid as the product.
