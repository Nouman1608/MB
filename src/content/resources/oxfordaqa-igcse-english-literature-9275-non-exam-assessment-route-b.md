This guide covers **3.3.3 Non-exam assessment** (NEA) in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Literature (9275). The NEA belongs to **Route B only**: Route B candidates sit Paper 1, Paper 2b and
the NEA, while Route A candidates sit an unseen prose section on Paper 2a instead. For the whole of
Route B, see the
[Route B Poetry and non-exam assessment study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-route-b-poetry-and-non-exam-assessment-study-guide/).

## What 3.3.3 requires

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Task | One extended response to a prose fiction text |
| Text | A substantial **whole** prose fiction text |
| Exclusion | Not one of the prescribed set texts for 9275 |
| Range | Any literary period; from the literary canon of the British Isles or seminal world literature |
| Standard | Similar in quality, scope and challenge to the Paper 1 set texts |
| Marks | 30 marks, 20% of the qualification |
| Marking | Internally assessed by your teachers using the same assessment objectives, then moderated |

The specification describes "one extended response"; check with your centre whether any word guidance
applies. Your teacher will explain the rules
on supervision, drafting and feedback that your centre applies.

## How the NEA is marked

The NEA is marked against the same three assessment objectives as the exams:

| AO | Focus | Weighting in the NEA |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Understanding of, and engagement with, themes, ideas and contexts | 8% |
| AO2 | Analysis of how writers create meanings and effects | 8% |
| AO3 | Informed, personal responses, using appropriate terminology and coherent, accurate writing | 4% |

The mark scheme has six levels. Level 6 (26–30) describes convincing, critical analysis and
exploration; Level 4 (16–20) describes clear understanding; Level 1 (1–5) describes simple, explicit
comments that are narrative or descriptive. From Level 3 upwards, the descriptors refer to the
**whole text**, so an essay that only covers a few chapters limits itself.

## Why the NEA is different from an exam answer

The NEA tests the same skills as Paper 1, but the conditions change what is possible and what is
expected:

- **Time.** You can reread the novel, choose evidence carefully and revise your argument. Your teachers
  and the moderator will expect more precise evidence and a better-structured argument than a 45-minute
  exam answer.
- **Text in hand.** You can quote accurately and from across the whole text, so a conceptualised
  argument tracing patterns across the novel is achievable.
- **A chosen task.** There is no question paper setting the focus for you, so your task must be
  focused enough to give your essay a clear argument.

## Step 1: choosing the text

Your teacher will guide or confirm the choice. A good NEA text:

- is a **substantial** novel, not a short novella or single short story;
- is **not** on the 9275 prescribed list (the Paper 1 prose list includes *Great Expectations*, *To
  Kill a Mockingbird*, *Purple Hibiscus*, *Never Let Me Go*, *The Book Thief* and the OxfordAQA short
  stories anthology, and the list changes with the revised specification from September 2026);
- offers enough to analyse in narrative method, structure and language, not just plot;
- has themes and contexts you can discuss with confidence.

Novels such as *Jane Eyre*, *Frankenstein* and *Things Fall Apart* are examples of the kind of text
that meets the standard. Choose a text you will enjoy rereading several times.

## Step 2: framing the task

The task should ask **how** the writer presents an idea, character or relationship across the whole
text. Compare:

| Weak task | Strong task |
|---|---|
| "Write about the main character." | "How does the writer present the main character's struggle between duty and desire?" |
| "What happens in the novel?" | "How does the writer use the novel's structure to shape the reader's sympathy?" |
| "Is the ending good?" | "How far does the ending resolve the novel's central conflict about belonging?" |

A strong task has a focus, invites an argument and points you towards the writer's methods.

## Step 3: building a thesis

A thesis is a one or two sentence answer to the task that you will spend the essay proving. It should
be arguable: someone could reasonably disagree. For example: *"The novel presents independence as
something its heroine must win in speech before she can win it in circumstance."* A thesis like this
gives every paragraph a purpose.

## Worked example: an NEA plan

Task: *How does the writer present the theme of isolation in your chosen novel?*

1. **Introduction:** thesis that isolation is both a punishment and a source of self-knowledge; brief
   reference to context where it shapes the theme.
2. **Opening chapters:** how the narrative voice establishes isolation, with two or three short
   quotations and analysis of viewpoint.
3. **Setting:** how places in the novel embody isolation; analysis of description and symbol.
4. **Relationships:** a key relationship that breaks or deepens isolation; analysis of dialogue and
   structure (where the relationship appears in the novel).
5. **Turning point:** the moment the protagonist's view of isolation changes; structural significance.
6. **Ending:** whether isolation is overcome, accepted or made permanent; how the final pages echo the
   opening.
7. **Conclusion:** return to the thesis and offer a personal judgement, perhaps acknowledging an
   alternative reading.

Each section should include method analysis (AO2), connection to themes and context (AO1), and an
argued voice (AO3). The plan also makes sure evidence comes from the beginning, middle and end of the
novel.

## Step 4: drafting and revising

- Write a full first draft before polishing any part of it.
- Revise for **argument** first: does every paragraph advance the thesis? Can any be cut?
- Revise for **analysis**: after each quotation, have you explained the method and its effect?
- Then edit for **accuracy**: quotations checked against the text, spelling, punctuation and
  terminology.
- Keep notes and drafts, as your centre may need them to confirm the work is your own.

## Using context and critical views

Context belongs in the NEA when it explains a choice the writer makes, for example how attitudes to
class or gender at the time sharpen a scene. Critical views from other readers can help AO3 if you
put them in your own words, acknowledge where they came from, and then respond with your own
judgement. Never let either replace your own analysis.

## Authenticity

The work you submit must be your own. Do not copy from websites, study guides or other students, and
do not submit text produced by anyone else, including AI tools, as your own writing. Record any
sources you use, and follow any declaration your centre asks you to sign.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a set text** or a text that is too short or slight.
- **Writing about part of the novel**, when the levels reward a whole-text response.
- **Retelling the plot**, which keeps the work at the lowest level.
- **An unfocused task** that produces a list of observations rather than an argument.
- **Bolted-on context** with no link to the writer's choices.
- **Over-quoting**, using long passages instead of analysing a few precise words.
- **Leaving it late**, so that there is no time to revise the argument.

## Planning your time

Work backwards from your centre's deadline. Allow time to reread the novel with the task in mind,
gather and organise evidence, write a plan, complete a draft, revise the argument and do a final
accuracy check. Short, regular sessions are better than one long weekend.
