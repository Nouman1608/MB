## Questions

**1.** Explain one supply-side policy that raises the quality of labour and one that raises the quantity of capital. **[4]**

**2.** In Country L, income up to 12,000 is untaxed, income from 12,000 to 48,000 is taxed at 25%, and income above 48,000 at 45%. A worker earns 60,000.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid. **[2]**
**(b)** The top rate falls to 40%. Calculate how much of a 3,000 pay rise the worker keeps, before and after the cut. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason the cut may not raise the hours this worker supplies. **[2]**

**3.** A worker pays 20% tax and loses benefit equal to 60% of net earnings on any extra pay.

**(a)** Calculate the effective marginal rate on an extra 200. **[2]**
**(b)** The benefit loss falls to 40% of net earnings. Calculate the new rate. **[2]**

**4.** Country P raises its NMW from 8.00 to 8.40 an hour. 20,000 workers earn it, each working 25 hours a week. Wage elasticity of demand for labour is −0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in the wage. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in employment, in workers. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the new weekly wage bill and its percentage change. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between a National Minimum Wage and a living wage. **[3]**

**6.** A job in Region S pays 2,800 more a year than the same job in Region N, but housing costs 3,500 more a year.

**(a)** Calculate the net gain from moving. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one housing policy that would improve labour market flexibility. **[2]**

**7.** Country M has population 25m and GDP 750bn. Net immigration of 0.2m adds 150,000 workers, each producing 30,000.

**(a)** Calculate new GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate GDP per head before and after, and comment on the change. **[3]**

**8.** Explain one benefit and one risk of privatising a state-owned firm. **[4]**

**9.** In a market, demand is P = 80 − Q and supply is P = 20 + 2Q.

**(a)** Find equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** A subsidy of 9 per unit shifts supply to P = 11 + 2Q. Find the new quantity and the price consumers pay. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the price producers receive and the cost to the government. **[2]**
**(d)** State one limitation of subsidies. **[1]**

**10.** A new rail link saves 15 minutes on each of 8,000 journeys a day. Time is valued at 12 an hour and there are 250 days a year. The link costs 80m.

**(a)** Calculate the annual time-saving benefit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of years to repay the cost, ignoring discounting, and explain why this may overstate the project's worth. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how trade union reform might raise potential output, and give one limitation. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of supply-side policies in achieving a government's macroeconomic objectives. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Education and training build workers' skills, lifting productivity [1], which lowers unit costs and improves the quality of labour [1]. Infrastructure investment, such as a rail link, adds to the capital stock [1], which lowers firms' transport costs and shifts LRAS right [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the factor each policy changes and give a link to output; naming a policy alone is descriptive.

**2. (a)** Tax = 0.25 × 36,000 = 9,000 [1] plus 0.45 × 12,000 = 5,400 gives **14,400** [1].
**(b)** Before: 3,000 × 0.55 = 1,650 [1]. After: 3,000 × 0.60 = **1,800** [1].
**(c)** The higher take-home pay creates an income effect [1]: the worker can reach the same income with fewer hours and may choose more leisure [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Work band by band; applying one rate to all income is the usual slip. Part (c) needs a reason, not a repeat of the theory.

**3. (a)** Tax = 40, net = 160 [1]. Benefit lost = 96, kept = 64, so rate = **68%** [1].
**(b)** Benefit lost = 64, kept = 96, rate = **52%** [1]. The rate falls by 16 percentage points [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the taper to net, not gross, earnings, and state the answer as a rate on the extra 200.

**4. (a)** 0.40 ÷ 8.00 = **5%** [1].
**(b)** −0.4 × 5% = −2% [1]; 2% of 20,000 = **400 workers lost** [1].
**(c)** Old bill = 20,000 × 25 × 8.00 = 4,000,000. New bill = 19,600 × 25 × 8.40 = **4,116,000** [1], a rise of **2.9%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the old and new bill; setting out each step lets an examiner see your method even if a slip occurs.

**5.** The NMW is a legal minimum which employers must pay [1]. A living wage is a rate linked to estimated living costs [1] which is promoted and paid voluntarily [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs both items contrasted, ideally on compulsion.

**6. (a)** 2,800 − 3,500 = **−700** [1], a net loss, so the worker will not move [1].
**(b)** Easing planning restrictions allows more homes to be built [1], lowering rents in Region S and making the move worthwhile, which reduces vacancies [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the policy to the figures in the stem rather than writing generally about housing.

**7. (a)** Extra output = 150,000 × 30,000 = 4.5bn [1]. New GDP = **754.5bn** [1].
**(b)** Before: 750bn ÷ 25m = **30,000** [1]. After: 754.5bn ÷ 25.2m = **29,940** [1]. GDP rises 0.6% but GDP per head falls 0.2%, because migrants produce less than the average [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the new population; using the old one hides the fall.

**8.** Benefit: the profit motive and shareholder pressure encourage cost cutting [1], which can lower costs and prices [1]. Risk: a private monopoly may use its market power to raise prices [1], harming consumers unless regulated [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs a developed chain; "efficiency" alone is an assertion.

**9. (a)** 80 − Q = 20 + 2Q gives Q = 20 and P = **60** [1].
**(b)** 80 − Q = 11 + 2Q gives 3Q = 69, so Q = **23** [1] and consumers pay P = **57** [1].
**(c)** Producers receive 57 + 9 = **66** [1]. Cost = 9 × 23 = **207** [1].
**(d)** The subsidy is a cost to taxpayers and may keep inefficient firms going [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Producers receive price plus subsidy; many answers stop at the consumer price.

**10. (a)** 8,000 × (15 ÷ 60) × 12 = 24,000 a day [1]; × 250 = **6m** [1].
**(b)** 80 ÷ 6 = **13.3 years** [1]. Benefits arrive over time and are worth less than money now, and the link may also carry costs, so ignoring discounting overstates the value [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a unit with each figure and a reason for the limitation.

**11.** Union reform, such as limits on strikes, may reduce days lost and make hiring and pay more flexible [1], lowering unit labour costs [1]. Lower costs raise profitability and investment, so LRAS shifts right [1]. Limitation: unions may also raise productivity through worker voice, so productivity could fall if morale drops [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach the shift in LRAS; stopping at "less strikes" leaves the chain unfinished.

**12.** Definition: supply-side policies raise productive capacity and shift LRAS right [1]. Chain 1: training raises productivity, lowers unit costs, so the trend growth rate and competitiveness improve [2]. Chain 2: lower marginal tax and reformed benefits raise labour supply, so unemployment falls and output rises [2]. Evaluation: policies take years to work [1]; they cost money, for example subsidies or infrastructure [1]; gains depend on spare capacity and demand [1]; tax cuts and weaker unions may widen inequality [1]. Judgement: policies work best on growth and structural unemployment, and are weaker on short-term inflation, so a mix with demand-side policy suits best [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the measures against each other rather than listing them, and end with a reasoned judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting AD instead of LRAS.
- Applying one tax rate to all income.
- Dividing by the old population in per-head figures.
- Forgetting that producers receive the subsidy.
- Giving the NMW effect with no elasticity.
- Listing policies without a chain.
- Omitting units.
- Ending with a summary that never picks a side.
