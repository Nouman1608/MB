These notes condense section 2.7, The labour market, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The care-worker wage example and two full net-pay calculations are in the [2.7 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-labour-market-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.7 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-labour-market-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All tax rates here are invented, not real UK rates.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour market | The market where workers sell their labour and employers buy it |
| Wage | The price of labour, per hour, week or year |
| Demand for labour | The number of workers employers want to hire at each wage |
| Supply of labour | The number of people willing and able to work in a job at each wage |
| Derived demand | Labour is demanded because of demand for the product it makes |
| Equilibrium wage | The wage at which labour demand equals labour supply |
| Trade union | An organisation that represents workers in bargaining with employers |
| Gross pay | Earnings before any deductions |
| Net pay | Take-home pay after deductions |
| Income tax | A tax on income, paid to the government |
| National insurance | A deduction from pay, paid to the government |
| Pension contribution | Part of pay saved for the worker's retirement |

## Role and operation

- Matches workers to jobs; the wage is the price.
- Interaction stages: recruitment, selection, agreement, employment, bargaining.
- Bargaining power: workers stronger when skills are scarce, unemployment is low or a union bargains for them; employers stronger when many can do the job or unemployment is high.

## Wage determination

```
Demand for labour: downward sloping (cheaper labour → more hired)
Supply of labour: upward sloping (higher wage → more willing workers)
Equilibrium: where they cross
Wage below equilibrium → shortage of workers → wages rise
Wage above equilibrium → surplus of workers → wages fall
```

**Worked reminder.** Demand for electricians shifts right by 200 at every wage. At the old £20 an hour there are 1,200 jobs and 1,000 willing workers: a shortage of **200**, so wages rise until the market clears.

## Factors shifting labour demand

- Demand for the product.
- Productivity of workers.
- Price and capability of machinery.
- Costs of employing each worker.

## Factors shifting labour supply

- Skills and training needed.
- Wages in other occupations.
- Working conditions and non-wage benefits.
- Size of the working population (including migration).

## Why pay differs

| High pay | Low pay |
|---|---|
| Scarce skills, long training | Many people can do the job |
| High value added (strong demand) | Low value added |
| Dangerous or unsociable work | Pleasant, flexible work |
| Strong bargaining power | Weak bargaining power |

## Gross and net pay method

```
1. Gross pay (hourly × hours × weeks, plus overtime or bonus)
2. Income tax on each band separately
3. National insurance on each band separately
4. Pension as % of gross
5. Net pay = gross − tax − NI − pension
6. Convert to monthly or weekly if asked
```

**Worked reminder (invented rules: tax 20% above £12,000; NI 10% above £12,000; pension 5%).** Gross £20,000. Tax = 8,000 × 0.20 = £1,600; NI = 8,000 × 0.10 = £800; pension = £1,000. Net = **£16,600**, or £1,383.33 a month.

## Higher-rate trap

With a 40% band above £50,000, only income **above** £50,000 is taxed at 40%. On £60,000: 38,000 × 0.20 + 10,000 × 0.40 = £11,600, not 60,000 × 0.40.

## Exam traps

- Workers are the suppliers in a labour market, not the buyers.
- A wage change is a movement along the curves, not a shift.
- Pay differences explained without supply and demand.
- Higher rate applied to the whole income.
- Pension left out of deductions.
- Annual and monthly figures mixed.

## Quick self-test

1. Who supplies and who demands labour?
2. Explain what is meant by derived demand.
3. A new factory opens in a town. What happens to demand for labour there?
4. Give two factors that affect the supply of nurses.
5. At a wage of £15, 500 workers are demanded and 650 supplied. Name and size the imbalance.
6. What happens to the wage in question 5?
7. Give one reason a footballer in a top league may earn far more than a cleaner.
8. Define net pay.
9. A worker earns £12 an hour for 40 hours a week. Calculate weekly gross pay.
10. Using the rules in the worked reminder, calculate net pay on a gross salary of £30,000.
11. On a salary of £56,000 with a 40% rate above £50,000, how much income is taxed at 40%?
12. Give one way a trade union can affect wages.

### Answers

1. Workers supply labour; employers demand it.
2. Labour is wanted because of the demand for the goods or services it produces.
3. Demand for labour increases (shifts right).
4. Length and cost of training, pay in other jobs, working conditions, size of the working population (any two).
5. Surplus of 650 − 500 = **150** workers.
6. Wages tend to fall until demand equals supply.
7. Very few people have the talent (low supply) and the player brings in large revenue for the club (high demand).
8. Pay after deductions such as income tax, national insurance and pension contributions.
9. 12 × 40 = **£480**.
10. Tax = 18,000 × 0.20 = £3,600; NI = 18,000 × 0.10 = £1,800; pension = £1,500; net = 30,000 − 6,900 = **£23,100**.
11. £56,000 − £50,000 = **£6,000**.
12. By bargaining collectively, it can increase workers' bargaining power and push wages above what each worker could win alone.
