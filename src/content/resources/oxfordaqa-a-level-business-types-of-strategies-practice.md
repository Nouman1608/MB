## Questions

**1.** State two economies of scale named in the specification. **[2]**

**2.** Larkmoor Furniture makes dining chairs. In March its fixed costs were £84,000, its variable cost was £35 per chair and it made 2,800 chairs. Its maximum possible output is 3,500 chairs a month. Calculate Larkmoor's unit cost and its capacity utilisation for March. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between product innovation and process innovation, using an example of each. **[3]**

**4.** Cindervale Batteries sells into a market worth £250m this year and £230m last year. The four largest firms sell £70m, £55m, £40m and £35m.

**(a)** Calculate the four firm concentration ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the market growth rate, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** Thornleigh Coffee, a UK roaster, buys beans priced at USD 4,500 per tonne. The exchange rate moves from £1 = USD 1.25 to £1 = USD 1.35. Calculate the change in the sterling cost per tonne and the percentage change, to one decimal place. **[4]**

**6.** Wrenhollow Toys sells in two markets. In market X its PED is −0.8; in market Y its YED is +2.5.

**(a)** Wrenhollow cuts its price in market X by 5%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded and in sales revenue. **[3]**
**(b)** Incomes in market Y are forecast to fall by 2%. Calculate the expected change in quantity demanded. **[1]**

**7.** Elmsridge Hotels reports revenue of £12m and net profit of £0.84m. During the year 66 staff left; average employment was 440.

**(a)** Calculate the net profit margin. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate labour turnover. **[2]**

**8.** Tidewell Laundries runs six dry-cleaning shops and wants to reach sixty within five years. Analyse two advantages to Tidewell of growing by franchising rather than opening its own shops. **[6]**

**9.** Larkmoor Furniture's demand has fallen, and its second factory now runs well below capacity. Explain two reasons for, and one problem of, closing the second factory. **[5]**

**10.** Marlowcote Locks makes door locks. Rivals have launched app-controlled smart locks, and Marlowcote's sales are falling. Its managers disagree: one wants a Kaizen programme on the existing range, another a large R&D budget for a smart lock. Assess which approach Marlowcote should take. **[10]**

**11.** Halvering Kettles is based in an EU country. It makes kettles in a factory in Asia at €6.40 a unit, plus €0.90 shipping. The EU now applies a 15% tariff to these kettles, charged on the factory cost. Halvering could re-shore production to a new plant at home costing €1.5m, where the unit cost including delivery would be €7.80. It sells 500,000 kettles a year.

**(a)** Calculate the new landed cost per unit from Asia, and the annual saving from re-shoring. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Halvering should re-shore production. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: purchasing economies (bulk-buying discounts) [1]; technical economies (larger, more efficient equipment) [1]; financial economies (cheaper borrowing). **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "state" question rewards the named economies; a vague answer such as "it gets cheaper to make things" names no specific economy and is unlikely to gain credit.

**2.** Total cost = 84,000 + (35 × 2,800) = £182,000 [1]. Unit cost = 182,000 ÷ 2,800 [1] = **£65** [1]. Capacity utilisation = 2,800 ÷ 3,500 × 100 = **80%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the total cost line; a unit cost found by dividing by 3,500 (capacity) is wrong, and visible working is what lets an examiner credit the correct parts.

**3.** Product innovation is a new or improved good or service, such as a phone with a longer battery life [1]. Process innovation is a new or improved way of making or delivering it, such as robotic packing in a warehouse [1]. Product innovation aims at sales and a USP; process innovation mainly at lower costs and better quality [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two stand-alone definitions do not show a difference; answers gain more credit when they say what each type of innovation achieves for the business.

**4. (a)** (70 + 55 + 40 + 35) ÷ 250 × 100 [1] = **80%** [1].
**(b)** (250 − 230) ÷ 230 × 100 [1] = **8.7%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by last year's figure for growth; dividing by 250 gives 8.0%, a common slip.

**5.** Old cost = 4,500 ÷ 1.25 = £3,600 [1]. New cost = 4,500 ÷ 1.35 = £3,333.33 [1]. Change = **a fall of £266.67** per tonne [1]. Percentage change = 266.67 ÷ 3,600 × 100 = **a fall of 7.4%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The pound has strengthened, so imports get cheaper; if your answer shows a rise, you have multiplied by the rate.

**6. (a)** Change in quantity = −0.8 × −5 = **+4%** [1]. New revenue = 0.95 × 1.04 = 0.988 of the old revenue [1], so revenue **falls by 1.2%** [1].
**(b)** 2.5 × −2 = **a 5% fall** in quantity demanded [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* With inelastic demand a price cut lowers revenue; say so in words after the number to show you understand the result.

**7. (a)** 0.84 ÷ 12 × 100 [1] = **7%** [1].
**(b)** 66 ÷ 440 × 100 [1] = **15%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the average number employed; a percentage sign is expected on both answers.

**8.** Advantage 1: franchisees pay to open shops with their own capital [1], so Tidewell can reach sixty shops without borrowing heavily or diluting its ownership [1], which keeps gearing and interest costs down while it expands fast [1].
Advantage 2: franchisees are owner-managers who keep part of the profit [1], so they have a strong incentive to run each shop well [1], which matters in a service where staff quality and customer care drive repeat custom [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each advantage needs a chain of reasoning tied to Tidewell's target of sixty shops; generic franchising points earn little credit for application.

**9.** Reason 1: low capacity utilisation spreads fixed costs over few units, raising unit cost [1]; closing one factory and moving output to the other lifts utilisation and cuts unit cost [1].
Reason 2: selling the site releases cash [1] that could reduce debt or fund new products [1].
Problem: redundancy pay and lower morale among remaining staff, and too little capacity if demand recovers [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the first reason to unit cost and utilisation; "to save money" without saying how is too vague for the second mark.

**10.** Kaizen means continuous small improvements from staff [1]; R&D is technical work to create a new product [1].
Kaizen could lower Marlowcote's costs and raise quality cheaply, with little risk [1], but it improves the existing mechanical range and does not answer rivals' smart locks [1]. As customers switch, a cheaper traditional lock may still lose sales [1].
R&D could give Marlowcote its own smart lock, and a patent could protect its design [1]. However, it is costly, slow and uncertain, and Marlowcote may lack software engineers [1], and finance must fund the spending before any revenue [1].
Judgement: the threat is a product change, so Kaizen alone is unlikely to stop the sales decline [1]. Marlowcote should fund targeted R&D, perhaps through a cross-functional team or a joint venture with a software firm, while using Kaizen to protect margins on its current range; this depends on how fast the market is switching and on its finances [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers weigh both options against Marlowcote's actual problem (falling sales to smart locks) and reach a conditional judgement; one-sided answers limit the evaluation credit.

**11. (a)** Tariff = 15% × 6.40 = €0.96 [1]. Landed cost = 6.40 + 0.90 + 0.96 = **€8.26** [1]. Saving per unit = 8.26 − 7.80 = €0.46 [1]. Annual saving = 0.46 × 500,000 = **€230,000** [1].
**(b)** For re-shoring: the tariff has made Asian supply €0.46 a unit dearer than home production [1]; re-shoring also shortens lead times and reduces supply-chain risk [1], and a "made in the EU" claim may support sustainability marketing [1].
Against: the €1.5m plant takes about 6.5 years to repay from the €230,000 saving [1]. Before the tariff, the landed cost was €7.30, below €7.80, so if the tariff is cut the case disappears [1]. Halvering would also need to recruit and train a new workforce, and home wages may rise faster than expected [1].
Judgement: re-shoring is justified only if Halvering expects the tariff to last and values supply security; otherwise it could negotiate with its supplier or move to a lower-tariff country [1]. A sensible option is to re-shore part of output first, to test costs [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your own part (a) figures in part (b); a judgement that rests on the tariff's likely duration is far stronger than one that ignores it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing total cost by capacity rather than actual output.
- Dividing by the new figure instead of the old one in growth and percentage change.
- Multiplying by the exchange rate when converting a foreign-currency cost into pounds or euros.
- Losing the minus sign in PED, then predicting the wrong direction of change.
- Using year-end staff numbers in labour turnover.
- Writing generic advantages of franchising or acquisition with no link to the business in the question.
- Treating Kaizen and R&D as the same thing because both "improve products".
- Giving an evaluation with no judgement, or a judgement with no condition.
