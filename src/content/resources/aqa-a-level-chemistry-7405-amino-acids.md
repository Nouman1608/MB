## Structure and names

An **α-amino acid** has an amino group, –NH₂, and a carboxyl group, –COOH, attached to the **same carbon atom**. The general formula is:

```
      R
      |
H₂N – C – COOH
      |
      H
```

R is a side chain that differs from one amino acid to another.

| Amino acid | R group | IUPAC name |
|---|---|---|
| Glycine | H | 2-aminoethanoic acid |
| Alanine | CH₃ | 2-aminopropanoic acid |
| Serine | CH₂OH | 2-amino-3-hydroxypropanoic acid |
| Cysteine | CH₂SH | 2-amino-3-sulfanylpropanoic acid |

In the IUPAC name the –COOH carbon is carbon 1, and the number 2 shows where the amino group is. Because the carbon bearing both groups has four different groups (H, NH₂, COOH and R) for every amino acid except glycine, those amino acids are **chiral** and exist as two optical isomers (see the Optical isomerism sub-topic). Glycine, with R = H, has two identical hydrogen atoms on that carbon and is not chiral. The structures of the common amino acids are given in the Chemistry Data Booklet.

## Acidic and basic properties

An amino acid contains both an acidic group and a basic group.

- The **carboxyl group**, –COOH, is acidic. It can donate H⁺.
- The **amino group**, –NH₂, is basic. Its nitrogen has a lone pair that accepts H⁺.

A molecule with both acidic and basic groups is **amphoteric**. This is why amino acids react with both acids and alkalis.

## The zwitterion

In the solid state and in aqueous solution at a suitable pH, the proton transfers from the –COOH group to the –NH₂ group within the same molecule. The result is a **zwitterion**: a species with both a positive and a negative charge, and no overall charge.

```
H₂N–CHR–COOH   →   ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻
```

When drawing a zwitterion:

1. Draw the central carbon with H, R, and the two groups.
2. Change –NH₂ into **–NH₃⁺**.
3. Change –COOH into **–COO⁻**.
4. Do not write an overall charge outside the structure.

For glycine the zwitterion is ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻, and for alanine it is ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻.

## The ion in acid solution

In acidic solution there are plenty of H⁺ ions. The –COO⁻ group of the zwitterion accepts H⁺ and becomes –COOH again. The amino group stays as –NH₃⁺. The amino acid is now a **cation** with an overall charge of 1+.

```
⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ + H⁺ → ⁺H₃N–CHR–COOH
```

For glycine in acid: ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH. This is the cation. In a reaction with hydrochloric acid, the product can be written as ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH Cl⁻.

## The ion in alkaline solution

In alkaline solution there are plenty of OH⁻ ions. The –NH₃⁺ group of the zwitterion loses H⁺ to hydroxide and becomes –NH₂. The carboxylate group stays as –COO⁻. The amino acid is now an **anion** with an overall charge of 1−.

```
⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ + OH⁻ → H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ + H₂O
```

For glycine in alkali: H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻. The sodium salt, sodium aminoethanoate, forms if sodium hydroxide is used.

| Condition | Form of glycine | Overall charge |
|---|---|---|
| Strongly acidic | ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH | 1+ |
| Neutral (zwitterion) | ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻ | 0 |
| Strongly alkaline | H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ | 1− |

## Properties explained by the ionic form

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting point (they often decompose on heating rather than melting at a low temperature) | In the solid the zwitterions are held in a lattice by strong ionic attractions between ⁺H₃N– and –COO⁻ groups |
| Soluble in water | The charged ends interact strongly with water molecules |
| Poorly soluble in non-polar solvents | The charged zwitterion has no attraction to non-polar molecules |
| Behave differently from separate amines and acids | The two groups interact within the molecule |

## Which form is present?

The form of an amino acid depends on the acidity of the solution.

| Solution | Which group has changed | Form of alanine |
|---|---|---|
| Strongly acidic (excess H⁺) | –COO⁻ has gained H⁺ | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH |
| Near neutral | Proton has moved from –COOH to –NH₂ | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ |
| Strongly alkaline (excess OH⁻) | –NH₃⁺ has lost H⁺ | H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ |

A useful check is to count the charge. The cation has +1, the zwitterion has +1 and −1, and the anion has −1. If your drawing does not give one of these totals, it contains an error. Also check that every nitrogen in an uncharged –NH₂ group still has its lone pair available, because that is the group that accepts H⁺.

## Comparing an amino acid with a simple acid and a simple amine

| Feature | Ethanoic acid | Ethylamine | Glycine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic group | –COOH | None | –COOH |
| Basic group | None | –NH₂ | –NH₂ |
| Reacts with NaOH | Yes | No | Yes |
| Reacts with HCl | No | Yes | Yes |
| Exists as ions in the solid | No | No | Yes (zwitterion) |
| Melting behaviour | Low melting point | Low boiling point | High, often decomposes |

This comparison shows what is special about an amino acid: the same molecule has both groups, and each can react with the other, so the properties are those of an ionic compound, not a simple molecule.

Alanine is H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH. Move one proton from –COOH to –NH₂:

⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻

## Worked example 2: ions of serine

Serine is H₂N–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH.

- In acid: ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH
- Zwitterion: ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂OH)–COO⁻
- In alkali: H₂N–CH(CH₂OH)–COO⁻

The –OH in the side chain is unchanged in each case. Only the amino and carboxyl groups change.

## Worked example 3: titration amounts

25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid exactly reacts with a solution of alanine (Mᵣ = 89.0). Calculate the mass of alanine.

```
⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ + H⁺ → ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH      (1 : 1)
n(HCl)       = 0.0250 × 0.100 = 0.00250 mol
n(alanine)   = 0.00250 mol
mass         = 0.00250 × 89.0 = 0.2225 g
```

## Worked example 4: the mass of a dipeptide

Two amino acids can join with the loss of water. For glycine (Mᵣ = 75.0) and alanine (Mᵣ = 89.0):

```
Mr of dipeptide = 75.0 + 89.0 − 18.0 = 146.0
```

This is the same condensation link that is studied in the Proteins sub-topic.

## Reactions of the two groups

| Group | Reacts with | Example |
|---|---|---|
| –NH₂ (as a base) | Acids | H₂N–CH₂–COOH + HCl → ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH Cl⁻ |
| –COOH (as an acid) | Alkalis | H₂N–CH₂–COOH + NaOH → H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂O |
| –COOH | Alcohols (acid catalyst) | Forms an ester |
| –NH₂ and –COOH | Another amino acid | Forms a peptide link |

## Common mistakes

- Writing the zwitterion as H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ (the anion) or ⁺H₃N–CHR–COOH (the cation).
- Writing –NH₃ instead of –NH₃⁺, or –COO instead of –COO⁻.
- Adding an overall charge to the zwitterion.
- Putting the amino group on the wrong carbon, or putting a charge on the R group.
- Confusing the ion in acid and in alkali: acid gives the 1+ cation and alkali gives the 1− anion.
- Not stating that the –NH₂ group is basic and the –COOH group is acidic.
- Claiming that amino acids have low melting points because they are small molecules.
- Calling a zwitterion "neutral" without saying it has both charges.

## Exam technique

- **Draw a zwitterion**: ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻, and check that the charges cancel.
- **Draw the ion in acid**: add H⁺ to –COO⁻ (the group gives –NH₃⁺ and –COOH).
- **Draw the ion in alkali**: remove H⁺ from –NH₃⁺ (the group gives –NH₂ and –COO⁻).
- **Explain properties**: refer to the ionic lattice and to interaction with water.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the proton move inside the molecule?**
The carboxyl group is acidic and the amino group is basic. The transfer produces the more stable charged form in water and in the solid.

**Is a zwitterion the same as a neutral molecule?**
No. It is neutral overall but has a positive and a negative charge in different places.

**Which amino acid is not chiral?**
Glycine, because its R group is hydrogen, so the central carbon has two identical groups.
