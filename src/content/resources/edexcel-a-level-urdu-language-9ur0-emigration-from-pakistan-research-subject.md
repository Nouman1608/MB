This guide is for students who choose **emigration from Pakistan (پاکستان سے ہجرت)**, the research subject of Theme 3, in Pearson Edexcel A Level Urdu (9UR0). The specification sets three aspects, all of which you must research:

- **ہجرت کی سیاسی اور معاشی وجوہات**: the political and economic reasons for emigration
- **اعلیٰ تعلیم یافتہ طبقے کا ترکِ وطن**: the emigration of the highly educated
- **پاکستانی معاشرے پر وطن لوٹنے والوں کے مثبت اثرات**: the positive effects on Pakistani society of those who return

Research must be based on **Pakistan only**. The [emigration revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-emigration-from-pakistan-revision-notes/) describe the main waves of emigration, remittances and families left behind. This guide maps the three aspects precisely, especially the third, which is easy to neglect, and shows how to turn research into a Paper 1 Section C answer. Practise with the [emigration practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-9ur0-emigration-from-pakistan-research-subject-practice/).

## The rules in brief

- Research all three aspects, independently, using at least two authentic Urdu sources; not your Paper 2 works.
- In the exam: no notes, no dictionary, one unseen Urdu text and a statement; about 320–370 words in Urdu.
- Three grids: research knowledge and analysis (AO4, 20), response to the text (AO2, 10), language (AO3, 10).
- For Theme 3, any content about another country's society gains no marks. Writing about destination countries is relevant only as it bears on emigration **from Pakistan**.

## Aspect 1: political and economic reasons

The aspect names **two kinds** of reason. Keep them separate, then show how they interact.

**Economic reasons (معاشی وجوہات):**

- Unemployment and underemployment, especially among educated young people.
- Wage gaps: the same skill earns far more in the Gulf, Europe or North America.
- Inflation and currency depreciation, which erode savings and salaries.
- Demand abroad: construction and services in the Gulf, health and technology elsewhere.

**Political reasons (سیاسی وجوہات):**

- Political instability (**سیاسی عدم استحکام**): frequent changes of government and uncertainty about the future.
- Insecurity: periods of terrorism and violence have pushed some families to leave.
- Concerns about rights, freedoms or discrimination, which some emigrants give as reasons.

**Interaction:** political uncertainty discourages investment, which reduces jobs, which drives economic emigration. Showing this chain earns analysis marks that a simple list does not.

Research task: find **one source** that gives an emigrant's own stated reasons, and **one** that gives an official or expert explanation. Comparing the two is excellent AO4 material.

## Aspect 2: emigration of the highly educated

**اعلیٰ تعلیم یافتہ طبقہ** means "the highly educated class": doctors, engineers, IT professionals, academics, nurses, accountants. This is the **brain drain** (**ذہین افراد کا انخلا**) debate. Prepare these angles:

1. **Who leaves and why**: professionals cite pay, research facilities, career structure, working conditions and their children's future.
2. **The cost to Pakistan**: public money spent on their education; shortages in hospitals and universities; fewer experienced mentors for the next generation.
3. **The counter-argument**: emigrant professionals send remittances, build networks, share knowledge online, and some return. Some economists prefer the term **brain circulation**: skills move out and back.
4. **What the state does**: Pakistan registers workers going abroad for employment through the Bureau of Emigration and Overseas Employment and offers schemes aimed at overseas Pakistanis, such as the State Bank's Roshan Digital Account, launched in 2020 for overseas investors. Check what your sources say about how well such schemes work.

## Aspect 3: the positive effects of returnees

This aspect is about people who come **back**, and the wording specifies **positive** effects. Your research needs concrete examples in each of these areas:

- **Money and enterprise**: returning workers invest savings in houses, shops, farms, transport and small businesses, creating local jobs.
- **Skills and technology**: returnees bring trade skills from the Gulf or professional expertise from abroad and pass them on.
- **Institutions**: returning doctors, academics and entrepreneurs may found clinics, schools, firms or research groups, or bring international standards to existing ones.
- **Ideas and attitudes**: experience of other systems can encourage values such as punctuality, regulation, women's education or civic participation; handle this point with care, avoiding any suggestion that everything foreign is better.
- **Philanthropy**: returnees and those abroad fund hospitals, schools and relief efforts in their home districts.

Because the aspect names positive effects, the statement in the exam may invite you to weigh them. Know the limits too: some returnees struggle to readjust, find their qualifications not recognised, or return because of job losses abroad rather than by choice.

## Worked example: plan for a Section C statement

Statement: «بیرونِ ملک جانے والے پاکستانی ملک کا نقصان نہیں بلکہ اثاثہ ہیں۔» (Pakistanis who go abroad are not a loss to the country but an asset.)

Suppose the unseen text describes a doctor who left for the Gulf and a software engineer who returned to Lahore to start a company.

**Plan:**

1. **Position**: partly agree; the balance depends on whether links with Pakistan are kept.
2. **Aspect 1**: why they leave: economic and political reasons from research; link to the doctor in the text.
3. **Aspect 2**: the cost of losing professionals: research evidence about shortages in a sector.
4. **Aspect 3**: the asset: remittances, investment and the returning engineer from the text, plus one research example.
5. **Conclusion**: emigrants become an asset when policies encourage contact, investment and return.

## Worked example: a model paragraph on returnees

> متن میں جس سافٹ ویئر انجینئر کا ذکر ہے، اس کی مثال وطن لوٹنے والوں کے مثبت اثرات کی عکاسی کرتی ہے: اس نے بیرونِ ملک حاصل کیا ہوا تجربہ لاہور میں ایک کمپنی کی صورت میں منتقل کیا اور مقامی نوجوانوں کو روزگار دیا۔ میری تحقیق سے بھی پتا چلتا ہے کہ خلیجی ممالک سے لوٹنے والے بہت سے کارکن اپنی بچت سے دکانیں اور چھوٹے کاروبار شروع کرتے ہیں۔ البتہ یہ اثرات اسی وقت دیرپا ہوتے ہیں جب واپس آنے والوں کو سازگار ماحول ملے؛ ورنہ وہ دوبارہ ملک چھوڑنے پر مجبور ہو جاتے ہیں۔

(The software engineer mentioned in the text illustrates the positive effects of returnees: he transferred experience gained abroad into a company in Lahore and gave local young people jobs. My research also shows that many workers returning from the Gulf countries start shops and small businesses with their savings. However, these effects last only when returnees find a favourable environment; otherwise they are forced to leave the country again.)

**Why it works:** the text is used as evidence, not retold (AO2); a research finding about a different group of returnees is signalled (AO4); **البتہ** introduces a limit, giving analysis; the participle **حاصل کیا ہوا تجربہ** and the semicolon-linked conditional show range (AO3). In your own answer, name the source of your research point.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting Aspect 3 entirely, or writing only about problems when it asks for **positive** effects.
- Describing life in Britain or the Gulf at length instead of the effects on Pakistan.
- Mixing up emigration (**ترکِ وطن**, leaving) and immigration (arriving).
- Quoting remittance or emigration figures without a named, dated source.
- Making political claims about named individuals or parties that you cannot support.

## Exam technique

- Use one paragraph per aspect where possible, because examiners look for knowledge across the research subject.
- Signal each source clearly: **متن کے مطابق** for the unseen text, **میری تحقیق کے مطابق** or a named source for research.
- Answer the statement's key word (here **اثاثہ**, asset) in your conclusion, so the judgement links to your argument.
- Leave three minutes to check agreement in perfective sentences, which carry many of the errors examiners describe as hindering clarity.
