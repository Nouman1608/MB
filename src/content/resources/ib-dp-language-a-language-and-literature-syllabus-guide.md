This guide covers the full syllabus structure of **IB Diploma Programme
Language A: Language and Literature**, first assessment 2021, for both
Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL) students. It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-subject-guide/)
and the
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by setting out what the course actually studies --
three syllabus components, each drawing on both literary works and
non-literary text types.

Total teaching hours are 150 at SL and 240 at HL, split evenly across
three components (50 hours each at SL, 80 hours each at HL).

## Readers, writers and texts (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Introduces close reading and analysis: how choices of language, form and
structure by a writer shape meaning, and how readers actively construct
interpretations rather than simply receiving them. This component
establishes the analytical vocabulary the other two components then
apply. Because this course studies both literary and non-literary
material, this foundational component builds analytical skills across
both -- a newspaper editorial and a poem are approached with the same
underlying question of how form and language choices create meaning,
even though the specific techniques being analysed will differ.

## Time and space (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Examines how texts relate to the context in which they were written and
received -- historical period, culture, and place -- and how the same
text can be read differently across different times and places. For
non-literary texts especially, this component asks students to consider
how the same event or issue might be represented very differently by
different media, institutions or audiences at different moments.

## Intertextuality: connecting texts (50 hours SL / 80 hours HL)

Explores relationships between texts: how one text echoes, responds to
or subverts another, and how meaning is shaped by putting texts into
conversation with each other. This component is where the course's dual
focus is most visible in practice, since a literary work and a
non-literary text type are often studied side by side to see how the
same theme or issue is treated differently across genres.

## Why "language and literature" rather than "literature" alone

The course's name is a deliberate statement of scope: it treats
literature as one important category of text among several, rather than
the exclusive object of study. This reflects a view that the skills of
close reading, contextual awareness and critical analysis developed
through literary study transfer directly to, and are reinforced by,
studying how meaning is made in non-literary communication -- an
advertisement, a political speech and a novel all use deliberate choices
of language and structure to shape how an audience responds, even though
the techniques and conventions available to each differ substantially.

## The defining feature: language and literature together

What distinguishes this course from
[Language A: Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/)
is that all three components above draw on **both** literary works (such
as novels, poetry and drama) **and** non-literary text types (such as
journalism, advertising, speeches and digital media). Paper 1's guided
textual analysis is set on unseen **non-literary** passages specifically,
which is the clearest structural signal of how central non-literary text
study is to this course -- it is not an occasional supplement to
literary study but has its own dedicated external-assessment component.

## How to approach it

Because all three components share the same non-literary-plus-literary
approach, the most useful revision strategy is to build a bank of
non-literary text types you have practised analysing (adverts, opinion
columns, social media posts, speeches) alongside your literary works, so
that Paper 1's unseen non-literary passage does not feel unfamiliar on
exam day. For Paper 2's comparative essay, remember it is set on
**literary works only**, so keep your literary-text preparation and
non-literary-text preparation as two genuinely separate revision tracks
even though the syllabus components themselves blend both. The HL
essay's 1,200-1,500 word coursework can be written on either a literary
work or a non-literary body of work -- decide which route suits your
strengths early, since the two require different critical approaches.

Because the non-literary side of this course spans such a wide range of
text types -- advertising, journalism, digital and social media,
speeches, and more -- it is worth deliberately practising with unfamiliar
text types you have not studied in class, not just the ones your teacher
has covered, since Paper 1 sets genuinely unseen material and a narrow
practice diet leaves gaps. A useful habit is to keep a running list of
the language features specific to each text type you encounter (the
persuasive techniques of an advert, the structural conventions of a news
report, the rhetorical devices of a speech) so that when an unfamiliar
passage appears in the exam, you can quickly identify its type and bring
the right analytical toolkit to it.

For the individual oral, which asks students to connect one literary
work and one non-literary body of work to a common global issue, start
identifying possible pairings well before the assessment itself --
finding a genuine, well-supported connection between a literary text and
a body of non-literary material takes real thought, and rushing this
stage tends to produce oral commentaries that assert a connection
without properly demonstrating it.

Total teaching hours are identical to
[Language A: Literature](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/)
(150 SL / 240 HL, split evenly across the same three named components),
which reflects how closely related the two DP language A courses are in
skill development -- the real difference between them is entirely in
what kind of texts fill that shared structure, not in how much time is
allocated or how the course is organised.
