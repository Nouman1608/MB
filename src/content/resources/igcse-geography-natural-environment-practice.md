## Section A

**1.** Name the three types of plate boundary, and state what happens at each. **[3]**

**2.** Define "epicentre" and "focus" as used to describe an earthquake. **[2]**

**3.** Name two instruments used to collect weather data, and state what each measures. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** Study the description: "A river erodes a wide bend in its course. Over time, the neck of the
bend narrows until the river cuts through it directly, leaving the old bend isolated as a separate,
curved body of water."

**(a)** Identify the landform being described. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the process by which this landform forms, referring to erosion and deposition. **[4]**

**5.** A country lies along a destructive plate boundary, where one tectonic plate is being subducted
beneath another.

**(a)** Explain why this location is associated with both frequent earthquakes and volcanic activity. **[4]**
**(b)** Discuss why living in this location presents both hazards and opportunities, rather than
hazards alone. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why a hot desert climate has a large diurnal (day-to-night) temperature range compared
with an equatorial climate, referring to at least one climatic factor. **[3]**

**7.** A stretch of coastline shows a headland with caves, an arch, and an isolated rock stack a short
distance offshore.

**(a)** Explain the sequence of erosion that produces a stack from an original headland. **[4]**
**(b)** Name the erosive process primarily responsible, and identify the agent of erosion. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why deforestation of a tropical rainforest has effects that extend beyond the local
area where the forest is cleared. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Constructive/divergent — plates move apart [1]. Destructive/convergent — plates move together,
often producing subduction of one plate beneath another [1]. Conservative — plates slide past each
other without significant creation or destruction of crust [1].

**2.** The epicentre is the point on the Earth's surface directly above where the earthquake
originates [1]. The focus is the actual point underground where the earthquake originates [1].

**3.** Any two of: rain gauge (measures rainfall), maximum-minimum thermometer (measures highest and
lowest temperature), wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (measures humidity), anemometer (measures wind
speed), wind vane (measures wind direction), barometer (measures air pressure), sunshine recorder
(measures hours of sunshine) [2].

**4. (a)** An oxbow lake [1].
**(b)** As a river flows around a wide bend (meander), erosion is concentrated on the outer bank where
the current is fastest, while deposition occurs on the inner bank where the current is slower [1–2].
Over time, continued erosion narrows the neck of land between the two sides of the bend, until during
a period of high flow the river cuts directly through the neck, taking the shorter, straighter course
[1]. Deposition then seals off the old, curved section of the channel, isolating it as a separate body
of water — the oxbow lake [1].

**5. (a)** At a destructive boundary, one plate is forced (subducted) beneath the other [1]. This
subduction generates significant friction and stress, which is released as earthquakes [1], while
the subducted plate melts as it descends into the mantle, and the resulting magma rises to the
surface, producing volcanic activity [1–2].
**(b)** Hazards include the direct risk to life and property from earthquakes and volcanic eruptions
[1–2]. Opportunities include fertile volcanic soils that support productive agriculture, geothermal
energy potential, and tourism drawn to volcanic landscapes [1–2] — a complete answer must cover both
sides, since a one-sided answer describing hazards or opportunities alone does not meet what this
sub-topic explicitly requires.

**6.** Hot deserts typically have very low humidity and little cloud cover, so heat escapes rapidly
from the ground at night with little atmospheric moisture to trap it, producing a large diurnal
temperature range [1–2]. Equatorial climates have consistently high humidity and persistent cloud
cover from frequent convectional rainfall, which traps heat at night and keeps the temperature range
between day and night much smaller [1].

**7. (a)** Waves erode weaknesses in a headland, such as joints or cracks, widening them into caves on
either side [1]. Continued erosion eventually breaks through the headland where two caves meet, forming
an arch [1]. Further erosion widens the arch until its roof can no longer support itself and collapses,
leaving an isolated pillar of rock — a stack — separated from the headland [1–2].
**(b)** The process is erosion (specifically hydraulic action and abrasion) [1]; the agent is the sea
(wave action) [1].

**8.** Locally, deforestation removes habitats and increases soil erosion once the protective canopy
and root systems are gone [1]. Beyond the local area, large-scale deforestation reduces the volume of
carbon dioxide absorbed by the forest and can release stored carbon when vegetation is burned or
decays, contributing to global climate change [1–2]; rainforests also influence regional and global
rainfall patterns through the water they release into the atmosphere, so large-scale clearance can
disrupt rainfall well beyond the immediate area cleared [1].

## A note on case-study preparation

None of the questions above require a specific named case study, since a genuine case study needs to
be prepared from real, verified sources rather than invented for a practice set. When revising the
required case studies for this theme (one earthquake, one volcano, a river, a coastline, a rainforest
area and a desert area), practise answering the same style of question above using your own chosen,
real example in place of the generic scenario described — the process explanation stays the same, but
a strong answer adds the location, date, scale and named management response your own case study
supplies.
