Condensed recall for **7.1 The variety of urban environments** (Option G: Urban environments). For
the full explanation and worked examples, read the
[Urban environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-urban-environments-study-guide/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Urbanisation | Growth in the **share** of the population living in towns and cities |
| Urban growth | Increase in the **size** of the urban population |
| Conurbation | Several urban areas merged into one continuous built-up area |
| Megacity | Commonly a city of over 10 million people |
| World (global) city | A city with strong command over finance, trade and culture |
| Primate city | A city far larger than the next largest in the country |

Urbanisation has been fastest in recent decades in Asia and Africa. Many cities in high-income
countries have more established urban systems, and some older industrial areas have declined.

More terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Site | The physical land a settlement is built on (a river crossing, a defensive hill, a natural harbour) |
| Situation | Its location relative to surrounding places, routes and resources |
| Urban function | The main economic or administrative role of a city: port, capital, industrial, financial, tourist |
| Informal sector | Work that is not registered, taxed or regulated, such as street vending |
| Informal settlement | Housing built without legal title or planning permission |

## Variety in examples

- **Megacities:** Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Dhaka, São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Mumbai and Lagos are
  all widely listed. Tokyo's growth has largely stopped, while Lagos and Dhaka are still growing fast.
- **World cities:** London and New York are usually placed at the top of world-city rankings for
  their concentration of finance, corporate headquarters, media and connections.
- **Primate cities:** Bangkok is many times larger than any other Thai city; Lima dominates Peru; Paris
  dominates France. Primacy is often linked to a colonial or centralised political history.
- **Contrasting functions:** Rotterdam (port), Canberra and Brasília (planned capitals), Detroit
  (manufacturing in decline), Dubai (trade, aviation and tourism).

## Models of urban structure

| Model | What it predicts |
|---|---|
| Burgess (concentric zones) | Rings of land use around the central business district (CBD) |
| Hoyt (sector) | Wedge-shaped sectors spreading outward, often along transport routes |
| Harris and Ullman (multiple nuclei) | Several separate centres of activity besides the CBD |

Latin American and other models adapt these ideas to different contexts. For each model, learn what
it predicts **and** where it fails: real cities are shaped by history, topography, planning and
inequality.

More detail on each:

- **Burgess (1925, based on Chicago):** CBD; a zone in transition with older housing and industry;
  working-class housing; better residential areas; and an outer commuter zone. Assumes flat land and
  equal access in all directions.
- **Hoyt (1939):** high-status housing extends in a sector along attractive land or routes, while
  industry and low-cost housing follow railways or rivers.
- **Harris and Ullman (1945):** cities have several nuclei, such as a port, a university or an outer
  business district, reflecting the car age and separate specialised activities.
- **Latin American city model (Griffin and Ford, 1980):** a CBD with a commercial **spine** and
  elite residential sector extending outwards; zones of maturity and of gradual improvement
  ("in situ accretion"); and a ring of peripheral informal settlements. Wealth decreases with
  distance from the centre, the opposite of the Burgess pattern for many US cities.

**Bid-rent theory** explains land values: competition for the most accessible central land means
shops and offices, which can pay most, occupy the CBD, while housing and land-hungry uses locate
further out.

## Urban hierarchy: the rank-size rule

The nth city has a population of 1/n times that of the largest city. If the largest city has 8
million people, the rule predicts 4 million (second), 2.67 million (third) and 2 million (fourth). A
real second city of only 1.5 million would show a **primate** pattern, suggesting investment and
function are concentrated in one place.

**Primacy check (invented figures):** largest city 12 million, second city 1.2 million. The rule
predicts 6 million for the second city, so the actual city is one-fifth of the predicted size, and the
largest city is ten times the second: strongly primate.

## Characteristics worth knowing

- Land values are highest at the CBD and generally fall with distance.
- Density gradients, functional zoning, accessibility and housing quality differ between zones.
- Informal settlements are common in rapidly growing cities in lower-income countries; housing may
  lack secure tenure and services.

**Informal settlement example: Dharavi, Mumbai.** One of Asia's largest informal settlements, very
densely built on a small area near the city centre. It has a large informal economy, including
recycling, leather goods, textiles and pottery, that sells across India and abroad. Redevelopment
plans have been debated for decades; residents worry about losing homes, workshops and the
community networks that support them. Dharavi shows that informal settlements are places of
enterprise as well as of poor sanitation and overcrowding.

UN-Habitat defines a slum household as lacking one or more of: durable housing, sufficient living
space, access to improved water, access to improved sanitation and security of tenure.

## Model paragraph (to what extent)

*"The Burgess model fits parts of Chicago, where it was developed, but it fits Rio de Janeiro poorly:
wealthy districts run along the coast in a sector, as Hoyt would predict, and many favelas occupy
steep hillsides close to rich areas because that land was left unbuilt. Physical geography and
inequality therefore shape Rio more than distance from the CBD."*

## Exam traps

- Using urbanisation and urban growth as synonyms.
- Describing a model without saying where it does not fit.
- Treating informal settlements only as a problem.
- Writing about a generic city rather than a named one.
- Confusing site with situation.
- Assuming every megacity is in a lower-income country, or still growing.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish urbanisation from urban growth.
2. What does Hoyt's model predict?
3. If the largest city has 6 million people, what does the rank-size rule predict for the third?
4. What does a primate city pattern suggest?
5. Where are land values usually highest?
6. Distinguish site from situation.
7. Give two features of the Latin American city model.
8. What does bid-rent theory explain?
9. Name a primate city and its country.
10. Give two characteristics UN-Habitat uses to define a slum household.
11. Why is Dharavi used as an example of enterprise as well as deprivation?

**Answers:** 1. Urbanisation is a rising share of people in urban areas; urban growth is an increase in
the number of urban dwellers. 2. Land uses develop in wedge-shaped sectors outward from the centre,
often along transport routes. 3. 6 / 3 = 2 million. 4. Investment and function concentrated in one
city, with a much smaller second city. 5. At the CBD. 6. Site is the land a settlement is built on;
situation is its location relative to surrounding places. 7. For example a commercial spine with an
elite sector, and peripheral informal settlements. 8. Why the most accessible central land is used by
activities that can pay most. 9. For example Bangkok, Thailand. 10. For example lack of secure tenure
and lack of improved sanitation. 11. It has a large informal economy of recycling and manufacturing.
