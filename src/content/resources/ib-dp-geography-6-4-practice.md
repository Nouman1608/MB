## Section A

**1.** Define the term yield gap. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between land sparing and land sharing. **[2]**

**3.** Outline the difference between the views of Malthus and Boserup on population and food. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how index-based crop insurance works. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a highly efficient food system may be less resilient. **[2]**

**6.** Outline why universal health coverage can improve food security as well as health. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An invented country produces 18 million tonnes of cereal on 9 million hectares. Demand is projected to rise to 27 million tonnes.

**(a)** Calculate the current average yield. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the farmland needed in future if yields do not change. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the yield needed to meet future demand on the current farmland. **[1]**

**(d)** Food loss and waste are cut so that only 24.3 million tonnes must be grown. Calculate the percentage reduction in the demand that must be grown, and the land needed at a yield of 2.7 tonnes per hectare. **[2]**

**8.** A farming region faces more frequent drought.

**(a)** Suggest three features that would make its food system more resilient. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why one of your features may reduce efficiency in normal years. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how climate change may affect the future geography of vector-borne disease, and suggest one response. **[4]**

**10.** Using evidence, explain why the global record since the mid-twentieth century is often said to support Boserup more than Malthus. **[3]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the potential of sustainable intensification to achieve future food security. **[10]**

**12.** "Future threats to health are a greater challenge than future threats to food security." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between the yield farmers actually achieve [1] and the yield possible on the same land with the best available practices, water and inputs [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Land sparing farms a smaller area as intensively as possible and leaves the rest as protected habitat [1]; land sharing farms a larger area in a wildlife-friendly way, with lower yields, so that farming and nature coexist on the same land [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Malthus argued that population tends to grow faster than food supply, so growth is eventually checked by famine or disease [1]; Boserup argued that population pressure stimulates innovation and intensification, raising food production [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It pays out when a measured index, such as rainfall at a weather station, falls below a set level [1], so farmers receive money quickly without each farm's losses having to be inspected [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Efficiency often removes spare capacity, such as stocks, alternative suppliers or extra transport routes [1], so when a shock disrupts one part of the system there is nothing to fall back on [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Without coverage, illness forces households to pay for care, using up savings or selling assets [1], which reduces their ability to buy food, so financial protection supports access to food [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 18 / 9 = **2 tonnes per hectare** [1].

**(b)** 27 / 2 = **13.5 million hectares** [1].

**(c)** 27 / 9 = **3 tonnes per hectare** [1].

**(d)** Reduction = (27 - 24.3) / 27 x 100 = **10%** [1]; land needed = 24.3 / 2.7 = **9 million hectares**, so no expansion is required [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Any three, for example: diversity of crops and drought-tolerant varieties [1]; household or community grain reserves [1]; water storage and efficient irrigation [1]; safety-net payments in drought years [1]; index-based crop insurance [1]; diverse non-farm incomes [1]. Maximum [3].

**(b)** For example, grain reserves tie up food and money and need storage and management [1], so in normal years they cost more than relying on buying food when needed [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Warming may allow vectors such as mosquitoes to survive at higher altitudes and latitudes [1], so diseases such as malaria or dengue could appear in areas where people have little immunity and health services have little experience [1]. Changing rainfall may lengthen or shift transmission seasons [1]. Response: extend surveillance and vector control, such as drainage and bed nets, to newly at-risk areas [1]. **[4]**

**10.** World food output has grown faster than population over this period [1], largely through higher yields from new varieties, fertiliser and irrigation, as in the Green Revolution [1], showing innovation responding to rising demand, as Boserup predicted [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Definition:** raising yields on existing farmland without further environmental damage or land clearance [1].
- **Potential:** large yield gaps among smallholders mean big gains are possible [1]; avoids clearing forest, supporting land sparing [1].
- **Methods:** improved varieties, precision fertiliser, efficient irrigation and soil conservation [1].
- **Limits on access:** gains depend on credit, advice and affordable inputs reaching small farmers [1].
- **Environmental risks:** intensification can still pollute water or deplete groundwater, as in Punjab after the Green Revolution [1].
- **Rebound risk:** higher profits may encourage more land clearance [1].
- **Other dimensions:** sustainable intensification raises availability but not necessarily access, utilisation or stability [1].
- **Complements:** waste reduction, diet change and safety nets [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, it has great potential for availability but achieves food security only with policies on access and environmental protection [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Health threats:** emerging zoonoses and pandemics [1]; antimicrobial resistance making common infections harder to treat [1]; ageing and non-communicable disease raising costs [1].
- **Food threats:** climate change affecting yields and water [1]; soil degradation and biodiversity loss [1]; price volatility and conflict, as shown in 2022 [1].
- **Links:** poor nutrition worsens infection; illness reduces nutrient absorption and income [1]; One Health links human, animal and environmental health [1].
- **Uneven geography:** both threats fall hardest on low-income, import-dependent or conflict-affected places [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, the threats are interlinked, so treating either as greater oversimplifies; a supported position on which is harder to manage, with reasons [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "discuss" rewards answers that show the links between food and health threats and reach a reasoned conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing area by production instead of production by area when calculating yield.
- Treating technology as automatically sustainable.
- Presenting Malthus as wrong and Boserup as right without qualification.
- Discussing food or health futures without considering uncertainty.
