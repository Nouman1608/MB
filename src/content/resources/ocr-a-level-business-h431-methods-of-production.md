## The four methods

| Method | What it is | Typical products | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Job** | One-off items made to a customer's order, often by skilled workers | Bespoke suits, a bridge, a wedding cake, a specialist machine | High flexibility; high quality and customisation; premium price | High cost per unit; slow; needs skilled labour; hard to plan |
| **Batch** | A group of identical items made together, then the process is changed for the next batch | Bread, paint, clothing ranges, pharmaceuticals | Some flexibility; lower cost than job; can vary products | Time lost in changeovers; stock builds between stages; scheduling is complex |
| **Flow** | Continuous production of standardised items along a line, often automated | Cars, bottled drinks, chemicals, electronics | Lowest unit cost at high volume; consistent quality; fast | Very high set-up cost; inflexible; breakdown stops everything; boring work |
| **Cell** | Production split into self-contained teams (cells), each making a complete product or family of similar products | Furniture components, electronic assemblies | Teamwork and multi-skilled staff; more flexibility than a line; less work in progress | Needs training and layout changes; may be less efficient than flow at very high volume |

Cell production is often found in **lean** systems, where each cell has the tools and staff to complete its task without moving work between departments.

## Choosing a method

Factors in the choice:

- **Volume and variety of demand:** high volume of a standard product suits flow; low volume and high variety suits job or batch.
- **Customer requirements:** bespoke goods require job production.
- **Capital available:** flow requires heavy investment.
- **Cost per unit** at expected output.
- **Skills and labour available.**
- **Flexibility needed** if demand or fashion changes quickly.
- **Quality and speed.**
- **Technology** that makes automated flow more affordable.

### Worked example 1: unit cost at different volumes

Fenwick Cabinets could make a standard storage unit by three methods. Each has a fixed cost per year and a variable cost per unit.

| | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost (£) | 20,000 | 60,000 | 300,000 |
| Variable cost per unit (£) | 45 | 32 | 18 |

Unit cost = (fixed cost + variable cost per unit × output) ÷ output.

| Output | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000 units | (20,000 + 90,000) ÷ 2,000 = **£55.00** | (60,000 + 64,000) ÷ 2,000 = **£62.00** | (300,000 + 36,000) ÷ 2,000 = **£168.00** |
| 10,000 units | (20,000 + 450,000) ÷ 10,000 = **£47.00** | (60,000 + 320,000) ÷ 10,000 = **£38.00** | (300,000 + 180,000) ÷ 10,000 = **£48.00** |
| 50,000 units | (20,000 + 2,250,000) ÷ 50,000 = **£45.40** | (60,000 + 1,600,000) ÷ 50,000 = **£33.20** | (300,000 + 900,000) ÷ 50,000 = **£24.00** |

**Where does each become cheapest?** Set the total costs equal.

- Job and batch: 20,000 + 45q = 60,000 + 32q, so 13q = 40,000 and q = **3,077 units**. Below this output job is cheaper; above it, batch.
- Batch and flow: 60,000 + 32q = 300,000 + 18q, so 14q = 240,000 and q = **17,143 units**. Above this output, flow is the cheapest.

**Interpretation.** At 2,000 units, job production is cheapest (£55). At 10,000 units, batch is cheapest (£38). At 50,000 units, flow is cheapest (£24). The best method depends on the volume. Fenwick should also consider that flow needs £300,000 of fixed spending a year, which is a serious risk if demand falls: at 10,000 units it is the most expensive of the three (£48.00). Costs are not the only factor: a customer wanting a custom size needs job production regardless.

## Impact and importance of the choice

The choice affects:

- **Costs and prices** (as above).
- **Quality and consistency**: flow gives consistency; job gives customisation.
- **Flexibility and lead time**: how fast the business can change products.
- **Staff**: job production needs skilled workers, flow may use machine-minders and fewer staff, cell uses multi-skilled teams.
- **Customers**: tailored products or low prices.
- **Suppliers**: regular large orders for flow, irregular for job.
- **The business's strategy and image**: a luxury brand may rely on job production as a mark of quality.

A wrong choice is costly: a business that invests in flow for a product that does not sell is stuck with high fixed costs, while one that uses job production for a mass-market product is undercut by competitors.

## Specialisation and division of labour

**Specialisation** means that people, machines or businesses concentrate on a limited set of tasks. **Division of labour** is the splitting of the production process into separate tasks, each done by a different worker or group. Reasons for introducing them:

- workers become faster and more accurate at one task through repetition;
- less time is lost moving between tasks and tools;
- less training is needed per worker;
- machinery can be used for specific tasks;
- output rises and cost per unit falls.

### Worked example 2: dividing the work

Fenwick makes drawer units. Each unit needs four tasks: cutting (6 minutes), sewing the drawer lining (14 minutes), finishing (8 minutes) and packing (4 minutes), a total of **32 minutes**. Eight workers work 480 minutes a day and are paid **£96** a day each.

**Before: each worker makes whole units.**

1. Output per worker = 480 ÷ 32 = **15** units a day.
2. Total output = 8 × 15 = **120** units a day.
3. Labour cost per unit = (8 × £96) ÷ 120 = £768 ÷ 120 = **£6.40**.

**After: workers specialise.** Repetition makes each task 25% quicker, so the total time falls from 32 to **24 minutes** (cutting 4.5, sewing 10.5, finishing 6, packing 3). If the tasks could be perfectly balanced across the eight workers, total output would be 8 × 480 ÷ 24 = **160** units. In practice some workers wait for others, and the line is 90% efficient.

4. Realistic output = 160 × 0.90 = **144** units a day, an increase of **20%** (144 ÷ 120 = 1.20).
5. Labour cost per unit = £768 ÷ 144 = **£5.33**, a fall of £1.07 (16.7%).

**Costs and disadvantages.** Repetitive work can cause boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes and absence. If one worker is absent, the line may stop. Workers are less able to cover other tasks, so the business is less flexible. Training in the specialised task is needed, and workers may have less pay progression if skills are narrow. Many businesses respond by rotating tasks or by using cell production, in which workers share a wider range of tasks.

**Importance.** For the business, the gain in output and the cost saving are valuable. For employees, specialisation can give security in a skill but may reduce job satisfaction. For customers, lower costs may mean lower prices. The benefit depends on the volume of demand: specialisation only pays if the extra output can be sold.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a method without saying when it is suitable.
- Saying that flow production is best. It is cheapest only at high volumes.
- Confusing batch with flow: batch has stoppages for changeovers.
- Forgetting the disadvantages of specialisation for workers and flexibility.
- Calculating unit cost without including the fixed cost.

## Exam technique

Match the method to the product and volume in the case, use figures when given, state one advantage and one disadvantage, and end with a judgement about the best choice and why.
