Evaluation is the skill of judging how far a set of results, and the method that produced them, can be trusted. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 1.1.4 you must be able to evaluate results and draw conclusions, identify anomalies in experimental measurements, identify limitations in experimental procedures, discuss the precision and accuracy of measurements and data (including margins of error, percentage errors and uncertainties in apparatus), and refine an experimental design by suggesting improvements to the procedures and apparatus. Evaluation answers earn marks when they are specific: they name a weakness, say what effect it had on the results, and give a precise improvement.

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.1.4(a) to (e) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the module-wide [planning, implementing, analysing and evaluating study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-planning-implementing-analysing-and-evaluating/). Use the [evaluation revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-evaluation-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [evaluation practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-evaluation-practice/) to test yourself.

## Evaluating results and conclusions (a)

Ask three questions of any set of results.

1. **Do the data support the conclusion?** Quote values: "the mean rate rose from 4.2 to 16.2 cm³ min⁻¹ between 20 and 40 °C".
2. **How strong is the evidence?** Consider the number of repeats, the spread of the repeats, the range of the independent variable, and any statistical test.
3. **What else could explain the result?** Name an uncontrolled variable and say how it could have changed the data.

A conclusion is limited to the conditions tested. Data between 20 and 60 °C say nothing about 80 °C.

## Anomalies (b)

An **anomaly** is a measurement that does not fit the pattern shown by the others. To deal with one:

1. **Identify it** by comparing repeats. Three repeats of 8.0, 8.5 and 12.5 cm³ show 12.5 as anomalous because it is far from the other two.
2. **Suggest a cause**, such as an air bubble in the syringe, a timing error, a different piece of tissue, or contamination.
3. **Decide what to do.** Repeat the measurement if you can. If you must calculate a mean, say that you have left the anomaly out.
4. **Show the effect.** With the anomaly the mean is (8.0 + 8.5 + 12.5) ÷ 3 = 9.7 cm³. Without it the mean is (8.0 + 8.5) ÷ 2 = **8.25 cm³**.

Do not remove a result only because it spoils the pattern. An anomaly is identified by comparison with the repeats and the trend, and the decision is explained.

## Limitations of procedures (c)

A limitation is a feature of the method that reduces how far the results can be trusted. Common limitations, and their effects, are:

| Limitation | Effect on results |
|---|---|
| Temperature of the water bath drifts | The rate is measured at the wrong temperature, so the trend is distorted |
| Few repeats | Mean is unreliable and anomalies cannot be spotted |
| Only three values of the independent variable | The optimum cannot be located |
| End point judged by eye | Different judgments give different times (subjective) |
| Pieces of tissue of different size | Differences in surface area or mass affect the rate |
| Time between taking tubes from a bath and reading | The temperature changes before the reading |

When you write a limitation, finish it with "so...": "the end point is judged by eye, so the time at which the colour disappears varies between repeats".

## Precision, accuracy and uncertainty (d)

**Precision** is how close repeated measurements are to each other. It is shown by a small spread or a small standard deviation.

**Accuracy** is how close a measurement is to the true value.

A thermometer that always reads 2 °C too high gives precise but inaccurate readings. A set of readings scattered either side of the true value is accurate on average but imprecise.

- A **random error** makes readings scatter unpredictably. Repeating and taking a mean reduces its effect.
- A **systematic error** shifts every reading the same way. Repeating does not remove it. It needs a better method or calibration.

**Uncertainty in apparatus.** Every instrument has an uncertainty. A common estimate is half the smallest division, so a balance reading to 0.01 g has an uncertainty of ±0.005 g, and a syringe read to 0.1 cm³ has an uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³.

**Percentage error.** The specification gives the formula

> percentage error (uncertainty) = 2 × absolute uncertainty ÷ quantity measured × 100 %

**Worked example 1.** A syringe is read to the nearest 0.1 cm³ and measures 4.0 cm³.

Absolute uncertainty = ±0.05 cm³. Percentage error = 2 × 0.05 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **2.5 %**.

**Worked example 2.** A balance reading to 0.01 g measures a mass of 2.40 g.

Percentage error = 2 × 0.005 ÷ 2.40 × 100 = **0.42 %**.

The same instrument gives a larger percentage error for a smaller quantity. A 10.0 cm³ measuring cylinder with 0.5 cm³ divisions has an uncertainty of ±0.25 cm³, and that matters much more when measuring 1.0 cm³ than 10.0 cm³.

**Combining uncertainties.** When a result is found from a difference between two readings, the uncertainties add. A potato cylinder goes from 3.10 g to 2.79 g. Each reading has an uncertainty of ±0.005 g. The mass change is 0.31 g, with an uncertainty of ±0.01 g.

Percentage error in the change = 0.01 ÷ 0.31 × 100 = **3.2 %**.

This is much larger than the 0.4 % error in either mass, because the quantity measured, the difference, is small. This is why a small change measured by a balance gives a large percentage error, and a larger piece of tissue or a bigger change reduces it.

**Margin of error.** A result can be written as mean ± uncertainty, such as 8.5 ± 0.5 cm³. Two means whose ranges overlap may not truly differ. A statistical test is needed to decide.

**Improving precision.** Use apparatus with a smaller uncertainty (a syringe in place of a measuring cylinder), measure a larger quantity, and repeat. **Improving accuracy** needs calibration and the removal of systematic errors.

## Refining the design (e)

An improvement is useful only if it is specific and linked to a weakness. Use this pattern: **weakness, effect, improvement**.

| Weakness | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Water bath drifts by several °C | Temperature is not controlled | Use a thermostatically controlled water bath, check with a digital thermometer |
| Gas collected in a measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions | Uncertainty is large for small volumes | Use a gas syringe with 0.1 cm³ divisions |
| Only 20, 40 and 60 °C tested | Optimum cannot be identified | Test every 5 or 10 °C between 20 and 60 °C |
| Reading the end point by eye | Subjective, imprecise | Use a colorimeter to measure absorbance |
| One result at each value | No mean, anomalies unseen | Repeat three times and calculate a mean |
| Pieces of liver of different size | Different enzyme amount and surface area | Cut pieces to the same mass or size |

Avoid vague improvements: "be more careful", "use more accurate equipment", "do it again".

## Worked example 3: evaluating a result

A student's means from an enzyme investigation at four temperatures are 4.2, 8.2, 16.2 and 10.7 cm³ min⁻¹ at 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. Three repeats were taken at each, and the standard deviations were 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.3 cm³ min⁻¹.

- **Conclusion.** The rate increases from 20 to 40 °C and falls at 50 °C, which suggests that the optimum is near 40 °C. The fall at 50 °C is consistent with denaturation.
- **Reliability.** The standard deviations are small compared with the differences between the means, so the repeats are precise and the differences are likely to be real.
- **Limitation.** The temperatures are 10 °C apart, so the optimum could lie anywhere between 30 and 50 °C.
- **Improvement.** Test at 35, 40, 45 °C as well, using a thermostatically controlled water bath.

## Exam technique

- **Name** the weakness, give its **effect**, and offer a **specific improvement**.
- **Quote** data and calculate when the question gives values.
- Use **precision** for agreement between repeats and **accuracy** for closeness to the true value.
- **Show the formula** for percentage error and give the unit or a percentage.
- Answer the question asked: "limitations" and "improvements" are different marks.

## Common mistakes

- Using "reliable" or "accurate" without saying what is meant.
- Describing human error ("the student made a mistake") rather than a weakness in the method.
- Suggesting more repeats as the only improvement.
- Removing an anomaly without giving a reason.
- Forgetting that a difference between two readings has a larger percentage error than either reading.
- Writing an improvement that is not linked to a weakness.
