## The three outcomes at a glance

| Ref | Outcome | One-line summary |
|---|---|---|
| 3.3.1 | Concept and calculation of revenue, fixed and variable costs, total costs, profit and loss | Revenue − total costs = profit or loss |
| 3.3.2 | Concept of break-even and calculation of break-even level of output, from formula or diagram | Output where revenue = total costs |
| 3.3.3 | Interpret break-even charts: impact of changes in revenue and costs; limitations | Lines move, break-even moves; the model has weaknesses |

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Money received from sales: price per unit × quantity sold |
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance) |
| Variable costs | Costs that change in direct proportion to output (materials, packaging) |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + total variable costs |
| Profit | Revenue − total costs, when the answer is positive |
| Loss | Revenue − total costs, when the answer is negative |
| Break-even level of output | Output at which revenue equals total costs, so profit is zero |
| Contribution per unit | Selling price per unit − variable cost per unit (standard form; not given in the specification) |

## Formulas

| What you want | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | price × quantity |
| Total variable cost | variable cost per unit × quantity |
| Total costs | fixed costs + total variable costs |
| Profit or loss | revenue − total costs |
| Break-even output | fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Profit above break-even | (output − break-even output) × contribution per unit |
| Break-even revenue | break-even output × price |

The specification does not give a break-even formula, so the standard form above is what you learn. Rounding break-even output up to a whole unit is the writer's own advice, not specification text.

## Method in steps: calculating break-even

1. Write down price per unit, variable cost per unit and fixed costs.
2. Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit.
3. Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
4. Round up to a whole unit if needed and state the unit (bars, cups, repairs).
5. If asked, find break-even revenue or profit at a given output.
6. Check: at that output, does revenue equal total costs?

### Worked reminder

A workshop sells wooden stools at £30. Variable cost is £18 a stool. Fixed costs are £6,600 a month.

- Contribution = 30 − 18 = £12.
- Break-even = 6,600 ÷ 12 = **550 stools**. Break-even revenue = 550 × 30 = £16,500.
- If variable cost rises to £20, contribution is £10 and break-even is 660.
- If fixed costs rise to £7,800, break-even is 7,800 ÷ 12 = 650.
- If price rises to £32, contribution is £14 and break-even is 6,600 ÷ 14 = 471.4, so **472 stools**.

## Method in steps: reading a break-even chart

1. Find the crossing of the revenue line and the total costs line.
2. Read down to the horizontal axis for break-even output.
3. Read across to the vertical axis for break-even revenue (equal to total costs there).
4. Read the fixed costs from where the total costs line meets the vertical axis.
5. At any output, profit or loss is the vertical gap between the revenue and total costs lines. Where the revenue line is below the total costs line the gap is a loss; where it is above, the gap is a profit.
6. Variable cost per unit is the rise in total costs per unit of output (divide the rise in total costs by the rise in output).

### What each line looks like

| Line | Where it starts | Slope |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | At the fixed cost figure | Flat |
| Total costs | At the fixed cost figure | Rises by the variable cost per unit |
| Revenue | At zero | Rises by the price per unit |

## Impact of changes

| Change | Line that moves | Break-even output |
|---|---|---|
| Price up | Revenue line steeper | Falls |
| Price down | Revenue line flatter | Rises |
| Variable cost per unit up | Total costs line steeper | Rises |
| Variable cost per unit down | Total costs line flatter | Falls |
| Fixed costs up | Total costs line starts higher | Rises |
| Fixed costs down | Total costs line starts lower | Falls |

A change in sales volume does not move the lines. It moves the business along them, to a different profit or loss.

A lower break-even output is not automatically better. A price rise may lower break-even output but cut the number of customers.

## Limitations of break-even charts

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Straight-line revenue | Discounts and special offers change the price per unit |
| Constant variable cost per unit | Bulk buying or overtime pay changes it |
| Fixed costs stay fixed | A bigger site or extra machine adds a step |
| All output is sold | Unsold stock is a cost, so profit is overstated |
| Based on forecasts | Costs and prices change |
| Does not show demand | A break-even figure says nothing about whether customers will buy it |
| One product | A business selling many products needs more assumptions |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Variable cost per unit versus total variable cost.** The first stays constant; the second grows with output.
- **Contribution versus profit.** Contribution is per unit, before fixed costs. Profit is after all costs.
- **Break-even output versus break-even revenue.** One is units, the other is money.
- **Moving along a line versus shifting a line.** Sales volume moves you along; price and cost changes shift or tilt lines.
- **Loss versus low profit.** Below break-even is a loss. Above it, any gap is profit.

## Quick self-test

1. Define revenue.
2. A business has revenue of £56,000 and total costs of £61,250. State its profit or loss.
3. A firm sells 250 units at £14.40. Calculate revenue.
4. Variable cost is £6.25 a unit. Calculate total variable cost for 250 units.
5. Fixed costs are £3,000 and total variable costs are £4,800. Calculate total costs.
6. Price £20, variable cost £8, fixed costs £15,000. Calculate break-even output.
7. Using the same figures, calculate profit when 1,500 units are sold.
8. Fixed costs rise to £18,000. Calculate the new break-even output.
9. Fixed costs are £2,800 and variable cost is £9 a unit. What price gives break-even at 400 units?
10. State where fixed costs can be read from on a break-even chart.
11. Outline one reason why a break-even chart may overstate profit.
12. Explain why a rise in variable cost per unit raises break-even output.

### Answers

1. The money a business receives from selling its output (price × quantity sold).
2. 56,000 − 61,250 = a **loss of £5,250**.
3. 250 × 14.40 = **£3,600**.
4. 250 × 6.25 = **£1,562.50**.
5. 3,000 + 4,800 = **£7,800**.
6. Contribution = 12. 15,000 ÷ 12 = **1,250 units**.
7. 250 units above break-even × £12 = **£3,000**. Check: revenue 30,000, total costs 15,000 + 12,000 = 27,000, profit 3,000.
8. 18,000 ÷ 12 = **1,500 units**.
9. Fixed cost per unit at break-even = 2,800 ÷ 400 = 7. Contribution must be 7, so price = 9 + 7 = **£16**.
10. At the vertical axis: the total costs line starts at the fixed cost figure when output is zero (the flat fixed costs line is at the same height).
11. The chart assumes all output is sold. Unsold stock has still been paid for, so the profit shown is too high.
12. Each unit now leaves less contribution towards fixed costs, so more units must be sold to cover them. The total costs line is steeper, so it meets the revenue line later.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the selling price instead of the contribution per unit as the divisor.
- Leaving the answer as 466.7 units rather than a whole unit.
- Mixing up variable cost per unit and total variable cost in a total costs sum.
- Starting the total costs line at zero on a plotted chart.
- Writing "profit" when the answer is negative: say loss.
- Missing units or the pound sign on an answer.
- Giving a limitation as a list word ("assumptions") with no link to the business.
- Writing that a lower break-even always means more profit.
- Using two points in Explain answers: the command word asks for a statement and two expansion points.
- Skipping the workings, when the Calculate command word asks for them.
