## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget | A plan for a future period, in money and/or quantities |
| Budgetary control | Comparing actual results with budget, investigating variances and taking action |
| Budget holder | The manager responsible for a budget |
| Principal budget factor | The factor that limits activity (often sales demand); its budget is prepared first |
| Master budget | The budgeted income statement and statement of financial position (often with the cash budget), built from all the other budgets |
| Variance | Actual figure minus budgeted figure |
| Favourable (F) | The difference increases profit: more revenue or less cost than budget |
| Adverse (A) | The difference reduces profit: less revenue or more cost than budget |
| Flexed budget | The budget recalculated at actual output: variable items change, fixed costs do not |
| Incremental budgeting | Last period's figures adjusted for expected changes |
| Zero-based budgeting (ZBB) | Every activity justified from zero each period |

## Purpose of budgeting (3.9)

Planning, co-ordination, communication, control, motivation, clear responsibility and performance
evaluation. Learn one sentence of explanation for each, not just the word.

## Order of preparation

1. Sales budget (units and £)
2. Production budget (units)
3. Materials usage and purchases budget (quantities and £)
4. Labour budget (hours and £)
5. Cash budget
6. Budgeted income statement
7. Budgeted statement of financial position

## Formulas

| Budget | Formula |
|---|---|
| Sales | Units × selling price |
| Production (units) | Sales + closing finished goods − opening finished goods |
| Materials usage | Production × quantity per unit |
| Materials purchases (quantity) | Usage + closing raw materials − opening raw materials |
| Materials purchases (£) | Purchase quantity × price per unit of material |
| Labour hours | Production × hours per unit |
| Labour cost | Hours × rate (overtime hours × premium rate where hours exceed capacity) |
| Closing bank balance | Opening balance + receipts − payments |
| Flexed variable cost | Actual output × budgeted cost per unit |
| Volume effect on profit | Flexed budget profit − fixed budget profit |

## Method in steps: cash budget

1. Build a **receipts schedule**: cash sales in the month of sale; credit sales in the month(s)
   received, net of any discount allowed and excluding expected irrecoverable debts.
2. Build a **payments schedule** on the same timing logic for purchases and expenses.
3. Add capital items: loans, capital introduced, asset purchases, drawings, dividends.
4. Leave out non-cash items: depreciation, irrecoverable debts, provisions, accruals.
5. Opening balance + receipts − payments = closing balance; carry it forward.
6. Show overdrafts in brackets.

Worked reminder: a business makes 20% of sales for cash. Credit customers pay half in the month after
sale and half two months after. April sales £28 000, March £25 000, February £22 000.
April receipts = 20% × 28 000 + 80% × 25 000 × 50% + 80% × 22 000 × 50% = 5 600 + 10 000 + 8 800 =
**£24 400**.

## Method in steps: budgeted statements

1. Revenue and expenses on the **accruals** basis for the period, not cash amounts.
2. Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory.
3. Add depreciation (time-apportioned for a part year) and adjust for accruals and prepayments.
4. Closing trade receivables = opening + credit sales − receipts; trade payables likewise.
5. Bank balance comes from the cash budget.
6. Check: closing capital = opening capital + profit − drawings = net assets.

## Method in steps: variances

1. Flex the budget to actual output (revenue and variable costs only).
2. Variance for each line = actual − flexed.
3. Label each F or A by its effect on profit, never by its sign alone.
4. Volume variance = flexed profit − fixed budget profit.
5. Reconcile: budgeted profit ± volume variance ± flexed variances = actual profit.
6. Interpret: give a likely cause, say whether it is controllable, and note links between variances
   (a cheaper material may cause more wastage; a price cut may raise volume).

## Must-know distinctions

| | Cash budget | Budgeted income statement |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Cash received and paid | Accruals |
| Depreciation | Excluded | Included |
| Loan received, asset bought, drawings | Included | Excluded (loan interest is included) |
| Result | Bank balance | Profit or loss |

| | Incremental | Zero-based |
|---|---|---|
| Start point | Last period's figures | Zero |
| Cost and time | Low | High |
| Waste | Carried forward | Challenged |
| Best for | Stable, routine costs | Discretionary costs; periodic reviews |

| | Fixed budget comparison | Flexed budget comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Output basis | Planned output | Actual output |
| Strength | Simple | Separates the volume effect from price and efficiency |
| Weakness | Mixes volume with efficiency | Needs costs split into fixed and variable |

## Benefits and limitations in brief

- **Benefits:** forward planning; co-ordinated departments; early warning of cash shortages;
  targets for motivation; a benchmark for control.
- **Limitations:** forecasts may be wrong; time and cost; rigidity; budgetary slack; "spend it or
  lose it"; demotivation if imposed; departmental goals placed above the business's goals.
- **Specific budgets:** each one's benefit is the planning it allows; each one's limitation is the
  uncertainty in its own assumption. Errors in the sales budget flow into every other budget.

| Budget | Main benefit | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Sets the activity level for the whole business | Demand forecasts are the least certain figure |
| Production | Plans capacity and finished goods inventory | Volatile demand makes it go out of date quickly |
| Purchases | Avoids stock-outs and excess raw materials | Supplier prices and lead times may change |
| Labour | Shows overtime or recruitment needs in time | Absence and productivity changes are hard to predict |
| Cash | Warns of shortages so finance can be arranged | Customers may not pay when assumed |
| Budgeted income statement and statement of financial position | Test whether the plans give acceptable profit and liquidity | Only as reliable as every budget beneath them |

## Answering "evaluate" questions

The specification's skills annex asks you to "prepare, analyse and evaluate budgets", so be ready to
judge budgeting as well as calculate it. A strong answer:

1. Uses the business in the question (its size, its managers, its figures), not generic points.
2. Gives benefits and limitations, each explained with a consequence ("so managers can...", "which
   means...").
3. Weighs them: which matters more for this business, and why?
4. Ends with a clear judgement, ideally with a condition ("ZBB is worth it for the marketing budget
   but not for rent and insurance").

Worked reminder: a small firm with one owner-manager and stable costs gains little from ZBB, because
the owner already knows every cost and the review time is better spent selling. A large firm with
many departments and rising discretionary spending gains more, because waste is harder to see.

## Small worked reminder: flexing

Budget: 1 000 units, variable cost £5 per unit, fixed cost £3 000. Actual: 1 200 units, total cost
£9 400. Fixed budget cost £8 000, so the raw comparison shows £1 400 adverse. Flexed cost is
1 200 × 5 + 3 000 = £9 000, so the true cost variance is only **£400 adverse**. The other £1 000
is simply the cost of making 200 more units.

## Quick self-test

1. Sales 800 units; opening finished goods 120 units; closing 150 units. Production?
2. Production 1 000 units at 3 kg each; opening raw materials 400 kg; closing 550 kg. Purchases in kg?
3. March credit sales £10 000; 70% pay in April and take a 2% discount. April receipt from March
   sales?
4. Which belongs in a cash budget: depreciation, drawings or a provision for doubtful debts?
5. Budgeted materials £4 per unit. Actual output 5 400 units; actual materials £22 140. Variance
   against the flexed budget?
6. Budgeted revenue £48 000; actual £50 000. Variance?
7. Define zero-based budgeting.
8. What is the principal budget factor, and why does it matter?
9. Labour budget: 600 units at 0.75 hours each, £16 per hour. Cost?
10. Opening overdraft £2 000; receipts £15 000; payments £14 200. Closing balance?

### Answers

1. 800 + 150 − 120 = **830 units**.
2. 3 000 + 550 − 400 = **3 150 kg**.
3. 10 000 × 70% × 98% = **£6 860**.
4. **Drawings** (a cash outflow). The other two involve no cash.
5. Flexed 5 400 × 4 = £21 600; 22 140 − 21 600 = **£540 adverse**.
6. **£2 000 favourable**.
7. Each budget starts from zero, and every activity and its cost must be justified afresh.
8. The factor that limits activity (often sales demand); its budget is set first because it
   determines every other budget.
9. 600 × 0.75 × 16 = **£7 200**.
10. −2 000 + 15 000 − 14 200 = **£1 200 overdrawn**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Including depreciation, irrecoverable debts or provisions in a cash budget.
- Leaving out loans received, asset purchases or drawings from a cash budget.
- Receiving credit sales in the month of sale, or ignoring discount allowed.
- Using this month's sales instead of next month's to set closing inventory.
- Forgetting that opening inventory is last month's closing inventory.
- Costing purchases on usage instead of purchase quantity.
- Calling a cost under budget "adverse", or giving a variance with no F or A.
- Comparing with the fixed budget when actual output differs.
- Listing benefits of ZBB without evaluating them against its cost and time.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you calculate the production budget?**
Production = budgeted sales + closing inventory − opening inventory. Each month's closing inventory becomes the next month's opening inventory.

**What is the difference between a favourable and an adverse variance?**
A favourable variance means actual profit is higher than budget, from revenue above budget or a cost below it. An adverse variance means actual profit is lower, from revenue below budget or a cost above it.

**What is budgetary slack?**
Budgetary slack is when managers pad their cost budgets to make targets easier to meet. It is one of the limitations of budgeting, alongside reliance on forecasts and the time and cost of preparation.

**Why is the budgeted income statement different from the cash budget?**
The budgeted income statement uses the accruals basis, so it includes non-cash items such as depreciation and records income and expenses when earned or incurred. The cash budget shows only cash received and paid.
