This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.4.8 Control of heart rate**, in Unit 4, Control. Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only.

The section answers two questions. First, how does the heart beat on its own, in the right order? Second, how does the body change the rate to match its needs? The answer to the second question is a negative feedback loop with receptors, the autonomic nervous system and effectors, so it connects directly to homeostasis. ## Myogenic stimulation

Cardiac muscle is **myogenic**, which means that it contracts and relaxes without nervous or hormonal stimulation. The beat is initiated within the heart itself. Nerves and hormones change the rate, but they do not start each beat.

### The sequence of events in one heartbeat

1. The **sinoatrial node (SAN)**, a small patch of specialised muscle cells in the wall of the right atrium, initiates a wave of electrical activity at regular intervals. For this reason it is called the pacemaker.
2. The wave spreads across the walls of both **atria**, causing them to contract together (atrial systole). Blood is forced into the ventricles.
3. A layer of connective tissue between the atria and the ventricles does not conduct the wave. The wave cannot pass directly to the ventricles.
4. The wave reaches the **atrioventricular node (AVN)**, which is the only route to the ventricles. The AVN delays the wave briefly.
5. The wave passes from the AVN down the **bundle of His**, a group of conducting fibres that runs down the septum between the ventricles.
6. At the base of the heart, the bundle divides into **Purkinje fibres (Purkinje tissue)** which carry the wave up the walls of both ventricles from the bottom.
7. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards (ventricular systole), forcing blood up and out into the arteries.

### Why the delay and the route matter

- The **delay at the AVN** gives the atria time to finish contracting and the ventricles time to fill with blood before the ventricles contract.
- The **non-conducting layer** ensures that the ventricles contract only after the wave has travelled through the AVN, so atria and ventricles do not contract together.
- The wave travels quickly along the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres so that the walls of both ventricles contract at almost the same time, and contraction **from the base upwards** pushes blood towards the arteries, not trapped at the bottom.

**Worked example 1: heart rate from an ECG.** An electrocardiogram (ECG) trace shows the electrical activity of the heart. The gap between the peaks of successive beats (the R-R interval) is 0.8 seconds.

1. Beats per minute = 60 / interval = 60 / 0.8.
2. Heart rate = **75 beats per minute**.
3. If the interval fell to 0.5 s, the rate would be 60 / 0.5 = 120 beats per minute.

**Worked example 2: heart rate from a count.** Five peaks are counted in a 4.0 s recording. The rate is 5 per 4.0 s, so per minute it is 5 x (60 / 4.0) = 75 beats per minute.

## Controlling the rate

The heart rate changes to meet the body's needs. At rest, it is lower; in exercise, it is higher. The change is controlled by the **medulla oblongata**, a region of the brain, which has a centre for cardiac control. The centre receives information from receptors and sends signals through the **autonomic nervous system** (which is involuntary and has two parts) to the heart, where the SAN is the effector that changes its rate.

### Receptors

- **Chemoreceptors** in the walls of the carotid arteries and the aorta detect changes in the chemical composition of the blood, mainly the concentration of carbon dioxide, which affects pH. A rise in carbon dioxide concentration lowers the pH of the blood. For this section, the stimulus to learn is the fall in pH caused by carbon dioxide.
- **Pressure receptors (baroreceptors)** in the walls of the carotid arteries and the aorta detect changes in blood pressure.

### The two nerves

- The **sympathetic nerve** carries impulses to the SAN that **increase** heart rate. It releases the transmitter noradrenaline.
- The **parasympathetic nerve** (the vagus nerve) carries impulses to the SAN that **decrease** heart rate. It releases acetylcholine.

### Increasing the rate in exercise

1. Muscles respire faster, producing more carbon dioxide, which lowers the pH of the blood.
2. Chemoreceptors in the carotid arteries and aorta detect the change and send more impulses to the cardiac centre in the medulla.
3. The centre sends more impulses along the sympathetic nerve to the SAN.
4. The SAN produces waves of excitation more frequently, so the heart rate increases.
5. The greater cardiac output delivers more oxygen to the muscles and removes carbon dioxide faster, so the carbon dioxide concentration falls. The chemoreceptors then send fewer impulses, which is negative feedback.

### Decreasing the rate

If blood pressure is too high, pressure receptors send more impulses to the cardiac centre. The centre increases impulses along the parasympathetic (vagus) nerve to the SAN, which slows the heart and lowers the pressure. If blood pressure is too low, the pressure receptors send fewer impulses, the sympathetic nerve is stimulated and the rate rises. After exercise, as carbon dioxide falls, impulses along the parasympathetic nerve slow the heart again.

| Stimulus | Receptor | Nerve to the SAN | Effect on heart rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rise in carbon dioxide, fall in pH | Chemoreceptors | Sympathetic | Increases |
| High blood pressure | Pressure receptors | Parasympathetic (vagus) | Decreases |
| Low blood pressure | Pressure receptors | Sympathetic | Increases |
| Fall in carbon dioxide, rise in pH | Chemoreceptors | Parasympathetic | Decreases |

## Cardiac output

**Cardiac output = stroke volume x heart rate**, where stroke volume is the volume of blood pumped out by one ventricle in each beat. It gives the volume pumped per minute.

**Worked example 3.** At rest, a person has a heart rate of 72 beats per minute and a stroke volume of 70 cm3. During exercise, the heart rate is 150 beats per minute and the stroke volume is 110 cm3.

1. Cardiac output at rest = 72 x 70 = 5,040 cm3 per minute (5.04 dm3 per minute).
2. Cardiac output in exercise = 150 x 110 = 16,500 cm3 per minute (16.5 dm3 per minute).
3. Percentage increase = (16,500 - 5,040) / 5,040 x 100 = **227 %**.
4. The heart rate rose by 108 % and the stroke volume by 57 %. Both contribute to the large increase.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the brain starts each heartbeat. Cardiac muscle is myogenic, and the SAN starts the beat.
- Saying that the AVN "starts" the beat in the ventricles. It passes on, and delays, the wave from the atria.
- Writing that the wave of excitation passes straight from the atria to the ventricles through the muscle. The non-conducting layer prevents that.
- Confusing sympathetic and parasympathetic: sympathetic speeds the heart up, parasympathetic slows it down.
- Saying that the chemoreceptors in this control system detect only oxygen. The stimulus to describe is the fall in pH caused by more carbon dioxide.
- Forgetting to name the cardiac centre in the medulla and the SAN as the effector.
- Saying that the Purkinje fibres are in the atria. They are in the ventricle walls.

## Exam technique

- **Write sequences in order**, with the structures named: SAN, atria, non-conducting layer, AVN (delay), bundle of His, Purkinje fibres, ventricles.
- **For control**, use the pattern stimulus, receptor, coordinator (medulla), nerve, effector (SAN), response.
- **Include the negative feedback**: the response reduces the original stimulus.
- **For calculations**, show the formula before the numbers, and give units.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the AV node the same as the bundle of His?**
No. The AVN is a node at the junction between the atria and ventricles. The bundle of His is a group of conducting fibres that carries the wave from the AVN to the Purkinje fibres.

**What does myogenic mean?**
It means that the contraction starts within the muscle itself, without a signal from a nerve.
