This guide covers the full content of **Paper 2 Core Human Geography**,
for Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), 2025–2026
series. Like Paper 1 (Core Physical Geography), Paper 2 is compulsory for
every candidate and forms 50% of the AS award and 25% of the full A
Level. It is examined in a 1 hour 30 minute paper worth 60 marks.

## Where this fits in 9696

AS Level candidates sit both Paper 1 and Paper 2 in their first year of
study, giving a balanced physical/human foundation before A Level
candidates go on to choose two options each from the more specialised
Paper 3 (Advanced Physical Geography) and Paper 4 (Advanced Human
Geography). Core Human Geography is built around three topics —
Population, Migration and Settlement Dynamics — each of which the
syllabus expects candidates to illustrate with real examples and case
studies drawn from low income countries (LICs), middle income countries
(MICs) and high income countries (HICs).

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE A-LEVEL GEOGRAPHY (9696) — PAPER 2 CORE HUMAN GEOGRAPHY**

- 4.1 Natural increase as a component of population change — natural
increase rate, birth rate, death rate, fertility rate and infant
mortality rate; the social, economic, environmental and political
factors affecting fertility and mortality; interpreting age/sex
structure diagrams; population structure, dependency and the dependency
ratio
- 4.2 Demographic transition — changes in birth and death rate over
time; critical appreciation of the demographic transition model, Stages
1–5; youthful and ageing populations; links between population and
development through changes in infant mortality and life expectancy
- 4.3 Population–resource relationships — food security and the causes
and consequences of food shortages; the role of technology and
innovation in food production; constraints such as war and climatic
hazards; carrying capacity; critical evaluation of optimum population,
overpopulation and underpopulation
- 4.4 The management of natural increase — a required case study of one
country's population policy on natural increase, including attempts to
alter the natural increase rate and manage its results
- 5.1 Migration as a component of population change — movements
excluding those under one year's duration; push and pull factors;
processes including chain migration; patterns by distance and age;
constraints, obstacles and barriers such as cost and national borders
- 5.2 Internal migration — rural–urban and urban–rural movements and
their causes and impacts on source and destination areas; stepped
migration within the settlement hierarchy; urban–urban movements;
intra-urban movements
- 5.3 International migration — voluntary and forced (involuntary)
movements; causes and patterns including economic migration and refugee
flows; impacts on source and destination areas
- 5.4 The management of international migration — a required case study
of one international migration stream: its causes, character, scale,
pattern and impacts
- 6.1 Changes in rural settlements — contemporary issues such as
depopulation and service provision in LICs, MICs and HICs; impacts of
internal migration and urban growth; a required case study of a rural
settlement or area
- 6.2 Urban trends and issues of urbanisation — urban growth and the
process, causes and consequences of urbanisation including
counterurbanisation, re-urbanisation, competition for land and urban
renewal; the concept of a world city and the hierarchy of world cities
- 6.3 The changing structure of urban settlements — factors affecting
the location of activities within urban areas; the changing central
business district; competition for space, bid rent and functional
zonation; residential segregation, its causes and processes
- 6.4 The management of urban settlements — a required case study
covering both a shanty town (squatter settlement) in an LIC or MIC and
the provision of infrastructure (power or transport) for a city

## How to approach it

The three topics build on each other — population change (Topic 4) sets
up why people move (Topic 5, Migration), and both feed into how
settlements change shape and grow (Topic 6). Revising them in that order,
rather than jumping between them, makes the links between population
structure, migration flows and urban change easier to hold onto.

Each topic ends with a "management" sub-topic requiring a named case
study — 4.4, 5.4 and 6.4 are not optional extras but examinable content
in their own right, so prepare specific, locatable case studies (a real
country's population policy, a real migration stream, a real rural
settlement, a real shanty town and a real infrastructure project) rather
than generic description. Examiners reward named places, real data and
an evaluation of how well the response worked, not just a description of
what happened.

Because the syllabus explicitly expects examples across LICs, MICs and
HICs, avoid building a case-study bank drawn entirely from one income
group — a question may ask you to contrast experiences across income
levels, and having only high-income-country examples (or only
low-income-country examples) leaves you unable to answer that kind of
question well.

## Assessment objectives

Paper 2 is weighted identically to Paper 1: 30% AO1 (knowledge of terms,
principles and located places), 30% AO2 (understanding how processes
bring change, and applying knowledge to unfamiliar contexts), 28% AO3
(interpreting and communicating geographical data) and 12% AO4
(evaluating the success of initiatives and the viewpoints of different
groups). The AO4 weighting is exactly where the three management
sub-topics (4.4, 5.4, 6.4) earn their marks — a case study that only
describes what a policy or scheme did, without assessing how well it
worked or whose interests it served, is answering AO1 when the question
is very often testing AO4.
