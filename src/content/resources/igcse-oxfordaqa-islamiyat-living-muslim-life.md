This guide covers **Paper 1 Living the Muslim Life**, one of two
equally weighted papers in OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiyat
(9237). Paper 1 and Paper 2 (The Life of Hazrat Muhammad (pbuh) and
Sources of Authority in Islam) are each 1 hour 30 minutes, 50 marks
and 50% of the GCSE, with two sections of 25 marks each.

## Where this fits in 9237

Paper 1 focuses on the core beliefs of Islam and how they are put into
practice in daily life, establishing the theological foundation that
Paper 2's study of the life of the Prophet (pbuh) and sources of
authority builds on. Together, the two papers give a rounded picture
of Islamic belief, practice and history across the full GCSE.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE ISLAMIYAT (9237) — PAPER 1 LIVING THE
MUSLIM LIFE**

- Section A: Islam: Beliefs and Teachings — the core beliefs of Islam
and their basis in Islamic teaching
- Section B: Islam: Practices — how the beliefs of Islam are expressed
through practice in a Muslim's daily life

## How to approach it

Beliefs and Teachings (Section A) rewards being able to explain not
just what Muslims believe but why, with reference to relevant sources
-- structure answers around belief, evidence and significance rather
than description alone. Practices (Section B) benefits from concrete,
specific detail about how a belief translates into practice in daily
life, since exam questions typically ask students to connect belief to
lived practice rather than treat the two as separate lists. Because
both sections are equally weighted within the same paper, allocate
revision time evenly across beliefs and practices rather than
favouring one. Keeping notes organised by named belief or practice --
rather than as a single continuous account -- makes it easier to
recall relevant, specific content quickly when answering a focused
exam question under time pressure, and makes revision more manageable
in the run-up to the exam.

## The Five Pillars

The Pillars are the framework of practice, and questions ask both what each involves and **why it matters** — the second half is where marks are lost.

| Pillar | Practice | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Shahadah | Declaration of faith | Entry to Islam; states tawhid and prophethood together |
| Salah | Five daily prayers | Regular direct connection with God; discipline and equality in the rows |
| Zakat | Annual charity, ordinarily 2.5% of qualifying wealth | Purifies wealth; reduces inequality; treats wealth as a trust |
| Sawm | Fasting in Ramadan, dawn to sunset | Develops taqwa and self-restraint; builds empathy with the poor |
| Hajj | Pilgrimage to Makkah, once if able | Demonstrates unity and equality; recalls events of Ibrahim's family |

Conditions and exemptions matter: Salah has prescribed times and requires wudu; Zakat applies only above the **nisab** threshold; Sawm exempts the ill, travelling, pregnant, nursing and elderly; Hajj requires both physical and financial capability.

**Hajj is made up of specific rites, and questions often ask for these by name rather than for a general description.** Pilgrims enter **Ihram** — a state of purity marked by simple white garments — before performing **Tawaf** (seven circuits of the Ka'bah) and **Sa'i** (walking between the hills of Safa and Marwah). Standing at **Arafat** is the essential rite, commemorating the Prophet's Farewell Sermon; pilgrims then stone the pillars at Mina and make the sacrifice. The white ihram garments carry the central meaning behind the whole pilgrimage: because every pilgrim dresses identically, wealth and status become invisible, so all stand equal before God -- that symbolism, not just the list of rites, is what a full-mark "explain the significance of Hajj" answer needs to reach.

## Articles of faith

Six beliefs shape the Muslim worldview: **tawhid** (the oneness of God), belief in the **angels**, the revealed **books**, the **prophets**, the **Day of Judgement**, and **al-qadr** (divine decree).

Tawhid is the foundation, and its opposite — **shirk**, associating partners with God — is the gravest sin. Its practical consequences run through everything: worship directed to God alone, no intermediaries, and the equality of all believers before Him.

## Worship, festivals and daily life

Beyond the Pillars: the mosque as a place of prayer, learning and community; **Jummah** congregational prayer on Friday; **du'a** as personal supplication, distinct from the prescribed Salah; and recitation of the Qur'an. The five daily prayers -- **Fajr** (dawn), **Zuhr** (midday), **Asr** (afternoon), **Maghrib** (sunset) and **Isha** (night) -- are performed facing the **Qiblah**, the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah, in sequences of movements and recitations called **rak'ahs**. Jummah's significance is specifically communal: it unites the community and provides shared teaching through the khutbah, which an individual's private prayer does not.

**Eid-ul-Fitr** marks the end of Ramadan, with zakat-ul-fitr given before the prayer. **Eid-ul-Adha** falls during Hajj and commemorates Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son, the qurbani meat traditionally shared in three parts — family, relatives and the poor.

Daily conduct is shaped by **halal** and **haram**: dietary rules, honesty in trade, the prohibition of interest, and duties to parents, neighbours and the needy.

## Worked example

Explain why Zakat is significant for a Muslim community.

```
Individual  purifies remaining wealth and guards against greed
            recognises wealth as held in trust from God

Community   redistributes to the poor, orphans, travellers, the indebted
            reduces inequality and social resentment
            strengthens mutual responsibility

Key point   zakat is an obligatory act of worship, not optional charity
            -- this is what distinguishes it from sadaqah
```

That final distinction is often the difference between a good answer and a full-mark one.

## Common mistakes

Confusing zakat with sadaqah, or omitting the nisab. Describing the fast without the dawn-to-sunset timing or the exemptions. Saying Hajj is obligatory for every Muslim without the condition of ability. Listing the Pillars without explaining significance. Confusing the two Eids. Treating shirk as a minor matter.

## Quick revision checklist

- Describe each Pillar with its conditions and exemptions, and explain its significance.
- State the six articles of faith and explain tawhid and its consequences.
- Distinguish Salah from du'a, and zakat from sadaqah.
- Explain the origin, practices and meaning of both Eids.
- Explain how belief shapes daily conduct through halal, haram and social obligation.
