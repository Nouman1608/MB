This guide covers **sub-theme 3.2, attitudes towards immigration (ہجرت (ترکِ وطن) کی جانب رجحانات و طرزِ فکر)**, of Theme 3 in Pearson Edexcel A Level Urdu (9UR0). The specification names three aspects:

- **معاشرے کو یکجا کرنے کے لئے کمیونٹی کی سرگرمیاں**: community activities to bring society together
- **ہجرت (ترکِ وطن) کی جانب عوام کا طرزِ فکر**: public attitudes towards immigration
- **مہاجرین کا نکتۂ نظر**: the migrants' point of view

Theme 3 is set in Pakistan. The [attitudes revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-attitudes-towards-immigration-revision-notes/) set out welcoming and hostile attitudes and the role of the media. This guide develops the two aspects those notes treat more briefly, community activities and the migrants' own viewpoint, and shows how the sub-theme becomes Paper 3 summary and evaluation work. See the [Theme 3 study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-theme-3-study-guide/) for the theme, and practise with the [attitudes practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-9ur0-attitudes-towards-immigration-practice/).

## Who are the migrants in a Pakistani context?

To discuss attitudes precisely, name the groups. Texts on this sub-theme may concern:

- **Afghan refugees** (**افغان پناہ گزین**), present in large numbers since the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979; some have lived in Pakistan for decades, and many children were born there.
- **Communities of other origins in Karachi**, including people of Bengali and Burmese origin, many of whom have reported difficulty obtaining identity documents.
- **Internal migrants** who move from one province to another, especially to Karachi and other large cities. Strictly they are not immigrants, but attitudes towards them raise the same questions of welcome, competition and belonging.
- **The memory of 1947**, when Pakistan itself was founded amid a vast migration. This history shapes how many Pakistanis talk about migrants.

## Aspect 1: community activities that bring society together

The wording **یکجا کرنے** ("to bring together, unite") asks what ordinary people and organisations do, not what governments decide. Collect examples under four headings:

1. **Shared worship and charity**: mosques and shrines where newcomers and locals pray side by side; **افطار** gatherings in Ramadan; **لنگر** and food distribution open to all.
2. **Education**: schools where refugee and local children study together; adult literacy and Urdu classes that help newcomers take part in daily life.
3. **Sport and culture**: neighbourhood cricket and football tournaments; celebrations of Independence Day and Eid in mixed neighbourhoods; local fairs.
4. **Welfare organisations**: charities and community groups that offer health camps, food or legal advice to anyone in need.

Explain **how** each activity builds unity: contact reduces stereotypes; shared goals (a match, a festival, a clean street) create common identity; mutual help builds trust. A helpful Urdu phrase is **میل جول بڑھتا ہے تو غلط فہمیاں کم ہوتی ہیں** (when contact increases, misunderstandings decrease).

## Aspect 2: public attitudes towards immigration

**طرزِ فکر** means "way of thinking, mindset". Go beyond "for and against" by asking **why** attitudes differ and **how** they change:

- **Religious and cultural ties**: shared faith and, for Afghans, shared languages such as Pashto and Dari or Persian, encourage welcome. The example of the Ansar of Madina, who received the Muhajirin, is often invoked.
- **Economic pressure**: competition for jobs, housing and public services can harden attitudes, especially when the economy is weak.
- **Security**: fears about crime or militancy, often encouraged by headlines, can turn suspicion on whole communities.
- **Length of stay**: attitudes can shift from sympathy towards a temporary guest (**مہمان**) to resentment when a stay lasts decades. The language of hospitality itself assumes that guests eventually leave.
- **Personal contact**: people who work or study with migrants often hold more positive views than those who know them only through the news.

A strong answer distinguishes the **attitude** (feeling) from the **policy** (what the state decides). Public sympathy and official pressure to return can exist at the same time.

## Aspect 3: the migrants' point of view (مہاجرین کا نکتۂ نظر)

This aspect asks you to see immigration from the inside. Themes that appear in migrants' accounts:

- **Belonging and identity**: a young person born in Pakistan to refugee parents may feel Pakistani, speak Urdu fluently and never have seen the parents' country, yet lack Pakistani citizenship.
- **Documents and security**: without valid documents, access to schooling, work, bank accounts and mobile phone connections becomes difficult, and the fear of being sent back shapes daily life.
- **Gratitude and grievance**: many express gratitude for shelter and opportunity, alongside hurt at being blamed for crime or economic problems.
- **Memory and loss**: the homeland, family left behind and the hope or fear of returning.
- **Contribution**: migrants often stress the work they do and the businesses they run.

When you write about this aspect, use the vocabulary of feeling precisely: **بے یقینی** (uncertainty), **اپنائیت** (a sense of belonging), **بیگانگی** (alienation), **احسان مندی** (gratitude), **شکوہ** (complaint, grievance).

## Worked example: reported speech for Paper 3 summaries

Paper 3 Section B asks you to summarise viewpoints from a recording and a text. Speakers use direct speech; your summary should usually report it. In Urdu, reported speech after **کہ** normally keeps the speaker's original tense, but pronouns change.

Direct (from an imagined interview):

> "میں یہاں پیدا ہوا، یہیں پڑھا، مگر آج بھی مجھے اجنبی سمجھا جاتا ہے۔"

Reported:

> انٹرویو دینے والے نوجوان کا کہنا ہے کہ وہ یہیں پیدا ہوا اور اس نے یہیں تعلیم حاصل کی، لیکن اس کے باوجود اسے آج بھی غیر سمجھا جاتا ہے۔

**What changed and why:**

- **میں → وہ**, **مجھے → اسے**: first person becomes third person.
- The tenses (**پیدا ہوا**, **سمجھا جاتا ہے**) stay the same.
- Vocabulary is varied to show your own words: **پڑھا → تعلیم حاصل کی**, **اجنبی → غیر**, **مگر → لیکن اس کے باوجود**. Because **تعلیم حاصل کرنا** is transitive, its perfective needs the ergative subject **اس نے**; the verb then agrees with تعلیم (feminine): **حاصل کی**.
- The reporting frame **کا کہنا ہے کہ** attributes the view clearly.

## Worked example: translating into Urdu (Paper 2, Section A)

> Many refugee families have lived here for more than thirty years. Their children speak Urdu fluently and consider this country their home.

**Model:** بہت سے پناہ گزین خاندان تیس سال سے زیادہ عرصے سے یہاں رہ رہے ہیں۔ ان کے بچے روانی سے اردو بولتے ہیں اور اس ملک کو اپنا گھر سمجھتے ہیں۔

- "Have lived ... for more than thirty years" is a continuing state, so Urdu uses the present continuous **رہ رہے ہیں** with **سے**, not a perfect tense.
- **X کو Y سمجھنا** renders "consider X Y".
- **روانی سے** gives "fluently".

## Common mistakes

- Treating the sub-theme as only "for or against immigration" and ignoring the community activities and migrants' viewpoint named in the specification.
- Writing about Britain or Europe when the theme is set in Pakistan.
- Generalising about whole communities, which weakens an evaluation and can read as prejudice.
- In summaries, leaving pronouns in the first person, so that it is unclear who holds the view.
- Using **مہمان** (guest) without noticing the assumption it carries about a temporary stay.

## Exam technique

- In listening, attitudes are often signalled by tone words: **افسوس کی بات ہے کہ**, **خوش آئند بات یہ ہے کہ** (it is encouraging that), **بدقسمتی سے**. Note them on first hearing.
- In Paper 3 evaluation, judge each view on its evidence and fairness, then state which you agree with and why; the specification asks you to say which views you agree with and give reasons.
- Keep balance without sitting on the fence: a clear conclusion such as **پالیسی سخت ہو سکتی ہے، مگر رویے میں انسانیت لازم ہے** answers the question.
