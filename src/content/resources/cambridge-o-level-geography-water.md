of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The

## What 3.6 asks you to do

| Part | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Supply and use | Describe methods of water supply, and the proportions of water used for agriculture, domestic and industrial purposes in countries at different levels of economic development |
| Shortages and management | Explain why there are water shortages in some areas, and show that careful management is needed to ensure future supplies |
| Case study | Water supply in a country or area |

## Methods of water supply

| Method | How it works | Points to evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| Reservoirs and dams | A dam stores river water in a lake for use through the year | Large, reliable store; floods land, displaces people, loses water to evaporation in hot climates |
| Wells and boreholes | Water is pumped from **aquifers**, rocks that hold groundwater | Cheap for villages; over-pumping lowers the water table and wells dry up; near coasts, seawater can seep in |
| River abstraction | Water is pumped directly from rivers | Simple; supply falls in dry seasons; pollution from upstream |
| Desalination | Salt is removed from seawater, often by forcing it through membranes | Unlimited raw water for coastal countries; uses a lot of energy and is expensive; produces salty brine waste |
| Water recycling | Waste water is treated so it can be reused | Reduces demand on rivers; high treatment cost; some public resistance |
| Water transfer | Pipelines or canals move water from wet areas to dry areas | Supplies cities far from rivers; very expensive; can harm the source area |
| Rainwater harvesting | Rain from roofs is collected in tanks | Cheap and local; depends on rainfall |

## Water use and development

Water use is split into three sectors: **agriculture** (mainly
irrigation), **industry** (cooling in power stations, processing and
manufacturing) and **domestic** (drinking, cooking, washing, toilets).
Globally, agriculture takes the largest share, around seven-tenths of
the fresh water withdrawn.

**Illustrative pattern** (the general shape you should be able to
describe; figures are for teaching only):

| Country type | Agriculture | Industry | Domestic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low income | 85% | 5% | 10% |
| Middle income | 70% | 18% | 12% |
| High income | 40% | 45% | 15% |

Explaining the pattern:

- In low-income countries most people work in farming and many depend
  on irrigation, while there is little industry. Domestic use per
  person is low because many homes have no piped supply.
- As countries industrialise, factories and power stations need large
  amounts of water, so the industrial share rises.
- In high-income countries, almost every home has piped water, showers
  and appliances, so domestic use per person is high, even though the
  domestic *share* of the total can stay modest.

Be careful to separate **share** (percentage) from **amount** (volume).
A high-income country can use a smaller percentage for agriculture but
still a large volume.

## Why some areas have water shortages

**Physical causes**

- Low or unreliable rainfall, as in hot deserts and semi-arid areas.
- High temperatures, which increase evaporation from reservoirs and
  soils.
- Drought years, which may become more frequent with climate change.
- Small countries or islands with few rivers or little storage space.

**Human causes**

- Population growth and urbanisation, which increase demand in cities.
- Expansion of irrigated farming.
- Over-abstraction of groundwater faster than aquifers recharge.
- Pollution of rivers and aquifers by sewage, farm chemicals and
  industry, which makes water unusable.
- Lack of money to build dams, pipes and treatment works (**economic
  water scarcity**), so people lack safe water even where rainfall is
  adequate.
- Leaking pipes and wasteful use.

## Managing water for the future

Management either **increases supply** or **reduces demand**:

- *Increase supply*: new reservoirs, desalination plants, water
  recycling, transfers and rainwater harvesting.
- *Reduce demand*: water meters and pricing, fixing leaks,
  water-efficient appliances, drip irrigation instead of flooding
  fields, education campaigns, and controls on pumping groundwater.
- *Protect quality*: sewage treatment and limits on industrial and
  farm pollution.

Careful management is needed because demand keeps rising while the
supply of usable fresh water is limited; without it, aquifers are
exhausted and rivers polluted, leaving less for the future.

## Case study: water supply in Singapore

Singapore is a small, densely populated island city-state at the
southern tip of the Malay Peninsula. Despite heavy tropical rainfall, it
has limited land to collect and store water and no large rivers or
aquifers. Its national water agency, PUB, manages supply through the
**"Four National Taps"**:

1. **Local catchment water**: rain is collected through drains, canals
   and rivers into reservoirs. A large part of the island's land area
   now acts as water catchment. The Marina Barrage, completed in 2008,
   dammed the mouth of the Marina Channel to create a freshwater
   reservoir in the city centre that also helps to control flooding.
2. **Imported water**: water is piped from Johor in Malaysia under a
   long-term agreement that runs until 2061.
3. **NEWater**: used water is treated to a very high standard using
   membranes and ultraviolet light. It is mainly supplied to industry,
   such as wafer fabrication plants, with some added to reservoirs.
4. **Desalination**: seawater is treated in plants such as those at
   Tuas.

Singapore also manages **demand** through water pricing, a water
conservation tax and campaigns encouraging households to save water.

Evaluation: Singapore shows how a country with little natural storage
can secure supply through technology and planning, but desalination and
NEWater use a lot of energy, and the approach needs wealth that many
countries do not have.

## Worked example: describe and explain

**Question:** Using Table 1 (the illustrative pattern above), describe
how the proportion of water used by industry changes with development,
and explain the change. **[4]**

```
Describe:  industry's share rises from 5% in low-income countries to
           18% in middle-income and 45% in high-income countries,
           so it is nine times greater in high- than low-income
           countries                                           [2]
Explain:   industrialisation brings factories and thermal power
           stations that need water for processing and cooling;
           low-income economies are dominated by farming, so
           agriculture takes most water                        [2]
```

Quote figures from the resource and make a clear comparison
("nine times greater") before giving reasons.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing percentage share with total volume.
- Giving only physical causes of shortages and forgetting human causes
  such as pollution and over-abstraction.
- Naming desalination as a simple solution without its cost and energy
  use.
- Case studies with general statements and no named schemes or places.
