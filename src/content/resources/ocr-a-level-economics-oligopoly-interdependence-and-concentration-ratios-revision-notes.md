## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oligopoly | A market shared by a few big sellers whose choices affect one another |
| Interdependence | One firm's best action depends on how rivals respond |
| Non-price competition | Rivalry by branding, quality, service, innovation or loyalty rather than price |
| Collusion | Cooperation to limit competition, formal (cartel) or tacit (price leadership) |
| Dominant strategy | A choice that is best whatever the rival does |
| Nash equilibrium | Neither player can gain by changing alone, given the other's choice |
| Concentration ratio (CRn) | Combined market share of the n largest firms |
| Product differentiation | Making a product distinct through features, design, brand or service |

## Characteristics of oligopoly

- Few large firms; high concentration
- High barriers to entry and exit
- Interdependence
- Differentiated (or sometimes identical) products
- Collusion possible
- Price stability; non-price competition

## Interdependence: game theory

Method for reading a pay-off matrix:

1. Fix the rival's choice and find your best reply. Repeat for the rival's other choice.
2. If one choice wins in both cases, it is a dominant strategy.
3. A Nash equilibrium is a cell where both are playing a best reply.
4. Compare it with the best joint outcome. If they differ, there is a dilemma.

Reminder (profits in £m, invented; A first, B second):

| | B: High | B: Low |
|---|---|---|
| **A: High** | 15, 15 | 4, 21 |
| **A: Low** | 21, 4 | 9, 9 |

Low is dominant for both. Nash equilibrium: Low/Low (9, 9), total 18. Best joint: High/High (15, 15), total 30.

A second type of game: if A earns −3, 12, 2, 5 and B earns −3, 2, 12, 5 across the four cells (Launch/Launch, Launch/Stay, Stay/Launch, Stay/Stay), neither player has a dominant strategy. There are two Nash equilibria: one firm launches while the other stays out. Which one occurs depends on who commits first.

Points to remember:

- Explains price wars and why collusion is unstable: either firm gains by cheating (15 to 21).
- Repeated games can support cooperation, because cheating can be punished.
- Some games have no dominant strategy and have more than one Nash equilibrium.
- Limit: real firms have imperfect information, and the payoffs are estimates.

## Interdependence: kinked demand curve

- Price rise: rivals stay put, so demand above the kink is elastic.
- Price cut: rivals match, so demand below the kink is inelastic.
- MR has a vertical gap at the kink output.
- If MC cuts MR in the gap, price and output do not change.

```
Price
 |\  elastic
 | \
 P* + - K
 |    \ \
 |     \  \  inelastic
 | MR gap |
 +--------+---- Quantity
```

Reminder figures: kink at 1,000 units and £8. Upper MR is £4 and lower MR is −£2 there, so any MC from −2 to 4 gives the same price. Elasticity: 2.0 above, 0.8 below.

Evaluation of the model:

- Explains price stickiness and why non-price competition is used.
- Silent on how the starting price arose.
- Rivals' reactions are assumed, not always seen in practice.
- Prices do change; price wars occur; a big cost rise (MC outside the gap) moves price.

## Types of collusion

| Type | Example | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Formal (overt) | Cartel agreeing price or quota | Clear, but easy for authorities to detect |
| Tacit (informal) | Price leadership, following a convention | Hard to prove |

Why cartels fail: each member can gain by cheating, and cheating is hard to detect with many firms.

Reminder numbers: demand P = 96 − Q, MC = £24. Monopoly outcome Q = 36, P = £60, profit 1,296, so four firms get 324 each. One firm cheats at 13: price £56, cheat earns 416, others 288 each.

Conditions that help collusion: few firms, similar products, stable demand, barriers to entry, easy monitoring.

## Non-price competition and differentiation

- Forms: advertising, branding, quality, design, after-sales service, loyalty schemes, delivery, innovation.
- Why: price cuts are copied quickly, while brand loyalty is not.
- Effects: demand becomes less elastic; entry is harder; choice may rise; costs may rise.
- Break-even test for a campaign: extra units needed = campaign cost ÷ contribution per unit. Reminder: £280,000 ÷ £4 = 70,000 extra units.

## Advantages and disadvantages of oligopoly

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale | Price above MC: allocative inefficiency |
| Funds for research and innovation (dynamic efficiency) | Higher prices if firms collude |
| Price stability | Barriers to entry protect profit |
| Quality competition and choice | Wasteful advertising; possible X-inefficiency |

Diagram in words: price at the kink is above MC; AR may exceed AC (supernormal profit); output below the lowest point of AC.

Evaluate with: degree of rivalry, threat of entry, use of profits, regulation, time period.

## Concentration ratios

CRn = (sales of the top n firms added together ÷ whole-market sales) × 100

Method in steps:

1. Find total market sales (include all small sellers).
2. Rank firms from largest down.
3. Add the top n and divide by the total.
4. State the answer as a percentage and give n.

Reminder: sales £m: 88, 74, 61, 47, 30, 22, others 78; total 400. CR3 = 223 ÷ 400 = 55.75%. CR4 = 67.5%. If the third and fourth firms merge (108), CR3 = 270 ÷ 400 = 67.5%.

Uses and limits:

- A high ratio suggests market power; a rising ratio suggests growing concentration.
- Depends on how the market is defined (local or national).
- Ignores foreign rivals and the split among leaders.
- Says nothing about how contestable the market is.

## Comparing the four structures

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Firms | Very many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Often differentiated | Unique |
| Entry barriers | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Long-run profit | Normal | Normal | Supernormal possible | Supernormal possible |
| Allocative efficiency | P = MC | P > MC | Usually P > MC | P > MC |
| Productive efficiency | Yes, long run | No: excess capacity | Not guaranteed | Not guaranteed |

Reminder numbers: demand P = 96 − Q, MC = £24. Competitive: Q = 72, P = £24. Monopoly: Q = 36, P = £60. Deadweight loss = ½ × 36 × 36 = 648.

Firm-level reminder (invented): TC = 100 + 10q + 0.1q², long-run demand AR = 20 − 0.15q. At q = 20, AC = 17 = AR, so profit is normal. MR = MC = 14, so P − MC = 3. Lowest AC is £16.32 at q = 31.6, so excess capacity is about 11.6 units. The comparison with perfect competition: the competitive firm produces at the lowest AC and charges P = MC.

Diagram prompts to practise: long-run perfect competition (price at minimum AC); long-run monopolistic competition (demand tangent to AC, left of minimum AC); monopoly (MR = MC, price read up to AR); oligopoly (kinked demand).

## Evaluating the theory of the firm

| Supports the theory | Challenges it |
|---|---|
| Gives benchmarks such as P = MC | Firms pursue growth, sales or satisficing, not only profit |
| Predicts entry when profits are high | Principal-agent problem: owners and managers differ |
| Explains price rigidity and non-price rivalry | Real firms rarely know MC and MR |
| | Markets are hard to fit into four boxes |

Judgement frame: it is a useful benchmark, strongest where firms are profit-driven and markets stable, weaker where objectives are mixed. Always give an example.

## Quick self-test

1. State two characteristics of oligopoly.
2. Why is demand elastic above the kink?
3. In the pay-off matrix above, why is Low a dominant strategy for A?
4. A market has total sales £500m. The four largest firms sell £120m, £90m, £80m and £60m. Calculate CR4.
5. Give one reason why a cartel is unstable.
6. In the kinked model, MC rises from £2 to £3 and stays within the MR gap. What happens to price?
7. State one limitation of a concentration ratio.
8. Under perfect competition (demand P = 96 − Q, MC = £24), what are price and output?
9. Define a Nash equilibrium.
10. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of non-price competition.

### Answers

1. Any two: few large firms, high entry barriers, interdependence, product differentiation, possible collusion.
2. Rivals do not follow a price rise, so customers switch away.
3. Low pays 21 against High and 9 against Low, more than High's 15 and 4.
4. (120 + 90 + 80 + 60) ÷ 500 = 350 ÷ 500 = 70%.
5. Each member gains by secretly cheating on the quota, as in the 416 against 324 example.
6. It stays the same, because MC still cuts the gap in MR.
7. For example: it ignores foreign competition, or depends on the market definition.
8. P = MC = £24, so Q = 72.
9. An outcome where neither player can gain by changing its own choice, given the other's.
10. Advantage: builds loyalty and less elastic demand. Disadvantage: costs can exceed the extra sales, and rivals can match it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "market share" without saying whether it is by sales, output or employment.
- Reading a concentration ratio as a number of firms.
- Labelling the elasticities the wrong way round at the kink.
- Giving a Nash equilibrium without checking best replies for both players.
- Asserting that collusion always raises profit, with no mention of cheating.
- Drawing a diagram with unlabelled axes or curves, or no marked equilibrium.
- Comparing market structures feature by feature without a view on efficiency.
- Ending an evaluation question with no conclusion or no example.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the prisoner's dilemma in oligopoly?**
The prisoner's dilemma is a situation where self-interest drives both firms to an outcome that leaves each worse off than if they had cooperated, such as both choosing a low price.

**What is the difference between formal and tacit collusion?**
Formal (overt) collusion is an explicit agreement, such as a cartel fixing price or output quotas. Tacit collusion involves no agreement, but firms follow a common practice, such as price leadership or avoiding price cuts.

**What does the kinked demand curve explain?**
It explains price rigidity in oligopoly: because a price rise is not followed and a price cut is matched, the firm has little reason to change its price while marginal cost stays within the gap in marginal revenue.

**How useful is the theory of the firm in explaining real behaviour?**
The OCR A Level Economics (H460) notes judge it a useful benchmark, strongest where firms are profit-driven and markets stable, and weaker where firms have mixed objectives. Always support the judgement with an example.
