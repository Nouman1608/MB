## 4.2.3(a)(i): Quickfit set-ups

Quickfit glassware joins by standard ground-glass joints. Clamp the flask and condenser. Add anti-bumping granules. Heat flammable liquids with a heating mantle or water bath, not a flame.

| Feature | Heating under reflux | Distillation |
|---|---|---|
| Condenser position | Vertical, above the flask | Sloping down, from a still head to a receiver |
| Top of apparatus | Open to air, never stoppered | Thermometer in the still head |
| Thermometer | Not needed | Bulb level with the side arm |
| Water flow | In at the bottom, out at the top | In at the bottom, out at the top |
| What happens to vapour | Condenses and returns to the flask | Condenses and is collected |
| Use | Long heating without losing volatile substances | Removing a product as it forms; separating by boiling point |

**Oxidising a primary alcohol with K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄:**

- Aldehyde: **distil** as it forms, so it escapes before further oxidation.
- Carboxylic acid: **reflux** with **excess** oxidising agent.
- Colour change for both: orange to green.

## 4.2.3(a)(ii): Purifying an organic liquid

### Method in steps

1. Heat the reactants under reflux.
2. Distil off the crude organic product.
3. Shake with water in a separating funnel; vent through the tap often.
4. Identify layers by density: the denser liquid is at the bottom. Keep the organic layer.
5. Neutralise leftover acid by shaking with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, releasing the CO₂ through the tap; finish with a water wash.
6. Dry with an anhydrous salt, e.g. anhydrous MgSO₄ or CaCl₂, until the liquid is clear and some solid moves freely.
7. Decant or filter off the drying agent.
8. Redistil; collect the fraction within a narrow range around the boiling point.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Washing** removes water-soluble impurities (acid, salts). **Drying** removes the traces of water left after washing.
- **Distillation** of the reaction mixture gives a crude product. **Redistillation** of the dried liquid gives the pure product.
- **Density, not identity,** decides the layer order. Many bromo and iodo compounds sink below water; most hydrocarbons and alcohols float.

### Worked reminder: mass from volume

Mass (g) = volume (cm³) × density (g cm⁻³). For 6.00 cm³ of a liquid of density 0.785 g cm⁻³, the mass is 4.71 g. Then n = mass ÷ M, as in 2.1.3.

## 4.2.3(b)(i): Spotting functional groups

| Group | Look for | Classify by |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene | C=C | Position in the chain |
| Alcohol | C–OH | Primary (–CH₂OH), secondary (>CHOH), tertiary (no H on the C–OH carbon) |
| Haloalkane | C–F, C–Cl, C–Br, C–I | The halogen |
| Aldehyde | –CHO at a chain end | -- |
| Ketone | C=O with a carbon on each side | -- |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | -- |

In a structural formula, the carboxyl group is written COOH. A tertiary alcohol carbon looks like C(OH) with three carbon groups attached, for example (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂–.

## 4.2.3(b)(ii): Predicting properties and reactions

**Properties**

- O–H present: hydrogen bonding, so higher boiling point than an alkane of similar Mr, and some water solubility.
- C=C present: reactive, because the π-bond has a relatively low bond enthalpy.
- C–X present: polar bond; the carbon is attacked by nucleophiles such as OH⁻.

**Reactions: test each reagent against each group**

| Reagent | Reacts with | Observation or product |
|---|---|---|
| Br₂ (bromine water) | C=C | Orange to colourless; dibromo product |
| H₂ / Ni | C=C | Alkane chain |
| HBr | C=C | Bromoalkane (Markownikoff) |
| K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄ | 1° and 2° OH, CHO | Orange to green; 3° OH unchanged; ketones unchanged |
| H₃PO₄, heat | OH | Alkene, often as a mixture |
| NaBr / H₂SO₄ | OH | Bromoalkane |
| NaOH(aq), heat | C–X | Alcohol |

## 4.2.3(c): Two-stage routes

### The Module 4 map

- Alkane → haloalkane: Cl₂ or Br₂, UV
- Haloalkane → alcohol: NaOH(aq), heat under reflux
- Alkene → haloalkane: HBr or HCl
- Alkene → alcohol: steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst
- Alcohol → alkene: H₃PO₄ or H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat
- Alkene → alkane: H₂, Ni catalyst
- Alcohol → haloalkane: NaBr, H₂SO₄
- Primary alcohol → aldehyde: Cr₂O₇²⁻ / H⁺, distil; aldehyde → carboxylic acid: Cr₂O₇²⁻ / H⁺, reflux
- Secondary alcohol → ketone: Cr₂O₇²⁻ / H⁺, reflux

### Method in steps

1. Mark which groups change and confirm the carbon chain is unchanged.
2. Work back one step from the target.
3. Check the intermediate is one step from the starting material.
4. State reagent **and** conditions for both steps, and give the intermediate's structure.
5. Check the reagents do not destroy another group you need.
6. If the question supplies a reaction, use it as written.

### Small worked reminders

- Haloalkane to ketone: the haloalkane must be secondary. Hydrolyse with NaOH(aq), then oxidise the secondary alcohol under reflux.
- Alkene to alkane via an alcohol is a wasted step: H₂ / Ni does it directly.
- Alcohol to a different-position alcohol: dehydrate, then add steam. Markownikoff's rule decides the main product, so check which carbocation is more stable.

### Oxidation outcomes at a glance

| Alcohol type | Distil with K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄ | Reflux with excess K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄ |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Aldehyde | Carboxylic acid |
| Secondary | Ketone | Ketone |
| Tertiary | No reaction; stays orange | No reaction; stays orange |

### Using a reaction the question gives you

Exam papers may give a reaction that is not in Module 4. Read it as a new row in your toolkit:

1. Note the functional group it starts from and the group it makes.
2. Copy the reagents and conditions exactly as stated.
3. Fit it into the route as either step 1 or step 2, then add a Module 4 step to complete the route.
4. Check whether its product needs Markownikoff's rule or gives a mixture.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the position of the thermometer bulb in a distillation.
2. Why is the top of a reflux condenser left open?
3. State the purpose of anti-bumping granules.
4. Name two anhydrous salts used to dry an organic liquid.
5. How do you know an organic liquid is dry?
6. A product has density 0.88 g cm⁻³. Which layer is it in when shaken with water?
7. Why is sodium hydrogencarbonate solution used during washing, and why must you vent?
8. Name the groups in HOCH₂CH₂CH=CHCOOH.
9. Which group in (CH₃)₃COH resists oxidation, and why does that matter in a route?
10. Give a two-stage route from 2-chloropropane to propanone.
11. 0.0400 mol of propan-2-ol gives 3.10 g of 2-bromopropane (M = 122.9 g mol⁻¹). Calculate the percentage yield.
12. Why is a heating mantle used instead of a Bunsen burner to heat the reaction flask?

### Answers

1. Level with the side arm of the still head.
2. To avoid heating a sealed system, which could build pressure and shatter.
3. To make the liquid boil smoothly, without bumping.
4. Anhydrous magnesium sulfate and anhydrous calcium chloride.
5. The cloudy liquid turns clear and some drying agent moves freely without clumping.
6. The upper layer, because it is less dense than water.
7. It neutralises and removes acid. The reaction gives off CO₂, so pressure builds in the stoppered funnel.
8. Primary alcohol (–CH₂OH), alkene (C=C) and carboxylic acid (–COOH).
9. The tertiary alcohol group. It is not oxidised by acidified dichromate, so no route can turn it into an aldehyde, ketone or acid by oxidation.
10. NaOH(aq), heat under reflux, gives propan-2-ol; then K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄, reflux, gives propanone.
11. Theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 122.9 = 4.916 g; yield = 3.10 ÷ 4.916 × 100 = **63.1%**.
12. Organic liquids and their vapours are flammable, and a mantle has no naked flame that could ignite any vapour that escapes.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing a stoppered reflux condenser, or showing cooling water entering at the top.
- Writing "heat" for an alcohol oxidation without saying reflux or distil, which leaves the product unclear.
- Leaving out "acidified" or "excess" when oxidising a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid.
- Calling the top layer organic without using the density data in the question.
- Saying "drying removes impurities" instead of "removes water".
- Missing the redistillation step, or not saying which fraction to collect.
- Spotting only two of three functional groups in a multi-group molecule.
- Giving a route that oxidises a tertiary alcohol, or ignores Markownikoff's rule with HBr.
- Giving a reagent with no conditions, such as "NaOH" without "aqueous" and "heat".
