AQA GCSE English Language (8700) is a linear, non-tiered qualification of two equally weighted written
papers. Paper 1 (Explorations in Creative Reading and Writing) is 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks, 50% of
the GCSE, split into a 40-mark Reading section (one fiction extract) and a 40-mark Writing section
(descriptive or narrative). Paper 2 (Writers' Viewpoints and Perspectives) has the same length, mark
total and weighting, split into a 40-mark Reading section (two linked non-fiction extracts) and a 40-mark
Writing section. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Paper 1](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-paper-1/) and
[Paper 2](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-paper-2/).

## Every paper is an even split, and time should match

Because Reading and Writing are worth exactly 40 marks each within every paper, the traditional instinct
to spend most of the time on the (often longer-feeling) Reading questions is not supported by the mark
scheme. **Exam-preparation priority**: allocate roughly equal time to the Reading and Writing sections
within each paper -- as a rough guide, close to half the 1 hour 45 minutes for each half of the marks.

## Reading marks are concentrated in a few high-value questions

Both papers' Reading sections use a small number of questions worth increasingly large marks -- language
analysis and comparison/synthesis questions typically carry more marks than short retrieval questions.
**Exam-preparation priority**: check each question's mark allocation before answering and spend time
proportionally, rather than giving every question equal attention regardless of its worth.

## Writing sections reward planning time, not just drafting time

Both Writing sections are marked on content/organisation and technical accuracy together, and a few
minutes spent planning structure before writing consistently produces a more coherent response than
writing straight through. **Exam-preparation priority**: build a short, fixed planning routine (opening
idea, structure, closing idea) into every timed writing practice, so it becomes automatic rather than
skipped under pressure.

## Paper 2's two linked non-fiction extracts add a comparison step Paper 1 does not have

Paper 1 works from a single fiction extract; Paper 2 requires comparing viewpoints or methods across two
linked non-fiction texts. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise comparison questions specifically for
Paper 2, since the skill of identifying similarity and difference across two texts does not transfer
automatically from Paper 1's single-text analysis.

## Worked routine: allocating time within a 1 hour 45 minute paper

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - read the whole extract(s) once before answering anything:
Roughly 5 minutes -- do not start Question 1 cold.

Step 2 - work through the Reading questions in mark order:
Time roughly proportional to marks: a 4-mark question gets less
time than an 8-mark question, not the same time.

Step 3 - leave a clear midpoint checkpoint:
By roughly 50 minutes in, Reading should be finished and Writing
about to begin.

Step 4 - plan the Writing answer for 3-5 minutes before drafting:
Opening idea, rough structure, closing idea -- on paper, briefly.

Step 5 - leave 2-3 minutes at the end to check the Writing answer:
Spelling, punctuation and that the response actually answers the
task set.
```

Step 3's checkpoint is the single most useful habit to build in practice: candidates who run over on
Reading consistently lose more marks on a rushed Writing section than they gained from extra Reading
time.

## Both papers assess the same overarching skills, taught once

Reading comprehension, inference, language analysis and structured writing all recur across Paper 1 and
Paper 2, just applied to fiction versus non-fiction material. **Exam-preparation priority**: content and
skills revision does not need duplicating between the two papers -- the same close-reading and writing
technique transfers across both -- but timed practice under exam conditions should still be done for each
paper separately, since the specific text types differ.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise both papers under full timed conditions; check mark allocations before
  answering rather than assuming equal weight per question; drill the comparison skill specifically for
  Paper 2.
- **During any paper**: read the whole extract(s) first, hit the Reading/Writing midpoint checkpoint
  around 50 minutes, and plan before drafting the Writing answer.
- **At the end**: leave time to check spelling, punctuation and that the Writing response answers the
  task actually set.

## Self-test

1. How are marks split between Reading and Writing within each paper?
2. Why should time within the Reading section not be split equally across all its questions?
3. What skill does Paper 2 require that Paper 1 does not?
4. Why is a midpoint timing checkpoint useful during the exam?

**Answers:** 1. Each paper is 80 marks total, split evenly into a 40-mark Reading section and a 40-mark
Writing section. 2. Because Reading questions carry different mark allocations, so time should be spent
roughly proportional to each question's marks, not divided equally regardless of value. 3. Comparing
viewpoints or methods across two linked non-fiction extracts, since Paper 1 works from a single fiction
extract only. 4. It catches candidates running over on Reading before that overrun forces a rushed
Writing section, which typically costs more marks than the extra Reading time gained.
