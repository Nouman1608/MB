## Questions

**1.** Answer these about how the halogens look.

**(a)** State the colour and physical state of iodine at room temperature. **[1]**
**(b)** State the colour of iodine dissolved in hexane. **[1]**
**(c)** State the colour of a dilute aqueous solution of bromine. **[1]**

**2.** Use these values for the halogens.

| | Cl₂ | Br₂ | I₂ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting temperature / °C | −101 | −7 | 114 |
| Boiling temperature / °C | −34 | 59 | 184 |

**(a)** Use the data to deduce the physical state of bromine at 25 °C. Give a reason. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the trend in boiling temperature from chlorine to iodine. **[3]**

**3.** A few drops of bromine water are added to two tubes: one holds sodium chloride solution, the other
sodium iodide solution. Both tubes are then shaken with hexane.

**(a)** State the colour of the hexane layer in each tube. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation for the reaction that takes place. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify the oxidising agent in this reaction and explain your answer in terms of electrons. **[2]**

**4.** Magnesium ribbon burns in iodine vapour to form magnesium iodide.

**(a)** Write the equation and give the oxidation numbers of magnesium and iodine before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of iodine that reacts with 0.486 g of magnesium, and the mass of magnesium iodide formed. **[2]**

**5.** Bleach is made by bubbling chlorine into 120 cm³ of cold, dilute 0.850 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide
until all the alkali has reacted.

**(a)** Give the equation for this reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Use oxidation numbers to explain why this is a disproportionation reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of chlorine that reacts. **[2]**
**(d)** The solution is made up to 150 cm³ with water. Calculate the concentration of sodium chlorate(I). **[1]**

**6.** A colourless solution contains calcium ions and one type of halide ion. Adding dilute nitric acid,
then silver nitrate, produces a white precipitate that dissolves in dilute aqueous ammonia.

**(a)** Identify the halide ion. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why dilute nitric acid is added before the silver nitrate. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the ionic equation, with state symbols, for the precipitation. **[1]**
**(d)** The solution is 20.0 cm³ of 0.0750 mol dm⁻³ calcium halide. Calculate the volume of 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ silver nitrate that reacts exactly with all the halide ions. **[2]**

**7.** Concentrated sulfuric acid is added drop by drop to solid potassium iodide in a fume cupboard.

**(a)** Write the equation for the first step, which forms hydrogen iodide. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe three observations. **[3]**
**(c)** One reaction forms hydrogen sulfide. Write its equation and state the change in oxidation number of sulfur. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why solid potassium chloride gives no redox reaction with concentrated sulfuric acid. **[2]**
**(e)** Assume all the iodide in 3.32 g of potassium iodide ends up as iodine by the hydrogen sulfide reaction. Calculate the mass of iodine formed. **[2]**

**8.** Chlorine is passed into hot potassium hydroxide solution.

**(a)** Write a balanced equation for this reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of potassium chlorate(V) formed from 4.26 g of chlorine. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage of the chlorine atoms that end up in potassium chlorate(V). **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the minimum volume of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ potassium hydroxide needed. **[1]**

**9.** 2.34 g of sodium chloride is warmed with excess concentrated sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Give a balanced equation, with the sodium-containing product. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of hydrogen chloride formed, in cm³, at room temperature and pressure. **[2]**
**(c)** All the gas is absorbed in water, then made up to 250 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration of the acid. **[1]**
**(d)** State what you would see if the gas met ammonia gas, and name the product. **[1]**

**10.** This question asks you to make predictions about fluorine and astatine.

**(a)** Predict the physical state of astatine at room temperature. **[1]**
**(b)** Predict whether chlorine water reacts with sodium astatide solution. Write an ionic equation if it does. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the appearance of silver astatide and its behaviour with concentrated aqueous ammonia. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why fluorine is expected to be the strongest oxidising agent in the group. **[2]**
**(e)** Suggest why fluorine does not form a chlorate(V)-type ion when it reacts with hot alkali. **[1]**

**11.** Chlorine is added to the water supply of a town.

**(a)** Write the equation for chlorine reacting with water. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the oxidation number of chlorine in each chlorine-containing product. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate the use of chlorine in water treatment. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Grey-black (shiny) solid [1]
**(b)** Violet (purple) [1]
**(c)** Yellow (to orange) [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Colour answers must match the conditions asked for; "brown" for iodine in hexane describes the aqueous colour, so it is unlikely to gain credit.

**2. (a)** Liquid: 25 °C is above its melting temperature (−7 °C) but below its boiling temperature (59 °C) [1]
**(b)** All are simple molecular with London forces between molecules [1]. Molecules have more electrons down the group, so London forces are stronger [1]. Separating the molecules therefore takes more energy, and the boiling temperature rises [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mentioning broken Cl–Cl or I–I bonds during boiling contradicts the answer and usually costs credit.

**3. (a)** Sodium chloride tube: orange (no reaction) [1]; sodium iodide tube: violet [1]
**(b)** Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ [1]
**(c)** The oxidising agent is bromine [1]; it accepts electrons from iodide ions, becoming Br⁻ [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For the chloride tube, say the orange colour is the bromine itself; "no change" alone is too vague.

**4. (a)** Mg + I₂ → MgI₂ [1]; Mg 0 → +2 and I 0 → −1 [1]
**(b)** n(Mg) = 0.486 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0200 mol; mass of I₂ = 0.0200 × 253.8 = **5.08 g** [1]; mass of MgI₂ = 0.0200 × 278.1 = **5.56 g** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use Mr(I₂) = 253.8, not Ar(I); the factor-of-two slip is the usual source of a wrong mass here.

**5. (a)** Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O [1]
**(b)** In NaCl chlorine is −1, reduced from 0; in NaClO it is +1, oxidised from 0 [1]; the same element in one species is oxidised and reduced at once [1]
**(c)** n(NaOH) = 0.120 × 0.850 = 0.102 mol, so n(Cl₂) = 0.0510 mol [1]; mass = 0.0510 × 71.0 = **3.62 g** [1]
**(d)** n(NaClO) = 0.0510 mol; concentration = 0.0510 ÷ 0.150 = **0.340 mol dm⁻³** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Both oxidation number changes must be stated for disproportionation; giving only the reduction to −1 does not show the definition.

**6. (a)** Chloride, Cl⁻ [1]
**(b)** To remove carbonate (or other) ions that would also form a precipitate with silver ions [1]
**(c)** Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) [1]
**(d)** n(Cl⁻) = 2 × (0.0200 × 0.0750) = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; volume = 3.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0800 = 0.0375 dm³ = **37.5 cm³** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Calcium chloride provides two chloride ions per formula unit; missing the factor of 2 halves the answer.

**7. (a)** KI + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HI [1]
**(b)** Any three from: steamy fumes [1]; purple vapour or black solid (iodine) [1]; yellow solid (sulfur) or smell of bad eggs (hydrogen sulfide) [1]
**(c)** 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O [1]; sulfur goes from +6 to −2, a change of 8 [1]
**(d)** HCl is the weakest reducing agent of the hydrogen halides [1]; the small chloride ion holds its outer electrons tightly and does not lose them to sulfuric acid [1]
**(e)** n(KI) = 3.32 ÷ 166.0 = 0.0200 mol, so n(I₂) = 0.0100 mol [1]; mass = 0.0100 × 253.8 = **2.54 g** [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Observations must say what is seen or smelled; "hydrogen sulfide" without "bad-egg smell" may not count.

**8. (a)** 3Cl₂ + 6KOH → 5KCl + KClO₃ + 3H₂O [1]
**(b)** n(Cl₂) = 4.26 ÷ 71.0 = 0.0600 mol [1]; n(KClO₃) = 0.0600 ÷ 3 = 0.0200 mol [1]; mass = 0.0200 × 122.6 = **2.45 g** [1]
**(c)** One of the six chlorine atoms: **16.7%** [1]
**(d)** n(KOH) = 2 × 0.0600 = 0.120 mol; volume = 0.120 ÷ 2.00 = 0.0600 dm³ = **60.0 cm³** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the cold-alkali equation spoils every later step; check the 3 : 1 ratio of Cl₂ to KClO₃ first.

**9. (a)** NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl [1]
**(b)** n(NaCl) = 2.34 ÷ 58.5 = 0.0400 mol = n(HCl) [1]; volume = 0.0400 × 24 000 = **960 cm³** [1]
**(c)** 0.0400 ÷ 0.250 = **0.160 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(d)** White smoke of ammonium chloride, NH₄Cl [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for cm³, so multiply by 24 000; an answer of 0.960 with no unit does not answer the question as set.

**10. (a)** Solid [1]
**(b)** Yes, chlorine is the stronger oxidising agent [1]; Cl₂ + 2At⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + At₂ [1]
**(c)** Dark (darker than yellow) precipitate [1]; insoluble in concentrated ammonia [1]
**(d)** Fluorine atoms are the smallest, with the least shielding [1]; so an incoming electron is attracted most strongly and fluorine gains electrons most readily [1]
**(e)** Fluorine is the most electronegative element, so it cannot take a positive oxidation number such as +5 [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each prediction to a trend; a reason that contradicts the prediction, such as weaker London forces, can lose the mark.

**11. (a)** Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO [1]
**(b)** −1 in HCl and +1 in HClO [1]
**(c)** Chloric(I) acid kills bacteria [1]; this cuts waterborne disease such as cholera [1]; chlorine is toxic, so the dose must be controlled [1]; it can form possibly harmful chlorinated organic compounds with organic matter, a risk to weigh against the benefit [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement backed by points for and against is expected; a list of benefits alone limits the credit available.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing NaClO₃ for cold alkali.
- Stating that bromine displaces chloride ions.
- Calling iodine in hexane brown.
- Forgetting the acid–base first step with concentrated sulfuric acid.
- Using Ar(I) where Mr(I₂) is needed.
- Missing the factor of 2 for halide ions in calcium or magnesium halides.
- Explaining boiling temperatures with covalent bond strength.
- Giving only the reduction when explaining disproportionation.
