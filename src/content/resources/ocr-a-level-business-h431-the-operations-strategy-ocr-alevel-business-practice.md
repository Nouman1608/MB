## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by an operations strategy. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why an operations strategy must be consistent with a business's objectives. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** way in which a business's operations strategy might conflict with its marketing strategy. **[2]**

**4.** A business has a target unit cost of £25.00 and an actual unit cost of £26.50. Calculate the actual cost as a percentage above target. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be part of an operations strategy?

A: The choice of the method of production and the location of a new factory.

B: The setting of the dividend paid to shareholders.

C: The design of the logo.

D: The wording of a job advertisement.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Dunlin Bakery has 12 shops and is losing sales to supermarkets. The directors compare two operations strategies over five years.

Option 1: build a central kitchen, costing £360,000. If it works fully, savings are £95,000 a year (probability 0.7). If it works partly, savings are £40,000 a year (probability 0.3).

Option 2: refit the shop ovens and train staff to bake fresh in store, costing £120,000. If it works fully, extra profit is £38,000 a year (probability 0.9). If it works partly, £20,000 a year (probability 0.1).

**(a)** Calculate the expected annual benefit of each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the expected five-year net benefit of each option. **[3]**

**(c)** Recommend which strategy Dunlin Bakery should adopt. Justify your answer. **[9]**

**7.** Dunlin Bakery adopts Option 2. After a year, it reviews its results.

| Measure | Target | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Waste (percentage of bakes thrown away) | 6.0% | 7.5% |
| Customer satisfaction | 88% | 84% |

**(a)** Calculate by how many percentage points each measure misses its target. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse what the directors should do next. **[6]**

**8.** Evaluate the importance of an operations strategy to a car manufacturer and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An operations strategy is a long-term plan for how the operations function will use the business's resources [1] to support the objectives of the whole business [1]. **[2]**

**2.** If the strategy pulls in a different direction from the objectives, resources are wasted [1]. For example, a business aiming to be the cheapest would fail if its operations strategy focused on expensive, hand-made quality [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Marketing may promise fast delivery or a wide choice of products [1], while operations has chosen a low-cost strategy with large batches and limited variety, so it cannot deliver what marketing has promised [1]. Accept other reasoned conflicts. **[2]**

**4.** (26.50 − 25.00) ÷ 25.00 [1] × 100 = **6%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **A** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Option 1: (0.7 × £95,000) + (0.3 × £40,000) [1] = £66,500 + £12,000 = **£78,500** [1]. Option 2: (0.9 × £38,000) + (0.1 × £20,000) [1] = £34,200 + £2,000 = **£36,200** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Option 1: 5 × £78,500 = £392,500; less £360,000 = **£32,500** [1] [1]. Option 2: 5 × £36,200 = £181,000; less £120,000 = **£61,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Option 2 gives the higher expected net benefit, £61,000 against £32,500 [1], with a much lower investment, so less is at risk [1]. Expected payback is 120,000 ÷ 36,200 = 3.3 years for Option 2 and 360,000 ÷ 78,500 = 4.6 years for Option 1 [1].
Option 1 has a larger potential gain: if fully successful it brings 5 × £95,000 − £360,000 = £115,000, against 5 × £38,000 − £120,000 = £70,000 for Option 2 [1]. If it only partly works it gives 5 × £40,000 − £360,000 = −£160,000, a loss [1].
The probabilities are estimates, and a small change could alter the result [1]. A central kitchen would also change deliveries and stock control, and may reduce the freshness that competes with supermarkets [1].
Option 2 trains staff and fits the aim of offering fresh baking in store, which differs from supermarkets [1]. It may raise staff motivation, although it depends on customers noticing the difference.
Judgement: Dunlin should choose Option 2, because the expected return is higher, the risk is lower and it supports the point of difference with supermarkets; it could add a central kitchen later for the products that do not need fresh baking in store [1]. **[9]**

**7. (a)** Waste: 7.5 − 6.0 = **1.5 percentage points** above target [1]. Satisfaction: 88 − 84 = **4 percentage points** below target [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Both measures are worse than target, so the strategy is not yet delivering [1]. The directors should find the causes before changing the strategy [1]. Waste may be high because staff are still learning to judge how much to bake, or because forecasts of demand are poor [1]; more training or better sales data in each shop could correct this [1]. Satisfaction may be low if products are sold out late in the day, or if bake times are too long [1]. They should allow time and keep measuring, because one year may be too short to judge a change in the way staff work [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Importance to the business: car manufacture needs large investment in plant and long planning times, so decisions on capacity, technology and suppliers are costly to reverse [1]. A strategy makes sure these decisions support each other and the chosen position, such as low cost or premium quality [1]. It also prepares the business for external change, such as new emission rules or a shift to electric cars [1].
Stakeholders: employees depend on investment decisions for job security and training [1]; suppliers depend on the strategy for the volume and standards they must meet [1]; customers benefit from the quality, price and delivery that the strategy sets [1]; the community is affected by jobs, traffic and emissions [1].
Limits: a strategy may be wrong if demand changes, as with the shift to electric vehicles, and it may be hard to change quickly [1].
Judgement: an operations strategy is essential for a car manufacturer because decisions are so large and slow to reverse, so it should be reviewed regularly against external change [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing operations decisions without linking them to the business's objectives.
- Recommending a strategy without calculating its cost or risk.
- Treating cost as the only goal.
- Ignoring the people who must carry out the strategy.
- Not saying what the recommendation depends on.
