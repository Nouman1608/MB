## 6.2.5(a)(i): Organic preparation

- **Heating under reflux** keeps a slow reaction hot for a long time. The vertical condenser is open at the top, and vapour condenses back into the flask.
- **Distillation** removes a volatile liquid. The thermometer bulb sits beside the side arm of the still head.

Water enters the condenser at its lower end. Use an electric heater and anti-bumping granules.

When the product is a solid, the preparation usually ends by making it leave solution: cool in ice, or change the pH so an insoluble form precipitates. You then collect and purify it.

## 6.2.5(a)(ii): Purifying an organic solid

### Filtration under reduced pressure

A Büchner funnel holds a flat filter paper and sits in a side-arm flask joined to a vacuum source such as a water pump. Wet the paper with cold solvent so it seals, then pour in the mixture. Suction pulls the liquid through quickly and leaves the crystals drier than gravity filtration. Rinse them with a few drops of ice-cold solvent to remove clinging solution.

### Recrystallisation

Choose a solvent in which the product is very soluble when hot but only slightly soluble when cold.

1. Add hot solvent bit by bit until the crude solid just dissolves: use the **minimum volume**.
2. Filter off any insoluble bits while hot.
3. Cool slowly, then in ice, so crystals form.
4. Filter under reduced pressure; rinse with a few drops of ice-cold solvent.
5. Dry the crystals, e.g. in a desiccator.

Soluble impurities are present in small amounts, so they never reach saturation and stay in solution. Excess solvent lowers the yield.

### Measuring a melting point

Pack dry, powdered solid into a capillary tube sealed at one end. Heat it in a melting point apparatus, slowly near the expected value, and record the range from first liquid to complete melting.

- A **pure** substance melts sharply, over about 1 °C, close to its known value.
- **Impurities** lower the melting point and spread melting over several degrees.

### Worked example 1: benzoic acid from methyl benzoate

A student heats 5.00 cm³ of methyl benzoate (density 1.09 g cm⁻³, supplied) under reflux with excess aqueous sodium hydroxide. After cooling, she adds dilute hydrochloric acid until the mixture is acidic, and benzoic acid precipitates. After recrystallisation from hot water and drying, 3.62 g of benzoic acid is obtained. Use H = 1.0, O = 16.0 and C = 12.0.

C₆H₅COOCH₃ + NaOH → C₆H₅COONa + CH₃OH
C₆H₅COONa + HCl → C₆H₅COOH + NaCl

1. Reflux is used because alkaline hydrolysis (6.1.3(d)(ii)) is slow and volatile substances must not escape.
2. Sodium benzoate is ionic and stays dissolved; benzoic acid is much less soluble in cold water, so acidifying precipitates it.
3. Mass of ester = 5.00 × 1.09 = 5.45 g. M(C₈H₈O₂) = 136.0 g mol⁻¹, so n = 5.45 ÷ 136.0 = 0.04007 mol.
4. One mole of ester gives one mole of acid. M(C₇H₆O₂) = 122.0 g mol⁻¹, so the theoretical mass = 0.04007 × 122.0 = 4.889 g.
5. Percentage yield = 3.62 ÷ 4.889 × 100 = **74.0%**.

### Worked example 2: reading melting point results

Take the melting point of pure benzoic acid as 122 °C. The crude solid melted from 117 °C to 120 °C. The recrystallised solid melted from 121 °C to 122 °C.

1. The crude sample melts below 122 °C and over 3 °C, so it is impure.
2. The recrystallised sample melts over 1 °C, close to 122 °C, so it is much purer.

## 6.2.5(b): Molecules with several functional groups

You need the Module 4 groups plus these. Treat each group separately, then check whether one reagent attacks two.

| Group | How to spot it | Reactions to predict |
|---|---|---|
| Arene | Benzene ring | Electrophilic substitution: nitration, halogenation, Friedel–Crafts |
| Phenol | OH bonded directly to the ring | Reacts with NaOH, not carbonates; brominated without a halogen carrier |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | Oxidised to acid; reduced by NaBH₄; HCN addition; 2,4-DNP and Tollens' positive |
| Ketone | C=O with a carbon on each side | Reduced by NaBH₄; HCN addition; 2,4-DNP positive, Tollens' negative |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | Acid reactions; esterified; SOCl₂ gives acyl chloride |
| Ester | –COO– between two carbons | Hydrolysed by hot aqueous acid or alkali |
| Acyl chloride | –COCl | Gives esters, acids and amides |
| Amine | N bonded to carbon | Base: forms salts with acids |
| Amide | –CONH– or –CONH₂ | Hydrolysed by acid or alkali |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | Reduced to amine; hydrolysed to acid |

Groups that hydrogen bond (O–H, N–H, COOH) raise melting and boiling points and increase water solubility.

### Worked example 3: a keto acid

Compound K is HOC₆H₄COCH₂CH₂COOH, with the OH and the side chain at positions 1 and 4 of the ring.

**(b)(i) Groups.** K contains a phenol (OH on the ring), a ketone (C=O between a ring carbon and a chain carbon) and a carboxylic acid.

**(b)(ii) Predictions.**

1. **Sodium carbonate solution.** Only the COOH reacts, giving effervescence (CO₂).
2. **Excess sodium hydroxide.** Both acidic groups react: NaOC₆H₄COCH₂CH₂COONa forms.
3. **2,4-DNP.** An orange precipitate shows the carbonyl. **Tollens' reagent** gives no silver mirror, because a ketone is not oxidised.
4. **NaBH₄.** The ketone is reduced to a secondary alcohol, HOC₆H₄CH(OH)CH₂CH₂COOH. The COOH is unchanged. The new CH(OH) carbon is a chiral centre (6.2.2(d)).
5. **Ethanol with concentrated H₂SO₄, heated.** The COOH forms an ethyl ester. The phenol OH is not readily esterified by a carboxylic acid.
6. **Properties.** Two O–H groups hydrogen bond, so K is a solid with a fairly high melting point.

## 6.2.5(c): Multi-stage synthetic routes

You may need to link any functional groups in the specification, in several steps. A paper may supply an unfamiliar reaction; use it as stated.

### Module 6 reaction toolkit

Combine this with the Module 4 table in the 4.2.3 guide.

| From | To | Reagents and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Arene | Nitroarene | Concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ |
| Arene | Haloarene | Cl₂ or Br₂ with a halogen carrier, e.g. AlCl₃ or FeBr₃ |
| Arene | Alkylarene or aromatic ketone | Haloalkane or acyl chloride with a halogen carrier (Friedel–Crafts) |
| Phenol | 2,4,6-tribromophenol; 2- and 4-nitrophenol | Br₂; dilute HNO₃ |
| Nitroarene | Aromatic amine | Tin and concentrated HCl, then aqueous alkali |
| Haloalkane | Amine | Excess ethanolic NH₃ (an amine gives a secondary or tertiary amine) |
| Haloalkane | Nitrile (one more carbon) | CN⁻ (e.g. KCN) in ethanol |
| Aldehyde or ketone | Hydroxynitrile (one more carbon) | NaCN(aq) and H⁺(aq) |
| Aldehyde or ketone | Alcohol | NaBH₄ |
| Aldehyde | Carboxylic acid | K₂Cr₂O₇ and H₂SO₄, heat under reflux |
| Nitrile | Amine | H₂ with Ni catalyst |
| Nitrile | Carboxylic acid | Dilute aqueous acid, heat under reflux |
| Carboxylic acid | Ester | Alcohol with concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst |
| Acid anhydride | Ester | Alcohol |
| Ester | Acid + alcohol, or carboxylate + alcohol | Hot aqueous acid, or hot aqueous alkali |
| Carboxylic acid | Acyl chloride | SOCl₂ |
| Acyl chloride | Ester, acid, primary or secondary amide | Alcohol or phenol; water; NH₃; primary amine |

### Planning method

1. Name the groups in the start and the target. Count the carbons. One extra carbon means a nitrile or hydroxynitrile step; a new carbon on a ring means Friedel–Crafts.
2. Work backwards from the target, one step at a time, until you reach something you can make from the start.
3. On a ring, place substituents in an order that the directing effects allow (6.1.1(k)–(l)). Introduce groups that would react with a later reagent, such as NH₂, as late as possible.
4. For each step give the reagent, the conditions and the intermediate's structure.

### Worked example 4: pent-1-ene to 2-methylpentan-1-amine

The target, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂NH₂, has six carbons; the start has five. A CH₂NH₂ group at the end of a branch comes from reducing a nitrile.

1. HBr adds to pent-1-ene. By Markownikoff's rule the major product is **2-bromopentane**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CHBrCH₃.
2. KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux. CN⁻ substitutes for Br⁻, giving **2-methylpentanenitrile**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH(CH₃)CN. This adds the sixth carbon.
3. H₂ with a nickel catalyst reduces C≡N to CH₂NH₂: **2-methylpentan-1-amine**.

Ammonia on 2-bromopentane would give a five-carbon amine.

### Worked example 5: benzene to 1-(3-aminophenyl)ethanone

Information supplied: the COCH₃ group is 3-directing. The target has NH₂ and COCH₃ at positions 1 and 3 of the ring.

1. Ethanoyl chloride, CH₃COCl, with AlCl₃ (Friedel–Crafts acylation) gives **phenylethanone**, C₆H₅COCH₃.
2. Concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄. The COCH₃ group directs the nitro group to position 3: **1-(3-nitrophenyl)ethanone**.
3. Tin and concentrated HCl, then aqueous NaOH, reduce NO₂ to NH₂: **1-(3-aminophenyl)ethanone**.

The amine must come last. NH₂ is 2- and 4-directing, and it would react with ethanoyl chloride to form an amide.

### Worked example 6: phenyl propanoate from propan-1-ol and phenol

1. Excess K₂Cr₂O₇ and H₂SO₄, heat under reflux, oxidises propan-1-ol to **propanoic acid**.
2. SOCl₂ converts the acid to **propanoyl chloride**, CH₃CH₂COCl.
3. Phenol reacts with propanoyl chloride: C₆H₅OH + CH₃CH₂COCl → CH₃CH₂COOC₆H₅ + HCl.

Step 2 is needed because phenol is not readily esterified by a carboxylic acid.

## Common errors

- Using a large volume of hot solvent, so product stays dissolved.
- Washing crystals with solvent that is not ice-cold.
- Calling an impure sample's melting point "higher" or "sharper".
- Using ammonia when the target amine needs an extra carbon.
- Esterifying a phenol with a carboxylic acid.
- Making NH₂ before a later acyl chloride or substitution step.
- Leaving out conditions such as reflux, catalyst or solvent.
