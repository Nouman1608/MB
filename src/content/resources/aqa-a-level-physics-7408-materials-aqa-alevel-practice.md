These questions cover section 3.4.2 Materials (3.4.2.1 Bulk properties of solids and 3.4.2.2 The Young modulus) of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), including Required practical 4. Section A has short questions; Section B has structured questions. Learn the content first with the [Materials study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-materials-aqa-alevel/) and the [Materials revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-materials-aqa-alevel-revision-notes/). Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². Material values given in the questions are illustrative.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A brass block has mass 1.70 kg and volume 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. Define density and calculate the density of brass. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** State Hooke's law. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit. **[2]**

**3.** A spring's length increases from 15.0 cm to 19.0 cm when a 3.2 N load is added.

**(a)** Calculate the spring constant. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the elastic strain energy stored. **[1]**

**4.** Define tensile stress and tensile strain. **[2]**

**5.** Describe how the stress–strain graph of a brittle material differs from that of a ductile metal. **[2]**

**6.** State the unit of the Young modulus and explain why tensile strain has no unit. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A steel wire of length 3.0 m and diameter 0.80 mm supports a load of 120 N. The Young modulus of the steel is 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa.

**(a)** Calculate the extension of the wire. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the elastic strain energy stored. **[1]**

**8.** A catapult's elastic obeys Hooke's law. It is pulled back 0.15 m with a final force of 60 N and releases a 0.050 kg stone.

**(a)** Calculate the energy stored in the elastic. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum possible launch speed of the stone. **[2]**
**(c)** Give one reason why the actual launch speed is lower. **[1]**

**9.** In Required practical 4, a student loads a wire of length 2.00 m and diameter 0.40 mm.

| Load / N | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extension / mm | 1.22 | 2.45 | 3.67 | 4.90 |

**(a)** Show that the wire obeys Hooke's law over this range. **[1]**
**(b)** Use the data to calculate the Young modulus of the wire. **[3]**
**(c)** The diameter was measured with a micrometer reading to ±0.01 mm. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the cross-sectional area. **[2]**

**10.** A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit and then the load is removed. Describe the force–extension graph for loading and unloading, and explain what happens to the energy. **[3]**

**11.** A steel cable must support 12 kN. The breaking stress of the steel is 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa. Calculate the minimum diameter of the cable. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how the plastic deformation of a car's crumple zone reduces the risk of injury in a collision. **[3]**

**13.** Wires X and Y are made of the same metal. Y is twice as long as X and has half the diameter. They carry equal loads. Calculate the ratio extension of Y ÷ extension of X. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Density is mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V [1]. ρ = 1.70 / 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = **8500 kg m⁻³** [1].

**2. (a)** The extension is directly proportional to the force applied, up to the limit of proportionality [1].
**(b)** The limit of proportionality is the point beyond which force and extension are no longer proportional [1]. The elastic limit is the largest force after which the material still returns to its original length when the force is removed [1].

**3. (a)** Extension = 4.0 cm = 0.040 m [1]; k = 3.2 / 0.040 = **80 N m⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** E = ½FΔL = ½ × 3.2 × 0.040 = **0.064 J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the extension, not the new length of 19.0 cm.

**4.** Tensile stress = tensile force ÷ cross-sectional area [1]. Tensile strain = extension ÷ original length [1].

**5.** A brittle material's graph is a straight line up to fracture, with no plastic region [1]; a ductile metal's graph has a straight section followed by a large region of plastic deformation before fracture [1].

**6.** **Pa** (N m⁻²) [1]. Strain is a length divided by a length, so the units cancel [1].

**7. (a)** A = π(0.40 × 10⁻³)² = 5.03 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]. Stress = 120 / 5.03 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.39 × 10⁸ Pa [1]. Strain = 2.39 × 10⁸ / 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 1.19 × 10⁻³ [1]. ΔL = 1.19 × 10⁻³ × 3.0 = **3.6 × 10⁻³ m** (3.6 mm) [1].
**(b)** ½FΔL = ½ × 120 × 3.58 × 10⁻³ = **0.21 J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The radius is 0.40 mm, half the diameter. Using 0.80 mm as the radius makes the area four times too large.

**8. (a)** E = ½FΔL = ½ × 60 × 0.15 = **4.5 J** [1].
**(b)** ½mv² = 4.5 [1], so v = √(2 × 4.5 / 0.050) = **13 m s⁻¹** (13.4) [1].
**(c)** Any one: some energy heats the elastic or is not returned (the elastic is not perfectly elastic); the elastic itself gains kinetic energy; air resistance [1].

**9. (a)** Extension rises by about 1.22 mm for each 10 N, so it is proportional to the load (the ratio F/ΔL is constant) [1].
**(b)** k = 40 / 4.90 × 10⁻³ = 8.16 × 10³ N m⁻¹ [1]. A = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]. E = kL/A = 8.16 × 10³ × 2.00 / 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = **1.3 × 10¹¹ Pa** [1].
**(c)** Percentage uncertainty in d = 0.01/0.40 × 100 = 2.5% [1]; area depends on d², so **5%** [1].

**10.** The loading graph is a straight line, then curves beyond the limit of proportionality as the wire deforms plastically [1]. The unloading line is straight and parallel to the original straight section, meeting the extension axis at a permanent extension [1]. The area under the unloading line (energy returned) is less than the area under the loading curve (work done); the difference has gone into permanently deforming and heating the wire [1].

**11.** A = F/stress = 12 000 / 4.0 × 10⁸ = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ m² [1]. d = √(4A/π) [1] = **6.2 × 10⁻³ m** (6.2 mm) [1].

**12.** The crumple zone deforms plastically, so the car's kinetic energy is transferred as work done in permanently deforming the material rather than being returned elastically [1]. The deformation takes place over a longer distance and time [1], so the force on the occupants, for the same change in momentum, is smaller [1].

**13.** ΔL = FL/(AE). L doubles, giving × 2 [1]; halving d quarters A, giving × 4; ratio = **8** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a new length instead of an extension.
- Treating the diameter as the radius.
- Forgetting to square d when finding the percentage uncertainty in A.
- Using ½FΔL on a curved section of a graph.
- Describing the unloading line of a plastically deformed wire as retracing the loading curve.
- Explaining crumple zones without mentioning energy absorbed by plastic deformation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you find the Young modulus from a force–extension graph?**
Find the gradient F/ΔL of the straight section, then multiply by the original length and divide by the cross-sectional area: E = (F/ΔL) × L/A.

**Why does halving a wire's diameter quadruple its extension?**
Extension is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area, and area depends on the square of the diameter. Halving the diameter quarters the area, so the extension is four times larger for the same load.
