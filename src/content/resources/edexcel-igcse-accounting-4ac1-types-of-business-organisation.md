This guide covers section 1.1, Types of business organisation, in Topic 1 (The accounting environment) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. Topic 1 is assessed in Paper 1. All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign.

The [types of business organisation and accounting concepts overview](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-types-of-business-organisation-and-accounting-concepts/) lists the characteristics and the stakeholder groups. This page goes further into 1.1 alone: comparing organisations by the questions an examiner asks, a sole-trader-to-partnership decision worked with figures, and stakeholders whose interests conflict. Condensed notes are in the [types of business organisation revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-types-of-business-organisation-revision-notes/) and you can practise with the [types of business organisation practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-types-of-business-organisation-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| 1.1 a | Explain the characteristics of public sector organisations, private sector organisations, sole traders and partnerships |
| 1.1 b | Explain the connection between stakeholders and a business, identifying the stakeholders' use of the business's financial statements |

The specification names the organisations and the task but does not list the characteristics or the stakeholder groups. The characteristics below are standard accounting knowledge used to answer it. "Explain" means each point needs a reason or a consequence, not just a label.

## Comparing organisations by five questions

Examiners test characteristics by asking about ownership, aim, finance, liability and control. Answering each in turn gives a complete comparison.

| Question | Public sector | Sole trader | Partnership |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns it? | National or local government, on behalf of the public | One person | Two or more partners |
| What is the main aim? | To provide a service, such as health, education or refuse collection | Usually profit for the owner | Usually profit for the partners |
| Where does the money come from? | Mainly taxes, plus some charges and government borrowing | The owner's capital, retained profit, loans | The partners' capital, retained profit, loans |
| Who bears the debts? | The government | The owner, with unlimited liability | The partners, who are personally responsible for the firm's debts |
| Who controls it and who is it accountable to? | Managers appointed by government; accountable to government and taxpayers | The owner alone | The partners together, under their partnership agreement |

**Private sector** means every organisation owned by individuals rather than government. Sole traders and partnerships are the two private sector forms the specification names.

### Sole traders in more depth

- **Unlimited liability.** In law, the owner and the business are the same person, so a business debt is the owner's personal debt.
- **Business entity in the accounts.** Even so, the accounting records treat the business separately. The owner's money paid in is **capital**; money and goods taken out are **drawings**, not expenses.
- **Continuity.** The business depends on one person: illness or death can end it.
- **Decisions** are quick, but rest on one person's skills and judgement.
- **Privacy.** A sole trader's financial statements are mainly for the owner, the tax authority and any lender.

### Partnerships in more depth

- **Partnership agreement.** Partners usually agree in writing how profits and losses are shared, how much capital each contributes, and the rules for drawings. Disputes are fewer when this is clear.
- **More capital and skills.** Each partner brings money and expertise, so the firm can grow faster than one owner could.
- **Shared liability and trust.** Each partner can be responsible for debts arising from decisions made by the others.
- **Shared profit.** Every dollar of profit is split, so a partner's share can fall even when the firm grows.
- **Continuity.** When a partner leaves or dies, the partnership in its old form ends and must be re-formed.

## Worked example: should a sole trader take a partner?

Imani Okafor runs Okafor Garden Design as a sole trader. Profit for last year was 36,000. Her friend Tomasz Wieczorek, a qualified landscape architect, offers to join as a partner, bringing 20,000 of capital. They forecast profit of 54,000 a year, shared 2 : 1 in Imani's favour.

- Imani's share = 54,000 × 2/3 = **36,000**, the same as she earns now.
- Tomasz's share = 54,000 × 1/3 = **18,000**.
- The firm gains 20,000 of capital and Tomasz's design skills.

**Evaluation.** In favour: Imani keeps her income while sharing the workload, the extra capital could buy equipment for larger contracts, and a second person provides cover during illness. Against: she now shares decisions, she becomes responsible for debts from Tomasz's decisions, and if the 54,000 forecast is not met her income falls; at the old profit of 36,000 her two-thirds share would be only 24,000. **Judgement:** the partnership suits Imani if the forecast is realistic and a written agreement sets out profit shares, capital and drawings.

This shape of answer (calculation, points for and against, judgement) is what an "evaluate" or "advise" question in Section B expects.

## Stakeholders and the financial statements (1.1 b)

A **stakeholder** is any person or group with an interest in the business, who is affected by it and can affect it. **Internal** stakeholders are inside the business (owners, partners, managers, employees); **external** stakeholders are outside it (lenders, suppliers, customers, government, the local community).

The link to accounting is that each stakeholder uses the **statement of profit or loss** or the **statement of financial position** for a different decision:

| Stakeholder | Statement used | Item they look at | Decision it supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| Partner | Statement of profit or loss | Profit for the year and their share of it | Whether to stay, or to draw less and reinvest |
| Prospective partner | Both | Profit trend; capital and liabilities | Whether to join and how much capital to put in |
| Bank | Both | Profit; non-current assets; existing loans | Whether to lend and what security to ask for |
| Supplier | Statement of financial position | Bank balance and receivables against payables | Whether to give credit, and how much |
| Employee | Statement of profit or loss | Profit | Job security; whether a pay rise is affordable |
| Tax authority | Statement of profit or loss | Profit | Tax due |
| Taxpayers (public sector) | Statements of the organisation | Spending against funding | Whether money is used well |

**Conflicting interests.** Stakeholders can want opposite things from the same figures. Suppose the Okafor partnership makes profit of 54,000 and the partners plan drawings of 50,000. The partners gain income now. A bank considering a loan would prefer profit to be kept in the business, because drawings reduce capital and the cash available to repay it. Employees hoping for a pay rise would also prefer profit retained. Recognising a conflict like this, and naming the figure behind it, is how 1.1 b moves from description to explanation.

## Common mistakes

- Saying sole traders have limited liability.
- Calling a sole trader "public" because it serves the public.
- Treating "private sector" as meaning only large companies.
- Naming a stakeholder without the statement, the figure or the decision.
- Saying the business entity concept makes the owner legally separate: it applies to the records only.
- Comparing sole traders and partnerships with one-sided points and no judgement when the question says "evaluate".

## Exam technique

The specification's assessment objectives reward knowledge (AO1), application to the case (AO2) and analysis and evaluation with reasoned explanation (AO3). For Topic 1 questions:

- **Explain**: point, then "because" or "so", then the effect on the owner or the business.
- **Identify**: name the stakeholder or characteristic only.
- **Evaluate** or **advise**: use the figures given, make points on both sides, then state a judgement with a condition ("if the forecast is realistic...").
- Always use the case: name the business and its people, and quote its figures.
