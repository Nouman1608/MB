This guide covers **3.1.5 Safety in public transport** (statements a to e) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The [momentum, collisions and road safety guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-momentum-collisions-and-safety-in-road-transport/) lists each statement; this page goes deeper into the calculations behind stopping a vehicle, why braking distance grows with the square of speed, and how to analyse stopping-distance data. Condensed notes are in the [Safety in public transport revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [Safety in public transport practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-safety-in-public-transport-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | At steady speed in a straight line, resistive forces balance the driving force |
| b | Greater speed needs a greater braking force to stop in a given distance; greater braking force gives greater deceleration; large decelerations can overheat brakes or cause loss of control; for a given braking force, greater speed means greater stopping distance |
| c | Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance; reaction time is affected by tiredness, distractions, drugs and alcohol |
| d | Braking: work done by friction between brakes and wheel reduces the kinetic energy of the vehicle and heats the brakes |
| e | Braking distance is affected by adverse road and weather conditions (wet or icy) and poor condition of the brakes or tyres |

The examples here use buses and trams, but the physics is the same for any road vehicle.

## Steady speed: balanced forces

A bus moving at a steady speed in a straight line has zero resultant force (Newton's First Law, from section 3.1.3). The forward driving force equals the total of the resistive forces: air resistance and friction. If the driver presses harder on the accelerator, the driving force becomes larger than the resistive forces and the bus speeds up. As it goes faster, air resistance grows until the forces balance again at a new, higher steady speed.

## Braking force and deceleration

Once the brakes are applied, the braking force is the main resultant force, acting backwards. From F = m × a:

```
deceleration = braking force / mass
```

**Worked example 1: a city bus.** A bus of mass 12 000 kg travels at 15 m/s. The driver applies a braking force of 30 000 N.

```
deceleration = 30 000 / 12 000 = 2.5 m/s^2
time to stop = change in velocity / deceleration = 15 / 2.5 = 6.0 s
```

A heavier vehicle needs a larger braking force for the same deceleration. A full bus therefore needs more braking force than an empty one to stop in the same distance.

### Why large decelerations are dangerous

Statement b names two risks:

- **Overheating brakes.** A large braking force does a lot of work in a short time, so the brakes heat up quickly. Very hot brakes become less effective.
- **Loss of control.** If the braking force needed is greater than the grip between tyres and road, the wheels lock and skid, and the driver can no longer steer.

On public transport there is a third, closely linked concern: standing passengers. When a bus decelerates sharply, a passenger who is not holding on continues forwards at the original speed until a force acts on them, so they can fall. This is the same First Law reasoning in a new context.

## Braking as work done

Statement d links braking to energy. The friction force between the brake pads and the wheel does work on the vehicle, and that work removes the vehicle's kinetic energy. The energy is transferred to the brakes by heating, and then to the surroundings.

Using the energy equations from section 3.2.1:

```
work done by braking force = braking force x braking distance
kinetic energy = 1/2 x m x v^2
```

If the braking force is constant and does all the work of stopping the vehicle:

```
braking force x braking distance = 1/2 x m x v^2
```

**Worked example 2: braking distance from energy.** Continue with the 12 000 kg bus at 15 m/s and a braking force of 30 000 N.

```
kinetic energy = 0.5 x 12 000 x 15^2 = 1 350 000 J
braking distance = 1 350 000 / 30 000 = 45 m
```

The 1 350 000 J is transferred mainly to the brakes, which is why their temperature rises.

## Why braking distance depends on speed squared

Kinetic energy depends on v². For the same braking force, the braking distance is proportional to the kinetic energy, so:

| Speed multiplied by | Kinetic energy multiplied by | Braking distance multiplied by |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 4 | 4 |
| 3 | 9 | 9 |

This is the physics behind statement b's note: for a given braking force, a greater speed gives a greater stopping distance. To stop a faster vehicle in the **same** distance, the braking force must be larger, which raises the risks of overheating and skidding.

## Thinking distance and reaction time

Thinking distance is the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time, before the brakes act. The vehicle is still at its original speed, so:

```
thinking distance = speed x reaction time
```

Thinking distance is **directly proportional** to speed. Tiredness, distractions (such as a phone), drugs and alcohol all lengthen reaction time, so they increase thinking distance but not braking distance.

**Worked example 3: full stopping distance.** The bus driver has a reaction time of 0.80 s.

| Speed | Thinking distance | Braking distance | Stopping distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 m/s | 15 × 0.80 = 12 m | 45 m | 57 m |
| 30 m/s | 30 × 0.80 = 24 m | 45 × 4 = 180 m | 204 m |

Doubling the speed doubles the thinking distance but quadruples the braking distance, so the stopping distance more than triples here.

## Road conditions and vehicle condition

Braking distance depends on the grip between tyres and road. The specification limits "adverse conditions" to wet or icy roads, and "poor condition" to the brakes or tyres.

| Factor | Effect on braking distance | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Wet road | Increases | Water reduces the friction between tyres and road, so the maximum braking force before skidding is smaller |
| Icy road | Increases greatly | Ice gives very little friction |
| Worn tyres | Increases | Less tread means less grip, especially in the wet |
| Worn brakes | Increases | The brakes produce a smaller braking force |

Distinguish the two groups clearly: driver factors change **thinking** distance; road and vehicle factors change **braking** distance; speed changes both.

## Reading stopping-distance data

Questions often give a table or graph and ask what it shows. Check:

- Does thinking distance double when speed doubles? Then it is directly proportional to speed.
- Does braking distance quadruple when speed doubles? Then it depends on speed squared.
- Divide thinking distance by speed to find the reaction time used to produce the data.

## Common mistakes

- Saying alcohol increases braking distance. It increases reaction time, so thinking distance.
- Saying wet roads increase thinking distance.
- Assuming braking distance doubles when speed doubles.
- Forgetting to square the speed in ½ m v².
- Saying the brakes "use up" energy. The kinetic energy is transferred to the brakes by heating.
- Using F = m × a with the driving force instead of the resultant (braking) force.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** why stopping distance increases with speed: refer to both thinking distance (proportional to speed) and braking distance (more kinetic energy to remove).
- **Describe** the energy transfer when braking: kinetic energy of the vehicle, work done by friction, thermal energy of the brakes, then the surroundings.
- **Suggest** a reason for a set of data: link the change to one named factor, such as an icy road.
- **Calculate**: show each stage; braking questions often need two or three equations in sequence.
