## Recombinant human proteins (19.2.1)

A **recombinant human protein** is made by organisms (bacteria, yeast or cultured mammalian cells) that have been given the human gene. The cells are grown in large fermenters and the protein is purified.

### Insulin

People with type 1 diabetes cannot make insulin and need regular injections. Before recombinant insulin, insulin was extracted from the pancreases of slaughtered pigs and cattle. These animal insulins differ from human insulin by one or a few amino acids.

**Advantages of recombinant human insulin**

- Same amino acid sequence as human insulin, so **less likely to trigger an immune response** and works the same way as the body's own.
- **Large, reliable supply** that does not depend on the number of animals slaughtered.
- **Consistent purity**, because it is made under controlled conditions.
- Acceptable to people who object to animal products for religious or ethical reasons.

### Factor VIII

Factor VIII is a blood-clotting protein. People with haemophilia A have a faulty allele of its gene on the X chromosome, so their blood clots slowly. They are treated with injections of factor VIII.

Factor VIII used to be purified from donated blood plasma pooled from many donors. In the 1970s and 1980s, before donated blood was screened effectively, many people with haemophilia were infected with HIV and hepatitis C this way.

**Advantages of recombinant factor VIII**

- **No risk of transmitting blood-borne pathogens** from donors.
- Supply does not depend on blood donations.

Factor VIII is a large glycoprotein that must be modified after translation. Bacteria cannot carry out these modifications, so it is made in **cultured mammalian cells** such as hamster cell lines.

### Adenosine deaminase (ADA)

ADA is an enzyme that breaks down a toxic product of nucleotide metabolism. Without functional ADA, the product builds up and kills developing **lymphocytes**, so the person has almost no working T cells or B cells: a form of **severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)**.

**Advantages of recombinant ADA**

- Can be injected regularly as **enzyme replacement therapy**, restoring some immune function.
- Earlier enzyme replacement used ADA obtained from cattle; a recombinant source removes dependence on animal tissue and gives a consistent product.

Enzyme replacement treats the symptoms, not the cause, so injections continue for life. That is one reason SCID was an early target for gene therapy.

## Genetic screening (19.2.2)

**Genetic screening** is testing a person's DNA for alleles associated with a genetic disease. It can be done on adults, on embryos created by IVF (before implantation), on a fetus during pregnancy, or on newborn babies.

| Condition | Gene and inheritance | Advantages of screening |
|---|---|---|
| Breast cancer | Certain alleles of **BRCA1** and **BRCA2**, tumour suppressor genes involved in repairing DNA. Inheriting one faulty allele raises the risk of breast (and ovarian) cancer | Women with a faulty allele can have more frequent checks (e.g. mammograms or MRI) so tumours are found early; they can choose preventive surgery; relatives can be tested |
| Huntington's disease | **Dominant** allele of the HTT gene with an expanded number of CAG repeats; symptoms usually begin in middle age, often after the person has had children | Person can plan their life and make informed decisions about having children; can choose embryo testing with IVF; ends uncertainty for those who test negative |
| Cystic fibrosis | **Recessive** alleles of the CFTR gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel protein; a common allele is a deletion of three bases that removes one amino acid | Carrier testing lets couples know their risk (1 in 4 for each child if both are carriers); embryo or fetal testing; newborn screening allows early treatment such as physiotherapy and antibiotics |

**Worked example.** *Both parents are carriers of a cystic fibrosis allele (Ff). Explain why screening an IVF embryo could be offered.*

- Each child has a 1 in 4 chance of being ff, 1 in 2 of being Ff (a carrier), and 1 in 4 of being FF.
- One cell is removed from each early embryo and its DNA tested.
- Embryos without the ff genotype can be chosen for implantation, so the child will not have cystic fibrosis.

## Gene therapy (19.2.3)

**Gene therapy** treats a genetic disease by inserting a functional (normal) allele into the person's cells. It is used for **recessive** conditions caused by a missing protein, because adding one working allele restores the protein.

The allele is carried by a **vector**:

| Vector | Notes |
|---|---|
| Retrovirus or lentivirus | Inserts the allele into the host's chromosomes, so it is passed to daughter cells; risk of inserting into or beside another gene |
| Adeno-associated virus (AAV) | Small; usually does not insert into chromosomes; persists in non-dividing cells; causes little immune response |
| Liposome | Lipid sphere that fuses with cell membranes; no virus, but less efficient |

### SCID

1. **Bone marrow stem cells** (or T cells) are removed from the patient.
2. The normal **ADA allele** is inserted into them using a **retrovirus** (or lentivirus) vector.
3. The modified cells are grown and returned to the patient's bone marrow.
4. The stem cells divide to give lymphocytes that make ADA, restoring immune function.

**Why stem cells?** Mature T cells eventually die, so treatment using them must be repeated. Stem cells keep dividing, so one treatment can last longer.

**Risk:** in early trials for a form of SCID, the vector inserted near a gene that controls cell division in some children, who then developed leukaemia. Newer vectors are designed to reduce this risk.

### Inherited eye diseases

Some forms of inherited blindness are caused by recessive alleles of genes needed by cells of the retina. A harmless vector, commonly an AAV, carrying the normal allele is injected directly under the retina.

The eye suits gene therapy because:

- it is small and enclosed, so a small dose reaches the target cells;
- retinal cells do not divide, so the added allele is not diluted out;
- the eye triggers relatively weak immune responses;
- one eye can be treated and the other compared.

### Somatic and germ-line therapy

All approved gene therapies alter **somatic** (body) cells: the change is not inherited by the patient's children. Altering **germ-line** cells (gametes or early embryos) would pass the change to future generations and is prohibited in many countries.

## Social and ethical considerations (19.2.4)

"Discuss" needs points on both sides. A useful plan: benefit → concern → how the concern might be reduced.

| Issue | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge of risk | Early diagnosis, prevention, planning | Anxiety, especially for incurable conditions such as Huntington's disease |
| Family | Relatives can be warned and tested | Results reveal information about relatives who may not want to know |
| Discrimination | — | Employers or insurers might use results unfairly |
| Embryo selection | Prevents a child inheriting a serious condition | Embryos are discarded; fears of selecting for non-medical traits |
| Gene therapy safety | Can cure rather than manage | Immune reactions to vectors; risk of cancer from insertion |
| Cost and access | Long-term savings if cured | Very expensive; unequal access between and within countries |
| Germ-line change | Could remove a disease from a family line | Future generations cannot consent; unknown long-term effects |

## Common mistakes

- Saying recombinant insulin is "identical" because it comes from humans. It comes from microorganisms carrying the human gene.
- Forgetting why animal or donor-derived proteins were a problem (immune response; infection).
- Calling Huntington's disease recessive, or cystic fibrosis dominant.
- Saying gene therapy "removes the faulty allele". It adds a functional allele.
- Claiming somatic gene therapy affects children. It does not.
- One-sided ethics answers.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the advantages**: compare with the older source (animal pancreas, donated blood, cattle enzyme).
- **Outline**: one condition, one gene, one advantage per point.
- **Outline how … treated**: vector → target cells → allele expressed → protein restored.
- **Discuss**: at least two points for and two against, ideally with a named example.
