## Questions

**1.** Define economic growth. **[2]**

**2.** Country Dell's nominal GDP is 2,400 billion currency units in year 1 and 2,568 billion in year 2. Its price index rises from 100 to 105.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage growth in nominal GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate real GDP in year 2, at year 1 prices. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate real growth. **[2]**

**3.** State the stage of the economic cycle shown in each case.

**(a)** Growth slows from 4% to 1% but stays positive. **[1]**
**(b)** Growth moves from -2% to +1.5% while unemployment is still high. **[1]**
**(c)** Output falls for two quarters in a row. **[1]**
**(d)** Growth is well above trend and firms cannot find enough workers. **[1]**

**4.** Explain how a boom is likely to affect inflation and unemployment. **[4]**

**5.** Country Fenn has real GDP of 3,000 billion and 60 million people. A year later real GDP is 3,150 billion and the population is 61.5 million.

**(a)** Calculate GDP per head in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage growth in GDP per head, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**
**(c)** State one reason why GDP per head is more useful than total GDP. **[1]**

**6.** Explain two limitations of GDP as a measure of economic growth. **[4]**

**7.** Country Gale's real GDP falls from 500 billion to 485 billion in year 1 and then to 480 billion in year 2.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in year 1. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall over the two years. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why unemployment is likely to be high in a recession. **[2]**

**8.** In Country Haze, output rises 5% while output per worker rises 2%. Employment was 8.0 million.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in employment, to 2 decimal places. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why growth may not create many jobs. **[2]**

**9.** Five households in Country Ivor have these incomes (currency units) before and after a period of growth.

| | H1 | H2 | H3 | H4 | H5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 15 | 25 | 40 | 50 | 120 |
| After | 15 | 25 | 43 | 55 | 152 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean income before and after, and the percentage rise. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what the table suggests about using average income to judge living standards and poverty. **[3]**

**10.** In Country Jolt, output rises 6% and emissions per unit of output fall 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in total emissions, to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way growth can harm the environment and one way it can help. **[4]**

**11.** A frontier shows that Country Kest can make at most 80 units of consumer goods or 40 units of capital goods. After growth the figures are 88 and 44.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in productive potential. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how this differs from growth that happens when unemployed resources are put to work. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the statement: "Economic growth always raises standards of living and reduces poverty." **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An increase in the output of goods and services [1] over time, measured by a rise in real GDP [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition is stronger when it contains both ideas, the rise in output and the time period. A bare "GDP going up" leaves out the real adjustment.

**2. (a)** (2,568 - 2,400) / 2,400 × 100 [1] = **7%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 2,568 / 1.05 [1] = **2,445.71 billion** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** (2,445.71 - 2,400) / 2,400 × 100 [1] = **1.9%** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each step of the working. State clearly which figure is real and which is nominal, and avoid reporting the 7% as growth in output.

**3. (a)** **Downturn.** [1] **(b)** **Recovery.** [1] **(c)** **Recession.** [1] **(d)** **Boom.** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the exact stage names from the specification. In (a), the word "slows" is the clue that growth is still positive.

**4.** Spending is high in a boom [1], so demand tends to outrun what firms can produce and prices rise, which raises inflation [1]. Firms hire more workers to meet demand [1], so unemployment falls to a low level and labour may become scarce [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A sound explanation starts with a fact (inflation rises, unemployment falls) and then adds the reason. Give both variables, not just one.

**5. (a)** Year 1: 3,000 billion / 60 million = **50,000** [1]. Year 2: 3,150 billion / 61.5 million = **51,220** (51,219.5) [1] **[2]**
**(b)** (51,219.5 - 50,000) / 50,000 × 100 [1] = **2.44%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** It shows output per person, so it allows for population changes [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not subtract the percentage growth figures (5% - 2.5% = 2.5%). Compute the per-head amounts and then the change, and keep enough decimal places until the last step.

**6.** Any two limitations, each named and developed. For example: GDP ignores unrecorded work, so output is understated [1]; the cash economy and unpaid work therefore go uncounted [1]. GDP is an average and hides inequality [1], so many people may not gain from a rise [1]. Also acceptable: inflation, pollution, population, quality of life. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a limitation is not enough. Say why it distorts GDP or why it matters for living standards.

**7. (a)** (485 - 500) / 500 × 100 [1] = **-3.0%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** (480 - 500) / 500 × 100 [1] = **-4.0%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Output falls so firms need fewer workers [1], and they cut jobs or stop hiring, so unemployment rises [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The two-year fall is measured against the starting value of 500, not by adding -3.0% and -1.03%. Include the sign in a decrease.

**8. (a)** Employment change = 1.05 / 1.02 - 1 [1] = 1.0294 - 1 [1] = **2.94%** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Output may rise because each worker produces more, so extra workers are not needed [1]. Firms may replace workers with machines, so output rises while employment does not [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A rough answer of 3% (5% - 2%) is close, but the question asks for 2 decimal places, so show the ratio method.

**9. (a)** Before: 250 / 5 = **50** [1]. After: 290 / 5 = **58** [1]. Rise = 8 / 50 × 100 = **16%** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** The mean rose 16% [1], but H1 and H2 gained nothing [1], and H5's share of income rose from 48% to 52.4%, so the average overstates the gain for typical households and may hide rising relative poverty [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the table. A comparison of the mean with what happened to the lowest incomes is more convincing than a general claim about inequality.

**10. (a)** 1.06 × 0.97 [1] = 1.0282 [1], a rise of **2.8%** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Harm: more production uses up resources [1] and creates pollution and waste [1]. Help: higher income lets a country afford cleaner technology [1] and stricter protection of the environment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Include both directions, and link each to growth. The calculation shows that emissions can still rise even when output is cleaner per unit.

**11. (a)** (88 - 80) / 80 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Higher potential means the maximum possible output has risen, the frontier shifting outward [1]. Using unemployed resources moves the economy from inside the frontier towards it [1], and the frontier itself does not move [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the two kinds of change and say what each does to the frontier. Mention that the frontier itself does not move in the second case.

**12.** For: real output per head rises [1], more jobs reduce unemployment and poverty [1], and higher tax revenue pays for health and education [1]. Against: the gains may go mainly to high earners, so poverty may fall little or relative poverty rise [1]. Nominal growth or population growth may leave living standards unchanged [1]. Pollution and resource use may harm health [1]. Long hours and unpaid work are missed by GDP [1]. Judgement: growth is most likely to help when it is real, exceeds population growth and is shared [1], so "always" is too strong, and the answer depends on how the gains are distributed and the time period considered [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" asks for a supported conclusion. Weigh the points and say which factor your judgement depends on, rather than listing advantages and disadvantages side by side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting nominal growth as real growth.
- Subtracting percentages instead of using ratios.
- Confusing a downturn with a recession.
- Describing the effect on growth but not on inflation or unemployment.
- Naming a limitation of GDP without explaining the distortion.
- Saying unemployment falls straight away in a recovery.
- Forgetting the base for a percentage fall.
- Concluding without weighing the points in an evaluation.
