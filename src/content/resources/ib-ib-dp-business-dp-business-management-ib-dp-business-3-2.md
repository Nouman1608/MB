This guide goes deeper into section 3.2 (Sources of finance) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The section applies to SL and HL. Internal and external sources are set at AO2, and the appropriateness of short- or long-term sources for a given situation is set at AO3, which is where most marks in longer questions sit.

The broader [3.1 and 3.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-introduction-to-finance-and-sources-of-finance/) defines each source. This page adds a classification grid, three costed comparisons and a step-by-step method for choosing a source. ## A classification grid

Each source can be placed on two scales at once: where the money comes from (internal or external) and how long it is normally used for (short or long term). The placements below are the usual ones; a case may describe a source differently, so follow the case.

| Source | Internal or external | Usual term | Has to be repaid? | Gives up ownership? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Personal funds (sole trader) | Internal | Long | No | No |
| Retained profit | Internal | Long | No | No |
| Sale of assets | Internal | Long or short | No | No |
| Share capital | External | Long | No | Yes |
| Loan capital | External | Long (medium) | Yes, with interest | No |
| Overdraft | External | Short | Yes, with interest | No |
| Trade credit | External | Short | Yes, by the due date | No |
| Crowdfunding | External | Long | Depends on the model | Sometimes |
| Leasing | External | Medium to long | Regular payments | No, and the asset is not owned |
| Microfinance provider | External | Short to medium | Yes, with interest | No |
| Business angel | External | Long | No | Yes |

The two right-hand columns matter most in evaluation. Debt keeps control but creates fixed payments. Equity removes fixed payments but shares control and future profits.

## Which sources can this business actually use?

Before judging the best source, rule out those the business cannot access.

* **Legal form.** Only companies can issue share capital. A sole trader cannot sell shares but can use personal funds.
* **Age and profit record.** A start-up has no retained profit, and banks may be reluctant to lend without a trading record.
* **Collateral.** Without assets to pledge, loan capital is harder to get, which is one reason microfinance providers exist.
* **Assets owned.** Sale of assets is only possible if the business owns assets it no longer needs.
* **Ambition and story.** Crowdfunding and business angels suit businesses with an appealing product or high growth potential.

## Costing the options (AO2 with numbers)

Case studies often give figures that let you compare sources. Three common comparisons follow. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars.

### 1. Trade credit versus paying early

Juniper Hardware buys 20,000 of stock. The supplier allows 60 days' credit but offers a 2% discount for payment within 10 days.

* Discount for paying early: 2% × 20,000 = **400**.
* Paying early means paying 19,600 on day 10 instead of 20,000 on day 60, so 50 days sooner.
* If Juniper must use its overdraft at 12% a year to pay early, the interest is 19,600 × 0.12 × 50 ÷ 365 = **322** (to the nearest dollar).

Paying early and using the overdraft saves about 78. Taking the full credit period is "free" only in the sense of no interest charge: it costs the lost discount. If the overdraft rate were much higher, or the overdraft limit were already close, taking the credit would be the better choice.

### 2. Leasing versus buying with a loan

Juniper needs a delivery van for four years.

* **Buy:** price 36,000, funded by a four-year loan at 8% simple interest on the amount borrowed: interest = 36,000 × 0.08 × 4 = 11,520. Servicing costs 900 a year, so 3,600 over four years. The van can be sold for about 12,000 at the end. Net cost = 36,000 + 11,520 + 3,600 − 12,000 = **39,120**.
* **Lease:** 850 a month including servicing, so 850 × 48 = **40,800**. The van is returned at the end.

Buying is about 1,680 cheaper on these estimates, but the result depends on the resale value. If the van were worth only 10,000 after four years, buying would cost 41,120 and leasing would be cheaper. Leasing also passes the risk of breakdown costs to the leasing company, which may matter to a small business with limited cash.

### 3. Retained profit and the gap

Juniper's profit after tax is 48,000 and the owners take 30,000 out as dividends. Retained profit for the year is 48,000 − 30,000 = **18,000**. If Juniper wants a 25,000 shop extension, retained profit covers 18,000 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = **72%**, leaving 7,000 to find elsewhere. A useful point: retained profit is a record of profit kept in the business, and some of it may already have been spent on stock or equipment, so the cash may not all be available.

## Choosing a source: the AO3 method

Work through these questions in order, using the case.

1. **What is the money for, and how long will the benefit last?** Match short-term needs (a seasonal stock build-up, a late customer payment) with short-term sources. Match long-lived assets with long-term sources.
2. **How much is needed?** Small sums may come from personal funds, trade credit or microfinance. Large sums may need loan or share capital.
3. **What will it cost?** Interest, lost discounts, lease payments, dividends, and the value of any ownership given up.
4. **What does it do to control and risk?** Are the owners willing to share control? Can the business meet fixed repayments if sales fall?
5. **Which sources can this business access?** Legal form, record, collateral and assets.
6. **Judge and give a condition.**

### Annotated model answer

*Case: Sorrel Organics, a sole trader growing vegetables, needs 15,000 for a polytunnel that will last ten years. The owner has 4,000 of savings, no assets to pledge and no business loan history. A local microfinance provider lends up to 10,000.*

> The polytunnel will last ten years, so long-term finance fits better than an overdraft that the bank could withdraw. **[matches term to purpose]** As a sole trader, Sorrel cannot issue shares, and without collateral or a record a bank loan may be refused. **[rules out by access]** The owner's 4,000 of savings cost no interest and keep control, but leave 11,000 to find. A microfinance loan of up to 10,000 is designed for borrowers without collateral, though its interest rate may be higher than a bank's. **[two sources weighed]** Together these give 14,000, still 1,000 short, which could come from trade credit on seeds and compost that frees up cash. **[uses the numbers]** I recommend personal funds plus a microfinance loan, because they are the sources Sorrel can actually access. If Sorrel had a trading record and an asset to pledge, a bank loan at a lower rate would be preferable. **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

* Recommending share capital for a sole trader or partnership.
* Recommending an overdraft for a long-lived asset.
* Calling trade credit free without mentioning lost discounts or supplier relationships.
* Treating retained profit as cash in the bank.
* Weighing sources the business cannot access instead of ruling them out first.
* Giving advantages and disadvantages of each source but no final choice.

## Exam technique

* **Explain** a source (AO2): what it is, and one consequence for the business in the case.
* **Compare** or **contrast**: use the grid columns (term, repayment, control, cost).
* **Discuss**, **evaluate**, **recommend** or **justify** (AO3): follow the six-step method, use any figures given, and end with a conditional judgement.
