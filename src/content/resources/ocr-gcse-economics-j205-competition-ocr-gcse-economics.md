## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain competition between producers in a market economy, including the reasons why producers compete;
- analyse how competition affects price;
- evaluate the economic impact of competition on producers and consumers;
- explain the meaning of monopoly and oligopoly and how they differ from competitive markets.

## Competition in a market economy

In a market economy, most goods are produced by private firms that are free to set prices, enter markets and leave them. **Competition** is the rivalry between these firms for the same customers. Producers compete because customers can choose between them: a firm that does not offer good value loses sales to rivals.

Reasons producers compete:

- **Profit:** more customers mean more revenue.
- **Market share:** a bigger share brings recognition and bargaining power with suppliers.
- **Survival:** a firm that loses too many customers makes a loss and may close.
- **Growth:** a growing firm can benefit from lower average costs as it gets bigger.

## Forms of competition

| Type | Methods | When it is most used |
|---|---|---|
| Price competition | Price cuts, discounts, special offers, loyalty pricing | When products are similar and customers compare prices easily |
| Non-price competition | Quality, design, branding, advertising, customer service, after-sales care, convenient location, delivery speed | When products can be made different, or a price war would hurt all firms |

Non-price competition matters because it can make demand for a firm's product **less price elastic**: loyal customers are less likely to switch when the price rises. That link to section 2.2 is a useful piece of analysis.

## How competition affects price

### Rivals undercutting each other

**Worked example: phone repairs in Linford (invented).** Two shops each repair 100 phone screens a week at £60. Each repair costs £35 in parts and labour.

| | Shop A | Shop B |
|---|---|---|
| Starting price | £60 | £60 |
| Repairs a week | 100 | 100 |
| Profit a week | (60 − 35) × 100 = £2,500 | (60 − 35) × 100 = £2,500 |

**Step 1.** Shop B cuts its price to £50 and wins 40 of Shop A's customers.

```
Shop A: 60 repairs × (60 − 35) = £1,500 profit
Shop B: 140 repairs × (50 − 35) = £2,100 profit
```

**Step 2.** Shop A matches the £50 price. Customers split evenly again.

```
Each shop: 100 repairs × (50 − 35) = £1,500 profit
Consumers save £10 on each of 200 repairs = £2,000 a week
```

Competition has pushed the price down by 10 ÷ 60 × 100 = **16.7%**. Consumers gain £2,000 a week; each shop's profit falls by £1,000 a week. This is the mechanism to describe in an "analyse" answer: a price cut by one firm shifts customers, rivals respond to protect sales, and the market price falls.

### New firms entering

When a market earns high profits and entry is easy, new firms arrive. Supply increases, the supply curve shifts right and the price falls. The combined study guide works through this case with a demand and supply table. Together the two mechanisms explain why prices tend to be lowest in markets with many firms and low barriers to entry.

### Competition can also lower costs

To survive at lower prices, firms look for cheaper suppliers, new technology and better ways of working. Lower costs allow lower prices without losses, so competition affects price over the long run as well as immediately.

## Monopoly

A **monopoly** is a market with a single producer, or one producer so dominant that it faces little effective competition. A monopoly can keep its position only if **barriers to entry** stop rivals arriving.

| Barrier | How it protects the firm | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Legal protection | Patents or licences forbid others from copying or entering | A new medicine protected by a patent |
| Very high set-up costs | A newcomer would need huge investment before earning anything | A network of water pipes to every home |
| Control of a key resource | Rivals cannot obtain an essential input | The only quarry of a particular stone |
| Brand loyalty | Customers will not try newcomers | A long-established brand name |
| Economies of scale | The large firm's low average cost lets it undercut entrants | A dominant manufacturer |

Possible effects of monopoly:

- **Against consumers:** higher prices, less choice and possibly lower quality, because customers have nowhere else to go.
- **For consumers, sometimes:** a monopoly may achieve lower average costs from its size, and its profits can pay for research, such as developing new medicines.

## Oligopoly

An **oligopoly** is a market dominated by a few large firms. A common measure is the **concentration ratio**: the combined market share of the largest firms.

**Worked example: a national coffee-shop market (invented).** Annual sales in £ million:

| Firm | Sales | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Firm 1 | 128 | 32% |
| Firm 2 | 100 | 25% |
| Firm 3 | 72 | 18% |
| Firm 4 | 40 | 10% |
| Firm 5 | 20 | 5% |
| All others | 40 | 10% |
| Total | 400 | 100% |

The three largest firms hold (128 + 100 + 72) ÷ 400 × 100 = **75%** of sales. A few firms dominate, so this is an oligopoly.

Features of oligopoly:

- **Interdependence:** each firm must consider how rivals will react before it changes its price.
- **Non-price competition** is common, because price cuts are quickly matched and lower profits for all.
- **High barriers to entry**, such as brand strength and the cost of building a national chain.

Firms in an oligopoly might be tempted to agree prices with each other rather than compete. Such agreements are illegal in the UK, because they would harm consumers in the same way as a monopoly.

## How monopoly and oligopoly differ from competitive markets

| Feature | Competitive market | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | Many | A few dominate | One, or one dominant |
| Influence over price | Little; firms accept the market price | Some; must watch rivals | Most |
| Barriers to entry | Low | High | Very high |
| Main form of competition | Price | Non-price | Little competition |
| Likely price for consumers | Lowest | Middle | Highest, unless large economies of scale |

## Evaluating the impact of competition

**On consumers:**

- *Benefits:* lower prices, more choice, better quality and service, more innovation.
- *Limits:* firms cutting costs may cut quality or staff; if a price war drives rivals out, the survivors may raise prices later.

**On producers:**

- *Benefits:* pressure to become efficient and innovative; successful firms grow.
- *Costs:* lower profit margins; less money for investment; weaker firms close, with job losses.

**What the judgement depends on:** how easily customers can compare and switch; whether firms can cut costs without cutting quality; how many firms survive; and whether barriers to entry are low enough for new rivals to keep prices in check.

**Model conclusion:** "Competition usually benefits consumers through lower prices and more choice, as the Linford example shows. For producers it is a mixed outcome: it rewards efficient firms but squeezes profit for all. The benefit to consumers lasts only while enough firms remain; if competition drives most rivals out, the market could become an oligopoly with higher prices."

## Common mistakes

- Calling a market with a few large firms a monopoly.
- Describing competition only as price cutting, ignoring non-price methods.
- Saying competition always lowers quality, or always raises it.
- Treating monopoly as always bad with no mention of economies of scale or research.
- Forgetting producers when the question says "producers and consumers".

## Exam technique

- **Explain why producers compete:** give a reason and link it to the market economy (customers can choose).
- **Analyse how competition affects price:** use a chain: price cut → customers switch → rivals respond → market price falls; or entry → supply shifts right → price falls.
- **Explain monopoly and oligopoly:** define, give a feature, then contrast with a competitive market.
- **Evaluate:** both groups, the conditions that matter, and a judgement.
