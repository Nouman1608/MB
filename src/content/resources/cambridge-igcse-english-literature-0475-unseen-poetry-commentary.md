**This guide covers the poetry option on Paper 4 Unseen.** The syllabus says that both Paper 4
questions "require candidates to write a critical commentary on a poem or prose extract printed in
the question paper and to demonstrate an appreciation of the text", and that one question "is based
on a poem or extract from a poem". The [Paper 4 Unseen study guide](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-4-unseen/)
explains the paper's format and the choice between poem and prose; this page teaches how to read a
poem you have never seen and write a commentary on it, using an original poem written for the
purpose. The prose option has its own guide:
[the unseen prose critical commentary](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-unseen-prose-commentary/).

## What a critical commentary is

A critical commentary is a sustained, organised piece of analysis of one text, guided by the focus in
the question. It is not a line-by-line paraphrase and not a list of techniques. It explains what the
poem is about, how its language, structure and form create that meaning, and how you respond to it.
"Demonstrate an appreciation of the text" means showing that you understand why the poem works as it
does, including its subtleties.

On an unseen poem, AO1 means close reference to the printed words, AO2 means understanding the
situation, feelings and ideas, AO3 means analysing methods, and AO4 means a sensitive personal
response. All four are equally weighted.

## A reading routine for the 20 minutes

The syllabus advises "around 20 minutes reading the questions and planning". For a poem, use that
time in four passes.

**Pass 1: literal sense.** Read the poem through once without a pen. Then answer: who is speaking,
to whom, where, and what is happening? If the poem is an extract, note where it seems to begin and
end. Read the title and any note printed with the poem.

**Pass 2: the question's focus.** Reread the question and underline its key words. Read the poem
again, marking every moment that relates to them.

**Pass 3: methods.** Annotate for imagery (and what kind: natural, domestic, violent), sound
(alliteration, rhyme, rhythm), structure (stanzas, the turn, the ending) and form (line length,
enjambment, end-stopping, any regular pattern).

**Pass 4: shape and plan.** Decide how the poem moves from start to finish and choose four or five
points that follow that movement. Write a one-sentence answer to the question.

## Interpreting without certainty

On a seen poem, you have heard ideas discussed in class. On an unseen poem, you form your reading on
the spot, so tentative but confident language is appropriate:

- "This **suggests**…", "**perhaps** the speaker…", "the image **could** also imply…".
- Offer a second reading where the words genuinely support it: "The 'empty chair' may be literal,
  but it also reads as a sign of someone lost."
- Avoid two extremes: certainty about things the poem does not state ("the poet's mother has died")
  and vague hedging that commits to nothing.

Examiners reward readings grounded in the words; there is rarely one correct interpretation.

## Worked example: an original poem

The poem below was written for this guide. It is not from any anthology.

> **Inventory** *(original poem)*
>
> One coat, still shaped by someone's shoulders.
> Two cups, one chipped, which no one chose.
> A radio that only catches weather:
> rain in the north, a frost by morning.
>
> I count them as the clearance men
> count stairs, and how much each will weigh.
> I list the house as if a list
> could keep it. Nothing stays.
>
> Except, behind the door, the height
> of every child, in pencil, climbing.

**Question:** How does the poet movingly convey the experience of clearing a house?

**Planning notes (from the 20 minutes):**
- *Situation:* the speaker is emptying a house, probably after a death; the poem never says whose.
- *Title:* "Inventory" is a cold, official word for something emotional; the form of a list echoes it.
- *Stanza 1:* objects listed with numbers ("One coat", "Two cups"); the coat "still shaped by
  someone's shoulders" makes the absent person present through their shape.
- *"which no one chose":* the chipped cup suggests ordinary, unplanned life.
- *The radio:* "only catches weather", a forecast of "frost by morning", hints at coldness and
  ending.
- *Stanza 2:* comparison with the "clearance men" who count practically, by weight; the speaker is
  doing the same and dislikes it.
- *"as if a list / could keep it. Nothing stays.":* the turn of the poem; enjambment pushes
  "could keep it" onto the next line, and the short sentence "Nothing stays." is blunt.
- *Final couplet:* "Except" reverses "Nothing stays"; children's heights in pencil "climbing" are a
  record of growth that cannot be moved, ending on life rather than loss.
- *Form:* irregular lines without rhyme, like a working list; the final two-line stanza stands apart.

**One-sentence answer:** The poet makes clearing the house moving by showing the speaker trying to
manage grief through a cold list, until a mark that cannot be listed or removed breaks through.

**Model paragraph:**

> The turn of the poem comes when the speaker admits that listing is a way of coping: "I list the
> house as if a list / could keep it." The line break leaves "as if a list" hanging, so for a moment
> the reader shares the speaker's hope, before the next line reveals it as impossible. The short
> sentence that follows, "Nothing stays.", is the bluntest in the poem, its two words as final as the
> clearance itself. That is why the closing "Except" is so moving to me: the pencil heights behind the
> door are the one thing the inventory cannot count, and the present participle "climbing" leaves the
> children still growing, as if the house has kept its memory of them after all.

*Why it works:* the paragraph follows the poem's turn; it analyses enjambment, sentence length and a
single word; it uses tentative language where the poem is open ("as if"); and the personal response
emerges from close reading.

## Structuring the commentary

1. **Introduction (three or four sentences):** the situation, your one-sentence answer, and how the
   poem moves.
2. **Body (four or five paragraphs):** follow the poem's movement, stage by stage, each with two or
   three short quotations analysed closely.
3. **A paragraph on form and structure** if it has not been woven in: line lengths, stanza shape,
   the ending.
4. **Conclusion:** a considered judgement on the question's key word.

Following the poem's order usually gives the clearest argument for a poem. Organise by method only if
each paragraph still answers the question.

## Handling an extract from a longer poem

When the printed text is part of a longer poem, do not guess at what comes before or after. Treat the
extract's first and last lines as its opening and ending, and comment on how it builds within itself.

## Common mistakes

- **Paraphrase.** Restating each line in other words without analysis.
- **Technique-spotting.** Naming alliteration or enjambment without explaining its effect.
- **Inventing biography.** Claiming facts about the poet's life that the poem does not give.
- **Ignoring the title** and the ending.
- **Over-reading.** Building an argument on a single word while ignoring the rest of the poem.
- **No plan.** Skipping the planning time leads to a commentary without a shape.

## Exam technique for "Explore" and the question's focus

"Explore" means "write in detail about particular aspects": choose the aspects that serve the
question rather than everything you notice. When a question asks how the poet "movingly",
"vividly" or "strikingly" conveys something, that adverb defines what you must explain.
