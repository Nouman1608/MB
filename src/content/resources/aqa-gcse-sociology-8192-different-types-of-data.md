This guide covers the "different types of data" row of section 3.7 Sociological research methods in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification. The specification asks you to "assess the usefulness of different types of data, qualitative and quantitative data, and official and non-official statistics". The key word is **assess**: you must judge how useful a type of data is for a particular purpose, not just describe it. Methods are assessed in both Paper 1 and Paper 2.

The [research methods study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods/) gives a short comparison table. This guide shows how to test any piece of data for usefulness, explains how official statistics are produced and why that matters, and works through examples from families, education and crime. Revise with the [different types of data revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data-revision-notes/) and practise with the [different types of data practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data-practice/).

## What "usefulness" means

Data are never simply useful or useless. They are useful **for something**. A divorce rate is very useful for showing whether divorce has become more common, and of little use for explaining how it feels to go through a divorce. Every assessment should therefore start from the research aim.

### The usefulness checklist

Ask these questions of any data:

1. **Purpose:** were the data collected for this aim, or for another one?
2. **Validity:** do they give a true picture of what they claim to show?
3. **Reliability:** would the same method produce the same figures again?
4. **Representativeness:** who is included and who is missing?
5. **Definitions:** how were key terms, such as "poverty" or "crime", defined?
6. **Source and bias:** who produced the data, and might they have an interest in the result?
7. **Date:** are the data still relevant?

Using two or three of these in a short answer, and most of them in an extended answer, gives your evaluation structure.

## Quantitative data

**Quantitative data** are numerical: percentages, rates, averages and counts.

**Useful because:**
- they show **patterns** and **trends** clearly, such as changes in the proportion of children born outside marriage;
- they allow **comparison** between groups, such as exam results by gender or ethnicity;
- they are usually collected in a standard way, so they tend to be **reliable**;
- large data sets can represent whole populations.

**Limited because:**
- numbers show *what* and *how many*, not *why*;
- turning experiences into numbers can lose meaning, so **validity** may be lower;
- figures depend on definitions chosen by whoever collected them.

## Qualitative data

**Qualitative data** are descriptive: interview transcripts, field notes from observation, open answers, diaries, letters, autobiographies and media content.

**Useful because:**
- they reveal meanings, motives and feelings in people's own words;
- they give depth and context, so **validity** tends to be high;
- they can uncover issues the researcher did not expect.

**Limited because:**
- they are hard to compare or count;
- they usually come from small samples, so they may not be **representative**;
- the researcher's interpretation shapes what is selected and reported, so there is a risk of **bias** and **selective use of data**;
- personal documents may be written for an audience, exaggerated or incomplete.

## Official statistics

**Official statistics** are numerical data produced by government and its agencies. In the UK, the Office for National Statistics (ONS) is the main producer.

### Examples across the course

| Topic | Official statistics |
|---|---|
| Families | Registered marriages, divorces and births; household types from the census |
| Education | Exam results, school exclusions, school census data on pupils |
| Crime | Police recorded crime; court and prison figures; the Crime Survey for England and Wales |
| Stratification | Income, employment and benefit figures; the census |

The **census** is a survey of the whole population carried out every ten years in the UK. Because it covers everyone, it is the most representative data source available, although some people are still missed.

### How official statistics are produced

Some official statistics come from **registration**, such as births, marriages and deaths, which must be legally recorded. These are generally seen as accurate.

Others come from **decisions by people**. Recorded crime depends on a victim noticing a crime, choosing to report it, and the police deciding to record it. The crimes that never appear in official figures are called the **dark figure of crime**. Exclusion figures depend on decisions by head teachers. This is why **interactionists** see such statistics as **social constructs**: they tell us about the behaviour of the people who produce them as well as about the behaviour they count.

### Assessing official statistics

**Strengths:** often national coverage; collected regularly, so trends can be tracked; free and quick to access; produced on a large scale with standard procedures.

**Limitations:** definitions are set by government and can change, which breaks trends; some events go unrecorded; statistics may be presented in a way that suits the government of the day; they rarely explain causes.

## Non-official statistics

**Non-official statistics** are produced by organisations outside government, such as universities, charities, pressure groups, think tanks, polling companies and businesses.

**Strengths:** they can study topics government does not, or define issues differently; independent researchers may challenge official pictures, as Townsend did when his relative deprivation measure found far more poverty than the state standard.

**Limitations:** samples may be smaller; methods may be less consistent over time; the producer may have an interest in the result, such as a charity wanting to show its cause is urgent, or a company wanting to sell a product. Always ask who paid for and published the data.

## Worked example: assessing a source

> *A charity campaigning against youth homelessness publishes a survey of 300 young people using its hostels. It finds that 70% left home after arguments with a parent or step-parent.* (fictional)

- **Type:** non-official, quantitative data.
- **Useful for:** showing a common reason among young people using these hostels.
- **Representativeness:** only hostel users were surveyed; young people sleeping on friends' sofas are missing.
- **Bias:** the charity has an interest in showing family breakdown as a cause, which may affect how questions were worded.
- **Validity:** a tick-box reason may simplify complex situations; interviews could add depth.
- **Judgement:** useful as evidence of a pattern among hostel users, but not proof of the main cause of youth homelessness in general.

## Official versus non-official: a comparison

| | Official statistics | Non-official statistics |
|---|---|---|
| Producer | Government and its agencies | Universities, charities, pressure groups, businesses |
| Coverage | Often national | Often smaller or local |
| Definitions | Set by government | Set by the researcher, may differ |
| Possible bias | Political presentation; recording decisions | Interest of the producer |
| Cost to sociologist | Usually free | Varies |

## Common mistakes

- Calling official statistics "always accurate" or "useless". Both are wrong; usefulness depends on the topic.
- Assuming non-official means unreliable. Many university studies are carefully designed.
- Treating qualitative data as "opinion" and quantitative data as "fact".
- Forgetting to say **useful for what**.
- Using "biased" without saying who might be biased and why.

## Exam technique

- When asked to **assess the usefulness** of data, give at least one strength and one limitation linked to the stated aim.
- In an item-based question, quote the source and its producer, and say what is missing from the data.
- In an extended answer, compare the type of data with an alternative and finish with a judgement about fitness for purpose.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are official statistics primary or secondary data?**
For a sociologist who uses them, they are secondary data: someone else collected them for another purpose.

**Why do interactionists criticise official statistics?**
Because many figures, such as recorded crime, depend on decisions by victims, police and officials, so they are social constructs.
