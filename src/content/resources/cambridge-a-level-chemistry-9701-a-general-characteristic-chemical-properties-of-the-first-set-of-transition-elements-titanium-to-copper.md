This guide covers subtopic **28.2, General characteristic chemical properties of the first set of
transition elements, titanium to copper**, from Topic 28 of Cambridge International AS & A Level
Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Transition Elements: Properties, Complexes and Redox Chemistry guide](/resources/a-transition-elements-properties-complexes-and-redox-chemistry/)
introduces ligands and one iron titration; this page works through predicting formulae and
charges, the full set of copper(II) and cobalt(II) reactions the syllabus names, feasibility
from E⦵ values, and the three named titration systems.

## What the syllabus asks for

The learning outcomes for 28.2 cover:

- reactions of transition elements with ligands to form complexes, including **copper(II) and
  cobalt(II) ions with water, ammonia, hydroxide ions and chloride ions**, and ligand exchange in
  these systems;
- definitions of **ligand** and **complex**;
- **monodentate** (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻), **bidentate** (1,2-diaminoethane, en, and ethanedioate,
  C₂O₄²⁻) and **polydentate** (EDTA⁴⁻) ligands;
- geometry of **linear, square planar, tetrahedral and octahedral** complexes;
- **coordination number**, and predicting the **formula and charge** of a complex;
- predicting the **feasibility of redox reactions** using E⦵ values;
- reactions and calculations for **MnO₄⁻/C₂O₄²⁻**, **MnO₄⁻/Fe²⁺** and **Cu²⁺/I⁻**, and other redox
  systems given suitable data.

## Ligands and complexes

A **ligand** is a species that contains a **lone pair of electrons that forms a dative covalent
bond to a central metal atom or ion**. A **complex** is a molecule or ion formed by a **central
metal atom or ion surrounded by one or more ligands**.

| Type | Bonds to the metal per ligand | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| monodentate | 1 | H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻ |
| bidentate | 2 | H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ (en), through both N atoms; C₂O₄²⁻, through two O atoms |
| polydentate | more than 2 | EDTA⁴⁻, which forms 6 bonds |

The **coordination number** is the **number of dative bonds** from ligands to the central ion,
not the number of ligands. [Ni(en)₃]²⁺ has three ligands but coordination number 6.

## Shapes and bond angles

| Coordination number | Shape | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | [CuCl₄]²⁻, [CoCl₄]²⁻ |
| 4 | square planar | 90° | [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] |
| 6 | octahedral | 90° | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ |

## Predicting formula and charge

**Charge of complex = charge of metal ion + sum of ligand charges.**

**Worked example 1.**

- Fe³⁺ with three ethanedioate ligands: coordination number 6; charge = +3 + 3(−2) = −3, so
  **[Fe(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻**.
- Co³⁺ with en, octahedral: three en ligands give six bonds; en is neutral, so
  **[Co(en)₃]³⁺**.
- Fe²⁺ with cyanide, coordination number 6: +2 + 6(−1) = −4, so **[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻**.
- Cu²⁺ with EDTA⁴⁻: one ligand provides six bonds; +2 − 4 = −2, so **[Cu(EDTA)]²⁻**.

## Copper(II) complexes and ligand exchange

Start with the pale blue octahedral ion **[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺**.

**With hydroxide ions (aqueous NaOH):** a **pale blue precipitate** forms.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄ + 2H₂O

**With a little aqueous ammonia:** the same pale blue precipitate, because ammonia acts as a base
and supplies OH⁻.

**With excess aqueous ammonia:** the precipitate dissolves to give a **deep blue solution**.
Four water ligands are replaced by ammonia:

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O

The shape stays (distorted) octahedral; NH₃ and H₂O are similar in size and both neutral, so the
charge is unchanged.

**With concentrated hydrochloric acid:** the solution turns **yellow-green** and then yellow.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O

Chloride ions are larger than water molecules, so only four fit: the shape changes from
octahedral to **tetrahedral** and the charge from 2+ to 2−. Diluting with water reverses the
change.

## Cobalt(II) complexes and ligand exchange

Start with the pink octahedral ion **[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺**.

**With hydroxide ions, or a little aqueous ammonia:** a **blue precipitate** of
Co(OH)₂(H₂O)₄ forms.

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Co(OH)₂(H₂O)₄ + 2H₂O

**With excess aqueous ammonia:** the precipitate dissolves, and all six water ligands are
replaced, giving a **pale brown (straw-coloured)** solution that darkens in air as cobalt(II) is
oxidised to cobalt(III).

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 6NH₃ → [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ + 6H₂O

**With concentrated hydrochloric acid:** a **blue** solution of tetrahedral [CoCl₄]²⁻ forms.

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O

This equilibrium moves back to pink on adding water, which is why paper soaked in cobalt(II)
chloride can be used to test for water.

Note the contrast with copper: ammonia replaces **all six** water ligands on Co²⁺ but only
**four** on Cu²⁺.

## Feasibility of redox reactions from E⦵

Use E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = E⦵(species reduced) − E⦵(species oxidised); a positive value means
feasible under standard conditions.

**Worked example 2.** Explain why potassium manganate(VII) titrations are acidified with dilute
sulfuric acid rather than hydrochloric acid. (MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ +1.52 V; Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V)

E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 1.52 − 1.36 = +0.16 V. Manganate(VII) can oxidise chloride ions to
chlorine, so some MnO₄⁻ would be used up by the acid and the titre would be too large. Sulfate
ions are not oxidised.

## The three named titration systems

**MnO₄⁻ / Fe²⁺ (acid solution):**

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O (ratio 1 : 5)

Self-indicating: the end point is the first permanent pale pink colour.

*Worked example 3 (invented data).* An iron tablet (1.20 g) containing FeSO₄·7H₂O
(M = 278.0 g mol⁻¹) is dissolved in dilute H₂SO₄ and needs 24.60 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³
KMnO₄.

mol MnO₄⁻ = 0.02460 × 0.0200 = 4.92 × 10⁻⁴; mol Fe²⁺ = 5 × 4.92 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.46 × 10⁻³

mass FeSO₄·7H₂O = 2.46 × 10⁻³ × 278.0 = 0.684 g; percentage = 0.684 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = **57.0%**

**MnO₄⁻ / C₂O₄²⁻ (acid solution, warmed to about 60 °C):**

2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O (ratio 2 : 5)

The reaction starts slowly because both reactants are negative ions; Mn²⁺ formed catalyses it,
so it speeds up (autocatalysis). If 21.40 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ MnO₄⁻ reacts with 25.0 cm³ of
ethanedioate, mol C₂O₄²⁻ = 2.5 × 4.28 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.07 × 10⁻³ and the concentration is
**0.0428 mol dm⁻³**.

**Cu²⁺ / I⁻, then thiosulfate:**

2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I₂ (white precipitate of CuI; brown iodine)

I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻

So 2Cu²⁺ ≡ I₂ ≡ 2S₂O₃²⁻: **one mole of thiosulfate per mole of Cu²⁺**. Starch is added near the
end point; the blue-black colour disappears at the end point.

*Worked example 4 (invented data).* 0.500 g of brass is dissolved, neutralised and treated with
excess KI. The iodine needs 18.90 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ Na₂S₂O₃.

mol Cu²⁺ = mol S₂O₃²⁻ = 1.89 × 10⁻³; mass Cu = 1.89 × 10⁻³ × 63.5 = 0.120 g;
percentage = **24.0%**

## Common mistakes

- Giving the number of ligands as the coordination number for bidentate ligands.
- Forgetting ligand charges when working out the overall charge.
- Writing [Cu(NH₃)₆]²⁺ instead of [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ with excess ammonia.
- Saying a little ammonia gives a complex rather than a hydroxide precipitate.
- Using 1 : 1 instead of 1 : 5 (Fe²⁺) or 2 : 5 (C₂O₄²⁻) ratios.
- Forgetting that each I₂ comes from two Cu²⁺ ions.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** observations: give the colour and state (solution or precipitate) before and after.
- **Write equations** for ligand exchange with the full formulae of both complexes.
- **Predict** formulae: show the charge sum.
- **Deduce** feasibility: show the E⦵<sub>cell</sub> subtraction and state the sign.
- **Calculate**: moles of titrant → mole ratio from the equation → moles, mass or concentration.
