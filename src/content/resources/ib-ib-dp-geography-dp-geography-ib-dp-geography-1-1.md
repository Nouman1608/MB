## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Treat the drainage basin as an open system and explain how water moves between its stores.
- Explain how the nature of the input (rain or snow, gentle or intense) changes the flows.
- Link river discharge to channel processes: erosion, transport and deposition.
- Explain the formation of typical river landforms by process, from source to mouth.

## Precipitation: the input is not one thing

The same annual total can produce very different rivers. What matters is the **type**, **intensity**, **duration** and **timing** of precipitation.

- **Intensity.** A convectional thunderstorm may deliver 40 mm in an hour. If the soil's infiltration capacity is 15 mm per hour, more than half of that rain cannot soak in, so it becomes overland flow and reaches the channel within minutes. The same 40 mm spread over a day of frontal drizzle can all infiltrate and move slowly as throughflow and groundwater flow.
- **Type.** Snow is a store, not an immediate flow. A basin where winter precipitation falls as snow releases it weeks or months later during the melt, giving a spring or summer discharge peak.
- **Duration.** Long, steady rain gradually fills soil pores. Once the soil is saturated, even gentle rain runs off over the surface.
- **Antecedent conditions.** Rain falling on a soil that is already wet from earlier storms generates far more runoff than the same rain on a dry soil.

In an answer, avoid writing that "more rain causes more runoff". Explain **which flow** the extra water takes and **why**.

## How long water stays in each store

Stores differ in how long water remains in them, and that difference controls how quickly a basin responds.

| Store | Typical residence | Effect on the river |
|---|---|---|
| Interception (leaves, branches) | Minutes to hours | Delays and reduces water reaching the ground; much evaporates |
| Surface storage (puddles, depressions) | Hours to days | Feeds overland flow or infiltrates |
| Soil moisture | Days to weeks | Supplies throughflow and plants |
| Groundwater | Months to thousands of years | Supplies baseflow in dry periods |
| Channel storage | Hours to days | The water you see as discharge |

A basin with large groundwater stores, such as one on chalk or sandstone, keeps rivers flowing through dry seasons. A basin on impermeable rock with thin soils has little long-term storage, so rivers rise fast after rain and may almost dry up between storms.

## Worked example 1: a soil water budget

A soil water budget compares monthly precipitation (P) with potential evapotranspiration (PET) for an illustrative station over four consecutive months. Figures are invented; the soil can hold 100 mm of water when full and is full at the start.

| Month | P (mm) | PET (mm) | P - PET (mm) | Soil store at month end (mm) | State |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70 | 30 | +40 | 100 | Surplus: store already full, so the extra 40 mm drains or runs off |
| 2 | 55 | 85 | -30 | 70 | Utilisation: plants draw 30 mm from the store |
| 3 | 40 | 140 | -100 | 0 | Store used up; deficit of 30 mm |
| 4 | 65 | 55 | +10 | 10 | Recharge: the store starts to refill |

Reading month 3: demand exceeds rain by 140 - 40 = 100 mm. The store can supply only the 70 mm it held at the end of month 2, so it falls to 0 mm and a **deficit** of 100 - 70 = 30 mm remains: water plants needed but could not get. In month 4 the 10 mm surplus goes into the empty store (**recharge**), not into the river. The river consequences: during utilisation and deficit, almost no water drains from the soil, so discharge depends on groundwater alone; during the surplus, drainage feeds high flows and flood risk is greatest.

## Channel efficiency and hydraulic radius

Discharge equals cross-sectional area multiplied by mean velocity, but velocity itself depends on how much water is in contact with the bed and banks, where friction slows it. Geographers measure this with the **hydraulic radius**:

**hydraulic radius = cross-sectional area / wetted perimeter**

The wetted perimeter is the length of bed and banks touching the water.

## Worked example 2: two channels

Both channels have a cross-sectional area of 12 m squared.

```
Channel A: 12 m wide, 1 m deep (wide and shallow)
  wetted perimeter = 1 + 12 + 1 = 14 m
  hydraulic radius = 12 / 14 = 0.86 m

Channel B: 4 m wide, 3 m deep (narrow and deep)
  wetted perimeter = 3 + 4 + 3 = 10 m
  hydraulic radius = 12 / 10 = 1.2 m
```

Channel B is more **efficient**: proportionally less water is slowed by friction, so it flows faster for the same gradient. This is one reason velocity tends to rise downstream even though the gradient falls: lowland channels are deeper and smoother, so more of the river's energy goes into flow rather than overcoming friction. It also explains why a meander's deep outer side (the pool) flows faster than the shallow riffle between bends.

## Load: what the river carries and where it drops it

- **Bedload** moves along the bed by traction (rolling) and saltation (bouncing).
- **Suspended load** is fine material carried within the flow.
- **Dissolved load** is carried in solution and is invisible.

Two terms help explain deposition:

- **Competence** is the largest particle a river can move at a given velocity.
- **Capacity** is the total amount of load it can carry.

Both rise steeply in floods and fall when velocity drops. This gives the key to landform location: deposition happens wherever velocity falls. That means the inner bank of a meander (point bars), the edge of the channel during overbank flow (levees), the floodplain beyond (fine silts), and the river mouth where flow meets standing water (deltas).

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how the intensity of precipitation affects the movement of water through a drainage basin.* **(sample paragraph)**

"When rainfall intensity is greater than the soil's infiltration capacity, water cannot enter the soil quickly enough and moves over the surface as infiltration-excess overland flow **[process named precisely]**. This flow is fast, so water reaches the channel within a short time, giving a short lag time and a high peak discharge **[consequence for the river]**. Lower-intensity rain of the same total can infiltrate, moving slowly through the soil as throughflow or percolating to groundwater, so the river rises later and less sharply **[contrast completes the explanation]**. However, if gentle rain continues until the soil is saturated, saturation-excess overland flow occurs, so intensity is not the only control: antecedent soil moisture also matters **[qualification shows depth]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating precipitation as a single number and ignoring its type, intensity and timing.
- Confusing residence time with store size: a small store can still matter if water moves through it fast.
- Calculating a soil water budget without carrying the store forward from the previous month.
- Saying velocity falls downstream because the gradient falls, without considering channel efficiency.
- Explaining landforms with no reference to changes in velocity, competence and capacity.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** needs a brief account or summary. **Describe** needs a detailed account of what is shown. **Explain** needs reasons or causes: use "because" and "so".
- **Distinguish** needs the difference made explicit, such as competence (largest particle) versus capacity (total load).
- **Examine** asks you to consider an argument or concept so that its assumptions and links are revealed: for example, whether vegetation always reduces runoff.
- When given a hydrograph or budget graph, quote values with units and time, then interpret them.
- For landforms, give a sequence of numbered steps and say where velocity rises or falls at each step.
