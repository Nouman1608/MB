## 20.3.1 Untreated sewage and excess fertiliser in water

### Untreated sewage

Sewage contains faeces, urine and waste water. If it enters a river untreated, two things happen:

1. **Organic matter feeds decomposers.** Bacteria in the water break down the organic matter. They respire **aerobically**, using up the **dissolved oxygen** in the water. Fish, mayfly larvae and other animals that need plenty of dissolved oxygen die or move away.
2. **Mineral ions feed producers.** Sewage releases nitrate and phosphate ions as it is decomposed, which cause rapid growth of algae.

### Excess fertiliser

When farmers apply more fertiliser than the crop can absorb, or rain falls soon after spreading, nitrate and other ions are washed (leached) from the soil into streams and lakes. The ions cause rapid growth of algae and water plants. A thick layer of algae on the surface blocks light, so plants growing below cannot photosynthesise and die. When the algae and plants die, decomposers break them down, and their respiration uses up dissolved oxygen.

### Worked example 1: river data

Dissolved oxygen was measured at five sites along a river. Untreated sewage enters between site 1 and site 2 (illustrative data).

| Site | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance downstream / km | 0 | 0.5 | 2 | 5 | 10 |
| Dissolved oxygen / mg per dm³ | 9.6 | 3.2 | 2.4 | 5.8 | 9.0 |
| Number of fish species | 11 | 2 | 1 | 6 | 10 |

**(a) Describe the changes.** Dissolved oxygen falls sharply from 9.6 to 3.2 mg per dm³ just below the sewage, reaches its lowest value, 2.4, at site 3, then rises again to 9.0 at site 5. Fish species follow the same pattern.

**(b) Explain the fall.** Bacteria decompose the organic matter in the sewage and respire aerobically, using dissolved oxygen faster than it is replaced.

**(c) Suggest why oxygen recovers downstream.** The organic matter has been broken down, so fewer decomposers are respiring; oxygen dissolves from the air and is released by photosynthesis of water plants.

**(d) Percentage decrease from site 1 to site 3:** (9.6 − 2.4) ÷ 9.6 × 100 = **75%**.

## 20.3.2 Non-biodegradable plastics

**Non-biodegradable** means decomposers cannot break the material down, so it stays in the environment for a very long time.

| Ecosystem | Effects |
|---|---|
| **Aquatic** (rivers, seas) | animals such as turtles, seals and seabirds become entangled in nets, rings and bags, and drown or cannot feed; animals mistake plastic for food, and it blocks or fills their gut so they starve; plastic breaks into very small pieces that are eaten by small animals and pass along food chains; floating plastic can carry organisms to new habitats |
| **Terrestrial** (land) | plastic waste fills landfill sites and stays there; animals eat plastic litter or become trapped in it; litter covers soil and makes habitats less suitable |

The key idea linking the effects: plastics **persist** because decomposers cannot digest them, so the damage continues for many years and spreads through food chains.

## 20.3.3 Methane and carbon dioxide

### Sources

| Gas | Sources |
|---|---|
| **Carbon dioxide** | combustion of fossil fuels in vehicles, power stations and factories; burning of forests during deforestation |
| **Methane** | digestion in cattle and other livestock; rice grown in flooded fields; decomposition of organic waste in landfill sites |

### The enhanced greenhouse effect

1. The Sun's radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
2. The warm surface gives out **thermal energy** (infrared radiation).
3. Carbon dioxide and methane **absorb** some of this thermal energy, so it is not all lost to space; some is radiated back towards the Earth.
4. This natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth warm enough for life.
5. Human activities increase the concentrations of these gases, so **more** thermal energy is absorbed: the **enhanced greenhouse effect**.
6. The **mean global temperature rises**.

### Climate change

A rise in mean global temperature causes **climate change**. Effects you can describe:

- melting ice caps and glaciers, and rising sea levels, flooding low-lying coasts;
- changing rainfall patterns, with more droughts in some places and floods in others;
- more frequent extreme weather;
- habitats changing faster than some species can adapt, so populations move or decline.

## 20.3.4 Eutrophication (Extended only)

The syllabus limits the explanation to six steps. Each one causes the next, so learn them as a chain and use the connecting words.

1. **Increased availability of nitrate and other ions** in the water, from fertiliser run-off or sewage.
2. **Increased growth of producers**, such as algae and water plants.
3. Producers die, so there is **increased decomposition** after their death. (Plants below a surface layer of algae die sooner because they receive too little light.)
4. **Increased aerobic respiration by decomposers**, such as bacteria, which feed on the dead material.
5. **Reduction in dissolved oxygen** in the water, because the decomposers use it faster than it is replaced.
6. **Death of organisms requiring dissolved oxygen**, such as fish and many invertebrates.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** A farmer spreads fertiliser on a field next to a lake. Heavy rain follows. Explain how this can lead to the death of fish in the lake. [6]

**Answer:** Rain washes nitrate and other ions from the fertiliser into the lake (1). The ions cause rapid growth of algae and other producers (1). When the producers die, there is more dead material to be decomposed (1). The number of decomposing bacteria increases and they respire aerobically (1), using up dissolved oxygen, so the concentration of oxygen in the water falls (1). Fish cannot get enough oxygen for respiration and die (1).

**Why it scores:** all six steps appear in order. The two most often missed are step 3 (the producers must die) and step 4 (it is the **decomposers'** aerobic respiration that uses the oxygen, not the algae).

## Common mistakes

- Saying sewage kills fish because it is "poisonous". The main effect is oxygen loss from decomposers' respiration.
- Writing that algae use up the oxygen. Living algae release oxygen in daylight; it is decomposers that use it up.
- Calling plastic "toxic" as its only effect. Describe entanglement, ingestion and persistence.
- Forgetting terrestrial effects when the question asks for both ecosystems.
- Saying the greenhouse effect is caused by the "hole in the ozone layer". These are separate issues.
- Writing "global warming" when asked for effects; the syllabus terms are the **enhanced greenhouse effect** and **climate change**.
- Giving only carbon dioxide sources when methane is asked for.

## Exam technique for 20.3 command words

- **Describe** effects of sewage or fertiliser: name the change (oxygen falls, algae grow) and the consequence for organisms.
- **Describe** plastic effects: aquatic and terrestrial points, each with what happens to the animal or habitat.
- **Describe** sources and effects of gases: name the source, then the absorption of thermal energy, rise in temperature and climate change.
- **Explain** eutrophication (Extended): six steps, in order, with "so" or "which" between them.
- **Interpret** data: quote values with units; link changes to distance from the source.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is eutrophication Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Explaining eutrophication step by step is Supplement content, so it is Extended only. Describing the effects of untreated sewage and excess fertiliser on aquatic ecosystems is Core.

**Why do fish die after sewage enters a river?**
Bacteria decompose the organic matter in the sewage and use dissolved oxygen in aerobic respiration, so there is too little oxygen left for the fish.

**What are the main sources of methane?**
Digestion in cattle and other livestock, rice grown in flooded fields, and decomposition of organic waste in landfill sites.
