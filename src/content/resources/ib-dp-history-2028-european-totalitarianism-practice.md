> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 8 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-european-totalitarianism-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-european-totalitarianism/).

## Questions

**1.** State two features of the Weimar Constitution that made it vulnerable. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the Matteotti murder did not bring down Mussolini. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "Last year the factory sent men home. This year there is work for all, but wages stay low and the union is gone. They say we are one people. I say we are one people with one boss." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of a German metalworker in 1936.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about Nazi economic policy. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of the extract as evidence of how Germans felt about Nazi rule. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why Hitler was appointed chancellor in January 1933. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons why Stalin defeated his rivals in the 1920s. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the extent of totalitarian control in Fascist Italy. **[6]**

**7.** "Fear, rather than consent, explains the stability of Hitler's regime between 1933 and 1939." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the economic policies of Stalin and Hitler. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about whether the Great Terror was planned by Stalin. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: proportional representation led to many small parties and weak coalitions; Article 48 let the president rule by decree, which could be misused; the republic was linked to defeat and the Versailles Treaty; the system left old elites in the army, courts and civil service. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. The king and conservative elites did not want to remove him and feared socialism more (1); the opposition deputies who left parliament in the Aventine Secession lost influence rather than forcing a crisis (1); the army, police and press were under pressure, and the Fascists already held much power (1); Mussolini took responsibility in his speech of January 1925 and moved to a dictatorship (1). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: unemployment has fallen, showing rearmament and public works (1); wages remain low and consumers gain little (1); trade unions were abolished, so workers have no way to bargain (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one worker's voice and may not reflect farmers, women or the middle class (1); the extract is constructed, and critical comments would be dangerous in Nazi Germany, so real opinions might be hidden (1); it says nothing about support for Hitler on grounds of national pride or foreign policy (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. The Depression and rising unemployment helped the Nazis become the largest party, with 37.3 per cent in July 1932 (2). Conservative politicians such as Papen believed they could control Hitler in a coalition and persuaded Hindenburg, after failures of Brüning, Papen and Schleicher (2). **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. Stalin's post as General Secretary let him build a network of supporters through appointments (1); the use of party rules and committees to remove opponents (1); allied first with Bukharin against Trotsky, then turned on Bukharin (1); his policy of "Socialism in One Country" appealed more than Trotsky's "permanent revolution" (1); opponents underestimated him and were divided, and Trotsky was a poor organiser (1); a judgement on the main factor, such as skill in the party machine (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: the ban on other parties, press control and the secret police (OVRA) gave a strong state (1); propaganda built a cult of the Duce (1); youth and leisure organisations shaped daily life (1); the monarchy remained and could dismiss Mussolini, as happened in 1943 (1); the Church kept independence under the Lateran Pacts (1); a reasoned judgement that Italy was only partly totalitarian (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim, weighing fear against consent (3). Fear: Gestapo, SS, camps, the end of free press and the banning of parties and unions (3). Consent: falling unemployment, restored national pride, popular foreign policy successes, propaganda and social organisations (3). Perspectives: historians who stress terror and those who stress consent and denunciation by ordinary Germans (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for instance that consent and fear worked together, with many Germans conforming to avoid trouble (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Both used state direction of the economy for national goals, Stalin through Five-Year Plans and Hitler through the New Plan and Four-Year Plan (1); both gave priority to heavy industry and military needs (1); Stalin abolished private ownership of land and industry, whereas Hitler worked with existing firms and private owners (1); a judgement on difference, for example the degree of state ownership (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view sees a planned terror to remove all possible opposition (2); another stresses that local officials and quotas drove numbers, and that Stalin reacted to events (2); explains why they differ, for example the effect of archive evidence opened after 1991 (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
