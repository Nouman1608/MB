**Option 1A Aspects of Tragedy** is one of the two genre options for Paper 1 Literary Genres in AQA
A-level English Literature B (7717). You study three texts and write about them in a closed-book
exam. The [Paper 1 Literary Genres overview](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-literature-b-literary-genres/)
explains the paper as a whole; this guide is only about the tragedy option, its texts and how
AQA's idea of tragedy should shape your answers.

## What you study

AQA describes the option as measuring later texts "against a literary genre which is long
established". You study three texts: one Shakespeare play, a second drama text and one further
text, and at least one of the two non-Shakespeare texts must be written before 1900.

**Shakespeare (one of):** *Othello*; *King Lear*.

**Two further texts, including one drama, from:**

| Text | Author | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Richard II* | Shakespeare | Drama, pre-1900 |
| *Death of a Salesman* | Arthur Miller | Drama |
| *Tess of the D'Urbervilles* | Thomas Hardy | Novel, pre-1900 |
| *The Great Gatsby* | F. Scott Fitzgerald | Novel |
| "Lamia", "Isabella or The Pot of Basil", "La Belle Dame Sans Merci", "The Eve of St. Agnes" | John Keats | Poetry, pre-1900 |
| AQA English Literature B Poetry anthology (tragedy) | Various | Poetry |

The anthology contains extracts from the Prologue of "The Monk's Tale" and "The Monk's Tale"
(Chaucer), "Jessie Cameron" (Christina Rossetti), an extract from *Paradise Lost* (Milton),
"Tithonus" (Tennyson), "The Convergence of the Twain" (Hardy), "The Death of Cuchulain" (Yeats),
"'Out, out…'" (Frost), "Death in Leamington" (Betjeman) and "Miss Gee" (Auden).

Paper 1 is closed book: you may not take any set text into the exam. Set texts are reviewed
periodically, so confirm your list with your teacher.

## AQA's aspects of tragedy

AQA's starting point is that "at the core of all the set texts is a tragic hero or heroine who is
flawed in some way, who suffers and causes suffering to others", and that in all of them "there is
an interplay between what might be seen as villains and victims". It then offers areas to explore,
not a closed list. The ones that most often decide the quality of an answer are these.

**Type of tragedy.** AQA contrasts tragedy that is "classical and about public figures, like Lear"
with tragedy that is "domestic and about representations of ordinary people, like Tess". Most of
your texts sit somewhere between: *Othello* is a general's tragedy played out in a marriage.

**The protagonist's journey.** Flaws, "pride and folly", "blindness and insight", "discovery and
learning", and being "a mix of good and evil". Ask whether recognition comes, and at what cost.

**Villain and victim.** The villain "engages in a contest of power and is partly responsible for
the hero's demise". "Partly" is the important word: an essay that blames Iago or Edmund entirely
loses the hero's responsibility.

**Fate.** Whether "the hero's end is inevitable". Hardy's narrator in *Tess* ends by ironically
crediting the President of the Immortals, so fate becomes a target of the novel's anger.

**Effect on the world.** How the hero's behaviour spreads chaos, from Lear's divided kingdom to the
deaths that follow Gatsby's.

**Violence, revenge, humour and happiness.** AQA includes "humour and moments of happiness". The
Fool in *King Lear*, the comic touches in "Miss Gee", and the lovers' escape at the end of "The Eve
of St. Agnes" all matter, because happiness defines what is lost and can test whether a text is
tragic at all.

**Structure.** "Complication to catastrophe, from order to disorder, through climax to resolution."
Plots and sub-plots, such as the Gloucester plot in *King Lear*, mirror and intensify the main
action.

**Language and audience.** Language that heightens the tragedy, and the audience moved "through
pity and fear to an understanding of the human condition".

**Absence.** "The absence of an 'aspect' can be as significant as its presence." A villain-less
tragedy, or a poem with no catastrophe, is still worth analysing as tragedy.

## How the three sections use the texts

**Section A** prints a passage from your Shakespeare play. Analyse the passage closely as tragedy,
then connect it to the play as a whole from memory.

**Section B** is an essay on the same Shakespeare play, built from evidence across the whole text.

**Section C** asks you to connect your two other texts. Its rubric requires that one of the two
texts be a drama text and one written before 1900, which your text choices are designed to meet.
Connections (AO4) need to be argued, not listed.

All five assessment objectives can apply across the paper: AO1 (an informed, personal response in
accurate writing, using concepts and terminology), AO2 (how meanings are shaped), AO3 (contexts),
AO4 (connections across texts) and AO5 (different interpretations).

## Worked example: passage analysis

Suppose the passage is Othello's speech before the murder in Act 5 Scene 2. A strong answer might
argue that the speech shows the hero's blindness at the very moment he believes he sees most
clearly. The repeated "put out the light" turns the candle into an image for Desdemona's life,
and the contrast between the light that can be relit and the life that cannot reveals insight
arriving within the delusion: Othello understands the irreversibility of death but not his own
error. His sense that he acts as justice, rather than as a jealous husband, shows how fully Iago's
contest of power has been won. Connecting outward, the answer would link this to Othello's
earlier confidence in his own judgement, to Iago's soliloquies, and to the final speech, where
recognition comes too late. AO5 enters naturally: some readers find Othello noble even here,
others see self-deception, and the passage supports a debate between them.

## Worked example: Section C link

**Question shape (original):** "In tragedy, the victims are always more interesting than the
villains." Discuss with reference to two texts.

**Plan using *Tess of the D'Urbervilles* and *Death of a Salesman*:** both texts are domestic
tragedies of ordinary people, so the villain is less a person than a system. In *Tess*, Alec is a
clear villain, but Angel's rejection and the social code that judges her are as destructive; in
*Salesman* there is no single villain, but Howard's dismissal of Willy and the dream of success play
the opponent's role. Argue that both texts make the victim more interesting because the villainy is
diffused into society, then weigh the counter-view that Alec is a vivid, deliberate creation.
Contexts: Victorian sexual morality; post-war American capitalism. Interpretation: a feminist
reading of Tess; Miller's defence of the common man as tragic.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying a checklist.** Labels such as hamartia are useful only if you argue how the text uses,
  bends or rejects the idea.
- **Blaming the villain entirely.** AQA's wording is that the villain is "partly responsible".
- **Treating Section C as two mini-essays.** Organise by point of connection.
- **Ignoring happiness and humour**, which AQA names explicitly.
- **Misquoting from memory.** Paper 1 is closed book; short, accurate quotations are best.

## Exam technique

Questions on tragedy often turn on a critical view or on the significance of an aspect, and
reward debate rather than agreement. Paper 1 lasts 2 hours 30 minutes for three answers of equal
value, so about 50 minutes each is proportionate. Plan Section C with care: choosing the right
moments from two texts takes longer than it seems.
