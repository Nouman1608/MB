## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A cube has sides of 2 cm. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio. **[2]**

**2.** Name the openings through which air enters an insect's tracheal system. **[1]**

**3.** Which change occurs during inspiration?

A: The diaphragm relaxes and rises.

B: The volume of the thorax decreases.

C: The pressure in the thorax falls below atmospheric pressure.

D: The external intercostal muscles relax. **[1]**

**4.** State two features of a xerophytic leaf that reduce water loss. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a flatworm needs no gas exchange system, but a large mammal does. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**6.** Describe how the structure of an alveolus makes it efficient as a gas exchange surface. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how the diaphragm and the external intercostal muscles cause air to enter the lungs. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows data from three people measured at rest.

| Person | FVC / dm3 | FEV1 / dm3 | Tidal volume / dm3 | Breaths per minute |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (healthy) | 4.8 | 3.8 | 0.50 | 14 |
| B (fibrosis) | 2.5 | 2.1 | 0.30 | 22 |
| C (asthma) | 3.6 | 1.9 | 0.40 | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate FEV1 as a percentage of FVC for each person. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the pulmonary ventilation, in dm3 per minute, of person C. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the differences between persons A, B and C in the percentage you calculated in part (a). **[4]**

**9.** An insect closes its spiracles for much of the time when resting. Explain the advantage and the disadvantage of this behaviour. **[3]**

**10.** The table shows the percentage saturation of two types of haemoglobin at different partial pressures of oxygen.

| Partial pressure of oxygen / kPa | Haemoglobin X / % saturation | Haemoglobin Y / % saturation |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 | 30 |
| 4 | 38 | 70 |
| 6 | 72 | 90 |
| 8 | 90 | 96 |
| 10 | 96 | 98 |

**(a)** Which haemoglobin has the higher affinity for oxygen? Use data to support your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Haemoglobin Y comes from an animal that lives in a burrow. Suggest how this haemoglobin is an adaptation to its environment. **[3]**

**(c)** Blood leaving the lungs was 97 % saturated, and blood leaving a muscle was 40 % saturated. Calculate the percentage of the oxygen that was unloaded in the muscle, relative to the oxygen carried leaving the lungs. **[2]**

**11.** Explain the effect of a high partial pressure of carbon dioxide on the oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin, and why this is an advantage in active muscle. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Surface area = 6 x 2 x 2 = 24 cm2 and volume = 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 cm3 [1]. Ratio = 24 / 8 = 3 : 1 [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Spiracles [1]. **[1]**

**3.** **C** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Pressure falls because the volume of the thorax increases. Air then moves in from the higher outside pressure.

**4.** Any two from: thick waxy cuticle; sunken stomata; rolled leaves; hairs; reduced leaf area [1 each]. **[2]**

**5.** The flatworm is flat and thin, so it has a large surface area to volume ratio and a short diffusion distance [1]. A large mammal has a small surface area to volume ratio and a long diffusion distance to its inner cells [1], so it needs a specialised exchange surface and a transport system to supply all its cells [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The wall is one cell thick (flattened epithelium), so the diffusion distance is short [1]. There are very many alveoli, giving a large surface area [1]. A dense network of capillaries with thin walls removes oxygen and brings carbon dioxide, so a steep concentration gradient is maintained [1]. Ventilation keeps the oxygen concentration in the alveoli high [1]. **[4]**

**7.** The diaphragm contracts and flattens [1]. The external intercostal muscles contract, raising the ribs up and out [1]. The volume of the thorax increases, so the pressure inside falls below atmospheric pressure [1]. Air moves into the lungs down the pressure gradient [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** A: 3.8 / 4.8 x 100 = 79.2 % [1]. B: 2.1 / 2.5 x 100 = 84.0 % [1]. C: 1.9 / 3.6 x 100 = 52.8 % [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** 0.40 x 20 = 8.0 dm3 per minute [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Person B has a higher percentage than A, and a lower FVC [1]. Fibrosis scars the lung tissue, so the lungs cannot expand fully, which reduces FVC; but the air that can be moved leaves quickly, so the ratio remains high [1]. Person C has a much lower percentage [1], because asthma narrows the airways, so air leaves the lungs slowly and FEV1 is reduced more than FVC [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each disease to the structure that changes, and then to the measurement that changes.

**9.** Advantage: less water vapour is lost from the tracheae by evaporation [1]. Disadvantage: less oxygen enters and less carbon dioxide leaves, so the gradients are not maintained [1], limiting the amount of aerobic respiration the insect can sustain [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Haemoglobin Y [1]. At 4 kPa it is 70 % saturated compared with 38 % for X (or any other pair of values at the same pressure) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The air in a burrow has a low partial pressure of oxygen [1]. Haemoglobin Y has a high affinity, so it becomes saturated at a low partial pressure of oxygen [1], so it loads more oxygen in the lungs even though the oxygen supply is low [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** 97 - 40 = 57 percentage points [1]. 57 / 97 x 100 = 58.8 % (accept 59 %) [1]. **[2]**

**11.** The curve shifts to the right [1]. At any given partial pressure of oxygen, haemoglobin has a lower saturation, so it releases more oxygen [1]. Active muscle respires faster and produces more carbon dioxide [1], so more oxygen is delivered to the tissue that needs it most [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "bigger organisms have less surface area" without "in proportion to volume".
- Saying the diaphragm "moves down", with no mention of contraction or pressure.
- Not linking a change in structure (thickened walls, narrowed airways) to a change in a measurement.
- Reversing left and right in curve-shift answers.
- Giving a calculation without units.
