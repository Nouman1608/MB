In every calculation the wave reaching the first filter is already plane-polarised.

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by a plane-polarised wave. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the fact that light can be polarised is evidence that light is a transverse wave. **[2]**

**3.** Plane-polarised light of intensity 64 W m⁻² is incident on a polarising filter whose transmission axis is at 45° to the plane of polarisation. Calculate the transmitted intensity. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the angle between the plane of polarisation and the transmission axis of a filter that transmits 10% of the intensity of plane-polarised light. **[2]**

**5.** A polarising filter is rotated through 360° in a beam of plane-polarised light. Explain why the transmitted intensity reaches a maximum twice. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**6.** A microwave transmitter emits plane-polarised microwaves towards a receiver connected to a meter whose reading is proportional to the intensity received.

**(a)** Describe how the receiver could be used to show that the microwaves are plane-polarised. **[2]**

**(b)** With transmitter and receiver aligned, the reading is 80 units. The receiver is rotated through 30° about the line joining it to the transmitter. Calculate the new reading. **[2]**

**(c)** The receiver is returned to its aligned position, and a grille of parallel metal rods is placed between transmitter and receiver. State the orientation of the rods, relative to the electric field of the microwaves, that gives **(i)** a maximum reading and **(ii)** a minimum reading. **[2]**

**7.** Plane-polarised light of intensity 150 W m⁻² passes through two polarising filters in turn. The transmission axis of the first filter is at 15° to the original plane of polarisation; that of the second is at 75° to the original plane, in the same sense.

**(a)** Calculate the intensity after the first filter. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the intensity after the second filter. **[2]**

**(c)** A student obtains 10 W m⁻² for **(b)**. Explain the error. **[1]**

**8.** Plane-polarised light of intensity I₀ falls on filter A, whose axis is parallel to the plane of polarisation. Filter B, placed after A, has its axis at 90° to that of A.

**(a)** Explain why no light emerges from B. **[1]**

**(b)** A third filter C is placed between A and B, with its axis at 30° to that of A. Calculate the intensity emerging from B as a fraction of I₀. **[3]**

**(c)** State the angle between the axes of A and C that would make the intensity emerging from B as large as possible. **[1]**

**9.** A plane-polarised electromagnetic wave of amplitude A₀ passes through a filter whose axis is at 50° to the plane of polarisation.

**(a)** Calculate the amplitude of the transmitted wave as a fraction of A₀. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the transmitted intensity as a fraction of the incident intensity. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the two fractions are different. **[1]**

**10.** Plane-polarised light of intensity I₀ passes through three polarising filters in turn. The axis of each filter is at 30° to the axis of the one before it, all rotated in the same sense, and the first is at 30° to the original plane of polarisation.

**(a)** Calculate the final intensity as a fraction of I₀. **[2]**

**(b)** State the angle between the plane of polarisation of the emerging light and the original plane of polarisation. **[1]**

**(c)** Compare your answer to **(a)** with the intensity transmitted by a single filter at that angle to the original plane. **[1]**

**11.** Describe the graph of transmitted intensity I against angle θ (from 0° to 180°) when plane-polarised light of intensity I₀ passes through a polarising filter whose axis makes angle θ with the plane of polarisation. **[3]**

**12.** A student directs sound from a loudspeaker through a narrow vertical slot in a board towards a microphone, then rotates the slot to horizontal. Predict, with a reason, whether the reading changes because of polarisation. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A transverse wave in which the oscillations are confined to **one direction** [1], perpendicular to the direction of travel (one plane containing the direction of travel) [1].

**2.** Polarisation restricts oscillations to one direction **perpendicular to the direction of travel** [1]. Only transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to their direction of travel; a longitudinal wave could not be polarised, so light must be **transverse** [1].

**3.** I = I₀cos²θ = 64 × cos²45° [1] = **32 W m⁻²** [1].

**4.** cos²θ = 0.10, so cos θ = 0.316 [1]; θ = **72°** [1].

**5.** Intensity follows cos²θ, which is maximum when θ = 0° and when θ = 180° [1]: at both angles the filter axis lies **along the same line** as the plane of polarisation [1].

**6. (a)** Rotate the receiver about the line joining it to the transmitter [1]; the reading falls from a **maximum to (nearly) zero** after a 90° rotation, showing a single plane of polarisation [1].

**(b)** 80 × cos²30° [1] = **60 units** [1].

**(c) (i)** Rods **perpendicular** to the electric field [1]. **(ii)** Rods **parallel** to the electric field [1].

**7. (a)** I₁ = 150 × cos²15° [1] = **140 W m⁻²** [1].

**(b)** Angle between filter axes = 75° − 15° = 60° [1]; I₂ = 140 × cos²60° = **35 W m⁻²** [1].

**(c)** The student applied Malus's law **once with 75°** to the original intensity (150 × cos²75° = 10 W m⁻²), instead of using each filter in turn with the angle **between successive axes** [1].

**8. (a)** The light leaving A is polarised along A's axis, which is at **90°** to B's axis, and cos²90° = 0 [1].

**(b)** After A: I₀ [1]. After C: I₀ × cos²30° = 0.75I₀ [1]. C to B is 60°, so after B: 0.75I₀ × cos²60° = **0.19I₀** (0.1875I₀) [1].

**(c)** **45°** [1].

**9. (a)** cos 50° = **0.64** [1].

**(b)** cos²50° = **0.41** [1].

**(c)** The filter transmits the amplitude component A₀cos θ, but intensity is proportional to **amplitude squared** [1].

**10. (a)** Each filter transmits cos²30° = 0.75 of the intensity reaching it [1], so the final intensity = 0.75³I₀ = **0.42I₀** [1].

**(b)** **90°** [1].

**(c)** A single filter at 90° would transmit **nothing**, whereas three filters at 30° steps transmit 0.42I₀ [1].

**11.** Maximum **I₀ at θ = 0°** and again at **θ = 180°** [1]; **zero at θ = 90°** [1]; a smooth cos²θ curve that is never negative (symmetrical about 90°) [1].

**12.** No change due to polarisation [1]: sound is a **longitudinal** wave, oscillating along its direction of travel, so it cannot be polarised [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using cos θ instead of cos²θ (Questions 4 and 9).
- Measuring the second filter's angle from the original plane (Questions 7 and 8).
- Assuming crossed filters always block light, even with a filter between them (Question 8).
- Sketching a cos²θ graph with negative values or with zeros at 0° (Question 11).
- Saying sound cannot be polarised because it is not an electromagnetic wave, instead of because it is longitudinal (Question 12).
- Leaving the calculator in radians.
