This guide goes deeper into subtopic **9.1 Properties of metals** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Metal Properties and Reactivity](/resources/metal-properties-and-reactivity/) guide covers 9.1 together with the reactivity series and corrosion, and spends most of its space on reactivity. This page concentrates on the two 9.1 outcomes themselves: comparing metals with non-metals, and describing the three kinds of reaction that metals have in common. For quick recall use the [properties of metals revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-properties-of-metals-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [properties of metals practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-properties-of-metals-practice/).

## What 9.1 asks you to do

Subtopic 9.1 has two learning outcomes, and **both are Core**. There is no Supplement content in 9.1, so every 0620 candidate needs all of it.

| Outcome | Command word | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Compare | Give similarities and differences between metals and non-metals for thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, malleability and ductility, and melting and boiling points |
| 2 | Describe | Give the main features of the reactions of metals with dilute acids, with cold water and steam, and with oxygen |

The syllabus says these are **general** properties. Examiners expect the typical behaviour first, then accept a well-known exception if you name it correctly.

## Comparing physical properties

| Property | Typical metal | Typical non-metal | Exceptions worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermal conductivity | Good conductor of heat | Poor conductor (insulator) | Diamond conducts heat well |
| Electrical conductivity | Good conductor in the solid and liquid states | Poor conductor | Graphite, a form of carbon, conducts electricity |
| Malleability and ductility | Malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires) | Solids are brittle and break or crumble when hit | None needed at this level |
| Melting and boiling points | Usually high; most are solids at room temperature | Usually low; many are gases at room temperature | Mercury is a liquid metal; sodium and potassium melt below 100 °C; diamond and graphite have very high melting points |

Three points make these comparisons easier to remember and to use.

**Conductivity is the most reliable test.** If a solid element conducts electricity, it is almost certainly a metal; graphite is the one non-metal exception you are expected to know. A melting point on its own is a weaker clue, because carbon has a very high melting point and mercury a very low one.

**Malleable and ductile are different words.** Malleable means the metal can be hammered or pressed into a new shape, such as a sheet. Ductile means it can be stretched or drawn out into a wire. A comparison answer that says only "metals are bendy" earns nothing; use the two terms.

**Metals feel cold.** A metal handrail at room temperature feels colder than a wooden one at the same temperature, because the metal conducts heat away from your hand quickly. This is a useful way to explain thermal conductivity in a "suggest" question.

### Why metals behave like this

The explanation in terms of a giant lattice of positive ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons belongs to subtopic 2.7 Metallic bonding, which is Supplement content. For 9.1 you only need to **state and compare** the properties. Extended candidates can add the structural reason when a question asks them to explain; the [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) guide covers it.

## The general chemical properties of metals

Outcome 2 limits the chemistry to three reactions. Learn the general word equation for each, because the products are what examiners test.

### 1. With dilute acids

```
metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen
magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
zinc + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate + hydrogen
Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2
```

What you see: bubbles of colourless gas (effervescence), the metal gradually disappears, and the mixture becomes warm. The gas gives a "squeaky pop" with a lighted splint, which is the test for hydrogen. The salt takes its name from the acid: hydrochloric acid gives chlorides and sulfuric acid gives sulfates.

Not every metal reacts. Copper, silver and gold show no reaction with dilute acids; why this happens is explained by the reactivity series in 9.4.

### 2. With cold water and steam

```
metal + cold water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
calcium + water → calcium hydroxide + hydrogen
Ca + 2H2O → Ca(OH)2 + H2

metal + steam → metal oxide + hydrogen
magnesium + steam → magnesium oxide + hydrogen
Mg + H2O → MgO + H2
```

The products are different: **cold water gives a hydroxide, steam gives an oxide**. Hydrogen is formed in both. The solution left after a metal reacts with cold water is alkaline, because the metal hydroxide dissolves, so universal indicator added to it turns blue or purple.

### 3. With oxygen

```
metal + oxygen → metal oxide
magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
copper + oxygen → copper(II) oxide
2Cu + O2 → 2CuO
```

Magnesium ribbon burns with a very bright white flame and leaves a white powder. Copper does not burn when heated in air; its surface turns black as copper(II) oxide forms. Sodium tarnishes in air within seconds, which is why it is stored under oil. Metal oxides are generally **basic**, which links this outcome to subtopic 7.2 Oxides: a non-metal oxide such as carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide is acidic instead.

## Worked example 1: classifying elements from data

*The data below are invented for practice.*

| Element | Melting point / °C | Conducts electricity as a solid? | Effect of hammering | Oxide added to water and tested with universal indicator |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 1085 | Yes | Flattens | Oxide does not dissolve; reacts with dilute acid |
| R | 113 | No | Shatters | Acidic solution |
| T | 3500 | Yes | Crumbles | Acidic solution |
| W | 98 | Yes | Flattens | Alkaline solution |

**Question.** Deduce which elements are metals. Give reasons. [4]

**Model answer, annotated.**

- P is a metal: it conducts electricity, is malleable and forms a basic oxide (it reacts with acid). *Three pieces of evidence agree.*
- R is a non-metal: it does not conduct, is brittle and has a low melting point. *Again the evidence agrees.*
- T is a non-metal even though it conducts: it is brittle and its oxide is acidic. Its properties match graphite. *This is the trap: one metallic property is not enough when the others point the other way.*
- W is a metal despite its low melting point: it conducts, is malleable and gives an alkaline solution, as sodium does. *A low melting point is a weak clue on its own.*

The examiner credits a correct identification **with** a reason drawn from the table. "W is a metal" with no reason scores nothing.

## Worked example 2: a "describe" answer

**Question.** Describe what is seen when a piece of magnesium ribbon is (a) heated in air and (b) added to dilute hydrochloric acid. Name the products in each case. [5]

**Model answer, annotated.**

(a) The magnesium burns with a bright white flame [1] and a white powder is left [1], which is magnesium oxide [1].

(b) Bubbles of gas are given off and the magnesium dissolves [1]; the products are magnesium chloride and hydrogen [1].

*Each mark is a separate observation or product. Writing "it reacts" earns nothing: describe what you would actually see. Do not write "hydrogen is seen"; you see bubbles, and a test identifies the gas.*

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "metals conduct" without saying what.** The syllabus lists thermal and electrical conductivity separately, so say which one, or both.
- **Comparing only one side.** "Metals have high melting points" is not a comparison. Add "whereas non-metals usually have low melting points".
- **Giving a hydroxide as the product with steam.** Steam gives the metal **oxide**; cold water gives the hydroxide.
- **Forgetting hydrogen.** Both acid and water reactions produce hydrogen gas; the oxygen reaction does not.
- **Describing observations as products.** "Magnesium chloride forms" is a product, not something you see. Bubbles, a flame, a colour change and the metal disappearing are observations.
- **Using an exception as the rule.** Mercury, sodium and graphite are exceptions. Do not use them as the general pattern unless the question is about them.

## Exam technique for 9.1 command words

- **Compare:** write paired sentences using "whereas" or "but", one property at a time. Two separate lists, one for metals and one for non-metals, often miss the linking marks.
- **Describe:** give observations for reactions and the general features for properties. Name products when asked.
- **State:** a short answer is enough, such as "hydrogen" or "metal oxide".
- **Deduce:** use the evidence you are given, and quote it in your reason.
- **Suggest:** apply the general properties to an unfamiliar situation, such as why a saucepan is made of metal but has a plastic handle (the metal conducts heat to the food; the plastic is a poor thermal conductor).
