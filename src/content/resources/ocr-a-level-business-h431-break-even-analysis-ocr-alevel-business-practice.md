## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the margin of safety. **[2]**

**2.** Pellam Printing sells posters at £25 each. Variable cost per poster is £10 and fixed costs are £60,000 a year. Calculate its break-even output. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a stepped fixed cost. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following would **lower** a business's break-even point?

A: A rise in rent.

B: A fall in selling price.

C: A fall in variable cost per unit.

D: A rise in wages of office staff. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** limitation of break-even analysis. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Pellam Printing expects to sell 5,000 posters next year at £25. Variable cost is £10 each and fixed costs are £60,000.

**(a)** Calculate the margin of safety in units and as a percentage of planned sales. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the planned profit. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the output needed to make a profit of £30,000. **[2]**

**(d)** Complete the table of total cost and total revenue for outputs of 0, 2,000, 4,000 and 6,000, and state where the lines cross. **[4]**

**7.** Fixed costs at Pellam rise to £66,000, and the price falls to £23.

**(a)** Calculate the new break-even output. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether Pellam should cut its price to £23. **[6]**

**8.** Pellam's machine can print 6,000 posters. A second machine would add £12,000 to fixed costs and allow output of up to 8,000 posters.

**(a)** Calculate the profit at 6,000 posters and at 7,000 posters with the second machine. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the output at which profit with two machines equals the profit at 6,000 posters with one. **[3]**

**9.** A supermarket chain offers to buy 600 posters at £16 each. Pellam has spare capacity and fixed costs would not change. Evaluate whether Pellam should accept the order. **[6]**

**10.** Recommend and justify **one** way for Pellam Printing to lower its break-even point. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of break-even analysis to a new restaurant and its stakeholders. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The margin of safety is how far sales can fall [1] before the business reaches break-even and starts to make a loss; it equals actual or planned output minus break-even output [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Contribution = £25 − £10 = £15 [1]. Break-even = £60,000 ÷ £15 [1] = **4,000 posters** [1]. **[3]**

**3.** A stepped fixed cost is a fixed cost that stays the same over a range of output [1] but jumps to a higher level when capacity is passed, for example when a second machine or workshop is needed [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** It assumes that all output is sold at one price [1], and that variable cost per unit is constant, whereas discounts, bulk-buying and changes in demand make this unrealistic [1]. (Accept: it ignores demand; it depends on forecast figures.) **[2]**

**6. (a)** Break-even = 4,000 (from question 2). Margin of safety = 5,000 − 4,000 = **1,000 posters** [1] [1]. Percentage = 1,000 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Profit = (£15 × 5,000) − £60,000 [1] = £75,000 − £60,000 = **£15,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** (£60,000 + £30,000) ÷ £15 [1] = **6,000 posters** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** At 0: total cost £60,000, revenue £0. At 2,000: total cost £80,000, revenue £50,000. At 4,000: total cost £100,000, revenue £100,000. At 6,000: total cost £120,000, revenue £150,000 [1] [1] [1]. The lines cross at 4,000 posters and £100,000 [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** New contribution = £23 − £10 = £13 [1]. Break-even = £66,000 ÷ £13 = 5,076.9 [1] = **5,077 posters** (rounded up) [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Against: break-even rises from 4,000 to 5,077, which is above the planned 5,000, so the business would make a loss at the planned sales [1]; the margin of safety disappears [1]. The price cut reduces contribution by £2 on every poster, a cost of £10,000 on 5,000 posters [1].
For: a lower price might increase sales if demand is sensitive, and the figures in the question include a rise in fixed costs, which is a separate cause of the problem [1].
Judgement: Pellam should not cut the price unless it expects a large rise in sales, since it would have to sell more than 5,077 just to break even [1], and it should look at the cause of the fixed cost rise instead [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** One machine, 6,000 posters: (£15 × 6,000) − £60,000 = **£30,000** [1] [1]. Two machines, 7,000 posters: (£15 × 7,000) − £72,000 = £105,000 − £72,000 = **£33,000** [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Profit of £30,000 with fixed costs of £72,000: output = (£72,000 + £30,000) ÷ £15 [1] = £102,000 ÷ £15 [1] = **6,800 posters** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Contribution = £16 − £10 = £6 per poster; total = 600 × £6 = **£3,600** [1] [1]. Pellam should accept if there is spare capacity and fixed costs do not rise, since the order adds £3,600 to profit [1].
Risks: regular customers paying £25 may find out and demand a lower price [1]; if the 600 posters use capacity that could be sold at £25, Pellam gives up £9 contribution each, which is £5,400 [1].
Judgement: accept if the order uses spare capacity and the buyer is not a threat to regular sales, because it is extra contribution at no extra fixed cost [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Recommendation: cut fixed costs, for example by moving to cheaper premises or sharing equipment [1]. A cut of £6,000 lowers the break-even to £54,000 ÷ £15 = 3,600 posters, 400 fewer [1] [1].
Alternatives: raising the price by £1 raises contribution to £16 and gives break-even of 3,750 [1], but may reduce demand [1].
Judgement: reducing fixed costs is the better option because it does not affect the customers or the price, but Pellam must check that a move does not reduce capacity or quality [1]. **[6]**

**11.** For: a new restaurant can estimate fixed costs (rent, staff) and variable costs (food) and find how many covers it must sell [1]; the margin of safety shows how far sales can fall [1]; it helps set menu prices and supports a loan application [1]; "what if" tests show the effect of a rent rise [1].
Stakeholders: owners use it to set targets [1]; lenders use it to judge risk [1].
Limits: it assumes one price and constant variable cost, but a restaurant sells many items at different margins [1]; the forecast of sales is uncertain for a new business [1].
Judgement: it is a useful starting point for planning but should be backed by market research, since break-even tells the owner how many customers are needed, not whether they will come [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by price instead of contribution.
- Starting the total cost line at zero.
- Forgetting to round break-even up.
- Forgetting that a price cut reduces contribution on every unit.
- Using the special-order price without subtracting variable cost.
