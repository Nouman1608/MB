## Section A

**1.** Explain how two resistors connected in series across a supply can provide an output p.d. that is a fixed fraction of the supply p.d. **[2]**

**2.** A 12 V supply of negligible internal resistance is connected across a 4.7 kΩ resistor in series with a 2.2 kΩ resistor. Calculate the p.d. across the 2.2 kΩ resistor. **[2]**

**3.** In a potentiometer circuit, explain what is meant by the *balance point*. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a potentiometer at balance measures the e.m.f. of a test cell rather than its terminal p.d. **[2]**

**5.** A student cannot find a balance point anywhere on a potentiometer wire. Suggest **two** possible reasons. **[2]**

**6.** An LDR and a fixed resistor are connected in series across a supply. The output is taken across the fixed resistor. State and explain what happens to the output p.d. when the light intensity increases. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** A potentiometer wire of length 1.000 m and resistance 4.0 Ω is connected in series with a 6.0 Ω resistor and a driver cell of e.m.f. 2.0 V and negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** Calculate the current in the wire. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the whole wire. **[1]**

**(c)** A thermocouple is connected to the potentiometer and balances at 62.5 cm. Calculate the p.d. produced by the thermocouple. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the 6.0 Ω resistor is included in the driver circuit. **[2]**

**8.** Using the same driver circuit, cell A (e.m.f. 1.50 V) balances at 72.0 cm and cell B balances at 54.0 cm.

**(a)** Calculate the e.m.f. of cell B. **[2]**

**(b)** A 5.0 Ω resistor is connected across the terminals of cell A, and the new balance length is 60.0 cm. Calculate the terminal p.d. of A and the current it supplies. **[2]**

**(c)** Determine the internal resistance of cell A. **[2]**

**9.** A thermistor and a 3.0 kΩ fixed resistor are connected in series across a 9.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. The output is taken across the fixed resistor. The thermistor's resistance is 12 kΩ at 10 °C and 1.5 kΩ at 70 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the output p.d. at 10 °C and at 70 °C. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest a use for this circuit. **[1]**

**(c)** State how the circuit should be changed so that the output p.d. falls as the temperature rises. **[1]**

**10.** A 12 V supply of negligible internal resistance is connected across a 20 kΩ resistor in series with a 10 kΩ resistor. The output is taken across the 10 kΩ resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the output p.d. when nothing is connected to the output. **[1]**

**(b)** A voltmeter of resistance 10 kΩ is connected across the output. Calculate its reading. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest how the voltmeter could be changed so that its reading is closer to your answer to (a). **[1]**

**11.** A lamp of resistance 10 Ω (assumed constant) is to be dimmed using a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance.

**(a)** The lamp is first connected in series with a variable resistor of range 0 to 20 Ω. Calculate the minimum p.d. across the lamp. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how a potentiometer (a resistor with a sliding contact, used as a potential divider) would allow the p.d. across the lamp to be reduced to zero. **[2]**

**12.** An LDR and a 4.7 kΩ fixed resistor are connected in series across a 5.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. The output is taken across the LDR and is connected to a switching circuit (which draws negligible current) that turns a street lamp on when its input exceeds 2.5 V. The LDR has a resistance of 500 Ω in daylight and 20 kΩ at night.

**(a)** Calculate the output p.d. in daylight and at night. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain whether the lamp is on in daylight and at night. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The same current passes through both resistors, so the p.d. across each is proportional to its resistance (V = IR) [1]. The p.d. across one resistor is therefore the fraction R₂/(R₁ + R₂) of the supply p.d., fixed by the choice of resistors [1].

**2.** V = 12 × 2.2 ÷ (4.7 + 2.2) [1] = 3.8 V [1].

**3.** The position of the sliding contact on the wire [1] at which the galvanometer shows zero current (no deflection) [1].

**4.** At balance there is no current in the test cell [1], so there is no p.d. across its internal resistance (Ir = 0) and the p.d. balanced equals the e.m.f. [1].

**5.** Any two [1 each]: the p.d. across the whole wire is smaller than the p.d. being measured (driver e.m.f. too small or series resistance too large); the test cell is connected with its positive terminal to the opposite end from the driver's positive terminal; a break in the circuit or a loose connection.

**6.** The resistance of the LDR decreases [1], so the LDR takes a smaller share of the supply p.d. [1]; the p.d. across the fixed resistor therefore increases [1].

**7. (a)** I = 2.0 ÷ (4.0 + 6.0) = 0.20 A [1].

**(b)** V = 0.20 × 4.0 = 0.80 V [1].

**(c)** p.d. per metre = 0.80 V m⁻¹ [1]; thermocouple p.d. = 0.80 × 0.625 = 0.50 V [1].

**(d)** It reduces the p.d. across the wire, so a small p.d. balances at a long length [1]; this gives a smaller percentage uncertainty in the balance length and hence in the measured p.d. [1].

**8. (a)** ε_B = 1.50 × 54.0 ÷ 72.0 [1] = 1.13 V [1].

**(b)** V = 1.50 × 60.0 ÷ 72.0 = 1.25 V [1]; I = 1.25 ÷ 5.0 = 0.25 A [1].

**(c)** Ir = ε − V = 1.50 − 1.25 = 0.25 V [1]; r = 0.25 ÷ 0.25 = 1.0 Ω [1].

**9. (a)** At 10 °C: V = 9.0 × 3.0 ÷ (12 + 3.0) = 1.8 V [1]. At 70 °C: V = 9.0 × 3.0 ÷ (1.5 + 3.0) = 6.0 V [1]. The output rises as the temperature rises [1].

**(b)** An over-temperature (fire) alarm or a switch for a cooling fan [1].

**(c)** Take the output across the thermistor instead (or swap the positions of the thermistor and the fixed resistor) [1].

**10. (a)** V = 12 × 10 ÷ 30 = 4.0 V [1].

**(b)** Voltmeter in parallel with 10 kΩ: combined resistance 5.0 kΩ [1]; V = 12 × 5.0 ÷ (20 + 5.0) [1] = 2.4 V [1].

**(c)** Use a voltmeter of much higher resistance (much larger than 10 kΩ) [1].

**11. (a)** Maximum total resistance = 30 Ω, so I = 6.0 ÷ 30 = 0.20 A [1]; lamp p.d. = 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 V [1].

**(b)** The supply is connected across the whole potentiometer and the lamp is connected between one end and the sliding contact [1]; moving the contact to that end makes the resistance (and so the p.d.) between the lamp's connections zero, so the lamp's p.d. can be varied continuously from zero to 6.0 V [1].

**12. (a)** Daylight: V = 5.0 × 0.50 ÷ (4.7 + 0.50) = 0.48 V [1]. Night: V = 5.0 × 20 ÷ (4.7 + 20) = 4.0 V [1].

**(b)** In daylight 0.48 V is less than 2.5 V, so the lamp is off [1]; at night 4.0 V exceeds 2.5 V, so the lamp is on [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the output across the wrong resistor in the divider equation.
- Ignoring the parallel combination when a voltmeter or load is connected.
- Describing the galvanometer as measuring a p.d. rather than detecting zero current.
- Not stating that no current flows in the test cell at balance.
- Getting the direction of a sensor output wrong by skipping a step in the reasoning.
- Forgetting that a variable resistor in series cannot reduce a component's p.d. to zero.
