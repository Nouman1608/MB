## What managers do

The specification says the role of managers should include five activities. They form a loop, not a list. Reviewing feeds back into the next round of objectives.

| Activity | What it means | Example in a small firm |
|---|---|---|
| Setting objectives | Deciding what the team or business is trying to achieve, with targets that can be measured | Kestrel Bindery, a hand-binding firm, aims to bind 600 books a month by March |
| Analysing | Examining information to understand the situation and the options | The manager compares binding time per book with the price customers will pay |
| Leading | Guiding, motivating and directing people towards the objectives | Explaining why the March target matters and who does which task |
| Making decisions | Choosing between courses of action | Choosing to buy a second sewing frame rather than hire another binder |
| Reviewing | Checking results against objectives and learning from the gap | In April, comparing 540 books bound against the target of 600 and asking why |

Two points help in exam answers. First, the activities overlap. A manager who reviews results is also analysing. Second, "leading" is one of the five, so leadership is part of management as the specification frames it. When a question asks you to analyse a manager's role, link each activity to the case rather than listing the five words.

## Management and leadership styles

A style is the typical way a manager or leader behaves towards the people they work with: how much they decide alone, how much they involve others, and how much they focus on the task compared with the people. The specification asks for types of style, and the two named theories describe them from different angles.

Common labels, which are standard terms rather than a list the specification prints, are these.

- **Autocratic**: the manager decides and expects instructions to be followed. Fast and clear, but little involvement.
- **Paternalistic**: the manager decides but explains the decision and acts in what they believe is the employees' interest.
- **Democratic**: the manager involves staff in the decision and may go with the group view. Slower, but draws on more ideas.
- **Laissez-faire**: the manager sets limits and leaves staff to decide how to work. Suits capable, self-directed people.

You will see how these map on to the theories below.

## The Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum

The continuum shows leadership behaviour as a line, not as separate boxes. At one end the manager uses authority and makes the decision alone. At the other end the team has freedom to decide within limits the manager sets. As the manager moves along the line, the manager uses less authority and the team has more freedom.

The standard form of the continuum has seven positions. The wording below is a standard summary, not a quotation.

| Position | Manager behaviour |
|---|---|
| 1 | Makes the decision and announces it |
| 2 | Makes the decision and "sells" it, persuading staff it is right |
| 3 | Presents ideas and invites questions |
| 4 | Presents a tentative decision that may be changed |
| 5 | Presents the problem, gets suggestions, then decides |
| 6 | States the limits and asks the group to decide |
| 7 | Lets the team act within limits the manager has defined |

Positions 1 and 2 sit close to autocratic and paternalistic behaviour. Positions 3 to 5 are consultative and shade into democratic. Positions 6 and 7 move towards laissez-faire, though still within limits. The theory also stresses that the right position depends on the manager, the team and the situation. There is no single best point on the line.

### Worked example: placing a manager on the continuum

At Kestrel Bindery the owner needs to change the weekend working pattern. She tells the three binders: "Here are three possible rotas and the cost of each. Tell me what you think, then I will choose." Where does this sit?

1. She has not decided in advance, so she is not at position 1 or 2.
2. She presents the options and asks for views (this is position 5, since she asks for suggestions or opinions).
3. She keeps the final decision, so she is not at position 6 or 7.

So this is position 5, a consultative style that keeps authority with the owner. A suitable evaluation point: it uses the binders' knowledge of the workshop but takes longer than announcing a rota.

## The Blake Mouton grid

The grid plots a manager on two scales, each running from 1 (low) to 9 (high): **concern for production** (the task, output and results) and **concern for people** (the needs, relationships and morale of staff). A position is written as (production, people).

| Grid position | Name (standard form) | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| (1,1) | Impoverished | Little concern for either. The manager does the minimum |
| (9,1) | Produce or perish (authority-compliance) | High task focus, people treated as a means to output |
| (1,9) | Country club | High concern for people, little pressure on results |
| (5,5) | Middle of the road | Balances the two by compromise, with neither fully met |
| (9,9) | Team management | High on both, with commitment built through shared goals |

The theory suggests (9,9) as the style to aim for, but a good answer treats this as a claim to test, not a fact. Reaching high scores on both takes time, skilled managers and a team that wants to engage.

### Worked example: reading the grid from evidence

Marsh End Cycles has two assembly teams. Team Alpha's manager checks hourly output, rarely speaks to staff about anything else, and has never held a team meeting. Team Beta's manager sets monthly targets with the team and spends time on training and problems.

| Measure for the year | Alpha | Beta |
|---|---|---|
| Average staff | 24 | 20 |
| Leavers | 9 | 2 |
| Bikes assembled | 4,320 | 3,400 |
| Defective bikes | 216 | 68 |

Calculations (formulae are the standard forms):

- Labour turnover = leavers ÷ average staff × 100. Alpha: 9 ÷ 24 × 100 = **37.5%**. Beta: 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = **10%**.
- Output per employee = bikes ÷ average staff. Alpha: 4,320 ÷ 24 = **180**. Beta: 3,400 ÷ 20 = **170**.
- Defect rate = defective ÷ total × 100. Alpha: 216 ÷ 4,320 × 100 = **5%**. Beta: 68 ÷ 3,400 × 100 = **2%**.
- Good bikes per employee: Alpha (4,320 − 216) ÷ 24 = **171**. Beta (3,400 − 68) ÷ 20 = **166.6**.

Alpha looks like (9,1) and Beta nearer (9,9). Alpha produces slightly more per employee, and the gap shrinks once defects are removed (171 against 166.6). But Alpha loses more than a third of its staff each year. If replacing a leaver costs £2,500, Alpha spends £22,500 on replacement against £5,000 for Beta. The evidence supports Beta's style for the long term. It does not prove it, since one year and two teams is thin evidence, and the teams may do different work.

## Influences on management and leadership styles

The specification asks for the influences on styles. These are the main ones, with the direction each pushes in.

- **The task and its urgency.** A safety-critical or time-pressed task pushes towards a directive style. A creative task favours involvement.
- **Skills and experience of the workforce.** Skilled, motivated staff can be trusted with more freedom. New staff may need clear instructions.
- **The manager's own personality, beliefs and skills.** A manager who trusts people tends to delegate, while one who doubts staff tends to control.
- **The culture of the business.** A long-standing hierarchical culture makes a democratic style hard to introduce.
- **Size and structure of the business.** A tall, large organisation makes consultation costly. A small firm allows direct conversation.
- **Time and the risk involved.** Higher risk or less time pushes decisions up to the manager.
- **The external situation**, such as a crisis, strong competition or a labour market where staff can easily leave.

Influences interact. A strong manager in a skilled team facing a crisis may use a directive style for a week, then return to consultation.

## The effectiveness of different styles

Effectiveness means how far a style helps the business meet its objectives. Judge it against evidence, not against what sounds pleasant. Useful measures are output, quality, labour turnover, absenteeism, speed of decisions and staff motivation.

| Style | Likely strengths | Likely weaknesses | Where it tends to fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic (continuum 1, grid 9,1) | Speed, clarity, control | Low motivation, ideas unused, turnover | Crisis, untrained staff, simple routine work |
| Paternalistic (continuum 2) | Loyalty, a sense of being cared for | Staff may feel talked down to | Where trust matters and staff look for guidance |
| Democratic (continuum 3 to 5) | Better ideas, commitment to the decision | Slow, risk of compromise | Complex problems with skilled staff and time |
| Laissez-faire (continuum 6 to 7) | Freedom, creativity, development | Drift, inconsistent standards | Expert teams with clear objectives |
| Team management (grid 9,9) | High commitment and results | Needs time, skill and trust | Long-term, skilled teams |

Evaluation draws on several lines of argument.

- **Context decides.** The same style can succeed in one business and fail in another.
- **Time matters.** An autocratic style may bring quick results and costly turnover later.
- **Mixed styles.** Many managers move along the continuum depending on the decision.
- **Measures are limited.** Output may rise because of demand, not style.

### Worked example: judging a style for a case

Ferrymoor Logistics is about to open an overnight sorting shift in a rush, staffed by 12 agency workers on their first week. Which style fits?

1. The staff are new and untrained, so they need clear instructions (supports a directive style, continuum 1 or 2).
2. The deadline is short, so there is no time for lengthy consultation.
3. But the shift will run for years, and the depot will want staff to stay and suggest improvements.
4. **Judgement:** begin with positions 1 and 2 on the continuum, with clear explanations of why, then move towards 4 and 5 as skills grow. The decisive factor is the time horizon: a style right for week one will be costly if it continues.

## Common errors

- Writing "democratic is best" without tying it to the case.
- Treating the continuum as four boxes. It is a line of seven positions.
- Reading the grid as one score. You need both numbers, written in the order (production, people).
- Assuming (9,9) always works, or that a (9,1) manager must fail.
- Listing the five activities of a manager without linking them to the business.
- Saying a style "motivates" staff with no evidence such as turnover or output.
- Confusing management and leadership styles with decision making methods, which belong to 3.2.2.
