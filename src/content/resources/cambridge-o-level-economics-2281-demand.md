## 2.3.1 Definition and the demand curve

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price, over a given period of time.

- **Willing and able:** wanting a good is not enough; the consumer must also be able to pay. This is sometimes called effective demand.
- **At each possible price:** demand is the whole relationship between price and quantity, not one number.
- **Over a given period:** "200 cups a day" means something; "200 cups" does not.

### Worked example 1: drawing a demand curve

The demand schedule for bubble tea in the town of Rindu (invented):

| Price per cup (cu) | Quantity demanded (cups a day) |
|---|---|
| 5 | 100 |
| 4 | 160 |
| 3 | 220 |
| 2 | 280 |
| 1 | 340 |

To draw it:

1. Put **price** on the vertical axis and **quantity demanded** (with the time period) on the horizontal axis. Label both.
2. Choose even scales from zero.
3. Plot the five points and join them. Label the line D.

The curve slopes downwards from left to right: as price falls, quantity demanded rises.

### Why the curve slopes down

- At a lower price, the good is more affordable, so consumers with limited incomes can buy more of it.
- At a lower price, the good is cheaper compared with substitutes, so some consumers switch to it.
- At a higher price, the reverse happens.

### Interpreting the diagram

Read across from a price to the curve, then down to the quantity. At 3 cu, quantity demanded is 220 cups a day. Read up from a quantity to find the highest price consumers are willing to pay for that amount: for 160 cups, the price is 4 cu.

## 2.3.2 Movements along the curve

A change in the **good's own price** causes a **movement along** the existing demand curve. The curve itself does not move.

- **Extension in demand:** a fall in price leads to a rise in quantity demanded.
- **Contraction in demand:** a rise in price leads to a fall in quantity demanded.

**Worked example 2.** The price of bubble tea falls from 4 cu to 2 cu.

- Quantity demanded rises from 160 to 280 cups: an **extension** of 120 cups a day.
- Percentage change in quantity = 120 ÷ 160 × 100 = 75%.
- Percentage change in price = (2 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = −50%.

If the price then rises from 2 cu to 3 cu, quantity demanded falls from 280 to 220: a **contraction** of 60 cups.

## 2.3.3 Individual and market demand

**Individual demand** is the demand of one consumer. **Market demand** is the total demand of all consumers in the market at each price. It is found by **aggregation**: adding the quantities demanded by every consumer at each price.

**Worked example 3.** Three groups buy bubble tea in Rindu each day.

| Price (cu) | Students | Office workers | Tourists | Market demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 20 | 50 | 30 | 100 |
| 4 | 50 | 70 | 40 | 160 |
| 3 | 80 | 90 | 50 | 220 |
| 2 | 110 | 110 | 60 | 280 |
| 1 | 140 | 130 | 70 | 340 |

At 3 cu: 80 + 90 + 50 = 220. On a diagram this is horizontal adding: at each price, the market curve lies at the sum of the individual quantities, so it is further to the right than any individual curve.

If the tourist season ends and tourists stop buying, market demand at 3 cu falls to 170. The number of consumers is itself a condition of demand.

## 2.3.4 Conditions of demand: shifts of the curve

A change in anything that affects demand **other than the good's own price** shifts the whole curve.

- **Increase in demand:** more is demanded at every price; the curve shifts to the **right**.
- **Decrease in demand:** less is demanded at every price; the curve shifts to the **left**.

| Condition | Change | Effect on demand | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Rises | Increase (for most goods) | Consumers are more able to buy |
| Price of a substitute | Rises | Increase | Consumers switch away from the substitute |
| Price of a complement | Rises | Decrease | The good used with it is now more costly |
| Tastes and fashion | More popular | Increase | More consumers are willing to buy |
| Advertising | Successful campaign | Increase | Raises willingness to buy |
| Population | Rises, or more people in the age group that buys it | Increase | More consumers |
| Income tax | Rises | Decrease | Disposable income falls |
| Weather or season | Hot weather (for cold drinks) | Increase | Greater willingness to buy |

**Worked example 4: a substitute.** The price of iced coffee in Rindu rises. Some coffee drinkers switch to bubble tea. Demand for bubble tea increases by 40 cups a day at every price. At 3 cu, quantity demanded is now 260 cups instead of 220. The curve shifts right from D1 to D2.

### Worked example 5: a price change and a shift together

Incomes in Rindu rise, increasing demand by 40 cups at every price, and in the same week the price rises from 3 cu to 4 cu.

| Price (cu) | Old demand (D1) | New demand (D2) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 160 | 200 |
| 3 | 220 | 260 |

- Before: price 3 cu on D1 → 220 cups.
- After: price 4 cu on D2 → 200 cups.
- Net change: a fall of 20 cups.

Separate the two effects. The rise in income is an **increase in demand** (shift from D1 to D2). The rise in price is a **contraction** along D2 (from 260 to 200). The net result depends on which effect is bigger.

## Common errors

- Writing "demand falls" when the good's own price rises. The correct phrase is "quantity demanded contracts" or "a contraction in demand".
- Shifting the curve when the good's own price changes.
- Leaving out "willing and able" or the time period from the definition.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when finding market demand.
- Putting price on the horizontal axis.
- Stating a condition of demand with no direction: "the price of a complement changes" is not enough; say "rises" and "demand decreases".

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use all three parts of the definition.
- **Draw**: label axes (price; quantity with time period), label curves D1 and D2, and show the direction of the shift with an arrow.
- **Explain** a shift: the cause, why consumers are more or less willing or able to buy, and the direction of the shift.
- **Analyse** a combined change: separate the movement from the shift, as in worked example 5.
- In multiple-choice questions, first ask: is the good's own price changing (movement) or something else (shift)?

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between an extension in demand and an increase in demand?**
An extension is a movement along the demand curve caused by a fall in the good's own price. An increase is a shift of the whole curve to the right, caused by a change in a condition of demand such as income.

**How is market demand found?**
By adding the quantities demanded by all individual consumers at each price. This is called aggregation.
