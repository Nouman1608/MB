These questions cover section **4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification,
for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.1). The content is examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and
Higher Tier. Where an ion's charge is not from Groups 1, 2, 6 or 7, the question gives it.

Learn the content first in the [ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds/)

## Section A

**1.** This question is about the structure of ionic compounds.

**(a)** Name the type of structure an ionic compound has. **[1]**
**(b)** State what holds the particles together. **[1]**
**(c)** State the directions in which these forces act. **[1]**

**2.** Give the empirical formula of the ionic compound shown by each model.

**(a)** 8 sodium ions and 4 oxide ions **[1]**
**(b)** 3 barium ions and 6 chloride ions **[1]**
**(c)** 10 chromium ions (Cr³⁺) and 15 oxide ions (O²⁻) **[1]**

**3.** A space-filling model of sodium chloride uses two sizes of sphere. Identify which sphere represents
the chloride ion and give a reason. **[2]**

**4.** Calcium chloride has the formula CaCl₂. Explain why this formula does not describe a molecule. **[2]**

**5.** A diagram shows particles labelled K⁺ and I⁻ arranged in an alternating grid that continues to the
edges of the page. Give **two** reasons why this shows an ionic compound. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by the statement "in an ionic lattice the forces act in all directions". **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The diagram shows part of one layer of an ionic compound. A = barium ion (Ba²⁺), B = chloride ion (Cl⁻).

```
A  B  B  A  B  B
A  B  B  A  B  B
A  B  B  A  B  B
```

Work out the empirical formula of the compound, and show that it is electrically neutral. **[3]**

**8.** Sodium chloride can be shown as a ball and stick model or as a 3D space-filling model. Give **one**
advantage and **one** limitation of each model. **[4]**

**9.** A student counts 12 Na⁺ ions and 12 Cl⁻ ions in a model. The student writes the formula Na₁₂Cl₁₂ and
calls it "a molecule of sodium chloride".

**(a)** Identify **two** errors in the student's work. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the correct formula. **[1]**

**10.** Potassium bromide is an ionic compound. Describe the structure and bonding in solid potassium
bromide. **[4]**

**11.** A model of lithium nitride contains 9 lithium ions (Li⁺) and 3 nitride ions (N³⁻).

**(a)** Work out the empirical formula. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that your formula has no overall charge. **[1]**
**(c)** Give **two** reasons why a dot and cross diagram showing one nitrogen and three lithium particles is
a limited representation of solid lithium nitride. **[2]**

**12.** A student builds a model of sodium chloride from polystyrene balls of two sizes joined by cocktail
sticks. Discuss what the model shows correctly about the structure and bonding of sodium chloride, and
what it shows incorrectly. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A **giant ionic lattice** (giant structure of ions) [1]
**(b)** Strong **electrostatic forces of attraction** between **oppositely charged ions** [1]
**(c)** In **all directions** [1]

**2. (a)** **Na₂O** [1]
**(b)** **BaCl₂** [1]
**(c)** 10 : 15 = 2 : 3, so **Cr₂O₃** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Raw counts such as Na₈O₄ score zero; the formula must be the simplest ratio.

**3.** The **larger** sphere represents the chloride ion [1], because the chloride ion is **bigger** than
the sodium ion [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Calcium chloride is a **giant structure** with no separate molecules [1]; CaCl₂ shows only the
**ratio** of ions, one calcium ion for every two chloride ions [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It shows **positive and negative ions** (oppositely charged ions) [1]; they are in a **regular,
repeating** arrangement that continues, showing a giant lattice [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A key naming a metal ion (potassium) and a non-metal ion (iodide) is also acceptable
for one mark.

**6.** Each ion is attracted to **all** the oppositely charged ions around it [1], not to one partner ion,
so the attractions run through the whole lattice in three dimensions [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Count: A = 2 per row × 3 rows = 6; B = 4 per row × 3 rows = 12 [1]. Ratio 6 : 12 = 1 : 2, so
**BaCl₂** [1]. Charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Ball and stick, advantage: shows the 3D arrangement / that each ion has six neighbours [1].
Limitation: sticks suggest bonds between particular ions / shows gaps between ions that are not there [1].
Space-filling, advantage: shows the relative sizes of the ions / how closely they pack [1].
Limitation: ions inside are hidden, so the arrangement is hard to see / forces not shown [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** The ratio has **not been simplified** [1]; sodium chloride is a **giant lattice, not a
molecule** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **NaCl** [1]

**10.** **Giant** ionic lattice [1] of **positive potassium ions and negative bromide ions** [1] in a
regular, alternating arrangement [1]; held by **strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged
ions acting in all directions** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Any mention of "molecules" or "covalent bonds" contradicts the answer and limits the
mark.

**11. (a)** 9 : 3 [1] = 3 : 1, so **Li₃N** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 3 × (+1) + (−3) = 0 [1]
**(c)** Any two [1] [1]: it shows only one formula unit, not the **giant lattice**; it gives no idea of the
**3D arrangement**; it does not show the **relative sizes** of the ions; it does not show the forces acting
**in all directions**. **[2]**

**12.** Indicative content, one mark per point, up to 6:

Shows correctly:
- two kinds of particle for two kinds of ion [1]
- different sizes, with the larger balls as chloride ions [1]
- a regular, repeating 3D arrangement with ions alternating [1]
- each ion surrounded by six of the other kind [1]

Shows incorrectly:
- sticks suggest bonds to particular ions, but the electrostatic forces act in all directions [1]
- gaps between balls, but the ions are packed closely together [1]
- no charges shown on the ions [1]
- only a small fragment of a lattice with a vast number of ions [1]

**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer needs points from both lists; six points on what is wrong, with
nothing on what is right, does not answer "discuss".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing unsimplified formulae from raw counts.
- Using the word "molecule" for an ionic compound.
- Giving limitations without naming the feature of the model.
- Forgetting to show the charge check when a question says "show that".
- Calling the particles atoms rather than ions.
