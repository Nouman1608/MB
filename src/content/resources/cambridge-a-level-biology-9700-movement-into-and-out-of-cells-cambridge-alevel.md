This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 4.2, Movement into and out of cells, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, and its investigations also build the practical skills tested on the practical papers. The six learning outcomes cover the six transport processes; investigations of diffusion and osmosis with plant tissue, Visking tubing and agar; calculating surface areas and volumes; agar blocks of different sizes; estimating the water potential of a tissue; and explaining water movement in plant and animal cells.

The [cell membranes and transport study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-cell-membranes-and-transport/) introduces each process and works through agar cubes and potato cylinders. This page adds what affects the rate of each process, how to read uptake data, a second way of estimating water potential (counting plasmolysed cells), and further SA:V and agar calculations. Afterwards, use the [movement into and out of cells revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-movement-into-and-out-of-cells-cambridge-alevel-revision-notes/) and the [movement into and out of cells practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-movement-into-and-out-of-cells-cambridge-alevel-practice/).

## 4.2.1 What controls the rate of each process

### Simple diffusion

Net movement down a concentration gradient by random motion, directly through the phospholipid bilayer. The rate is faster when:

- the **concentration gradient** is steeper;
- the **surface area** is larger;
- the **diffusion distance** is shorter;
- the **temperature** is higher (more kinetic energy);
- the molecule is **small and non-polar**, so it passes the hydrophobic core easily.

### Facilitated diffusion

Still passive and down the gradient, but through **channel** or **carrier proteins**, so it suits ions and polar molecules. The rate depends on the gradient **and on the number of protein molecules**.

**Worked example 1 (invented data).** Glucose uptake by red blood cells:

| External glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | 0 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uptake rate / arbitrary units | 0 | 40 | 66 | 88 | 98 | 100 |

At low concentrations the rate rises steeply with concentration. Above about 16 mmol dm⁻³ it levels off, because all the carrier proteins are working at full capacity; adding more glucose cannot increase the rate. For simple diffusion, the rate would keep rising in proportion to the gradient. A levelling-off curve is evidence that a membrane protein is involved.

### Active transport

Movement **against** a concentration gradient by carrier proteins, using energy from **ATP** made in respiration. Anything that reduces respiration reduces active transport.

**Worked example 2 (invented data).** Root tissue in a solution of potassium ions, with and without oxygen:

| Condition | K⁺ inside cells / mmol dm⁻³ | K⁺ in solution / mmol dm⁻³ |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen bubbled through | 95 | 5 |
| No oxygen | 8 | 5 |

With oxygen, cells accumulate potassium to 95 ÷ 5 = **19 times** the external concentration, so uptake must be against the gradient. Without oxygen, aerobic respiration stops, less ATP is made, and uptake stays close to what diffusion alone allows.

### Endocytosis and exocytosis

Bulk transport in vesicles, for material too large to pass through proteins. **Endocytosis** brings material in as the membrane folds round it and pinches off; **exocytosis** releases material as vesicles fuse with the cell surface membrane, for example secretion of digestive enzymes or hormones. Both need ATP, and both depend on the membrane's fluidity, which lets vesicles bud and fuse.

## 4.2.3 SA:V calculations with cylinders and cuboids

The syllabus requires calculations for cuboids and cylinders.

```
Cuboid:   SA = 2(lw + lh + wh)     V = lwh
Cylinder: SA = 2πr² + 2πrl          V = πr²l
```

**Worked example 3: same volume, different shape.**

| Cylinder | r / cm | l / cm | SA / cm² | V / cm³ | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short and wide | 1.0 | 2 | 2π(1)² + 2π(1)(2) = 18.85 | π(1)²(2) = 6.28 | 3.0 : 1 |
| Long and thin | 0.5 | 8 | 2π(0.5)² + 2π(0.5)(8) = 26.70 | π(0.5)²(8) = 6.28 | 4.25 : 1 |

The volumes are equal, but the long, thin cylinder has 42% more surface. This is why cells and organs that exchange a lot are often flattened or elongated.

## 4.2.4 Agar blocks: using time as a measure of rate

Cubes of alkaline agar containing a pH indicator are placed in acid, and the time for each to lose its colour completely is recorded. Rate can be expressed as 1 000 ÷ time.

**Worked example 4 (invented data).**

| Cube side / cm | SA:V | Time to decolourise / s | Rate (1 000 ÷ t) / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 : 1 | 240 | 4.17 |
| 2 | 3 : 1 | 950 | 1.05 |
| 3 | 2 : 1 | 2 150 | 0.47 |

Doubling the side halves SA:V but increases the time about four-fold, because acid must also travel twice as far to the centre. Larger blocks have less surface per unit volume **and** a longer diffusion distance. Control acid concentration and volume, temperature and agar batch; judge the end point against a white background, and repeat to calculate means.

## 4.2.2 Visking tubing as a model cell

Visking tubing is partially permeable: water and small molecules pass, large molecules do not.

- **Diffusion:** tubing containing starch suspension is placed in dilute iodine solution. The contents turn blue-black but the outside stays brown, because iodine molecules diffuse in while starch cannot leave.
- **Osmosis:** tubing containing concentrated sucrose solution is weighed, placed in water, then reweighed. It gains mass, because water moves by osmosis from the higher water potential outside to the lower water potential inside.

## 4.2.5 Estimating water potential by counting plasmolysed cells

Strips of red onion epidermis are left in a range of sucrose solutions, then viewed under the microscope. In each, the percentage of cells in which the protoplast has pulled away from the wall is counted. At **incipient plasmolysis**, taken as 50% of cells plasmolysed, the average cell is in equilibrium with the solution, so the tissue's water potential equals that of the solution.

**Worked example 5 (invented data).**

| Sucrose / mol dm⁻³ | 0.30 | 0.35 | 0.40 | 0.45 | 0.50 | 0.55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cells plasmolysed / % | 0 | 10 | 35 | 65 | 90 | 100 |

50% lies between 0.40 (35%) and 0.45 (65%). It is (50 − 35) ÷ (65 − 35) = 0.5 of the way across, so the concentration is 0.40 + 0.5 × 0.05 = **0.425 mol dm⁻³**. The water potential of this solution, read from a supplied table or graph, is the estimate for the tissue. Counting at least 20 cells in several fields of view makes each percentage more reliable.

## 4.2.6 Following water through tissues

Water always moves from **higher (less negative)** to **lower (more negative)** water potential. In a row of cells A (−400 kPa), B (−600 kPa) and C (−800 kPa), water moves A → B → C. Adding solutes lowers water potential; removing water by osmosis raises the concentration of solutes left behind.

Plant cells have a cell wall, so water entering makes them turgid but they do not burst. Animal cells have no wall, so they swell and may burst in a solution of higher water potential. Knowledge of solute potential and pressure potential is not expected.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Explain how you could tell, from uptake data, whether a substance enters cells by facilitated diffusion or by active transport. **[4]**

> If the concentration inside the cells becomes higher than outside, the substance is moving against its gradient, which only active transport can do. *(First distinguishing evidence.)*
>
> If uptake falls when respiration is stopped, for example by removing oxygen or adding a respiratory inhibitor, ATP is needed, so the process is active transport. *(Second test, explained.)*
>
> Facilitated diffusion stops when concentrations are equal on both sides and is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. *(Contrast.)*
>
> Both show a rate that levels off at high concentration, because both use proteins that become saturated, so this feature alone cannot tell them apart. *(Shows understanding of a common trap.)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying facilitated diffusion uses ATP, or that active transport uses channel proteins.
- Explaining a levelling-off rate as "the cells are full" instead of carrier proteins working at full capacity.
- Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
- Saying larger agar blocks take longer only because of SA:V, without the longer distance to the centre.
- Taking 0% plasmolysis, not 50%, as the equilibrium point.
- Writing that water moves to a "higher" water potential.
