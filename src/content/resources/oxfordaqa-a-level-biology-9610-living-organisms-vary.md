This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.6 Living organisms vary**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

This is a short section, but it carries a lot of data-handling skill. You must understand how a sample is chosen, how the spread of data is described, and what causes differences between individuals. ## Two kinds of variation

**Intraspecific variation** is variation between members of the same species, for example the heights of adult humans or the leaf lengths on different holly trees.

**Interspecific variation** is variation between members of different species, for example the difference between the beak sizes of different species of finch.

A reliable test: ask whether the individuals compared belong to the same species. If yes, the variation is intraspecific. If no, it is interspecific. The size of the difference does not decide the word.

Data can help. If two groups of measurements overlap a lot, they probably belong to the same species. If the ranges do not overlap, they may be different species, though this depends on the sample sizes.

## Causes of variation

Similarities and differences between individuals within a species may be the result of **genetic factors**, differences in **environmental factors**, or a combination of both.

- **Genetic factors:** different alleles of genes. Examples are blood group and flower colour in some plants. These characteristics are inherited and are little affected by the environment.
- **Environmental factors:** climate, soil, diet, light, disease. For example, a plant grown in shade is taller and paler than a genetically identical plant grown in full sun.
- **Both:** most characteristics, such as human height, depend on genes that set a potential and environmental factors, such as diet, that determine how much of it is achieved.

To separate the two causes, compare genetically identical individuals in different environments, or genetically different individuals in the same environment. Identical twins raised apart, or cloned plants grown in different soils, are classic examples. If the clones differ, the cause is environmental. If genetically different individuals in the same environment differ, the cause is at least partly genetic.

## Investigating variation: sampling

You cannot measure every member of a population, so you measure a **sample**. The aim is for the sample to represent the whole population.

### Random sampling

A sample is **random** when every individual in the population has an equal chance of being chosen. Random sampling avoids **bias**, for example choosing the tallest plants or the easiest to reach.

A standard method in the field:

1. Lay out a grid (using two tape measures along the sides of the area).
2. Use a random number generator or random number tables to produce pairs of coordinates.
3. Place the sampling frame (a quadrat, for example) at each coordinate and record the measurement.

### Chance and sample size

Even random samples from the same population differ from each other and from the population mean, **by chance**. A small sample is more likely to be unrepresentative, because a few extreme individuals can have a large effect on the mean.

An **appropriate sample size** is needed so that the data are representative. A larger sample reduces the effect of chance, and the sample mean is more likely to be close to the population mean.

The effect is easy to see in a simulation. Imagine a population with a mean of 45 mm and a standard deviation of 4 mm. In a computer simulation, 200 random samples of 5 individuals gave sample means between 38.8 and 49.7 mm. When the same simulation was repeated with 200 samples of 100 individuals, the means ranged only from 43.9 to 46.3 mm. Larger samples give means that cluster more tightly around the true value.

## The normal distribution

For many characteristics, measurements in a population form a **normal distribution**: a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve with most values near the **mean** and fewer at the extremes. The mean, median and mode are all at the centre.

**Mean** is the sum of the values divided by the number of values. It describes the centre of the data, but it does not show the spread.

**Standard deviation (SD)** describes how widely values spread around the mean. A small SD means the values are clustered close to the mean. A large SD means they are widely spread.

For a normal distribution:

- about 68 % of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean;
- about 95 % lie within two standard deviations of the mean.

You will not be asked to calculate SD in a written paper, but you may be given values and asked to interpret them. The two samples below show why.

**Worked example 1.** Two samples of ten leaves were measured (lengths in mm).

Sample A: 42, 45, 47, 44, 46, 43, 48, 45, 44, 46.
Sample B: 38, 52, 45, 41, 49, 36, 54, 45, 47, 43.

1. Mean of A: 450 / 10 = 45.0 mm. Mean of B: 450 / 10 = 45.0 mm.
2. The means are identical, but the data are very different.
3. The ranges are 42 to 48 (6 mm) for A and 36 to 54 (18 mm) for B.
4. The standard deviations (calculated here for illustration) are 1.8 mm for A and 5.8 mm for B.
5. Interpretation: sample B is much more variable. For B, 95 % of values would be expected between about 33 and 57 mm (the mean plus or minus 2 standard deviations), whereas for A the range would be much narrower.

The lesson for the examination: **do not compare samples using the mean alone**. Comment on the spread too.

### Is a difference real?

When two sample means differ, ask whether the difference could be due to chance. If the ranges of one standard deviation either side of the two means overlap greatly, the difference is probably not significant. If the ranges are well separated, the difference is more likely to be real. The specification asks only for this level of interpretation, not for a statistical test, though you should be able to say that more data and a statistical test would increase confidence.

## Worked example 2: interpreting interspecific and intraspecific data

**Data:** the mean wing length of two bird species and the standard deviation of each: species P has a mean of 62 mm (SD 2 mm); species Q has a mean of 70 mm (SD 3 mm).

1. The range within two standard deviations is 58 to 66 mm for P and 64 to 76 mm for Q.
2. The ranges overlap between 64 and 66 mm, so a few individuals could not be identified with certainty from wing length alone.
3. The mean difference (8 mm) is interspecific variation. The spread within each species is intraspecific variation.
4. A conclusion: wing length separates the species well on average, but there is some overlap, so other features are needed to identify the individuals.

## Common mistakes

- Using "interspecific" for differences within a species, or the reverse.
- Describing random sampling as "choosing without thinking". It needs a method such as random numbers.
- Comparing means and ignoring the spread.
- Saying a larger sample "removes" chance. It reduces its effect.
- Attributing a characteristic wholly to genes, or wholly to the environment, without evidence.
- Stating that a small SD means the mean is small. SD describes spread, not size.

## Exam technique

- **Give the reason for the sampling method.** "Random, to avoid bias" is the core idea.
- **Quote values.** Refer to specific means, ranges and standard deviations.
- **Use cautious wording.** Say "suggests", "may be due to chance" and "more data are needed".

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to calculate standard deviation in the examination?**
No. The specification states that you will not be required to calculate it in questions on written papers. You do need to know what it measures and how to use it.

**Is variation always a normal distribution?**
No. Some characteristics, such as blood group, show discontinuous variation with distinct categories. The normal distribution applies to continuous characteristics such as height or length.
