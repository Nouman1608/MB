These notes condense section 1.1.2, Economic assumptions, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, examined in Paper 1. There is a single tier of entry. All figures are invented. For explanations and a worked café example, read the [economic assumptions study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-economic-assumptions/); to practise, use the [economic assumptions practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-economic-assumptions-practice/).

## The two assumptions

| Agent | Assumed aim | Meaning | What it explains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Maximise their benefit | Get the most satisfaction from limited income | Demand curve slopes down: lower price, more benefit per unit of money, more bought |
| Businesses | Maximise their profit | Choose the output and price where total revenue minus total cost is largest | Supply curve slopes up: higher price, more profit per unit, more offered |

An assumption is a simplification used to build models and make predictions. It does not claim that everyone always behaves this way.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Benefit | The satisfaction a consumer gets from a good or service |
| Profit | Total revenue − total costs |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity sold |
| Profit maximisation | Producing where profit is greatest |
| Revenue maximisation | Producing where total revenue is greatest |
| Sales maximisation | Selling the greatest volume, usually without making a loss |
| Habit | A repeated purchase made without re-checking whether it is still the best choice |
| Copying behaviour | Buying what others buy rather than what gives the most personal benefit |

## Consumers: three reasons they may not maximise benefit

| Reason (specification wording) | Recognise it when… | Invented example |
|---|---|---|
| Not always good at calculating their benefits | The choice involves offers, pack sizes, contracts, upfront fees or future costs | Choosing a phone plan with a low monthly fee but a large upfront charge that makes it dearer overall |
| Habits that are hard to give up | The consumer keeps buying the same thing although a better option exists | Buying the usual brand of rice after a cheaper one of equal quality appears |
| Sometimes copy others' behaviour | The choice follows friends, celebrities or crowds | Buying a costly jacket because classmates have it |

**Evaluation points:** habits and copying can save time and effort; some goods give more benefit when others use them, such as a social media app.

## Producers: three reasons they may not maximise profit

| Reason (specification wording) | Recognise it when… | Invented example |
|---|---|---|
| Managers revenue maximise or sales maximise | Managers' pay, bonuses or prestige depend on sales, revenue or market share | Cutting prices to grow sales beyond the profit-maximising level |
| Prioritise caring for customers | The firm takes on costs to look after customers | Free home delivery for elderly shoppers |
| Complete charitable work | The firm gives money, goods or staff time to good causes | Donating surplus bread to a shelter |

**Evaluation points:** customer care and charity can build loyalty and reputation, raising long-run profit; firms in fierce competition have less freedom to depart from profit maximisation.

## Reading a profit table: method

1. Total revenue = price × output.
2. Profit = total revenue − total cost.
3. Profit maximising output: highest profit.
4. Revenue maximising output: highest total revenue.
5. Sales maximising output: highest output with profit of zero or more.
6. Profit given up = maximum profit − profit at the chosen output.

*Example (invented):*

| Output | Total revenue | Total cost | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 1 000 | 700 | 300 |
| 60 | 1 140 | 780 | 360 |
| 70 | 1 190 | 860 | 330 |
| 80 | 1 200 | 940 | 260 |
| 90 | 1 170 | 1 020 | 150 |

- Profit maximising: 60 units (profit 360).
- Revenue maximising: 80 units (revenue 1 200); profit given up = 360 − 260 = **100**.
- Sales maximising (no loss): 90 units; profit given up = 360 − 150 = **210**.

## Chains to learn

- **Sales-maximising managers:** bonus linked to sales → managers cut price and raise output → more sold → profit below its maximum.
- **Customer care:** free services → costs rise → profit falls now → loyalty and reputation grow → profit may rise later.
- **Poor calculation:** offer looks cheaper → consumer does not work out total cost → pays more for the same benefit → benefit not maximised.

## Exam traps

- Highest **revenue** is not highest **profit**: always subtract costs.
- Use the **specification's three reasons** on each side when asked for them.
- Consumers usually *want* the most benefit; the point is that they may *fail* to get it.
- Do not assume charity or customer care always lowers profit in the long run.
- In a data-response question, **name** the reason and **quote** the evidence from the case.
- Choosing the revenue-maximising output when asked for profit maximisation.
- Inventing reasons not in the specification.
- Describing a reason without linking it to the case in the question.
- Forgetting the long-run evaluation point for customer care and charity.

## Quick self-test

1. State the assumption economists make about consumers.
2. State the assumption economists make about businesses.
3. Give the formula for profit.
4. Name the three reasons in the specification why consumers may not maximise their benefit.
5. Name the three reasons why producers may not maximise their profit.
6. A shopper keeps buying a brand of soap after its price rises above a similar brand. Which reason is this?
7. Why might managers aim to maximise sales rather than profit?
8. Explain how caring for customers could increase profit in the long run.
9. Revenue is highest at 500 units (profit 2 000); profit is highest at 400 units (profit 2 600). How much profit is given up by revenue maximising?
10. Give one reason why copying others' behaviour may not reduce a consumer's benefit.
11. A firm gives 50 meals a week to a charity; each costs 4 to make. By how much do its weekly costs rise?
12. Why does the assumption of profit maximisation help to explain the upward-sloping supply curve?

### Answers

1. Consumers aim to maximise their benefit.
2. Businesses aim to maximise their profit.
3. Profit = total revenue − total costs.
4. They are not always good at calculating their benefits; they have habits that are hard to give up; they sometimes copy others' behaviour.
5. Managers may revenue maximise or sales maximise; they may prioritise caring for customers; they may complete charitable work.
6. A habit that is hard to give up.
7. Their pay, bonuses or prestige may depend on sales, revenue or market share rather than profit.
8. It builds loyalty and reputation, so customers return and recommend the firm, raising future revenue.
9. 2 600 − 2 000 = **600**.
10. It saves time spent researching, or the good gives more benefit when others use it too.
11. 50 × 4 = **200** a week.
12. A higher price means more profit on each unit, so profit-seeking firms offer more for sale.
