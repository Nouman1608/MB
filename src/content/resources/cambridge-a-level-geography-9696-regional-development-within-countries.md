## Regional disparities: what and how to measure

A **regional disparity** is a difference in development between regions of one country. It has economic dimensions (income, output, unemployment, wages) and social dimensions (life expectancy, infant mortality, school attainment, access to health care, housing quality, internet access).

**Common ways of measuring:**

- **Index relative to the national average.** The region's value divided by the national value, multiplied by 100. A score above 100 means above average.
- **Ratio of richest to poorest region.** A single figure for the spread.
- **Absolute gap.** The difference between two regions in the original units.

**Worked example (fictional country, GDP per capita in US dollars; national average 8,000).**

| Region | GDP per capita | Index (national average = 100) |
|---|---|---|
| North | 12,400 | 155 |
| Centre | 8,800 | 110 |
| East | 6,000 | 75 |
| South | 5,200 | 65 |

The ratio of richest to poorest region is 12,400 ÷ 5,200 = 2.4. North is the core; South and East form the periphery.

**Relative and absolute measures can disagree.** Suppose a core region's GDP per capita rises from 20,000 to 40,000 while a peripheral region's rises from 5,000 to 12,000. The ratio falls from 4.0 to 3.3, which is relative convergence. But the absolute gap widens from 15,000 to 28,000. Always state which measure supports your argument.

**Social and economic measures can also disagree.** A peripheral region may have a low income but good life expectancy because of strong public health services, or a resource-rich region may have high output but poor social indicators because profits leave the region.

## Core and periphery

The **core** is the region with the highest concentration of population, investment, decision-making and wealth, usually around the capital or the main port city. The **periphery** has lower incomes and depends on the core for capital, markets and services. Between them there may be an **upward transition** zone growing because of links to the core, and a **downward transition** or resource frontier zone.

Core and periphery exist at several scales: between world regions, within countries, and within regions (a provincial capital and its rural hinterland). Using the right scale for the question is a frequent discriminator.

## Kinds of initial advantage

Cumulative causation begins with one or more advantages that attract the first investment.

| Type of advantage | Example of the idea |
|---|---|
| Natural resource | Coal or iron ore attracting early heavy industry |
| Site and access | A deep-water port or river crossing on a trade route |
| Political function | The capital city, with government spending and jobs |
| Early start | The first region to industrialise, gaining a head start in skills and infrastructure |
| Climate or environment | Fertile land or a mild climate attracting settlement and farming surpluses |

## Cumulative causation step by step

1. **Initial advantage** attracts a new industry.
2. **Jobs and wages** rise, and workers migrate in.
3. **Multiplier effect:** spending by workers and firms creates demand for local services, construction and suppliers.
4. **Agglomeration economies:** firms benefit from being close together, sharing suppliers, skilled workers, ideas and infrastructure.
5. **Tax revenue** rises, paying for better roads, schools and hospitals.
6. **Better infrastructure and skills** attract further investment, and the cycle repeats.

The same logic works in reverse in a declining region: job losses cut spending, tax revenue and services, encouraging more out-migration. This downward spiral is why peripheral decline can be hard to reverse.

## Spread and backwash effects

| Backwash effects (harm the periphery) | Spread effects (help the periphery) |
|---|---|
| Young, skilled workers migrate to the core | The core buys food, raw materials and components from the periphery |
| Savings and capital flow to the core for higher returns | Firms relocate as core land, wages and congestion costs rise |
| Core firms out-compete peripheral producers | Remittances from migrants return to source areas |
| Services concentrate in the core, where demand is highest | Innovations and investment diffuse outwards along transport routes |

**When does spread outweigh backwash?** When the core suffers **diseconomies of agglomeration** (congestion, high land prices, pollution, labour shortages); when transport links make peripheral sites accessible; when governments invest in the periphery or offer incentives; and when the periphery has its own assets, such as resources, tourism potential or a regional city able to become a sub-core.

## Divergence and convergence

- **Divergence**: disparities widen as backwash dominates.
- **Convergence**: disparities narrow as spread dominates or policy intervenes.

Jeffrey Williamson (1965) argued that regional inequality tends to rise in the early stages of national development and fall later, an inverted-U pattern similar to the later stages of Friedmann's model. Evaluate it: the pattern fits some countries, but convergence is not automatic, and regions that lose their initial advantage (for example, old coalfield regions) can decline even in high-income countries.

## Applied example: Brazil

Brazil illustrates core-periphery and cumulative causation at the national scale.

- **Core.** The South-east, centred on São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, concentrates industry, finance and decision-making. Its initial advantages included ports, coffee wealth in the 19th and early 20th centuries that funded railways and early industry, and later the concentration of manufacturing around São Paulo.
- **Periphery.** The North-east has lower incomes and weaker social indicators. Drought in the semi-arid interior (the sertão) has repeatedly damaged farming.
- **Backwash.** Large-scale migration from the North-east to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro supplied labour to the core but removed young workers from the periphery.
- **Spread and policy.** The move of the capital to Brasília (inaugurated in 1960) and the Manaus Free Trade Zone (created in 1967) were attempts to push development inland. These belong in a 14.4 answer if you choose Brazil as your policy case study.

**Evaluation.** Brazil shows how an early advantage became self-reinforcing through migration, investment and infrastructure. It also shows that physical constraints (drought) and human factors (land ownership, investment decisions) combine. Whether disparities have narrowed depends on the indicator and period you choose.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a disparity without giving a measure or a scale.
- Describing Myrdal's model with no mechanism between steps.
- Listing backwash effects only; the syllabus names spread effects too.
- Claiming convergence from a ratio without checking the absolute gap.
- Treating the periphery as uniform; sub-cores and resource regions can grow.
- Mixing up 14.3 theory with 14.4 policy: in 14.3, policy is one condition for spread, not the main focus.

## Command words

- **Describe** a regional disparity: name regions, indicators and values.
- **Explain** cumulative causation: give the chain from initial advantage to self-reinforcing growth.
- **Assess** the relative importance of spread and backwash: weigh both, with evidence, and conclude.
