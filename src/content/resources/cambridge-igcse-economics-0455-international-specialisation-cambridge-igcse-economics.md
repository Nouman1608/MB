The syllabus asks for the basis of specialisation "in broad terms", so you need to explain the two reasons clearly; you are not required to carry out formal trade-theory calculations, although opportunity cost reasoning can support an answer.

## Specialisation at a national level

**Specialisation** means concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products. At national level, a country devotes much of its land, labour, capital and enterprise to producing particular goods or services, exports some of them, and imports many of the other things it needs. It is the same idea as the division of labour within a firm, applied to whole economies.

No country is fully specialised. The question is always how far a country concentrates its resources, and what it gains and risks by doing so.

## 6.1.1 The first basis: superior resource allocation

Factors of production differ in quantity and quality between countries. Specialisation lets each country use its resources where they are most productive.

| Country (invented) | Resources it has in abundance | Likely specialisms |
|---|---|---|
| Country A | Fertile land, warm climate, plentiful rain | Fruit, coffee, cut flowers |
| Country B | Large mineral deposits | Copper and iron ore |
| Country C | Highly educated workforce, research universities | Software, medicines, financial services |
| Country D | Large, lower-wage labour force | Clothing and assembly of electronic goods |
| Country E | Coastline, historic sites, sunshine | Tourism |

If Country A tried to make medicines and Country C tried to grow coffee, both would get far less output from the same resources. Allocating each country's resources to their most productive use raises total output, which is the first reason for specialisation.

Resource endowments can change. A country that invests in education can move into skilled services; a country that exhausts a mineral loses that basis.

## 6.1.1 The second basis: cheaper production methods

A country may produce a good more cheaply because of:

- **Technology:** better machines and methods give more output per worker.
- **Economies of scale:** large industries that serve world markets spread fixed costs over more units and can buy inputs in bulk.
- **Skills built up over time:** workers and managers in an established industry become expert, and supporting suppliers grow up nearby.
- **Lower input costs:** cheaper labour, energy or raw materials.

### Worked example 1: does importing pay once transport is included?

A tonne of glassware costs 50 to make in Country F. Country G can make it for 35, because its factories are larger and more automated.

| | Transport and trade cost per tonne | Cost of imported glassware | Cheaper option for Country F |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case 1 | 8 | 35 + 8 = 43 | Import (43 < 50) |
| Case 2 | 18 | 35 + 18 = 53 | Make at home (50 < 53) |

Specialisation and trade only pay when the cost advantage is larger than the cost of moving goods between countries. Lower transport costs, for example from containers and better ports, make more specialisation worthwhile. Heavy, low-value or perishable goods are often still produced near where they are used.

## 6.1.2 Advantages and disadvantages for each group

### Consumers

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Lower prices, because goods come from the cheapest producers | Less choice of home-produced goods as some industries shrink |
| Wider choice, including goods the country cannot make at all | Supply can be interrupted by problems abroad, such as poor harvests or transport delays |
| Often better quality, because producers specialise and compete in world markets | Prices of imports rise if the exchange rate falls |

### Firms

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Access to larger, world-wide markets | Competition from cheaper foreign producers can force firms in non-specialised industries to close |
| Economies of scale lower average cost | Dependence on foreign buyers: a fall in demand abroad cuts sales |
| Cheaper imported raw materials and components | Imported inputs can be delayed or become dearer |

### The economy

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More output from the same resources, so higher living standards | **Over-specialisation**: dependence on a few products leaves income exposed to falls in their price or demand |
| Export earnings pay for imports and can support investment | **Structural unemployment** as declining industries close and workers lack skills for new ones |
| Tax revenue from successful export industries | Resources such as minerals or soil can be used up faster |
| Faster growth as resources move to productive uses | Dependence on other countries for essentials such as food or fuel |

### Worked example 2: who gains and who loses inside one country

Country H (invented) opens its market to imported furniture, which is 30% cheaper than local furniture. It specialises instead in processed foods, where it has a large farming base.

- **Consumers** buying a table that cost 400 now pay 400 × 0.7 = 280, saving 120.
- **Food firms** sell to new export markets; suppose one doubles output from 50,000 to 100,000 units and, with fixed costs of 200,000 spread more widely, the fixed cost per unit falls from 4 to 2.
- **Furniture workers** whose firms close may face months of unemployment while they retrain.

The economy as a whole gains, but the gains and losses fall on different people. A good answer names who gains and who loses.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Discuss whether a country benefits from specialising in a narrow range of products. **[8]**

> By concentrating resources where they are most productive, such as fertile land on crops, the country gets more output from the same resources, which can raise income per head. *(Superior resource allocation.)*
>
> Large export industries gain economies of scale, lowering costs, and export earnings pay for a wide range of imports, so consumers gain lower prices and more choice. *(Cheaper production methods; consumer benefit.)*
>
> However, if most export earnings come from one or two products, a fall in world price or demand cuts income, jobs and tax revenue together. *(Over-specialisation.)*
>
> Workers in industries that cannot compete may become structurally unemployed, and some resources, such as minerals, may run out. *(Further costs.)*
>
> A country benefits from specialisation when its products have steady demand and when it keeps some variety in its exports. A very narrow range is risky, so most countries gain from specialising in several products rather than one. *(Judgement with a condition.)*

## Exam technique

- **Define:** "Specialisation at a national level is when a country concentrates its resources on producing a limited range of goods and services."
- **Explain:** state a basis, then the result, such as fertile land → higher crop output per worker → lower cost.
- **Analyse:** follow a chain from specialisation to lower costs, to lower prices, to higher real incomes.
- **Discuss:** cover consumers, firms and the economy if the question names them, and reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Giving only the advantages, or only for one group.
- Describing specialisation by individual workers when the question is about countries.
- Ignoring transport costs when judging whether importing is cheaper.
- Saying specialisation "causes unemployment" without explaining structural unemployment in declining industries.
- Forgetting that resource endowments can change over time.
