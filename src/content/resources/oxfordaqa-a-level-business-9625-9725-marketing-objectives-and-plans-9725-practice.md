This set tests **3.1.2.1 Marketing objectives and plans** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 1 Business and markets). Section A has short-answer and calculation questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Every business and figure is invented. Before you start, use the [marketing objectives and plans study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-marketing-objectives-and-plans-9725/) and the [marketing objectives and plans revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-marketing-objectives-and-plans-9725-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term market share. **[2]**

**2.** State the three elements of a marketing plan. **[3]**

**3.** Orla's Bakery sold 18,500 loaves last month at an average selling price of $3.20. Calculate its sales value for the month. **[2]**

**4.** Brightfield Broadband started the year with 24,000 customers. During the year it gained 4,200 new customers and it ended the year with 25,800 customers. Calculate its customer retention rate. **[2]**

**5.** Harlow Paints had sales of $4.2m in a market worth $30m in Year 1. In Year 2 its sales were $4.5m and the market was worth $36m.

**(a)** Calculate Harlow's market share in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what these figures show about Harlow's performance. **[2]**

**6.** A software firm spent $12,500 on a webinar campaign. It estimates that the campaign generated $20,000 of profit from marketing activities. Calculate the return on marketing spending. **[2]**

**7.** Calder Cycles had sales of $3.15m last year from 7 stores. It employed an average of 45 employees. Calculate:

**(a)** sales per store **[2]**

**(b)** sales per employee. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** way in which strong brand loyalty can help a business when setting its prices. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Pemberly Pet Foods ran two campaigns last year.

| | Campaign X: television | Campaign Y: influencer posts |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing spend | $24,000 | $8,000 |
| Profit from marketing activities | $30,000 | $14,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the return on marketing spending for each campaign. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why Pemberly might still choose to repeat Campaign X. **[4]**

**10.** Fennick Juices makes cold-pressed juice. Its factory is running at 97% capacity utilisation. The marketing manager wants a plan to raise sales volume by 20% next year. Analyse how Fennick's capacity could influence its marketing plan. **[6]**

**11.** Ashgrove Interiors makes premium furniture. Explain how a fall in consumer incomes could affect its marketing objectives. **[4]**

**12.** Tidewell Coffee runs 30 cafés in a city where the coffee shop market grew by 2% last year, down from 8% the year before. Tidewell's market share rose from 11% to 12%, mainly from a two-for-one offer. Its customer retention rate for loyalty-app users fell from 82% to 74%. A new rival chain plans to open 10 cafés next year. The marketing director wants next year's main objective to be a market share of 14%. Evaluate whether Tidewell should make market share or customer retention its main marketing objective. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The sales of one business [1] as a percentage of the total sales of all businesses in that market [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Marketing objectives [1]; the marketing mix [1]; the marketing budget [1]. **[3]**

**3.** 18,500 × 3.20 [1] = **$59,200** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** (25,800 − 4,200) ÷ 24,000 × 100 [1] = 21,600 ÷ 24,000 × 100 = **90%** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Year 1: 4.2 ÷ 30 × 100 = **14%** [1]. Year 2: 4.5 ÷ 36 × 100 = **12.5%** [1].

**(b)** Harlow's sales rose, but its market share fell by 1.5 percentage points [1], because the market grew by 20% while Harlow's sales grew by only about 7%, so rivals gained more of the extra demand [1]. **[4]**

**6.** 20,000 ÷ 12,500 × 100 [1] = **160%** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 3,150,000 ÷ 7 [1] = **$450,000** per store [1].

**(b)** 3,150,000 ÷ 45 [1] = **$70,000** per employee [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Loyal customers keep buying the brand rather than switching to rivals [1], so their demand is less sensitive to price [1]. The business can charge a premium price, or raise prices, without losing many sales, which raises its sales value and margin [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** X: 30,000 ÷ 24,000 × 100 [1] = **125%** [1]. Y: 14,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 [1] = **175%** [1].

**(b)** Campaign X produced more profit in total ($30,000 against $14,000) [1], so if Pemberly's aim is the largest profit rather than the best ratio, X contributes more [1]. Campaign Y may not keep its high return if it is scaled up, because the most interested followers have already been reached [1], whereas television reaches a wider audience that influencer posts may never reach [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* In part (b) a reason earns its second mark only when it is linked to the figures or to Pemberly's situation.

**10.** At 97% utilisation Fennick has only about 3% spare capacity [1], so a 20% rise in sales volume could not be supplied from the current factory [1]. If promotion raises demand beyond output, shops will run out and customers may switch to rival juices, harming the brand [1]. The plan may therefore set a smaller volume objective, or one focused on sales value through a higher price instead of volume [1]. The budget may move from mass promotion towards activities that raise price or loyalty [1]. Alternatively, the plan could be timed to match extra capacity, which links marketing to operations and finance decisions [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Premium furniture is a luxury purchase that customers can delay [1], so a fall in incomes is likely to reduce demand for Ashgrove's products [1]. A sales volume or growth objective may become unrealistic, so Ashgrove may switch to protecting market share among wealthier customers [1] or to retention and repeat sales through after-sales service, which are easier to achieve when few new buyers are entering the market [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a supported judgement, for example:

- A share objective builds on recent success: share rose from 11% to 12% [1].
- With market growth slowing to 2%, extra share must be taken from rivals [1], which needs more price promotions like the two-for-one offer [1].
- Those offers lower sales value per customer and margin, so share may rise while profit falls [1].
- Reaching 14% will be harder as a new rival opens 10 cafés and competes for the same customers [1].
- The fall in retention from 82% to 74% means about one in four loyalty-app users is lost each year [1], so new customers won by offers are replacing customers who leave rather than adding to the total [1].
- Customers attracted by two-for-one deals may be price-sensitive and the most likely to try the new rival [1].
- A retention objective is likely to be cheaper to achieve, through the app, service and rewards, than winning new customers [1].
- Higher retention gives repeat sales and a stable base before the rival arrives [1].
- However, if the director's share target is linked to the corporate objective of growth, retention alone may not satisfy the owners [1].
- Judgement: Tidewell should make retention its main objective for the next year, with share as a secondary target, because the slowing market and new rival make 14% costly to reach, and stopping the loss of customers protects both share and profit [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers use the 2% growth figure, the fall in retention and the new rival together, rather than discussing the two objectives in general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing market share as a decimal or forgetting the % sign.
- Using end customers ÷ start customers for retention, which counts new customers as kept.
- Saying the campaign with the higher % made more profit.
- Describing capacity in general without linking it to the size of the planned rise in demand.
- Ending an evaluation without a clear, justified choice.
