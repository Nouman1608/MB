This guide covers **The role of markets**, the second content section of Component 01, Microeconomics, in the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), for first assessment in 2021. The section runs from 2.1 Specialisation and trade to 2.11 Government intervention. Rather than re-teach each of its eleven topics separately, this guide shows how they form one argument and then follows a single invented market, for e-bikes, through every stage of it. That is how data-response and essay questions use the section: one market, several tools.

For condensed recall use [the role of markets revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-the-role-of-markets-revision-notes/), and for exam-style work [the role of markets practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-the-role-of-markets-practice/). Topic-by-topic teaching is in [demand, supply and consumer and producer surplus](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-demand-supply-and-consumer-and-producer-surplus/), [elasticity of demand and supply](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-elasticity-of-demand-and-supply-ped-yed-xed-pes/), [market efficiency and market failure](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-economic-efficiency-and-the-nature-of-market-failure/) and [government intervention](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-government-intervention-cost-benefit-analysis-and/).

## The eleven topics as one argument

| Stage | Topics | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Why markets exist | 2.1 Specialisation and trade | Why do people trade, and why do they use money rather than barter? |
| How markets work | 2.2 Demand, 2.3 Supply, 2.5 The interaction of markets | How are price and quantity set, and how do related markets affect each other? |
| Who gains | 2.4 Consumer and producer surplus | How much do buyers and sellers benefit from trade? |
| How responsive | 2.6 Elasticity, 2.7 The concept of the margin | By how much do quantities change when price or income changes? |
| When markets fail | 2.8 Externalities, 2.9 Information failure, 2.10 Public goods | Why might the market outcome differ from the best outcome for society? |
| What government can do | 2.11 Government intervention | Which tools correct failure, and when do they make things worse? |

Specialisation raises output but forces people to exchange; money makes exchange cheap by removing the need for a double coincidence of wants. Markets then set prices through demand and supply. Those prices allocate resources well unless some costs or benefits are ignored, information is poor, or a good cannot be sold to individuals. Then the case for intervention arises, along with the risk of government failure.

## The e-bike market, step by step

All figures are invented. P is the price in currency units (cu) and Q is thousands of e-bikes a year in one city.

- Demand: Qd = 1,000 − 0.5P
- Supply: Qs = 0.75P − 250

### Step 1: equilibrium (2.2, 2.3, 2.5)

Setting Qd = Qs: 1,000 − 0.5P = 0.75P − 250, so 1.25P = 1,250 and **P = 1,000 cu**, **Q = 500 thousand**. At any price above 1,000 there is excess supply, and sellers cut prices; below it there is excess demand, and buyers bid prices up. Each shift analysed below changes one factor only: this is **ceteris paribus**, which the specification lists under 2.5.

### Step 2: surplus (2.4)

Demand reaches zero at P = 2,000, and supply starts at P = 250 ÷ 0.75 = 333.3.

- **Consumer surplus** = ½ × 500 × (2,000 − 1,000) = **250,000 thousand cu**, or 250 million cu.
- **Producer surplus** = ½ × 500 × (1,000 − 333.3) = **166.7 million cu**.

Consumer surplus is the area below demand and above price: the gap between what buyers would pay and what they do pay. Producer surplus is the area above supply and below price.

### Step 3: elasticity at the equilibrium (2.6)

For a straight line, PED = (change in Q ÷ change in P) × P ÷ Q.

- PED = −0.5 × 1,000 ÷ 500 = **−1.0**, unit elastic. A small price change leaves total revenue (500,000 thousand cu) almost unchanged.
- PES = 0.75 × 1,000 ÷ 500 = **1.5**, price elastic supply.

### Step 4: a rise in income

Incomes in the city rise by 10%, and demand rises by 100 thousand at every price: Qd = 1,100 − 0.5P.

- At the old price, quantity demanded rises from 500 to 600, a 20% rise. **YED** = 20 ÷ 10 = **+2.0**: e-bikes are a normal good and income elastic.
- New equilibrium: 1,100 − 0.5P = 0.75P − 250, so P = **1,080 cu** and Q = **560 thousand**.
- The shift of demand causes an **extension** of supply along the supply curve, not a shift of supply.

### Step 5: related markets (2.2, 2.3, 2.5)

The specification names several links between markets.

| Link | Meaning | E-bike example | Effect of the rise in e-bike demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joint demand | Goods bought together | E-bikes and helmets or batteries | Demand for helmets rises |
| Competitive demand | Substitutes | E-bikes and bus travel | Demand for bus travel falls |
| Composite demand | One good wanted for several uses | Lithium, used in bike and car batteries | Less lithium is left for other uses, so its price rises for every user |
| Joint supply | Producing one good also yields another | Not e-bikes; beef and leather is the classic case | — |
| Competitive supply | Resources used for one good cannot make another | A factory switching from pedal bikes to e-bikes | Supply of pedal bikes falls |

**Cross elasticity (XED)** measures these links. If bus journeys fall by 4% when e-bike prices fall by 10%, XED = −4 ÷ −10 = **+0.4**: weak substitutes.

### Step 6: the margin (2.7)

Marginal values are the change in a total from one more unit. If a rider's total utility from her first, second and third weekly e-bike rental is 30, 52 and 66 units, marginal utility is 30, 22 and 14: **diminishing marginal utility**. Each extra ride is worth less, so she will take more rides only at a lower price, which is why the individual demand curve slopes down.

### Step 7: an externality (2.8)

Return to the original demand curve. Each e-bike used in place of a car reduces congestion and emissions. Suppose this **marginal external benefit** is 200 cu per bike.

- Marginal social benefit = marginal private benefit + 200, so in quantity form MSB: Q = 1,100 − 0.5P.
- With no external cost, marginal social cost is the supply curve. The social optimum is where MSB = MSC: P = 1,080 cu and Q = **560 thousand**.
- The market produces only 500 thousand, an under-consumption of 60 thousand.
- **Welfare loss** = ½ × 60 × 200 = **6,000 thousand cu**, or 6 million cu.

This is a **positive consumption externality**: third parties gain, but buyers ignore that gain, so too few are bought.

### Step 8: intervention and government failure (2.11)

A subsidy of 200 cu per bike paid to producers shifts supply to Qs = 0.75(P + 200) − 250 = 0.75P − 100.

- New equilibrium with the original demand: 1,000 − 0.5P = 0.75P − 100, so **P = 880 cu** paid by consumers and **Q = 560 thousand**, the social optimum.
- Producers receive 880 + 200 = 1,080 cu.
- Consumers gain 120 cu per bike (60% of the subsidy) and producers 80 cu (40%), because demand (PED −1.0) is less elastic than supply (PES 1.5): the less elastic side gains more.
- Cost to the government = 200 × 560 thousand = **112 million cu** a year.

**Government failure risks.** The 200 cu figure is an estimate. If the true external benefit were 100 cu, a 200 cu subsidy would push output past the social optimum and create a new welfare loss. The 112 million cu has an opportunity cost in other public spending. Some subsidised buyers would have bought an e-bike anyway, so part of the money changes no behaviour. Alternatives include information provision, regulation of car use, or building cycle lanes.

### Step 9: information failure and public goods (2.9, 2.10)

- **Information failure.** If buyers underestimate the fuel and fare savings from an e-bike, they buy fewer than they would with full information. Information provision tackles the cause directly and costs far less than a subsidy.
- **Cycle lanes** are close to a **quasi-public good**: it is hard to exclude riders who have not paid, but lanes become rival when crowded. Free-riding means a private firm could not charge for them, which is why they are usually provided by government.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting supply when only demand has changed; a demand shift causes an extension or contraction of supply.
- Calculating PED from the demand intercept instead of at the equilibrium price and quantity.
- Measuring a welfare loss triangle with the wrong height: it is the external benefit per unit, not the price.
- Assuming a subsidy is always worthwhile; the size of the external benefit must be estimated first.
- Mixing up joint demand (complements) with joint supply (by-products).

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the equation you set equal, then the price, then the quantity, with units.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** label the axes (price, quantity), the curves (D, S, MSB, MSC) and the old and new equilibria.
- **Evaluate:** in intervention questions, weigh effectiveness against cost and the risk of government failure, and reach a verdict that depends on a stated factor, such as how accurately the external benefit can be measured.
