This set covers section 10.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: direct
and indirect models of media effects, their strengths and limitations, and evidence about how far
behaviour is influenced by the media. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed
explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Outline the hypodermic syringe model of media effects. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by an *opinion leader* in the two-step flow model. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** of the uses or gratifications described by McQuail, Blumler and Brown
(1972). **[3]**

**4.** Explain what Klapper meant by *selective perception*. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what Gerbner meant by *mean world syndrome*. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why Cantril's study of the 1938 *War of the Worlds* broadcast is a problem for the
hypodermic syringe model. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** difference between the normative model and the cultural effects model.
**[4]**

**8.** Identify the **three** types of reading in Hall's encoding/decoding model. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** limitations of the uses and gratifications model. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which research methods affect conclusions about media effects.
**[8]**

**11.** "Indirect effects models explain media influence better than direct effects models."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "The media has only a limited influence on human behaviour." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** Media messages are "injected" into the audience [1], which is seen as a passive mass of
isolated individuals [1], producing immediate and similar effects on everyone exposed [1]. **[3]**

**2.** A person who pays close attention to the media [1] and passes on and interprets its messages
for others in their social group, so influence is indirect [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any three: diversion [1]; personal relationships [1]; personal identity [1];
surveillance [1]. **[3]**

**4.** People interpret media content in line with their existing beliefs [1], so the same message
can be understood differently and usually reinforces what people already think [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Gerbner's finding that heavy television viewers were more likely to see the world as
dangerous [1] than light viewers [1], suggesting that long-term exposure to violent content
cultivates fear and distrust [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The broadcast is often cited as proof of direct effects [1]. Cantril (1940) found that
reactions varied [1]: listeners with more education or who checked other sources were less likely
to be frightened, so the audience was not uniform or passive [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The normative model says media influence depends on group norms [1], so messages that fit
are accepted and others rejected [1]. The cultural effects model says repeated messages over many
years slowly shape "common sense" [1], so the focus is long-term cumulative change rather than
group filtering [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Dominant or preferred [1]; negotiated [1]; oppositional [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per limitation: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example or link to
another model [1].
- **Assumes people know their needs** [1]: the model relies on audiences reporting why they use
  media [1], but people may not be aware of influences on them [1]; the cultural effects model
  suggests long-term shaping happens without notice [1].
- **Media may create the needs they satisfy** [1]: advertising and programming can generate
  desires, for example for particular body images or consumer goods [1]; so the audience's
  "choice" is partly produced by the media [1]; Marxists would add that this serves commercial
  interests [1].
- Also creditable: underplays the power of media owners; individualistic, neglecting class and
  group influence. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study [1], evaluate [1].
- **Laboratory experiments** [1]: control variables and show short-term imitation [1], as in
  Bandura's Bobo doll studies [1]; but they are artificial and may exaggerate effects, supporting
  direct-effects claims too easily [1].
- **Qualitative audience research** [1]: interviews and group discussions reveal how people
  interpret media [1], as in Morley's *Nationwide* study (1980) or Philo's *Seeing and Believing*
  (1990) [1]; they support active-audience or cultural effects models but are hard to generalise
  [1].
- Also creditable: panel surveys (*The People's Choice*, 1944) show personal influence; cultivation
  surveys show correlation, not cause. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level explains direct and several indirect models accurately,
compares them using evidence and methods, and reaches a judgement. Indicative content:
- **Direct effects:** hypodermic syringe; propaganda fears; Bandura; strengths of simplicity and
  experimental control; limitations of artificiality and passivity; Cantril (1940) undermines the
  classic case.
- **Indirect effects:** two-step flow (Lazarsfeld et al., 1944; Katz and Lazarsfeld, 1955); uses
  and gratifications (McQuail, Blumler and Brown, 1972); normative model; Klapper (1960) on
  reinforcement; cultural effects (Gerbner; Philo, 1990); reception analysis (Hall; Morley).
- **Evaluation:** indirect models fit evidence of varied responses; but cultural effects research
  suggests influence can still be powerful over time; online "influencers" complicate two-step
  flow; each model depends on its method.
- **Judgement:** for example, indirect models explain more, but the most convincing combines
  active audiences with long-term cultural effects. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs evidence of limited influence against evidence of
long-term or group-specific influence and distinguishes attitudes from behaviour. Indicative
content:
- **For limited influence:** Klapper (1960) on selective exposure and reinforcement; personal
  influence (Lazarsfeld et al., 1944); active readings (Hall; Morley); Cantril (1940) on varied
  responses; uses and gratifications.
- **Against:** cultivation and mean world syndrome (Gerbner); Philo (1990) on news shaping beliefs
  about events; normative model on influence where other agencies are weak; link to 10.3 on
  imitation, moral panics and body image.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish short-term behaviour from long-term attitudes; influence varies by
  group, age and access to other sources; methods affect findings.
- **Judgement:** for example, direct short-term effects on behaviour are limited, but cumulative
  influence on attitudes and beliefs is substantial. **[35]**
