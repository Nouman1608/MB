This guide follows the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification, version 1.2, for exams from May/June 2026. It covers the manufacturing organisations part of section 3.4, Preparation of financial statements. The qualification is untiered, and the specification lists content 3 to 5 for Paper 2, Financial Statements. You learn how a business that makes its own goods works out what the goods cost, and how that figure feeds the income statement and the statement of financial position. ## What the specification asks for

| Specification point | Where it is taught here |
|---|---|
| Financial statements of manufacturers: manufacturing account, income statement, statement of financial position | Sections 1, 5, 6, 7 |
| Prime costs | Section 2 |
| Overhead cost | Section 3 |
| Factory cost of finished goods | Section 4 |
| Adjustments from the use of concepts (other payables, other receivables, depreciation, provision for doubtful debts, goods taken for own use, inventory) | Sections 3, 5, 7 |
| Comment on the statements | Section 8 |

The specification says the provision for unrealised profit will not be examined. That means finished goods are moved from the factory to the income statement at cost, with no profit added by the factory.

## 1. Why a manufacturer needs a manufacturing account

A trading business buys goods and sells them. A manufacturer buys materials, pays workers and runs machines to turn them into goods. The manufacturing account collects every cost of making the goods and ends with one figure, the **factory cost of finished goods**. That figure then takes the place of purchases in the income statement.

The three statements fit together like this.

| Statement | Question it answers | Ends with |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing account | What did it cost to make the goods finished this year? | Factory cost of finished goods |
| Income statement | Did selling the goods make a profit? | Profit for the year |
| Statement of financial position | What does the business own and owe on the last day? | Net assets equal to equity |

## 2. Prime cost

**Prime cost** is the cost of the materials used in the goods plus the wages of the people who make them. Both are **direct costs**, which means you can trace them to the goods.

| Line | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Opening inventory of raw materials | Add |
| Purchases of raw materials | Add |
| Carriage inwards on raw materials | Add |
| Returns outwards of raw materials | Deduct |
| Closing inventory of raw materials | Deduct |
| **Cost of raw materials used** | The result |
| Direct wages | Add |
| **Prime cost** | Raw materials used plus direct wages |

Carriage inwards is the cost of bringing materials to the factory, so it belongs here. Carriage outwards is the cost of delivering to customers, so it belongs in the income statement.

## 3. Overhead cost

**Overheads** (factory overheads) are the costs of running the factory that you cannot trace to one unit of output. Typical items are indirect wages (supervisors, cleaners, maintenance staff), factory power, factory insurance, factory rent and rates, and depreciation of machinery.

Four rules from the adjustments in the specification apply.

- **Other payables.** A cost owing at the year end is added to the amount paid. Power paid plus power owing gives the power cost for the year.
- **Other receivables.** A cost paid in advance is deducted from the amount paid. A prepayment is an other receivable in the statement of financial position.
- **Depreciation.** Depreciation of factory machinery is a factory overhead. Depreciation of office equipment is not. The specification names the straight line and reducing balance methods.
- **Shared costs.** When one bill covers both the factory and the office, split it on the basis the question gives, such as floor area, and charge each part to its own section.

## 4. Factory cost of finished goods

Add the overheads to the prime cost:

Factory cost of finished goods = prime cost + overheads

The result is the cost of the goods completed in the year. The factory then passes the goods to the finished goods store at this cost. The specification does not name work in progress as a topic, so the examples here have none. If a question gives work in progress figures, treat them as a further adjustment and follow the instruction on the paper.

## 5. The income statement of a manufacturer

The top half changes. The cost of sales uses the factory cost, not purchases.

| Line | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Revenue less sales returns | Net sales |
| Opening inventory of finished goods | Add |
| Factory cost of finished goods | Add |
| Closing inventory of finished goods | Deduct |
| Goods taken by the owner for own use | Deduct, then debit drawings |
| **Cost of sales** | The result |
| **Gross profit** | Net sales less cost of sales |

Below gross profit, list the expenses of selling, distribution and administration, including adjustments for the provision for doubtful debts and irrecoverable debts. Gross profit less these expenses is the **profit for the year**.

## 6. The statement of financial position

The headings are the ones used for any sole trader. A manufacturer has two things to remember.

- Inventories are shown in two lines: raw materials and finished goods. Both are current assets at the closing figures used in the statements.
- Machinery and factory buildings sit with the non-current assets, at cost less accumulated depreciation.

## 7. Worked example: Ashvane Joinery

## 8. Commenting on the statements

You may be asked to comment on a manufacturer's results. Work from figures you can calculate from the statements.

- **Overheads as a share of prime cost.** Ashvane's overheads are 46,700 against a prime cost of 119,900, or 38.9%. If this share rises year on year, ask whether factory running costs are being controlled.
- **Gross profit margin.** Gross profit of 94,100 on net sales of 258,000 is 36.5%. A fall in the margin can come from a higher factory cost or lower selling prices.
- **Cost of materials against wages.** Materials used (71,300) and direct wages (48,600) are the two parts of prime cost. A change in either points to a different problem: a price from suppliers, or labour hours.

A comment needs a figure, a comparison and a reason. State that a limit applies: one year of figures cannot show a trend.

## 9. Common errors

- Putting office depreciation or office salaries in the manufacturing account.
- Deducting closing inventory of finished goods in the manufacturing account. It belongs in the income statement.
- Using purchases of raw materials as the prime cost without adjusting for inventory.
- Charging all of a shared bill to the factory.
- Forgetting that goods taken for own use are deducted from cost of sales and added to drawings.
- Showing only one inventories line in the statement of financial position.
