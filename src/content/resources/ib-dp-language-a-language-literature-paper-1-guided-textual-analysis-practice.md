## Questions

### Passage A -- public information poster (original text written for Marlbridge)

> [Layout: a tall poster. The top half is a photograph of a single drop of water hanging from a
> tap, shown very large against a dark blue background. The headline is in large white capitals;
> the bullet points are smaller.]
>
> **EVERY DROP HAS SOMEWHERE TO BE.**
>
> Don't let it be the drain.
>
> Last August, Eastholm's reservoirs fell to their lowest level in twenty years. You saw the brown
> grass. You lived with the hosepipe ban. This summer, you can do something about it.
>
> - Turn off the tap while you brush.
> - Fix that drip. It takes ten minutes.
> - Water the garden after sunset, not at noon.
>
> Eastholm Water Board. Your water. Your town. Your choice.

**1.** Identify the text type, the target audience and the purpose of Passage A. **[3]**

**2.** Describe one visual feature of Passage A and explain its effect. **[2]**

**3.** Analyse how the final line of Passage A supports the poster's purpose. **[3]**

### Passage B -- opinion column (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Let the library be loud**
>
> Last Tuesday I watched a librarian shush a four-year-old. The child had found a book about
> volcanoes and wanted, very badly, to tell someone. She was told to whisper. She put the book back.
>
> Ask yourself: who is actually in our library on a weekday afternoon? Pensioners who have not
> spoken to anyone since Sunday. Teenagers with nowhere warm to go. Parents with toddlers who have
> run out of ideas.
>
> These people do not need a reading room. They need a living room.
>
> Of course, the council will say that noise drives serious readers away. Put them upstairs. The
> building has three floors and one rule; it could easily have three floors and three rules.
>
> A library that only allows whispering is teaching a lesson, and it is the wrong one: that books
> are for keeping quiet about. Let the volcano girl shout. She has found something worth shouting
> about.

**4.** Explain how the opening anecdote prepares the reader for the writer's argument. **[3]**

**5.** Analyse how the writer deals with the council's objection in the fourth paragraph. **[3]**

**6.** Analyse the effect of the one-line third paragraph. **[3]**

### Passage C -- speech at a bridge reopening (original text written for Marlbridge)

> Friends, neighbours, and those of you who have driven the long way round for eighteen months --
> welcome back to the river.
>
> A hundred years ago, on this spot, our great-grandparents opened the first bridge. They had rope, stone and stubbornness. When the flood took their bridge in
> March of last year, some said we should not rebuild it. Too costly. Too slow. Too old-fashioned.
>
> Well, look behind me.
>
> This bridge is steel where theirs was stone. But it stands exactly where theirs stood, because a
> town is not only its people. It is the paths its people have always walked.
>
> So walk it. Walk it today, walk it tomorrow, and walk it when the next flood comes, because we
> will build it again.

**7.** Explain how the speech depends on the time and place in which it is delivered. **[4]**

**8.** Here is a weak paragraph on Passage A, written in answer to the guiding question "How does
the poster persuade readers to change their habits?"

*The poster uses a lot of techniques. It uses a picture of a tap. It says "Last August, Eastholm's
reservoirs fell to their lowest level in twenty years." It also uses bullet points. This makes the
reader want to save water.*

Identify two weaknesses and rewrite the first two sentences to fix them. **[4]**

**9.** Extended task. Plan a full guided analysis of Passage B in response to the guiding
question: **How does the writer try to change the reader's view of what a library is for?** Give a
thesis and a paragraph plan with evidence. **[8]**

**10.** Extended task. Write the introduction and one body paragraph of a guided analysis of
Passage C in response to the guiding question: **How does the speaker connect the new bridge to
the town's past?** **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Text type: a **public information poster** from a water company. [1] Audience: **households
in Eastholm**, who lived through last summer's drought. [1] Purpose: **to persuade them to use
less water this summer** through small daily changes. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "People" is too vague; strong answers name the reader the text assumes.

**2.** Feature: a **single drop of water shown very large** in the top half, against dark blue. [1]
Effect: something tiny becomes the dominant image, so each drop seems precious, as the headline
"EVERY DROP" confirms. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A visual point needs precise detail (size, position, colour) plus an effect.

**3.** A **list of three** [1] with the repeated possessive **"Your"**. [1] It moves from resource
(water) to community (town) to individual (choice), so after three imperatives, saving water ends as
the reader's own decision, not an order. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers explain the order of the three items; weak ones only count them.

**4.** The anecdote shows the rule's cost in one small, sympathetic moment. [1] The short final
sentence "She put the book back." makes the loss of curiosity quiet and sad. [1] The child returns at
the end ("Let the volcano girl shout"), so the opening frames the whole column. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Linking opening to ending shows you are analysing structure, not one paragraph.

**5.** The writer states the objection fairly: "noise drives serious readers away". [1] The reply
is an immediate, blunt imperative: "Put them upstairs." [1] The practical detail "three floors and
three rules" makes the proposal sound simple, so the writer seems fair and confident. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The concession is half the point; credit goes to explaining how the rebuttal
turns the objection to the writer's advantage.

**6.** The paragraph is built on **contrast in a parallel structure**: "reading room" against
"living room". [1] Isolated on its own line, it stands out as the column's central claim. [1]
"Living room" connotes warmth, home and conversation, which fits the lonely people just listed, so
the library's purpose is redefined in two short sentences. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers connect the layout (one line) to the language (the contrast).

**7.** Place: the speech is given at the river, "on this spot", to people who can see the bridge. [1]
"Well, look behind me." uses the bridge itself as evidence, which works only for a live audience. [1] Time: "A hundred years ago" links the reopening to the first bridge, giving the
occasion a sense of history. [1] "March of last year" and "eighteen months" recall a recent loss
the audience shares, so the celebration means more to them than to an outside reader. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Context earns credit only when tied to an effect on this audience; "it is a
speech at a bridge" is description.

**8.** Weaknesses, any two of: no claim answering the guiding question; techniques listed, not
analysed; a long quotation dropped in without comment; a generic effect. First weakness [1],
second weakness [1]. Rewrite, for example: "The poster persuades readers to change
their habits by reminding them of a crisis they lived through. The phrase 'lowest level in twenty
years' gives the drought a precise, local scale," [1] "so the reader sees the request as a
response to their own town's problem." [1] (Claim mark, then embedded-quotation-with-effect mark.)
*Examiner insight:* The fastest fix for a weak paragraph is a first sentence that answers the
guiding question.

**9.** The real Paper 1 is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point. These
are indicative points.

Thesis: the writer redefines the library from a place of silent study into a shared social space,
by making readers feel the cost of silence and then showing that a noisy library is easy to
achieve. [1]

- P1: the anecdote makes the rule's cost concrete ("She put the book back."). [1]
- P2: the rhetorical question "who is actually in our library...?" and the list of pensioners,
  teenagers and parents shift attention from the rule to real users. [1]
- P3: the contrast "reading room" / "living room" states the new purpose in one memorable line. [1]
- P4: the concession and practical rebuttal ("three floors and three rules") make the change sound
  easy. [1]
- P5: the ending returns to the child and turns the rule into "the wrong" lesson, so readers leave
  with the new view. [1]
- Context: a local column addressed to "our library" users, with the council as opponent, so the
  inclusive "our" invites residents to claim the building. [1]
- Organisation: paragraphs ordered by idea, each linked to the question, and a conclusion on how
  the features combine. [1]

*Examiner insight:* Strong plans are organised by argument; weak plans follow the text in order and
slide into summary.

**10.** The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria; these are indicative points.

Model introduction:

> This speech was given at the reopening of a town's bridge, a hundred years after the first
> bridge was built and eighteen months after a flood destroyed it. [1] Its audience is local
> residents, many of whom have "driven the long way round" [1], and its purpose is to celebrate the
> new bridge and build pride in the town's resilience. [1] The speaker connects the new bridge to the
> past by presenting it as the latest act in a century-old tradition of stubborn rebuilding. [1]

Model body paragraph:

> The speaker presents the new bridge as a continuation of the old one, not a replacement. [1] The
> great-grandparents are remembered for "rope, stone and stubbornness" [1], and the final quality,
> not a material at all, is the one the present town has inherited. [1] The contrast "steel where
> theirs was stone" admits change [1], but the next sentence insists the bridge "stands exactly
> where theirs stood", so the modern materials sit on a historic path. [1] By tying new steel to
> the old spot, the speaker makes rebuilding feel like loyalty to the past. [1]

*Examiner insight:* Keep every sentence pointed at the guiding question; a good paragraph that
never mentions the past has drifted.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Starting to write before choosing a thesis, so paragraphs wander away from the guiding question.
- Naming techniques without explaining their effect on the target audience.
- Dropping long quotations in without comment instead of embedding a few key words.
- Ignoring layout, images and typography in a multimodal text such as Passage A.
- Mentioning context only in the introduction instead of using it as evidence.
- Poor timing: a strong opening paragraph and no conclusion.
