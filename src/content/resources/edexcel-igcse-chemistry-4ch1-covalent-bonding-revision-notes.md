These notes condense sub-topic 1(g) Covalent bonding (points 1.44 to 1.51) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). None of the points has a C reference, so all can appear on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Worked dot-and-cross examples and model answers are in the [covalent bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-covalent-bonding/); then try the [covalent bonding practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-covalent-bonding-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Double bond | Two shared pairs between the same two atoms |
| Lone pair | A pair of outer electrons not used in bonding |
| Simple molecular structure | Small molecules with strong covalent bonds inside and weak intermolecular forces between them |
| Intermolecular forces | Weak attractions between molecules |
| Giant covalent structure | A continuous network of atoms joined by covalent bonds; no separate molecules |
| Delocalised electrons | Electrons not fixed to one atom or bond, free to move through a structure |

## Why a covalent bond holds (1.45)

The shared pair (negative) is attracted electrostatically to the nuclei of both atoms (positive). Both nuclei pull on the same pair, holding the atoms together.

## Dot-and-cross summary (1.46)

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Lone pairs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 1 | 0 | Each H has 2 electrons |
| Cl₂ (and other halogens) | 1 | 3 on each Cl | |
| HCl (and other hydrogen halides) | 1 | 3 on the halogen | |
| O₂ | 2 (double bond) | 2 on each O | |
| N₂ | 3 (triple bond) | 1 on each N | |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 on O | |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 on N | |
| CO₂ | 4 (two double bonds) | 2 on each O | C has no lone pairs |
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 | |
| C₂H₆ | 7 | 0 | One C–C single bond |
| C₂H₄ | 6 (one is the second pair of C=C) | 0 | C=C double bond plus four C–H |
| CH₃Cl | 4 | 3 on Cl | |

Bonds needed: H 1, halogen 1, O 2, N 3, C 4. No brackets or charges on covalent diagrams.

## Properties by structure (1.47 to 1.51)

| | Simple molecular | Giant covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, halogens, C60 | Diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide |
| What is overcome on melting | Weak intermolecular forces | Many strong covalent bonds |
| Melting and boiling points | Low; often gases or liquids | High; solids |
| Conductivity | Does not conduct | Does not conduct, except graphite |

Mr trend (1.48): larger Mr → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed → higher boiling point.

## Carbon allotropes (1.50)

```
Diamond:  each C -> 4 bonds; rigid 3-D network; very hard; very high mp; no free electrons -> insulator
Graphite: each C -> 3 bonds; layers of hexagons; weak forces between layers -> soft, slippery;
          1 delocalised electron per C -> conducts along the layers; very high mp
C60:      separate ball-shaped molecules; weak intermolecular forces -> soft, lower mp; poor conductor
```

## Answer frames

- **Low boiling point (simple molecular):** "Weak intermolecular forces between molecules; little energy needed to overcome them; covalent bonds are not broken."
- **High melting point (giant covalent):** "Giant covalent structure; many strong covalent bonds must be broken; a lot of energy needed."
- **Graphite conducts:** "Each C forms three bonds; one electron per atom is delocalised; these electrons move and carry charge."
- **Graphite is slippery:** "Weak forces between layers, so layers can slide over each other."

## Exam traps

- "Sharing electrons" without "a pair".
- "Covalent bonds break when water boils": wrong; intermolecular forces are overcome.
- "Weak covalent bonds between the layers in graphite": wrong; they are weak forces, not bonds.
- Missing lone pairs on N, O or halogens.
- Giving CO₂ single bonds.
- Calling C60 a giant covalent structure.
- Saying diamond conducts because it is carbon.

## Self-test

1. State what is meant by a covalent bond.
2. Explain, in terms of electrostatic attraction, how a covalent bond holds two atoms together.
3. How many shared pairs and lone pairs are there in an ammonia molecule?
4. How many shared pairs are there in a molecule of ethene?
5. Describe the bonding in a nitrogen molecule.
6. State the number of lone pairs on the chlorine atom in hydrogen chloride.
7. Explain why chlorine is a gas at room temperature.
8. Bromine (Mr 160) has a higher boiling point than chlorine (Mr 71). Explain why.
9. Explain why silicon dioxide has a very high melting point.
10. Explain why graphite is used as a lubricant.
11. Explain why diamond does not conduct electricity.
12. Explain why C60 fullerene has a lower melting point than diamond.

### Answers

1. A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.
2. The shared pair of electrons is attracted to the nuclei of both atoms.
3. Three shared pairs; one lone pair (on N).
4. Six (four C–H pairs and two pairs in C=C).
5. A triple bond: three shared pairs; each N also has one lone pair.
6. Three.
7. It is simple molecular; weak intermolecular forces between Cl₂ molecules need little energy to overcome.
8. Bromine molecules are larger, so the intermolecular forces are stronger and need more energy to overcome.
9. It has a giant covalent structure; many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy.
10. Its layers are held by weak forces, so they slide over each other.
11. All four outer electrons of each carbon atom are used in covalent bonds, so there are no free (delocalised) electrons.
12. C60 is made of separate molecules; melting overcomes only weak intermolecular forces, whereas diamond needs strong covalent bonds broken.
