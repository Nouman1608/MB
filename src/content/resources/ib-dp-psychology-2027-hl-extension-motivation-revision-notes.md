These notes are for **HL only**. The motivation extension is assessed in Paper 3 (1 hour 45 minutes, 30 marks). They condense the IB Psychology guide for first assessment 2027. For explanations read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-motivation/) and then use the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-motivation-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **Motivation:** the impetus that gives purpose or direction to behaviour. It can be conscious or unconscious.
- **Primary motives:** hunger, thirst, need for sleep.
- **Secondary motives:** affiliation, competition, interests and goals.
- **Intrinsic motivation:** internal (interest, enjoyment). **Extrinsic motivation:** rewards or punishments.
- **Self-determination theory (SDT):** innate growth tendencies and psychological needs influence choices in the absence of external influences.
- Links to concepts: **change** and **causality**; also measurement, bias and responsibility.
- Motivation for learning, especially intrinsic, has been linked with long-term improvements in cognition.

## Measuring motivation

| Type | How the guide says it is measured | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Primary motive | Physiological tests, for example glucose tests to study motivation to eat in people with eating disorders | Indirect; links between physiology and motivation are not simple |
| Secondary motive | Direct methods such as asking people why they acted | Self-report, demand characteristics, motives may be unconscious |
| Effect of external motivators | Experiments | Lab tasks may lack realism |

Other operationalisations: time on task, persistence, effort, questionnaire scores on intrinsic motivation, task choice in free time.

## Areas of inquiry (from the guide)

- **Health and well-being:** motivational theories underpinning prevention or treatment; motivation in changing behaviour.
- **Human development:** motivational theories in development; extrinsic motivators in a child's social development.
- **Human relationships:** motivation and group behaviour; strategies to change individual behaviours; motivators, employee satisfaction and performance.
- **Learning and cognition:** motivation in enhancing a cognitive process; strategies for independent learners; whether motivation can be assessed.

## Self-determination theory

| Need | Meaning | Classroom example |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomy | Control over own actions | Choice of project topic |
| Competence | Feeling effective | Achievable goals with feedback |
| Relatedness | Connection to others | Collaborative tasks |

- Supported needs lead to more internal motivation and better well-being.
- External pressure can reduce internal motivation.
- Strengths: wide support in education, health and sport. Limits: self-report measures, possible cultural differences in the value of autonomy.

## Examples to use

- **Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973):** children who expected an award for drawing later spent less free time drawing. **Overjustification effect.** Field experiment with behavioural measure; young children, one setting.
- **Maslow (1943):** hierarchy of needs; links primary and secondary motives; hard to test.
- **Keys' semi-starvation study:** preoccupation with food shows the strength of a primary motive; ethical concerns.
- **Locke and Latham:** specific, challenging goals with feedback raise performance.
- **Prochaska and DiClemente:** stages of change in health behaviour.
- **Dweck:** growth mindset; mixed evidence, good for replication.

## Strategies for application

| Context | Strategy | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| Health | Motivational support for a patient to choose their own goals | Works if the person is ready to change; relies on self-report |
| Development | Praise for effort instead of rewards for outcomes | Rewards may lower later interest |
| Relationships | Fair recognition and goals at work | Short-term gains may fade |
| Learning | Autonomy-supportive teaching | Hard to separate from teacher quality |

## Paper 3 technique

| Question | Skill | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explain one issue that limits interpretation of a source | 3 |
| 2 | Analyse findings from one source and state a conclusion | 6 |
| 3 | Credibility, bias or transferability | 6 |
| 4 | Synthesis of at least three sources for a claim | 15 |

For motivation:
- Question 1: self-report, short follow-up, one measure of motivation, correlational design.
- Question 2: compare groups using numbers from the source, state a conclusion no stronger than the data.
- Question 3: link credibility to triangulation and to detail on how motivation was measured; bias to researcher expectation and demand characteristics.
- Question 4: use at least three sources, add theory (SDT, overjustification), weigh opposing views and conclude.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that rewards always reduce motivation.
- Confusing intrinsic with primary, or extrinsic with secondary. They are different classifications.
- Treating motivation as directly observable.
- Forgetting that a correlation between motivation and achievement can run in both directions.

## Self-test

1. Give one example of a primary and one of a secondary motive.
2. State the three psychological needs in SDT.
3. What is the overjustification effect?
4. Why are self-reports of motive open to demand characteristics?
5. How could motivation to eat be measured physiologically, according to the guide?
6. Why is a correlation between motivation and grades hard to interpret?
7. Name one strength and one limitation of an experiment on external motivators.

**Answers:** 1. Primary: hunger (also thirst, sleep); secondary: affiliation or competition. 2. Autonomy, competence, relatedness. 3. An external reward for an already enjoyed activity reduces later interest in the activity. 4. Participants may give answers they think the researcher wants or that look socially acceptable, and may not know their real motives. 5. Glucose tests. 6. The direction of the cause is unclear (bidirectional ambiguity) and a third variable could explain both. 7. Strength: control allows causal claims; limitation: artificial tasks lack mundane realism.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the guide name the motivation theories I must learn?**
It names self-determination theory. For other theories and studies, choose examples that fit each context.

**Is motivation only about learning?**
No. The extension is applied in health, development, relationships and learning and cognition.

**Do SL students study motivation?**
No. It is an HL extension.
