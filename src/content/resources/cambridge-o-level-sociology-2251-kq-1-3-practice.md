These questions practise key question 1.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *How do sociologists
investigate society?* Section A follows the shape the syllabus describes for the compulsory Paper 1
Question 1, parts (a) to (e). Section B gives extra practice on research design, sampling, methods and
question types.

---

## Source A (invented for practice)

*A researcher wanted to find out how much time secondary school students spend helping with
housework. She obtained the register of one large school with 1,200 students and selected every 20th
name, giving a sample of 60. After a pilot study with five students, she rewrote two questions that
students had found confusing. The final questionnaire, made up mostly of closed and scaled questions,
was handed out by form tutors, and 51 students returned it. The results showed that girls reported
spending nearly twice as many hours on housework each week as boys.*

---

## Section A — Research methods question

**(a)(i)** Using Source A, identify the sampling technique the researcher used. **[1]**

**(a)(ii)** Using Source A, explain why the researcher carried out a pilot study. **[3]**

**(a)(iii)** Using Source A, explain **two** problems with the research. **[4]**

**(b)** Define the terms *sampling frame* and *operationalisation*. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ethical problems with covert participant observation. **[6]**

**(d)** Evaluate the view that questionnaires are the most useful primary method for sociologists.
**[14]**

**(e)** You have been asked to investigate the working conditions of migrant workers in a large city.
Explain the primary research methods, sampling and secondary evidence you would use, and give reasons
for your choices. **[8]**

## Section B — Design, sampling and methods

**(f)** Describe **two** stages in research design that come before a researcher chooses a method.
**[4]**

**(g)** Explain **two** differences between structured and unstructured interviews. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** reasons why a market researcher might use quota sampling. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** problems with using scaled questions. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** practical issues that could affect a study of a street gang. **[4]**

## Answers

**(a)(i) [1]** Systematic sampling (selecting every 20th name from the register) (1).

**(a)(ii) [3]** A pilot is a small trial run before the main study (1). Here it showed that two
questions confused students (1), so rewriting them before the main study improved the validity of the
answers and avoided wasting the full sample on poor questions (1).

**(a)(iii) [4]** Any two, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for applying it to the source):
- A sample from one school may not be representative of students elsewhere, so findings cannot be
  generalised (2).
- Nine students did not return the questionnaire; non-respondents may differ from respondents, for
  example by doing more or less housework (2).
- Self-reported hours may be inaccurate: students may exaggerate or forget, lowering validity (2).
- Questionnaires handed out by form tutors might lead students to give socially acceptable answers
  or feel pressured to take part, affecting both validity and consent (2).
- Closed and scaled questions show how much housework is done but not why girls do more (2).

**(b) [4]** Sampling frame: a list of all members of the target population from which a sample is
selected (1), such as a school register or electoral roll (1). Operationalisation: deciding how an abstract
idea will be measured in practice (1), for example measuring social class by occupation
(1).

**(c) [6]** Three problems, 2 marks each:
- No informed consent: participants do not know they are being studied, so they cannot agree or
  refuse (2).
- Deception: the researcher lies about their identity and purpose, which breaks trust and could damage
  the reputation of research (2).
- Risk of harm: if the researcher is discovered, both researcher and group may be harmed, and
  publication may expose participants (2).
- Legality: the researcher may witness or be drawn into illegal activity, as in Patrick's study of a
  Glasgow gang (1973) (2).
- Privacy: personal information is gathered without permission (2).

**(d) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, with accurate concepts and a supported conclusion;
7–10 developed but less balanced; 4–6 limited or mainly descriptive; 1–3 basic.

*For:*
- They can reach large, geographically spread samples quickly and cheaply, so findings can be
  representative and generalisable.
- Standardised questions make them reliable: another researcher could repeat the study.
- Closed, multiple-choice and scaled questions produce quantitative data that can show patterns,
  trends and correlations, which positivists value.
- Anonymous questionnaires may encourage honest answers on embarrassing topics, and there is no
  interviewer effect.
- They raise few ethical problems, since respondents choose whether to take part.

*Against:*
- Response rates for postal and online questionnaires can be low, and non-respondents may differ from
  respondents, so the sample becomes unrepresentative.
- Interpretivists argue they lack validity: closed questions impose the researcher's categories, and
  respondents cannot explain meanings.
- No one is present to clarify confusing questions, though a pilot study reduces this problem.
- People may give socially desirable answers or misremember behaviour.
- Other methods are more useful for some aims: unstructured interviews, as in Oakley's study of
  housewives (1974), or participant observation, as in Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), reveal
  meanings and hidden groups that questionnaires miss.

*Conclusion:* questionnaires are the most useful method for measuring patterns in large populations,
but not for understanding meanings or studying hidden groups, so usefulness depends on the research
aim.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear plan with methods, sampling and secondary evidence all justified;
4–6 some justification; 1–3 a list with little reasoning. Evaluation is not required. Example points:
- **Method:** semi-structured interviews, because hours, pay, safety and treatment by employers need
  depth while common questions allow comparison; interpreters for workers who speak other languages.
- **Sampling:** snowball sampling through community groups or places of worship, because there is no
  complete list of migrant workers and some may distrust officials; a gatekeeper may help access.
- **Secondary evidence:** official migration statistics for the city, to show numbers and countries
  of origin; reports by NGOs that support migrants.
- **Ethics:** informed consent, confidentiality and protection from harm, since some workers may fear
  their employer or authorities.

**(f) [4]** Any two, 2 marks each: **selection of topic**, influenced by interests, values, social
issues and funding (2); **setting aims**, deciding what the research will find out (2); **forming a
hypothesis**, a testable prediction (2); **identifying the target population**, the group the research
is about (2).

**(g) [4]** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Structured interviews use the same questions in the same order for everyone; unstructured
  interviews are flexible conversations guided by the respondent's answers (2).
- Structured interviews produce quantitative, comparable and reliable data; unstructured interviews
  produce qualitative data with more depth and validity (2).
- Structured interviews are quicker and suit larger samples; unstructured interviews need skilled
  interviewers and time, so samples are smaller (2).

**(h) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- It is quick and cheap, because interviewers approach people in public until quotas are filled (2).
- No sampling frame is needed, which helps when no list of the population exists (2).
- Quotas ensure the sample contains the right numbers of key groups, such as age or gender groups
  (2).

**(i) [4]** Two problems, 2 marks each:
- People interpret points on a scale differently, so "agree" may mean different things to different
  respondents, reducing validity (2).
- Respondents may choose the middle option to avoid committing themselves, hiding real opinions (2).
- Scales impose the researcher's statements, so respondents cannot explain the reasons behind their
  answers (2).

**(j) [4]** Two issues, 2 marks each:
- **Access:** gang members may refuse to take part or distrust outsiders, so a gatekeeper may be
  needed (2).
- **Time:** gaining trust in a closed group may take months of fieldwork (2).
- **Cost/funding:** long fieldwork and protecting the researcher are expensive (2).
- **Response rate:** a questionnaire would probably be ignored or not returned (2).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does (a)(iii) need the source?** Problems must be tied to details of the research described,
such as the sample from one school or the nine non-returns, not general weaknesses of questionnaires.

**Should a part (e) plan include ethics?** It is not required, but a brief ethical point linked to the
group studied strengthens the reasons for your choices.
