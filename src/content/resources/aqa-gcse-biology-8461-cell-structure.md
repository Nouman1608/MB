## 4.1.1.1 Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

**Eukaryotic cells** include plant and animal cells; fungi and protists are also eukaryotes. They have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material enclosed in a **nucleus**.

**Prokaryotic cells** include bacteria; archaea are also prokaryotes. In the bacterial model used here, a cell has cytoplasm and a cell membrane, usually with a cell wall. Its genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus and is usually one main circular DNA molecule; some bacteria differ. There may also be one or more small rings of DNA called **plasmids**.

### Scale, prefixes and standard form

You need the prefixes centi, milli, micro and nano.

| Unit | Symbol | In metres |
|---|---|---|
| centimetre | cm | 10⁻² m |
| millimetre | mm | 10⁻³ m |
| micrometre | μm | 10⁻⁶ m |
| nanometre | nm | 10⁻⁹ m |

Each step from mm to μm to nm is a factor of 1000: multiply going to the smaller unit, divide going to the larger.

Most bacteria are a few micrometres across or less; a plant or animal cell can be up to about 100 times wider than a typical bacterium.

An **order of magnitude** is a factor of 10. Two sizes that differ by a factor of 100 (10²) differ by two orders of magnitude.

**Worked example 1.** A plant cell is 0.1 mm long. A bacterium is 1 μm long. Write both lengths in metres in standard form and find how many orders of magnitude apart they are.

```
plant cell: 0.1 mm = 0.1 × 1000 = 100 μm = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m
bacterium:  1 μm = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m
ratio = (1 × 10⁻⁴) / (1 × 10⁻⁶) = 10²
```

The plant cell is 100 times longer: **two orders of magnitude**. Subtracting the powers (−4 − (−6) = 2) is quicker.

## 4.1.1.2 Animal and plant cells

Most **animal cells** have a nucleus, cytoplasm, a cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes. **Plant cells** often have these plus **chloroplasts** and a **permanent vacuole filled with cell sap**. Plant cells have a **cellulose cell wall**, which strengthens the cell. Algal cell-wall composition varies among groups; many have cellulose, but it is not universal.

### Structure related to function

| Structure | Function, and how its features fit it |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Holds the genetic material, which controls the activities of the cell |
| Cytoplasm | Where most chemical reactions happen |
| Cell membrane | Controls which substances pass into and out of the cell |
| Mitochondria | Site of aerobic respiration, releasing energy; cells that need a lot of energy have many |
| Ribosomes | Where protein synthesis happens |
| Chloroplasts (plants) | Contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis |
| Permanent vacuole (plants) | Filled with cell sap; helps keep the cell firm |
| Cellulose cell wall (plants; many algae) | Strengthens the cell |
| Plasmids (bacteria) | Small rings of extra DNA, separate from the main DNA loop |

For a 2-mark "explain", name the structure, then say what it does: "many mitochondria [1] release energy for contraction [1]".

### Using estimation

Estimate when an exact measurement is not possible or not needed: a structure has unclear edges or an irregular shape, or you only need to compare sizes. Count how many times one structure fits into another, or treat a shape as a rectangle or circle.

**Worked example 2.** In a micrograph, a leaf cell is 50 μm long and about 20 μm wide. About 10 chloroplasts fit end to end along its length. The vacuole fills about three-quarters of the cell.

```
chloroplast length ≈ 50 / 10 = 5 μm
cell area ≈ 50 × 20 = 1000 μm²   (treat the cell as a rectangle)
vacuole area ≈ 0.75 × 1000 = 750 μm²
```

Give these as "about 5 μm" and "about 750 μm²".

## 4.1.1.4 Cell differentiation

As an organism develops, cells **differentiate** to form different types of cells. As a cell differentiates, it gains the sub-cellular structures it needs for a certain function. It has then become a **specialised cell**.

- Most types of **animal** cell differentiate at an early stage.
- Many types of **plant** cell keep the ability to differentiate throughout life.
- In mature animals, cell division is mainly restricted to **repair and replacement**.

**Why differentiation matters:** a multicellular organism needs many cell types for different jobs. Differentiation produces them, and they then form tissues and organs.

## 4.1.1.5 Microscopy

Light microscopes came first; the **electron microscope** came later. It has much higher **magnification** and much higher **resolving power** than a light microscope.

- **Magnification** is how many times bigger the image is than the real object.
- **Resolution** is the ability to tell apart two points that are close together.

A light microscope cannot resolve detail finer than about 200 nm, however much the image is enlarged. The electron microscope resolves far finer detail, so biologists could see and understand many more sub-cellular structures, such as ribosomes.

### The magnification equation

```
magnification = size of image / size of real object
```

Rearranged: real size = image size / magnification. Both sizes **must be in the same unit**. Magnification has no unit.

**Worked example 3.** In an electron micrograph a mitochondrion is 36 mm long. The magnification is ×18 000. Find its real length in micrometres and in metres in standard form.

```
real size = 36 / 18 000 = 0.002 mm
0.002 mm × 1000 = 2 μm
2 μm = 2 × 10⁻⁶ m
```

**Worked example 4 (scale bar).** A scale bar is 10 mm long and labelled 10 μm. A cell in the same image is 45 mm long.

```
magnification = 10 mm / 10 μm = 10 000 μm / 10 μm = ×1000
real length = 45 mm / 1000 = 0.045 mm = 45 μm
```

## 4.1.1.6 Culturing microorganisms

Bacteria multiply by simple cell division called **binary fission**. This can happen as often as once every 20 minutes if there are enough nutrients and a suitable temperature. Bacteria can be grown in a **nutrient broth** solution or as **colonies on an agar gel plate**.

Uncontaminated cultures are needed to investigate the action of disinfectants and antibiotics.

### Aseptic technique, and why each step matters

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Sterilise Petri dishes and culture media before use | Kills any microorganisms already present, so only the chosen bacteria grow |
| Use sterile equipment and an approved aseptic method | Reduces contamination; the exact method and equipment are set by the teacher or technician using the school's risk assessment |
| If a reusable loop is flamed, allow it to cool before touching a culture | A hot loop can kill the microorganisms being transferred; students should use this method only when directed and supervised |
| Secure the lid with small pieces of tape, without sealing the dish completely | Helps prevent accidental opening while avoiding complete sealing; follow the current school procedure |
| Store the dish upside down | Condensation does not drip onto the agar |
| Use the incubation conditions set by the approved school procedure (commonly 25°C in school practicals) | Lower temperatures reduce the likelihood of encouraging growth of organisms harmful to humans; follow the local risk assessment |

Do not open an incubated plate that has grown from an environmental or human sample. Follow the teacher's instructions for handling and disposal. AQA's suggested method uses pre-inoculated plates and says students do not need to prepare plates themselves; the school is responsible for an appropriate risk assessment.

### Calculating bacterial numbers

```
number of divisions = time available / mean division time
bacteria at end = bacteria at start × 2^(number of divisions)
```

This calculation is an ideal model: it assumes a constant division time and no deaths, so real cultures may not follow it as nutrients and conditions change.

**Worked example 5.** A culture starts with 150 bacteria. The mean division time is 30 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 3 hours?

```
3 hours = 180 minutes
divisions = 180 / 30 = 6
number = 150 × 2⁶ = 150 × 64 = 9600
```

**Higher tier only:** give the answer in standard form: **9.6 × 10³ bacteria**.

### Area of a colony or clear zone

Use πr², where r is the radius (half the diameter).

**Worked example 6.** A clear zone around an antibiotic disc has a diameter of 18 mm.

```
r = 18 / 2 = 9 mm
area = π × 9² = 254.469... ≈ 254 mm²
```

Spread bacteria evenly over an agar plate using the approved aseptic method. Add paper discs soaked in different antiseptics (or antibiotics), plus a control disc soaked in sterile water. Incubate under the approved school conditions. A clear **zone of inhibition** forms where bacterial growth is stopped. Measure each zone's diameter and calculate its area with πr²; state whether your measurement includes the disc footprint. A larger zone can indicate greater inhibition when conditions are standardised, but zone size also depends on how readily the substance diffuses through agar. Do not use zone area alone to compare the intrinsic potency of different compounds. Control disc size, soaking time, bacterium, temperature and incubation time.

## Common errors

- Writing that bacteria have "no DNA". They have DNA: a single loop, plus plasmids, but no nucleus.
- Giving "vacuole" for plant cells without "permanent".
- Giving bacteria a cellulose cell wall. Plant cell walls contain cellulose; algal wall composition varies, and prokaryotic walls are not made of cellulose.
- Multiplying when you should divide in a unit conversion. mm → μm is ×1000; μm → mm is ÷1000.
- Using the diameter instead of the radius in πr².
- Treating magnification and resolution as the same.
- Counting divisions wrongly: 2 hours at 20 minutes per division is 6 divisions.
- Saying cultures are incubated at 25°C "because bacteria grow best there". The reason is safety.
