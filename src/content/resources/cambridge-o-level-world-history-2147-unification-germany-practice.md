These questions cover key question 1.3 of Cambridge O Level History 2147, "How was Germany unified?", which was the
Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2025.

## Section A

**1.** Describe the Zollverein. **[4]**

**2.** Identify **two** weaknesses of the Frankfurt Parliament. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the events of 1848 in Prussia. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the war of 1864 and the settlement that followed it. **[4]**

**5.** Identify **two** territories annexed by Prussia in 1866. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the terms of the Treaty of Frankfurt, 1871, and the proclamation of the Empire. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Why was Austria able to re-establish its influence in Germany by 1850? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**8.** Why did Prussia defeat Austria in 1866? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**9.** "Bismarck planned the unification of Germany from the start." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**10.** "France was responsible for its own defeat in 1870." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**11.** Study Source A, which is invented for practice.

*Source A (invented)*: from an imagined diary entry by a Bavarian schoolteacher, August 1870. "A month ago my
neighbours cursed Prussia as a nest of Protestant soldiers. Today the same men march off singing beside them against
the French. The Emperor Napoleon has done in one week what Berlin could not do in four years."

What can you learn from Source A about the effects of the Franco-Prussian War on the southern German states? Use the
source and your knowledge to explain your answer. **[6]**

**12.** Study Source B, which is invented for practice.

*Source B (invented)*: a description of an imaginary cartoon published in a London magazine in 1871, written for this
page. A giant Prussian soldier wearing a spiked helmet stands over a map of Germany, holding a pen labelled
"diplomacy" in one hand and a sword dripping ink in the other. Small figures labelled "Austria" and "France" lie at his
feet.

What is the message of the cartoon described in Source B? Use details of the source and your knowledge to explain your
answer. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** Any four, 1 mark each: a customs union of German states (1); formed in 1834 (1); led by Prussia (1); it removed
tariffs on trade between member states (1); Austria was excluded (1); most German states had joined by the 1850s (1);
it encouraged railway building and industrial growth (1); it made other states economically dependent on Prussia (1).
**[4]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: it had no army of its own (1); it had no power to raise taxes (1); members were divided
over greater or smaller Germany (1); it depended on the princes accepting its decisions (1); it was slow, spending
months on debates about rights (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any four, 1 mark each: there was street fighting in Berlin in March 1848 (1); Frederick William IV withdrew his
troops from the city (1); he promised a constitution (1); he appeared to support German unity, wearing the national
colours (1); a Prussian assembly was elected (1); in November the army returned to Berlin (1); the assembly was
dissolved and the king issued his own constitution in December 1848 (1). **[4]**

**4.** Any four, 1 mark each: the Danish king accepted a constitution that tied Schleswig more closely to Denmark (1);
Prussia and Austria together declared war on Denmark in 1864 (1); Denmark was quickly defeated, for example at
Düppel (1); Denmark gave up Schleswig and Holstein to Austria and Prussia jointly (1); the Gastein Convention (1865)
gave Prussia control of Schleswig (1) and Austria control of Holstein (1). **[4]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: Hanover (1); Hesse-Kassel (1); Nassau (1); Frankfurt (1); Schleswig-Holstein (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any four, 1 mark each: William I was proclaimed German Emperor (1) in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles (1) on
18 January 1871 (1); the treaty gave Germany Alsace (1) and much of Lorraine (1); France had to pay a large indemnity
(1); German troops occupied part of France until it was paid (1). **[4]**

**7.** Levels. Level 4 (6): explains two reasons. Level 3 (4–5): explains one reason; 5 for a developed explanation.
Level 2 (2–3): identifies or describes reasons. Level 1 (1): general comment. Indicative content: **the failure of the
Frankfurt Parliament**, which left no rival national authority once Frederick William IV refused the crown; **the
recovery of the Austrian army** after crushing revolution in Vienna and Hungary (with Russian help); **Russian support**
for Austria against Prussia's Erfurt Union; **Prussian weakness of will**: Frederick William IV would not risk war and
gave way at Olmütz in 1850. **[6]**

**8.** Levels as Question 7. Indicative content: **Bismarck's diplomacy** left Austria without allies, while Italy
tied down Austrian troops in the south; **Prussian military reforms** by Roon produced a larger, better trained army;
**Moltke's use of railways** concentrated forces quickly; **the needle gun** gave a faster rate of fire; **Austrian
weaknesses**, including financial problems and the need to fight on two fronts. Answers must link each factor to the
victory at Königgrätz. **[6]**

**9.** Levels. Level 5 (9–10): explained arguments on both sides and a supported judgement. Level 4 (7–8): explains
both sides. Level 3 (4–6): explains one side. Level 2 (2–3): describes. Level 1 (1): general points. **Agree:** the
"blood and iron" speech of 1862 shows he expected force to settle the German question; he isolated Austria step by
step in 1864–66; he arranged secret alliances with the southern states in 1866 that brought them into the war of 1870;
his own memoirs present a plan. **Disagree:** he co-operated with Austria in 1864 and may have been willing to share
Germany if Austria accepted Prussian dominance in the north; the Spanish candidature crisis arose by chance and he
exploited it; his memoirs were written after 1890 and justify his career; his first aim was a stronger Prussia, not
German nationalism. **Judgement:** for example, he had a consistent aim of Prussian dominance but took opportunities
as they came, so "planned from the start" goes too far. **[10]**

**10.** Levels as Question 9. **Agree:** Napoleon III's demand for guarantees after the candidature was withdrawn
turned a French diplomatic success into a crisis; France declared war, so it appeared the aggressor and gained no
allies; its army was poorly organised and slow to mobilise; Napoleon himself was captured at Sedan. **Disagree:**
Bismarck edited the Ems telegram to provoke France; he had already isolated France and secured the southern alliances;
Moltke's planning and Prussian artillery and railways were superior; German nationalism united the states against
France. **Judgement:** France's mistakes gave Bismarck the war he wanted, but the defeat itself came from Prussian
military superiority that France could not have matched however it behaved. **[10]**

**11.** Levels. Level 3 (5–6): developed inferences supported from the source and context. Level 2 (2–4): inferences
or details explained. Level 1 (1): copies or paraphrases. Indicative content: the war turned southern hostility to
Prussia into national enthusiasm ("march off singing beside them"); religious and political suspicion of Protestant
Prussia had been strong in Catholic Bavaria; the French declaration of war made the southern states honour their 1866
military alliances; the writer implies war achieved unity that diplomacy since 1866 had not. Contextual support: the
southern states joined the North German Confederation to form the Empire in 1871. **[6]**

**12.** Levels. Level 4 (6–7): explains a message using details and context. Level 3 (4–5): explains a message
supported by details. Level 2 (2–3): describes details or gives an unsupported message. Level 1 (1): surface reading.
Indicative content: **message**: Germany was unified by Prussian military power and by Bismarck's diplomacy together;
the sword "dripping ink" suggests the wars and the treaties were the same weapon; the giant soldier implies a new and
threatening military power in Europe. **Context**: defeats of Austria (1866) and France (1870–71); a British magazine
might be expressing unease about the new balance of power. **[7]**
