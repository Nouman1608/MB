This guide covers **3.3.1 Language Investigation**, the larger part of the non-exam assessment in AQA A-level
English Language (7702): a 2,000-word investigation, excluding data, worth 50 of the NEA's 100 marks. The
[Language in Action NEA guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-nea-language-in-action/) explains the
required sections and how to narrow a topic. This page goes into what happens after that: research design, data
collection, ethics, and writing each section so that it earns credit.

## What the specification requires

You choose an area of individual interest, in consultation with your supervising teacher, **collect your own data**
and **select your own approach to analysis**. The specification's example areas include representations of
individuals or groups, regional dialect, gendered talk, the language of new communication technologies, children's
language, the language of the media, code-switching, occupational language and historical change. Data may be
spoken, written or multimodal, word lists, or material on attitudes, uses or views of language.

The finished investigation contains an **introduction** (reasons and aims), a **methodology** (an evaluative
account of how data was collected and organised, and approaches to analysis), an **analysis** (interpretation of
findings, critical consideration of relevant concepts and issues, and contextual influences on the data), a
**conclusion** linked to the aim, **references** and **appendices** (clean copies of data and evidence for
quantitative approaches).

## Step 1: from aim to research questions

An aim states what you want to find out. Research questions break it into answerable parts, each pointing to
specific features and data.

```
Aim:     To explore how two football commentators construct excitement.
RQ1      How do the commentators' grammatical choices (tense, ellipsis,
         sentence length) change at moments of high action?
RQ2      What prosodic features (pace, pitch, volume) accompany these moments?
RQ3      How does the radio or television context shape the language used?
```

Some students frame a **hypothesis** ("Commentator A will use more ellipsis during goals"). That can work, but
research questions are often more flexible, because language data rarely gives simple yes or no answers.

## Step 2: designing data collection

| Data type | Collection method | Design decisions to justify |
|---|---|---|
| Spontaneous speech | Audio or video recording, then transcription | Length of sample, number of speakers, setting, transcription conventions |
| Broadcast or published speech | Recording from a public source | Selection of extracts, comparability (same sport, same length) |
| Written texts | Collected set (adverts, reports, articles, posts) | Size of set, date range, source, matching texts for comparison |
| Children's language | Recordings or writing samples, with consent from parents and school | Age, task given, setting |
| Attitudes | Questionnaires or interviews | Question wording, sample size and spread, leading questions avoided |
| Word lists | Collected lexis (new words, shop names) | Source, period, criteria for inclusion |

**Comparability matters.** If you compare two groups or two sets of texts, keep other variables as similar as
possible: similar length, similar context, similar date. Otherwise you cannot tell whether a difference comes from
the factor you are studying.

**Ethics.** Obtain informed consent before recording people, anonymise participants (use initials or labels such
as Speaker A), and store data responsibly. Covert recording raises ethical problems and should be discussed with
your teacher.

**The observer's paradox.** William Labov described the problem that researchers want to observe how people talk
when not observed. Recording may make speech more careful or formal. Strategies include longer recordings (people
relax), familiar settings, or recording an activity that absorbs attention. Acknowledge any remaining effect.

## Writing the introduction

Keep it brief: why this focus interests you and why it matters linguistically (for example, a gap between popular
claims and real usage), the aim, and the research questions. A sentence or two placing the topic in relevant
research shows the reader which concepts the analysis will test. Avoid long personal narratives: the introduction
earns its words by setting up the analysis.

## Step 3: writing an evaluative methodology

The specification asks for an **evaluative** account, not a description. Compare:

**Descriptive:** *"I recorded my family at dinner for 20 minutes and transcribed it."*

**Evaluative:** *"I recorded a 20-minute family meal because a familiar, routine setting was likely to reduce the
observer's paradox; the first five minutes were excluded from analysis as participants were visibly aware of the
recorder. A single meal limits how far the findings can be generalised, and a second recording on a different day
would have strengthened reliability."*

The evaluative version explains *why* each choice was made, identifies its weaknesses and suggests improvements.
It also states the approach to analysis: which levels and frameworks, and whether analysis is quantitative,
qualitative or both.

## Step 4: organising the analysis

Organise by **research question or feature**, not by text or speaker one after another. Each section should:

- present the evidence (examples, counts, percentages, referenced to the appendices);
- interpret it using concepts and terminology from the relevant level;
- bring in relevant research or theory critically, testing it against your data rather than simply confirming it;
- explain **contextual influences** (audience, purpose, genre, mode, relationships, setting);
- note complexities and counter-examples.

**Example analysis paragraph:** *"Commentator A's turns during the goal sequence are dominated by verbless clauses
('Smith... wide... crosses... header!'), whereas his build-up turns use complete clauses with present progressive
verbs ('Smith is moving down the left'). The ellipsis compresses information to match the speed of action, which
radio requires because listeners cannot see the pitch. Commentator B, on television, uses fewer verbless clauses at
the same moment, suggesting that the visual channel reduces the need for continuous description."*

## Step 5: a cautious conclusion

Answer each research question in turn, state what the data suggests, and qualify claims by sample size and context.
Words such as "suggests", "in this data" and "may" are signs of appropriate caution. Identify what further research
could test.

## References and appendices

List every source used, paper and web-based, in a consistent format. Appendices hold clean copies of all data (with
a transcription key where relevant) and the evidence behind any counts or percentages, such as coding tables. The
2,000 words exclude the data, but analysis quotations should be brief and purposeful.

## Common mistakes

- An aim with no research questions, so the analysis wanders.
- Data sets that cannot be compared fairly.
- A methodology that narrates steps without evaluating them.
- Analysis organised text by text, which turns into description.
- Theory applied as fact, or quoted without testing it against the data.
- Conclusions that generalise from tiny samples ("all men interrupt").
- Missing consent, anonymisation or a transcription key.

## How it is assessed

From the NEA assessment criteria, the investigation is marked on applying methods of language analysis with
terminology and coherent expression (AO1), critical understanding of concepts and issues (AO2), and analysing and
evaluating how contextual factors and language features construct meaning (AO3). Contextual analysis therefore
needs to run through the whole analysis, not sit in a single paragraph.
