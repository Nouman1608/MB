These questions cover section 3.7.3 overall performance of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-internal-position-overall-performance/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-internal-position-overall-performance-revision-notes/).

Questions 6 to 8 use the data below for Wexley Dairies.

| Measure | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Average employees | 240 | 220 |
| Leavers | 18 | 33 |
| Output (million litres) | 2.4 | 2.31 |
| Maximum capacity (million litres) | 3.0 | 3.0 |
| Sales revenue | £7.5m | £8.4m |
| Total market sales | £30m | £42m |
| Customer complaints per 1,000 deliveries | 2.0 | 4.5 |

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A bakery made 4,200 loaves a day with a maximum capacity of 6,000. What is its capacity utilisation? **[1]**

A: 42%

B: 70%

C: 1.43%

D: 30%

**2.** Which **one** of the following is part of Elkington's Triple Bottom Line? **[1]**

A: Price

B: Planet

C: Product

D: Promotion

**3.** Define "core competence". **[2]**

**4.** A company with an average of 400 employees saw 52 leave in a year. Calculate its labour turnover. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a rising number of customer complaints might be an early warning of falling profit. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Calculate for Wexley Dairies:

**(a)** labour turnover in Year 1 and Year 2 **[2]**

**(b)** output per employee in Year 1 and Year 2, in litres **[2]**

**(c)** market share in Year 1 and Year 2 **[2]**

**7.** Calculate the capacity utilisation in Year 2, to the nearest whole percent, and state what it suggests about the dairy's costs. **[3]**

**8.** Using your answers to questions 6 and 7, and the other data, evaluate whether Wexley Dairies' overall performance has improved. **[12]**

**9.** Explain **two** possible effects on a business of a high rate of labour turnover. **[4]**

**10.** A manufacturer of garden tools has a core competence in designing products that are easy to grip and use. Analyse how this competence could help the business when it chooses its strategy. **[6]**

**11.** A listed company raised profit by 20% in a year by cutting its spending on staff training and research. Assess whether this shows that its performance has improved. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the Triple Bottom Line as a measure of a business's performance. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 4,200 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 70%. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The three parts are profit, people and planet. **[1]**

**3.** Something a business does especially well [1] that is valued by customers, hard for rivals to copy and usable across more than one product or market [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Labour turnover = leavers ÷ average employees × 100 [1] = 52 ÷ 400 × 100 = **13%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Complaints suggest customers are dissatisfied with quality, delivery or service [1]. Unhappy customers may stop buying and may tell others, so sales fall [1], and profit falls because of lost revenue and the cost of fixing problems, refunds and replacement; the warning comes before the fall appears in the accounts [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Year 1: 18 ÷ 240 × 100 = **7.5%** [1]; Year 2: 33 ÷ 220 × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 2,400,000 ÷ 240 = **10,000 litres** [1]; Year 2: 2,310,000 ÷ 220 = **10,500 litres** [1].

**(c)** Year 1: 7.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = **25%** [1]; Year 2: 8.4 ÷ 42 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Capacity utilisation = 2.31 ÷ 3.0 × 100 [1] = **77%** [1]. It suggests spare capacity [1], so fixed costs are spread over fewer litres than at full output and unit cost is higher than it could be. **[3]**

**8.** Improvement: output per employee rose from 10,000 to 10,500 litres, up 5% [1], and sales revenue rose 12% from £7.5m to £8.4m [1]. Deterioration: labour turnover doubled from 7.5% to 15% [1], which raises recruitment and training costs and may reduce quality [1]. Complaints more than doubled, from 2.0 to 4.5 per 1,000 deliveries [1], a sign of weaker service. Market share fell from 25% to 20% [1] because the market grew by 40% while Wexley's sales grew 12% [1]. Capacity utilisation fell from 80% to 77% [1], so unit costs may have risen. Judgement: the rise in productivity may reflect fewer staff doing more, which may explain the higher turnover and complaints [1]. Overall performance has probably worsened, because the signs point to loss of ground in a growing market [1]. Long term, falling share and rising complaints threaten future sales more than the productivity gain helps [1]. Further data needed: profit, the reasons staff are leaving, and competitors' performance [1]. **[12]**

**9.** Effect 1: higher costs [1]. The business must spend on advertising, recruiting and training replacements [1]. Effect 2: lower quality and productivity [1]. New staff are less experienced, and remaining staff may be overloaded or demoralised, so mistakes and complaints rise [1]. (Credit also: loss of skills and knowledge; poor reputation as an employer.) **[4]**

**10.** The competence is valued by customers because easy-to-use tools are a clear benefit [1], so the business can charge a premium or win market share [1]. It is hard to copy because it depends on design knowledge and testing built up over time [1]. The business can apply it to new products, such as new tool ranges or related products for DIY [1], so a strategy of product development is lower risk than diversifying into unrelated products [1]. It also suggests concentrating resources on design while outsourcing activities like packaging [1]. **[6]**

**11.** For: profit up 20% is a clear improvement in the short term, and shareholders may be pleased [1]. Lower costs may suggest greater efficiency if the training or research was wasteful [1]. Against: training cuts can reduce skills, quality and motivation [1], and cutting research reduces the flow of new products [1], so the profit gain may be at the expense of future profits [1]. It may also raise labour turnover and customer complaints later. Factors: how long the cuts last, and what the competitors are doing [1]; whether the training and research was needed [1]. Judgement: the result alone does not show improved performance [1]; it should be judged alongside long-term measures such as market share, innovation and staff turnover [1]. **[9]**

**12.** For: it recognises that businesses depend on employees, customers and the environment as well as profit [1]. It encourages managers to avoid actions that damage reputation or invite regulation [1]. Stakeholders increasingly expect evidence of social and environmental performance, so reporting it can win customers and investors [1]. It supports long-term thinking, because poor treatment of staff or the environment tends to harm profit later [1]. Against: profit is easily measured while people and planet are not, so it is hard to combine the three into one result [1]. Firms may choose favourable measures [1]. The three may conflict, for example a greener process may reduce profit [1]. A business that cannot make profit cannot survive to benefit anyone [1]. Factors: the industry (a mining firm's planet impact is larger than a consultancy's) [1], and whether the measures are independently checked [1]. Judgement: the Triple Bottom Line is a useful addition to profit, not a replacement [1], and is most useful where the measures are clear and checked [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The best answers interpret each figure, link the figures to each other and finish with a judgement that names what further data they would want.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading out numbers from a table without saying what they mean.
- Seeing rising sales as improved performance, ignoring a falling market share.
- Treating a core competence as a product.
- Using only financial evidence when asked to assess overall performance.
- Treating the Triple Bottom Line as a way of replacing profit.
