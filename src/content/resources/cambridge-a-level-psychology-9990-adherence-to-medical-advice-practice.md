**This practice set covers topic 3.2, Adherence to medical advice, in Specialist Option 3 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios that combine the topic with research methods. All patients, clinics and figures are fictional.

Related: [Adherence study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-adherence-to-medical-advice/) · [Adherence revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-adherence-to-medical-advice-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline the two types of non-adherence named in the syllabus. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by rational non-adherence, using an example. **[3]**

**3.** Outline **one** objective measure of non-adherence and **one** weakness of it. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how contracts and customising treatment can improve adherence. **[4]**

**5.** Many patients do not follow the advice they are given.

**(a)** Describe how the Health Belief Model explains non-adherence. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate explanations of non-adherence, including a discussion of reductionism versus holism. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Mr Diallo has high blood pressure. He takes his tablets for a week, feels no different, and then stops because they make him dizzy.

**(a)** Using the Health Belief Model, explain **two** reasons why he stopped. **[4]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** way his doctor could improve his adherence. **[2]**

**7.** A researcher compares three measures of adherence in 30 patients taking antibiotics: a semi-structured interview, a pill count and a urine test. She finds that 27 patients say they took every dose, 22 have the correct number of pills left, and 18 have the drug present in their urine.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of patients whose urine test indicated adherence. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why the interview gave the highest figure. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the urine test may be the most valid of the three measures. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** ethical issue with using urine tests in this study. **[2]**

**8.** A children's hospital finds that many young children with asthma do not use their inhaler spacer.

**(a)** Using research into improving adherence in children, suggest how the hospital could increase use. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** problem with relying on parents' reports of how often the child uses the device. **[2]**

**9.** Yokley and Glenwick (1984) randomly allocated families to six conditions.

**(a)** Explain why random allocation was used. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the study included a no-contact control group as well as a contact control group. **[2]**

**10.** A dental practice finds that a quarter of patients miss their check-ups.

**(a)** Suggest **two** prompts the practice could use. **[2]**

**(b)** Using Yokley and Glenwick (1984), explain why a specific prompt might be better value than a monetary incentive. **[2]**

**11.** A psychologist plans a semi-structured interview with teenagers who have diabetes about why they sometimes miss insulin doses. Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of using a semi-structured interview with this sample. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each: failure to follow treatments, e.g. missing doses or stopping early (1); failure to attend appointments, e.g. missing a check-up (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. A deliberate, reasoned decision not to follow advice (1), based on weighing up the costs and benefits of treatment (1). Example: a patient stops a medicine because the side effects interfere with work and they doubt it is helping (1).

**3.** Measure, up to 2: pill counting compares the number of pills left with the number that should be left (1+1); or an electronic dispenser records the time and date of each opening (1+1). Weakness, 1 mark: patients can remove pills without taking them, so adherence is overestimated (1).

**4.** Two marks for each technique. Contracts: a written agreement between patient and practitioner setting out what the patient will do and the reward (1); writing and signing it increases commitment (1). Customising treatment: fitting the regime to the patient's routine, e.g. once-daily dosing at breakfast (1), which reduces forgetting and inconvenience (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. The Health Belief Model proposes that health behaviour depends on beliefs (1). Perceived susceptibility: if a patient believes they are not at risk, they may not adhere (1). Perceived severity: if the illness seems mild, treatment seems unnecessary (1). Perceived benefits are weighed against perceived barriers such as side effects, cost and inconvenience (1); if barriers outweigh benefits, the patient does not adhere (1). Cues to action, such as symptoms or reminders, trigger adherence; without them the patient may not act (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Reductionism versus holism*: the Health Belief Model and rational non-adherence reduce adherence to a cost-benefit judgement, which makes them testable; but adherence also depends on emotion, habit, family support and culture, which a holistic account would include.
- *Unintentional non-adherence*: neither explanation accounts for simply forgetting.
- *Support*: research on rational non-adherence (e.g. Laba et al., 2012) examines how features of a medication, such as side effects and cost, influence adherence choices.
- *Application*: explanations lead to practical changes such as reducing side effects and adding reminders.
- *Individual and situational explanations*: beliefs are individual, while cost and access are situational.
- *Method*: much evidence relies on self-report, which may overstate adherence.
- *Conclusion*: useful for deliberate non-adherence but incomplete.

**6.**

**(a)** Two marks for each reason, applied. Perceived benefits are low, because he feels no different, so he doubts the tablets work (1+1). Perceived barriers are high, because the tablets make him dizzy (1+1). Credit low perceived severity if linked to high blood pressure having no symptoms.

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Change to a medicine with fewer side effects, or explain that high blood pressure has no symptoms but is serious (1), which reduces barriers or increases perceived severity (1).

**7.**

**(a)** 18 ÷ 30 × 100 = 60% (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Patients may give socially desirable answers to appear good patients (1), or may forget doses they missed, so self-report overestimates adherence (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. It detects the drug in the body directly (1), so it does not rely on honesty or on the pills having been swallowed rather than removed (1).

**(d)** Up to 2 marks. Collecting urine is invasive and may be embarrassing (1), so fully informed consent and the right to withdraw must be ensured, and results kept confidential (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Use a device designed to be fun, such as a toy-like spacer (1); Chaney et al. (2004) found this improved adherence in young children with asthma compared with the usual device (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Parents may give socially desirable answers or not know about every dose, e.g. at school (1), so reports may overestimate use and lack validity (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. To spread family differences, such as motivation or distance from the clinic, evenly across conditions (1), so differences in immunisation rates can be attributed to the intervention (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. The contact control shows whether simply being contacted increases immunisation (1); the no-contact control gives a baseline with no contact at all, so the effect of each intervention beyond contact can be measured (1).

**10.**

**(a)** One mark each: a text message giving the patient's own appointment date and time (1); a phone call the day before (1). Credit a postcard or email.

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Specific prompts improved immunisation compared with controls at low cost (1), whereas the monetary incentive was more effective but more expensive, so specific prompts were the most cost-effective (1).

**11.** Strength, up to 2: follow-up questions allow teenagers to explain their own reasons, e.g. embarrassment injecting in front of friends (1), giving rich qualitative data about rational non-adherence (1). Weakness, up to 2: teenagers may give socially desirable answers to an adult interviewer (1), so they may underreport missed doses, reducing validity (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 3, describing a subjective measure when an objective one is asked for.
- In question 6(a), naming parts of the model without linking them to Mr Diallo.
- In question 7(a), giving the figure without the percentage sign.
