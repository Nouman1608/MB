**This practice set covers the research methods in section 3.1.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A has short-answer and scenario questions, some linked to core studies. Section B is a planning question with evaluation. All scenarios are fictional.

## Section A: short-answer and scenario questions

**1.** The study by Piliavin et al. was a field experiment. Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of this research method, using the study as an example. **[4]**

**2.** Explain why the study by Saavedra and Silverman is described as a case study. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **one** difference between a structured and a semi-structured interview. **[2]**

**4.** Hölzel et al. used an experiment with a longitudinal design. Outline **one** weakness of longitudinal designs. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by *counterbalancing*, using an example of two memory tasks. **[3]**

**6.** Kemal wants to know whether people who use social media more sleep less. He asks 50 students to record their daily screen time on social media and their hours of sleep for a week.

**(a)** Identify the research method Kemal is using. **[1]**

**(b)** Operationally define **one** of his co-variables. **[2]**

**(c)** Kemal finds a negative correlation. Explain why he cannot conclude that social media use causes students to sleep less. **[2]**

**7.** Dr Osei plans to compare reaction times after drinking an energy drink or water. She is deciding between an independent measures design and a repeated measures design.

**(a)** Explain **one** reason for choosing repeated measures. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** problem with repeated measures in this study, and how it could be reduced. **[3]**

**8.** A researcher observes how often toddlers share toys in a nursery. Suggest **two** behavioural categories she could use, and explain why behavioural categories improve reliability. **[3]**

**9.** A researcher plans to follow the same group of children from age three to age six, testing their vocabulary every six months.

**(a)** Explain **one** reason why a longitudinal design is suitable for this aim. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** problem with re-contacting the participants for repeated testing, and how it could be reduced. **[2]**

## Section B: planning question

**10.** A psychologist wants to investigate whether customers in a café are more likely to leave a tip when the server smiles.

**(a)** Plan a field experiment to investigate this. Your plan must include details about the IV, the DV, controls, the experimental design and the location. **[10]**

**(b)** Explain **one** strength of your plan in terms of validity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** weakness of your plan in terms of reliability, and how it could be addressed. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** 2 marks each. Strength: it took place on a real subway train with passengers who did not know they were being studied (1), so behaviour was natural, with high ecological validity and no demand characteristics (1). Weakness: the researchers could not control who was in the carriage, such as the number or characteristics of passengers in each trial (1), so these uncontrolled variables could have affected helping and the conditions were not equivalent (1). Accept ethical weaknesses (no consent, no debrief) if linked to the method.

**2.** Up to 2 marks. It studied a single participant, a nine-year-old boy with button phobia (1), in detail over time, using several techniques such as interviews, rating scales and observation (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks. A structured interview asks every participant the same questions in the same order with no deviation (1), whereas a semi-structured interview has set questions but lets the interviewer ask follow-up questions depending on the answers (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. For example: participants are measured more than once, so they may become familiar with the tests or procedure (1); in Hölzel et al. this could affect self-report scores at the second measurement, so some change might be due to practice rather than the programme (1). Or: attrition, if participants drop out between measurements and those who stay differ from those who leave.

**5.** Up to 3 marks. Counterbalancing is a way of dealing with order effects in a repeated measures design (1). Half the participants do memory task A then task B, and the other half do task B then task A (1), so any practice or fatigue effects are spread equally across both conditions (1).

**6.** **(a)** 1 mark: a correlation (correlational study).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: social media use is the number of minutes per day (1) shown by the phone's screen-time record for social media apps, averaged over the seven days (1). Or: sleep is the number of hours from going to bed to waking, recorded in a diary each morning, averaged over the week.

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Neither variable was manipulated, so a correlation only shows that the variables are related (1). A third variable, such as stress from coursework, could lead to both more social media use and less sleep, or the direction could be reversed, with poor sleep leading to more late-night phone use (1).

**7.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. Reaction times vary a lot between people (1), so testing the same participants in both conditions removes participant variables, so any difference is more likely to be due to the drink (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Order effects: participants may get faster on the second test because of practice, or the energy drink could still be acting if it came first (1). Counterbalancing: half drink water first and half the energy drink first (1), and the two sessions should be on different days so the energy drink has worn off (1).

**8.** Up to 3 marks. 1 mark each for two operationally defined categories, such as: "hands a toy to another child"; "allows another child to take a toy without resistance"; "offers a toy verbally". 1 mark for the explanation: clear categories mean observers record the same behaviours in the same way, so two observers' tallies can be compared and should agree (inter-observer reliability).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. Vocabulary develops over time, so the aim is about change (1); testing the same children repeatedly shows how each child's vocabulary grows and removes participant variables that would affect a comparison of different children at different ages (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Families may move away or lose interest, so children drop out (attrition) and the remaining sample may be biased (1). This could be reduced by collecting several contact details at the start, sending reminders before each test, and making sessions short and convenient, for example at the child's nursery (1).

**10.** **(a)** Level-based, up to 10 marks. 9–10 marks: a detailed, coherent and replicable plan covering all required features, with justification. Indicative content:

- *IV*: the server smiles broadly at the customer when taking the order and delivering the bill, or keeps a neutral expression; the smile is defined (for example, a full smile with teeth showing for at least two seconds).
- *DV*: whether a tip is left (yes or no) and the amount, recorded by the server on a coded sheet after each table leaves.
- *Location*: a single independent café during weekday lunchtimes, so customers are in their normal setting.
- *Design*: independent measures, because each table is served once; condition decided by a random list before each shift.
- *Controls*: the same server for all tables; the same uniform, greeting script and speed of service; payment by the same method; only tables of two included.
- *Procedure*: run for two weeks; the server checks the random list for each new table, applies the condition and records the outcome; a second researcher sits in the café on some days to check the smile was produced as defined.
- *Data*: quantitative (whether a tip was left and its amount).

Lower levels for plans missing features, lacking detail or not replicable.

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: customers do not know they are in a study (1), so their tipping is natural and not affected by demand characteristics, giving high ecological validity (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. For example: the server's smile might vary in how warm it looks from table to table, so the IV is not applied consistently (1); training the server beforehand and having an observer check a sample of interactions against the definition would make the procedure more standardised (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need ethics in a Paper 2 plan?**
No. The syllabus states that candidates will not be expected to evaluate the sample, generalisability or ethics of their plan, or to include aims or hypotheses.

**Can I choose any design for a field experiment?**
Yes, if you justify it for the scenario. Independent measures is often the most practical in field settings.
