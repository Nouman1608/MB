## Step 1: how markets should allocate resources

Prices perform three functions that coordinate buyers and sellers:

- **Rationing:** a higher price reduces quantity demanded so that a scarce good goes to those most willing and
  able to pay.
- **Signalling:** a rising price tells producers that consumers want more and tells consumers that the good is
  becoming scarcer.
- **Incentive:** a higher price raises expected profit, encouraging firms to move resources into producing more.

**Example.** A drought cuts the supply of avocados. The price rises (signal), shoppers buy fewer (rationing),
and growers in other regions plant more because it is profitable (incentive).

The specification asks you to assess the view that the price mechanism is **impersonal**: it allocates by
willingness and ability to pay, not by need. It also asks whether extending markets into new areas is
desirable, using the example of a market for blood: paying donors changes the nature of the transaction and the
motives of those who give.

## Step 2: name the failure

Market failure occurs "whenever a market leads to a misallocation of resources". It is **complete** when a
market is missing altogether, as with a pure public good, and **partial** when a market exists but produces the
wrong quantity or price.

| Cause | Why the market gets it wrong | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Public goods | Non-rival and non-excludable, so free riders will not pay | Missing market |
| Negative externality | Social cost exceeds private cost; third parties are not compensated | Over-production |
| Positive externality | Social benefit exceeds private benefit | Under-production |
| Merit good | Consumers undervalue benefits, often through imperfect information | Under-consumption |
| Demerit good | Consumers underestimate harms | Over-consumption |
| Monopoly power | Output restricted, price raised above marginal cost | Under-production, higher prices |
| Immobility of factors | Workers and capital cannot move to where they are needed | Unemployment, shortages |
| Asymmetric information | One side of a transaction knows more than the other | Wrong quantity or quality traded |
| Inequality | Market outcomes may give an inequitable distribution | Judged unfair (a value judgement) |

Two specification points are often missed. The classification of merit and demerit goods **depends on a value
judgement**, and not every good with an externality in consumption is a merit or demerit good. The **tragedy of
the commons** describes how a shared, unowned resource such as a fishery is overused because no user bears the
full cost, which links the absence of **property rights** to environmental market failure.

## Step 3: measure it

**Worked example: a negative production externality (invented).** In a market for cement:

- Marginal private benefit (demand): P = 100 − Q
- Marginal private cost (supply): P = 20 + Q
- Each unit causes external pollution costs of 20, so marginal social cost: P = 40 + Q

**Market outcome:** 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so **Q = 40** and **P = 60**.

**Social optimum:** 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so **Q = 30** and **P = 70**.

The market over-produces by 10 units. On each of those units, marginal social cost exceeds marginal social
benefit. The gap is 20 at Q = 40 (MSC is 80, MSB is 60) and zero at Q = 30, so the **welfare loss** is a
triangle: ½ × 10 × 20 = **100**.

**A tax equal to the external cost** (20 per unit) shifts the supply curve up to MSC. The new equilibrium is
Q = 30. Buyers pay 70, producers keep 50 after tax, and the government raises 20 × 30 = **600**. The externality
has been **internalised**: the polluter now faces the cost it imposes.

**Worked example: a positive consumption externality (invented).** For a vaccination programme, marginal
private benefit is P = 80 − Q, marginal cost is P = 20 + Q, and each vaccination gives others a benefit of 20,
so MSB is P = 100 − Q. The market gives 80 − Q = 20 + Q, **Q = 30**; the optimum is 100 − Q = 20 + Q,
**Q = 40**. A subsidy of 20 per vaccination would close the gap.

## Step 4: choose the intervention

The specification lists "indirect taxation, subsidies, price controls, state provision and regulation, the
extension of property rights and pollution permits". Match the tool to the failure:

| Failure | Possible interventions |
|---|---|
| Negative externality | Indirect tax, regulation, pollution permits, extending property rights |
| Positive externality or merit good | Subsidy, state provision, information campaigns, regulation (compulsory schooling) |
| Demerit good | Indirect tax, regulation or bans, information |
| Public good | State provision financed by taxation |
| Monopoly power | Competition policy, regulation of prices, public ownership |
| Inequality | Progressive taxes, benefits, state provision of services |

**Price controls.** A maximum price below equilibrium creates a shortage. In an invented rental market with
Qd = 500 − 10P and Qs = 20P − 100, equilibrium is **P = 20**, **Q = 300**. A maximum rent of 15 gives
Qd = 350 and Qs = 200: a **shortage of 150** homes, so non-price rationing, waiting lists or illegal side
payments may appear, and fewer homes are let than before.

**Pollution permits** set the total quantity of pollution and let firms trade the right to pollute. Firms that
can cut emissions cheaply do so and sell spare permits, so the target is met at lower total cost than a uniform
rule would achieve. The difficulty is choosing the right number of permits.

**Competition policy and ownership.** UK competition policy aims to prevent abuse of market power, for example
by investigating mergers and anti-competitive agreements; detailed knowledge of the law is not required.
Privatisation, regulation and deregulation each have arguments for and against; one risk the specification
names is **regulatory capture**, when a regulator comes to serve the interests of the firms it regulates.

## Step 5: test for government failure

Government failure occurs "when government intervention in the economy leads to a misallocation of resources".
The specification names inadequate information, conflicting objectives and administrative costs as sources,
and notes that intervention can create distortions and unintended consequences.

Apply each to the cement tax:

- **Information:** if the true external cost is 12 rather than 20, a tax of 20 cuts output below the optimum.
- **Conflicting objectives:** the government may also want cheap housebuilding, which the tax raises in cost.
- **Administrative costs:** measuring emissions and collecting the tax uses resources.
- **Unintended consequences:** production may move to a country with weaker rules, so pollution is shifted, not
  reduced.

The specification's key point: "even when there is market failure, government intervention will not necessarily
improve economic welfare." A strong conclusion compares the welfare loss from the failure with the likely cost of
the government's mistakes.

## Common errors

- Defining market failure as a market that has "collapsed". It means any misallocation of resources.
- Drawing an externality diagram without labelling MPC, MSC, MPB and MSB, or without showing the welfare-loss
  area.
- Stating that a tax "solves" an externality without asking whether the tax equals the external cost.
- Treating merit goods as identical to goods with positive externalities.
- Assuming state provision means "free" with no opportunity cost.
- Listing government failures without linking them to the policy in the question.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a failure in a chain: cause, divergence between private and social values, quantity compared with
  the optimum, welfare loss.
- **Calculate** welfare losses as triangle areas: ½ × base × height, with the base measured in units and the
  height as the gap between social cost and benefit.
- **Evaluate** an intervention against at least two criteria (effectiveness, cost, equity, risk of government
  failure) and reach a judgement for the specific market.
