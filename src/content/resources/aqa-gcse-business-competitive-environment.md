The competitive environment is the set of rival businesses a firm faces in its market, together with the uncertainty
and risk that come with trading. AQA GCSE Business (8132) section 3.2.6 asks you to understand what a market and
competition are, analyse how competition affects a business, spot situations with little or no competition, explain
why every business faces uncertainty and risk, and explain why entrepreneurs still start businesses and how they can
reduce risk.

This guide covers section 3.2.6 Competitive environment, the last part of 3.2 Influences on business, in the AQA GCSE
Business specification (Version 1.0, 19 August 2016) for first teaching from September 2017. Influences on business
is assessed on **both** papers (Paper 1 and Paper 2, each 1 hour 45 minutes and 90 marks), so questions on
competition can be linked to operations, human resources, marketing or finance. The specification gives no tier
labels for this section.

## What the specification asks for

| Content (3.2.6) | Students should be able to |
|---|---|
| Impact on businesses of operating in competitive markets | Understand the meaning of a market and competition |
| | Analyse potential impacts of competition on businesses, and identify situations when businesses face minimal or no competition |
| Uncertainty and risks businesses face | Understand the risks businesses face and the reasons why all businesses face uncertainty |
| | Understand why entrepreneurs embark on running businesses and the activities businesses can undertake to minimise risks |

## What is a market, and what is competition?

A **market** is anywhere buyers and sellers come together to exchange goods or services. It can be a physical place,
such as a high street, or online. **Competition** is the rivalry between businesses that sell similar goods or
services to the same customers. A business's **competitors** are those rival firms.

Markets differ in how competitive they are. A town centre with six coffee shops is highly competitive; a remote
island with one ferry operator has almost no competition. The more competitive a market, the more choice customers
have, and the harder each business must work to win and keep them.

## How does competition affect a business?

In an "analyse" question you need a chain of reasoning: what the rival does, how the business responds, and what
that does to sales, costs or profit.

| What rivals do | Likely response | Possible effect on the business |
|---|---|---|
| Charge lower prices | Cut prices, run promotions | Sales protected, but profit per item falls |
| Offer better quality | Improve quality or design | Higher costs in the short term; stronger reputation |
| Launch new products | Innovate | Development spending; risk that the new product fails |
| Give better service | Train staff, improve service | Training costs; more loyal customers |
| Advertise heavily | Spend more on promotion | Higher costs; protects brand awareness |
| Win customers away | Cut costs and waste | Greater efficiency, or falling market share and profit |

Competition is good for **customers** (more choice, lower prices, better quality). For the **business** it can be
good or bad: it pushes the firm to become efficient and innovative, but it can squeeze profit and, if the firm fails
to respond, lead to closure.

### How does competition link to the functional areas?

The specification says you must be aware of how influences affect the four functional areas.

| Functional area | Example of the impact of competition |
|---|---|
| Business operations | Reduce waste to lower unit costs; raise quality to match rivals |
| Human resources | Train staff in customer service; offer better pay to keep skilled staff that rivals want |
| Marketing | Change prices, run promotions, develop features rivals lack |
| Finance | Lower profit margins; find money for new products or advertising |

## When do businesses face little or no competition?

A business may face minimal or no competition when:

- it is the **only supplier in an area**, such as the only shop in a small village;
- it has a **new or unique product** that rivals cannot yet copy, for example one protected by a patent;
- it is **very expensive to start up** in the market, so few firms can enter (for example running a railway);
- it serves a **small, specialist market** that is not big enough to attract rivals.

With little competition a business can usually charge higher prices and earn higher profits, and it may spend less
on promotion. The drawbacks are that it has less pressure to keep costs down or to innovate, and high profits can
**attract new competitors**, who may catch it unprepared.

## Why does every business face uncertainty and risk?

**Uncertainty** means the future cannot be known. **Risk** is the chance that something goes wrong, such as making a
loss or the business failing. Section 3.1.1 explains why uncertainty never disappears: businesses face a
**constantly changing business environment** because of changes in technology, the economic situation, legislation
and environmental expectations.

| Source of risk | Example | Possible effect |
|---|---|---|
| Competitors | A rival opens nearby or cuts its prices | Lower sales or a forced price cut |
| Customers | Tastes or fashions change | Demand falls; unsold stock |
| Economic climate | Interest rates rise or unemployment increases | Customers spend less; loan repayments rise |
| Technology | A new technology makes a product out of date | Sales collapse unless the firm adapts |
| Legislation | A new law raises costs, such as a higher minimum wage | Higher costs; lower profit |
| Globalisation | Exchange rates change, or overseas rivals enter | Import costs rise or prices are undercut |
| Suppliers | Late deliveries or rising prices | Production delayed; costs rise |

## Why do entrepreneurs take the risk?

An **entrepreneur** spots an opportunity, organises resources and takes the risk of starting a business. Section 3.1.1
lists the objectives of an entrepreneur, which explain why people start businesses despite the risk:

- to be their own boss;
- flexible working hours;
- to pursue an interest;
- to earn more money;
- to fill a gap they have identified in the market;
- dissatisfaction with their current job.

Entrepreneurs judge that the possible rewards outweigh the risk. Higher potential reward usually comes with higher
risk.

## How can businesses minimise risk?

| Activity | How it reduces risk |
|---|---|
| Market research | Checks demand and what rivals offer before money is committed |
| Business planning | Sets objectives and forecasts, and highlights problems in advance |
| Cash-flow forecasting | Warns of cash shortages so finance can be arranged in time |
| Starting small or testing the idea | Limits the amount lost if the idea fails |
| Insurance | Pays out after events such as fire, theft or injury claims |
| Limited liability | Owners can lose only what they invested, not personal assets |
| A range of products or markets | A fall in one does not sink the whole business |
| Keeping cash reserves | Helps the business survive a difficult period |
| Monitoring competitors and customers | Allows a fast response to change |

No activity removes risk completely. A strong evaluation says which activity matters most for the business in the
case study, and why.

## Worked example: responding to a new competitor

Hartley's Bakery sells 600 loaves a week at £3.00, so weekly revenue is 600 x £3.00 = **£1,800**. A supermarket
opens nearby selling loaves at £1.50.

- **Option A: cut the price to £2.50** and keep 600 sales: 600 x £2.50 = **£1,500** (£300 less a week).
- **Option B: keep £3.00** and sales fall by 15%: 15% of 600 = 90, so 510 x £3.00 = **£1,530** (£270 less a week).

On revenue alone, Option B loses slightly less. A good answer adds that Option B keeps the bakery's quality image and
fewer loaves means lower ingredient costs, so profit may hold up better; but if sales keep falling, the bakery may
need to compete on quality and service rather than price, because it cannot match the supermarket's costs.

## How should you answer competition questions?

- **Explain (usually 2 to 4 marks):** make a point and develop it with a consequence ("so... which means...").
- **Analyse (usually 6 marks):** build two or three chains linked to the case.
- **Evaluate (usually 9 or 12 marks):** weigh both sides and reach a justified judgement, often "it depends on..."
  followed by the most important factor in the case.

Use the figures and details in the case study; generic answers score lower.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is competition always bad for a business?**
No. It can squeeze profit, but it also pushes a business to improve quality, cut waste and innovate, which can make
it stronger in the long run.

**What is the difference between risk and uncertainty?**
Uncertainty is the general fact that the future cannot be known. Risk is the chance of a particular bad outcome, such
as a loss, which can often be reduced by planning, research or insurance.

**Why might a business with no competitors still fail?**
It may become inefficient or stop innovating, so when a rival enters, or customer tastes change, it is unprepared
and loses sales quickly.

**Can market research remove risk?**
No. It reduces risk by giving better information, but tastes, the economy and rivals can still change after the
research is done.

## Common errors

- Saying competition is "bad" without explaining its effect on price, sales or profit.
- Writing about benefits to customers when the question asks about the business.
- Defining a market only as a physical place.
- Claiming that planning or research removes risk entirely.
- Giving reasons for starting a business that are not linked to the case study.
- Listing points in an evaluation without reaching a judgement.
