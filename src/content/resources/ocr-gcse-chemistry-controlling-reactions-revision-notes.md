## Core definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | The speed at which reactants are consumed or products made |
| Mean rate | Change in amount ÷ time taken, over an interval |
| Activation energy | Lowest energy a collision must have for the particles to react |
| Collision theory | Particles react only when they collide with at least the activation energy |
| Surface area to volume ratio | Total surface area ÷ volume of a solid; bigger for smaller pieces |
| Catalyst | A substance that speeds up a reaction and is not used up |
| Enzyme | A catalyst in a biological system |

## C5.2a and CM5.2i: measuring rate

| Method | What you record | Best for | Rate unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas syringe, or upturned measuring cylinder or burette over water | Gas volume at set times | Any reaction giving a gas | cm³/s |
| Flask on a balance, cotton wool plug | Mass at set times | Reactions giving a heavy gas such as CO₂ | g/s |
| Disappearing cross | Time for a cross to vanish under a cloudy mixture | Thiosulfate + acid (sulfur precipitate) | 1/t in s⁻¹ |
| Time to disappear | Time for a fixed piece of solid to react | Magnesium ribbon in acid | 1/t in s⁻¹ |

**Method in steps: mean rate**
1. Find the change (cm³ or g) over the interval.
2. Divide by the time in seconds.
3. Give the unit.

*Worked reminder.* 105 cm³ of gas in 70 s → 105 ÷ 70 = **1.5 cm³/s**.

Mass loss is poor for hydrogen-producing reactions: hydrogen is so light that the mass barely changes.

**Fair test checklist.** One independent variable; control the volume and concentration of acid, mass and size of solid, temperature; repeat, ignore anomalies, average.

## C5.2b, CM5.2ii and CM5.2iii: rate graphs

- Product–time curves start steep and level off. Steeper = faster.
- Flat = **stopped**, because a reactant has run out.
- Same final height = same amount of limiting reactant, whatever the rate.
- A faster run with the same reactant amounts reaches the **same** plateau **sooner**.
- Doubling the limiting reactant doubles the plateau height.

**Method in steps: rate from a tangent**
1. Rule a line that just grazes the curve at the time given.
2. Extend it and choose two points far apart **on the tangent**.
3. Gradient = (change in y) ÷ (change in x), with units.

*Worked reminder.* A tangent passes through (10 s, 28 cm³) and (50 s, 76 cm³). Gradient = (76 − 28) ÷ (50 − 10) = 48 ÷ 40 = **1.2 cm³/s**.

**1/t.** For a fixed amount of change, rate ∝ 1/t. A run lasting 80 s has 1/t = 0.0125 s⁻¹; one lasting 20 s has 1/t = 0.050 s⁻¹, so it is **4 times** as fast. The topic does not ask for orders of reaction.

## C5.2c and C5.2d: what changes the rate, and why

| Factor increased | Rate | Collision frequency | Collision energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Up | Up | Up: more collisions reach the activation energy |
| Concentration | Up | Up | No change |
| Pressure (gases) | Up | Up | No change |
| Surface area | Up | Up | No change |

**Model sentence for temperature.** "Heated particles move faster. They meet more often, and a larger share of those meetings carry at least the activation energy, so successful collisions happen more often each second."

**Model sentence for concentration or pressure.** "Reacting particles are packed more closely, so they meet more often and successful collisions happen more often each second."

## C5.2e and CM5.2iv: surface area to volume ratio

**Method in steps**
1. Surface area of one piece = sum of the areas of its faces (cube: 6 × side²).
2. Volume of one piece (cube: side³).
3. Multiply both by the number of pieces if you cut it up; the total volume does not change.
4. Ratio = surface area ÷ volume.

*Worked reminder.* A 5 mm cube: 150 mm² ÷ 125 mm³ = **1.2 : 1**. Cut into 1 mm cubes: 125 × 6 = 750 mm² ÷ 125 mm³ = **6 : 1**. The smaller the pieces, the bigger the ratio, the more frequent the collisions, the faster the reaction.

## C5.2f, C5.2g and C5.2h: catalysts

**Characteristics.** A catalyst:
- increases the rate;
- is not used up, and has the same mass and chemical make-up at the end;
- does not change the amount of product or the overall energy change;
- is needed only in small amounts;
- usually works for one reaction (or type of reaction) only.

**Identifying one.** Faster **and** recovered with unchanged mass. A substance that speeds things up but loses mass is reacting, so it is not acting as a catalyst.

**Examples from the practical suggestions.** Manganese(IV) oxide for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide; copper powder for zinc with dilute sulfuric acid.

**How it works.** It provides an **alternative pathway with a lower activation energy**. At the same temperature, a bigger proportion of collisions now have enough energy, so successful collisions are more frequent.

**Reaction profile with a catalyst.** Same reactant level, same product level, lower hump. Activation energy is drawn from the reactant line up to the peak.

## C5.2i: enzymes

Enzymes are **biological catalysts**: they speed up reactions in living things and are not used up.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mean rate** (over an interval) vs **rate at a moment** (tangent gradient).
- **Reaction stopped** (flat line) vs **reaction slow** (shallow but rising).
- **Frequency** of collisions (concentration, pressure, surface area) vs **frequency and energy** (temperature).
- **Lowering activation energy** (catalyst) vs **giving particles more energy** (heating).
- **Catalyst** (unchanged mass) vs **reactant** (used up).
- **Surface area** (goes up when cut) vs **volume** (stays the same when cut).

## Quick self-test

1. 90 cm³ of oxygen forms in 75 s. Calculate the mean rate.
2. A flask loses 0.66 g in 150 s. Calculate the mean rate.
3. The cross vanishes after 45 s. Calculate 1/t to 3 significant figures.
4. Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of an 8 mm cube.
5. A tangent passes through (0 s, 9 cm³) and (30 s, 57 cm³). Calculate its gradient.
6. Why does the gradient of a gas volume curve fall with time?
7. Name two factors that change only the frequency of collisions.
8. Explain why raising the pressure speeds up a gas reaction.
9. Give two pieces of evidence that a powder is a catalyst.
10. How does a catalyst change a reaction profile?
11. What is an enzyme?
12. Run X takes 30 s and run Y takes 90 s for the same change. How many times faster is X?

### Answers

1. 90 ÷ 75 = **1.2 cm³/s**.
2. 0.66 ÷ 150 = **0.0044 g/s**.
3. 1 ÷ 45 = **0.0222 s⁻¹**.
4. Surface area 6 × 64 = 384 mm²; volume 512 mm³; ratio **0.75 : 1**.
5. (57 − 9) ÷ (30 − 0) = **1.6 cm³/s**.
6. Reactants are used up, so particles are less concentrated and collisions become less frequent.
7. Any two of: concentration, pressure, surface area.
8. The gas particles are pushed closer together, so they collide more often.
9. The rate increases, and the same mass of powder is recovered at the end.
10. The activation energy hump is lower; the reactant and product levels do not change.
11. A catalyst in a biological system.
12. 90 ÷ 30 = **3 times** faster (1/t is three times bigger).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving the unit off a rate, or writing cm³ when the data were masses.
- Reading tangent points from the curve rather than the tangent line, or using two points too close together.
- Saying a flat line means a "constant rate"; it means the reaction has finished.
- Drawing a faster curve that ends at a higher volume when the amount of limiting reactant has not changed.
- Writing "more collisions" with no reference to time ("more often", "each second").
- Explaining temperature only through faster particles, with no mention of activation energy.
- Claiming a catalyst energises the particles or gets used up.
- Calling a substance a catalyst without the evidence that its mass is unchanged.
- Treating a longer time as a faster rate when using 1/t.
- In surface area questions, forgetting to multiply by the number of small pieces.
