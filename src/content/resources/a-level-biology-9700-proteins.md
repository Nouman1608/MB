## 2.3.1 Amino acids and the peptide bond

### The general structure

Every amino acid has the same core. A central carbon atom carries four different groups:

- an **amine group**, –NH₂
- a **carboxyl group**, –COOH
- a **hydrogen atom**, –H
- an **R group** (side chain), which differs between amino acids.

```
        H     R     O
         \    |    //
          N — C — C
         /    |    \
        H     H     O — H
   amine group       carboxyl group
```

About 20 different amino acids are used to build proteins, differing only in their R group. Glycine's R group is a single hydrogen atom. R groups may be non-polar, polar or charged, and cysteine's contains sulfur. These differences decide how a chain folds.

When you draw an amino acid, show the –OH of the carboxyl group and both hydrogens of the amine group.

### Forming a peptide bond: condensation

Two amino acids join by a **condensation reaction**. The –OH from the carboxyl group of one amino acid and an –H from the amine group of the other are removed as **one molecule of water**. A covalent **peptide bond**, –CO–NH–, forms between the carbon of the first and the nitrogen of the second.

```
H₂N–CH(R₁)–COOH + H₂N–CH(R₂)–COOH  →  H₂N–CH(R₁)–CO–NH–CH(R₂)–COOH + H₂O
                                                     peptide bond
```

Two amino acids make a **dipeptide**; many make a **polypeptide**, with a free amine group at one end and a free carboxyl group at the other. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a specific shape.

### Breaking a peptide bond: hydrolysis

**Hydrolysis** is the reverse. A molecule of water is added across the peptide bond, which breaks and re-forms the –COOH and –NH₂ groups. In cells, protease enzymes catalyse this. In the laboratory, boiling with acid also hydrolyses peptide bonds.

**Worked example 1.** Glycine has Mr 75 and alanine has Mr 89. (Mr of water = 18.)

(a) Calculate the Mr of the dipeptide Gly–Ala.
(b) How many water molecules are released when a single polypeptide of 120 amino acids is made?
(c) A protein has two polypeptide chains of 60 and 45 amino acids. How many peptide bonds does it contain?

*Solution.*
(a) One condensation removes one water: 75 + 89 − 18 = **146**.
(b) A chain of n amino acids has n − 1 peptide bonds, so 120 − 1 = **119 water molecules**.
(c) Count each chain separately, because the chains are not joined by peptide bonds: (60 − 1) + (45 − 1) = **103 peptide bonds**.

## 2.3.2 The four levels of protein structure

**Primary structure** is the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, held by peptide bonds. Changing one amino acid can change how the whole chain folds, because every later level depends on the R groups the sequence sets.

**Secondary structure** is the regular coiling or folding of the polypeptide backbone, held by **hydrogen bonds** between the –C=O of one amino acid and the –N–H of another. The R groups are not involved. Two forms:

- **α-helix**: the chain coils into a spiral. Each –N–H forms a hydrogen bond with the –C=O of the amino acid four places earlier in the chain. The R groups point outwards from the helix.
- **β-pleated sheet**: lengths of chain lie side by side, and hydrogen bonds form between the backbones of neighbouring lengths, giving a flat, folded sheet.

**Tertiary structure** is the further folding of the whole chain, helices and sheets included, into a precise three-dimensional shape. It is held by interactions between **R groups** that may be far apart in the sequence.

**Quaternary structure** is the association of **two or more polypeptide chains** into one functional protein. The same interactions as in tertiary structure hold the chains together. A protein made of a single chain has no quaternary structure.

## 2.3.3 The interactions that hold proteins in shape

| Interaction | Between | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrophobic interactions | Non-polar R groups | They cluster together away from water, usually in the middle of a globular protein |
| Hydrogen bonds | Polar R groups (for example –OH, –NH₂); also backbone C=O and N–H in secondary structure | Individually weak, but there are many |
| Ionic bonds | R groups carrying opposite charges, such as –NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻ | Broken by changes in pH, which alter the charges |
| Covalent bonds, including disulfide bonds | Peptide bonds join amino acids; disulfide bonds (–S–S–) form between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups | Disulfide bonds are the strongest of the R group interactions |

Name the actual interaction and the groups involved: "an ionic bond between oppositely charged R groups" scores, "a bond between amino acids" does not. Covalent bonding covers both the peptide bonds of the primary structure and the disulfide bonds of tertiary and quaternary structure.

**Worked example 2.** In a folded protein, a cysteine at position 23 lies next to a cysteine at position 88, a lysine R group (–NH₃⁺) lies next to an aspartate R group (–COO⁻), and several leucine R groups (non-polar) sit in the centre. Name the interaction in each case and state which is most likely to survive heating to 60 °C.

*Solution.* Cysteine–cysteine: a **disulfide bond**. Lysine–aspartate: an **ionic bond**. Leucines: **hydrophobic interactions**. The disulfide bond is covalent, so it is the strongest and most likely to survive heating; hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions are weaker and are disrupted first.

## 2.3.4 Globular and fibrous proteins

| | Globular proteins | Fibrous proteins |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Chains folded into a compact, roughly spherical shape | Long strands or fibres |
| Solubility | Generally soluble in water | Generally insoluble |
| Role | Physiological (metabolic): transport, catalysis, signalling | Structural: strength and support |
| Examples | Haemoglobin, enzymes, antibodies | Collagen, keratin |

Globular proteins are soluble because hydrophobic R groups fold inside and hydrophilic (polar or charged) R groups face outwards, interacting with water.

## 2.3.5 The structure of haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is the globular protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells. One molecule has:

- **four polypeptide chains**: two **α-globin** chains and two **β-globin** chains. In adult haemoglobin each α chain has 141 amino acids and each β chain has 146.
- one **haem group** attached to each chain, so four haem groups per molecule. Haem is a non-protein (prosthetic) group with an **iron ion (Fe²⁺)** at its centre.

Each chain has its own primary, secondary (mostly α-helix) and tertiary structure, folding to leave a pocket that holds its haem group. The four chains fit together into the **quaternary structure**, held by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds.

## 2.3.6 Relating haemoglobin's structure to its function

- **Iron in haem binds oxygen.** Each Fe²⁺ binds one oxygen molecule reversibly, so one haemoglobin molecule carries up to **four O₂ molecules**. Without enough iron, less haemoglobin is made and the blood carries less oxygen.
- **Solubility.** Hydrophilic R groups face outwards, so haemoglobin stays dissolved at a very high concentration inside red blood cells.
- **Quaternary structure allows cooperative binding.** When one oxygen molecule binds, the molecule changes shape slightly, making it easier for the next oxygen to bind. This is why the oxygen dissociation curve in Topic 8 is S-shaped.
- **Reversible binding.** Oxygen loads in the lungs and unloads in respiring tissues.

**Worked example 3.** A red blood cell contains about 2.8 × 10⁸ haemoglobin molecules. Calculate the maximum number of oxygen molecules it can carry.

*Solution.* Four haem groups per molecule, one O₂ per haem: 4 × 2.8 × 10⁸ = **1.12 × 10⁹ oxygen molecules**.

For transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the dissociation curve, see the [Transport in mammals study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-transport-in-mammals/).

## 2.3.7 The structure of collagen

**One collagen molecule** is made of **three polypeptide chains**. Each chain is a long, tightly wound helix (not an α-helix). The three chains wind round each other to form a **triple helix**, rather like a rope. **Hydrogen bonds** between the chains hold the triple helix together.

The primary structure is a repeating pattern: **almost every third amino acid is glycine**, often written Gly–X–Y. Proline and hydroxyproline are common in the other positions. Glycine has the smallest R group (a hydrogen atom). Every third amino acid sits in the centre of the triple helix, where there is only room for this small R group, so the three chains can pack very closely.

**From molecules to fibres.**

1. Collagen molecules lie side by side in parallel rows.
2. Neighbouring molecules are **staggered**, so their ends do not line up. This avoids a line of weakness running across the bundle.
3. **Covalent cross-links** form between R groups (lysine residues) of neighbouring molecules.
4. The cross-linked molecules form **fibrils**; many fibrils bundle together into **collagen fibres**.

**Worked example 4.** The triple-helical part of one collagen chain has 1011 amino acids in a Gly–X–Y repeat. How many glycine residues are in this region of one chain, and in one collagen molecule?

*Solution.* One in every three: 1011 ÷ 3 = **337 per chain**. Three chains: 3 × 337 = **1011 per molecule**.

## 2.3.8 Relating collagen's structure to its function

Collagen is a structural protein in skin, tendons, ligaments, bone, cartilage and blood vessel walls. It must resist pulling forces.

- **Triple helix with many hydrogen bonds** → each molecule is strong and resists stretching.
- **Glycine every third position** → tight packing of the three chains, adding strength.
- **Staggered ends** → no weak line across the fibre.
- **Covalent cross-links** → molecules cannot slide past each other, giving very high **tensile strength**.
- **Many molecules in fibrils and fibres** → strength over large distances, for example along a tendon.
- **Insoluble** → it stays in place in tissues and is not dissolved by body fluids.

## Common errors

- Saying the peptide bond forms between two R groups. It forms between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amine group of the next.
- Saying secondary structure is held by bonds between R groups. It is held by hydrogen bonds in the backbone.
- Giving "disulfide bonds" as an interaction between any two amino acids. They form only between cysteines.
- Writing that haemoglobin has "one haem group". There are four: one per chain.
- Describing collagen as a single helix, or calling its chains α-helices.
- Forgetting the cross-links and the staggered arrangement when asked how fibres resist tension.
