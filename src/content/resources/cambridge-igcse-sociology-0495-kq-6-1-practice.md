These questions practise key question 6.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What are crime, deviance and social control?* Section A uses short-answer styles; Section B uses the longer Paper 2 styles.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *deviance*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *self-report study*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** examples of informal sanctions. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** examples of cyber crime. **[2]**

**5.** Define the term *green crime*. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** reasons why the police may not record a crime that has been reported to them. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** ways in which crime and deviance are relative. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** ways in which traditional and digital media act as agencies of social control. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** strengths of victim surveys as a way of measuring crime. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss how formal agencies of social control try to prevent crime and deviance.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss the usefulness of self-report studies for measuring crime.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that informal social control is more effective than formal social control in preventing crime and deviance.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** Behaviour that breaks the social norms of a group or society (1) and attracts disapproval or negative sanctions, whether or not it is illegal (1).

**2. [2]** A survey, usually an anonymous questionnaire (1), asking people to say which crimes or deviant acts they have committed, whether or not they were caught (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: praise or approval; gossip; ridicule; being ignored or excluded by peers; grounding by parents; a disapproving look.

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: hacking; online fraud or phishing; ransomware attacks; identity theft online; online harassment or cyber-stalking; distributing illegal content.

**5. [2]** Crime that harms the natural environment (1), such as illegal dumping of toxic waste, illegal logging or illegal wildlife trading (1).

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: police judge that no crime occurred; insufficient evidence; police discretion that the matter is too minor; pressure from targets; the victim withdraws the complaint.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three ways, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Between societies**: drinking alcohol is legal in many countries but prohibited in others.
- **Over time**: smoking in enclosed public places became illegal in England in 2007; acts once criminal, such as sex between men in England and Wales before 1967, have been decriminalised.
- **By situation**: killing is murder in peacetime but may be lawful for a soldier in war.
- **Within a society or culture**: behaviour accepted in one subculture may be deviant to the wider society.
- **By who is labelled**: Becker argued deviance depends on whether an act is labelled, so the same act may be treated differently depending on who commits it.

**8. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **Setting norms**: news and entertainment show what is acceptable and what is punished, shaping norms.
- **Moral panics and labelling**: media coverage can label groups as folk devils and increase pressure for formal control (Stanley Cohen).
- **Public shaming online**: social media users criticise or "cancel" people who break norms, acting as a negative sanction.
- **Platform moderation**: online platforms remove posts and ban users who break rules.
- **Surveillance and evidence**: shared videos and posts can expose offenders and support police investigations.

**9. [6]** Three strengths, 1 + 1 each:
- **Reveal unreported crime**: victims may tell a survey about crimes they did not report to the police, reducing the dark figure.
- **Independent of police recording**: results are not affected by police discretion or targets, so trends can be compared with official statistics.
- **Detailed victim information**: they show who is most at risk by age, gender, area or ethnicity.
- **Local surveys show local problems**: the Islington Crime Survey (1986) found higher inner-city victimisation than national figures suggested.
- **Large national samples**: such as the Crime Survey for England and Wales, which allow trends over time.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- **Police**: patrols, investigation, arrest and stop and search; visible presence deters.
- **Courts**: sentences punish offenders and deter others, making an example of offenders.
- **Penal system**: prison incapacitates; probation and rehabilitation programmes try to change behaviour.
- **Government**: creates laws defining crime, funds policing and sets priorities, such as against cyber crime.
- **Armed forces**: protect the state and may be used in emergencies to restore order.
- Evaluation: many crimes are never detected, so deterrence is limited; formal control may target some groups more than others.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Strengths**: reveal offending never reported or detected; show who commits crime rather than who is caught, testing claims that statistics reflect police targeting; anonymous, so people may admit offences; useful for victimless crimes such as drug use.
- **Limitations**: respondents may lie, forget or exaggerate (social desirability or bravado); usually ask about minor offences, so serious crime is under-represented; samples are often young people in schools, so may not be representative; validity is hard to check.
- Judgement: useful as one measure alongside official statistics and victim surveys.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Informal control is constant: family, peers and workplace are present every day.
- Norms are internalised through socialisation, so people control themselves.
- Hirschi's social bond theory: attachment, commitment, involvement and belief keep people from crime.
- Heidensohn: tight informal control of women helps explain their lower recorded offending.
- Religion and community pressure can be powerful where formal control is weak.
*Against the view:*
- Some people lack strong bonds, and informal control cannot stop determined or organised offenders.
- Peer groups and some online communities may encourage deviance rather than prevent it.
- Formal agencies have the power to arrest, imprison and incapacitate serious offenders.
- Laws and courts set clear rules and consequences that apply to everyone.
- Surveillance can deter crime; Foucault argued people who think they are watched discipline themselves.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that informal control explains most everyday conformity, while formal control is needed where informal control fails; the two work together.
