## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Elastic deformation | The object returns to its original shape and length when the forces are removed |
| Inelastic deformation | The object does not return to its original shape and length when the forces are removed |
| Extension | Increase in length: stretched length − original length |
| Compression | Decrease in length when an object is squashed |
| Spring constant (k) | Force per unit extension, in N/m; a measure of stiffness |
| Limit of proportionality | The point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to force |
| Linear relationship | A straight-line graph; for F and e, through the origin |
| Non-linear relationship | A curved graph |
| Elastic potential energy | Energy stored in a stretched or compressed elastic object |

## Why more than one force?

- One force on a stationary object → resultant force → the object accelerates (moves) instead of changing
  shape.
- Two or more forces → the object can stay still while it is stretched, bent or compressed.

| Action | Forces |
|---|---|
| Stretching | Two pulls in opposite directions (or a load pulling down and a support pulling up) |
| Bending | A push on one side and supports pushing the other way |
| Compressing | Two pushes towards each other |

## The equations

```
F = k e              recall        k = F / e      e = F / k
Eₑ = ½ k e²          equation sheet
F in N,  k in N/m,  e in m,  Eₑ in J
```

- Valid up to the **limit of proportionality**.
- F = k e also works for compression (e = compression).
- Work done stretching a spring = elastic potential energy stored, if not inelastically deformed.

### Unit conversions

```
mm → m   ÷ 1000        25 mm = 0.025 m
cm → m   ÷ 100         6.0 cm = 0.060 m
g  → kg  ÷ 1000        then weight = m × g
```

*Worked reminder.* 200 g hung on a spring, g = 9.8 N/kg, extension 4.9 cm.
F = 0.200 × 9.8 = 1.96 N; k = 1.96 / 0.049 = **40 N/m**.
Eₑ = 0.5 × 40 × 0.049² = **0.048 J**.

## Graph rules

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Straight line through the origin | Extension ∝ force (linear) |
| Point where the line starts to curve | Limit of proportionality |
| Gradient (F on y, e on x) | Spring constant k |
| Gradient (e on y, F on x) | 1/k |
| Area under F–e line | Work done = energy stored |
| Line does not return to zero when unloading | Inelastic deformation |

## Required practical 6 summary

```
apparatus  clamp stand (secured), spring, metre rule, pointer, mass hanger, slotted masses
measure    original length, then length after each added mass
calculate  extension = new length − original length
plot       force (y) against extension (x)
find       k from the gradient of the straight section
```

- Independent: force. Dependent: extension. Control: same spring.
- Read at eye level (avoid parallax); wait until the spring is still.
- Unload and remeasure to check for inelastic deformation.
- Safety: eye protection; secure the stand; keep feet clear.

## Must-know contrasts

- **Non-linear** (a graph shape) is not the same as **inelastic** (does not return to its original shape).
- **Extension** is not **length**.
- **Limit of proportionality** is a point on the graph; beyond it, F = k e and Eₑ = ½ k e² no longer
  apply.
- Doubling e doubles F but **quadruples** Eₑ.

## Exam traps

- Substituting a length instead of an extension.
- Leaving extension in cm in Eₑ = ½ k e²: the answer is out by 10 000.
- Calculating k from a point on the curved part of the graph.
- Forgetting the ½ or the square in Eₑ = ½ k e².
- Writing "one force bends it" in an "explain why more than one force" question.
- Stating "the spring is elastic" without saying it returns to its original length.

## Quick self-test

1. Explain why a single force cannot change the shape of a stationary object.
2. Describe the difference between elastic and inelastic deformation.
3. A spring's unstretched length is 15.0 cm; with a load it is 21.0 cm. Find the extension in metres.
4. A 4.5 N force stretches a spring by 0.090 m. Calculate k.
5. A spring with k = 60 N/m is stretched by 0.12 m. Calculate the force.
6. Calculate the elastic potential energy in the spring in question 5.
7. A car spring has k = 30 000 N/m and is compressed by 0.020 m. Calculate the force on it.
8. On a force–extension graph, how do you identify the limit of proportionality?
9. Extension is plotted on the y-axis and force on the x-axis. The gradient is 0.025 m/N. Find k.
10. The extension of a spring doubles. What happens to the energy stored, below the limit of
    proportionality?
11. In required practical 6, why is a pointer attached to the bottom of the spring?
12. How can you tell, at the end of required practical 6, whether the spring has been inelastically
    deformed?

### Answers

1. It would cause a resultant force, so the object would accelerate rather than stay still and deform.
2. Elastic: the object returns to its original shape and length when the forces are removed. Inelastic: it
   does not.
3. 21.0 − 15.0 = 6.0 cm = **0.060 m**.
4. 4.5 ÷ 0.090 = **50 N/m**.
5. 60 × 0.12 = **7.2 N**.
6. 0.5 × 60 × 0.12² = **0.432 J** (0.43 J).
7. 30 000 × 0.020 = **600 N**.
8. It is the point where the straight line through the origin starts to curve.
9. k = 1 ÷ 0.025 = **40 N/m**.
10. It becomes **four times** as large (Eₑ ∝ e²).
11. So that each reading is taken from the same point on the spring, making measurements consistent.
12. Remove all the masses and measure the length again; if it is longer than the original length, the spring
    has been inelastically deformed.
