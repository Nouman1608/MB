These questions cover **3.3.2.1 Fractional distillation of crude oil** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and able to reappear in Unit 5. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [fractional distillation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-2-1/) or the [fractional distillation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-2-1-revision-notes/) first. Take Ar values as C = 12.0 and H = 1.0. Boiling points are approximate.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Which compound is a saturated hydrocarbon?

A: C₂H₅OH

B: C₃H₆

C: C₄H₁₀

D: CH₃Cl **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by a fraction in the fractional distillation of petroleum. **[1]**

**3.** An alkane has Mr = 142.0.

**(a)** Give its molecular formula. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage by mass of hydrogen in it. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why fractional distillation of petroleum is described as a physical process. **[1]**

**5.** Propane and decane are both present in a sample of petroleum. State which is collected higher in a fractionating column, and explain why. **[2]**

**6.** Cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, is a saturated hydrocarbon but does not fit the formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Explain both parts of this statement. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Three isomers of C₇H₁₆ have these boiling points.

| Isomer | heptane | 2-methylhexane | 2,2-dimethylpentane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | 98 | 90 | 79 |

**(a)** Name the type of structural isomerism shown by these compounds. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the trend in boiling points. **[3]**

**8.** A laboratory model of a fractionating column has four collecting points. Going up the column, they are at 250 °C, 160 °C, 110 °C and 40 °C. Vapour still present above the 40 °C point leaves the top of the column. A vaporised mixture of dodecane (bp 216 °C), nonane (bp 151 °C), heptane (bp 98 °C) and pentane (bp 36 °C) enters at the base.

**(a)** State where each alkane is collected or leaves the column. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the mixture is fed in as a vapour at the base rather than as a liquid at the top. **[1]**

**9.** Explain why the boiling points of the unbranched alkanes increase from methane to octane. **[3]**

**10.** Fraction A contains alkanes with 5 to 8 carbon atoms. Fraction B contains alkanes with 20 to 25 carbon atoms.

**(a)** State which fraction is more viscous. **[1]**

**(b)** State which fraction is easier to ignite. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain both answers in terms of the forces between molecules. **[2]**

**11.** An unbranched alkane contains 83.7 % carbon by mass.

**(a)** Calculate its empirical formula. **[2]**

**(b)** Deduce its molecular formula, explaining why no other alkane has this empirical formula. **[2]**

**12.** Describe and explain how fractional distillation separates petroleum into fractions. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** C [1]

**2.** A mixture of hydrocarbons with boiling points in a narrow range [1]

**3.**

**(a)** 14n + 2 = 142.0, so n = 10: C₁₀H₂₂ [1]

**(b)** 22.0 ÷ 142.0 × 100 = 15.5 % [1]

**Total: 2**

**4.** No covalent bonds are broken; the compounds are separated by boiling point and are chemically unchanged [1]

**5.** Propane is collected higher (it leaves the top as a gas) [1]; it has a shorter chain and weaker van der Waals forces, so a lower boiling point, and it stays as a vapour where decane has already condensed [1]

**Total: 2**

**6.** Saturated: it has only single C–C bonds; hydrocarbon: it contains only carbon and hydrogen [1]; its carbon atoms form a ring, so it has two fewer hydrogen atoms than the chain alkane with six carbons (C₆H₁₄) and fits CₙH₂ₙ [1]

**Total: 2**

### Section B

**7.**

**(a)** Chain isomerism [1]

**(b)** Branching increases from heptane to 2,2-dimethylpentane, so the molecules become more compact [1]; there is less surface contact between molecules, so the van der Waals forces are weaker [1]; less energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point falls [1]

**Total: 4**

*Examiner insight:* All three isomers have the same number of electrons, so "more electrons" cannot be the reason. The difference here is shape.

**8.**

**(a)** Dodecane: at the 160 °C point [1]; nonane: at the 110 °C point [1]; heptane: at the 40 °C point [1]; pentane: leaves the top of the column [1]

**(b)** Separation depends on vapour rising and condensing at levels cooler than each boiling point; the column is hottest at the base, so all components must start as vapour there [1]

**Total: 5**

*Examiner insight:* Each compound condenses at the first point it reaches that is below its boiling point. Dodecane passes the 250 °C point as vapour, because 250 °C is above its boiling point.

**9.** As the chain gets longer, the molecules have more electrons and a larger surface area [1]; so the van der Waals forces between molecules (induced dipole attractions) become stronger [1]; so more energy is needed to overcome them, and the boiling point increases [1]

**Total: 3**

**10.**

**(a)** Fraction B [1]

**(b)** Fraction A [1]

**(c)** Fraction B has longer molecules with stronger van der Waals forces between them, so they flow past each other less easily [1]; fraction A has weaker van der Waals forces, so it evaporates more readily and forms enough vapour to ignite easily [1]

**Total: 4**

**11.**

**(a)** C: 83.7 ÷ 12.0 = 6.975; H: 16.3 ÷ 1.0 = 16.3 [1]; ratio 1 : 2.34, which is 3 : 7, so C₃H₇ [1]

**(b)** (C₃H₇)ₙ must fit CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: 7n = 2(3n) + 2 gives n = 2 [1]; so the formula is C₆H₁₄, and no other multiple of C₃H₇ fits the alkane formula [1]

**Total: 4**

*Examiner insight:* A ratio of 2.34 is not "2"; multiply by 3 to reach a whole number. Rounding too early gives CH₂, which is not an alkane.

**12.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid point, up to 6 [6]

- Petroleum is a mixture of alkanes with different chain lengths and so different boiling points.
- It is heated so that most of it vaporises, and the vapour enters near the base of the column.
- The column is hottest at the base and cooler towards the top (temperature gradient).
- Vapour rises and cools; each hydrocarbon condenses where the temperature falls below its boiling point and is collected there.
- Short-chain alkanes have weaker van der Waals forces and lower boiling points, so they condense higher up or leave as gases.
- Long-chain alkanes have stronger van der Waals forces and higher boiling points, so they condense low down; the heaviest leave as residue.
- Each fraction is a mixture with a narrow range of boiling points; no bonds are broken.

**Total: 6**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "stronger bonds" instead of "stronger van der Waals forces between molecules".
- Calling a fraction a single compound.
- Putting the hottest part of the column at the top.
- Rounding empirical formula ratios too early.
- Ignoring branching when isomers are compared.
