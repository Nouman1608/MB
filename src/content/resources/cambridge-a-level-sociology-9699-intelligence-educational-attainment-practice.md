## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by "heritability"? **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by "stereotype threat"? **[2]**

**3.** Describe two difficulties in defining intelligence. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what Lewontin's seed analogy shows about explaining differences between social groups. **[4]**

**5.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> Researchers gave the same reasoning test to two groups of 16-year-olds in the fictional city of Kestrel Bay. Group A was told the test "measures your natural intelligence". Group B was told it was "a puzzle task we are trying out". Pupils from a group that is often negatively stereotyped averaged 39 out of 60 in Group A and 48 out of 60 in Group B.

**(a)** Calculate each mean score as a percentage, to one decimal place, and the difference in percentage points. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what this suggests about IQ test scores. **[3]**

**6.** Explain two ways in which schools may construct pupils' "ability" rather than simply measure it. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why a correlation between IQ scores and exam results does not prove that intelligence causes attainment. **[6]**

**8.** Read the item.

> At Ashgrove School, a fictional school, every pupil takes a baseline test on entry. Those scoring in the bottom quarter are placed in a "foundation pathway" for all subjects and entered for lower-tier exams. A teacher says, "We know their ceiling from day one."

Using the item and sociological material, explain two ways this practice could lower attainment. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Differences in attainment between social classes are mainly caused by differences in inherited intelligence."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "IQ tests provide an objective measure of a pupil's ability."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that intelligence is the most important influence on educational attainment. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the extent to which IQ tests are influenced by social factors. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** The share of variation in a trait within a particular population [1] that is linked to genetic differences; it changes with the environment and does not apply to differences between groups [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Lower performance on a task [1] caused by awareness of a negative stereotype about one's own group, as shown by Steele and Aronson (1995) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Difficulty 1: psychologists disagree whether intelligence is one general ability or several [1]; Spearman's g and Gardner's multiple intelligences imply very different tests [1].
Difficulty 2: definitions vary between cultures [1]; some value speed and abstract reasoning, others practical wisdom or social responsibility, so a definition from one culture may misjudge another [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Within each field, height differences between plants may be largely genetic [1], but the difference between a rich and a poor field is caused entirely by the soil [1]. Likewise, heritability within a class or ethnic group cannot explain gaps between groups [1], which may result from unequal environments such as income, housing or schooling [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Group A: 39 ÷ 60 × 100 = 65.0% [1]. Group B: 48 ÷ 60 × 100 = 80.0% [1]. Difference: 80.0 − 65.0 = 15.0 percentage points [1].
**(b)** The same kind of pupils scored lower when told the test measured natural intelligence [1], which fits stereotype threat [1]. So scores reflect the social situation of testing, not only fixed ability [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Way 1: new IQism [1]. Gillborn and Youdell (2000) found schools treated baseline scores as fixed potential [1], placing pupils in lower sets and tiers that then limited what they could achieve [1].
Way 2: the ideology of giftedness [1]. Bourdieu and Passeron argued schools credit privileged pupils' cultural advantages as natural talent [1], so pupils from homes rich in cultural capital are labelled "bright" for what they learned at home [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Reason 1: a third factor may cause both [1]. Home advantages such as books, stable housing and educated parents may raise both IQ scores and exam results [1], so the correlation reflects background rather than innate ability [1].
Reason 2: tests and exams overlap [1]. Both reward similar learned skills such as reading speed, vocabulary and abstract reasoning [1], so a high correlation may show they measure similar things rather than that one causes the other [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Way 1: a self-fulfilling prophecy [1]. The teacher's belief in a fixed "ceiling" may lower expectations and the challenge of work offered [1], so pupils come to fit the label, as labelling theory predicts [1].
Way 2: structural limits [1]. Placement in a foundation pathway and lower-tier entries caps the grades pupils can reach [1]; Gillborn and Youdell found such decisions based on fixed-ability beliefs restricted attainment regardless of later progress [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: heritability cannot explain group gaps [1]. Lewontin's seed analogy shows that differences between groups can be wholly environmental even when within-group differences are partly genetic [1]. Turkheimer et al. (2003) found environment explained most IQ variation in poorer families [1], so class gaps are likely to reflect unequal environments [1].
Argument 2: measured intelligence is itself shaped by class [1]. Cultural bias, language and test practice favour middle-class pupils [1], and the Flynn effect shows scores rise too fast for genetic change [1]. Using IQ to explain class gaps is therefore circular [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: there is no agreed definition [1]. Gardner's multiple intelligences and cultural variation in what counts as intelligent show tests measure only one chosen version [1]. Gould argued IQ reifies an abstraction [1], so the score cannot be an objective measure of a real quality [1].
Argument 2: the testing situation shapes scores [1]. Steele and Aronson (1995) showed stereotype threat lowered Black American students' scores when a test was framed as measuring ability [1]. Practice and coaching also raise scores [1], so results reflect social conditions as well as ability [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** contested definitions (Spearman, Gardner, Sternberg, Goleman); IQ and its uses; hereditarian views (Jensen, Herrnstein and Murray); heritability; Lewontin; Turkheimer et al.; stereotype threat; Flynn effect.
- **Application (AO2):** selection tests and setting in a named system; rapid reversal of gender gaps; baseline testing for setting.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** correlation versus causation; circularity of explaining attainment with tests that resemble exams; new IQism and the ideology of giftedness; alternative factors from class, ethnicity and gender; individual versus group differences.
- **Judgement:** for example, intelligence helps explain differences between individuals but is a weak explanation of the main group patterns.

**Level guidance:** top answers interrogate the concept of intelligence and weigh it against other explanations to reach a reasoned conclusion; middle answers describe hereditarian and environmental views; low answers assert one view.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Social influences:** cultural bias in items; language of testing; practice and coaching; stereotype threat; the Flynn effect; environment altering heritability; Kamin's critique of Burt.
- **Counter-arguments:** modern standardisation and non-verbal items reduce obvious bias; scores are fairly stable and predict results; correlations between very different tests suggest a general ability.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish bias in individual items from deeper influences on skills tested; consider who designs tests and for what purpose; use evidence from more than one society.
- **Judgement:** for example, tests measure something real but socially shaped, so scores cannot be read as pure innate ability.

**Level guidance:** top answers separate different kinds of social influence and weigh them against the defence of testing; middle answers list sources of bias; low answers give unsupported claims.
