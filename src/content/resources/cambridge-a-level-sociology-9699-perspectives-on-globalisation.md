Section 7.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) is the foundation of the Globalisation section of Paper 4. The syllabus lists three strands: key definitions and issues, including globalisation, glocalisation, global culture and problems with defining globalisation; different dimensions of globalisation, including cultural, political and economic; and perspectives on who benefits from globalisation, including the Marxist, feminist, postmodernist, globalist, sceptic and transformationalist perspectives. It links to the key concepts of Social change and development and Socialisation, culture and identity.

The [globalisation debates guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-debates-and-perspectives/) introduces Giddens, Harvey, McLuhan, Robertson and a summary table of the six perspectives. This page goes further: sharper definitions, tools for analysing each dimension, and the reasoning inside each perspective, so that a 35-mark essay can weigh them rather than list them.

## Defining globalisation more precisely

**Malcolm Waters**, in *Globalization* (1995), offered a definition that is useful because it has three parts: globalisation is a social process in which the constraints of geography on social and cultural arrangements recede, in which people become increasingly aware that they are receding, and in which people act accordingly. The definition includes *awareness* and *action*, not only flows of goods and money.

**David Held and colleagues** (1999) broke globalisation into four measurable features:

| Feature | Question to ask | Example of change |
|---|---|---|
| **Extensity** | How far do networks stretch? | Supply chains spanning several continents |
| **Intensity** | How dense are connections? | Daily volume of international communication |
| **Velocity** | How fast do flows move? | Near-instant financial transfers |
| **Impact** | How much do distant events affect local life? | A crisis in one market affecting jobs elsewhere |

These four features let you answer "is globalisation new?" with nuance: earlier empires had extensity, but today's intensity and velocity are of a different order.

**A further problem of definition: ideology.** Some critics argue that "globalisation" is used rhetorically, presented as an unstoppable force to justify particular policies such as deregulation or cuts to public spending. On this view, the word disguises political choices as economic necessity. Use this to show that defining globalisation is itself contested, not merely difficult.

## Global culture: Appadurai's scapes

Global culture is sometimes described as a single shared consumer culture. **Arjun Appadurai** (1990; developed in *Modernity at Large*, 1996) argued instead that global culture is made of five overlapping and uneven flows, or "scapes":

- **ethnoscapes** — moving people: migrants, tourists, refugees, students;
- **mediascapes** — images and stories circulated by media;
- **technoscapes** — technology crossing borders;
- **financescapes** — flows of capital;
- **ideoscapes** — political ideas such as democracy and rights.

Because these flows move at different speeds and in different directions, global culture is **disjunctive**: it does not produce one uniform culture. This gives you a ready link to glocalisation and to cultural divergence in [globalisation and identity](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-identity/).

## The three dimensions in analysis

The overview defines economic, political and cultural dimensions. For an essay, the key analytical point is that the dimensions move at **different speeds** and benefit **different groups**.

- **Economic** globalisation is the most advanced for capital and goods, but labour moves far less freely than money.
- **Political** globalisation includes international organisations and agreements, yet states still control borders, citizenship and armed forces.
- **Cultural** globalisation spreads products and images quickly, but meanings are reinterpreted locally.

An essay that notes this unevenness can argue that "who benefits" depends on which dimension is being discussed.

## Who benefits? The reasoning inside each perspective

**Globalist.** Globalists treat globalisation as real and transformative. The optimistic, neo-liberal strand argues that open trade and investment spread growth; Jagdish Bhagwati's *In Defense of Globalization* (2004) argued that trade has helped reduce poverty and that critics blame globalisation for problems with other causes. **Benefit:** consumers, workers in growing export sectors, and countries that integrate successfully.

**Sceptic.** Hirst and Thompson argued that the international economy of the late 19th and early 20th centuries was in some respects as open as recent decades, and that trade and investment are concentrated in a **Triad** of Europe, North America and Japan. **Benefit:** the question is overstated; national policies still decide who gains.

**Transformationalist.** Held and colleagues argue globalisation is real but **uneven and open-ended**. States are not disappearing but changing role, sharing power with international bodies. **Benefit:** varies by country, class and region; outcomes depend on how societies respond.

**Marxist.** Leslie Sklair, in *The Transnational Capitalist Class* (2001), identified four fractions that drive and gain from capitalist globalisation: a **corporate** fraction (executives of transnational corporations), a **state** fraction (globalising politicians and officials), a **technical** fraction (globalising professionals) and a **consumerist** fraction (merchants and media). David Harvey's *A Brief History of Neoliberalism* (2005) argued that neo-liberal globalisation restored the power of economic elites. **Benefit:** a transnational capitalist class; costs fall on workers who compete across borders.

**Feminist.** Guy Standing (1989) described the **global feminisation of labour**: as employers sought flexible, low-paid workers, more jobs were filled by women, often on insecure terms. Feminists argue globalisation draws women into paid work but on unequal terms, while unpaid care work continues. **Benefit:** employers and consumers in richer countries; women may gain income and independence, but at a cost.

**Postmodernist.** Zygmunt Bauman, in *Globalization: The Human Consequences* (1998), contrasted **tourists**, who can move freely by choice, with **vagabonds**, who are either stuck in place or forced to move. Globalisation brings freedom for the mobile and insecurity for the immobile. Postmodernists also stress the fragmentation of identity and the decline of grand narratives. **Benefit:** those with the resources to choose mobility and identity.

## Building a 35-mark argument on "who benefits"

A strong essay structure:

1. **Define** globalisation (Waters) and note the problem of definition.
2. **Set up the debate** with the globalist–sceptic–transformationalist typology.
3. **Introduce conflict perspectives**: Marxist (Sklair, Harvey) and feminist (Standing, global care chains).
4. **Add the postmodern lens**: Bauman's tourists and vagabonds show that benefit depends on mobility.
5. **Apply** to at least two contrasting societies or groups.
6. **Judge**: for example, globalisation benefits those able to move capital, skills or themselves across borders; its costs fall on those who cannot.

## Worked paragraph

> The Marxist perspective argues that the main beneficiaries of globalisation form a transnational capitalist class. Sklair identifies corporate, state, technical and consumerist fractions whose interests lie in open markets rather than in any one nation. This explains why firms can relocate production to where labour is cheapest, while workers, who cannot move as freely, compete for jobs. However, the globalist Bhagwati argues that trade has also helped reduce poverty in many developing economies, so the gains are not confined to an elite. A transformationalist would conclude that who benefits depends on how each state manages integration.

The paragraph uses knowledge (Sklair), application (relocation of production), analysis (why mobility matters) and evaluation (Bhagwati and the transformationalist judgement).

## Common mistakes

- Listing all six perspectives in turn without comparing them.
- Treating "globalist" as meaning "pro-globalisation"; some globalists accept globalisation is real but criticise it.
- Using a single country's experience as if it applied everywhere.
- Defining global culture as simply Westernisation, which belongs to the identity debate and needs evaluating.

## Exam technique

Paper 4 essays require you to "explore contrasting views or evidence". Make each perspective respond to the previous one, use concepts precisely, and end with a judgement that answers the question's exact wording.
