All people and figures are invented.

## The definition

**The economic problem:** resources are **finite** and wants are **unlimited**, so **choices** have to be made.

| Word | Meaning to remember |
|---|---|
| Resources | Inputs used in production: land, labour, capital, enterprise. For households: income and time |
| Finite | Limited at a point in time; may grow, but never without limit |
| Wants | Everything people would like, including needs |
| Needs | Essentials for survival: food, water, shelter, clothing |
| Unlimited | Never fully satisfied; new wants replace those met; wants rise with income |
| Choice | Not every want can be met, so something is given up |

**Scarcity** = the result of finite resources and unlimited wants. It is permanent and universal.

**Scarcity versus shortage:**

- Scarcity: permanent; affects every person and country; cannot be removed.
- Shortage: a market situation where people want more than is available at the current price; usually temporary.

## The problem in four contexts (1.1.1)

For each, name the **limited resource**, the **competing wants** and the **choice**.

| Decision maker | Limited resource | Competing wants | Choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Income and time | Many goods and activities | What to buy and what to go without; how to spend time |
| Worker | Hours and effort | Pay versus leisure, rest, study, family | How many hours to work; which job to take |
| Producer | Land, labour, capital, finance | Output of different products; growth | What to produce, how much, with which inputs |
| Government | Tax revenue, borrowing, public land and workers | Health, education, defence, transport | Where to spend; which projects to delay |

**Quick example (consumer):** $40 budget; wants a book ($15), a shirt ($25) and a cinema trip ($12). Total wanted = $52, so $12 more than the budget: one item must go.

**Quick example (producer):** a workshop has 40 machine-hours a week; tables need 3 hours each and chairs 1 hour. Making 10 tables uses 30 hours, leaving only 10 hours for 10 chairs. More tables means fewer chairs.

**Quick example (government):** one budget, two projects. Workers, cement and finance used for a motorway cannot also build a hospital.

## Economic goods and free goods (1.1.2)

| | Economic good | Free good |
|---|---|---|
| Scarce? | Yes | No |
| Uses resources to supply? | Yes | No |
| Opportunity cost? | Yes | No |
| Price? | Usually, but not always | None |
| Examples | Food, cars, bottled water, healthcare | Air in an open place, sunlight, seawater in the ocean |

**The test:** does supplying one more unit use resources that could be used elsewhere? Yes → economic good.

**Zero price does not mean free good:** state healthcare, free school meals, free samples and free apps all use scarce resources. Someone pays: taxpayers, advertisers or the firm.

**Goods can change category:**

- Air: free in the open; economic in a diver's tank or when purifiers are needed in a polluted city.
- River water: effectively free when plentiful; economic when it must be treated, piped or rationed in a drought.
- Sand: free on a dune; economic when dug, bagged and delivered.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic problem | Finite resources and unlimited wants, so choices must be made |
| Scarcity | Limited resources relative to unlimited wants |
| Needs | Goods and services essential for survival |
| Wants | Everything people would like to have |
| Economic good | A good that uses scarce resources and has an opportunity cost |
| Free good | A good that is not scarce and has no opportunity cost |
| Shortage | Demand greater than supply at the current price |

## Exam traps

- Half definitions: always include both finite resources and unlimited wants, then choice.
- "Scarce means rare": no, it means limited relative to wants.
- "Only poor people face scarcity": rich people and countries face it too.
- "Zero price means free good": check whether resources are used.
- Forgetting time as a limited resource for consumers and workers.
- In multiple choice, distractors often mention price, rarity or poverty.

## Self-test

1. Define the economic problem.
2. What does "finite" mean?
3. Give two needs.
4. Why are wants described as unlimited?
5. Distinguish scarcity from a shortage.
6. State the two main limited resources of a consumer.
7. Explain the economic problem facing a worker.
8. Give an example of the economic problem for a government.
9. Define a free good.
10. Is healthcare given at no charge to patients a free good? Explain.
11. Give one example of a good that is free in one situation but economic in another.
12. A family has $200 and wants a fridge ($150), a bicycle ($90) and a school trip ($60). Calculate how much more they would need to have all three.

### Answers

1. Resources are finite and wants are unlimited, so choices must be made.
2. Limited in quantity at a point in time.
3. Any two: food, water, shelter, clothing.
4. When one want is satisfied another appears, and wants grow as incomes rise.
5. Scarcity is permanent and universal; a shortage is a temporary market situation in which demand exceeds supply at the current price.
6. Income and time.
7. A worker has limited hours and effort, but wants both income and leisure, so must choose how many hours to work and which job to take.
8. A government with limited tax revenue must choose between building a hospital and a motorway.
9. A good that is not scarce, so it uses no resources and has no opportunity cost.
10. No. Doctors, nurses and medicines are scarce resources with alternative uses; the cost is paid through taxes.
11. Air: free in an open field, economic in a diver's tank (or water, or sand).
12. 150 + 90 + 60 = 300; 300 − 200 = **$100** more.
