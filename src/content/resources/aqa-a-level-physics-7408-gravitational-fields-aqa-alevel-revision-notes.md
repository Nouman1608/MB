## Equations

| Equation | Meaning | Units |
|---|---|---|
| F = Gm₁m₂/r² | force between point masses | N |
| g = F/m | field strength definition | N kg⁻¹ |
| g = GM/r² | radial field | N kg⁻¹ |
| V = −GM/r | radial potential | J kg⁻¹ |
| ΔW = mΔV | work moving mass through ΔV | J |
| g = −ΔV/Δr | field from potential gradient | N kg⁻¹ |
| v = √(GM/r) | circular orbital speed | m s⁻¹ |
| T² = (4π²/GM)r³ | orbital period | s² |
| E_total = −GMm/2r | total energy in orbit | J |
| v_esc = √(2GM/R) | escape velocity | m s⁻¹ |

## 3.7.2.1–3.7.2.2 Force and field strength

- Gravity: universal, always **attractive**, between all matter.
- Spheres act as point masses at their centres: r is **centre to centre**.
- g = force per unit mass; a vector pointing towards the mass.
- Outside a sphere g ∝ 1/r². At r = 2R, g is a quarter; g halves at r = √2 R.
- Field lines: radial and inwards for a point mass; parallel and equally spaced (uniform) near the surface over small regions.

## 3.7.2.3 Potential

- **Definition**: work done per unit mass in bringing a small mass from infinity to the point.
- V = 0 at infinity, negative everywhere else.
- **Significance of minus sign**: gravity attracts, so work must be done on a mass to move it to infinity; the mass is bound.
- **Potential difference**: ΔV between two points; ΔW = mΔV.
- **Equipotentials**: perpendicular to field lines; no work done along them; spread out further from a mass.
- Potential is a **scalar**: add contributions directly.

**Graphs (r ≥ R)**

| Graph | Shape | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| g against r | falls as 1/r² from surface value | area = ΔV |
| V against r | negative, rises towards 0 as −1/r | gradient = −g |

- mgh is valid only when h ≪ R; otherwise use mΔV.

## 3.7.2.4 Orbits

- Gravity supplies centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
- Satellite mass cancels: v and T depend only on M and r.
- **T² ∝ r³**: log T against log r has gradient 1.5.
- Higher orbit → **slower**, longer period, **more** total energy.
- E_k = GMm/2r; E_p = −GMm/r; E_total = −GMm/2r = −E_k.
- Moving to a higher orbit: ΔE_total = (GMm/2)(1/r₁ − 1/r₂).
- **Escape velocity**: ½mv² = GMm/R; independent of m; equals √2 × orbital speed at that radius.
- **Synchronous orbit**: period = rotation period of the body orbited.
- **Geostationary**: synchronous, equatorial plane, west to east, radius about 4.2 × 10⁷ m; fixed above one point; communications.
- **Low orbit**: short period; close-up observation and imaging; many satellites needed for continuous coverage.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gravitational field strength | Force per unit mass |
| Gravitational potential | Work done per unit mass bringing a mass from infinity |
| Gravitational potential difference | Work done per unit mass moving between two points |
| Equipotential surface | Surface of constant potential |
| Escape velocity | Minimum surface speed to reach infinity without propulsion |
| Synchronous orbit | Orbit with period equal to the body's rotation period |
| Geostationary orbit | Synchronous equatorial orbit, west to east |

## Exam traps

- r must include the planet's radius when a height is given.
- Squaring r is easy to forget in g = GM/r²; it is not squared in V = −GM/r.
- Kinetic energy falls while total energy rises when a satellite moves out.
- Escape velocity uses R (the surface), not the orbital radius, unless escaping from orbit.
- Hours to seconds before using T² = 4π²r³/GM.
- A geostationary satellite must be over the equator; "same period as Earth" alone is synchronous.

## Self-test

**1.** Define gravitational potential.
**2.** Explain the minus sign in V = −GM/r.
**3.** g at the surface of a planet is 8.0 N kg⁻¹. What is g at a distance of 2R from the centre?
**4.** What does the area under a g–r graph represent?
**5.** Derive the relationship between T and r for a circular orbit (outline).
**6.** What is the gradient of a graph of log T against log r?
**7.** Why does a satellite's mass not affect its orbital period?
**8.** Write an expression for the total energy of a satellite in orbit.
**9.** What happens to the speed of a satellite moved to a higher orbit?
**10.** Escape velocity from a body is 3.0 km s⁻¹. What is the orbital speed just above its surface?
**11.** State three features of a geostationary orbit.
**12.** Give one advantage of a low orbit.

## Self-test answers

**1.** The work done per unit mass in bringing a small mass from infinity to that point.
**2.** V is zero at infinity and work must be done to move a mass out to infinity against the attractive force, so V is negative.
**3.** 8.0/4 = 2.0 N kg⁻¹.
**4.** The gravitational potential difference between the two radii.
**5.** GMm/r² = mv²/r with v = 2πr/T gives T² = (4π²/GM)r³.
**6.** 1.5.
**7.** It cancels from both sides of GMm/r² = mv²/r.
**8.** E_total = −GMm/2r.
**9.** It decreases.
**10.** 3.0/√2 = 2.1 km s⁻¹.
**11.** Period 24 hours (Earth's rotation period); in the equatorial plane; moving west to east (also radius about 4.2 × 10⁷ m).
**12.** Closer to the surface, so better for detailed imaging (or shorter period, so it scans more of the surface).
