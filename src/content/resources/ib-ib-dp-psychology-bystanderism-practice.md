## Section A

**1.** Define "pluralistic ignorance". **[2]**

**2.** State the five stages of Latané and Darley's decision model in order. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the stage of the decision model at which diffusion of responsibility operates. **[1]**

**4.** Outline the arousal: cost-reward model. **[3]**

**5.** State one finding of Latané and Rodin (1969). **[1]**

**6.** Give one ethical concern raised by field studies of bystanderism. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A woman collapses in a busy shopping centre. Several shoppers glance at her and walk on. Using the decision model, explain two stages at which the shoppers' helping might have failed. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the findings of Latané and Darley's (1968) smoke-filled room study support the idea of pluralistic ignorance. **[3]**

**9.** Invented field study: a researcher drops a bag of oranges in a station when 1, 3 or 6 other people are nearby. She records whether *any* bystander helps, and whether a *particular* target bystander, chosen in advance, helps.

| People nearby | Trials | Any bystander helped | Target bystander helped |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 | 22 | 22 |
| 3 | 40 | 28 | 12 |
| 6 | 40 | 32 | 7 |

(a) Calculate the percentage of trials in which any bystander helped, and the percentage in which the target bystander helped, for 1 and 6 people nearby.
(b) Explain how these results fit both the classic bystander effect and the findings of Philpot et al. (2020). **[6]**

**10.** Outline the two studies of Levine et al. (2005) and explain what they show about the role of social identity in helping. **[5]**

**11.** Explain one reason why Fischer et al. (2011) found a weaker bystander effect in dangerous emergencies. **[2]**

**12.** Explain one strength and one limitation of using CCTV recordings to study bystanderism. **[4]**

**13.** Discuss factors influencing bystanderism. **[22]**

## Answers

**1.** In an ambiguous situation, each bystander looks to others to decide whether there is an emergency **[1]**; because everyone appears calm, each concludes that there is no emergency, although each may privately be concerned **[1]**.

**2.** Notice the event; interpret it as an emergency; take responsibility **[1]**; decide how to help; act **[1]**.

**3.** Stage 3, taking responsibility **[1]**.

**4.** Seeing someone in distress causes unpleasant arousal that the bystander is motivated to reduce **[1]**. The bystander weighs the costs of helping, such as effort or danger, against the costs of not helping, such as guilt or blame **[1]**. Helping is most likely when costs of helping are low and costs of not helping are high **[1]**.

**5.** For example: participants who were alone helped most often; or pairs of friends helped more often than pairs of strangers **[1]**.

**6.** For example, participants cannot give informed consent and may be distressed by the staged emergency **[1]**.

**7.** Two stages, two marks each:
- **Interpretation:** seeing others walk past calmly, each shopper may conclude she is not seriously ill, for example that she is resting; this is pluralistic ignorance **[2]**.
- **Responsibility:** with many people present, each shopper may assume that someone else, such as staff, will deal with it, so responsibility is diffused **[2]**.
- Also creditable for two marks: noticing, because a busy setting distracts attention; or acting, because of fear of embarrassment.

**8.** Participants who were alone usually reported the smoke **[1]**, while those with passive confederates rarely did **[1]**. Non-reporters explained the smoke away as harmless, showing that others' calm led them to interpret the situation as not an emergency **[1]**.

**9.** (a) Any bystander: 1 person, 22 / 40 = 55% **[1]**; 6 people, 32 / 40 = 80% **[1]**. Target bystander: 1 person, 22 / 40 = 55% **[1]**; 6 people, 7 / 40 = 17.5% **[1]**.
(b) The target bystander helped far less often as group size rose, which is the classic bystander effect for an individual **[1]**, while the chance that someone helped rose with group size, matching Philpot et al.'s finding that more bystanders made intervention by at least one person more likely **[1]**.

**10.** In the first study, Manchester United fans whose club identity had been made salient helped a fallen jogger more often when he wore a Manchester United shirt than a Liverpool or plain shirt **[2]**. In the second, when their identity as football fans in general was salient, they helped the jogger in either club's shirt more than in a plain shirt **[2]**. Helping depends on seeing the victim as an in-group member, and the in-group boundary changes with the identity that is salient **[1]**.

**11.** Dangerous emergencies are recognised as emergencies more quickly and clearly, so pluralistic ignorance is less likely **[1]**; they also produce higher arousal, and other bystanders can provide physical support against a perpetrator **[1]**.

**12.** Strength: recordings show real behaviour in real emergencies, so ecological validity is high, and coding can be checked by several observers **[2]**. Limitation: researchers cannot control or know what bystanders were thinking, and some incidents may be missed or only partly visible on camera **[2]**.

**13.** Marked with the Paper 2 essay criteria:
- **A Focus:** defines bystanderism and identifies several factors **[2]**.
- **B Knowledge and understanding:** decision model; diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance, audience inhibition; cost-reward; social identity **[6]**.
- **C Use of research:** two or three of Latané and Darley (1968), Latané and Rodin (1969), Levine et al. (2005), Fischer et al. (2011) and Philpot et al. (2020) **[6]**.
- **D Critical thinking:** laboratory versus field realism; deception and consent; all-male samples; the individual versus group distinction; combining explanations; a conclusion **[6]**.
- **E Clarity and organisation:** a clear structure organised by factor **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** naming stages without applying them to the shopping centre.
- **Question 9(b):** describing the numbers without the individual versus group distinction.
- **Question 10:** describing only the first study.
- **Question 13:** organising the essay study by study instead of factor by factor.
