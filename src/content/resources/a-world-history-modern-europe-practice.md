## Paper 1 style: Historical Sources (one two-part question, 40 marks)

Candidates answer **both parts** of the single question set on whichever topic
is prescribed for Paper 1 in their series.

**Illustrative topic: Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924**

Study the sources, then answer both parts.

**Source A** — from a Petrograd factory delegate's account, written shortly after February 1917: "By the fourth day the queues for bread had become processions. When the Volynsky regiment was ordered to fire on us, the soldiers grounded their rifles and came over to our side. After that, nothing the government said could stop what had already happened in the streets."

**Source B** — from a government minister's report to the Council of Ministers, February 1917: "The disturbances are temporary and concentrated among the least disciplined elements of the capital's workforce. Reinforcements are being brought up, and I am confident that with firm handling the situation will be under control within days."

**(a)** Compare and contrast what Sources A and B suggest about the state of order in Petrograd in February 1917. **[10]**

**(b)** 'The sources show that the February Revolution succeeded primarily because the army refused to suppress it.' How far do Sources A and B support this view? Use the sources and your own knowledge. **[30]**

### Worked answer

**(a)** Both sources agree that unrest was under way in Petrograd, but they disagree sharply about its significance and trajectory. Source A, from a participant, presents the unrest as escalating and irreversible — queues becoming "processions" and, decisively, soldiers refusing to fire and joining the crowd, which the source treats as the point after which the outcome was no longer in the government's control. Source B, from a minister writing for his own superiors, minimises the unrest as "temporary" and confined to "the least disciplined elements," and expresses confidence that reinforcements and "firm handling" would restore order within days. The two sources' disagreement is partly about scale and partly about interpretation: Source B may reflect genuine ignorance of the army's loyalty at the time of writing, official complacency, or a minister's incentive to reassure superiors rather than alarm them — all of which a strong answer should weigh using provenance, not simply repeat as a factual disagreement. **[10]**

**(b)** A strong answer treats the claim as one plausible but incomplete explanation rather than either accepting or dismissing it outright. Source A directly supports the claim: it identifies the Volynsky regiment's refusal to fire as the specific moment after which the government lost control, which fits with the wider historical evidence that the loss of military loyalty was what turned a wave of unrest into a successful revolution, since previous instances of urban unrest under the Tsarist regime (including 1905) had been contained precisely because troops remained willing to fire on crowds. Source B does not directly address the army's loyalty, but its confident assumption that "reinforcements" and "firm handling" would work is itself evidence of how unexpected and decisive the army's refusal turned out to be — the minister's confidence assumed a loyal garrison, and its failure is exactly what Source A describes. Own knowledge should qualify the claim, however: economic collapse (bread and fuel shortages), the discrediting of the autocracy through Rasputin's influence over the Tsarina while Nicholas was at the front, and years of accumulated war-weariness all created the conditions that made the army's refusal politically decisive rather than an isolated event — a garrison that stayed loyal in 1905 refused in 1917 partly because of these deeper, longer-building pressures. A well-supported judgement should conclude that the army's refusal was the *immediate* trigger that converted unrest into regime collapse, but was itself the product of the economic and political collapse the sources only partially capture. **[30]** (Marks for accurate use of both sources with attention to provenance, own knowledge that both supports and qualifies the claim, and a sustained judgement.)

## Paper 2 style: Outline Study (two two-part questions, 60 marks)

### Topic: France, 1774–1814

**(a)** Explain **three** long-term causes of the French Revolution. **[10]**

**(b)** Assess the extent to which Napoleon consolidated rather than betrayed the Revolution. **[20]**

#### Worked answer

**(a)** Any three: the **fiscal crisis** caused by war debts, particularly from supporting the American Revolution, and a tax system that exempted the privileged [1–2 for explanation, not just identification]; the **inequality of the estate system**, in which the Third Estate paid the taxes but had no proportionate political voice [1–2]; **Enlightenment ideas** of natural rights, popular sovereignty and criticism of absolutism, which gave the grievances of the Third Estate an intellectual and political language [1–2]; the **weakness of Louis XVI's leadership** and his failure to push through reform against noble resistance at the Assembly of Notables [1–2]. Marks reward explanation of *why* each factor contributed to revolution, not just naming it. **[10]**

**(b)** **Consolidated:** the **Code Napoléon** entrenched equality before the law, secure property rights and the abolition of feudal privilege, and outlasted him across Europe. **Careers open to talent** — promotion by merit in the army and administration — was a genuine revolutionary principle he sustained. He **preserved the land settlement**, confirming peasants in possession of church and émigré land, which is why the settlement endured. The **Concordat** ended the religious civil war on terms favourable to the state.
**Betrayed:** he **destroyed political liberty** — plebiscites were manipulated, the press censored, and he crowned himself Emperor in 1804, restoring hereditary monarchy in all but name. He **created a new imperial nobility**, reintroducing the very hierarchy of titles the Revolution had abolished. He **reintroduced slavery in the colonies in 1802**, reversing the abolition of 1794 — the clearest betrayal of the Declaration of the Rights of Man. The Code **reduced the legal status of women**, subordinating wives to husbands.
**Judgement:** he consolidated the Revolution's social and legal gains while destroying its political ones. The bourgeoisie, who gained security of property and career, had reason to regard him as its heir; republicans and the enslaved people of France's colonies had reason to see him as its gravedigger. Which verdict one reaches depends on which of the Revolution's several aims is treated as its essence. **[20]** (Marks distributed across accurate knowledge of both "consolidated" and "betrayed" evidence, and a sustained, evidenced judgement — a list of points with no final judgement will not access the top band.)

### Topic: German unification, 1815–71

**(a)** Explain **three** obstacles to German unification that existed before 1862. **[10]**

**(b)** 'German unification was achieved primarily through Bismarck's diplomacy rather than through Prussian military strength.' How far do you agree? **[20]**

#### Worked answer

**(a)** Any three: **Austrian rivalry** — Austria, as president of the German Confederation, had every interest in blocking Prussian-led unification and preserving its own dominant influence over the German states; **particularism** — the individual German states, especially the larger ones like Bavaria and Saxony, valued their own sovereignty and were reluctant to be absorbed into a Prussian-dominated union; **religious and political division** — the largely Protestant north and largely Catholic south did not share a single obvious political centre, and liberal and conservative visions of what a unified Germany should look like (a constitutional, popularly-legitimated union versus a conservative, monarchical one) pulled in different directions, as the failure of the 1848 Frankfurt Parliament had shown. **[10]**

**(b)** A strong answer rejects the false choice the question poses and instead explains how Bismarck's diplomacy and Prussian military strength worked together. Bismarck's diplomacy created the political conditions for war on favourable terms — isolating Austria diplomatically before 1866 (securing Italian support and Russian and French neutrality), and provoking France into declaring war in 1870 in a way that left France diplomatically isolated and Germany appearing to be the aggrieved party, which helped rally the southern German states to Prussia's side. But diplomacy alone could not have delivered unification without Prussia's military capacity to win the resulting wars decisively and quickly — the Prussian army's success against Austria in 1866 (Sadowa) and France in 1870–71 (Sedan), built on Prussian military reforms, rail-based mobilisation and modern weaponry, converted Bismarck's diplomatic groundwork into irreversible political outcomes. A well-supported judgement should conclude that diplomacy determined *when* and *against whom* Prussia fought and *how isolated* its opponents were, while military strength determined that Prussia *won* — the two were interdependent rather than alternative explanations, and treating either alone as sufficient understates the other. **[20]** (Marks for accurate knowledge of both diplomatic and military factors, explicit engagement with their interaction, and a sustained judgement.)

### Topic: Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924

**(a)** Explain **three** reasons why Stolypin's land reforms, launched from 1906, failed to secure long-term support for the Tsarist regime. **[10]**

**(b)** Explain why the Bolsheviks were able to seize power in October 1917. **[20]**

#### Worked answer

**(a)** Any three: the reforms were **too slow to change most peasants' circumstances** before the regime collapsed in 1917, so they had not yet produced the stable, land-owning peasantry Stolypin hoped would support the autocracy; they **created new divisions within the peasantry**, as wealthier peasants (later associated with the term *kulak*) who left the communes for consolidated private holdings became resented by poorer peasants who remained under communal tenure, rather than uniting the peasantry behind the regime; and **Stolypin's assassination in 1911** removed the reform's chief political sponsor before it could be carried through consistently, after which support for continuing the reforms at the same pace weakened within the government itself. **[10]**

**(b)** The **Provisional Government made two fatal errors**: it **continued the war**, so the economic and military grievances that had caused February 1917 persisted, and it **postponed land reform** until a Constituent Assembly, while peasants were already seizing land on their own initiative. **Dual power** meant the Provisional Government could not act without the cooperation of the Petrograd Soviet, so it was structurally weak from the outset. Lenin's **April Theses gave the Bolsheviks a simple, uncompromising programme — "Peace, Bread, Land" and "All Power to the Soviets"** — which no other major party matched. The **Kornilov affair** in August was decisive: Kerensky armed the Bolsheviks to defend Petrograd against a perceived military coup, which restored their credibility after the failed July Days and left them armed and organised. Trotsky's chairmanship of the Petrograd Soviet and the **Military Revolutionary Committee** then gave the Bolsheviks the organised machinery to take power in Petrograd almost bloodlessly in October. **[20]** (Marks for a range of accurately explained factors and clear explanation of how each contributed to the outcome, rather than a narrative account alone.)

## Where marks are usually lost

- Narrating events chronologically on the (b) parts rather than sustaining a judgement against the specific question set.
- On source questions, repeating what a source says without weighing its provenance (who wrote it, when, and why).
- Treating Napoleon, or Bismarck's diplomacy and Prussian military strength, as simple either/or questions rather than explaining how the two sides of the argument interact.
- Assuming all three topics could appear on either paper in the same series — check the rotation table, since exactly one topic is fixed for Paper 1 and the other two for Paper 2.
