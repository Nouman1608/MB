## Section A

**1.** State **three** long-term causes of the French Revolution. **[3]**

**2.** Name the three estates of the Ancien Régime and state one grievance of the Third Estate. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain the significance of the following in the French Revolution: the Estates-General of 1789, the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the Terror. **[9]**

**4.** Assess the extent to which Napoleon consolidated rather than betrayed the Revolution. **[12]**

**5.** Explain **three** causes of the February Revolution of 1917 in Russia. **[9]**

**6.** Explain why the Bolsheviks were able to seize power in October 1917. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three: the **fiscal crisis** caused by war debts, particularly from supporting the American Revolution, and a tax system that exempted the privileged [1]; the **inequality of the estate system**, in which the Third Estate paid the taxes but had no political voice [1]; **Enlightenment ideas** of natural rights, popular sovereignty and criticism of absolutism [1]; the **weakness of Louis XVI's leadership** and his failure to push through reform against noble resistance [1].

**2.** **First Estate** — the clergy [1]. **Second Estate** — the nobility [1]. **Third Estate** — everyone else, from wealthy bourgeois to peasants [1]. Grievance: they **paid nearly all the taxation while enjoying no privileges**, and were consistently outvoted two to one by estate [1].

**3. The Estates-General (1789)** — the first meeting since 1614 [1]; the **dispute over voting by estate rather than by head** led the Third Estate to declare itself the National Assembly [1], which transferred **sovereignty from the king to the nation** — the decisive constitutional break [1].
**The Declaration of the Rights of Man** — proclaimed **liberty, equality before the law, and popular sovereignty** [1]; it **abolished privilege in principle** and became the foundational text of the Revolution [1], though its silence on women and slaves showed the limits of its universalism [1].
**The Terror** — the Committee of Public Safety used **mass execution and emergency powers against internal and external enemies** [1]; it arguably **saved the Republic militarily** [1] but **discredited revolutionary idealism**, and its collapse in Thermidor opened the way to conservative and ultimately military rule [1].

**4. Consolidated:** the **Code Napoléon entrenched equality before the law, secure property rights and the abolition of feudal privilege**, and outlasted him across Europe [1] [1]. **Careers open to talent** — promotion by merit in the army and administration — was a genuine revolutionary principle he sustained [1]. He **preserved the land settlement**, confirming peasants in possession of church and émigré land, which is why the settlement endured [1]. The **Concordat** ended the religious civil war on terms favourable to the state [1].
**Betrayed:** he **destroyed political liberty** — plebiscites were manipulated, the press censored, and he crowned himself Emperor in 1804, restoring hereditary monarchy in all but name [1] [1]. He **created a new imperial nobility**, reintroducing the very hierarchy of titles the Revolution had abolished [1]. He **reintroduced slavery in the colonies in 1802**, reversing the abolition of 1794 — the clearest betrayal of the Declaration [1]. The Code **reduced the legal status of women**, subordinating wives to husbands [1].
**Judgement:** he consolidated the Revolution's **social and legal gains while destroying its political ones** [1] [1]. The bourgeoisie, who gained security of property and career, had reason to regard him as its heir; republicans and slaves had reason to see him as its gravedigger [1]. Which verdict one reaches depends on **which of the Revolution's several aims is treated as its essence** [1].

**5.** Any three, 3 marks each: **The war** — catastrophic casualties, poor supply and the Tsar's decision to take personal command in 1915 meant **defeats were now blamed directly on him** [1] [1] [1]. **Economic collapse** — inflation, the diversion of transport to the front and **acute bread and fuel shortages in Petrograd** in a bitter winter brought crowds onto the streets [1] [1] [1]. **The discredited autocracy** — Nicholas's absence left government to the Tsarina and Rasputin, whose influence produced a **collapse of confidence even among the nobility and the Duma** [1] [1] [1]. **The loss of the army's loyalty** — when the Petrograd garrison **refused to fire on demonstrators and joined them**, the regime lost its last instrument of coercion [1] [1] [1].

**6.** The **Provisional Government made two fatal errors**: it **continued the war**, so the grievances of February persisted [1] [1], and it **postponed land reform** until a Constituent Assembly, while peasants were already seizing land [1]. **Dual power** meant it could not act without the Petrograd Soviet, so it was weak from the outset [1]. Lenin's **April Theses gave the Bolsheviks a simple, uncompromising programme — "Peace, Bread, Land" and "All Power to the Soviets"** — which no other party matched [1] [1]. The **Kornilov affair** in August was decisive: Kerensky armed the Bolsheviks to defend Petrograd, which **restored their credibility after the July Days and left them armed and organised** [1] [1]. Trotsky's chairmanship of the Petrograd Soviet and the **Military Revolutionary Committee** gave them the machinery to take power almost bloodlessly in October [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Narrating the Revolution chronologically rather than assessing significance.
- Treating Napoleon as simply a dictator or simply an heir, without weighing both.
- Explaining February 1917 solely through the war.
- Underestimating the Kornilov affair in the Bolshevik seizure of power.
