Research methods are how sociologists collect and judge evidence about society, and in Cambridge
IGCSE Sociology (0495) they form the compulsory Question 1 of Paper 1. The syllabus organises the
topic as three key questions: how sociologists approach the study of society (1.1), what types of
data and evidence they use (1.2), and how they investigate society through research design,
sampling and methods (1.3). Question 1(e) asks you to plan your own research on a given topic, so
you must be able to justify choices, not just define terms.

For the Paper 1 overview, see the
[research methods, identity and inequality guide](/resources/igcse-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/).

## How do positivists and interpretivists study society? (1.1)

| | Positivist approach (1.1.1) | Interpretivist approach (1.1.2) |
|---|---|---|
| View of society | **Macro** — society as a structure of **social facts** that shape individuals | **Micro** — society built from people's interactions and meanings |
| Aim | Find **patterns and trends**, **causation and correlation** | Understand **meanings and motivations** in depth |
| Model | The **scientific method**: hypothesis, measurement, testing | Empathetic understanding — **verstehen** (Max Weber) |
| Data | **Quantitative** | **Qualitative** |
| Strength valued | **Objectivity and reliability** | **Validity** |
| Weakness | Misses meaning; imposes researcher's categories | **Subjectivity**; hard to repeat or generalise |

Émile Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) is the classic positivist study: he compared official suicide
rates across countries and religious groups and argued that levels of social integration and
regulation — social facts — explained the differences. Interpretivists reply that suicide
statistics depend on how officials interpret deaths.

**Correlation** means two things vary together; **causation** means one produces the other. A
correlation between poverty and low exam results does not on its own prove poverty causes them.

## Can researchers combine approaches? (1.1.3)

- **Triangulation** — using more than one method or source to cross-check findings, for example a
  questionnaire followed by interviews with some respondents.
- **Longitudinal studies** — studying the same group repeatedly over time to see change; the British
  birth cohort studies, which have followed people born in a single week (in 1958 and 1970, for
  example) through their lives, are well-known examples. Strength: shows change and sequence.
  Limitations: expensive and people drop out (**attrition**).

## How do you evaluate a research choice? (1.1.4)

| Concept | Meaning | Example problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Bias** | Distortion from the researcher's values or the design | Leading questions |
| **Interviewer effect** | Answers change because of who the interviewer is | Teenagers telling an adult interviewer what they think is acceptable |
| **Hawthorne / observer effect** | People change behaviour because they know they are studied | Named after the Western Electric Hawthorne works studies (1920s–1930s) |
| **Validity** | A true picture of what is being studied | Closed questions miss real feelings |
| **Reliability** | The same results if repeated | Unstructured interviews differ each time |
| **Representativeness** | The sample is typical of the population | Online survey excludes people without internet |
| **Generalisability** | Findings can be applied to the wider population | Case study of one school |

## What is the difference between primary and secondary data? (1.2.1)

**Primary data** is collected by the researcher for the study (questionnaires, interviews,
observation). It fits the research aims but costs time and money. **Secondary data** already exists
(statistics, documents, media). It is cheap and may cover large populations or the past, but was
produced for other purposes. **Quantitative data** is numerical and easy to compare; **qualitative
data** is descriptive and rich in meaning but harder to analyse.

## How useful is secondary evidence? (1.2.2)

- **Official statistics** — government data on population, crime, employment, education. Strengths:
  large scale, regular, free. Limitations: definitions may change; some events go unrecorded.
- **Historical and personal documents** — diaries, letters, autobiographies, archives. Strengths:
  insight into the past and personal meaning. Limitations: authenticity, representativeness.
- **Digital sources** — websites, social media, online forums. Strengths: huge, current, often
  unprompted. Limitations: hard to verify; online users are not typical of all people; consent
  issues.
- **Media content** — news reports, films, adverts. Strengths: shows how issues are represented.
  Limitations: reflects the producers' agenda.

## How do you read tables, charts and graphs? (1.2.3)

In Question 1(a), identify the exact figure or trend asked for, quote it with units, and then
explain one problem with the evidence — for example a small sample, an old date, an unclear
definition or missing groups.

## What are the stages in research design? (1.3.1)

1. **Selection of topic** — influenced by interest, funding, theory.
2. **Aims** — what the research wants to find out.
3. **Hypothesis** — a testable prediction (mainly positivist).
4. **Target population** — the group the research is about.
5. **Methods** — chosen to fit aims and approach.
6. **Operationalisation** — turning concepts into measurable indicators (for example measuring
   "social class" by occupation).
7. **Pilot study** — a small trial to test questions and procedures.

## How do sociologists choose a sample? (1.3.2)

A **sampling frame** is the list of the target population (school register, electoral roll).

| Technique | How it works | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Random** | Everyone has an equal chance | Avoids researcher bias | May not include small groups |
| **Systematic** | Every *n*th name on the list | Simple | Hidden patterns in the list |
| **Stratified** | Divide by key characteristics, then sample in proportion | Representative | Needs detailed sampling frame |
| **Snowball** | Participants recruit others | Reaches hidden groups | Unrepresentative |
| **Quota** | Interviewer fills set numbers of each type | Quick | Interviewer chooses who, so bias |

## Which primary methods can be used? (1.3.3 and 1.3.4)

- **Surveys and questionnaires** — reach many people; types of question: **open**, **closed**,
  **multiple choice**, **scaled** (for example "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree").
- **Interviews** — **structured** (fixed questions), **semi-structured**, **unstructured**
  (conversational), **group**. Sudhir Venkatesh's *Gang Leader for a Day* (2008) shows the depth
  unstructured conversation and observation can bring.
- **Observations** — **participant** or **non-participant**, **overt** or **covert**.

## What issues affect research? (1.3.5 and 1.3.6)

**Theoretical and practical:** researcher perspective (positivist or interpretivist), access to the
sample, response rate, funding and cost, time.

**Ethical:** informed consent, privacy and confidentiality, deception, prevention of harm, legality.
Covert observation raises deception and consent issues; researching young people needs parental
consent.

## Frequently asked questions

**How should I plan a research answer for Question 1(e)?** The syllabus says to explain the primary
methods, sampling and secondary evidence you would use and give reasons; you are not expected to
evaluate your chosen methods.

**Is positivism better than interpretivism?** Neither is better in general; each suits different
research aims. Many studies combine them through triangulation.

**What is the most common mistake on sampling?** Confusing the sampling frame (the list) with the
sampling technique (the way people are picked from it).
