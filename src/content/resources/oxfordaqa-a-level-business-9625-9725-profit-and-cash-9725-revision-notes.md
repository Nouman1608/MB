These notes condense section 3.2.3.3 Profit and cash of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1, which is International AS content examined in Unit 2. It has three headings in the specification: **Profit**, **Budgeting** and **Cash flow forecasts**. The [profit and cash study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-profit-and-cash-9725/) teaches each one with longer worked examples; the [profit and cash practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-profit-and-cash-9725-practice/) test them. Every business named is invented.

## Formulas

| What | Formula |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating profit | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Net profit | Operating profit − interest − taxes |
| Any profit margin (%) | That profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Variance | Actual − budget, labelled F or A |
| Net cash flow | Total inflows − total outflows |
| Closing balance | Opening balance + net cash flow |
| Opening balance | Previous month's closing balance |
| Receivables days | Trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 |
| Payables days | Trade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Profit | Revenue minus costs for a period; rewards owners and funds investment |
| Profitability | Profit relative to revenue, shown as a margin |
| Cost of sales | Direct cost of the goods sold (materials, stock bought for resale) |
| Operating expenses | Overheads: rent, administration, marketing, salaries |
| Budget | A financial plan for a future period |
| Favourable variance | A difference from budget that raises profit |
| Adverse variance | A difference from budget that lowers profit |
| Cash flow forecast | A prediction of monthly cash inflows, outflows and balances |
| Receivables | Money customers owe the business |
| Payables | Money the business owes suppliers |
| Debt factoring | Selling unpaid invoices to a factor for immediate cash, minus a fee |

## Profit versus cash: the five gaps

1. Credit sales: profit now, cash later.
2. Credit purchases: cost now, cash out later.
3. Long-life assets: cash out now, cost spread over years.
4. Loans and share capital: cash in, but not revenue; loan repayments are cash out but not a cost.
5. Inventory bought but not yet sold: cash out before any profit.

**Learn this sentence:** a business can be profitable and still fail because it cannot pay its bills when they are due.

**Mini example: Larkhill Signs.** Quarter profit £12,000. Customers still owe £15,000 of the quarter's sales; the owner bought a £6,000 printer for cash. Cash is roughly 12,000 − 15,000 − 6,000 = £9,000 worse than profit suggests, so Larkhill may need an overdraft despite its profit. (This simple version assumes all of Larkhill's costs were paid in cash.)

## Reading margins

- Gross margin falls: prices cut, or direct costs (suppliers, materials) up.
- Operating margin falls but gross margin steady: overheads rising faster than revenue.
- Net margin falls but operating margin steady: more interest (more borrowing) or more tax.
- Rising profit with falling margin: the business is growing but earning less per pound of sales.

## Improving profit and profitability

| Method | Advantage | Challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Raise price | More profit per sale | Sales may fall |
| Cheaper suppliers | Higher gross margin | Quality or reliability may fall |
| Cut overheads | Higher operating margin | Cuts to marketing or staff can cut sales |
| Sell more | Fixed costs spread further | Promotion costs money; capacity limits |

## Budgets and variances

- Revenue above budget: **F**. Revenue below: **A**.
- Cost above budget: **A**. Cost below: **F**.
- Profit variance = net effect of all the others.
- **Amend** the budget when an assumption changes (price, volume, a cost), then compare actual with the amended figures.

**Mini example: Tamsin's Café, one month (£).** Budget revenue 22,000, actual 23,100: 1,100 F. Budget food costs 7,700, actual 8,250: 550 A. Wages on budget. Profit variance = 1,100 − 550 = 550 F.

**Value:** targets, control, co-ordination, early warning. **Limits:** only as good as the forecast; rigid budgets can block good opportunities; staff may pad budgets.

## Cash flow forecasts

- Work across one month at a time: inflows, outflows, net cash flow, opening, closing.
- Carry each closing balance forward.
- When amending, change the figure, then recalculate **every** later balance.
- Moving a payment or receipt changes the timing, not the total.
- Brackets mean a negative figure.
- **Value:** shows when an overdraft is needed, supports loan applications, helps plan timing of spending.

## Timing: payables and receivables days

If receivables days are higher than payables days, the business pays out before it is paid and must finance the gap. Example: receivables 45 days, payables 30 days gives a 15-day gap.

## Ways to improve cash flow

| Method | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Debt factoring | Cash immediately | Fee cuts profit |
| Shorter payment terms for customers | Cash sooner | Customers may go elsewhere |
| Early payment incentives | Faster payment | Discount cuts revenue |
| Credit checks | Fewer bad debts | Some sales refused |
| Longer payment time to suppliers | Cash stays longer | Supplier goodwill at risk |
| Reducing outgoings | Lower outflows | May harm quality or capacity |
| Better invoice management | Invoices sent and chased promptly | Staff time or software cost |

## Exam traps

- A loan is cash in, not revenue, and does not raise profit.
- Net margin uses net profit, not operating profit.
- A cost above budget is adverse even though the variance figure is positive.
- After amending a forecast, every later balance changes.
- Methods that help cash (factoring, discounts) often reduce profit: say so.
- State the unit: £, % or days.

## Self-test

1. Revenue £300,000, cost of sales £180,000. Calculate the gross margin.
2. Operating expenses are £84,000. Calculate operating profit and operating margin.
3. Interest £6,000 and tax £9,000. Calculate the net margin.
4. Give two reasons a profitable business may have a cash shortage.
5. Business X makes £40,000 profit on £800,000 revenue; Business Y makes £30,000 on £250,000. Which is more profitable?
6. Budgeted wages £14,000, actual £13,300. State the variance.
7. Budgeted revenue £60,000, actual £57,000. State the variance.
8. Opening balance £2,000, inflows £9,500, outflows £12,100. Calculate the closing balance.
9. Receivables £36,000, revenue £438,000. Calculate receivables days.
10. Payables £15,000, cost of sales £219,000. Calculate payables days and the gap with question 9.
11. Give one disadvantage of offering early payment discounts.
12. Why might a manager amend a budget during the year?

### Answers

1. Gross profit £120,000; 120,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = **40%**.
2. 120,000 − 84,000 = **£36,000**; margin **12%**.
3. Net profit 36,000 − 6,000 − 9,000 = £21,000; margin **7%**.
4. Any two: sales on credit not yet paid; buying assets for cash; building up inventory; repaying loans.
5. **Y**: 12% net margin against X's 5%, although X makes more profit.
6. **£700 favourable** (cost below budget).
7. **£3,000 adverse** (revenue below budget).
8. Net cash flow −2,600; closing balance **−£600** (overdrawn).
9. 36,000 ÷ 438,000 × 365 = **30 days**.
10. 15,000 ÷ 219,000 × 365 = **25 days**; customers take 5 days longer to pay than the business takes to pay suppliers.
11. It reduces the revenue received, lowering profit.
12. An assumption has changed, such as a price change or a new contract, so the original target is no longer a fair comparison.
