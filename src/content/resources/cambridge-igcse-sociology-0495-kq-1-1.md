Key question 1.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *How do sociologists approach the study of
society?* It is the theoretical base for the whole of Paper 1 Question 1. Whenever you explain why a
researcher chose a questionnaire, criticise a source or plan your own study, you are using the ideas
in this key question: the **positivist** approach, the **interpretivist** approach, ways of
**combining** them, and the concepts used to **evaluate** any research choice.

This guide goes deeper than the
[research approaches, data and investigation overview](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-research-approaches-data-and-investigation/),
which covers all three methods key questions in one page.

## Why there are two approaches at all

The disagreement starts with a question about people. Are human beings like objects in nature, whose
behaviour is pushed by outside forces that can be measured? Or are they thinking beings who act on
the basis of what situations mean to them? Positivists lean to the first answer, interpretivists to
the second, and each answer leads to a different idea of what good evidence looks like.

**Positivism** takes its name from Auguste Comte, the nineteenth-century French thinker who also
coined the word "sociology" and wanted a science of society modelled on the natural sciences.
Positivists argue that society has a **structure** (a **macro** view) made of **social facts** —
Émile Durkheim's term for ways of acting and thinking that exist outside any one individual and
constrain them, such as laws, religious rules or the expectation that children attend school. Because
social facts are real and external, they can be counted, so research should follow the **scientific
method**: state a hypothesis, operationalise the concepts, collect **quantitative** data, and test
whether the predicted relationship appears.

**Interpretivism** is linked above all to Max Weber, who argued that sociology must understand the
meaning behind social action. A wink and an involuntary twitch of the eye look identical to a
measuring instrument but mean completely different things. Interpretivists therefore take a **micro**
view, studying small groups and face-to-face interaction, and aim for **verstehen** — understanding
an action from the actor's own point of view. Their data is **qualitative** and **in-depth**: the
words, feelings and reasons of the people studied.

## The key terms explained through one example

Take a single research topic — *why some students leave school early* — and see how each named
syllabus point applies.

| Syllabus point | What it means for this topic |
|---|---|
| **Patterns and trends** | A positivist might use school records to show that early leaving is more common in some regions and has fallen over ten years |
| **Correlation** | Early leaving and low household income rise and fall together across regions |
| **Causation** | Showing that low income actually *produces* early leaving, for example because teenagers must earn money — a correlation alone cannot show this |
| **Objectivity** | The researcher uses standard questions so personal views about early leavers do not shape results |
| **Reliability** | Another researcher using the same questionnaire in another year would get comparable results |
| **Meanings and motivations** | An interpretivist asks what leaving school means to the young people: relief, shame, a step into adulthood? |
| **Subjectivity** | The interpretivist accepts that their own interpretation is part of the research and must be reflected on |
| **Validity** | Long, open conversations may reveal reasons a tick-box form would miss, such as caring for a sick parent |

The table shows the trade-off at the heart of 1.1: positivist research tends to be **reliable** but
may lack **validity**; interpretivist research tends to be **valid** but may lack **reliability**.

## Correlation and causation: three reasons a link can mislead

Exam sources often present a correlation. You gain marks by explaining why it may not show cause.

1. **A third factor.** Students who own more books may get higher grades, but both could be caused by
   parental income or education.
2. **Reverse direction.** Unemployment and poor mental health are linked; poor health might cause job
   loss rather than the other way round.
3. **Chance or a small sample.** A pattern found in one school may disappear in a larger study.

## Combining approaches (1.1.3)

Many sociologists reject a strict either-or choice.

**Triangulation** means using more than one method or source to study the same issue, so that the
weakness of one is checked by the strength of another. Eileen Barker's study of the Unification
Church, *The Making of a Moonie* (1984), is a well-known example: she combined participant
observation, in-depth interviews and a large questionnaire to investigate why people joined and
left the movement. The observation gave meaning; the questionnaire showed whether her impressions
held across many members.

**Methodological pluralism** is a related idea — using several methods to build a fuller picture
rather than to cross-check one result.

**Longitudinal studies** follow the same people over a period of time, collecting data at intervals.
They can be quantitative (repeated questionnaires to a cohort) or qualitative (repeated interviews
with a small group). Their special strength is showing **sequence**: whether a cause came before
its supposed effect, which helps move from correlation towards causation. Problems include cost,
**attrition** (people dropping out, so the remaining sample becomes less representative), the
researcher's funding and career needing to last as long as the study, and the possibility that
being studied repeatedly changes participants' behaviour.

## Evaluating research choices (1.1.4)

These seven concepts are the tools for every "explain a problem" and "evaluate" question. Learn to
connect each one to a cause in the research design.

- **Bias** — any systematic distortion. It can come from the researcher's values, from leading
  question wording ("Do you agree that homework is a waste of time?") or from who is left out of a
  sample.
- **Interviewer effect** — respondents answer differently because of the interviewer's
  characteristics or manner. A teacher asking students about cheating will hear less than an
  anonymous form would collect. Respondents may also give **socially desirable** answers.
- **Hawthorne / observer effect** — people change behaviour because they know they are being
  studied. The name comes from studies at the Western Electric Hawthorne works in the United States
  in the 1920s and 1930s, where workers' output changed in response to being observed rather than to
  the conditions being tested.
- **Validity** — whether the data gives a true picture. Questions about sensitive behaviour often
  have low validity because people under-report.
- **Reliability** — whether the research could be repeated with similar results. Standardised
  methods score highly; one researcher's unrecorded impressions do not.
- **Representativeness** — whether the sample has the same characteristics as the target
  population.
- **Generalisability** — whether findings can be applied beyond the people studied. It depends on
  representativeness: an unrepresentative sample cannot support general claims.

## Worked example: Question 1(d)-style evaluation

*Evaluate the view that interpretivist research is more useful than positivist research.*

> **For the view.** Interpretivist methods such as unstructured interviews allow people to explain
> their actions in their own words, so the researcher can achieve verstehen. This gives high
> validity: for example, interviews could show that some students who appear "unmotivated" in school
> statistics are in fact working night shifts to support their family. Positivist questionnaires
> would impose the researcher's categories and miss this meaning.
>
> **Against the view.** However, interpretivist studies use small samples, so findings may not be
> representative or generalisable, which limits their use for governments planning policy. Results
> depend on one researcher's interpretation, so subjectivity and low reliability are problems.
> Positivist research can identify patterns and trends across thousands of people and test
> correlations, which shows the scale of a problem.
>
> **Judgement.** Usefulness depends on the research aim: interpretivism is more useful for
> understanding *why*, positivism for measuring *how much*. Triangulation, as in Barker's study,
> suggests the most useful research combines both.

**Why it works:** each paragraph uses syllabus terms (verstehen, validity, representativeness,
generalisability, reliability, patterns and trends), applies them to an example, and the judgement
is reasoned rather than a restatement.

## Common mistakes

- Treating positivism and interpretivism as lists of methods rather than views about what society
  is and how it should be studied. Explain the **reason** behind each preference.
- Saying positivists are "objective" and interpretivists "biased". Interpretivists aim to be
  rigorous; they simply see some subjectivity as unavoidable.
- Describing a longitudinal study as "a long study". It must study the **same** group at intervals.
- Claiming triangulation makes research "100% accurate". It increases confidence; it does not
  guarantee truth.
- Naming an evaluation concept without linking it to the design (for example, "this lacks validity"
  with no reason).

## Exam technique for 0495 command words

- **Define** (give precise meaning): one accurate sentence, ideally with a brief example — "social
  facts are features of society external to individuals that shape their behaviour, such as laws".
- **Explain** (set out reasons, say why or how, supported by evidence): identify the point, then
  develop it with "because" or "this means that", and apply it.
- **Evaluate** (judge the quality or value): argue both for and against, use evaluation concepts,
  and finish with a judgement linked to research aims.
- **Discuss** (write in depth in a structured way): build several developed points in paragraphs
  rather than a list.

Under the syllabus, Question 1(e) asks you to explain and justify a research plan but does **not**
expect you to evaluate your own methodology — your knowledge of 1.1 is used there to give reasons,
for instance choosing interviews "to gain valid data on meanings".
