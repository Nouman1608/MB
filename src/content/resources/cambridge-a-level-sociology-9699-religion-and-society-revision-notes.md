## The argument in one paragraph

What sociologists find about religion depends on how they define and measure it. Exclusive,
substantive definitions make religion easier to count and decline easier to show; inclusive,
functional ones make religion look universal. Belief, belonging and practice can separate, and
every measure has validity problems. Religiosity varies by gender, age, ethnicity and class, with
structural and interactionist explanations for each. Religion can be compared with science and
ideologies as belief systems, though the line between open and closed systems is debated.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Substantive definition** | Defines religion by its content, such as belief in the supernatural |
| **Functional definition** | Defines religion by what it does for individuals or society |
| **Exclusive / inclusive** | Tight boundary around religion / includes anything with religious functions |
| **Sacred** | Set apart and forbidden; inspiring awe (Durkheim, 1912) |
| **Religiosity** | Extent of religious belief, belonging and practice |
| **Believing without belonging** | Holding belief without membership or attendance (Davie, 1994) |
| **Social desirability** | Giving answers that seem socially approved |
| **Ageing effect / generational effect** | People becoming more religious as they age / each generation less religious than the last |
| **Compensators** | Promises of future reward for those who lack rewards now (Stark and Bainbridge, 1987) |
| **Open / closed belief system** | Open to falsification / claims absolute truth and explains away contradictions |
| **Paradigm** | Shared framework that guides scientific work (Kuhn, 1962) |

## Defining religion

- **Durkheim (1912):** beliefs and practices relative to sacred things, uniting a moral community —
  substantive and functional together.
- **Geertz (1966):** a system of symbols creating moods and motivations through conceptions of a
  general order of existence — meaning-centred.
- **Why it matters:** substantive definitions favour findings of decline; functional definitions
  find religion in new forms.

## Measuring belief

| Problem | Example |
|---|---|
| Dimensions differ | Belief, belonging and practice can separate (Davie, 1994) |
| Census measures identity | England and Wales census religion question from 2001 |
| Over-reporting | Hadaway, Marler and Chaves (1993): US self-reports exceeded counts |
| Under-reporting | Where religion is stigmatised or risky |
| Counts miss some worshippers | English Church Census (Brierley) misses irregular attenders and home worship |
| Cross-cultural bias | Home-shrine worship in many Hindu households |
| Operationalisation | Glock and Stark's five dimensions |

**Methods:** positivists favour surveys and counts; interpretivists favour interviews and
observation; triangulation combines them.

## Social groups and religiosity

**Gender**
- Caring roles and rites of passage.
- Risk aversion: Miller and Hoffmann (1995).
- Structural location: time and opportunity.
- Varies by faith: some religions require men's public worship.

**Age**
- Ageing effect versus generational effect.
- Voas and Crockett (2005): generational replacement drives decline in Britain.
- Young people's interest in new movements and spirituality.

**Ethnicity**
- Higher religiosity among many minority and migrant groups.
- Cultural defence and cultural transition (Bruce).
- Community support and networks.
- Differences between groups and generations.

**Class**
- Stark and Bainbridge (1987): compensators attract the deprived to sects.
- Middle class: world-affirming movements; formal membership in some societies.
- Patterns vary by country and denomination.

## Religion and other belief systems

- Popper: science is open (falsifiable); religion often closed.
- Evans-Pritchard (1937): Azande explained oracle failures without abandoning belief.
- Polanyi: all belief systems, including science, protect themselves.
- Horton (1967): African traditional thought and science both explain and predict; science more
  open to alternatives.
- Kuhn (1962): science works within paradigms that resist challenge.
- Ideologies such as nationalism and Marxism as functional alternatives to religion.

## Exam traps

- State which definition a claim depends on.
- Belief ≠ belonging ≠ practice.
- A census measures identity, not belief.
- Avoid unsourced attendance or census figures.
- Evaluate explanations of group differences for particular faiths and societies.
- Do not forget "religion and other belief systems".

## Self-test

1. Distinguish exclusive from inclusive definitions of religion.
2. Give Durkheim's definition of religion in your own words.
3. Why might a functional definition make secularisation hard to prove?
4. What did Davie mean by "believing without belonging"?
5. What does the England and Wales census question on religion measure?
6. What did Hadaway, Marler and Chaves (1993) find?
7. Give one reason why attendance counts may misrepresent religiosity.
8. What explanation of women's greater religiosity did Miller and Hoffmann (1995) offer?
9. Distinguish the ageing effect from the generational effect.
10. What are compensators?
11. What did Evans-Pritchard's study of the Azande show about belief systems?
12. Why might Kuhn's work weaken the open/closed distinction?

### Answers

1. Exclusive definitions draw a tight boundary, usually belief in the supernatural; inclusive
   definitions include anything that performs religious functions.
2. A unified system of beliefs and practices about sacred things that unites believers into a
   single moral community.
3. Because anything performing religious functions counts, so religion can always be found in new
   forms.
4. That many people hold religious beliefs without belonging to or attending a church.
5. Religious identity or belonging, not belief or practice.
6. That Americans' self-reported church attendance substantially exceeded counts of people actually
   present.
7. Any one: misses irregular attenders; misses home worship; attendance may reflect social pressure
   rather than belief; attendance is less central in some faiths.
8. Women are more risk-averse than men, and not being religious can be seen as a risk.
9. Ageing effect: individuals become more religious as they get older. Generational effect: each
   generation is less religious than the one before.
10. Promises of future rewards that religion offers to people who lack rewards now.
11. That believers can explain away failures (for example, blaming procedure) without abandoning
    the belief system.
12. Because scientists also work within paradigms that resist challenge, so science is not
    completely open.
