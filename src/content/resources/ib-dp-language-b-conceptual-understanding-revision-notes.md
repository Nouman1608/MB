For full explanations and worked examples, read the [conceptual understanding study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-conceptual-understanding/) first. These notes are for the final weeks.

They cover the five **conceptual understandings** of IB Diploma Programme Language B (context, audience, purpose, meaning and variation) as named in the IB *Diploma Programme Subject Brief: Language B*, first assessment 2020, applied to texts from the human ingenuity, social organization and sharing the planet themes. SL and HL use the same concepts; HL Paper 1 demands more complex language. It follows the IB Language B subject brief for first assessment 2020, which remains the examined course until the new syllabus is first assessed in May 2029, so it applies to the 2026, 2027 and 2028 sessions.

| Why they matter | Students become "successful, effective communicators" by considering them |
| Assessment objective | Communicate "in a range of contexts and for a variety of purposes" |

## Definitions

| Concept | One-line definition | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Context | The situation around a text: place, time, medium, culture, the event behind it | Where would I find this, and what prompted it? |
| Audience | Who the text is for, and the writer–reader relationship | Who reads this, and how well do they know the writer? |
| Purpose | Why the text exists: inform, persuade, instruct, request, complain, thank, entertain, reflect | What should the reader know, feel or do afterwards? |
| Meaning | What the text communicates, stated (explicit) or suggested (implicit) | What is said, what is hinted, and which words hint it? |
| Variation | How language changes with the other four: register, tone, medium, regional variety | Why is it written this way and not another? |

## Register at a glance

| Register | Signals in English | Target-language checks |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | No contractions; passive or impersonal forms; "I am writing to..."; "Yours faithfully" | Formal "you" (e.g. *vous*, *usted*, *Sie*); polite verb forms; set openings and closings |
| Semi-formal | Clear paragraphs; some direct address; questions to readers; no slang | Acceptable forms of address for a teacher or editor |
| Informal | Contractions; exclamations; fragments; humour; "Hi!" | Informal "you" (e.g. *tu*, *tú*, *du*); everyday words |

**Tone** is attitude, not formality. Learn these in your target language: urgent, reassuring, enthusiastic, critical, sympathetic, humorous, ironic, neutral. Tone comes from intensifiers, modal verbs, punctuation and the balance of facts and feelings.

**Kinds of variation**

- Register: formal to informal.
- Tone: the writer's attitude.
- Medium: spoken is looser than written; a message is shorter than a letter.
- Regional variety: vocabulary, pronunciation and some grammar differ by country and region (for example, Spanish *vosotros* in most of Spain; *ustedes* for all plural "you" in Latin America).

## Method in steps

**Reading any text (Paper 2 or an oral stimulus)**

1. Text type and context: what is it and where does it come from?
2. Audience: who, and what relationship?
3. Purpose: main purpose, then a second one.
4. Meaning: explicit message, then one implied message with the words that imply it.
5. Variation: register and tone, each with a quoted feature.

**Decoding a writing task (Paper 1)**

1. Underline the context, the audience and the purpose in the task.
2. Choose or confirm the text type and its conventions.
3. Set the register from the audience and the tone from the purpose.
4. Plan content only after steps 1 to 3.
5. Check: does the opening, the form of "you" and the closing all match the same register?

**Shifting register (rewrite tasks)**

1. Keep the facts.
2. Change the form of address and the greeting.
3. Change sentence types (full and passive to short and active, or the reverse).
4. Swap vocabulary (official to everyday, or the reverse).
5. Check nothing from the old register is left behind.

## Worked reminders

*Original examples written for Marlbridge.*

- *Weak:* "The leaflet is formal." *Stronger:* "The leaflet is formal: it uses the impersonal 'Visitors are asked to' rather than 'please', which suits a council addressing strangers."
- *Weak:* "The purpose is to inform." *Stronger:* "It informs readers of the new bus times, but 'we apologise for any inconvenience' shows it also aims to calm complaints."
- *Weak:* "The blogger is angry." *Stronger:* "The blogger's tone is sarcastic: 'Great news, another car park!' praises the plan on the surface while mocking it."
- *Register shift:* "Participants are requested to arrive by 9 a.m." becomes, for a friend, "Get there by nine, OK?"

## The concepts across three themes

| Theme (from the brief) | Typical text and context | Audience and purpose | Likely register and tone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social organization | A school rule change announced to parents | Parents; inform and justify | Formal; reassuring |
| Sharing the planet | A campaign post about a river clean-up | Young local people; persuade to join | Informal to semi-formal; enthusiastic |
| Human ingenuity | A review of a new app built by students | Magazine readers; inform and evaluate | Semi-formal; balanced |

These pairings are typical, not fixed: always read the task or text for its own signals.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Register vs tone | Register is how formal; tone is what attitude. A formal letter can be furious |
| Context vs audience | Context is the situation; audience is the people in it who read the text |
| Purpose vs meaning | Purpose is what the writer wants to happen; meaning is what the text communicates |
| Explicit vs implicit | Stated in words vs suggested by choices or omissions |
| Formal vs polite | Formality is a register; politeness can exist at any register ("Could you...?" to a friend) |
| Variety vs error | A regional form used consistently is a variety, not a mistake; mixing varieties at random is inconsistent |
| SL vs HL oral stimulus | SL: a visual image; HL: an excerpt from one of the two literary works studied |

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five conceptual understandings listed in the brief.
2. What is the difference between register and tone?
3. "We regret to inform members that the pool will close early." Give the register and the main purpose.
4. In the sentence in question 3, what does "regret" add to the meaning?
5. A blog asks "Who needs another shopping centre?" Name the device and its likely purpose.
6. Rewrite for a friend: "Attendees are requested to bring their own water bottles."
7. Give two English signals of formal register.
8. A council notice says the park "will be monitored by cameras". What implicit meaning might a reader take from this?
9. What is the audience of a letter to a newspaper editor?
10. Which extra demands does HL Paper 1 make?
11. What is the stimulus for the individual oral at SL, and at HL?
12. Name one way your target language may mark the difference between writing to a head teacher and writing to a friend.

### Answers

1. Context, audience, purpose, meaning, variation.
2. Register is the level of formality; tone is the writer's attitude.
3. Formal; to inform (with a polite, apologetic tone).
4. It signals that the writer is sorry, softening bad news and showing respect for the readers.
5. A rhetorical question; to persuade readers that the new centre is unnecessary.
6. For example: "Bring your own water bottle!" (informal "you" in your target language).
7. Any two: no contractions; passive or impersonal forms; formal openings or closings; precise, official vocabulary.
8. That there has been trouble in the park (such as damage) and that behaviour will be watched.
9. The newspaper's readers, mainly the public; the editor chooses what to print.
10. More complex language and structures, higher-order thinking, and a higher word range.
11. SL: a visual image clearly relevant to one or more themes. HL: an excerpt from one of the two literary works studied.
12. For example: formal vs informal "you", polite verb forms, or different greetings and closings.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a register or tone without quoting the feature that shows it.
- Mixing registers inside one text, such as a formal complaint that closes with "Cheers".
- Switching between formal and informal "you" halfway through a text.
- Calling a text "informal" because it is emotional, when it is a formal text with a strong tone.
- Giving "everyone" as the audience instead of the specific group the text targets.
- Stating only the obvious purpose and missing the second one (defending, reassuring, selling).
- Presenting an implied meaning as a stated fact, without "suggests" or "implies".
- Planning content before identifying context, audience and purpose, then writing in the wrong text type.
- Translating English idioms word for word, which can break both meaning and register in the target language.
