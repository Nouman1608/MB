These questions cover section **4.6.2 Variation and evolution** (4.6.2.1 to 4.6.2.5) of the AQA GCSE Biology
(8461) specification, for first teaching from 2016. Questions 10 and 11 are on Biology only content (cloning).
Questions or parts marked **Higher tier only** cover (HT only) content. The organisms and data in questions 6 and
7 are invented examples. A calculator is useful for questions 6 and 7.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State whether each human characteristic is caused mainly by genes, mainly by the environment, or by both.

**(a)** Blood group **[1]**
**(b)** A scar on the knee **[1]**
**(c)** Body mass **[1]**

**2.** Complete the sentences about mutations.

**(a)** All genetic variants arise from ______. **[1]**
**(b)** Most mutations have ______ effect on the phenotype. **[1]**

**3.** Two populations of birds once belonged to the same species. State how scientists could tell whether they
are now two separate species. **[1]**

**4.** Give **two** characteristics that humans have selectively bred for in plants or animals. **[2]**

**5.** (Higher tier only) Name **two** types of vector used in genetic engineering. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**6.** A species of moth lives in a woodland. A disease kills most of the pale-barked trees, leaving mainly
dark-barked trees. Birds eat moths that they can see easily. Scientists record the percentage of dark moths in
the population.

| Year | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark moths (%) | 12 | 25 | 48 | 70 |

**(a)** Calculate the increase in percentage points between year 0 and year 30. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the change in the moth population, using the theory of natural selection. **[4]**
**(c)** A student says: "The moths turned dark so that birds could not see them." Explain why this is wrong. **[2]**

**7.** A farmer selectively breeds dairy cows for higher milk yield. The mean yield of the herd is recorded.

| Generation | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean milk yield (litres per day) | 18 | 20 | 23 | 25 | 27 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in mean yield from generation 1 to generation 5. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the farmer would have carried out the selective breeding. **[3]**
**(c)** After many generations, the herd is more often affected by a disease. Explain why. **[2]**

**8.** A company has produced a crop plant that is genetically engineered to be resistant to a herbicide.

**(a)** Explain why farmers might want to grow this crop. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **two** concerns people have about GM crops. **[2]**
**(c)** (Higher tier only) Describe the main steps used to produce the GM crop. **[4]**

**9.** Compare natural selection with selective breeding. **[4]**

**10.** A plant nursery wants to produce thousands of identical plants of a rare orchid.

**(a)** Explain why the nursery would use tissue culture rather than taking cuttings. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **one** disadvantage of producing all the orchids as clones. **[1]**

**11.** A farmer has a cow with excellent milk yield. A vet uses embryo transplants to produce several calves from
one embryo of this cow.

**(a)** Describe how embryo transplants are carried out. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the calves are genetically identical to each other but not to the cow that gave the embryo. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how adult cell cloning is used to produce a cloned animal, and evaluate its use in agriculture. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Genes [1]
**(b)** Environment [1]
**(c)** Both [1]

**2. (a)** Mutations [1]
**(b)** No [1]

**3.** Test whether they can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; if they cannot, they are separate species [1]

**4.** Any two: disease resistance in food crops; more meat or milk; gentle nature in dogs; large or unusual flowers [2]
*Examiner insight:* One mark for each characteristic; accept other sensible examples that are genetic, such as higher crop yield.

**5.** Bacterial plasmid [1]; virus [1]

**6. (a)** 70 − 12 = **58 percentage points** [1]
**(b)** One mark for each point:
- there was variation in colour in the moth population, caused by mutation [1]
- on dark bark, dark moths were better camouflaged, so birds ate fewer of them [1]
- dark moths were more likely to survive and breed, passing on the alleles for dark colour [1]
- over many generations, the proportion of dark moths increased [1]
**(c)** Mutations are random and are not caused by the need to survive [1]; dark moths were already present, and they became more common because they survived and bred more [1]

**7. (a)** (27 − 18) ÷ 18 × 100 [1] = **50%** [1]
**(b)** Choose the cows (and bulls from mothers) with the highest milk yield as parents [1]; breed them together [1]; select the highest-yielding offspring and breed them, repeating over many generations [1]
**(c)** Selective breeding from related animals causes inbreeding and reduces variation [1]; so the herd is more prone to disease, as there may be no alleles giving resistance [1]

**8. (a)** Farmers can spray herbicide to kill weeds without killing the crop [1]; so there is less competition and the yield increases [1]
**(b)** Effect on populations of wild flowers and insects [1]; effects of eating GM crops on human health have not been fully explored [1]
**(c)** Enzymes are used to isolate the herbicide-resistance gene [1]; the gene is inserted into a vector, such as a bacterial plasmid or virus [1]; the vector inserts the gene into the plant cells [1]; at an early stage of development, so the plants grow with the characteristic [1]

**9.** One mark for each point, up to 4:
- in natural selection the environment determines which individuals survive and breed, whereas in selective breeding humans choose the parents [1]
- natural selection favours characteristics that help survival, whereas selective breeding favours characteristics that are useful or attractive to humans [1]
- selective breeding is usually faster than natural selection [1]
- both act on existing genetic variation and change allele frequencies over many generations [1]

**10. (a)** Tissue culture uses small groups of cells, so many more plants can be produced from one parent [1]; this preserves a rare species without needing large parts of the parent plant [1]
**(b)** No variation, so a disease or environmental change could affect all the plants [1]

**11. (a)** Cells are split apart from a developing embryo [1]; before they become specialised [1]; the identical embryos are transplanted into host mothers [1]
**(b)** All the calves come from the same embryo, so they have the same genetic information [1]; the embryo formed by sexual reproduction, so it has a mix of alleles from the cow and the bull [1]

**12.** One mark for each point:
- the nucleus is removed from an unfertilised egg cell [1]
- the nucleus from an adult body cell (such as a skin cell) of the animal to be cloned is inserted into the egg [1]
- an electric shock stimulates the egg to divide and form an embryo, which is inserted into the womb of an adult female as a ball of cells [1]
- benefit: farmers can produce animals with known desirable characteristics, such as high yield [1]
- risk: the clones have no variation, so a new disease could affect all of them; some people have ethical objections [1]
- a justified conclusion that weighs benefit against risk [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying mutations happen because they are needed.
- Selective breeding steps that stop after one generation.
- Dividing by the final value in a percentage increase.
- Mixing up which animal a clone is identical to.
- Evaluations that list points without a conclusion.
