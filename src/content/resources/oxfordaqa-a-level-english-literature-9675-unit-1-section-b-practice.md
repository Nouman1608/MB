> **original**, written in a modern domestic style, and does not come from any set play. The real

## Section A: concepts

**1.** State two differences between the Section A task and the Section B task in Unit 1. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by domestic tragedy and summarise the case Miller made for it in 1949.
**[3]**

**3.** Explain two ways in which a modern playwright's stage directions can be analysed as authorial
method. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what tragicomedy means and how comic elements can intensify a tragic effect. **[3]**

**5.** Identify three pressures that take the place of fate in later tragedy, and explain how one of
them can make a protagonist's end feel inevitable. **[4]**

## Section B: an original scene, arguments and plans

**Extract (original).**

> *A kitchen at night. The refrigerator hums. RAY, sixty, in a suit too heavy for the season, sits
> at the table with his sample case unopened beside him. JUNE, his daughter, enters in a nurse's
> uniform.*
> **JUNE:** You're back early.
> **RAY:** They moved the meeting. *(He straightens the case so its edge lines up with the table.)*
> Thursday, maybe.
> **JUNE:** Dad, it's Saturday.
> **RAY:** I know what day it is. *(Pause.)* Thursday, I said. Next Thursday.
> *JUNE puts the kettle on. Neither of them looks at the case.*
> **JUNE:** Did you eat?
> **RAY:** I had a big lunch. Clients. *(He laughs, too loudly.)* They wanted to hear the one about
> the dog.
> *The refrigerator stops humming. In the silence RAY's hand goes to his tie and stays there.*

**6.** Analyse how the stage directions in the extract present Ray's decline. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the dialogue creates tension through what is not said. **[4]**

**8.** "Modern tragic protagonists are too ordinary to be truly tragic." Give two arguments for and
two arguments against this view that could be developed in an essay on a later tragedy. **[4]**

**9.** A student's evidence list for a whole-play essay contains five moments, all from the first
third of the play. Explain why this weakens the essay and how to correct it. **[3]**

**10.** For the question "The protagonist is destroyed by other people rather than by their own
choices. Discuss", three students write these opening theses:

- A: "In this essay I will discuss whether the protagonist is destroyed by other people."
- B: "The protagonist is destroyed by other people."
- C: "Other people deliver the final blow, but the play shows the protagonist choosing, again and
  again, the illusions that leave them defenceless."

Choose the strongest thesis and explain why it is stronger than each of the other two. **[4]**

**11.** Plan an essay on your own set play for the question: "The ending of the play offers the
audience no consolation." List five elements your plan should contain. **[5]**

**12.** Read this student paragraph on *A Streetcar Named Desire*: "Blanche is a tragic heroine
because she suffers a lot. Stanley is mean to her and she ends up being taken away. This is very
tragic and the audience feels sorry for her." Identify two weaknesses and explain how each could
be fixed. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, [1] each: Section A prints a passage from the play, Section B prints none; Section A
starts from close analysis of a passage, Section B builds an argument about the whole play; Section
A is on an Elizabethan or Jacobean tragedy, Section B on a later dramatic tragedy.

**2.** Domestic tragedy presents the suffering of ordinary people in private, family life rather
than kings or public figures [1]. In his 1949 essay "Tragedy and the Common Man" Miller argued that
the common man is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings [1], because the tragic feeling comes from a
person willing to sacrifice everything to secure their sense of personal dignity, whatever their
rank [1].

**3.** For each of two ways, way identified [1] and explained [1]. For example: setting and props
(a house, a portrait, a lantern) are chosen to carry meaning [1], so they can be analysed as symbols
in the same way as imagery [1]; sound and lighting directions (music only one character hears,
light that changes in memory scenes) [1] show inner experience the dialogue hides [1]; directions
for movement and silence [1] show power and avoidance between characters, such as one character
turning away [1].

**4.** Tragicomedy mixes tragic and comic elements within one work [1]. Comedy can intensify a
tragic effect by contrast, so that suffering seems more painful after laughter [1], or by making
characters' attempts to cope with despair (jokes, routines, games) a sign of how bleak their
situation is [1].

**5.** Any three pressures [1] each, up to [3]: social expectations of success or respectability;
money and debt; class; gender roles; colonial or military power; memory and psychology; time. One
explained [1]: for example, a protagonist who believes success depends on being admired cannot
change course without abandoning the belief that gives life meaning, so the end feels inevitable.

**6.** Any six, [1] each, for points linked to decline:

- The suit "too heavy for the season" suggests a man out of step with his surroundings and his
  time.
- The "unopened" sample case shows work he has not done, a sign of failure he cannot admit.
- Straightening the case so its edge lines up with the table is a small, controlling gesture,
  imposing order on objects when he cannot order his life.
- "Neither of them looks at the case" makes the case the unspoken subject of the scene; avoidance is
  shared.
- "He laughs, too loudly" shows performed confidence; the adverb tells the actor to make the
  pretence visible.
- The refrigerator's hum stopping creates a sudden silence that exposes the lie, using ordinary
  sound for dramatic effect.
- The hand going to his tie "and stays there" freezes him in the salesman's gesture of
  presentability, suggesting both habit and panic.
- The night setting and the daughter in work uniform contrast her working life with his empty day.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: Ray's vague "Thursday, maybe" avoids explaining why he is back; June's
correction "Dad, it's Saturday" exposes his confusion or lie without accusing him; his insistence
"I know what day it is" is defensive, revealing more than it hides; June changes the subject to
food rather than confront him, showing care and avoidance; the lie about "Clients" and the dog joke
invites the audience to recognise what June does not say.

**8.** Two for [1] each: their suffering lacks public consequence, since no kingdom falls; their
choices may seem small or confused rather than grand; some lack the insight or recognition older
tragedy gives its heroes. Two against [1] each: the specification includes tragedy about "ordinary
people"; Miller argued the common man is a fit subject for tragedy; ordinary protagonists invite
recognition and so intensify pity and fear; the scale of suffering within a family can be total for
those inside it.

**9.** Evidence from one part of the play cannot support an argument about the whole play's
development [1], and it leaves out the turning point and the ending, where tragic outcomes are
decided [1]. Correction: replace two or three items with moments from the middle and the end, so
the evidence covers the opening, a turning point and the conclusion [1].

**10.** C is the strongest [1]. It is stronger than A because A only announces a topic and takes no
position [1]. It is stronger than B because B simply agrees and leaves no room for debate [1]. C
weighs both sides and gives a specific line of argument that the essay can test [1].

**11.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on whether the ending consoles; what the ending
shows (deaths, survivors, order or its absence); a counter-argument identifying any consolation
(dignity, recognition, a survivor's hope, the audience's understanding); evidence from earlier in
the play that the ending answers or reverses; analysis of staging at the end (light, sound, final
image); a relevant context, such as the playwright's view of tragedy or the society portrayed; a
conclusion on what the audience is left with.

**12.** Two weaknesses, each identified [1] and fixed [1]:

- No analysis of method [1]: it should examine how Williams presents her suffering, for example
  through the Varsouviana polka she alone hears, or the paper lantern covering the bare bulb [1].
- No argument about tragedy [1]: "suffers a lot" and "very tragic" are assertions; it should ask
  how far Blanche's own illusions contribute to her fall, weighed against Stanley's violence [1].
- Credit also, as an alternative [1] + [1]: vague evidence ("ends up being taken away") replaced by
  a precise reference to the final scene and its effect on the audience.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the play rather than arguing.
- One-sided answers to "discuss": always weigh more than one view.
- Leaving out the stage, which is central to how these plays work.
- Conclusions that only restate the introduction.

For full practice, set yourself one 25-mark essay on your own play using the shapes in Questions 8,
10 and 11, and write for about an hour from memory.
