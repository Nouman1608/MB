These revision notes condense warfare and peacekeeping for IB MYP Individuals and Societies, aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Individuals and societies*, from 2014. They suit MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split). "Warfare and peacekeeping" is a topic the brief lists for the on-screen examinations; MYP has no prescribed content list, so schools choose their own case studies, and these notes give you a reliable core.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Long-term cause | A factor building up over years that made war more likely |
| Short-term cause | A development in the months before war that raised tension |
| Trigger | The event that started the fighting |
| Security dilemma | One state arms for safety; its rival feels threatened and arms too |
| Collective security | Members treat an attack on one as an attack on all and act together |
| Total war | War that mobilises the whole society and economy; civilians become targets |
| Limited war | War with deliberately restricted aims, area or weapons |
| Proxy war | Outside powers back opposing local sides instead of fighting each other |
| Peacemaking | Diplomacy to bring warring sides to an agreement |
| Peacekeeping | Impartial personnel deployed with the parties' consent to help keep a ceasefire |
| Peace enforcement | Force authorised by the Security Council under Chapter VII, without needing consent |
| Peacebuilding | Long-term work (institutions, justice, disarmament) so that war does not return |
| DDR | Disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration of former fighters |
| Negative / positive peace | Absence of fighting / causes of conflict also addressed (Johan Galtung) |

## Types of war: one example each

| Type | Example to quote |
|---|---|
| Interstate | Falklands War, 2 April – 14 June 1982 |
| Civil | Spanish Civil War, 1936–39; American Civil War, 1861–65 |
| Total | Second World War, 1939–45 |
| Limited | Korean War, 1950–53 |
| Proxy | Vietnam War, ending with the fall of Saigon, 30 April 1975 |
| Cold | USA and USSR, c.1947–1991 |

## UN Charter and Security Council facts

| Item | What it says or does |
|---|---|
| Article 2(4) | Bans the threat or use of force against other states |
| Article 51 | Keeps the right of self-defence |
| Chapter VI | Peaceful settlement: negotiation, mediation, arbitration |
| Chapter VII | Action against threats to peace: sanctions (Art. 41), force (Art. 42) |
| Security Council | 15 members: 5 permanent with veto (China, France, Russia, UK, USA) + 10 elected for two years |
| Peacekeeping principles | Consent of the parties; impartiality; non-use of force except self-defence and defence of the mandate |

## Method in steps

**Explaining causes (criterion A and D)**

1. Name each cause and label it long-term, short-term or trigger.
2. Give one precise, dated piece of evidence for each.
3. Link causes: show how one made another more dangerous.
4. Judge which mattered most and say why ("without the alliances, the assassination stays a local crisis").

**Judging whether a peace operation succeeded**

1. State the mandate: what was the mission asked to do?
2. Test short-term success: did the ceasefire hold, were civilians protected?
3. Test long-term success: was a political settlement reached (positive peace)?
4. Name the limits: consent, size, veto politics, resources.

**Evaluating a source (criterion B and D)**

1. Origin: who made it, when?
2. Purpose: why, and for whom?
3. Value: what can it reliably show for this research question?
4. Limitation: what can it not show, and why (use the same origin or purpose fact)?

## Worked reminder: percentage change

A fictional mission's troop strength fell from 5,000 to 3,500.

```
Fall             = 5,000 − 3,500 = 1,500
Percentage fall  = 1,500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 30%
```

Always divide by the **starting** value.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cause vs trigger.** The trigger starts the war; the causes explain why it became that war.
- **Peacekeeping vs peace enforcement.** Peacekeeping needs consent and uses minimal force (Cyprus, 1964). Enforcement is authorised under Chapter VII and does not need consent (Korea, 1950; Kuwait, 1990–91).
- **Peacekeeping vs peacebuilding.** Keeping a ceasefire (negative peace) vs removing causes of war (positive peace).
- **League vs UN.** The League had no US membership and no armed forces. The UN has the Security Council with Chapter VII powers, but troops are still lent by members and the veto can block action.
- **Civil war vs proxy war.** A civil war is fought inside a state; it becomes a proxy war when outside powers back the sides (Vietnam).
- **Consequence vs cause.** Versailles (1919) was a consequence of the First World War and a cause of the Second.

## Linking to concepts

- Key concepts from the brief: **change**, **global interactions**, **time, place and space**, **systems**.
- Related concept: **causality** fits every "why did war break out?" question.
- Global contexts: *fairness and development* (justice after war), *orientation in space and time* (turning points).

## Quick self-test

1. Define a trigger and give the trigger of the First World War with its date.
2. Name the three members of the Triple Entente.
3. Give two reasons the League of Nations failed in the 1930s.
4. When did the UN Charter come into force?
5. Which chapter of the UN Charter allows the Security Council to impose sanctions or authorise force?
6. State the three principles of UN peacekeeping.
7. Was the UN action in Korea in 1950 peacekeeping or peace enforcement? Explain.
8. Distinguish negative peace from positive peace.
9. What does DDR stand for?
10. A fictional mission's troops fell from 5,000 to 3,500. Calculate the percentage fall.
11. How many years passed between the UN Charter coming into force and the Nobel Peace Prize for UN peacekeeping forces?
12. Give one example of a consequence of one war that became a cause of another.

### Answers

1. The event that starts the fighting; the assassination of Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo, 28 June 1914.
2. Britain, France and Russia.
3. Any two: the USA never joined; it had no armed force; it failed to stop Japan in Manchuria (1931) or Italy in Abyssinia (1935).
4. 24 October 1945.
5. Chapter VII (Article 41 sanctions, Article 42 force).
6. Consent of the parties; impartiality; non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate.
7. Peace enforcement: the Security Council called on members to help South Korea fight the invasion; it did not depend on both sides' consent.
8. Negative peace is the absence of fighting; positive peace also removes the causes of conflict.
9. Disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration.
10. 1,500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **30%**.
11. 1988 − 1945 = **43 years**.
12. The Treaty of Versailles (1919), which fed German resentment exploited by the Nazis before 1939.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing causes without linking them, so there is no judgement about which mattered most.
- Calling the Korean War or the 1991 Gulf War "peacekeeping" instead of enforcement.
- Giving vague dates ("in the 1900s") when the precise year is well known.
- Treating the veto as a UN failure without explaining how it works (one permanent member can block a resolution).
- Judging a mission only by whether fighting stopped, ignoring whether a settlement followed.
- Forgetting that UN troops are contributed by member states, so size and quality depend on their willingness.
- Evaluating a source with generic "it may be biased" comments instead of linking bias to its origin and purpose.
- Dividing by the final value, not the starting value, when calculating percentage change in data tasks.
