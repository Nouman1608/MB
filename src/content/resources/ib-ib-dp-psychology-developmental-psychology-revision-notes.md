> examination session is November 2026. The replacement course is first assessed in May 2027.

Developmental psychology is one of the four IB DP Psychology options, examined on Paper 2 through 22-mark essays. It asks what influences children's cognitive and social development, how children develop as learners, and how they form an identity -- through attachment, gender identity and social roles, and the development of empathy and theory of mind -- drawing on biological, cognitive and sociocultural research.

## Key theories

| Theory | Claim | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Piaget's stages | Sensorimotor, pre-operational, concrete operational, formal operational; children actively construct knowledge through assimilation and accommodation | Influential and testable; underestimated young children's abilities; less attention to culture |
| Vygotsky | Learning is social; the zone of proximal development (ZPD) and scaffolding by more knowledgeable others; play builds self-regulation | Emphasises culture; harder to test precisely |
| Bowlby's attachment theory | Infants have an innate need to attach to a caregiver; an internal working model guides later relationships | Supported by animal and human research; critics question monotropy and the critical period |
| Social learning / gender schema | Gender roles are learned by observation and reinforcement (Bandura); children build gender schemas that organise information (Martin and Halverson, 1981) | Explains cultural variation; biological factors also play a part |

## Key studies

| Study | Finding | Content point |
|---|---|---|
| Harlow (1958) | Infant rhesus monkeys preferred a cloth "mother" for comfort over a wire mother that gave milk | Attachment (contact comfort) |
| Ainsworth et al. (1978) | Strange Situation classified infants as secure, avoidant or resistant; most of the Baltimore sample were secure | Attachment |
| Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) | Meta-analysis of Strange Situation studies in eight countries: secure attachment most common everywhere; avoidant more common in Germany, resistant in Japan and Israel | Attachment and culture |
| Hodges and Tizard (1989); Rutter et al. (English and Romanian Adoptees study) | Children raised in institutions showed later social difficulties; children adopted early from Romanian orphanages recovered better than those adopted later | Childhood trauma and resilience |
| Werner and Smith (Kauai longitudinal study) | About a third of high-risk children grew into competent adults; protective factors included a close bond with a caregiver and community support | Resilience |
| Luby et al. (2013) | Childhood poverty linked to smaller hippocampal and amygdala volumes, partly through caregiving quality and stressful events | Poverty and brain development |
| Hart and Risley (1995) | Large differences in the number of words children heard at home by family socio-economic status, linked to vocabulary growth | Poverty and cognitive development |
| Chugani (1987); Gogtay et al. (2004) | PET and longitudinal MRI studies show brain regions mature at different rates, with the prefrontal cortex maturing last | Brain development |
| Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) | Most autistic children failed the Sally-Anne false-belief task, unlike most children with Down syndrome and typically developing children | Theory of mind |
| Wimmer and Perner (1983) | Children typically pass false-belief tasks around age four | Theory of mind |
| Smith and Lloyd (1978) | Mothers chose gender-typed toys for infants according to the sex they were told the baby was | Gender identity and social roles |

## Worked example: linking a study to resilience

**Question:** Explain one study of resilience. **[part of a 22-mark essay]**

1. **Define resilience:** positive adaptation despite significant adversity.
2. **Study:** Werner and Smith followed all children born on the Hawaiian island of Kauai in 1955. Around 30% faced several risk factors such as poverty, family discord or parental mental illness.
3. **Finding:** about a third of these high-risk children were already developing into competent, caring young adults by age 18, and many others recovered in adulthood.
4. **Explanation:** protective factors -- an easy temperament, at least one close caregiver, school and community support -- helped them adapt.
5. **Evaluation:** long-term longitudinal design and a whole birth cohort; but one community, correlational data and attrition over decades.

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Describing Piaget's stages without linking them to a question on learning.** Focus on what they show about cognitive development.
- **Treating attachment types as fixed.** Research shows they can change with circumstances.
- **Using Harlow without ethical evaluation.** Animal research raises ethical issues and questions of generalisation to humans.
- **Equating poverty with neglect.** Explain mechanisms such as stress, nutrition and language exposure.
- **"Gender identity" essays that ignore social roles.** The content point includes both.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the developmental option examined?**
On Paper 2: three 22-mark ERQs per option; you answer one at SL, or one from each of two options at HL.

**Which theories are essential?**
Piaget and Vygotsky for cognitive development, Bowlby and Ainsworth for attachment, and at least one explanation of gender identity and of theory of mind.

**Can I use studies from the core?**
Yes, if they are relevant -- for example Bandura's social cognitive theory for gender roles -- but link them to development.

## Self-test

1. What is the zone of proximal development? *The gap between what a child can do alone and with help.*
2. Name the three main attachment types in the Strange Situation. *Secure, avoidant (insecure-avoidant), resistant (insecure-resistant).*
3. What does a false-belief task test? *Theory of mind -- understanding that others can hold beliefs different from reality.*
4. Which brain region matures last? *The prefrontal cortex.*
5. What is a protective factor? *A factor that reduces the effect of adversity, such as a supportive caregiver.*
