## Answers

**1. Sensory register** — capacity is **very large**, duration **under 2 seconds** [1] [1]. **Short-term memory** — capacity about **7 ± 2 items**, duration about **18–30 seconds** [1] [1]. **Long-term memory** — capacity **potentially unlimited**, duration **potentially a lifetime** [1] [1].

**2. Encoding** — **converting information into a form that can be stored** [1]. **Storage** — **holding the information over time** [1]. **Retrieval** — **recovering the stored information when it is needed** [1].

**3.** Any two, 2 marks each: it treats **short-term and long-term memory as single, unitary stores**, whereas evidence from brain-damaged patients shows **separate types of long-term memory** that can be damaged independently [1] [1]. It **overstates the role of rehearsal** — people remember flashbulb events vividly without rehearsing them, and rehearsed material is often forgotten [1] [1]. It is largely based on **artificial laboratory tasks such as recalling word lists**, which lack ecological validity compared with everyday remembering [1] [1].

**4. Episodic** — memory for **personal events and experiences**, including where and when, e.g. remembering your first day at school [1] [1]. **Semantic** — memory for **facts and general knowledge about the world**, without a personal context, e.g. knowing that Paris is the capital of France [1] [1]. **Procedural** — memory for **how to perform actions and skills**, often difficult to put into words, e.g. riding a bicycle [1] [1].

**5. (a)** The **serial position effect** [1]; recall is **best for words at the beginning and at the end of the list**, and poorest for words in the middle [1] [1].
**(b)** The **primacy effect** occurs because early words have been **rehearsed most and transferred into long-term memory** [1] [1]. The **recency effect** occurs because the last words are **still held in short-term memory** at the moment of recall [1]; words in the middle have been **displaced from short-term memory but not rehearsed enough to reach long-term memory** [1].
**(c)** Independent variable: the **position of the word in the list** [1]. Dependent variable: the **number of words correctly recalled** [1].

**6.** Any two, 3 marks each: **Interference** — other memories disrupt recall [1]; **proactive** interference is when older learning disrupts new, **retroactive** is when new learning disrupts old [1]; e.g. learning Spanish makes it harder to recall the French learned earlier [1]. **Retrieval failure (cue-dependent forgetting)** — the memory is stored but cannot be accessed without the right cue [1]; recall is better when the **context or emotional state at recall matches that at encoding** [1]; e.g. remembering something on returning to the room where you learned it [1]. (Also accept decay or displacement.)

**7.** A **laboratory experiment** is conducted in a **controlled artificial setting**; a **field experiment** takes place in the participants' **natural environment** [1] [1]. Laboratory advantage: **extraneous variables are tightly controlled**, so cause and effect can be established confidently and the study is easily replicated [1]. Field advantage: **higher ecological validity** — behaviour is more natural, so findings generalise better to real life [1].

**8. (a)** **Sensation** is the **raw data received by the senses** [1]; **perception** is the **brain's interpretation** of that data [1].

**(b)** **Gibson's direct theory** argues perception is **bottom-up** — the visual array contains enough information, through cues such as motion parallax and texture gradient, to perceive accurately without inference [2]. **Gregory's constructivist theory** argues perception is **top-down** — the brain makes inferences from prior knowledge and expectation, which is why illusions such as the Müller-Lyer figure deceive us, as the brain misapplies depth cues [2].

**9. (a)** A **strong positive relationship exists** — more sleep is associated with higher test scores [1]; this **cannot** show that sleep **causes** the higher scores [2].

**(b)** A plausible third variable, such as **general health or motivation**, could independently drive both more sleep and higher test scores [2].

**(c)** Any three: **informed consent**; **right to withdraw**; **protection from harm**; **confidentiality**; **debriefing** [3].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the duration of short-term memory as "a few minutes".
- Confusing episodic with semantic memory.
- Explaining the recency effect using long-term memory.
- Naming variables without stating how they were measured.
- Saying "correlation does not equal causation" without naming a plausible third variable.
- Confusing Gibson's bottom-up theory with Gregory's top-down theory.
