These notes condense section 11.3, Fuels, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). All seven outcomes are required for every candidate. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [fuels study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-fuels/); then try the [fuels practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-fuels-practice/).

## The seven outcomes in one line each

1. Fossil fuels: coal, natural gas and petroleum.
2. Methane is the main constituent of natural gas.
3. Hydrocarbons contain hydrogen and carbon **only**.
4. Petroleum is a **mixture** of hydrocarbons.
5. Petroleum is separated into useful fractions by fractional distillation.
6. Bottom to top of the column: decreasing chain length, higher volatility, lower boiling points, lower viscosity.
7. Uses of eight fractions, from refinery gas to bitumen.

## Fractional distillation step by step

1. Heat petroleum; most of it vaporises.
2. Vapour enters the bottom of the fractionating column.
3. Column: hot at the bottom, cool at the top (temperature gradient).
4. Vapours rise; each condenses where the temperature drops below its boiling point.
5. Fractions are drawn off at different heights; gases leave the top; bitumen collects at the base.

Each fraction is a **mixture** of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points and similar chain lengths.

## The column at a glance

```
           ┌───────┐ → refinery gas
  cool     │       │ → gasoline / petrol
   ↑       │       │ → naphtha
   │       │       │ → kerosene / paraffin
   │       │       │ → diesel oil / gas oil
   │       │       │ → fuel oil
  hot      │       │ → lubricating oil
 vapour →  └───────┘ → bitumen
```

Shorter chains, higher volatility, lower boiling points and lower viscosity towards the top.

## Fractions and uses

| Fraction | Use |
|---|---|
| Refinery gas | gas for heating and cooking |
| Gasoline / petrol | fuel for cars |
| Naphtha | chemical feedstock |
| Kerosene / paraffin | jet fuel |
| Diesel oil / gas oil | fuel for diesel engines |
| Fuel oil | fuel for ships and home heating systems |
| Lubricating oil | lubricants, waxes and polishes |
| Bitumen | making roads |

## Why the trends happen

| Longer chain means... | Because... |
|---|---|
| higher boiling point | stronger forces between molecules need more energy to overcome |
| lower volatility | molecules escape from the liquid less easily |
| higher viscosity | stronger forces between long chains make the liquid thicker |

## Linking property to use

| Fraction | Property | Why it suits the use |
|---|---|---|
| Refinery gas | gas at room temperature | can be piped or bottled and burned in cookers |
| Gasoline | highly volatile | vaporises easily to mix with air in an engine |
| Naphtha | medium chain length | raw material for making other chemicals |
| Lubricating oil | viscous | stays between moving parts |
| Bitumen | very viscous, solid when cool | binds stones in road surfaces |

## A four-mark description in four sentences

"Petroleum is heated until most of it vaporises. The vapour passes into a fractionating column, which is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top. As the vapours rise, each hydrocarbon condenses at the height where the temperature falls below its boiling point. Fractions with low boiling points are collected near the top and those with high boiling points near the bottom."

Each sentence carries one idea an examiner looks for: vaporising, temperature gradient, condensing at the boiling point, and the order of collection.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fossil fuel | fuel formed from the remains of ancient organisms: coal, natural gas, petroleum |
| Hydrocarbon | compound containing hydrogen and carbon only |
| Petroleum | a mixture of hydrocarbons (crude oil) |
| Fractional distillation | separation of a mixture of liquids by their different boiling points, using a fractionating column |
| Fraction | a mixture of hydrocarbons with boiling points in a similar range |
| Volatility | how easily a liquid evaporates |
| Viscosity | how thick a liquid is; how slowly it flows |
| Chemical feedstock | a raw material used to make other chemicals |

## Exam traps

- "Hydrogen and carbon **only**": without "only" the definition does not score.
- Petroleum and each fraction are **mixtures**, not pure compounds.
- Column: **hot at the bottom**, cool at the top.
- Volatility and viscosity move in **opposite** directions: high volatility goes with low viscosity.
- Explain position by **boiling point and condensation**, not by mass or density.
- Naphtha is a **feedstock**, not a car fuel.
- Kerosene is **jet fuel**; diesel oil is for **diesel engines**; fuel oil is for **ships and home heating**.

## Self-test

1. Name the three fossil fuels.
2. Name the main constituent of natural gas.
3. Define *hydrocarbon*.
4. Why does petroleum need to be separated before use?
5. Where in the column is the temperature highest?
6. Explain why a fraction condenses at a particular height in the column.
7. State how volatility changes from the bottom to the top of the column.
8. Which fraction is used as a chemical feedstock?
9. Name the fraction used for jet fuel.
10. Name the fraction used for making roads.
11. Explain why lubricating oil has a higher viscosity than gasoline.
12. Is the gasoline fraction a pure substance? Explain.

## Answers

1. Coal, natural gas and petroleum.
2. Methane.
3. A compound that contains hydrogen and carbon only.
4. It is a mixture with few uses as it is; separation gives fractions with useful properties.
5. At the bottom.
6. The vapour rises until it reaches a level where the temperature is below its boiling point, then condenses.
7. Volatility increases (becomes higher).
8. Naphtha.
9. Kerosene / paraffin.
10. Bitumen.
11. Its molecules have longer chains, so the forces between molecules are stronger and the liquid is thicker.
12. No: it is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points.
