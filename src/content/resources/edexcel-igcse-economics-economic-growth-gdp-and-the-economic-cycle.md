## Economic growth and GDP

**Economic growth** means a country is producing more goods and services than before. It is measured by the percentage increase in **gross domestic product (GDP)**, the total value of the goods and services produced within a country in a given period, usually a year.

The growth rate is:

growth rate (%) = (new GDP - old GDP) / old GDP × 100

Use **real** GDP, which is adjusted for price changes. **Nominal** GDP uses the prices of the year concerned, so a general rise in prices lifts it even when output is flat.

### Worked example 1: nominal and real growth

Country Alder (illustrative) has nominal GDP of 600 billion currency units in year 1 and 645 billion in year 2. Its price index rises from 100 to 104.

```
Nominal growth = (645 - 600) / 600 × 100 = 7.5%
Year 2 GDP at year 1 prices = 645 / 1.04 = 620.19 billion
Real growth = (620.19 - 600) / 600 × 100 = 3.37%, about 3.4%
```

Only about 3.4% is extra output. The rest of the 7.5% is higher prices.

### GDP per head

Total GDP says little about the average person if the population is also growing. **GDP per head** = GDP / population.

### Worked example 2: GDP per head

Country Brill (illustrative) has real GDP of 1,200 billion and 40 million people in year 1. In year 2, real GDP is 1,260 billion and the population is 40.8 million.

```
Year 1 per head = 1,200 billion / 40 million = 30,000
Year 2 per head = 1,260 billion / 40.8 million = 30,882
GDP growth = 5%; population growth = 2%
Growth in GDP per head = (30,882 - 30,000) / 30,000 × 100 = 2.94%
```

Output grew 5% but the average person gained only about 2.9%.

## Limitations of GDP as a measure of growth

GDP is a useful starting point, but it can overstate or hide what is happening to people.

- **Inflation.** Nominal GDP exaggerates growth (example 1).
- **Population.** Total GDP ignores the number of people sharing it (example 2).
- **Unrecorded activity.** Work paid in cash without being reported, and unpaid work such as childcare at home, is left out. GDP understates output. If unrecorded activity were 15% of the 600 billion recorded, a further 90 billion would be missing.
- **Distribution.** An average hides inequality (see the household example below).
- **Environment and resources.** Pollution and depleted resources are not subtracted.
- **What is produced.** GDP counts a factory and a hospital alike. It does not show quality, leisure time or health.
- **Data quality.** Figures may be estimated and later revised.

Use these as chains: state the limitation, explain why it distorts GDP, then say what it means for judging living standards.

## The economic cycle

Growth is not steady. Output tends to rise faster and slower in turn around a long-run trend. This pattern is the **economic cycle**.

```
Real GDP
  ^
  |            BOOM                      BOOM
  |           ******                    ******
  |         **      **                **
  |       **          ** DOWNTURN   **
  |     **              **        **   RECOVERY
  |   **                  **    **
  | **  RECOVERY            ****
  |*                      RECESSION (trough)
  +---------------------------------------------> Time
```

The vertical axis is real GDP, the horizontal axis is time. The curve rises to a peak (boom), falls through the downturn into the recession, bottoms out at the trough, then climbs in recovery. A long-run trend line, sloping upwards, runs through the middle.

| Stage | Growth | Inflation | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | High, at or above trend | Rising; demand may outrun supply | Low |
| Downturn | Positive but slowing | Rising more slowly, then easing | Starts to rise |
| Recession | Negative; output falls (commonly, two quarters in a row) | Low or falling | High and rising |
| Recovery | Turns positive, then speeds up | Low at first, then rising | High but starting to fall |

Unemployment usually reacts after output because firms hesitate to hire again until they are sure demand will last.

### Worked example 3: reading the cycle from data

Country Cedar (illustrative):

| Year | Real GDP growth % | Inflation % | Unemployment % |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.8 | 4.6 | 3.9 |
| 2 | 2.1 | 3.4 | 4.1 |
| 3 | -1.3 | 1.2 | 5.8 |
| 4 | -0.6 | 0.4 | 7.2 |
| 5 | 1.9 | 0.9 | 7.0 |
| 6 | 3.5 | 1.7 | 6.1 |

- Year 1 is a **boom**: high growth and inflation, low unemployment.
- Year 2 is a **downturn**: still growing, but slower.
- Years 3 and 4 are a **recession**: output falls both years. Over the two years it falls by 1 - (0.987 × 0.994) = 1.89%.
- Years 5 and 6 are **recovery**: growth returns and unemployment falls from its peak of 7.2%. Output regains its year 2 level during year 6.

## The impact of economic growth

### Employment

More output usually needs more workers, so employment rises. But the effect depends on how much extra output comes from higher output per worker.

Country Dune (illustrative): output rises 4% while output per worker rises 1.5%. Employment rises by 1.04 / 1.015 - 1 = 2.46%. If employment was 10.0 million, it becomes about 10.25 million. If output per worker rose by the full 4%, employment would not change at all: growth without new jobs.

### Standards of living

Higher real GDP per head lets people buy more goods and services, and gives government more tax revenue for health and education. Growth raises living standards only if it is spread widely and does not damage health or the environment.

Take five households in Country Ember (illustrative):

| | H1 | H2 | H3 | H4 | H5 | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income before | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 100 | 40 |
| Income after | 10 | 20 | 32 | 46 | 132 | 48 |

The mean rises 20%, yet H1 and H2 gain nothing. H5's share of income rises from 50% to 55%. The poorest household falls from 25% of the mean to 20.8% of it.

### Poverty

Growth can reduce poverty by creating jobs and raising tax revenue that pays for benefits and services. But as the Ember table shows, poverty may stay or become worse relative to others if the gains go mostly to the better off.

### Productive potential

Growth that comes from more or better resources (more workers, capital, improved skills, technology) raises **productive potential**, the maximum output the economy can produce. On a production possibility frontier, this is an outward shift. If the frontier moves from maximum 100 consumer goods or 50 capital goods to 110 and 55, potential output has risen by 10%.

Growth can also happen by using idle resources. The economy then moves from a point inside the frontier towards it, and the frontier does not move. See the [PPF page](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-scarcity-opportunity-cost-the-ppf-and-economic/) for the diagram.

### Inflation

If spending grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce, firms and workers can raise prices. Suppose total spending rises 6% but potential output only 3%: the gap, 1.06 / 1.03 - 1 = 2.9%, pushes up prices. Growth that matches rising capacity need not cause inflation.

### The environment

More production uses more resources and may create more pollution and waste. Much depends on the technology used. Output up 4% with emissions per unit of output down 1% means total emissions rise by 1.04 × 0.99 - 1 = 2.96%. If emissions per unit fall by 5%, total emissions fall by 1.2%.

### Worked example 4: an evaluation chain

Question: evaluate whether faster growth in Country Ember makes its people better off.

1. **For:** real output per head is higher, so average households can buy more. More jobs and more tax revenue can fund schools and clinics.
2. **Against:** the table shows the poorest two households gain nothing, so the average overstates the typical gain. Higher output may bring more pollution, and long working hours are not captured in GDP.
3. **Judgement:** growth helps most when it is real (adjusted for prices), when it exceeds population growth, and when the gains reach low-income households. In the short run, who gains matters most. Over the long run, the cost to the environment may matter more. Finish by saying which factor your conclusion depends on.

## Common errors

- Using nominal GDP and calling it growth.
- Saying "GDP falls" when only the growth rate has slowed. In a downturn output is still rising.
- Treating a recession as a fall in prices. It is a fall in output.
- Mixing up the two routes to growth: using idle resources moves the economy towards its frontier, while better or more resources move the frontier outwards.
- Listing limitations without saying how each one distorts the figure.
- Writing "growth is good" with no evaluation. Say who gains, who loses, and over what time period.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is economic growth measured?**
Economic growth is measured by the percentage increase in real gross domestic product (GDP), the total value of goods and services produced within a country in a period, usually a year. Real GDP is adjusted for price changes, so it shows the change in output rather than in prices.

**Why is GDP per head used instead of total GDP?**
Total GDP ignores how many people share the output. If population grows, GDP per head (GDP divided by population) rises more slowly than total GDP, so it gives a better guide to what the average person gains.

**Does economic growth always create jobs?**
Not always. If much of the extra output comes from higher output per worker, employment may rise only a little or not at all, giving growth without new jobs.

**Does economic growth always reduce poverty?**
Growth can reduce poverty by creating jobs and raising tax revenue for benefits and services. But if the gains go mainly to better-off households, the poorest may gain little and inequality can widen, so an average rise can overstate the typical gain.
