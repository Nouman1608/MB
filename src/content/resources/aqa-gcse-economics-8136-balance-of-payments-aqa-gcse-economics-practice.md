These questions cover **section 3.2.2.5 Balance of payments** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: the balance of trade and the current account, surpluses and deficits, and policies to influence them. There are no tiers. All countries and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [balance of payments study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-balance-of-payments-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [balance of payments revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-balance-of-payments-aqa-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a UK export of services? **[1]**

A: A UK resident pays for a hotel in Rome

B: A Brazilian firm pays a UK law firm for legal advice

C: A UK supermarket buys bananas from Ecuador

D: The UK government sends aid to another country

**2.** Which **one** of the following is recorded as a primary income debit for the UK? **[1]**

A: Dividends paid by a UK company to shareholders in Canada

B: UK exports of medicines to the USA

C: Money sent to UK families by relatives working abroad

D: Spending by UK tourists in Greece

**3.** State what is meant by a current account deficit. **[1]**

**4.** Country N's figures, in billion units, are: exports of goods 330, imports of goods 372, exports of services 205, imports of services 151, primary income credits 40 and debits 52, secondary income credits 6 and debits 19.

**(a)** Calculate the balance of trade. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the current account balance and state whether it is a deficit or a surplus. **[2]**

**(c)** Country N's GDP is 2,400 billion units. Calculate the current account balance as a percentage of GDP, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**5.** An invented country has a current account surplus of 8. Its goods balance is −15, its services balance +30 and its net secondary income −4. Calculate its net primary income. **[2]**

**6.** A current account deficit falls from 45 billion to 36 billion. Calculate the percentage change in the size of the deficit. **[2]**

**7.** State whether each transaction is a credit or a debit on the UK current account. **[4]**

- A Canadian tourist pays for a theatre ticket in London.
- A UK resident pays a subscription to a US-based music service.
- A UK pension fund receives interest on bonds issued abroad.
- A UK firm imports steel from India.

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why a country may have a current account surplus. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a current account deficit financed by borrowing from abroad may be more worrying than one financed by foreign firms building factories in the country. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in income tax could reduce a current account deficit, and **one** conflict with another government objective. **[4]**

**11.** Country Q's current account deficit has grown from 3.5% of GDP to 5.0% of GDP over four years. Its inflation rate has been higher than that of its main trading partners, consumer spending has risen strongly, and most of its imports are consumer goods.

**(a)** Identify **two** pieces of evidence that suggest the deficit is a cause for concern. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how inflation higher than that of trading partners contributes to the deficit. **[3]**

**12.** Country Q's government is considering two ways to reduce its deficit: allowing its currency to fall in value, or supply-side policies such as training and investment incentives. Evaluate which approach would be more effective. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Money flows into the UK for a service supplied by a UK firm. **[1]**

**2.** **A** [1]. Profits paid to foreign owners leave the UK as primary income. **[1]**

**3.** When the value of money flowing out on the current account (imports and income paid abroad) is greater than the value flowing in [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Goods: 330 − 372 = −42; services: 205 − 151 = +54 [1]. Balance of trade = **+12** [1].

**(b)** Primary income −12 and secondary income −13, so 12 − 12 − 13 = **−13** [1], a **deficit** [1].

**(c)** 13 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **0.5%** of GDP [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* A surplus on the balance of trade can sit alongside a current account deficit when income flows are negative.

**5.** −15 + 30 − 4 = +11 [1]. Primary income = 8 − 11 = **−3** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** (36 − 45) ÷ 45 × 100 [1] = **−20%**: the deficit shrank by 20% [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Canadian tourist: credit [1]. Music subscription: debit [1]. Interest from bonds abroad: credit [1]. Steel import: debit [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Any two developed: exports are price competitive, for example because inflation is lower than abroad [1], so foreign buyers purchase more of them [1]; strong non-price competitiveness such as quality and reputation [1] keeps export demand high even at high prices [1]; weak spending at home [1] keeps imports low [1]; fast growth in export markets [1] raises demand for the country's goods [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Borrowing adds to debt that must be repaid with interest [1], and interest payments are debits on primary income, which can widen future deficits [1]. Factories add to productive capacity and may produce exports later, so they can help reduce the deficit over time [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Higher income tax reduces disposable income [1], so households spend less, including on imports, which narrows the deficit [1]. Conflict: spending on domestic goods also falls [1], so growth slows and unemployment may rise [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Any two, one mark each: the deficit is large and rising as a share of GDP; it has lasted several years; imports are mainly consumer goods rather than machinery [1] [1].

**(b)** Higher inflation makes Country Q's goods dearer compared with foreign goods [1]. Foreign buyers buy fewer of its exports [1], and its own consumers switch to cheaper imports, so the deficit widens [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *A fall in the currency:*

- Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so spending switches towards domestic goods [1].
- It can act quickly [1].
- But dearer imports raise costs and prices, adding to Country Q's already high inflation, which could cancel out the gain in competitiveness [1] [1].

*Supply-side policies:*

- Training and investment raise productivity, lowering costs and improving quality [1], so the country becomes more competitive without raising inflation [1].
- But they take years to work and cost the government money [1].

*Judgement:* supply-side policies are more effective because the case shows a competitiveness problem caused by high inflation, which a currency fall would worsen [1]; a currency fall might help in the short term only if inflation is first brought under control [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the evidence in the case. High inflation is the key point here.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying foreign tourists' spending in the UK as an import.
- Forgetting to include primary and secondary income in the current account.
- Giving a balance with no "deficit" or "surplus".
- Calculating the percentage change from the new figure.
- Judging a deficit without using the size, duration, cause or financing shown in the data.
