These notes cover section 11.2 *Religion and social order* of Cambridge International A Level
Sociology (9699) Paper 4: functionalist and Marxist accounts of religion and the debate between
them. Full explanations are in the
[religion and social order study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-religion-and-social-order/);
exam-style questions are in the
[religion and social order practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-religion-and-social-order-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

Functionalists argue that religion maintains order by creating shared values and solidarity
(Durkheim), helping individuals cope with stress (Malinowski), supplying core values and meaning
(Parsons) and integrating diverse nations (Bellah). Marxists argue that religion maintains an
unequal order by legitimating the ruling class and compensating the oppressed with promises of an
afterlife. Both see religion as conservative. Neo-Marxists show it can also support resistance, and
interactionist and feminist critics argue that both theories ignore meaning and gender.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sacred / profane** | Set apart and treated with awe / ordinary and everyday |
| **Totem** | Sacred emblem of a clan, often an animal or plant |
| **Collective effervescence** | Intense shared emotion at collective ritual |
| **Collective conscience** | Shared norms, values and beliefs that make social life possible |
| **Social solidarity** | Integration and unity of society |
| **Cult of the individual** | Durkheim's predicted modern belief system centred on human rights |
| **Civil religion** | Sacred reverence for the nation (Bellah) |
| **Ideology** | Ideas that present ruling-class interests as natural or right |
| **Alienation** | Loss of control over one's life and labour |
| **Compensation** | Promise of reward in an afterlife for suffering now |
| **Halévy thesis** | Methodism helped prevent revolution in England |
| **Organic intellectuals** | Thinkers from the working class who develop alternative ideas (Gramsci) |
| **Relative autonomy** | Religion is partly independent of the economy (Maduro, 1982) |

## Functionalism

**Durkheim (1912) in six steps:** sacred/profane → totemism among Aboriginal Australian clans →
totem symbolises society → collective effervescence → collective conscience and solidarity →
cognitive categories of thought. Predicted a cult of the individual.

**Malinowski:** coping with stress and uncertainty; funeral rites reintegrate the bereaved.

**Parsons:** core values become norms; answers ultimate questions about suffering.

**Bellah:** civil religion unites diverse societies.

**Evidence:** national ceremonies; rites of passage; community support in crises.

**Criticisms**
- Worsley (1956): no sharp sacred/profane division among Aboriginal Australians; clans share totems.
- Religious conflict; Huntington (1996) on a clash of civilisations defined largely by religion
  (itself disputed).
- Fits small single-religion societies, not modern diversity.
- Ignores religion as a source of change.

## Marxism

**Marx (1844):** "the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world… the opium of
the people" — religion responds to real suffering but dulls awareness of its causes.

| Function | Example |
|---|---|
| Ideology | Divine right of kings |
| Compensation | Reward in the afterlife |
| Legitimation | Established churches allied with rulers |
| Product of alienation | Hopes projected onto a god |

**Lenin (1905):** "spiritual gin".

**Historical evidence:** Halévy thesis on Methodism; Thompson (1963) on Methodism disciplining
workers; caste hierarchy presented as sacred.

**Neo-Marxism**
- Engels: early Christianity as a movement of the oppressed.
- Gramsci: religion in the struggle for hegemony; clergy as organic intellectuals.
- Billings (1990): Kentucky miners' lay preachers supported unions; textile workers' churches were
  more conservative.
- Maduro (1982): relative autonomy.

**Criticisms:** ignores positive functions; assumes false consciousness; cannot explain religious
change easily; religion survives attempts to abolish it.

## Comparing the perspectives

- **Both:** structural, conservative views of religion.
- **Difference:** order for all (functionalism) versus order for the ruling class (Marxism).
- **Interactionist alternative:** Berger, *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) — religion as a canopy of
  meaning that makes the world feel ordered.
- **Feminist challenge:** both ignore patriarchy (12.2).
- **Diversity and secularisation:** weaken both (heading 12).

## Exam traps

- Explain "opium of the people"; do not just quote it.
- Give Durkheim's argument as steps, not a slogan.
- Both theories say religion is conservative; this is a key evaluation point.
- Compare the two, because the syllabus asks for strengths and limitations of both.
- Neo-Marxists are not the same as Marx.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish sacred from profane.
2. Why did Durkheim think worship of the totem was worship of society?
3. What is collective effervescence?
4. What did Worsley (1956) argue against Durkheim?
5. How does Malinowski explain religion's role in social order?
6. What is civil religion?
7. Explain Marx's idea of religion as compensation.
8. What did Lenin call religion?
9. What is the Halévy thesis?
10. What did Billings (1990) find?
11. Give one similarity between functionalist and Marxist views of religion.
12. How does Berger's idea of the sacred canopy differ from functionalism?

### Answers

1. Sacred things are set apart and treated with awe; profane things are ordinary and everyday.
2. Because the totem is the emblem of the clan, so it symbolises the society that existed before
   individuals and will outlast them.
3. Intense shared emotion at collective ritual, making people feel part of something greater.
4. That there is no sharp sacred/profane division among Aboriginal Australians and that clans share
   totems, so totems cannot simply symbolise one clan.
5. Religion helps individuals cope with stress and uncertainty, such as death, which might otherwise
   disrupt social life.
6. Sacred reverence for the nation and its symbols, which integrates a diverse society (Bellah).
7. Religion promises reward in an afterlife for suffering now, discouraging people from changing
   their situation.
8. "A kind of spiritual gin".
9. The argument that Methodism helped prevent a revolution in England by channelling working-class
   discontent into religion.
10. That among Kentucky coal miners religion supported union struggle, while among textile workers
    it was more conservative, so religion can support resistance or accommodation.
11. Both see religion as conservative, maintaining the existing social order.
12. Berger explains order through individuals' need for meaning, not society's needs.
