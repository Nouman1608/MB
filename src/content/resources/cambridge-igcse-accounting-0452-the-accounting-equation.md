This guide covers sub-topic **1.2 The accounting equation**. The syllabus lists three points: "assets, liabilities and owner's equity", "the accounting equation" and "how to apply the accounting equation". The examples follow one invented business, Juniper Lane Bakery, owned by Farah Idris. Amounts are in dollars.

## 1. The three elements

**Assets** are resources owned by the business that it expects to benefit from. **Non-current assets** are kept for use over more than one year, such as premises, machinery, vehicles, and fixtures and fittings. **Current assets** are expected to be used up or turned into cash within a year, such as inventory, trade receivables, other receivables, bank and cash.

**Liabilities** are amounts the business owes to people outside it. **Non-current liabilities** are due after more than one year, such as a loan repayable over several years. **Current liabilities** are due within a year, such as trade payables, other payables and a bank overdraft.

**Owner's equity** is the owner's interest in the business: the amount the business owes its owner. It starts as the capital the owner puts in, rises with profits and further capital, and falls with losses and drawings. The business entity concept explains why it is a claim *against* the business: the business and its owner are treated as separate.

**Classifying items at Juniper Lane Bakery**

| Item | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Ovens | Non-current asset | Used for several years |
| Flour and packaging | Current asset (inventory) | Used within weeks |
| A café that owes for bread supplied | Current asset (trade receivable) | Will be paid within the credit period |
| Bank overdraft | Current liability | Owed to the bank and repayable on demand |
| Loan from Farah's brother, repayable in 2031 | Non-current liability | Owed to someone outside the business, due after more than a year |
| Farah's private car | Not recorded | Belongs to Farah, not the business |
| Amount Farah has invested | Owner's equity | Owed by the business to its owner |

The brother's loan is a liability even though he is family: he is not the owner. The private car is left out entirely, again because of business entity.

## 2. The equation and its rearrangements

Everything the business has was provided either by outsiders or by the owner, so:

**Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity**

Any one element can be found from the other two:

- Owner's equity = Assets - Liabilities. Assets less liabilities is also called **net assets**, so owner's equity equals net assets.
- Liabilities = Assets - Owner's equity.

Over a period, owner's equity changes in a predictable way:

**Closing owner's equity = Opening owner's equity + Capital introduced + Profit - Drawings** (a loss is subtracted instead of profit being added).

## 3. Applying the equation to transactions

Every transaction changes at least two items, and the equation still balances afterwards. On 1 May Juniper Lane Bakery has equipment 14,000, inventory 2,500 and bank 6,500 (assets 23,000), trade payables 3,000, and owner's equity 20,000.

### Worked example 1: seven transactions in May

| | Transaction | Effect | Assets | Liabilities | Equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Start | | 23,000 | 3,000 | 20,000 |
| a | Buys flour on credit, 1,200 | Inventory +1,200; payables +1,200 | 24,200 | 4,200 | 20,000 |
| b | Sells bread costing 800 to a café on credit for 1,300 | Inventory -800; receivables +1,300; equity +500 | 24,700 | 4,200 | 20,500 |
| c | Pays wages by bank, 450 | Bank -450; equity -450 | 24,250 | 4,200 | 20,050 |
| d | Café pays 900 by bank transfer | Bank +900; receivables -900 | 24,250 | 4,200 | 20,050 |
| e | Farah takes cakes costing 300 for a family party | Inventory -300; equity -300 | 23,950 | 4,200 | 19,750 |
| f | Receives a bank loan of 5,000, repayable over four years | Bank +5,000; loan +5,000 | 28,950 | 9,200 | 19,750 |
| g | Pays a supplier 2,000 by bank transfer | Bank -2,000; payables -2,000 | 26,950 | 7,200 | 19,750 |

After every line, assets equal liabilities plus equity: at the end, 26,950 = 7,200 + 19,750.

**Reading the pattern.** Transactions fall into four kinds:

1. **Both sides rise** (a, f): an asset is gained by taking on a liability, or by the owner putting in capital.
2. **Both sides fall** (c, e, g): an asset is used to pay a liability, an expense or drawings.
3. **Assets change form** (d): one asset rises and another falls by the same amount, so totals do not move.
4. **Profit is earned** (b): assets rise by more than the cost of the goods given up, and the difference is added to equity.

**Checking equity.** Only profit or loss, capital introduced and drawings change owner's equity. Profit in May was 500 - 450 = 50 and drawings 300, so closing equity is 20,000 + 50 - 300 = 19,750, matching the table. Paying the supplier (g) and receiving the loan (f) did not change equity at all.

**Checking the individual balances.** Inventory 2,500 + 1,200 - 800 - 300 = 2,600; receivables 1,300 - 900 = 400; bank 6,500 - 450 + 900 + 5,000 - 2,000 = 9,950; equipment 14,000. Total 26,950. Payables 3,000 + 1,200 - 2,000 = 2,200; loan 5,000. Total 7,200.

## 4. Finding a missing figure

### Worked example 2: owner's equity from a list of balances

At 31 December 2026 the bakery's balances are: premises 45,000; fixtures 8,200; inventory 6,300; trade receivables 2,900; cash 250; bank overdraft 1,750; trade payables 4,100; loan repayable in 2032, 15,000.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Total assets | 45,000 + 8,200 + 6,300 + 2,900 + 250 | 62,650 |
| Total liabilities | 1,750 + 4,100 + 15,000 | 20,850 |
| Owner's equity | 62,650 - 20,850 | 41,800 |

The overdraft is the trap. It is money owed to the bank, so it goes with the liabilities, never with the assets.

## 5. Profit or loss from the change in owner's equity

If two positions are known, the equation shows the result for the period between them. Rearranging the formula in section 2:

**Profit (or loss) = Closing equity - Opening equity - Capital introduced + Drawings**

### Worked example 3: a year that made a loss

At 31 December 2027 the bakery has assets of 60,900 and liabilities of 22,300. During 2027 Farah introduced a further 3,000 and took drawings of 4,500. Opening equity is 41,800 from Worked example 2.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Closing equity | 60,900 - 22,300 | 38,600 |
| Change in equity | 38,600 - 41,800 | -3,200 |
| Remove capital introduced | -3,200 - 3,000 | -6,200 |
| Add back drawings | -6,200 + 4,500 | -1,700 |

The result is a **loss of 1,700**. Check: 41,800 + 3,000 - 1,700 - 4,500 = 38,600.

Why adjust for capital and drawings? Capital introduced raises equity without being earned, so it is taken out. Drawings lower equity without being an expense, so they are added back. What is left is the change caused by trading.

## Common mistakes

- Putting a bank overdraft with the assets because it "is in the bank".
- Treating drawings as an expense, or forgetting that goods taken for private use are drawings too.
- Recording a credit sale as an increase in equity equal to the full selling price; only the profit over cost increases equity.
- Using the total change in equity as the profit without adjusting for capital introduced and drawings.
- Recording the owner's private possessions as business assets.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** means "work out from given facts, figures or information". Write the rearranged equation first, then the figures, then the answer. A wrong final figure can still earn method marks if the working is visible.
- **State** the effect of a transaction by naming both items and the direction, for example: "inventory decreases by 300 and owner's equity decreases by 300".
- **Identify** questions often ask which transaction leaves total assets unchanged. Look for an asset changing form (a customer paying) or one liability replacing another.
- In multiple-choice questions, test each option against the equation rather than relying on memory: write the changes to assets, liabilities and equity beside each option.

**Annotated answer.** *Explain why the total of the assets always equals the total of the liabilities plus owner's equity.*

> All the resources the business has were supplied either by outsiders,
> who are owed liabilities, or by the owner, whose claim is owner's equity
> *(the two sources)*. Every transaction changes two items by the same
> amount, so the totals stay equal; for example, a loan of 5,000 raises bank
> and liabilities by 5,000 each *(duality, with a supporting example)*.
