This set covers section 7, The interdependent nature of business, of OCR GCSE (9-1) Business
(J204), Version 2.0 (January 2026). In the exam this section is tested through synoptic questions in
section B of the J204/02 paper, so most questions below ask you to link two or more functions. All
businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the
[interdependent nature of business study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-business-j204-interdependent-nature-of-business-ocr-gcse-business/)
and the [interdependent nature of business revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-business-j204-interdependent-nature-of-business-ocr-gcse-business-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Which of the following is **not** one of the four business functions named in the
specification? **[1]**

A: Operations &nbsp; B: Finance &nbsp; C: Marketing &nbsp; D: Research and development

**2.** A business has an opening cash balance of £3,000. Inflows for the month are £14,000 and
outflows are £15,200. What is the closing balance? **[1]**

A: £1,200 &nbsp; B: £1,800 &nbsp; C: £4,200 &nbsp; D: −£1,200

**3.** An investment costs £50,000 and is forecast to give an average annual profit of £7,500. What
is the average rate of return? **[1]**

A: 6.7% &nbsp; B: 7.5% &nbsp; C: 15% &nbsp; D: 25%

**4.** State **two** ways a business could reduce the risk of launching a new product. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain **one** way a decision by the human resources function to reduce staff numbers could
affect operations. **[2]**

**6.** Quillon Repairs has the following results.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £200,000 | £230,000 |
| Net profit | £18,000 | £18,400 |

**(a)** Calculate the net profit margin for each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the results show about the business's performance. **[2]**

**7.** Ashbury Pickles wins a contract to supply a supermarket chain, which will double its output.
The supermarket pays 60 days after delivery. Analyse how the contract could affect the finance
function. **[3]**

**8.** Brackenridge Gym is considering new equipment costing £36,000, forecast to make a total profit
of £10,800 over four years.

**(a)** Calculate the average rate of return. **[2]**
**(b)** Another project has an ARR of 12%. Explain **one** reason why the gym might still choose the
new equipment. **[2]**

**9.** Ferrous Lamps has fixed costs of £8,400 a month. It sells lamps at £35 each, and the variable
cost per lamp is £21.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The marketing team proposes a 10% price cut. Calculate the new break-even quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** effect on operations if the price cut leads to a large rise in sales. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how the risk of opening a second branch may differ for a business that started six
months ago compared with one that has traded for ten years. **[4]**

**11.** Pellmore Bakes plans to switch from batch production to flow production. Analyse the effect
of this decision on marketing **and** human resources. **[6]**

**12.** Tallowmere Candles has £22,000 in cash. It can choose **one** option:

- **Option A:** an online shop costing £15,000, forecast to make a total profit of £13,500 over three
  years.
- **Option B:** a new moulding machine costing £40,000, which would double output, forecast to make
  a total profit of £30,000 over three years.

Recommend which option Tallowmere Candles should choose. Use financial information and at least two
business functions in your answer. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **B: £1,800** [1]. 3,000 + 14,000 − 15,200 = 1,800. **[1]**

**3.** **C: 15%** [1]. 7,500 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 15. **[1]**

**4.** Any two from: carry out **market research** before launch [1]; **test the product** on a small
scale, for example in one area [1]. Also credit: keep a cash reserve; launch alongside existing
products rather than replacing them. **[2]**

**5.** With fewer staff, the business may **not be able to produce as much** or serve customers as
quickly [1], so output falls or quality suffers and orders may be delayed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the answer starts in HR and finishes in operations, which is what the question
asks.

**6. (a)** Year 1: 18,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **9%** [1]. Year 2: 18,400 ÷ 230,000 × 100 = **8%** [1].
**(b)** Revenue grew by 15% but the **net profit margin fell** [1], so costs rose faster than sales and
the business keeps less profit from each £1 of revenue, which it should investigate [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Doubling output means buying **more materials and paying more wages** now [1]. The supermarket
pays 60 days later, so **cash outflows come before inflows** [1], which could leave the business short
of cash, so finance may need to arrange an overdraft or other short-term funding [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Average annual profit = 10,800 ÷ 4 = £2,700 [1]; ARR = 2,700 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = **7.5%** [1].
**(b)** The equipment may **attract and keep members** [1], which supports the gym's marketing and
reputation in ways the ARR forecast does not capture [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Contribution = 35 − 21 = £14 [1]; 8,400 ÷ 14 = **600 lamps** [1].
**(b)** New price = £31.50, contribution = £10.50 [1]; 8,400 ÷ 10.50 = **800 lamps** [1].
**(c)** Operations would need to **produce more lamps** [1], which may need extra materials, overtime
or more capacity, otherwise orders could be delayed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* a price cut raises the break-even quantity, so sales must rise by a third (from
600 to 800 lamps) just to reach break-even again.

**10.** The new business has **less cash and no track record** [1], so if the second branch fails it
may not survive, making the risk greater [1]. The established business can use **retained profit or
reserves** and its experience of what customers want [1], so it can absorb a disappointing start more
easily [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Marketing: flow production gives **lower costs per unit** [1], so Pellmore could **cut prices**
to win customers [1], but there is **less variety** [1], so it may lose customers who want special
orders [1].
Human resources: machines replace some tasks, so the business may need **fewer staff** or staff with
**new skills** [1], and repetitive work may lower motivation, so HR must manage training and morale
[1]. **[6]**

**12.** Option A: ARR = (13,500 ÷ 3) ÷ 15,000 × 100 = 4,500 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = **30%** [1]. It can be
paid from the £22,000 cash, leaving £7,000 [1]. Marketing: it reaches **new customers** beyond the
local area [1]; operations must handle packing and delivery [1].
Option B: ARR = (30,000 ÷ 3) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **25%** [1]. It costs more than the cash available, so
finance would need a loan, adding interest and risk [1]. Doubling output only pays if marketing can
**find buyers** for the extra candles [1].
Recommendation: **Option A** [1], because it has the higher ARR, can be afforded without borrowing and
creates the extra demand that might justify Option B later [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* a recommendation needs a clear choice. Option B earns more profit in total, so an
answer that chooses B with a good reason, such as the owner's objective of growth, can also gain full
credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining one function when the question names two.
- Leaving out the units or the % sign in margin and ARR answers.
- Using the new price but the old contribution when recalculating break-even.
- Saying "profit is high so cash is fine".
- Recommending without using the figures given.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a synoptic question?**
A question that needs knowledge from different parts of the course, such as a finance decision that
affects marketing and human resources.

**Should I calculate even when the question does not ask me to?**
Where figures are given in a recommend or evaluate question, a quick calculation such as ARR usually
strengthens the judgement.
