These questions cover section 2.4 Motivation and rewards (points 2.4.1 and 2.4.2) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Use the [motivation and rewards study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-motivation-and-rewards/) and the [motivation and rewards revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-motivation-and-rewards-revision-notes/) first if you need them. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Give percentages to 1 decimal place and show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define job enrichment. **[2]**

**2.** Give two financial methods a business can use to motivate employees. **[2]**

**3.** State the level of Maslow's hierarchy of needs that each of these mainly meets.

**(a)** Regular team social events. **[1]**
**(b)** A permanent contract replacing a temporary one. **[1]**
**(c)** The chance for an experienced chef to design a new menu. **[1]**

**4.** A salesperson at Ferris Phones earns basic pay of 1,350 a month plus commission of 2.5% on sales. Her sales this month were 46,000. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate her commission. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate her total pay for the month. **[1]**
**(c)** Next month her sales fall by 20%. Calculate her total pay for that month. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one way in which a good reputation for motivating staff helps a business to attract employees. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one weakness of paying workers a piece rate. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one difference between hygiene factors and motivators in Herzberg's theory. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Ostley Textiles employs 30 machinists. Each made an average of 64 garments a day. The manager introduced job rotation and gave teams autonomy to organise their own work. Average output rose to 70 garments per machinist a day. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the extra garments produced each day by the whole workforce. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse how autonomy could have helped raise productivity at Ostley Textiles. **[4]**

**9.** Dev is a senior engineer at a large manufacturer. He is well paid, has a permanent contract and gets on well with his team, but he says he has stopped learning anything new. Analyse, using Maslow's hierarchy of needs, why a pay rise is unlikely to motivate Dev. **[4]**

**10.** Brisco Supermarkets has 900 employees. Staff turnover among its 300 checkout staff is high. Average checkout pay is 1,600 a month. The managers have two options.

- **Option A:** a 5% pay rise for all checkout staff.
- **Option B:** job rotation between checkouts, shelf filling and customer service, plus a 10% staff discount. Estimated cost: 95,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly cost of Option A. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify which option Brisco Supermarkets should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Tarrant Web Design has 12 designers. Many projects finish late. The owner is considering a bonus of 500 per designer each quarter, paid when all of that designer's projects finish on time. A manager suggests giving designers autonomy over their hours and which projects they take on instead.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum cost of the bonus scheme in one quarter. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the bonus scheme is the best way for Tarrant Web Design to motivate its designers. **[9]**

**12.** Lomond Hotels gives every employee a free meal on each shift. Each meal would cost an employee 6.00 to buy, but costs the hotel 2.40 to provide. A typical employee works 20 shifts a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly value of the meals to a typical employee. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly cost to the hotel of providing the meals for one employee. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why fringe benefits such as free meals can help a business retain employees. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Giving a job more responsibility and more demanding or interesting tasks [1], so that it uses more of the employee's skills, for example planning their own work or checking its quality [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not define it as "giving more tasks". Moving between tasks at the same level is job rotation; enrichment adds depth and responsibility.

**2.** Any two of: remuneration (wage or salary), bonus, commission, promotion, fringe benefits [1 each]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Social (belonging) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Safety [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Self-actualisation [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) could be argued as esteem if the case stressed recognition, but designing a menu uses the chef's creative potential, which is self-actualisation.

**4. (a)** 0.025 × 46,000 = **1,150** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 1,350 + 1,150 = **2,500** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** New sales = 46,000 × 0.8 = 36,800 [1]; pay = 1,350 + (0.025 × 36,800) = 1,350 + 920 = **2,270** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) shows why commission makes income unstable: a 20% fall in sales cuts her pay by 230.

**5.** Applicants hear that the business rewards staff well or offers interesting work [1], so more people apply for each vacancy and the business can choose the strongest candidates [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Workers are paid for the number of items they make [1], so they may rush to produce more [1], which can lower quality and increase waste or returns [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Other valid weaknesses: pay falls when machines break down, which is not the worker's fault, or it does not suit work where output is hard to count.

**7.** Hygiene factors, such as pay and working conditions, cause dissatisfaction when they are poor [1]. Improving them only removes dissatisfaction and does not motivate [1], whereas motivators, such as recognition and responsibility, come from the job itself and do create motivation [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** (70 − 64) ÷ 64 × 100 [1] = **9.4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 30 × (70 − 64) = **180** garments a day [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** With autonomy, teams decide how to organise their own work [1], so they can arrange tasks in the order that suits them and remove delays they have noticed [1]. Being trusted makes machinists feel responsible for the result, which Herzberg would see as a motivator [1], so they put in more effort and each produces more garments a day [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Dev's physiological and safety needs are met because he is well paid and has a permanent contract [1], and his social needs are met because he gets on well with his team [1]. According to Maslow, a need that is already met no longer motivates, so more pay targets a level he has already passed [1]. His unmet need is self-actualisation, shown by his wish to learn, so new challenges or projects would motivate him more than money [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** 5% of 1,600 = 80 a month per employee [1]; 300 × 80 × 12 = **288,000** a year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For Option A: higher pay helps retain staff who might leave for better-paid jobs [1], and it is simple to introduce [1]. Against Option A: it costs 288,000 a year, which is over three times Option B [1], and if pay is not the reason staff leave, the money may not cut turnover [1]. For Option B: rotation reduces the boredom of checkout work and the discount is valued by staff who shop there, at a cost of 95,000 [1]. Judgement: for example, choose Option B because it is far cheaper and tackles repetitive work, which is a likely cause of turnover in checkout roles, unless research shows staff leave mainly over pay [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two costs directly. A judgement that says what further information would change the decision shows good evaluation within a "justify" answer.

**11. (a)** 12 × 500 = **6,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** For the bonus: it links reward directly to the problem, late projects [1], so designers have a clear target and a reason to plan their time better [1]. It costs at most 6,000 a quarter, which is only paid if projects are on time, so the business pays for results [1]. Against the bonus: designers might rush work to meet deadlines, lowering quality and upsetting clients [1]. Projects may be late for reasons outside a designer's control, such as clients sending material late, so missing the bonus could demotivate [1]. For autonomy: designers are skilled creative workers, and freedom over hours and projects meets Herzberg's motivators such as responsibility [1], so they may manage their own time better without extra cost [1]. Against autonomy: some designers may choose easy projects, leaving difficult ones unassigned [1]. Judgement: for example, autonomy is likely to suit creative designers better than a Taylor-style reward, but the owner could combine it with clear deadlines agreed with each designer [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer notices the type of work. Taylor's ideas fit counted, repetitive output; creative work suits methods built on responsibility and trust.

**12. (a)** 20 × 6.00 = **120** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 20 × 2.40 = **48** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Employees would lose the benefit if they left for a job without it [1], so leaving would effectively reduce their income [1], which gives them a reason to stay and reduces the cost of recruiting replacements [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a theory with no link to the worker or business in the question.
- Treating commission and bonus as the same.
- Mixing up the Maslow levels, especially social and esteem.
- Calculating a percentage change on the new figure instead of the original.
- Recommending a costly option without comparing it with the cheaper one.
