This guide covers section 2.3 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "The purpose and use of various forms of representation, their advantages and disadvantages". The forms in question are the tables of section 2.1 and the diagrams named in section 2.2. Questions here are mostly written: choose a representation, justify it, state an advantage or disadvantage, or criticise a diagram someone else has drawn. Marks go to answers that are specific to the data in the question.

## Start from the purpose

Before naming a diagram, decide what the reader needs to see. Most questions fit one of six purposes.

| Purpose | Forms that suit it |
|---|---|
| Show how one whole divides into parts | Pie chart; percentage bar chart (one bar) |
| Compare amounts across categories | Bar chart; pictogram; table |
| Compare two or more groups across the same categories | Dual bar chart (amounts); percentage bar chart or comparative pie charts (proportions); sectional bar chart (totals and parts) |
| Show overlap between categories | Venn diagram; two-way table |
| Show the shape of a small numerical data set while keeping every value | Stem-and-leaf diagram (back-to-back for two sets) |
| Compare centre and spread of numerical data sets | Box-and-whisker diagrams on a common scale |

Then check the data type: qualitative categories suit pies, bars and pictograms; numerical data suit stem-and-leaf and box-and-whisker diagrams.

## Each form: strengths and weaknesses

**Tables.** Exact values are kept and can be used in calculations, and two-way tables cross-classify. But patterns are slower to see than in a diagram, and a large table is hard to scan.

**Pictograms.** Eye-catching and easy for a general audience. Fractions of symbols are hard to draw and to read, so values are approximate, and the key must be read before the picture makes sense.

**Pie charts.** Show each category as a share of the whole at a glance, especially one dominant share. Exact frequencies cannot be read without the total; small differences between angles are hard to judge; too many categories make thin, unreadable sectors.

**Comparative pie charts.** Show both the shares within each group and, through circle area, which group is larger. The difference in total size is hard to judge by eye, and comparing the amount in a sector across two charts needs calculation.

**Bar charts.** Heights are easy to compare and values can be read from the scale. They show amounts, not proportions of a whole, and are unsuitable for continuous data (use a histogram).

**Dual bar charts.** Compare two groups category by category. With many categories or more than two groups the chart becomes crowded, and the totals of each group are not shown directly.

**Sectional bar charts.** Show each group's total and how it is made up. Only the bottom section shares a common baseline, so the middle and top sections are hard to compare between bars.

**Percentage bar charts.** Compare proportions between groups of different sizes on equal footing. The actual totals are lost, so a large share of a small group can look as important as the same share of a large one.

**Venn diagrams.** Show "both", "either" and "neither" counts clearly. They become cluttered beyond three sets and show only counts of categories, not measurements.

**Stem-and-leaf diagrams.** Keep every original value while showing the shape, and the ordered leaves make the median and quartiles easy to find. They are impractical for large data sets.

**Box-and-whisker diagrams.** Summarise median, quartiles and extremes, and are excellent for comparing several data sets on one scale. Individual values, the mean and the number of observations are not shown, and two very different data sets can give the same box.

## Head-to-head comparisons

| Choice | Prefer the first when ... | Prefer the second when ... |
|---|---|---|
| Pie chart or bar chart | The aim is the share of a whole | Exact values or small differences matter |
| Dual bar or percentage bar chart | Group sizes are similar and amounts matter | Group sizes differ and proportions matter |
| Sectional or percentage bar chart | Totals matter as well as parts | Only proportions matter |
| Comparative pie charts or percentage bar chart | The difference in total size should be visible | Shares must be compared accurately |
| Stem-and-leaf or box-and-whisker | Raw values must be kept; small data set | Several sets are compared; large data set |
| Table or diagram | Exact values are needed for calculation | A pattern must be seen quickly |

## Representations that mislead

A disadvantage can come from the way a form is used.

- **Pictures scaled in two directions.** If a picture of a coin is drawn twice as tall to show twice the value, it is also twice as wide, so its area is four times as large and the increase looks much bigger.
- **Bar charts whose axis does not start at zero.** A small difference looks large.
- **Pictograms with symbols of different sizes or spacing.** Row length no longer matches frequency.
- **Pie charts with very many sectors**, or with no total given, so frequencies cannot be recovered.
- **Comparative pie charts drawn the same size** for different totals, hiding the difference in size.
- **Uneven scales**, for example steps of 10 then 50 on the same axis.

## Worked example 1: choosing and justifying

Two families record how they spend their monthly income. Family A spends 2400 units of currency and Family B spends 6000. The aim is to compare how each family divides its spending between food, housing, transport and other.

**Model answer.** A percentage bar chart, with one bar for each family, is suitable because the families' totals are very different, and the aim is to compare the proportions spent on each item, not the amounts. Comparative pie charts would also show the proportions, with the larger circle showing Family B's larger total, but small differences in sector angle are harder to judge than section heights.

The answer names a form, links it to the aim (proportions) and to a feature of the data (different totals), and acknowledges an alternative.

## Worked example 2: advantage in context

"Give one advantage of the stem-and-leaf diagram over the grouped frequency table for the 25 race times."

Weak: "It is easier to read."
Strong: "The stem-and-leaf diagram keeps each of the 25 individual times, so the median can be found exactly, whereas the grouped table only allows an estimate."

## Worked example 3: criticising a diagram

A leaflet shows a bag of rice, then a second bag drawn twice as tall and twice as wide, labelled "Our sales have doubled".

**Model answer.** The second picture is enlarged by a factor of 2 in both height and width, so its area is 4 times the first. Readers judge the size of a picture by its area, so sales appear to have quadrupled rather than doubled. The bags should be drawn the same size, with two bags in place of one, as in a pictogram with a key.

## Common errors

- Naming a diagram with no reason.
- Giving reasons that would apply to any diagram ("it is clear", "it looks nice").
- Recommending a pie chart to compare amounts between groups of different sizes.
- Recommending a stem-and-leaf diagram for hundreds of values.
- Forgetting that a box-and-whisker diagram hides the number of observations.
- Treating a percentage bar chart as if it shows totals.

## Exam technique

- **Suggest a suitable diagram**: name it and give one reason linked to the aim or the data.
- **Give an advantage of X over Y**: state something X shows that Y does not, using the context.
- **Give a disadvantage**: state what is lost or hard to judge.
- **Criticise**: point to the exact feature (axis starts at 40; symbols different widths) and its effect on the reader.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there one correct diagram for each data set?**
Often several are acceptable. Credit goes to a sensible choice with a reason tied to the purpose and the data.

**When should a pie chart not be used?**
When there are many categories, when exact values matter, or when amounts in groups of different size are being compared.

**Why might a table be better than a diagram?**
A table keeps exact values, which are needed for calculations, and can show two classifications at once.
