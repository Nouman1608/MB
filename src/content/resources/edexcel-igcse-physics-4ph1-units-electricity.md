This guide covers sub-topic **2(a) Units** of Topic 2, Electricity, in the
Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. Statement
**2.1** asks you to use the following units: ampere (A), coulomb (C),
joule (J), ohm (Ω), second (s), volt (V) and watt (W). It has no "P", so
it is assessed on both papers. For the circuit physics that uses these
units, see the
[Electricity study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-electricity/).

## The seven units

| Unit | Symbol | Quantity | Equation that links it |
|---|---|---|---|
| ampere | A | current, I | Q = I × t |
| coulomb | C | charge, Q | Q = I × t |
| joule | J | energy transferred, E | E = I × V × t, E = Q × V |
| ohm | Ω | resistance, R | V = I × R |
| second | s | time, t | Q = I × t, E = I × V × t |
| volt | V | voltage (potential difference), V | V = I × R, E = Q × V |
| watt | W | power, P | P = I × V |

Notice that **V** is both the symbol for the quantity voltage and the
symbol for its unit, the volt. In "V = 6.0 V" the first V is the quantity
and the second is the unit. Similarly W is the unit watt, but in Topic 1
W is used as the symbol for weight. Read each equation in context.

## What each unit means

**The coulomb** is the unit of charge. Electric current in a metal wire is
a flow of electrons, each carrying a tiny negative charge, so a coulomb is
the charge of a very large number of electrons.

**The ampere** is the unit of current, the rate of flow of charge. From
Q = I × t:

```
1 A = 1 C/s     (one coulomb passing a point each second)
```

**The volt** is the unit of voltage, the energy transferred per unit
charge. Statement 2.20 says you must know that the volt is a joule per
coulomb:

```
1 V = 1 J/C     (one joule transferred for each coulomb that passes)
```

A 9 V battery gives 9 J to every coulomb that passes through it; a 1.5 V
cell gives 1.5 J.

**The ohm** is the unit of resistance. From V = I × R, R = V ÷ I:

```
1 Ω = 1 V/A     (one volt is needed to drive one ampere through it)
```

**The watt** is the unit of power, the rate of energy transfer:

```
1 W = 1 J/s
```

From P = I × V, the watt is also an ampere multiplied by a volt:
1 A × 1 V = (1 C/s) × (1 J/C) = 1 J/s = 1 W.

**The joule** is the unit of energy. In circuits, energy transferred =
current × voltage × time, so 1 J = 1 A × 1 V × 1 s.

## Prefixes you will meet

Electrical data often uses prefixes. Convert to the base unit before
substituting.

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| milli | m | × 10⁻³ | 250 mA = 0.250 A |
| micro | μ | × 10⁻⁶ | 40 μA = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ A |
| kilo | k | × 10³ | 2.2 kΩ = 2200 Ω; 3.0 kW = 3000 W |
| mega | M | × 10⁶ | 1.5 MJ = 1 500 000 J |

Times given in minutes or hours must be converted to seconds: 5 minutes =
300 s; 2 hours = 7200 s.

Take care with capital and small letters. "mA" (milliampere) is a
thousandth of an ampere; "MΩ" (megohm) is a million ohms. Writing "Ma" or
"ma" is ambiguous, so copy the case exactly.

## Worked example 1: a kettle

A kettle connected to 230 V mains takes a current of 9.0 A and is switched
on for 2.5 minutes. Calculate (a) its power in kW and (b) the energy it
transfers in kJ.

```
(a) P = I × V = 9.0 × 230 = 2070 W = 2.07 kW ≈ 2.1 kW
(b) t = 2.5 × 60 = 150 s
    E = I × V × t = 9.0 × 230 × 150 = 310 500 J ≈ 310 kJ
```

*Commentary.* The time is converted to seconds first. Converting the power
to kW is a separate step at the end; dividing by 1000 is easy to forget
when the question asks for kW. To two significant figures (matching 9.0 A)
the energy is 3.1 × 10⁵ J, which is 310 kJ.

## Worked example 2: a small current

A sensor draws 20 mA from a 5.0 V supply. Calculate its resistance and
the charge that flows in 1 hour.

```
I = 20 mA = 0.020 A
R = V / I = 5.0 / 0.020 = 250 Ω
t = 1 hour = 3600 s
Q = I × t = 0.020 × 3600 = 72 C
```

Using 20 instead of 0.020 would give R = 0.25 Ω, a thousand times too
small.

## Checking with units

Because the units are linked, you can check an answer by working through
its units.

- E = Q × V: C × J/C = J. Correct.
- E = I × V × t: (C/s) × (J/C) × s = J. Correct.
- P = I × V: (C/s) × (J/C) = J/s = W. Correct.
- If you calculate a "resistance" as V × I, the unit is W, not Ω, so the
  equation has been rearranged wrongly.

## Common mistakes

- Using mA, kΩ, kW or minutes in an equation without converting.
- Confusing coulombs (charge) with amperes (rate of flow of charge).
- Giving power in J or energy in W. A watt is a joule **per second**.
- Writing the symbol for ohm as "O" or "0", which can be misread. Write Ω,
  or the word "ohm".
- Mixing up the quantity V (voltage) with the unit V (volt) when
  rearranging, especially in E = Q × V.

## Exam technique with command words

- **State** the unit: give the name or symbol, for example "coulomb" or
  "C". If asked for a unit "in terms of other units", give a combination
  such as J/C.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, convert data, substitute, give the
  answer with a unit and to a sensible number of significant figures.
- **Show that**: work to at least one more significant figure than the
  printed value and include each conversion.
- **Give** or **state** the meaning of "1 volt": "one joule of energy is
  transferred per coulomb of charge passing".
