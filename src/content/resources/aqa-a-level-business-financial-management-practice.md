## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between cash flow and profit, and explain how a profitable business can still run short of cash. **[4]**

**2.** Jarrowby Furniture spends £96,000 on a new workshop that adds £11,520 to annual profit. Its long-term funding is £150,000 of loans and £450,000 of equity.

**(a)** Calculate the return on investment. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the proportion of long-term funding that is debt, before and after it borrows a further £100,000. **[3]**

**3.** Kirkmoor Bikes set a monthly budget and recorded actual results.

| Item | Budget (£) | Actual (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 60,000 | 57,000 |
| Materials | 21,000 | 21,900 |
| Wages | 15,000 | 14,100 |
| Other costs | 9,000 | 9,600 |

**(a)** Calculate each variance and state whether it is favourable or adverse. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit variance. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the materials variance should be investigated even though it is only 4.3% of budget. **[2]**

**4.** Tidmarsh Crafts begins January with £4,000.

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash in (£) | 18,000 | 15,000 | 21,000 | 24,000 |
| Cash out (£) | 19,500 | 17,500 | 16,000 | 18,500 |

**(a)** Calculate the net cash flow and closing balance for each month. **[4]**
**(b)** Analyse whether Tidmarsh needs to arrange short-term finance. **[3]**

**5.** Lanchester Lamps sells lamps at £24. Variable cost is £14 per lamp and fixed costs are £36,000. It plans to sell 4,200.

**(a)** Calculate break-even output. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the margin of safety and the profit at planned output. **[3]**
**(c)** Variable cost rises to £16. Calculate the new break-even output and describe the effect on a break-even chart. **[3]**
**(d)** State two limitations of break-even analysis. **[2]**

**6.** Trenwith Studios had revenue £480,000, cost of sales £288,000, operating expenses £134,400 and finance costs £9,600. Tax was 25% of profit before tax. Last year's margins were gross profit 37.0%, profit from operations 9.0% and profit for the year 5.1%.

**(a)** Calculate gross profit, profit from operations and profit for the year, with each margin. **[5]**
**(b)** Analyse the change in profitability. **[4]**

**7.** Fulbrook Foods sells £45,000 of goods a month to retailers who pay after 60 days. It buys £20,000 of ingredients a month and pays suppliers after 30 days. It is profitable and growing. Analyse the cash flow problems this could cause and one way to reduce them. **[6]**

**8.** **(a)** Define venture capital. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify one internal source of finance and state one disadvantage of it. **[2]**

**9.** Redmarley Garden Rooms needs £120,000 for a showroom that should add £15,000 a year to profit before interest. It has £60,000 of loans and £240,000 of equity. A bank offers a loan at 8% a year; alternatively it could issue new shares.

**(a)** Calculate debt as a proportion of long-term funding after each option. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify which source Redmarley should use. **[8]**

**10.** Denholme Joinery has revenue £300,000, variable costs £165,000 and fixed costs £105,000. Option A is a new supplier that cuts variable costs by 4%. Option B raises prices by 6%, which is expected to cut volume by 5%. Evaluate which option the owner should choose. **[9]**

**11.** Nunthorpe Courses sells online lessons in a market with several larger rivals. Analyse how two of technology, competition and ethical influences might shape its financial decisions. **[6]**

**12.** Explain two benefits of budgeting. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Cash flow is the movement of money into and out of the business [1]; profit is revenue minus costs, counted when earned or incurred [1]. A sale on credit counts as profit at once but the cash arrives later [1], while wages and suppliers must be paid first, causing a shortage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit is gained by showing the timing mechanism; saying cash and profit are different without a reason is a weaker answer.

**2. (a)** 11,520 ÷ 96,000 [1] × 100 = **12%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 150 ÷ (150 + 450) = **25%** [1]. After: debt 250, so 250 ÷ (250 + 450) [1] = **35.7%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding new borrowing to debt only, not to the total, is a common slip; show the formula.

**3. (a)** Revenue £3,000 adverse [1]; materials £900 adverse [1]; wages £900 favourable [1]; other costs £600 adverse [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Budgeted profit 60,000 − 45,000 = £15,000 [1]; actual 57,000 − 45,600 = £11,400, so **£3,600 adverse** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Revenue was below budget, so fewer sales should have meant lower materials use [1]; the overspend is therefore worse than it looks and may signal waste or higher supplier prices [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Labels must be judged by effect on profit; a larger cost figure is adverse even though the number is positive.

**4. (a)** Jan: net −1,500, closing 2,500 [1]. Feb: net −2,500, closing 0 [1]. Mar: net +5,000, closing 5,000 [1]. Apr: net +5,500, closing 10,500 [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The balance never goes negative [1], but it falls to exactly zero in February, leaving no margin if customers pay late [1]. An agreed overdraft is a cheap safeguard, so short-term finance should be arranged for February but may not be used [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Reading the numbers and then applying them to the decision earns more than restating the table.

**5. (a)** Contribution = 24 − 14 = £10 [1]; 36,000 ÷ 10 [1] = **3,600 lamps** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Margin of safety = 4,200 − 3,600 = **600** [1]; total contribution = 10 × 4,200 = 42,000 [1]; profit = 42,000 − 36,000 = **£6,000** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Contribution = £8 [1]; break-even = 36,000 ÷ 8 = **4,500** [1]. The total cost line is steeper and the crossing moves right, so planned output (4,200) is now below break-even, a loss of £2,400 [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Any two: it assumes all output is sold [1]; it assumes price and costs stay constant, ignoring demand reacting to price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each calculation needs its formula or working visible; units (lamps, £) are part of a complete answer.

**6. (a)** Gross profit £192,000, margin 40.0% [1]. Profit from operations 192,000 − 134,400 = £57,600, margin 12.0% [1]. Profit before tax = 57,600 − 9,600 = £48,000 [1]. Profit for the year = 48,000 × 0.75 = **£36,000** [1], margin **7.5%** [1]. **[5]**
**(b)** All three margins improved [1]. Gross margin rose three points (37.0% to 40.0%), suggesting better pricing or purchasing [1]. Operating margin also rose three points, so operating expenses stayed about 28% of revenue [1]. Profit for the year rose only 2.4 points, so finance costs and tax absorbed part of the gain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the three profit measures with each other, not just with last year, shows analysis.

**7.** Customers pay two months after sale, while ingredients are paid after one month [1]. In the first months Fulbrook pays £20,000 a month with no cash in, so it is £20,000 out of pocket by the end of month two [1]. As sales grow, the gap widens because more stock must be funded before payment arrives [1]. A profitable business can therefore run out of cash and may need an overdraft or have to refuse orders [1]. One solution is debt factoring, giving cash on invoices at once [1], though a fee reduces profit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A numbered use of the case figures turns a general cash flow answer into an analysed one.

**8. (a)** Venture capital is money invested by a specialist investor in a risky or growing firm [1] in return for a share of ownership [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Retained profits [1]; limited to profit already earned and not paid out [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Only an internal source scores in (b); naming an overdraft would not.

**9. (a)** Loan: debt 180 ÷ (180 + 240) = **42.9%** [1]. Shares: 60 ÷ (60 + 360) = **14.3%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The loan costs 8% × 120,000 = £9,600 a year, leaving £5,400 of the extra £15,000 [1]. Debt would rise to 42.9% and the interest is fixed whatever sales do [1], which is risky if showroom sales disappoint [1]. Shares carry no repayment or interest, so debt stays at 14.3% and cash is safer [1]. However, existing owners are diluted and may expect dividends [1], and issuing shares may not be possible for a small firm [1]. The loan keeps control, but the lower risk of shares suits an unproven showroom, particularly if the market is uncertain [1]. A balanced recommendation, such as shares or a smaller loan, backed by the figures [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned decision that weighs both options against this firm's risk and control wins more credit than a list of advantages.

**10.** Base profit = 300,000 − 165,000 − 105,000 = £30,000 (10.0% margin) [1]. Option A: variable costs 165,000 × 0.96 = £158,400, profit £36,600 (12.2%) [1]. Option B: revenue 300,000 × 1.06 × 0.95 = £302,100; variable costs 165,000 × 0.95 = £156,750; profit **£40,350** (13.4%) [1]. B raises profit by £10,350, A by £6,600 [1]. A depends on the supplier keeping its price and quality, and a cheaper supplier risks reliability [1]. B depends on the volume fall being no worse than 5%, and competitors may not follow [1]. B has higher potential, but a larger fall in volume would cut its advantage quickly [1]. Judgement: B if demand is not price sensitive, otherwise A [1], and both could be combined [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The calculations are the evidence; a judgement that names the assumption it depends on is what completes the evaluation.

**11.** Technology: online crowd funding or software lowers the cost of raising money or forecasting [1], allowing Nunthorpe to avoid a bank loan and interest [1]. Competition: larger rivals can cut prices, so Nunthorpe may need a budget for promotions or accept lower margins [1], and may be unable to match their spending [1]. Ethical influences, such as fair treatment of customers, may limit aggressive pricing or debt chasing [1]. Any two developed with a link to Nunthorpe's finances [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Two influences developed with consequences for finance outweigh three listed ones.

**12.** Budgets set targets, giving managers something to aim at [1], which helps focus effort [1]. They let managers control spending by comparing actual with budget [1], so variances can be investigated early [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each benefit needs a developed reason to receive both marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up cash flow and profit, or using one word for both.
- Forgetting to show the formula before substituting numbers.
- Labelling variances by size of number instead of effect on profit.
- Leaving out units, such as lamps, pounds or percentages.
- Not linking a finance source to the firm's need, risk and time horizon.
- Writing "evaluate" or "justify" answers with no judgement.
- Skipping the case figures in analysis questions.
- Rounding break-even output down.
