These questions cover outcomes 1.1.13 to 1.1.20 of Pearson Edexcel International A Level Accounting (YAC11), Unit 1: non-current asset depreciation, ledger accounts, disposals and the schedule of non-current assets. Review the [depreciation study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-non-current-asset-depreciation/) or the [depreciation revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-non-current-asset-depreciation-revision-notes/) first. All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars. Round to the nearest dollar.

## Section A

**1.** State two causes of depreciation and two reasons for charging depreciation. **[4]**

**2.** A machine is bought on 1 May for $27,000. It has a residual value of $3,000 and a useful life of 6 years. Depreciation is straight line, charged by months. The year ends on 31 December. Calculate the depreciation for the first year. **[2]**

**3.** Equipment costs $50,000 and is depreciated at 20% a year by the reducing balance method. Calculate the depreciation for each of the first three years and the carrying amount at the end of Year 3. **[3]**

**4.** Loose tools were valued at $5,200 at the start of the year. Tools costing $1,700 were bought during the year, and the tools were valued at $4,900 at the end. Calculate the depreciation for the year. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why charging depreciation does not provide money to replace an asset. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Hollins Print bought a machine for $32,000 on 1 January Year 1 and a second machine for $12,000 on 1 July Year 2. Machinery is depreciated at 10% of cost a year, straight line, charged by months. The year ends on 31 December.

Prepare the machinery account and the provision for depreciation account for Years 1 and 2. **[8]**

**7.** A vehicle bought on 1 April Year 1 for $24,000 is depreciated at 25% of cost a year, straight line, charged by months. It is sold on 30 September Year 3 for $9,500. The year ends on 31 December.

**(a)** Calculate the accumulated depreciation on the vehicle at the date of sale. **[3]**

**(b)** Prepare the disposal account. **[4]**

**8.** An old machine cost $18,000 and has accumulated depreciation of $11,000. It is traded in for a new machine priced at $26,000. The supplier allows $5,000 for the old machine and the balance is paid by bank transfer.

**(a)** Prepare the disposal account. **[4]**

**(b)** State the entries for the new machine in the machinery account. **[2]**

**9.** A delivery van costs $60,000, has a residual value of $6,000 and a useful life of 4 years. Profit before depreciation is $80,000 in each of Years 1 and 2.

**(a)** Calculate profit for Years 1 and 2 using (i) straight line and (ii) reducing balance at 45%. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain which method better suits a delivery van. **[2]**

**10.** Equipment cost $48,000 and has been depreciated by the straight line method over 8 years with no residual value for 3 years. At the start of Year 4 the business changes to reducing balance at 25%.

**(a)** Calculate the depreciation for Year 4 under the new method, and the effect of the change on Year 4 profit. **[3]**

**(b)** Comment on the change with reference to the consistency concept. **[2]**

**11.** Ashby Distribution provides the following for the year ended 31 December.

- Equipment: cost at 1 January $60,000; accumulated depreciation $24,000. Equipment costing $15,000 was bought during the year. No equipment was sold. Depreciation is 10% of cost of equipment held at the year end.
- Vehicles: cost at 1 January $40,000; accumulated depreciation $22,000. A vehicle that cost $16,000, with accumulated depreciation of $12,000, was sold. A new vehicle costing $25,000 was bought. Depreciation is 20% of the cost of vehicles held at the year end, with none in the year of disposal.

Prepare the schedule of non-current assets for the year. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the use of the revaluation method for a hotel's crockery and cutlery. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Causes, any two: wear and tear; passage of time; obsolescence; depletion; inadequacy [2]. Reasons, any two: to match cost against the revenue the asset helps earn (accruals); to give a fairer profit each year; to show a realistic carrying amount; prudence [2]. **[4]**

**2.** Annual charge (27,000 − 3,000) ÷ 6 = 4,000 [1]. First year: 4,000 × 8/12 = **$2,667** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Year 1 **10,000**; Year 2 **8,000** [1]; Year 3 **6,400** [1]. Carrying amount **$25,600** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** 5,200 + 1,700 − 4,900 [1] = **$2,000** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Depreciation is a **non-cash** expense: the entries debit an expense and credit a provision, with no movement of cash [1]. It reduces profit, and so may reduce drawings, but no cash is set aside, so money for replacement is not guaranteed [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** (i) Straight line: (60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 4 = 13,500, so profit **$66,500** in each year [1] [1]. (ii) Reducing balance: Year 1 charge 27,000, profit **$53,000** [1]; Year 2 charge 33,000 × 45% = 14,850, profit **$65,150** [1].

**(b)** **Reducing balance** [1], because a van loses most of its value and usefulness in its early years, and higher early charges are balanced by rising repair costs later [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Carrying amount at the start of Year 4: 48,000 − 3 × 6,000 = 30,000 [1]. Year 4 charge: 30,000 × 25% = **$7,500** [1]. Profit is **$1,500 lower** than under straight line ($6,000) [1].

**(b)** The change breaks consistency, so Year 4 profit is not directly comparable with earlier years [1]; it is acceptable only if reducing balance better reflects how the equipment is used, with the reason and effect disclosed [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Up to 4 marks:

- Crockery and cutlery are **many small items** that are frequently broken or lost, so tracking each item's cost and depreciation is impractical [1].
- The method captures **breakage and loss** as well as wear in a single charge [1].
- The valuation is **subjective**, so depreciation can vary from year to year and profit may be manipulated [1].
- **Judgement:** suitable, provided the valuation basis is applied consistently each year [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong part-year policy or forgetting the months before a disposal.
- Deducting residual value in reducing balance.
- Leaving the disposed asset's depreciation in the provision account.
- Recording a part-exchanged asset at the cash paid only.
- A schedule whose closing figures do not reconcile.
- Evaluations without a conclusion.
