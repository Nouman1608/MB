## Questions

**1.** Define a population, and state how reproductive isolation is used to tell two populations of one species apart. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why ecologists estimate population size by random sampling instead of counting every individual, and what is meant by sampling error. **[3]**

**3.** Twelve random quadrats, each 1 m², were placed in a meadow of 2400 m². Counts of one sessile plant species were: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 2, 4, 5, 1.
**(a)** Calculate the mean number per quadrat. **[1]**
**(b)** Estimate the population in the meadow. **[2]**
**(c)** The standard deviation is 1.86. Explain what this indicates about how the plants are spread. **[2]**

**4.** Researchers marked 60 shrews and released them. A later sample contained 50 shrews, of which 12 were marked.
**(a)** Calculate the Lincoln index estimate. **[2]**
**(b)** State two assumptions made. **[2]**
**(c)** Marks fade between samples. State the effect on the estimate. **[1]**

**5.** **(a)** Define carrying capacity. **[1]**
**(b)** State one resource that can limit it. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how one density-dependent factor returns a population above carrying capacity towards it. **[2]**

**6.** The number of duckweed fronds in a tank was counted daily: 10, 13, 17, 22, 29.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase from day 0 to day 1. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how to use a graph to test whether growth is exponential. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this growth cannot continue indefinitely. **[2]**

**7.** Yeast cells (millions per mL) at 4-hour intervals from 0 h to 24 h were: 2, 6, 18, 46, 70, 78, 80.
**(a)** Identify the interval of fastest growth and calculate the rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Estimate the carrying capacity and explain the plateau. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a sigmoid curve is a model, not a description of every real population. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Define mutualism. **[1]**
**(b)** For legume root nodules, state a benefit to each organism. **[2]**
**(c)** For zooxanthellae in hard corals, state a benefit to each organism. **[2]**
**(d)** Distinguish mutualism from parasitism. **[1]**

**9.** A grass species X was introduced to a region where a native grass Y grows. Researchers removed X from some plots and left others untouched, then measured Y.
**(a)** Outline how the experiment should be designed and what result would indicate competition. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the result would indicate but not prove competition. **[2]**

**10.** Two snail species were surveyed at 60 pond sites. Both present: 4. Only the first present: 21. Only the second present: 19. Neither present: 16.
**(a)** State a null hypothesis. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the expected number of sites with both present. **[2]**
**(c)** The other expected values are 15.42, 13.42 and 21.58. Calculate χ². **[2]**
**(d)** Using a critical value of 3.84, state the degrees of freedom and the conclusion. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the result does not prove competition. **[2]**

**11.** Records of snowshoe hare and Canada lynx show numbers rising and falling in cycles of about ten years.
**(a)** Describe the pattern shown by the two populations. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain this as delayed negative feedback. **[2]**
**(c)** Distinguish top-down from bottom-up control, and state which the hare cycle suggests may also matter. **[2]**

**12.** **(a)** State one similarity between allelopathy and antibiotic secretion. **[1]**
**(b)** Give one example of each. **[2]**
**(c)** State which kind of organisms produce each. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Individuals of one species found together in one area, which normally breed with each other [1]. Two populations are distinguished because little or no interbreeding occurs between them (reproductive isolation) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both the same-species, same-area idea and the breeding link; "a group of animals" alone is too vague.

**2.** Counting all individuals is too slow, costly or impossible, and numbers may change during the count [1]. Samples must be random to avoid bias in where you sample [1]. Sampling error is the difference between the estimate and the true population size [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason for estimating and a reason for randomness are separate points; give both.

**3. (a)** Total 36 ÷ 12 = **3.0 per quadrat** [1]
**(b)** Density 3 per m² [1]; 3 × 2400 = **7200 plants** [1]
**(c)** SD is large compared with the mean (1.86 against 3) [1], so counts vary a lot: plants are unevenly spread or clumped [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the standard deviation with the mean; a number alone says nothing about spread.

**4. (a)** 60 × 50 ÷ 12 [1] = **250 shrews** [1]
**(b)** Marked animals mix fully with the population [1]; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples [1] (marks must not harm the animals or fade is also valid)
**(c)** Fewer marked animals are recognised, so R is too low and the estimate is too high [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the assumption in full. Say what would break it, as in part (c), rather than only naming "closed population".

**5. (a)** The maximum population size an environment can sustain indefinitely [1]
**(b)** Food, water, light, space or nesting sites (any one) [1]
**(c)** Example: disease spreads more easily when density is high [1], so deaths rise and numbers fall back towards carrying capacity (negative feedback) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A density-dependent answer should say why the effect grows with density, then link to the return to carrying capacity.

**6. (a)** (13 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **30%** [1]
**(b)** Plot the number of fronds on a logarithmic vertical axis against time on a normal horizontal axis [1]. A straight line indicates exponential growth, as the percentage increase is constant [1]
**(c)** Space, light and nutrients become limiting [1]; density-dependent factors reduce growth until births equal deaths [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State which axis is logarithmic, and what a straight line means.

**7. (a)** 8 to 12 h [1]: (46 − 18) ÷ 4 = **7 million cells per mL per hour** [1]
**(b)** About 80 million per mL [1]; births roughly equal deaths because nutrients and space are limiting [1]
**(c)** Real populations may overshoot, oscillate or crash [1]; the model ignores variation and interacting factors, so it simplifies a complex system [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Include units in rate answers, and show the subtraction.

**8. (a)** An interspecific relationship benefiting both species [1]
**(b)** Rhizobium fixes nitrogen for the plant [1]; the plant gives carbohydrate and shelter [1]
**(c)** Zooxanthellae supply sugars to the coral [1]; the coral gives shelter and carbon dioxide [1]
**(d)** In parasitism one species gains and its host is harmed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each organism needs its own benefit. A benefit for only one partner leaves the point unfinished.

**9. (a)** Several plots with X removed, plus control plots with X present, of similar size and conditions [1]; measure growth or density of Y in both [1]; if Y is higher where X was removed, competition is indicated [1]
**(b)** Y might benefit from other changes, for example disturbance or reduced shading [1]; competition is only one possible cause, so the result shows association, not proven cause [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Controls and replication are what make an experiment convincing; mention them.

**10. (a)** The two snail species are distributed independently of each other [1]
**(b)** Row total 25, column total 23: 25 × 23 ÷ 60 [1] = **9.58** [1]
**(c)** (4 − 9.58)² ÷ 9.58 = 3.25; (21 − 15.42)² ÷ 15.42 = 2.02; (19 − 13.42)² ÷ 13.42 = 2.32; (16 − 21.58)² ÷ 21.58 = 1.44 [1]; χ² = **9.04** [1]
**(d)** df = (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1 [1]; 9.04 exceeds 3.84, so reject the null: distribution is not independent (negative association) [1]
**(e)** Both together is fewer than expected, suggesting competition [1], but the snails may prefer different conditions, so cause is not shown [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the expected-value method and write the conclusion in context, not only "reject".

**11. (a)** Both populations cycle [1]; lynx peaks follow hare peaks by about a year [1]
**(b)** More hares support more lynx [1]; more lynx reduce hares, then lynx fall from lack of food [1]
**(c)** Top-down: predators limit prey; bottom-up: food supply limits populations [1]; hare food supply may also matter [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the words "delayed" and "negative feedback", and describe the lag.

**12. (a)** Both release chemicals that deter competitors [1]
**(b)** Allelopathy: black walnut releases juglone [1]. Antibiotic: Penicillium secretes penicillin [1]
**(c)** Plants; microorganisms (fungi or bacteria) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A named organism plus a named chemical is stronger than a general statement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the Lincoln index without clear M, N and R.
- Forgetting to scale quadrat density up to the whole area.
- Listing assumptions without the effect of breaking them.
- Missing which axis is logarithmic.
- Describing a plateau without balanced births and deaths.
- Concluding that a chi-squared result proves competition.
- Giving one-sided benefits for mutualism.
- Writing "reject" without saying what it means for the species.
