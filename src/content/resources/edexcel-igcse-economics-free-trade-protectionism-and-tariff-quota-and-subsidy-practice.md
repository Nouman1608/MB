These questions cover section 2.2.2 a), b) and c) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026: free trade, reasons for protection, and tariff, quota and subsidy diagrams. There is one tier of entry. Every country, firm and figure here is made up.

## Questions

**1.** What is meant by dumping? **[2]**

**2.** A small bakery in Country C spends 40% of its total costs on imported flour. Free trade cuts the flour price by 15%. Other costs do not change.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in the bakery's total costs. **[2]**
**(b)** State one other advantage of free trade for consumers. **[1]**

**3.** In Country C, demand for woollen blankets is Qd = 200 − 4P and home supply is Qs = 3P + 18 (thousand blankets a month, price in currency units). The world price is 20.

**(a)** Calculate imports under free trade. **[1]**
**(b)** The government adds a 10% tariff. Calculate the tariff revenue. **[3]**

**4.** Draw a supply and demand diagram to show the effect of a tariff on an imported good. **[4]**

**5.** Using the data in question 3, the government replaces the tariff with a quota of 14 thousand blankets.

**(a)** Calculate the new price. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain who receives the extra payment per blanket. **[2]**

**6.** Instead, the government pays home producers a subsidy of 3 per blanket (data from question 3).

**(a)** Calculate home output and the cost of the subsidy. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one advantage of the subsidy compared with the tariff in question 3. **[2]**
**(c)** State one disadvantage of the subsidy. **[1]**

**7.** Country D spends 900 a month on imports and earns 840 from exports.

**(a)** Calculate the current account balance on goods. **[1]**
**(b)** A tariff cuts imports by 10%. Calculate the new balance. **[2]**
**(c)** Country D's trading partner retaliates, and exports fall by 10%. Calculate the balance. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why a tariff may not reduce a deficit. **[3]**

**8.** Country E has a new bicycle firm. The world price is 30. Its unit cost falls as output grows.

| Output (thousand) | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit cost | 48 | 40 | 34 | 29 | 26 |

**(a)** A 25% tariff is introduced. Calculate the price of an imported bicycle. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the lowest output in the table at which the firm can match the import price without the tariff, and with it. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one risk of protecting an infant industry. **[3]**

**9.** Country A bans imported toys that fail safety tests and puts a tariff on other imported toys. Explain one reason for each policy. **[4]**

**10.** Country G's tariff on rice protects 30 jobs. Consumers pay 120 thousand a month more, and the government collects 45 thousand in revenue. A job pays 2.5 thousand a month.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost to consumers for each job protected. **[2]**
**(b)** Assess whether the tariff is worth keeping. **[6]**

**11.** Country H's steel industry is struggling against cheap imports. Evaluate whether a tariff, a quota or a subsidy is the best way to help it. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Selling goods in another country [1] at a price below the cost of production, or below the price charged at home [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit comes from covering the act of selling abroad and the low price together. Writing only "selling cheap goods abroad" gives no sense of the comparison.

**2. (a)** Fall in costs = 40% × 15% [1] = **6%** [1]
**(b)** Any one: greater choice; lower prices on other goods; access to goods not made at home [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Multiply the share by the price change. Writing 15% as the answer ignores that flour is only part of costs.

**3. (a)** Qd = 200 − 80 = 120; Qs = 60 + 18 = 78; imports = **42** [1]
**(b)** Tariff = 10% × 20 = 2, so price = 22 [1]. Qd = 112 and Qs = 84, so imports = 28 [1]. Revenue = 2 × 28 = **56 thousand** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Revenue uses imports after the tariff. Show the new price and the new imports as separate lines so each step of your working can be followed.

**4.** Axes are price and quantity, with D sloping down and S sloping up [1]. A horizontal line at the world price Pw [1]. A second horizontal line above it at Pw plus the tariff [1]. Imports shown as the gap between S and D at each price, smaller after the tariff [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Label each line and each quantity so a reader can see every feature. An unlabelled sketch hides whether you know what the shape shows.

**5. (a)** Imports = (200 − 4P) − (3P + 18) = 182 − 7P [1]. Set 182 − 7P = 14 [1]. So P = **24** [1]
**(b)** The gap is 24 − 20 = 4 per blanket [1]. It goes to the import licence holders, giving 4 × 14 = 56, unless the government sells the licences [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Say who receives the gap. "The government" is a tempting wrong answer, because a quota does not tax anyone by itself.

**6. (a)** Firms receive 20 + 3 = 23 [1]. Qs = 69 + 18 = **87** [1]. Cost = 3 × 87 = **261 thousand** [1]
**(b)** Consumers still pay the world price of 20 [1], so the price does not rise as it does with a tariff [1].
**(c)** Taxpayers must fund it, or it has an opportunity cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both items. Say what happens to price under the subsidy and contrast it with the tariff, rather than describing the subsidy alone.

**7. (a)** 840 − 900 = **−60** (a deficit) [1]
**(b)** Imports = 900 × 0.9 = 810 [1]. Balance = 840 − 810 = **+30** [1]
**(c)** Exports = 840 × 0.9 = 756 [1]. Balance = 756 − 810 = **−54** [1]
**(d)** Demand for imports may be inelastic [1], so spending on imports falls little when the price rises [1], and the deficit persists [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) builds on part (b), so show the new import figure explicitly. A clear chain, from elasticity to spending to the balance, is stronger than naming a reason alone.

**8. (a)** Tariff = 25% × 30 = 7.5 [1]. Price = **37.5** [1]
**(b)** Without tariff: output 30 (cost 29 is below 30) [1]. With tariff: output 20 (cost 34 is below 37.5) [1]
**(c)** The firm may not become efficient [1], so the tariff stays [1], and consumers keep paying a higher price than the world price [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Read the table for the first cost below the price, not the lowest cost. State the price you are comparing against in each case.

**9.** Ban: buyers cannot judge safety themselves [1], so banning failing toys protects children's health [1]. Tariff: raises the price of imports, which brings in revenue [1] that the government can spend on public services [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each reason to its policy. Giving two reasons for the ban, and none for the tariff, answers only half the question.

**10. (a)** Consumers' extra cost per job = 120 ÷ 30 [1] = **4 thousand** a month [1]
**(b)** For: protects 30 jobs and incomes [1]; the government gains 45 thousand, so the net cost is 75 thousand, or 2.5 thousand per job [1]. Against: 4 thousand per job exceeds the 2.5 thousand wage [1]; higher prices fall on all consumers [1]; retaliation could cost export jobs [1]. Judgement: on cost alone the tariff looks weak, because the net cost equals a wage, unless the jobs are in a region with few alternatives or the protection is temporary [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures in the data. An assessment that weighs the 4 thousand and the 2.5 thousand against each other gains more than general statements about jobs.

**11.** Tariff: raises revenue [1], but raises prices for steel users [1]. Quota: limits imports firmly [1], but licence holders gain the gap [1]. Subsidy: lowers home costs without raising import prices [1], but taxpayers pay [1]. Judgement: depends on aim, such as revenue, price or a firm limit, and on whether the support is temporary and others retaliate [1]. A supported conclusion, such as a time-limited subsidy if steel is a key input, or a tariff if revenue is needed [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Your final judgement should come out of the points above it. Setting the three methods side by side on cost and effect reads better than describing each one in turn.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining dumping as "cheap imports" with no comparison of price and cost.
- Calculating tariff revenue on the imports before the tariff.
- Saying a quota raises government revenue, without mentioning licences.
- Drawing diagrams without labelling the world price, tariff price or quantities.
- Listing reasons for protection without explaining the chain.
- Missing the unit, such as thousand blankets or currency units.
- Ignoring retaliation when judging a current account measure.
- Ending with a conclusion that restates the question without choosing.
