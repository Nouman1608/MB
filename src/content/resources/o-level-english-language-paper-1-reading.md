Paper 1 – Reading carries exactly half of the marks in Cambridge O Level
English Language (1123): 50 marks, 2 hours, worth 50% of the final
grade. Where the [Reading and Writing overview guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-english-language-reading-and-writing/)
maps the whole syllabus at a glance and the [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/)
covers the companion paper, this guide stays entirely inside Paper 1
and works through what each of its two sections actually asks you to
do, which assessment objectives they test, and how the marks break
down.

## What Paper 1 is testing

Paper 1 is weighted 80% Reading (AO1) and 20% Writing (AO2) -- the
mirror image of Paper 2, which is 80% Writing and 20% Reading. That
20% of Writing marks on a "Reading" paper comes from the summary
task in Section B, where you must produce your own piece of
continuous writing rather than simply lifting phrases from the text.
You cannot access full marks in Section B by copying sentences
verbatim from Text B; you have to select, condense and re-express the
relevant material in your own words.

The five Reading assessment objectives tested across both sections
are:

- **R1** – demonstrate understanding of explicit meanings
- **R2** – demonstrate understanding of implicit meanings and attitudes
- **R3** – analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions, using appropriate support from the text
- **R4** – demonstrate understanding of how writers achieve effects and influence readers
- **R5** – select and use information for specific purposes

Section A tests R1, R2 and R4 across its 25 marks. Section B tests R1
and R5 in the summary task (alongside two Writing objectives, W2 and
W3) and R2 in the short response task.

Candidates work from two source texts, both printed in a separate
question paper insert rather than on the question paper itself
(unlike Paper 2, where the source material appears directly on the
paper). No dictionaries are permitted in either paper.

## Section A: Comprehension and Use of Language (25 marks)

Section A is built entirely around **Text A**, a narrative text of
approximately 900 words drawn from the twentieth or twenty-first
century. It splits into two questions.

**Question 1 – Comprehension task (16 marks).** A series of
sub-questions requiring short answers that test understanding of both
explicit and implicit meaning in Text A. This question tests:

- **R1** – demonstrate understanding of explicit meanings
- **R2** – demonstrate understanding of implicit meanings and attitude

Explicit-meaning sub-questions are usually answerable by locating and
restating information stated directly in the text. Implicit-meaning
sub-questions require you to infer what a character thinks, feels or
implies without it being spelled out -- for these, examiners are
looking for a clear inference supported by a specific detail from the
text, not a guess disconnected from the wording used.

**Question 2 – Use of Language task (9 marks).** A series of
sub-questions, worth nine marks in total, on the author's use of
language in Text A and the effect this has on the reader. This
question tests a single objective:

- **R4** – demonstrate understanding of how writers achieve effects and influence readers

This is the one point in Paper 1 where the mark scheme credits
commentary on *how* the writer has written something, not just *what*
they have written, and it is marked against R4 specifically rather
than the R1/R2 objectives that cover Question 1. An answer that
accurately paraphrases what a quoted word or phrase means, without
addressing the effect it creates on the reader, is being marked
against the wrong objective even where the paraphrase itself is
correct.

## Section B: Summary and Short Response (25 marks)

Section B is built around **Text B**, a shorter non-fiction or
discursive text of approximately 550–600 words, and splits into two
tasks.

**Question 3a – Summary task (20 marks total: 10 Reading + 10
Writing).** Candidates write a summary, as continuous writing, of **no
more than 150 words**, in response to a specific instruction (for
example, summarising the reasons given for a particular course of
action, or the disadvantages of something described in the text).
This is the single most heavily weighted question on the paper. It
tests:

- **R1** – demonstrate understanding of explicit meanings
- **R5** – select and use information for specific purposes

and, on the writing side:

- **W2** – organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect
- **W3** – use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context

Because this task sits on the Reading paper but is marked partly
against Writing objectives, it rewards two skills at once: correctly
identifying which points in Text B are relevant to the specific
summary instruction (Reading), and re-expressing them concisely, in
your own words and as connected, organised prose rather than a
bullet-style list (Writing). Because the Reading marks (R1, R5) and Writing marks (W2, W3) are
awarded separately against the same 150-word answer, a summary can
lose marks on either side independently: missing a relevant point
costs Reading marks even in fluent prose, and disorganised or
repetitive prose costs Writing marks even where every point is
present. The 150-word limit applies to the summary as continuous
writing as a whole, not per point, so points made after the limit is
reached are not credited.

**Question 3b – Short response task (5 marks).** A short response
requiring you to show understanding of implicit meaning and attitude
in Text B. This question tests a single objective:

- **R2** – demonstrate understanding of implicit meanings and attitudes

As in Question 1's implicit-meaning sub-questions, the expectation is
a clear inference grounded in specific wording from Text B, not a
restatement of what the text says explicitly.

## Command words that appear on this paper

The syllabus defines a small, fixed set of command words used across
its question papers. On Paper 1, the ones most likely to shape how you
respond are:

- **Identify** – name/select/recognise
- **Explain** – set out purposes or reasons / make the relationships between things clear / say why and/or how and support with relevant evidence
- **Summarise** – select and present the main points, without detail
- **Give** – produce an answer from a given source or recall/memory

Question 3a is effectively a full-length application of "Summarise" --
main points only, condensed, without the incidental detail that Text B
may contain.

## Timing across the two hours

With Section A worth 25 marks and Section B worth 25 marks, and 2
hours for the whole paper, a reasonable split is around 10 minutes
reading both texts before starting, roughly 50–55 minutes on Section A
(Question 1 and Question 2 combined), and roughly 50–55 minutes on
Section B, with extra care taken over the 150-word limit in Question
3a since planning which points to include takes longer than writing
them. Leave a short buffer at the end to check Question 1 and 3b
answers are grounded in specific textual detail rather than general
impressions.

## How to approach it

Treat Section A and Section B as testing related but distinct reading
skills. Section A rewards close, detailed engagement with a single
narrative text -- both what it says and how it is written. Section B
rewards the ability to extract, select and re-express only the
material relevant to a specific instruction, under a strict word
limit. Practising the summary task specifically against real
100–150-word answers, then checking each point against the set
instruction (not just "is this true of the text"), is one of the most
direct ways to improve a Section B mark, since most lost marks there
come from including irrelevant or over-long material rather than
getting individual points wrong. Past papers and specimen mark
schemes, freely available from Cambridge International, show real
examples of how examiners credit implicit-meaning inferences and
summary points -- use them alongside this guide rather than as a
substitute for understanding the assessment structure first.
