## Section A

**1.** Explain what a population pyramid shows and what a wide base and a narrow apex indicate. **[4]**

**2.** Define dependency ratio and state how it is calculated. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain the differences you would expect between the population pyramids of a low-income and a high-income country, giving **three** differences with reasons. **[9]**

**4.** Explain **three** government policies used to influence population growth, giving an example and one problem of each. **[9]**

**5.** Explain the settlement hierarchy, and explain the concepts of sphere of influence and threshold population. **[7]**

**6.** Describe the typical land-use zones of a city in a high-income country and explain **two** reasons for the pattern. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A population pyramid shows the **number or percentage of the population in each age group, divided by sex** [1] [1]. A **wide base** indicates a **high birth rate** with many young dependants [1]; a **narrow apex** indicates **few people surviving to old age**, so life expectancy is low [1].

**2.** The **ratio of the dependent population — those under 15 and over 64 — to the working-age population** [1]. Calculated as (dependants ÷ working-age population) × 100 [1].

**3.** Any three, 3 marks each: the **low-income country has a much wider base** [1], because the **birth rate is higher** — high infant mortality, children as economic assets, limited family planning [1] [1]. The **high-income country has a much wider top** [1], because **life expectancy is far higher** thanks to better healthcare, diet and sanitation [1] [1]. The low-income pyramid **narrows sharply and steadily** [1], reflecting a **higher death rate at every age**, whereas the high-income pyramid has **near-vertical sides until old age** [1] [1]. The high-income country may show a **bulge in the middle-aged groups** [1], reflecting a **past baby boom that is now ageing** [1] [1].

**4.** Any three, 3 marks each: **Anti-natalist policies** — measures to reduce the birth rate, such as China's former one-child policy [1] [1]; problems included a **skewed sex ratio from sex-selective abortion, an ageing population and a shrinking workforce**, so the policy was abandoned [1].
**Pro-natalist policies** — measures to raise the birth rate, such as France's or Singapore's financial incentives, tax allowances and subsidised childcare [1] [1]; the problem is that they are **expensive and have limited effect**, since the decision to have children depends more on housing costs and career considerations than on a payment [1].
**Immigration policy** — encouraging migration to fill labour shortages, as in Germany's recruitment of workers [1] [1]; problems include **pressure on housing and services and political and social tension** if integration is not managed [1].

**5.** The **settlement hierarchy** ranks settlements by **size and by the range of services they provide** [1] — from isolated dwelling and hamlet, through village and town, to city and conurbation; as size increases, **the number of settlements falls but the range of services rises** [1] [1].
**Sphere of influence** is the **area served by a settlement — the area from which people travel to use its services** [1]; larger settlements have larger spheres [1].
**Threshold population** is the **minimum number of people required to make a service viable** [1]; a newsagent has a low threshold and is found in villages, while a hospital or university has a very high threshold and is found only in large cities [1].

**6.** A typical pattern is the **Central Business District at the core**, surrounded by an **inner city zone of older terraced housing and former industry**, then **inter-war suburbs**, then **modern outer suburbs and the rural-urban fringe** [1] [1] [1] [1].
Reasons, any two: **land values are highest at the centre**, because accessibility is greatest, so only high-value uses such as shops and offices can afford it, and buildings are built upwards to make the most of the small plots [1] [1]. **The city grew outwards over time**, so housing age and style decrease with distance from the centre, and the outer suburbs were built when cars allowed people to live further from work [1] [1]. **The fringe offers cheap, plentiful land with good road access**, which attracts retail parks, warehousing and business parks that need space and lorry access [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a pyramid's shape without explaining the reason.
- Giving the one-child policy without any of its consequences.
- Confusing threshold population with sphere of influence.
- Listing land-use zones without explaining the pattern.
