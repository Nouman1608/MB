## Internal energy

The particles of a body move randomly and interact with each other. Internal energy U is the **sum** of their **randomly distributed** kinetic and potential energies.

- **Kinetic energy** comes from random motion. Temperature is linked to the mean kinetic energy of the particles.
- **Potential energy** comes from the forces between particles and depends on their separation.
- "Randomly distributed" matters: the kinetic energy of a whole moving object, or its gravitational potential energy, is not internal energy.

## The first law of thermodynamics

ΔU = Q + W

Q is the energy transferred **to** the system by heating, and W the work done **on** the system. Both are negative if energy leaves the system that way.

| Situation | Q | W | ΔU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas heated in a sealed rigid container | + | 0 | + |
| Gas compressed quickly in an insulated cylinder | 0 | + | + |
| Gas expands, pushing a piston out, with no heating | 0 | − | − |
| Hot block cools in air | − | 0 | − |
| Gas expands while heated, temperature constant | + | − | 0 (for an ideal gas) |

### Worked example 1: heating while expanding

A gas receives 500 J by heating and expands, doing 180 J of work on its surroundings.

Q = +500 J. The gas does work on the surroundings, so the work done on the gas is W = −180 J.

ΔU = 500 + (−180) = **+320 J**. The gas gains internal energy, but less than the energy supplied by heating.

## Change of state

While a substance melts or boils at constant temperature, energy is still being supplied. The mean kinetic energy of the particles stays the same (the temperature is constant). The energy goes into **potential energy**: particles are separated further against the attractive forces between them. So the internal energy rises even though the temperature does not.

## The two energy equations

- Temperature change, no change of state: **Q = mcΔθ**. c is the specific heat capacity, the energy needed to raise 1 kg by 1 K, in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- Change of state, no temperature change: **Q = ml**. l is the specific latent heat, the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg without a temperature change, in J kg⁻¹. Fusion is melting; vaporisation is boiling.

A temperature change in K equals the same change in °C.

### Worked example 2: ice to warm water

How much energy turns 0.20 kg of ice at −15 °C into water at 40 °C?

| Stage | Equation | Energy / J |
|---|---|---|
| Warm the ice from −15 °C to 0 °C | 0.20 × 2100 × 15 | 6 300 |
| Melt the ice at 0 °C | 0.20 × 3.34 × 10⁵ | 66 800 |
| Warm the water from 0 °C to 40 °C | 0.20 × 4180 × 40 | 33 440 |
| Total | | **1.07 × 10⁵** |

Melting takes twice the energy of warming the water through 40 K. Missing a stage, or using the specific heat capacity of water for the ice, is the most common error.

### Worked example 3: mixing

A 0.15 kg copper block at 95 °C is dropped into 0.25 kg of water at 20 °C. Assuming no energy is lost to the surroundings or the container, find the final temperature θ.

Energy lost by copper = energy gained by water:

0.15 × 390 × (95 − θ) = 0.25 × 4180 × (θ − 20)

58.5(95 − θ) = 1045(θ − 20), so θ = (5558 + 20 900) / 1103.5 = **24 °C**.

The water's temperature hardly changes because its heat capacity (mc = 1045 J K⁻¹) is about 18 times that of the copper. In practice the container also absorbs energy, so a measured final temperature is slightly lower.

## Reading a heating curve

Heat a solid at constant power P, with no energy losses, and plot temperature against time.

- **Sloping section:** P = mc × (Δθ/Δt), so c = P / (m × gradient). A shallower slope means a larger c.
- **Flat section:** P × duration = ml, so l = P × duration / m.

### Worked example 4

A 40 W heater warms 0.25 kg of a solid. The solid's temperature rises at 0.080 K s⁻¹, then stays constant for 1500 s while it melts, then the liquid rises at 0.064 K s⁻¹.

c(solid) = 40 / (0.25 × 0.080) = **2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹**.

l = 40 × 1500 / 0.25 = **2.4 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹**.

c(liquid) = 40 / (0.25 × 0.064) = **2500 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹**.

## Continuous flow

When a fluid flows steadily past a heater or a hot surface, use rates:

power = (mass flow rate) × c × Δθ, where Δθ is the difference between the outlet and inlet temperatures.

### Worked example 5: engine cooling

Water is pumped through an engine at 0.050 kg s⁻¹ and leaves 12 K warmer than it enters. The rate at which it removes energy is 0.050 × 4180 × 12 = **2.5 kW**. To remove more energy at the same temperature rise, the pump must move water faster.

## Required practical 8: specific heat capacity by an electrical method

**Method:** a heater of known power (from V and I on meters) sits in a hole in an insulated metal block of known mass; a thermometer sits in a second hole with a little oil or water for good thermal contact. Switch on, start a stopwatch, and record the temperature at regular intervals.

**Analysis:** energy supplied E = VIt. Plot θ against t: the straight part has gradient VI/(mc), so c = VI / (m × gradient). Using the gradient avoids the slow start, when the heater and block are still warming up.

**Errors and improvements:**

- Energy lost to the surroundings makes Δθ too small and c too large. Insulate the block; start a few kelvin below room temperature and finish the same amount above, so gains and losses roughly cancel.
- The temperature keeps rising briefly after the heater is switched off: record the maximum.
- Read the meters throughout, since V and I can drift as the heater warms.

## Common mistakes

- Writing W as the work done **by** the gas in ΔU = Q + W.
- Saying the temperature is constant during melting because "no energy is being supplied".
- Leaving out a stage, or a change of state, in a multi-stage problem.
- Using the wrong c for the stage (ice and water differ).
- In a flow problem, using the total mass instead of the mass per second.
- In the practical, claiming heat losses make c smaller. They make the calculated c larger.

## Exam technique

- **Define** internal energy using the words "sum", "random", "kinetic" and "potential".
- **Explain** a temperature plateau in terms of particle potential energy rising while mean kinetic energy stays constant.
- **Calculate** in stages: one line per stage, then add.
- **Evaluate** the practical: name the error, its direction (c too big or too small) and a specific fix.
