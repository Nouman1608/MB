These notes condense section 3.1.5 Business location of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. The specification asks you to "understand the factors that influence where a business is located, including proximity to the market, availability of raw materials, labour, competition and costs." For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Business location study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-location/). Then apply what you know with the [Business location practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-location-practice/). The broader [Business location and Business planning notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-business-location-and-business-planning-revision-notes/) put this section alongside 3.1.6.

## Core recall

### The five factors and who feels each most

| Factor | Pulls hardest on | Effect to write about |
|---|---|---|
| Proximity to the market | Face-to-face services, shops relying on footfall, makers of fresh or fragile goods | More customers and sales; faster, cheaper delivery |
| Availability of raw materials | Businesses using heavy, bulky or perishable inputs | Lower transport cost per unit; fresher inputs |
| Labour | Businesses needing many workers or specialist skills | Easier recruitment; wage level affects costs |
| Competition | Businesses whose customers like to compare, or that want to be the only local option | Cluster brings customers but shares them; isolation brings captive customers but maybe low demand |
| Costs | Every business, most of all those with low profit margins | Rent, rates, utilities, transport and wages all reduce profit |

### Linking location to the four functions

- Operations: distance for inputs and outputs; size of the site.
- Human resources: whether staff can reach the site and have the right skills.
- Marketing: visibility, footfall and how easily customers can reach the business.
- Finance: the total of location costs compared with the revenue the site supports.

### Three rules of thumb

1. If customers come to the business, the market usually dominates.
2. If the inputs are heavier than the product, raw materials usually dominate.
3. If the product is sold online and posted, costs and labour usually dominate.

Rules of thumb are a starting point. Always check the case-study details, which may point the other way.

### Why the decision is hard to reverse

Leases, fitting out and staff who live nearby tie a business to a site. Moving costs money and may lose customers who knew the old address. So a good location answer looks ahead: will demand, rents or competition change?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Location | The place where a business operates its premises |
| Proximity | Closeness |
| Market (in location) | The area where the business's customers are |
| Footfall | The number of people passing or entering a site |
| Raw materials | Natural or basic inputs a business turns into its product |
| Labour | The supply of workers available to a business |
| Skilled labour | Workers with specialist training or experience |
| Cluster | A group of similar businesses located close together |
| Business rates | A local tax paid on business premises |
| Transport costs | The cost of moving inputs in and finished goods out |
| Relocation | Moving an existing business to a new site |

## Exam traps

- **Factor without effect.** "It is near raw materials" earns little. Add "so transport costs per unit fall, raising profit".
- **Cheapest equals best.** A low-rent site with no customers is not a bargain.
- **One factor only.** An evaluation needs at least two factors weighed against each other.
- **Generic answers.** Use the case study's product, customers and figures.
- **Competition always bad.** Clusters attract shoppers who want choice.
- **Forgetting labour costs.** A cheap site with a skills shortage may force higher wages or training spending.
- **Online means location does not matter.** Delivery access, costs and labour still matter.
- **Ignoring the future.** A site that suits today may not suit a growing business.

## Mini-method for a "justify" answer

1. Pick the site.
2. Give the strongest factor for that site, applied to the business, with figures if given.
3. Admit the main drawback of the chosen site.
4. Explain why the strength outweighs the drawback for this business.
5. State one change that would make the other site better.

### Quick worked reminder

Figures invented. Kestrel Kitchens fits bespoke kitchens in customers' homes. Showroom A, on a retail park, costs £5,200 a month; Workshop B, on an industrial estate, costs £2,900. Customers usually book after viewing a showroom display. The extra £2,300 a month is £27,600 a year. If each fitted kitchen earns £3,000 profit before premises costs, Showroom A needs to win about 10 extra kitchens a year to cover its higher rent (£27,600 ÷ £3,000 = 9.2, so 10 whole kitchens).

## Self-test

1. List the five location factors named in the specification.
2. Why does a hairdresser care more about proximity to the market than an online T-shirt seller?
3. Give one reason a sawmill might locate near a forest.
4. State two questions a business should ask about labour in an area.
5. Explain one benefit of locating in a cluster of similar businesses.
6. Give one risk of locating where there are no competitors.
7. Name three costs linked to location.
8. Why is a location decision hard to reverse?
9. A site costs £1,500 a month more than another. How much extra profit before premises costs must it generate a year to break even on the difference?
10. Which function of the business is most affected if skilled workers cannot reach a site?
11. Give one location factor not in the specification's list that a case study might raise.

### Answers

1. Proximity to the market, availability of raw materials, labour, competition and costs.
2. Customers visit a hairdresser in person and will not travel far for a routine cut, so a nearby site wins custom. The online seller posts goods, so its customers never visit.
3. Logs are heavy and bulky, and much of their weight is cut away, so a short journey lowers transport costs.
4. Any two of: are there enough workers; do they have the right skills; what wage rate is normal in the area.
5. Customers who like to compare choices are drawn to the area, so the business gets more visitors than it would alone.
6. There may be no rivals because demand in the area is too low.
7. Any three of: rent or purchase price, business rates, utilities, transport costs, wages.
8. Leases, fitting-out costs, staff who live nearby and customers who know the address all tie the business to the site.
9. £1,500 × 12 = £18,000 a year.
10. Human resources, because it cannot recruit or keep the staff it needs; operations also suffers if output falls.
11. Any one, for example transport links or room to expand.
