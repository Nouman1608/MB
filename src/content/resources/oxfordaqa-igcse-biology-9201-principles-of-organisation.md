This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.2 Principles of organisation** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE
Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and
content from any part of the specification can appear on either paper.

The statements in 3.1.2 are short, but they are the framework for the rest of Topic 1 and for much of the course.
The [Organisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation/) treats all of Topic 1 together.
This page concentrates on the four levels of organisation and on **size and scale**, with new worked examples
using estimation, unit conversion and surface area to volume ratio. Afterwards, use the
[Organisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation-revision-notes/) and the
[Principles of organisation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-principles-of-organisation-practice/).

## What 3.1.2 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Large multicellular organisms develop systems for exchanging materials; cells differentiate so that they can perform different functions |
| b | A tissue is a group of cells with similar structure and function |
| c | Organs are made of tissues; one organ may contain several tissues |
| d | Organ systems are groups of organs that perform a particular function |

The specification also says students should **develop an understanding of size and scale in relation to cells,
tissues, organs and systems**. That sentence is why calculations and estimates appear in questions on this
subtopic.

## Why organisms need levels of organisation

A single-celled organism can exchange everything it needs across its surface. A large multicellular organism
cannot, because most of its cells are deep inside the body and far from the outside world. So large organisms
develop **systems for exchanging materials**, such as the digestive system and the circulatory system. These
systems only work well if different parts specialise in different jobs.

Specialising is made possible by **differentiation**. During the development of a multicellular organism, cells
differentiate so that they can perform different functions. A differentiated cell has the structures that suit its
job (see 3.1.1), and the cell can then do that job more effectively than an unspecialised cell could.

### Worked example 1: why bigger organisms need exchange systems

Treat three animals as cubes. Oxygen is used by the whole volume, but it can only enter across the surface.
Compare cubes with sides of 2 mm, 4 mm and 8 mm.

```
surface area = 6 × side²,  volume = side³,  ratio = surface area ÷ volume
side 2 mm:  6 × 4  = 24 mm²,  8 mm³    → 24 ÷ 8   = 3.0
side 4 mm:  6 × 16 = 96 mm²,  64 mm³   → 96 ÷ 64  = 1.5
side 8 mm:  6 × 64 = 384 mm², 512 mm³  → 384 ÷ 512 = 0.75
```

Doubling the side halves the ratio. Larger bodies have less surface for each unit of volume, so diffusion across
the outer surface alone cannot supply the inside. That is the reason large organisms need specialised exchange
organs and transport systems. The same idea is developed in 3.1.5.

## The four levels

The levels run from small to large. Each level is built from the one below.

```
cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
```

| Level | Definition in the specification | Example (animal) |
|---|---|---|
| Cell | The basic unit; may be specialised | A smooth muscle cell |
| Tissue | A group of cells with similar structure and function | Muscular tissue |
| Organ | Made of tissues; may contain several tissues | The stomach |
| Organ system | A group of organs that perform a particular function | The digestive system |

Wording points that earn marks:

- A tissue has cells with **similar structure and function**. Both words are needed. "A group of the same cells"
  is not enough.
- An organ contains **more than one tissue** (often several), so one tissue alone is not an organ.
- An organ system is organs working together for **a particular function**.

### Worked example 2: deciding the level

Invented stimulus. A student writes down four things she sees. Place each at the right level.

| Observation | Level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| One red-stained cell with a visible nucleus | Cell | A single unit |
| A sheet of identical cells lining a tube | Tissue | Similar cells with a shared function |
| A kidney | Organ | Made of several tissues |
| The kidneys, ureters and bladder together | Organ system | A group of organs with one function |

If a question gives a plant example, apply the same rule: palisade mesophyll is a tissue, the leaf is an organ
(it also contains epidermis, spongy mesophyll and xylem and phloem), and the shoot system is a group of organs.

## Size and scale

Cells, tissues, organs and systems differ by factors of 10, 100 or more in size. You need a feel for the order of
magnitude and the ability to convert units.

| Unit | Relation | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mm | 1000 micrometres (μm) | Small tissue samples, the width of a pinhead |
| 1 cm | 10 mm | Many organs are several cm long |
| 1 μm | 0.001 mm | Most cells are between about 10 and 100 μm, and bacteria are a few μm |

### Worked example 3: converting and counting

A human red blood cell is about 7 μm across. How many could be lined up across 1 mm?

```
1 mm = 1000 μm
1000 ÷ 7 = 142.857...  → about 143 cells (about 140 to 2 significant figures)
```

Because the diameter is itself only approximate, quoting "about 140" is more honest than writing every digit.

### Worked example 4: estimating the number of cells in a tissue

Estimate the number of cells in a 1 cm cube of tissue if each cell is a cube 20 μm on each side.

```
1 cm = 10 000 μm
cells along one edge = 10 000 ÷ 20 = 500
cells in the cube = 500 × 500 × 500 = 125 000 000 = 1.25 × 10⁸
```

The answer is an estimate because real cells are not cubes and are not packed with no gaps. Show the idea of
"estimate" by saying "about 10⁸ cells". Notice that the number of cells in a small piece of tissue is enormous, so
differentiation and organisation are needed to coordinate them.

### Worked example 5: real size from a micrograph

A micrograph of a tissue is 24 mm wide and the magnification is ×400. Three cells span the full width.

```
real width = 24 ÷ 400 = 0.06 mm = 60 μm
width of one cell = 60 ÷ 3 = 20 μm
```

## Examples of organisation across the body

The table links the four levels in two animal systems that you meet again in later subtopics.

| System | Organs | Tissues in one organ (stomach) |
|---|---|---|
| Digestive | Stomach, small intestine, liver, pancreas | Muscular, glandular, epithelial |
| Circulatory | Heart, blood vessels | Muscular tissue in the heart wall, and tissues that line the vessels |

For plants, leaves, stems and roots are the organs, and the specification limits internal detail to the leaf and
the position of xylem and phloem in a root and stem (see 3.1.4).

## Building an answer on organisation

Long-answer questions often ask you to move between levels, for example "describe how the cells of the small
intestine are organised into a working organ". A reliable structure is to take one step at a time.

1. Start with the **cell**: name the specialised cell and one feature that suits its job.
2. Say that a group of similar cells forms a **tissue**, and name the tissue.
3. Say that the organ is made of **several tissues**, and name at least two.
4. Name the **organ system** and state its overall function.
5. Finish with the **purpose** for the organism, such as exchanging materials with the environment.

### Worked example 6: applying the five steps

Question: "Describe how the pancreas is part of an organ system." (3 marks, invented)

> The pancreas contains glandular tissue, which produces digestive juices, and it also contains other
> tissues [1]. Because it is made of several tissues, it is an organ [1]. It works with the stomach, small
> intestine and liver in the digestive system, a group of organs with the particular function of digesting and
> absorbing food [1].

Notice that each mark is earned by using a defined term (tissue, organ, organ system) correctly. Marks are not
given for vague words such as "part of the body".

## Common mistakes

- Defining a tissue as "a group of cells" with no mention of **similar structure and function**.
- Saying an organ is a "large tissue". An organ is made of several tissues.
- Confusing organ and organ system, such as calling the stomach an organ system.
- Writing that organisms need exchange systems because they are "heavier". The point is that the surface area to
  volume ratio falls as size rises.
- Mixing units in a conversion, for example dividing mm by μm.
- Giving too many significant figures for an estimate.

## Exam technique

- **Define** or **state**: use the specification wording almost exactly.
- **Give an example of** a tissue, organ or system: name it and, if asked, say what it does.
- In scale questions, convert everything to one unit before dividing, and show each step.
- For "suggest why" questions about large organisms, use the surface area to volume idea and name the problem
  (diffusion distance) before naming the solution (an exchange system).
