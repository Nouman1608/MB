## What the syllabus asks

1. Valuation at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
2. Calculating the value of inventory.
3. The effect of an incorrect valuation on gross profit, profit for the
year, equity and asset valuation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inventory | Goods held for resale (and, for manufacturers, materials and part-finished goods) |
| Cost | Purchase price less trade discount, plus costs of bringing goods to their present location and condition (such as carriage inwards) |
| Net realisable value (NRV) | Expected selling price less further costs to complete, repair or sell |
| Prudence | Losses recognised as soon as foreseen; assets and profit not overstated |
| Realisation | Profit recognised only when goods are sold |
| Mark-up | Gross profit as a percentage of cost |
| Margin | Gross profit as a percentage of selling price |

## The rule

**Lower of cost and NRV, item by item.**

- NRV below cost → use NRV (recognise the loss now: prudence).
- NRV above cost → use cost (do not recognise profit early: realisation).
- Never compare totals.

## Building blocks

```
Cost = purchase price − trade discount + carriage inwards (and similar)
NRV  = expected selling price − costs to repair/complete − costs to sell
```

**Quick example.** 30 jackets cost $40 each. A fault means they can be sold
for $45 only after alterations costing $12 each. NRV = $45 − $12 = $33.
Value = 30 × $33 = $990 (not 30 × $40 = $1,200). Write-down $210.

## Adjusting a count made after the year end

Ask: **was it in inventory at the year end?**

| Movement after year end | Adjustment to the count |
|---|---|
| Goods sold | Add back at **cost** |
| Goods bought and received | Deduct |
| Goods returned by customers | Deduct at **cost** |
| Goods returned to suppliers | Add back |

For a count made **before** the year end, reverse every sign.

**Quick example.** Year end 30 June; count on 3 July at cost $15,500.
After 30 June: sales of $1,200 at a 50% mark-up (cost $800); goods received
$650; goods returned to a supplier $90.
Year-end inventory = $15,500 + $800 − $650 + $90 = $15,740.

**Converting to cost**

- Mark-up 25%: cost = selling price × 100/125.
- Mark-up 50%: cost = selling price × 100/150.
- Margin 20%: cost = selling price × 80/100.

## Effects of an incorrect closing inventory

| Error | Year of error: gross profit, profit for the year, equity, current assets | Next year: gross profit and profit for the year |
|---|---|---|
| Overvalued by X | All overstated by X | Understated by X |
| Undervalued by X | All understated by X | Overstated by X |

Why: closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales and listed in current
assets; next year it becomes opening inventory and is added in cost of
sales.

Over two years, total profit is the same; the error moves profit between
years. Equity is correct again at the end of year two if the second count
is right.

## Where closing inventory appears

- Statement of profit or loss: deducted from opening inventory plus net
purchases and carriage inwards to give cost of sales.
- Statement of financial position: first item under current assets.
- Next year's statement of profit or loss: as opening inventory.
- Not in the trial balance (which shows opening inventory); it is given as a
note.

## Exam traps

- Selling costs reduce NRV; they are never added to cost.
- Carriage inwards is part of cost; carriage outwards is a selling expense.
- Add back goods sold after the year end at cost, not selling price.
- Mark-up and margin give different cost figures.
- State each figure the question names, with the direction and amount.
- Remember the reverse effect in the following year.

## Quick self-test

1. State the rule for valuing inventory.
2. Define "net realisable value".
3. Goods cost $500; selling price $560; selling costs $80. Value?
4. Goods cost $250; selling price $400; selling costs $30. Value?
5. Which concept explains not valuing goods above cost?
6. Why must the comparison be made item by item?
7. A count on 4 January found inventory of $9,200 at cost. Goods sold for
$1,000 at a 25% mark-up between 1 and 4 January. Calculate the year-end
inventory (no other movements).
8. Goods received from a supplier after the year end but before the count:
add or deduct?
9. Closing inventory was overvalued by $1,500. Effect on gross profit this
year?
10. Effect of the error in question 9 on next year's profit for the year?
11. Is carriage inwards part of cost or a deduction from NRV?
12. Closing inventory undervalued by $600. Effect on current assets and
equity?

### Answers

1. Lower of cost and net realisable value, item by item.
2. The expected selling price less further costs to complete, repair or
sell the goods.
3. NRV $480 is lower than cost: $480.
4. NRV $370 is higher than cost: $250.
5. Realisation (profit recognised only when goods are sold); prudence also
supports it.
6. Totals would let expected profits on some items hide losses on others.
7. $9,200 + ($1,000 × 100/125) = $9,200 + $800 = $10,000.
8. Deduct: the goods were not held at the year end.
9. Gross profit overstated by $1,500.
10. Profit for the year understated by $1,500.
11. Part of cost.
12. Both understated by $600.
