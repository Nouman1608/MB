## Section A

**1.** State the trend in reducing power of the halide ions from Cl⁻ to I⁻. **[1]**

**2.** Write an ionic equation, with state symbols, for the formation of the precipitate when aqueous silver nitrate is added to a solution containing iodide ions. **[2]**

**3.** State the colour of the silver halide precipitate formed with:

**(a)** chloride ions **[1]**

**(b)** bromide ions **[1]**

**4.** Explain why hydrochloric acid must not be used to acidify a solution before testing it with silver nitrate. **[1]**

**5.** Give the oxidation number of sulfur in H₂SO₄ and in H₂S. **[2]**

**6.** Write the equation for the reaction of solid potassium bromide with concentrated sulfuric acid to form hydrogen bromide. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Three unlabelled solutions each contain one of NaCl, NaBr and NaI. Describe how you would identify each using aqueous silver nitrate and aqueous ammonia. Give the result for each solution. **[5]**

**8.** Explain why silver chloride dissolves in dilute aqueous ammonia but silver iodide does not dissolve even in concentrated aqueous ammonia. **[3]**

**9.** Concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium chloride and, separately, to solid sodium bromide.

**(a)** Describe what is seen in each case. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the products are different, in terms of reducing power. **[2]**

**10.** Hydrogen iodide reduces concentrated sulfuric acid to hydrogen sulfide.

**(a)** Write the half-equation for the reduction of H₂SO₄ to H₂S. **[1]**

**(b)** Write the half-equation for the oxidation of HI to I₂. **[1]**

**(c)** Combine them to give a balanced equation for the overall reaction. **[1]**

**(d)** State the change in oxidation number of sulfur. **[1]**

**11.** Write a balanced equation for the reaction of hydrogen iodide with concentrated sulfuric acid in which sulfur is the reduced product. **[2]**

**12.** A student says: "Iodide ions reduce concentrated sulfuric acid further than bromide ions do, because iodine is a stronger oxidising agent than bromine."

**(a)** Identify the error in the student's reasoning. **[1]**

**(b)** Give a correct explanation, referring to the sizes of the ions. **[3]**

**13.** A solution is thought to contain both chloride and iodide ions.

**(a)** Describe what you would see when it is acidified with dilute nitric acid and aqueous silver nitrate is added. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how dilute aqueous ammonia could then be used to confirm that both ions are present. **[2]**

**14.** Solid potassium iodide reacts with concentrated sulfuric acid. Write a balanced overall equation, starting from KI, in which the sulfur-containing product is SO₂. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Reducing power increases from Cl⁻ to I⁻ [1].

**2.** Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s) [1 for equation, 1 for correct state symbols].

**3. (a)** White [1].

**(b)** Cream [1].

**4.** It contains chloride ions, which would give a white precipitate of AgCl whatever the sample contained [1].

**5.** H₂SO₄: +6 [1]. H₂S: −2 [1].

**6.** KBr + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HBr [1].

**7.** Acidify each solution with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate [1]. NaCl gives a white precipitate, NaBr a cream precipitate and NaI a yellow precipitate [1]. Add dilute aqueous ammonia: only the white precipitate (chloride) dissolves [1]. Add concentrated aqueous ammonia to the remaining precipitates: the cream precipitate (bromide) dissolves [1]; the yellow precipitate (iodide) does not dissolve [1].

**8.** The solubility of the silver halides in water decreases from AgCl to AgI [1]. Ammonia forms a soluble complex with Ag⁺ ions, removing them from solution so that more solid dissolves [1]. AgCl is soluble enough for dilute ammonia to dissolve it, but AgI is so insoluble that even concentrated ammonia cannot lower the Ag⁺ concentration enough [1].

**9. (a)** NaCl: steamy fumes only [1]. NaBr: steamy fumes [1], with an orange-brown vapour and a colourless, choking gas [1].

**(b)** Cl⁻ is too weak a reducing agent to reduce sulfuric acid, so only HCl forms [1]. Br⁻ is a stronger reducing agent and reduces sulfur from +6 to +4, giving SO₂ and Br₂ [1].

**10. (a)** H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O [1].

**(b)** 2HI → I₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ (or 8HI → 4I₂ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻) [1].

**(c)** 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O [1].

**(d)** From +6 to −2 (a decrease of 8) [1].

**11.** 6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O [1 for correct species, 1 for balancing].

**12. (a)** It is the reducing power of the iodide ion that matters, not the oxidising power of iodine; iodine is actually a weaker oxidising agent than bromine [1].

**(b)** I⁻ is larger than Br⁻ [1], so its outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded [1]. They are lost more easily, so I⁻ is a stronger reducing agent and reduces sulfur further, to H₂S [1].

**13. (a)** A pale yellow (or cream-yellow) precipitate, a mixture of white AgCl and yellow AgI [1].

**(b)** Add dilute ammonia: the AgCl dissolves, so some of the precipitate disappears, confirming chloride [1]. A yellow precipitate remains that does not dissolve, confirming iodide (AgI) [1].

**14.** 2KI + 3H₂SO₄ → 2KHSO₄ + I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O [1 for correct species, 1 for balancing].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using hydrochloric acid to acidify before adding silver nitrate.
- Calling silver bromide white or yellow instead of cream.
- Not saying whether the ammonia used is dilute or concentrated.
- Treating the formation of HX as a redox step.
- Equations in which the electrons transferred do not balance.
