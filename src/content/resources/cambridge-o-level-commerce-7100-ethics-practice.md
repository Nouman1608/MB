## Section A

**1.** Define the term **ethics**. **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by **fair trade**. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** examples of misleading advertising. **[2]**

**4.** State **one** example of harmful disposal of a product. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** A cocoa farmer sells 800 kg. The market price is $1.10 per kg. The fair trade minimum price is $1.50 per kg and the fair trade premium is $0.20 per kg.

**(a)** Calculate the farmer's income at the market price and at the fair trade price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in income from fair trade (to the nearest whole number). **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the amount of fair trade premium for the community. **[1]**

**6.** Explain **two** benefits of fair trade to producers. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **one** disadvantage of fair trade for a retailer. **[2]**

**8.** A company advertises a skin cream as "clinically proven to remove wrinkles in a day". The claim is false. 600 customers each paid $30 and are given refunds, and the company is fined $9,000. Calculate the total cost to the company of the refunds and the fine. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why misleading advertising is an ethical issue. **[3]**

**10.** An electronics company throws old batteries into ordinary rubbish. Explain **two** harmful effects of this. **[4]**

**11.** Describe **two** ways in which a business could dispose of products in a more ethical way. **[4]**

**12.** A shop claims a plastic bag is "100% eco-friendly" although it is not. Name this type of ethical issue and explain the effect on customers. **[3]**

**13.** Discuss whether a small business should pay the extra cost of selling fair trade products. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Moral principles about what is right and wrong [1].

**2.** A system that gives producers (in developing countries) a fair price for their goods [1] and decent working conditions [1].

**3.** Any two from: false claims; exaggeration; hidden charges; fake reviews; misleading images; greenwashing [1 each].

**4.** Any one from: dumping chemicals in a river; throwing away electronic waste; burning plastic; sending unsold goods to landfill [1].

**5.** **(a)** Market: 800 × 1.10 = $880 [1]. Fair trade: 800 × 1.50 = $1,200 [1].

**(b)** Increase = 1,200 − 880 = $320 [1]. 320 ÷ 880 × 100 = 36% [1].

**(c)** 800 × 0.20 = $160 [1].

**6.** Any two developed: a guaranteed minimum price [1] protects the farmer when market prices fall, so income is more secure [1]; a premium for community projects [1] pays for schools or clean water, which improves living standards [1]; safe working conditions [1] protect workers' health [1]. Maximum 4.

**7.** Fair trade products cost the retailer more to buy [1], so the shop must either raise prices, which may reduce sales, or accept lower profit [1].

**8.** Refunds = 600 × 30 = $18,000 [1]. Fine = $9,000. Total = 18,000 + 9,000 [1] = $27,000 [1].

**9.** It gives customers a false impression of the product [1], which can make them waste money or be harmed [1]. It is unfair to honest competitors and breaks trust, so it is wrong even if sales rise [1].

**10.** Batteries contain harmful chemicals and metals [1] that can leak into soil and water, polluting them [1]. This can harm wildlife and people's health [1], and the company may face fines and a damaged reputation [1]. Maximum 4.

**11.** Any two developed: recycle products and materials [1] so that fewer harmful materials end up in landfill [1]; run a take-back scheme where customers return old goods [1] so they can be disposed of safely [1]; treat hazardous waste before disposal [1] so it does not pollute [1]; donate unsold goods [1] so they are used instead of wasted [1]. Maximum 4.

**12.** Misleading advertising (greenwashing) [1]. Customers believe they are helping the environment when they are not [1], so they may buy the product because of a false claim and lose trust when they find out [1].

**13.** Any five from: fair trade goods attract customers who care about producers [1]; they improve the shop's reputation [1]; they help producers get a fair income [1]; but they cost more [1]; prices may have to rise, so some customers will go elsewhere [1]; a small business may have less money to cover extra cost [1]; it can limit the range to a few fair trade lines [1]; a justified decision [1]. Maximum 5.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 5(b): dividing by the new income instead of the original.
- Question 5(c): using the whole 800 kg but the wrong rate.
- Question 8: giving only the refund or only the fine.
- Question 9: describing the advert without saying why it is wrong.
- Question 10: giving one effect only.
- Question 13: no judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can something be legal but unethical?** Yes. Ethics asks whether an action is right and fair to everyone affected, not only whether the law allows it.

**Is fair trade the same as charity?** No. Fair trade is a trading system. Producers are paid a fair price for goods, and a premium is paid for community projects.
