This guide covers **3.2.3 Energy resources** (statements a to d) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The [energy transfers, efficiency and Sankey diagrams guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-energy-transfers-efficiency-sankey-diagrams-and-energy/) summarises each energy source; this page goes deeper into choosing between fuels using data, calculating the useful energy from a fuel, weighing up energy sources across extraction, use and disposal, and writing balanced evaluations. Quick recall is in the [Energy resources revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-resources-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [Energy resources practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-resources-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Fuels are a useful store of energy; different fuels suit different situations, chosen by factors such as ease of storage, energy content and safety |
| b | When a fuel is used, some energy is transferred to the surroundings; some fuels are more efficient than others |
| c | A range of energy sources is used nationally and globally; their use has implications for society, including renewability and the environmental impacts of extraction, use and disposal |
| d | Technologies such as wave, solar and geothermal power provide energy renewably; identify advantages and drawbacks of these and other examples |

## Fuels as energy stores

A fuel stores energy that can be released when it is burned (or, for nuclear fuel, when its nuclei undergo fission). The specification names three selection factors. Questions add data and ask you to choose.

| Factor | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Ease of storage | Is it a solid, liquid or gas? Does it need high-pressure tanks or cooling? Does it degrade over time? |
| Energy content | How much energy is released per kilogram? A vehicle or aircraft needs a fuel that stores a lot of energy in a small mass. |
| Safety | Is it flammable at room temperature? Does it produce toxic products? Is it explosive if a container leaks? |

Other factors that a question may introduce include cost, availability and the waste products. Use the ones the question gives.

**Worked example 1: choosing a fuel from data.** The figures below are invented for this example.

| Fuel | Energy content (MJ/kg) | State at room temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 46 | Liquid | Flammable vapour |
| B | 50 | Gas | Must be stored under pressure |
| C | 20 | Solid | Burns slowly, smoky |

*Which fuel suits a portable camping stove?* Fuel A stores nearly as much energy per kilogram as B, and as a liquid it is easy to carry in a sealed bottle without a pressure container. C would need far more mass for the same energy. A good answer compares at least two fuels on at least two factors, then reaches a conclusion.

## Fuel efficiency

Statement b links fuels to section 3.2.2: when a fuel is used, only part of the energy released does the useful job; the rest heats the surroundings.

```
useful energy = efficiency x energy released
energy released = mass of fuel x energy content
```

**Worked example 2: two heaters.** A heater burns 0.50 kg of a fuel with an energy content of 44 MJ/kg. Its efficiency is 0.80. A second heater burns the same mass of fuel at an efficiency of 0.55. (Figures invented for the example.)

```
energy released = 0.50 x 44 = 22 MJ
heater 1 useful energy = 0.80 x 22 = 17.6 MJ
heater 2 useful energy = 0.55 x 22 = 12.1 MJ
```

The first heater delivers 5.5 MJ more useful energy from the same fuel, so it costs less to run and releases fewer combustion products for the same heating.

## Energy sources at national and global scale

Electricity and heat are produced from a range of sources. The specification asks you to consider the **implications for society**, including renewability and the environmental impacts of **extraction, use and disposal**. Organising answers under those three headings keeps them balanced.

| Source | Renewable? | Extraction | Use | Disposal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | No | Mining scars land and can be dangerous | Burning releases carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide | Ash must be disposed of |
| Oil | No | Drilling; risk of spills | Burning releases carbon dioxide | Spilled oil harms wildlife |
| Natural gas | No | Drilling and pipelines | Burning releases carbon dioxide, less than coal for the same energy | Little solid waste |
| Nuclear fuel | No | Mining uranium | No carbon dioxide from generation; reliable output | Radioactive waste must be stored safely for a very long time |
| Biofuel | Yes, if replanted | Needs land for crops | Releases carbon dioxide, but growing plants absorb carbon dioxide | Little waste |

**Renewable** means the resource is replenished as it is used, so it will not run out. **Non-renewable** resources, such as fossil fuels and nuclear fuel, are being used up faster than they can be replaced.

Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas linked to global warming; sulfur dioxide contributes to acid rain. These are the two products to name when explaining the environmental effects of burning fossil fuels.

## Renewable technologies

Statement d names wave, solar and geothermal power, and asks for "these and other examples". Know at least the six below.

| Technology | How it provides energy | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wave power | Waves move floating generators or push air through turbines | No fuel cost, no carbon dioxide during operation | Output varies with weather; can affect coastal habitats; only at suitable coasts |
| Solar power | Solar cells produce electricity; solar panels heat water | No fuel; suits remote places | No output at night; less on cloudy days |
| Geothermal power | Hot rocks underground heat water to make steam for turbines, or heat buildings | Reliable, continuous output | Only practical where hot rocks are near the surface |
| Wind power | Wind turns turbine blades | No fuel, no carbon dioxide during operation | No output in calm weather; visual and noise effects |
| Hydroelectric power | Water stored behind a dam flows through turbines | Reliable; output can be started quickly | Flooding a valley destroys habitats and land |
| Tidal power | Tides flowing in and out of an estuary turn turbines | Tides are predictable | Few suitable sites; affects estuary habitats |

**Worked example 3: energy from a hydroelectric scheme.** In one hour, 2.0 × 10⁵ kg of water falls 50 m through the turbines of a small scheme. The scheme is 85% efficient. Take g = 9.8 N/kg.

```
Ep lost by water = m g h = 2.0 x 10^5 x 9.8 x 50 = 9.8 x 10^7 J
electrical energy = 0.85 x 9.8 x 10^7 = 8.33 x 10^7 J
```

The calculation uses the Ep equation from section 3.2.1 and efficiency from section 3.2.2; questions on energy resources often combine them.

## Writing an evaluation

An **evaluate** or **discuss** question about energy resources usually rewards:

1. Points for and against each option, linked to the data or context given.
2. Comparisons, not lists: "wave power is less reliable than geothermal because…".
3. A justified conclusion: which option you would choose for this situation, and why.

**Worked example 4: an island's electricity.** An island has strong winds, cloudy winters and no hot rocks near the surface. Geothermal power is ruled out by the geology. Solar output would be low in winter. Wind turbines suit the strong winds, but output falls in calm weather, so a back-up source is needed. A conclusion might recommend wind power with a reliable back-up, explaining why.

## Common mistakes

- Calling nuclear power renewable.
- Saying renewable sources cause "no pollution at all"; building them still has environmental impacts.
- Giving only advantages, or only drawbacks, when the question asks for both.
- Writing "it is cheap" without explaining why (for example, no fuel to buy).
- Ignoring the context: a solar farm is a weak choice for a country with long, dark winters.
- Muddling energy content (per kilogram) with total energy released.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Give** an advantage or drawback: one clear point is enough, but make it specific.
- **Compare**: a statement about both options, such as "coal is non-renewable, whereas wind is renewable".
- **Suggest**: apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation using the data provided.
- **Evaluate**: both sides, then a conclusion with a reason.
