These notes condense section 5.3 of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: 5.3.1 factors affecting population growth, 5.3.2 why rates differ between countries and 5.3.3 the effects of changes in the size and structure of population. You must interpret a population pyramid but not draw one. All figures are invented. Full explanations and a model answer are in the [population study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-population-cambridge-igcse-economics/); test yourself with the [population practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-population-cambridge-igcse-economics-practice/). ## 5.3.1 Core recall

- Population changes through **births**, **deaths** and **net migration**.
- **Net migration** = immigration (people arriving to live) − emigration (people leaving to live elsewhere).
- Rates are per 1,000 people per year; divide by 10 for a percentage.

```
Natural increase rate = birth rate - death rate
Growth rate           = natural increase rate + net migration rate
```

**Example (invented).** Birth rate 21, death rate 7, net migration −3 per 1,000.
Natural increase = 14 per 1,000. Growth = 11 per 1,000 = **1.1%**. On a population of 40 million: 40,000,000 × 0.011 = **440,000** more people.

**From numbers to rates.** 96,000 births in a population of 6 million: 96,000 ÷ 6,000,000 × 1,000 = **16 per 1,000**.

## 5.3.2 Why rates differ

| Rate | Higher where... | Lower where... |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | Children provide farm labour and old-age support; infant mortality is high; contraception is scarce; early marriage | Incomes are high; women are educated and in paid work; pensions exist; contraception is available |
| Death rate | Healthcare, sanitation and diet are poor; disease or conflict; many elderly people | Good healthcare, vaccination, clean water, young population |
| Net migration | Jobs, wages and safety attract immigrants | Conflict, low pay or unemployment drive emigration |

A rich country can have a higher death rate than a poor one because of its older age structure.

## 5.3.3 Optimum population

- **Optimum:** the population at which output per head is highest, given resources, capital and technology.
- **Under-population:** below the optimum; more people would raise output per head.
- **Over-population:** above the optimum; output per head falls as population rises.
- The optimum moves up with new technology or investment, and down with loss of resources.

## 5.3.3 Effects of change

| Change | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Larger population | More workers, bigger market, more taxpayers | Pressure on housing and services, congestion, unemployment, lower output per head beyond optimum |
| Smaller population | Less pressure on resources; higher output per head if over-populated | Labour shortages, smaller market, fewer taxpayers |
| Ageing population | Demand for care and leisure services; experienced workforce | Higher pension and health spending; fewer workers per pensioner; labour shortages |
| Young population | Large future labour force | Spending on schools now; need for many jobs later; risk of youth unemployment |
| Gender imbalance | More workers in industries that attract the larger group | Labour shortages in some industries; more elderly women needing care; possible fall in births |

**Workers per pensioner:** working-age population ÷ population over 65. A fall from 5 to 2.5 doubles the pension cost per worker if the pension is unchanged.

## Reading a pyramid

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wide base, narrow top | High birth and death rates; young population |
| Narrow base, wide top | Low birth rate, long life expectancy; ageing population |
| Base narrower than band above | Birth rate has recently fallen |
| Bulge | Period of high births or immigration of that age |
| Notch | Low births, emigration, war or disease |
| Female side wider at the top | Women live longer |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Birth rate | Live births per 1,000 people per year |
| Death rate | Deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Immigration | People entering a country to live |
| Emigration | People leaving a country to live elsewhere |
| Net migration | Immigration minus emigration |
| Natural increase | Births minus deaths |
| Optimum population | Population giving the highest output per head |
| Population pyramid | Chart of the share of each age group by sex |

## Exam traps

- Confusing the number of births with the birth rate.
- Leaving out net migration from total growth.
- Calling the optimum "the most people a country can support".
- Explaining a high death rate in a rich country through poor healthcare.
- Describing a pyramid's shape without saying what caused it or what it means for the economy.
- Assuming that more people is always good or always bad.

## Quick self-test

1. Define net migration.
2. Birth rate 28, death rate 10, net migration +2 per 1,000. Calculate the growth rate as a percentage.
3. 45,000 deaths occur in a population of 5 million. Calculate the death rate per 1,000.
4. Give two reasons why birth rates are often higher in low-income countries.
5. Why might a high-income country have a higher death rate than a low-income one?
6. Define optimum population.
7. How can new technology change a country's optimum population?
8. Give two costs of an ageing population.
9. Working-age population 24 million, over-65 population 6 million. Calculate workers per pensioner.
10. What might a notch in the 20–29 band of a pyramid show?
11. Give one benefit of a rising population for firms.
12. Why does an increase in births not add to the labour force immediately?

### Answers

1. Immigration minus emigration.
2. 28 − 10 + 2 = 20 per 1,000 = 2.0%.
3. 45,000 ÷ 5,000,000 × 1,000 = 9 per 1,000.
4. Any two: children provide labour or old-age support; high infant mortality; limited contraception; early marriage; cultural preference.
5. It may have many elderly people, who have a higher chance of dying.
6. The population size at which output per head is highest.
7. It raises output per worker, so the optimum rises.
8. Any two: higher pension spending; higher healthcare spending; fewer workers per pensioner; labour shortages.
9. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 workers per pensioner.
10. Emigration of young adults, low births 20 to 29 years earlier, or deaths from war or disease.
11. A larger market, allowing more sales and economies of scale (or a larger supply of workers).
12. Babies only join the labour force after they reach working age, about 15 years later.
