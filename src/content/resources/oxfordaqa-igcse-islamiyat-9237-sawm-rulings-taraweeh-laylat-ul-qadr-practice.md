## Answers

**How the parts are marked.** One mark for each correct answer in the 1- and 2-mark questions. In
the 4-mark questions, one mark for each point identified and one for explaining it, up to two marks
for each of the two (question 5: two marks for each term, defined and developed). The 6-mark
questions give four AO1 marks on that pattern plus up to two AO2 marks: **one** for a general,
accurate use of a source of authority, **two** for a specific use applied to the question. The
12-mark questions are marked by levels:

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Thorough examination of a range of points; logical reasoning with detailed evidence; excellent, direct use of the passage |
| 3 | 7–9 | Clear examination of more than one point; reasoning with some detail; good use of the passage |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some accurate examination; reasoning not always supported; general use of the passage. **Maximum Level 2 if only one point is made** |
| 1 | 1–3 | Limited examination with little reasoning. **Maximum Level 1 if the passage is not used** |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit |

### Section A

**1.** Suhoor [1].

**2.** Surah al-Qadr, Surah 97 [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: eating or drinking by genuine mistake; unintentional vomiting; brushing the teeth without swallowing water; swallowing one's own saliva; having a bath or shower; a sexual dream during sleep.

**4.** Any two, [1] each: taqwa or God-consciousness; nearness to Allah; forgiveness of sins; more time for the Qur'an; gratitude to Allah; sincerity, since the fast is known to Allah.

**5.** **Qada** is making up a missed fast by fasting the same number of days later [1], owed by those with a temporary reason such as illness, travel or menstruation [1]. **Fidya** is feeding a poor person for each day missed [1], paid by those who will never be able to fast, such as the very old or permanently ill, as in Qur'an 2:184 [1].

**6.** For each of two ways: way identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Staying in the mosque** [1] for the last ten days of Ramadhan, leaving only for necessities [1].
- **Increasing worship** [1]: extra prayer, Qur'an recitation and dhikr fill the time [1].
- **Avoiding worldly matters** [1]: no business, idle talk or marital relations (2:187), so attention stays on Allah [1].
- **Seeking Laylat ul-Qadr** [1]: being in worship through the odd nights so as not to miss it [1].

**7.** For each of two benefits: benefit identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Self-control** [1]: resisting food and drink trains a person to resist other wrong desires [1].
- **Honesty** [1]: a person could eat in secret, so keeping the fast shows integrity when unseen [1].
- **Patience** [1]: coping with hunger and tiredness builds patience in other hardships [1].
- **Control of speech and anger** [1]: the Prophet (pbuh) taught that a fasting person should not argue or speak falsely [1].

**8.** For each of two reasons: reason identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Recitation of the Qur'an** [1]: many mosques complete the whole Qur'an over the month in Taraweeh [1].
- **Forgiveness** [1]: standing in prayer in Ramadhan with faith brings forgiveness of past sins, according to the Hadith [1].
- **Community** [1]: Muslims gather nightly in the mosque, strengthening the Ummah [1].
- **Following the Sunnah** [1]: the Prophet (pbuh) led it, and the companions continued it under Hazrat Umar (RA) [1].

### Section B

**9.** AO1 **[4]**: for each of two reasons, the reason identified [1] and explained [1]. AO2 **[2]**: one source of authority used generally [1] or specifically and applied [2].

Reasons (AO1):

- **The Qur'an was revealed on it** [1], so it marks the start of the guidance that shapes Muslim life [1].
- **Its worship is multiplied** [1]: one night is worth more than a lifetime of worship [1].
- **Forgiveness** [1]: those who pray on it with faith and hope of reward are forgiven past sins [1].
- **Decree for the year** [1]: Muslims believe matters are decreed on it, so they pray for a good year [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- "The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months" (Qur'an 97:3), so Muslims spend it in prayer [2].
- The Prophet (pbuh) said to seek it in the odd nights of the last ten (Sahih al-Bukhari), which explains why worship intensifies at the end of Ramadhan [2].

**10.** AO1 **[4]**: as in question 9. AO2 **[2]**: as in question 9.

Reasons (AO1):

- **Empathy with the poor** [1]: feeling hunger makes Muslims understand those who go without, which leads to giving [1].
- **More charity** [1]: Zakat-ul-Fitr is paid before Eid so the poor can celebrate [1].
- **Unity** [1]: the whole Ummah fasts and breaks the fast together at the same times [1].
- **Family and community ties** [1]: shared iftar at home and in mosques brings people together [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- "Upon those who are able [to fast, but with hardship] – a ransom ... of feeding a poor person" (Qur'an 2:184): even the compensation for fasting helps the poor [2].
- The Prophet (pbuh) taught that whoever gives a fasting person food to break the fast gets a reward like theirs (reported by Tirmidhi), which encourages shared iftar [2].

**11.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **Exemptions** for the ill, travellers, the old, pregnant and breastfeeding women and children show compassion.
- **Forgetful eating** does not break the fast, so Muslims are not punished for genuine mistakes.
- **Fidya** gives a way to take part for those who can never fast.
- **Against the statement:** fasting is still demanding: long days without food or water, especially in summer or far from the equator; deliberate breaking carries kaffarah. Ease does not mean no effort.
- **Balanced view:** Islam asks for real effort but never more than a person can bear; hardship has a purpose (taqwa) and limits.

*Use of the passage*

- "Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship" directly supports the statement.
- "Whoever among you is ill or on a journey ... an equal number of days" shows that concessions are built into the command.
- "A ransom ... of feeding a poor person" shows an alternative for those who find fasting a hardship.
- "A limited number of days" shows that the duty is bounded.
- "To fast is best for you" shows that the effort itself is good for the believer.

**12.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- The Qur'an was **revealed in Ramadhan**, on Laylat ul-Qadr.
- **Taraweeh** often completes the Qur'an across the month; many Muslims read it in full.
- **Itikaaf** gives time for recitation and reflection.
- **Against the statement:** the passage gives taqwa as the purpose of fasting; other aims include self-discipline, gratitude, empathy and forgiveness; Ramadhan is also about obedience to the fourth pillar.
- **Balanced view:** closeness to the Qur'an and taqwa support each other: the Qur'an guides, fasting trains the self to follow it.

*Use of the passage*

- "The month of Ramadhan [is that] in which was revealed the Qur'an" links the month directly to the Qur'an.
- "A guidance for the people and clear proofs of guidance and criterion" explains why the Qur'an is central.
- "That you may become righteous" offers a different main purpose, taqwa.
- "To glorify Allah for that [to] which He has guided you" shows gratitude for the guidance of the Qur'an as an aim of the month.

An answer that explains a range of points, reasons about their implications and uses the passage directly reaches Level 4.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying that any eating, even by mistake, breaks the fast.
- Confusing qada, fidya and kaffarah.
- Describing Taraweeh or Itikaaf without explaining its significance.
- Giving a fixed date for Laylat ul-Qadr.
- In the 12-mark questions, quoting the passage without explaining what each phrase shows.
