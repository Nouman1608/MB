## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a price taker and a price maker. **[2]**

**2.** A firm is the only seller of a product. Its demand schedule is below. Marginal cost is a constant £20 per unit and fixed costs are £120.

| Output (Q) | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price (£) | 90 | 80 | 70 | 60 | 50 |

**(a)** Calculate marginal revenue between 6 and 8 units. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the profit-maximising output, price and profit. **[4]**
**(c)** Calculate price elasticity of demand at that output and comment on its value. **[2]**

**3.** Explain, with reference to a monopoly diagram, why a profit-maximising monopolist charges a price above marginal cost. **[4]**

**4.** Explain two factors that influence the degree of monopoly power a firm has. **[4]**

**5.** Two hairdressing salons, A and B, are in different towns. Both have marginal cost of £8. Salon A faces demand P = 40 − 2Q. Salon B, with many nearby rivals, faces P = 24 − Q.

**(a)** Find each salon's profit-maximising output, price and mark-up over marginal cost. **[3]**
**(b)** State which has greater monopoly power and explain why. **[2]**

**6.** Market demand for a product is P = 90 − 3Q. A competitive industry has constant marginal cost of £60. One firm then replaces it, with marginal cost £24 because of economies of scale.

**(a)** State the competitive price and output. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the monopoly price and output. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that the monopoly price equals (90 + MC)/2 and find the marginal cost at which the monopoly price equals the competitive price. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why consumers may not gain in practice. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why a cinema can price discriminate between adults and children, but a village shop selling tins of food cannot. **[4]**

**8.** A software seller has marginal cost of £10 per licence. Business users face P = 50 − Q₁ and students face P = 30 − Q₂. Quantities are in hundreds of licences.

**(a)** Write the marginal revenue function for each group. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the output and price in each group if the firm price discriminates. **[4]**
**(c)** Show that profit exceeds the profit from a single price of £25, at which 30 hundred licences are sold. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe the effect on each group compared with the single price. **[2]**

**9.** A firm faces demand P = 84 − 4Q with marginal cost £20 and no fixed costs.

**(a)** Find output, price and profit if it charges one price. **[3]**
**(b)** Find output and profit if it practises perfect price discrimination. **[2]**
**(c)** Compare consumer surplus in the two cases. **[1]**

**10.** Evaluate whether price discrimination is in the interests of consumers. **[9]**

**11.** A firm holds a patent on a medicine with no close substitute. Evaluate whether its high monopoly power is likely to benefit or harm society. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A price taker has no influence over price and must accept the market price, because demand for its product is perfectly elastic [1]. A price maker chooses its price, because it has market power and a downward-sloping demand curve [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason for each half, not just a label: link "price maker" to the slope of demand.

**2. (a)** TR at 6 units = 6 × 70 = £420 and TR at 8 units = 8 × 60 = £480 [1]. MR = (480 − 420)/2 = **£30** per unit [1].
**(b)** The demand line is P = 100 − 5Q, so MR = 100 − 10Q [1]. Set MR = MC: 100 − 10Q = 20, so **Q = 8** [1]. Price = 100 − 40 = **£60** [1]. Profit = TR − TC = 480 − (120 + 20 × 8) = 480 − 280 = **£200** [1].
**(c)** PED = (1/5) × (60/8) = **1.5** [1]. It is elastic, which is consistent with profit maximisation: MR is positive (£20) at this output, and a profit-maximiser never operates where demand is inelastic [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the TR and TC lines so the working shows. In part (c), link the value to MR being positive.

**3.** The monopolist faces a downward-sloping AR curve, so the MR curve lies below it [1]. It maximises profit at the output where MR = MC [1]. The price is read from the AR curve above that output, so price exceeds MC [1]. At this output, consumers value an extra unit above its marginal cost, but the firm does not produce it because the lower price needed would reduce profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the curve you read the price from. Stopping at "the monopolist restricts output" misses the diagram link.

**4.** Barriers to entry [1]: patents, high sunk costs or economies of scale stop rivals entering, so the firm keeps supernormal profit and can keep price above MC [1]. Product differentiation [1]: a distinctive product has fewer close substitutes, so demand is less price elastic and the firm can raise price with a smaller loss of sales [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow each factor with its effect on elasticity or profit. A bare list is thin.

**5. (a)** Salon A: MR = 40 − 4Q = 8, so Q = 8; P = 40 − 16 = £24; mark-up = £16 [1]. Salon B: MR = 24 − 2Q = 8, so Q = 8; P = 24 − 8 = £16; mark-up = £8 [1]. Both have the same output; the prices differ [1].
**(b)** Salon A has more monopoly power [1] because its mark-up (£16, or 66.7% of price) is larger, reflecting less elastic demand (PED 1.5 against 2.0) and fewer close substitutes [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Price maker" describes both salons, so the comparison should be about the size of the gap above marginal cost and why.

**6. (a)** Competitive: P = MC, so 90 − 3Q = 60, **Q = 10** [1] and **P = £60** [1].
**(b)** MR = 90 − 6Q = 24, so **Q = 11** [1]; P = 90 − 33 = **£57** [1].
**(c)** MR = MC gives Q = (90 − MC)/6, so P = 90 − 3 × (90 − MC)/6 = 90 − (90 − MC)/2 = (90 + MC)/2 [1]. Setting this equal to 60 gives 90 + MC = 120, so **MC = £30** [1].
**(d)** The saving must be large: at MC = £40 the monopoly price is £65, above £60 [1]. Even a large saving may be kept as profit unless regulation or the threat of entry forces it to be passed on [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (d), a worked threshold such as the £30 above is more persuasive than saying economies of scale "might" help.

**7.** Cinema: it can separate customers by age using proof of age or ticket type [1]; the ticket is for one showing and cannot sensibly be resold [1]; children and adults are likely to have different price elasticities of demand [1]. Village shop: goods such as tins can be bought at a low price and resold, and the shop cannot tell who belongs to which group, so the conditions fail [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the conditions to the named businesses. The shop contrast needs its own reason.

**8. (a)** Business: MR₁ = 50 − 2Q₁ [1]. Students: MR₂ = 30 − 2Q₂ [1].
**(b)** Business: 50 − 2Q₁ = 10, so **Q₁ = 20** [1]; P = 50 − 20 = **£30** [1]. Students: 30 − 2Q₂ = 10, so **Q₂ = 10** [1]; P = 30 − 10 = **£20** [1].
**(c)** Profit = (30 − 10) × 20 + (20 − 10) × 10 = 400 + 100 = 500, so **£50,000** as quantities are in hundreds [1]. At a single price of £25: (25 − 10) × 30 = 450, so **£45,000**, which is lower [1].
**(d)** Business users pay £5 (20%) more than the single price and buy 20 instead of 25 [1]. Students pay £5 (20%) less and buy 10 instead of 5 [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both profit figures when saying profit rises, and state units.

**9. (a)** MR = 84 − 8Q = 20, so **Q = 8** [1]; P = 84 − 32 = **£52** [1]; profit = (52 − 20) × 8 = **£256** [1].
**(b)** Output rises to where P = MC: 84 − 4Q = 20, so **Q = 16** [1]. Revenue is the area under demand, (84 + 20)/2 × 16 = 832, and cost is 20 × 16 = 320, so profit = **£512** [1].
**(c)** Consumer surplus is ½ × (84 − 52) × 8 = £128 under a single price and **zero** under perfect price discrimination [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Do the calculation first, then say what the zero means: the firm takes all the surplus.

**10.** Price discrimination means charging different prices for the same product that are not due to cost differences [1]. Some consumers gain: the group with more elastic demand, such as students or off-peak users, pays a lower price and may gain access to a service [1]. Spare capacity can be used, which may keep a service funded [1]. Output may rise in total if a new market is served [1]. Other consumers lose: the group with less elastic demand, such as business travellers or those who must travel at peak times, pays more [1]. Consumer surplus is transferred to the producer [1]. Evaluation: whether total output rises is the key test, since discrimination that only reshuffles a fixed quantity gives consumers little overall [1]. It matters how the extra profit is used: reinvested in better service or kept as dividend [1]. A judgement, such as that it is in the interests of consumers when it widens access and funds an otherwise unviable service, but not when the higher-paying group has no alternative [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Say who gains and who loses, then weigh them. Two lists without a judgement stay descriptive.

**11.** A patent is a legal barrier to entry, so the firm is the only seller [1]. It sets MR = MC and charges a price above MC, with output below the competitive level [1]. Supernormal profit is earned [1]. Because price exceeds marginal cost, resources are not allocated efficiently and there is a welfare loss [1]. On the other side, the patent rewards the firm for costly research, so supernormal profit funds further development (dynamic efficiency) and encourages invention [1]. Without protection, rivals could copy the medicine, and the incentive to invest would fall [1]. Evaluation: patents last for a limited time, after which competition can lower the price [1]. Whether the profit is reinvested in research is uncertain [1]. A medicine has few substitutes and demand is probably inelastic, so power is high and patients and health budgets are exposed [1]. Overall, the benefit depends on the length of protection, reinvestment and any regulation of the price [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply both sides to a medicine, not to monopoly in general, and name what the outcome depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the monopoly price off the MR curve when it belongs on the AR curve.
- Using MR = AR, or drawing MR above demand.
- Describing barriers to entry without saying how they protect profit.
- Calling every firm with a downward-sloping demand curve a monopoly, not a price maker with some power.
- Reciting the conditions for price discrimination without applying them to the business in the question.
- Saying price discrimination always benefits the firm and harms consumers, without weighing groups separately.
- Omitting units, or forgetting fixed costs.
