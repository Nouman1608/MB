## Section A

**1.** Give **two** reasons why judges need to interpret statutes. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** intrinsic aids and **two** extrinsic aids. **[4]**

**3.** State the case from which the mischief rule comes. **[1]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by the rule of language *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain the literal rule, using **one** case where it produced an unjust result. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the difference between the narrow and broad approaches to the golden rule, with a case for each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the conditions under which *Hansard* may be consulted, following *Pepper v Hart* (1993). **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the courts' duty under section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 affects statutory interpretation. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how membership of the European Union influenced the approach of English courts to statutory interpretation, and its significance since withdrawal. **[4]**

**10.** The Parks Act 2026 states: "No person shall bring a dog, cat or other animal into a children's play area." Fatima brings her guide dog into a play area to collect her child. Tom brings a pet snake. Apply **two** rules of statutory interpretation and **one** rule of language to advise whether each has committed an offence. **[8]**

**11.** The Public Safety Act 2026 makes it an offence to "use a drone within 100 metres of a hospital". Leon flies a drone from inside the hospital grounds. Explain how the golden rule and the purposive approach would apply. **[5]**

**12.** "The purposive approach is the best approach to statutory interpretation." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: the words may be broad or ambiguous [1]; drafting errors; new technology or situations Parliament did not foresee; changes in the meaning of words over time [1].

**2.** Intrinsic, any two: long title, short title, preamble, headings, interpretation sections, schedules [1] [1]. Extrinsic, any two: dictionaries, *Hansard*, Law Commission or committee reports, earlier statutes, explanatory notes, treaties [1] [1].

**3.** *Heydon's Case* (1584) [1].

**4.** Expressly mentioning one or more things of a particular class excludes other things of that class not mentioned [1], as in *R v Inhabitants of Sedgley* (1831), where "coal mines" excluded other types of mine [1].

**5.** Under the literal rule the words of the Act are given their plain, ordinary, dictionary meaning [1], even if this produces an absurd or unjust result, because the judge's role is to apply what Parliament has said [1]. In *LNER v Berriman* (1946) a railway worker was killed while oiling points, and the Act required a lookout for workers "relaying or repairing" track [1]. Oiling was maintenance, not relaying or repairing, so his widow could not recover compensation, an unjust result [1]. (Also accept *Whiteley v Chappell* or *Fisher v Bell*.)

**6.** The narrow approach applies where a word has more than one meaning, and the court chooses the meaning that avoids absurdity [1]; in *R v Allen* (1872) "marry" in the bigamy offence meant to go through a ceremony of marriage [1]. The broad approach applies where the words have one meaning but it would produce a repugnant result, so the court modifies it [1]; in *Re Sigsworth* (1935) a son who murdered his mother could not inherit as her "issue" [1].

**7.** The legislation must be ambiguous or obscure, or its literal meaning lead to absurdity [1]; the material relied on must be a statement by a minister or other promoter of the Bill [1]; and the statement must be clear [1].

**8.** Section 3 requires courts to read and give effect to legislation in a way compatible with Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so" [1]. This applies even where the words are not ambiguous, so it is stronger than the ordinary rules [1]; in *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* (2004) "as his or her wife or husband" was read to include a same-sex partner [1]. If a compatible reading is impossible, a higher court may make a declaration of incompatibility under section 4, but the Act remains in force [1].

**9.** While the UK was a member, courts had to interpret national legislation as far as possible to give effect to EU law [1], which encouraged a purposive approach and sometimes reading words into legislation, as in *Pickstone v Freemans* (1988) [1]. Since withdrawal this duty has largely ended for new law [1], but it helped make the purposive approach the dominant method of English judges [1].

**10.** Fatima: literally, a guide dog is a "dog", so she has committed the offence [1]. Under the mischief or purposive approach, the Act aims to protect children from fouling and animal-related harm in play areas, and a trained guide dog accompanying a disabled parent is not the mischief targeted [1]; excluding a disabled parent from collecting her child would also be absurd, supporting the golden rule [1]. She is probably not guilty under these approaches [1]. Tom: applying ejusdem generis, "other animal" follows "dog, cat", so it may be limited to animals of the same kind, such as common domestic pets [1]. A pet snake is arguably a domestic pet, so it may be within the class [1]; literally a snake is plainly an "animal" [1]. Tom is probably guilty under most approaches [1].

**11.** Literally, flying a drone inside the grounds may not be "within 100 metres of" the hospital if the court reads the words as referring only to places outside it [1]. Under the golden rule, it would be absurd for flying inside the grounds to be lawful while flying nearby is an offence [1], so, as in *Adler v George* (1964), the words would be read as "in or within 100 metres of" [1]. Under the purposive approach, Parliament's purpose is to protect hospitals, including air ambulances, from drones [1], so Leon's conduct falls within the Act and he is likely to be guilty [1].

**12.** For: it allows judges to achieve what Parliament intended rather than defeating it through wording, avoiding results like *Berriman* [1]; it deals with new technology and situations, as in *Quintavalle* (2003) [1]; it is consistent with the approach required by section 3 of the Human Rights Act and the former EU duty [1]; it allows wide use of extrinsic aids such as *Hansard* and explanatory notes [1]. Against: Parliament's purpose can be hard to identify, especially for broad Acts [1]; it gives judges more discretion, risking judicial law-making contrary to parliamentary supremacy [1]; it reduces predictability because outcomes depend on the judge's view of purpose [1]; the literal rule respects the words Parliament actually chose [1]. Conclusion: the purposive approach is usually the best for achieving justice, but should be used within the limits of the words so that judges do not replace Parliament's choices with their own [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a rule with no outcome for the person in the scenario.
- Applying only one rule when the question asks for more.
- Forgetting that ejusdem generis needs a list of at least two specific words before the general words.
- In essays, describing all four approaches instead of evaluating the one named.
