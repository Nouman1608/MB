## Section A

**1.** Define threshold wavelength. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the photoelectric equation refers to the **maximum** kinetic energy of the emitted electrons. **[2]**

**3.** A metal surface has a work function of 2.8 eV.

**(a)** Calculate the threshold frequency. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the threshold wavelength. **[1]**

**4.** Radiation of wavelength 380 nm falls on the metal in Question 3. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, in J and in eV. **[3]**

**5.** Calculate the maximum speed of the photoelectrons in Question 4. **[2]**

**6.** Radiation of a fixed frequency above the threshold frequency falls on a metal. Its intensity is increased. Which quantity increases?

A: the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons

B: the work function of the metal

C: the number of photoelectrons emitted per second

D: the threshold frequency

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain photoelectric emission in terms of photon energy and work function. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why no electrons are emitted when the frequency is below the threshold frequency, however intense the radiation. **[2]**

**8.** Ultraviolet radiation of wavelength 300 nm falls on a metal. The fastest photoelectrons have a speed of 5.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the energy of one photon. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the work function, in eV. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the threshold wavelength. **[1]**

**9.** A graph of maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons against frequency of radiation for a metal is a straight line. It crosses the frequency axis at 5.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz, and its gradient is 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.

**(a)** Calculate the work function in J and in eV. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons for radiation of frequency 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz. **[2]**

**(c)** A second metal has a larger work function. Describe how its line on the same axes compares with the first. **[2]**

**10.** Light of wavelength 400 nm and power 3.0 mW falls on a metal surface whose threshold wavelength is longer than 400 nm. The photoelectric current is 1.2 μA.

**(a)** Calculate the number of photons arriving per second. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of electrons emitted per second. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the fraction of photons that release an electron. **[1]**

**(d)** The intensity is doubled without changing the wavelength. State the effect on the current and on the maximum kinetic energy. **[2]**

**11.** Explain why the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is independent of the intensity of the radiation, whereas the photoelectric current is proportional to the intensity. **[4]**

**12.** State **three** features of photoelectric emission that cannot be explained if electromagnetic radiation is treated only as a wave. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The maximum wavelength of electromagnetic radiation [1] that causes the emission of electrons from the surface of a metal [1].

**2.** The work function is the minimum energy needed to free an electron, which applies to electrons at the surface [1]. Electrons from deeper in the metal lose energy in collisions before escaping, so they have less kinetic energy; only surface electrons leave with hf − Φ [1].

**3. (a)** Φ = 2.8 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.48 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; f₀ = Φ/h = 6.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz [1].

**(b)** λ₀ = c/f₀ = 4.4 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**4.** hf = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 380 × 10⁻⁹ = 5.23 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; max KE = 5.23 × 10⁻¹⁹ − 4.48 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 7.5 × 10⁻²⁰ J [1]; = 0.47 eV [1].

**5.** v = √(2 × 7.54 × 10⁻²⁰ ÷ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) [1] = 4.1 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹ [1].

**6.** C [1].

**7. (a)** Radiation consists of photons, each of energy hf [1]. A photon gives all its energy to a single electron [1]. If hf is at least the work function (the minimum energy to remove an electron from the surface), the electron is emitted, with the surplus as kinetic energy [1].

**(b)** Below f₀ each photon has energy less than the work function [1]. An electron absorbs one photon at a time and cannot accumulate energy from several, so more photons (greater intensity) cannot free any electron [1].

**8. (a)** E = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 300 × 10⁻⁹ = 6.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1].

**(b)** ½mv² = 0.5 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (5.0 × 10⁵)² = 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1].

**(c)** Φ = 6.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ − 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.49 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.4 eV [1].

**(d)** λ₀ = hc/Φ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 5.49 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**9. (a)** Φ = hf₀ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.5 × 10¹⁴ = 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; = 2.3 eV [1].

**(b)** Max KE = h(f − f₀) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × (8.0 − 5.5) × 10¹⁴ [1] = 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (1.0 eV) [1].

**(c)** The line has the same gradient (it is parallel) [1] but crosses the frequency axis at a higher frequency [1].

**10. (a)** Photon energy = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 400 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; photons per second = 3.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.0 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 7.5 × 10¹² s⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** 7.5 × 10¹² ÷ 6.0 × 10¹⁵ = 1.2 × 10⁻³ [1].

**(d)** The current doubles [1]; the maximum kinetic energy is unchanged [1].

**11.** Each electron is emitted by absorbing a single photon [1]. The energy of a photon, hf, depends only on the frequency, so the maximum kinetic energy hf − Φ does not depend on intensity [1]. Increasing the intensity at a fixed frequency increases the number of photons arriving per second [1], so more electrons are emitted per second and the current increases in proportion [1].

**12.** Any three of: there is a threshold frequency below which no emission occurs, whatever the intensity [1]; the maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency, not intensity [1]; emission begins immediately, with no delay, even at very low intensity [1]; (or) the maximum kinetic energy increases linearly with frequency.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining the work function without "minimum" or "from the surface".
- Subtracting a work function in eV from a photon energy in J.
- Calling the threshold wavelength a minimum.
- Linking intensity to the energy of the electrons.
- Forgetting to use the electron mass, or to take the square root, when finding a speed.
- Explaining the threshold without stating that one photon interacts with one electron.
