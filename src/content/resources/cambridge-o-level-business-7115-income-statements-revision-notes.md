Condensed notes on section **5.3 Income statements** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (for examination in 2026). For full explanations and two worked examples, use the [income statements study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-income-statements/). For exam-style questions, use the [income statements practice set](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-income-statements-practice/).

**Remember:** constructing an income statement is **not assessed**. You must read one, explain its lines and use it to make a decision based on profit calculations.

## The syllabus in two lines

- **5.3.1** How a profit is made; why profit matters to private sector businesses (reward for risk-taking and enterprise, source of finance); profit is not cash.
- **5.3.2** Main features: revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, profit, retained profit; use simple statements in decision-making.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Value of sales in a period: price × quantity sold |
| Cost of sales | Direct cost of the goods sold in the period |
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Expenses | Overheads not tied to each item sold, such as rent and salaries |
| Profit | Gross profit − expenses |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business after tax and payments to owners |
| Dividend | Share of profit paid to shareholders |
| Loss | When costs are greater than revenue |
| Income statement | Statement showing revenue, costs and profit over a period, usually a year |

## The chain to memorise

Revenue → minus cost of sales → **gross profit** → minus expenses → **profit** → minus tax and dividends → **retained profit**.

If you are given any two linked lines, you can find the third. For example, revenue 80,000 and gross profit 30,000 means cost of sales is 50,000.

## Three ways to raise profit, and their catches

| Method | Catch |
|---|---|
| Sell more | Extra sales may need discounts or extra advertising |
| Raise price | Customers may buy less, so revenue can fall |
| Cut costs | Cheaper inputs or fewer staff may lower quality and sales |

## Why profit matters (private sector)

- Reward for owners' risk-taking and enterprise.
- Source of finance: retained profit funds growth with no interest and no new owners.
- Measure of success and of managers' decisions.
- Helps attract loans and new investors.

## Profit versus cash

| Event | Effect on profit | Effect on cash now |
|---|---|---|
| Sale on credit | Revenue rises | No change until the customer pays |
| Buying inventory for cash | No cost until the goods are sold | Falls |
| Buying a machine for cash | Not an expense in the same way | Falls sharply |
| Taking out a loan | No change | Rises |
| Repaying a loan | No change | Falls |

So: profitable firms can run short of cash; cash-rich firms can be making losses.

## Reading two years of statements

1. Compare revenue and profit: did they move together?
2. Find the line where the change started: cost of sales or expenses?
3. Give a possible cause (supplier prices, price cuts, new staff, advertising).
4. State the consequence (less retained profit to reinvest, owners unhappy).
5. Suggest a response and its risk.

## Using a statement in a decision

- Change only the lines the proposal affects, then recalculate profit.
- When closing a loss-making branch or product, ask which costs would **really** disappear. Costs that continue are still paid.
- State the assumption your answer rests on (for example, sales volume stays the same).
- Add one factor the figures do not show (reputation, staff, quality).

## Exam traps

- Revenue is not profit.
- Gross profit is not profit.
- Retained profit is not a pile of cash.
- A loss-making branch can still contribute towards costs that do not go away.
- A price rise only raises revenue if sales do not fall too much.
- Income statements look back; decisions look forward.

## Self-test

1. Revenue is $64,000 and cost of sales is $40,000. Calculate gross profit.
2. Gross profit is $24,000 and expenses are $27,500. What is the result?
3. Profit is $50,000, tax is $10,000 and dividends are $15,000. Calculate retained profit.
4. Revenue is $90,000 and gross profit is $36,000. Calculate cost of sales.
5. Give two reasons why profit is important to a private sector business.
6. Why does a sale on credit increase profit but not cash?
7. State one way a business could be profitable but short of cash.
8. Revenue rose by 10% but profit fell. Give two possible causes.
9. A shop sells 2,000 items at $15. It raises the price to $16 and sales fall to 1,900. What happens to revenue?
10. A product makes a loss of $3,000 after being charged $5,000 of costs that would continue if it were dropped. What happens to total profit if it is dropped?
11. Why might retained profit fall even when profit is unchanged?
12. State one limitation of using an income statement to make a decision.

### Answers

1. 64,000 − 40,000 = **$24,000**.
2. 24,000 − 27,500 = a **loss of $3,500**.
3. 50,000 − 10,000 − 15,000 = **$25,000**.
4. 90,000 − 36,000 = **$54,000**.
5. Any two: reward for risk-taking and enterprise; source of finance; measure of success; helps attract lenders and investors.
6. The sale is counted as revenue when it is made, but the customer pays later, so cash only arrives later.
7. Any one: selling on credit, buying a lot of inventory, buying non-current assets for cash, repaying loans.
8. Any two: cost of sales rose faster than revenue (supplier prices); price cuts to win sales; higher expenses such as new staff or advertising.
9. Before: 2,000 × 15 = $30,000. After: 1,900 × 16 = $30,400. Revenue **rises by $400**.
10. The $3,000 loss disappears but $5,000 of costs remain, so total profit **falls by $2,000**.
11. Higher tax or higher dividends paid to owners.
12. Any one: it shows the past; it does not show cash; it ignores non-financial factors; forecasts based on it rest on assumptions.
