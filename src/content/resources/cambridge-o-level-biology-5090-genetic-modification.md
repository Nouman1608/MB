## What is genetic modification?

**Genetic modification** is changing the genetic material of an organism by **removing, changing or inserting individual genes**. An organism that has had a gene added from another species is called a **genetically modified (GM) organism**. The idea depends on three facts that you already know from the inheritance topic.

1. A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
2. The genetic code is universal in principle: the same four bases are used in all organisms, so a gene from one species can work in another.
3. DNA controls the cell by controlling the production of proteins, so a cell that receives a new gene can make a new protein.

## Making human insulin using bacteria

People with Type 1 diabetes need insulin, a protein hormone. Insulin used to be extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle. Now **the gene that controls the production of human insulin has been inserted into bacterial DNA**, so bacteria make human insulin for commercial production.

### The steps (outline)

1. The **human insulin gene** is identified and cut out of human DNA, using enzymes that cut DNA at specific points.
2. A **plasmid** is taken from a bacterium and cut open with the same type of enzyme.
3. The human insulin gene is inserted into the plasmid, and another enzyme joins them together. The plasmid now carries the human gene.
4. The plasmid is put back into bacteria.
5. The bacteria are grown in a **fermenter**, where they reproduce rapidly by binary fission. Each new cell has a copy of the plasmid.
6. The bacteria make human insulin, which is extracted, purified and used to treat people with diabetes.

The names of the enzymes are not required at this level. What matters is that a gene is cut out, inserted into a plasmid and carried into a bacterium, which then makes the protein.

### Why bacteria are used

This links to the biotechnology subtopic: bacteria reproduce rapidly, they contain plasmids, they can make complex protein molecules, and there are fewer ethical concerns than with using animals.

### Advantages of GM insulin

- It is identical to human insulin, so it is less likely to cause a reaction than insulin from animals.
- It can be produced in large quantities, cheaply and reliably.
- It does not depend on slaughtering animals.

### Worked example 1: how fast can the supply grow?

*One bacterium containing the human insulin gene divides every 30 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 6 hours?*

1. 6 hours = 360 minutes. Number of divisions = 360 divided by 30 = 12.
2. Number of bacteria = 2 to the power 12 = 4 096.

Each bacterium makes insulin, so the quantity of insulin rises as the bacteria multiply. This explains why a fermenter can produce large quantities.

## Genetically modified crop plants

Genes can be inserted into crop plants, usually when the plants are cells in culture, which are then grown into whole plants. The syllabus gives three examples.

### Resistance to herbicides

A gene for herbicide resistance is inserted into a crop such as soya or maize. The farmer can then spray the field with a herbicide (weedkiller). The weeds are killed, but the crop plants survive because they are resistant. This reduces competition from weeds for light, water and ions, so the yield rises, and the farmer may need fewer sprays.

### Resistance to insect pests

A gene from a bacterium that makes a protein poisonous to certain insects can be inserted into a crop such as cotton or maize. Insects that eat the plant are killed. The crop suffers less damage, so the yield is greater, and less insecticide needs to be sprayed.

### Additional vitamins

A gene can be inserted so that the crop makes an additional vitamin. For example, rice has been modified to make a substance that the body can turn into vitamin A. This can help to prevent vitamin deficiency in people whose diet is mainly rice.

### Worked example 2: yield data

*A farmer grew two fields of maize of the same size. The non-GM field produced 6.4 tonnes per hectare. The insect-resistant GM field produced 7.6 tonnes per hectare.*

1. Increase = 7.6 - 6.4 = 1.2 tonnes per hectare.
2. Percentage increase = 1.2 divided by 6.4 x 100 = 18.75 per cent, or 18.8 per cent to one decimal place.

An answer could add that the farmer saves money on insecticide and uses fewer sprays.

## Discussing advantages and risks

The syllabus asks you to **discuss** potential advantages and risks of genetic modification, limited to crop plants and bacteria. A balanced answer gives both.

| Advantages | Risks or concerns |
|---|---|
| Higher yields, so more food for a growing population | Possible harm to other organisms, such as harmless insects, if the toxin affects them |
| Less use of pesticides and herbicides, with lower costs | Genes could spread to wild plants (for example by pollen), possibly creating "superweeds" resistant to herbicides |
| Crops with extra nutrients can reduce deficiency diseases | Possible effects on human health that are not yet known (allergies) |
| Bacteria make useful products such as human insulin cheaply | Seeds from GM companies can be expensive, so poor farmers may be at a disadvantage |
| Crops can be made to grow in harsh conditions | Reduced biodiversity if fewer varieties are grown; some people have ethical or religious objections |

### Worked example 3: writing a discussion answer

*Discuss the use of GM crops that are resistant to insect pests.*

- The crop makes its own protein that kills pest insects, so less insecticide needs to be sprayed. [Advantage]
- Less damage by insects increases the yield, so more food is produced. [Advantage]
- The protein might harm insects that are not pests, such as bees or butterflies. [Risk]
- The pests may become resistant to the toxin by natural selection, because those that survive pass on resistance. [Risk]
- Genes might spread to wild relatives of the crop. [Risk]

Each point is made with a reason, not just a statement. A strong answer also reaches a balanced view.

## Genetic modification compared with selective breeding

| Feature | Selective breeding | Genetic modification |
|---|---|---|
| What is changed | Which individuals breed; many genes change together | One or a few individual genes |
| Source of new features | Variation already in the species | Can come from a different species |
| Speed | Many generations | One or a few generations to give the new feature |
| Precision | Low; other genes are carried along | High; the particular gene is chosen |

Both processes are used to produce organisms with features that are useful to people. Because GM can move genes between species, it raises extra questions of safety and ethics. Because the new gene is in the DNA of the organism, it is passed on to the offspring.

## Checking whether genetic modification has worked

Scientists need to find out whether the new gene is working. For a herbicide-resistant crop, young plants are sprayed with the herbicide. Those that survive have the gene, and those that die do not. For bacteria carrying the human insulin gene, a sample of the culture is tested for insulin. In practice the plants or bacteria that show the new feature are the ones that are kept and grown on, so the successful ones multiply.

### Worked example 4: success rate

*In an experiment, 500 plant cells were treated to insert a herbicide-resistance gene. After spraying with herbicide, the plants that grew from 35 of the cells survived. Calculate the percentage success.*

1. Success = 35 out of 500.
2. Percentage = 35 divided by 500 x 100 = 7 per cent.

Because the success rate is low, many cells have to be treated to get plants that carry the gene. This is one reason why GM crop production takes time and money.

## Common mistakes

- Defining genetic modification as "selective breeding". GM changes the genes directly, often by moving a gene from another species.
- Saying that bacteria "eat" the insulin gene. The gene is inserted into the plasmid, and the bacteria copy it and make the protein.
- Saying that insulin from GM bacteria cures diabetes. It treats the condition.
- Giving only advantages or only risks when asked to discuss.
- Writing "GM food is harmful" without evidence. Use "potential risks" and explain what they might be.
- Mixing up the three examples. Herbicide resistance protects the crop from weedkiller; insect resistance protects it from pests.
- Forgetting that the new gene is passed on when the plants reproduce.

## Command words

- **Describe**: define genetic modification and the steps in making insulin.
- **Outline**: give the three examples of GM crops briefly.
- **Discuss**: give advantages and risks, limited to crop plants and bacteria.
