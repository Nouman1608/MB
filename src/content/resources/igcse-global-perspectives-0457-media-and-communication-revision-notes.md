These notes are for Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457), using the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). "Media and communication" is in the topic list in section 3. The assessed skills come from sections 2 and 4. You can use the topic in Component 1 (Written Exam), Component 2 (Individual Report) or Component 3 (Team Project). **Topic knowledge is not assessed**: the syllabus says "Knowledge of content is not assessed in this syllabus". These notes show how to use the topic to practise the skills.

## The skills, in the syllabus's own words

- **AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation**: 68% of the qualification, and 100% of Component 1.
- **AO2 Reflection**: 15% of the qualification. It is assessed in Components 2 and 3, not Component 1.
- **AO3 Communication and collaboration**: 17% of the qualification.
- **Command words**: Analyse, Describe, Evaluate, Explain, Identify, Suggest.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Issue (syllabus) | "an important subject or problem for discussion" |
| Perspective (syllabus) | "a viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning" |
| Research (syllabus) | "the systematic study of a global issue to find evidence and develop understanding" |
| Evidence (syllabus) | Information that helps develop understanding or prove something true or false |
| Argument (syllabus) | "a series of statements containing reasons and evidence which support a claim" |
| Claim | A statement put forward as true |
| Fact | Can be checked as true or false |
| Opinion | A personal judgement |
| Generalisation | A broad statement applied to a whole group |
| Prediction | A statement about the future |
| Value | A belief about what matters or is right |
| Bias | A one-sided view |
| Vested interest | Gaining or losing from the outcome |
| Misinformation | False information shared by someone who believes it |
| Disinformation | False information shared on purpose to mislead |
| Press freedom | Journalists reporting without censorship or threats |
| Media literacy | The ability to find, judge and create media messages |

The first five are the syllabus's own definitions. Claim to vested interest are the terms the syllabus says you will benefit from understanding for Component 1. The last four are topic words for discussion, not assessed knowledge.

## Issues at a glance

| Level | Example issue |
|---|---|
| Global | Should technology platforms pay for the news they share? How can journalists be kept safe? |
| National | How should public service broadcasters be funded? How far should broadcasting be regulated? |
| Local | A local paper closes; online-only notices leave some residents out; rumours spread in group chats |

## Perspectives at a glance

| Group | What they usually want | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Journalists and editors | Independence, safety, steady income | Their work and jobs depend on it |
| Media owners | Audience and profit | They run businesses |
| Governments | Public order and trust | They answer to voters and are often reported on |
| Technology platforms | Few costs and rules | Payment and regulation laws affect their business |
| Advertisers | Cheap access to audiences | Advertising money follows attention |
| Audiences | Accurate, affordable, accessible news | They use news to make decisions |
| Minority-language communities | Being informed and represented | Being left out makes them less informed and less heard |

To turn a row into a perspective, add evidence and reasoning: who says it, what they base it on and what they gain or lose.

## Evidence and cases at a glance

| Who / what | What happened | Finding or outcome | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| McCombs and Shaw (1972), agenda-setting | Undecided voters in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, during the 1968 US presidential campaign | The issues voters rated most important matched the issues the media covered most | Shows a link, not cause; one town; before online news |
| Australia, News Media Bargaining Code (2021) | A law to make platforms negotiate payment with news publishers | Facebook briefly blocked news in Australia in February 2021, then signed deals; in 2024 Meta said it would not renew them | New money for some publishers, but the effect depended on platforms staying in |
| Canada, Online News Act (Royal Assent 22 June 2023) | A similar payment law | Meta began ending news on its platforms in Canada in August 2023; Google agreed to give C$100 million a year, indexed to inflation | Same law, opposite responses from two companies |
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 19 (1948) | The right to "seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media" | Used by different sides of media debates | One value can support different perspectives |
| UNESCO, Global Media and Information Literacy Week | Held each October | Promotes media and information literacy | Raises awareness, but its effect on behaviour is hard to measure |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue**: "Media and communication" is a topic. "Should public money fund local journalism?" is an issue you can argue about.
- **Opinion vs perspective**: a perspective has evidence and reasoning behind it.
- **Fact vs claim**: a fact can be checked; a claim might or might not be true.
- **Bias vs vested interest**: bias is in how something is presented. A vested interest is a reason someone might be biased. A writer can have a vested interest and still be accurate.
- **Misinformation vs disinformation**: the difference is intent.
- **Correlation vs cause**: media coverage that matches public concern does not prove the media caused that concern.
- **Analyse vs evaluate**: analyse shows meaning and links. Evaluate judges quality or value.
- **Individual Report vs Team Project**: a *global* issue that divides opinion (Component 2) versus a *local* issue the team acts on (Component 3).

## Mini essay plan: "assess actions" (Component 1 Question 4 style)

*Question (original):* "Assess which action would best improve people's ability to judge the reliability of news."

1. **Judgement first**: name the action you think works best, for example media literacy teaching in schools.
2. **Action 1, media literacy teaching**: builds lasting skills and does not require deciding which content to remove. Limits: slow, and it misses adults.
3. **Action 2, independent fact-checking**: quick corrections. Limits: corrections may reach fewer people than the original claim; some distrust fact-checkers.
4. **Action 3, stricter regulation of broadcasters**: clear rules on accuracy. Limits: concerns about free expression and who enforces the rules; it does not cover every source.
5. **Use the sources**: quote evidence for each action and judge how reliable it is.
6. **Weigh and conclude**: explain why your choice is better, for example "works best combined with fact-checking for adults", and give one condition under which your judgement would change.

## Quick self-test

1. What does the syllabus say about knowledge of content in 0457?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Which assessment objective is worth 0% of Component 1?
4. "Teenagers never read the news." What type of statement is this?
5. What is the difference between misinformation and disinformation?
6. A broadcaster publishes a survey praising its own news. Which syllabus term best describes the problem?
7. Which Component 1 question asks you to assess actions, and how many marks is it worth?
8. What must every Team Project plan include about the action, besides how it will be evidenced?
9. Rewrite "Fake news" as a global research question that divides opinion.
10. Give one limitation of McCombs and Shaw (1972).

### Answers

1. It is not assessed. The topics are a context for practising skills.
2. "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning."
3. AO2 Reflection.
4. A generalisation. It is also an opinion presented as if it were fact.
5. Intent. Misinformation is shared by someone who believes it is true; disinformation is shared on purpose to mislead.
6. Vested interest.
7. Question 4, worth 20 marks.
8. How the team will measure the success of the action.
9. For example: "Should governments be able to require online platforms to remove false news?"
10. It shows a link, not cause. It is also limited to one town, before online news existed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Bringing in outside facts about the media in the Written Exam instead of using the sources. Prior knowledge is not assessed.
- Saying a source is "unreliable" without naming why: the sample, the author's interest or loaded words.
- Labelling a statement (for example "opinion") without explaining how you can tell.
- Describing what each perspective says without explaining why the group holds it.
- Listing causes and consequences without linking them into a chain.
- In "assess actions" answers, praising every action and never reaching a judgement.
- Choosing an Individual Report issue that does not divide opinion, or that has no global dimension.
- Giving a Team Project action with no measure of success, or measuring effort ("we handed out 200 leaflets") instead of effect.
- Leaving out citations and references, which AO3 expects in Components 2 and 3.
