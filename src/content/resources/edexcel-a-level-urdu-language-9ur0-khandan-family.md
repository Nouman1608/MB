This guide goes deeper into **sub-theme 1.1, family (خاندان)**, of Theme 1 in Pearson Edexcel A Level Urdu (9UR0). The specification divides the sub-theme into three aspects:

- **خاندانی ساخت میں تبدیلیاں**: changes in family structure
- **خاندان میں عورتوں کا کردار اور مقام**: the role and position of women in the family
- **شادی کے بارے میں بدلتے ہوئے رویے**: changing attitudes towards marriage

Theme 1 is set in the context of Pakistan only. For core vocabulary and a model paragraph, use the [family revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-family-revision-notes/); for the whole theme, the [Theme 1 study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-theme-1-study-guide/). This page works through each aspect in more depth and shows how the sub-theme turns into exam tasks. Test yourself afterwards with the [family practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-9ur0-khandan-family-practice/).

## Where family appears in the exam

Family is not a research subject, so it never sets the Paper 1 Section C question. It can, however, supply the material for:

| Paper | Task where family can appear |
|---|---|
| Paper 1, Section A | An unseen Urdu passage to translate into English |
| Paper 1, Section B | Reading texts with multiple-choice and open questions in Urdu |
| Paper 2, Section A | An unseen English passage to translate into Urdu |
| Paper 3, Section A | Listening comprehension drawn from across all the themes |
| Paper 3, Section B | A recording and a text on one sub-theme: summarise both, then evaluate the views |

So you need two things: vocabulary you can recognise quickly when reading or listening, and vocabulary you can produce accurately when translating into Urdu or arguing in Paper 3.

## Aspect 1: changes in family structure

The revision notes set out the joint-versus-separate debate. Go one step further and learn to describe the **range** of arrangements, because exam texts rarely present only two options.

- **The joint household under one roof**: parents, married sons and their families share a house and often a kitchen. Decision-making tends to rest with the eldest generation.
- **The neighbouring household**: a married son sets up his own home in the same street or building. Meals, childcare and festivals may still be shared. In Urdu you can describe this as **الگ گھر مگر قریبی تعلق** (a separate house but a close connection).
- **The split household**: one or more members live away for work, in a city or abroad, while the rest of the family stays in the village. Money flows home; decisions may be taken by telephone.
- **The small urban family**: a couple and their children in a flat, with grandparents visiting or living elsewhere.

When you explain **why** structure changes, group the causes so that your writing has shape: economic causes (jobs in cities, cost and size of housing, salaries that let a couple live independently), educational causes (longer study, later marriage), and attitudinal causes (privacy, individual choice). Then add a counter-pressure: rising housing costs and the need for childcare can push families back **together**. Showing that change runs in more than one direction is exactly the kind of nuance that Paper 3 Section B rewards when you evaluate views.

Useful additional terms: **کنبہ** (household, family unit), **سربراہ** (head of the family), **کفالت** (financial support, maintenance), **وراثت** (inheritance), **آبائی گھر** (ancestral home).

## Aspect 2: the role and position of women in the family

The wording of this aspect asks about both **کردار** (role: what women do) and **مقام** (position: the standing and power they have). Keep these apart in your writing; a woman may carry a large role in running the home while having a limited say in major decisions.

Points to be able to discuss:

- **The household manager.** In many families the mother or mother-in-law manages the budget, food, guests and the upbringing of children. Influence can be considerable even where formal authority rests with men.
- **The daughter-in-law's position.** A newly married woman joining a joint household often starts with little authority, which is why the **ساس بہو** relationship is such a common theme in drama and fiction. Her position usually strengthens over time.
- **Education and paid work.** A woman who earns may gain more say in household decisions, but may also carry the **دوہری ذمہ داری** (double burden) of a job and the housework.
- **When men work away.** If the husband works in the Gulf or in another city, the wife may take over decisions about money, schooling and repairs, a shift in position that comes from necessity rather than from a change in attitudes.
- **Rights within the family.** Islamic law and Pakistani law give daughters a share of inheritance, and the marriage contract records the **حق مہر** (mahr, the bride's due). A common debate is the gap between rights on paper and what happens in practice. Present this as a debate, supported by sources, not as a claim about every family.

## Aspect 3: changing attitudes towards marriage

The key word is **رویے** (attitudes). Exam texts usually contrast the views of two generations, so organise your ideas around questions on which attitudes differ:

1. **Who chooses?** The arranged marriage with the couple's consent remains common; love marriage is more openly accepted than before, especially in cities. Many families now describe an "arranged introduction" in which the couple decide whether to proceed.
2. **Whom to marry?** Marriage within the extended family (**خاندان میں شادی**) is valued by some for trust and keeping property together; others prefer to look outside the family.
3. **When to marry?** Longer education and career plans tend to raise the age at which people marry. Child marriage is opposed by law, with the minimum age set in legislation; check the current provincial position in a source before quoting an age.
4. **How much to spend?** Lavish weddings and **جہیز** (dowry) are widely criticised, yet social pressure keeps them going. Simple weddings (**سادگی سے شادی**) are promoted by some families and campaigns.
5. **How are partners found?** Through relatives and matchmakers (**رشتہ کرانے والی**), and increasingly through online services.

## Worked example 1: translating a family passage into English

Paper 1 Section A is marked in short sections of language, one mark each, so every phrase must be accounted for. Here is a short original passage, written for this guide.

> پچھلی نسل میں شادی کا فیصلہ عموماً بزرگ کیا کرتے تھے، اور لڑکے لڑکی سے رائے لینا ضروری نہیں سمجھا جاتا تھا۔ آج صورتِ حال کافی بدل چکی ہے۔ زیادہ تر والدین اب بھی رشتہ تلاش کرتے ہیں، لیکن آخری فیصلہ اکثر نوجوان خود کرتے ہیں۔

**Model translation:** In the previous generation, the decision about marriage was usually made by the elders, and asking the young man and woman for their opinion was not considered necessary. Today the situation has changed considerably. Most parents still look for a match, but the final decision is often made by the young people themselves.

**Annotation of the tricky sections:**

- **کیا کرتے تھے** is habitual past ("used to make"), but "was usually made" is also accurate because عموماً already carries the idea of habit. Do not translate it as a simple "made".
- **ضروری نہیں سمجھا جاتا تھا** is a past habitual passive: "was not considered necessary". Losing the passive costs the mark for that section.
- **صورتِ حال کافی بدل چکی ہے**: چکی ہے signals completion, so "has changed considerably", not "is changing".
- **رشتہ تلاش کرتے ہیں**: رشتہ here means a marriage proposal or match, not "relationship". Context decides the meaning.
- **خود** at the end gives emphasis: "themselves".

## Worked example 2: summarising two views for Paper 3

In Paper 3 Section B you summarise the points of view in a recording and a text on the same sub-theme, then evaluate them. Imagine a speaker who argues that the joint family protects children, and a writer who argues that it limits young couples. A strong summary sentence reports the view in your own Urdu without copying:

> مقرر کے خیال میں مشترکہ خاندان بچوں کو تحفظ اور بزرگوں کا تجربہ فراہم کرتا ہے، جبکہ مضمون نگار کا کہنا ہے کہ اس نظام میں نئے جوڑوں کو اپنے فیصلے خود کرنے کی آزادی کم ملتی ہے۔

(In the speaker's view, the joint family gives children protection and the experience of elders, whereas the writer says that in this system new couples have less freedom to make their own decisions.)

Notice the reporting frames **کے خیال میں**, **کا کہنا ہے کہ** and the contrast word **جبکہ**. These let you attribute each view clearly before you give your own, which is what the evaluation part requires.

## Common mistakes

- Treating **کردار** and **مقام** as the same thing in an answer on women's position.
- Translating **رشتہ** as "relationship" in a marriage context, or **رشتے دار** as "relationship" rather than "relative".
- Presenting every Pakistani family as joint, or every city family as nuclear; exam texts usually describe variety.
- Writing your own opinion into a Paper 3 summary; the summary reports the sources, and your opinion belongs in the evaluation.
- Dropping the ergative **نے** in perfective sentences such as **والدین نے رشتہ طے کیا** (the parents arranged the match).

## Exam technique: command words in Urdu

All questions in Paper 1 Sections B and C and in Paper 3 are set in Urdu. Recognise these instructions instantly:

| Urdu instruction | What it asks you to do |
|---|---|
| بیان کیجیے | state or describe |
| وضاحت کیجیے | explain, giving reasons |
| اپنے الفاظ میں لکھیے | write in your own words, not the text's |
| خلاصہ لکھیے / خلاصہ پیش کیجیے | summarise the main points |
| آپ کس حد تک متفق ہیں؟ | to what extent do you agree? (give a judgement with reasons) |
| تنقیدی جائزہ لیجیے | evaluate critically, weighing both sides |
| موازنہ کیجیے | compare |

When a question says **اپنے الفاظ میں**, lifting a sentence from the text usually earns nothing, even if it contains the right information. Rephrase with synonyms: **ضروری** can become **لازمی**, **بدل چکی ہے** can become **تبدیلی آ چکی ہے**.
