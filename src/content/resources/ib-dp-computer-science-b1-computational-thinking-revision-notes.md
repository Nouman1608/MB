B.1 Computational thinking is the process that underpins the whole IB DP Computer Science course (first assessment 2027): specify the problem and its success criteria, decompose it, abstract and generalize it, then test and evaluate the solution. These notes summarise what the IB's public subject brief says; the detailed learning outcomes are in the full DP guide, so check your school's copy for exact content.

Full explanation: [B.1 Computational thinking study guide](/resources/ib-dp-computer-science-b1-computational-thinking/).

## Key facts from the subject brief

| Item | What the brief says |
|---|---|
| Where it sits | Theme B, "Computational thinking and problem-solving" |
| Teaching hours | 5 SL / 5 HL |
| Assessed in | Paper 2 (Theme B questions for SL and HL); applied in the computational solution (IA) |
| Paper 2 | SL 1 h 15 min (35%); HL 2 h (40%); some questions need programming, in Java or Python |
| Internal assessment | Computational solution to a real-world problem of the student's choosing; 35 hours; 30% SL, 20% HL |

## The four abilities (brief's wording)

| Ability | In the brief | What to be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| Specify | "specify problems in terms of their computational context and determine success criteria" | State the problem in computing terms and write testable success criteria |
| Decompose | "decompose complex real-world problems into more manageable problems" | Split a problem into smaller problems that can be solved one at a time |
| Abstract and generalize | "abstract problems and generalize them to enable algorithmic thinking and to develop solutions" | Keep what matters and express it generally so an algorithm can be designed |
| Test and evaluate | "test and evaluate solutions for improvements" | Check the solution against the success criteria and identify improvements |

## Linked assessment objectives

- **AO3** includes constructing, analysing and evaluating "problem specifications, system requirements, success criteria, testing strategies, and programs".
- **AO4**: "demonstrate the application of computational thinking skills to solve real-world problems using computer science solutions".

## Worked example: applying the cycle

Problem: a school club wants to know which members have not paid their subscription.

1. **Specify**: input is the member list with payment status; output is a list of unpaid members. Success criterion: every member with no payment recorded appears in the list, and no paid member does.
2. **Decompose**: read the member data; check each member's payment status; collect the unpaid members; display the result.
3. **Abstract and generalize**: only the name and payment status matter, not addresses or ages; the "check each item against a condition" step is general and could be reused for any filter.
4. **Test and evaluate**: run with a list where some have paid and some have not, plus an empty list; compare the output with the success criterion; note an improvement such as sorting the output.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Writing vague success criteria ("the program works well") that cannot be tested.
- Treating decomposition and abstraction as the same thing: decomposition splits a problem up; abstraction removes detail that does not matter.
- Testing a solution without referring back to the success criteria set when the problem was specified.
- Learning B.1 as definitions only. The brief describes it as a process applied throughout the course.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many hours is B.1?**
5 hours at SL and 5 hours at HL, according to the subject brief.

No. The brief says the course is underpinned by the computational thinking process, and the internal assessment uses it.
