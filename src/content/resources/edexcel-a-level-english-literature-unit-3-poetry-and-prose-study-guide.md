Unit 3 is the first of the two units that complete the International A Level after the International Advanced Subsidiary. It brings poetry and prose together, so you must be able to switch between reading a poem closely and following an argument across a whole novel. This guide covers the skills and structure that serve any text on the list, but you should work from the specific texts and question format set out in your own specification and past papers.

## Where this fits in the specification

Edexcel International A Level English Literature (YET01) has four units. Units 1 and 2 make up the International Advanced Subsidiary; Units 3 and 4 add the second half of the full qualification. Unit 3 is a two-hour examination of 50 marks, and its two sections are not equal: the guide to [exam pacing across the four units](/resources/edexcel-ial-english-literature-exam-preparation/) explains why the sections carry 20 and 30 marks and how that changes the time you allow. Unit 1 covers post-2000 writing, which is described in the guide to [Post-2000 Poetry and Prose](/resources/a-level-edexcel-english-literature-post-2000-poetry-and-prose/); Unit 3 builds on those reading habits with a broader range of texts.

Check your specification to confirm exactly which texts you are studying, whether any question asks you to compare, and which assessment objectives apply to each section.

## Poetry

Reading poetry well means moving from a general sense of a poem to a precise account of how it works.

### A method for any poem

1. **Establish the situation.** Who speaks, to whom, in what circumstances, and what is the emotional movement from beginning to end?
2. **Note the form.** Is there a fixed pattern such as a sonnet, or free verse? Where do lines break, and where does the punctuation stop you?
3. **Look at language.** Imagery, diction, sound patterns and tone, and any words or ideas that return.
4. **Find the turn.** Many poems change direction or attitude; identify where and how the writer signals it.
5. **Explain the effect.** What does each choice make the reader see, feel or question?

When you have studied a collection, think about **patterns across poems**: recurring themes, speakers or techniques. Being able to bring in a second poem briefly to support a point shows breadth, but only if it genuinely adds to the argument.

### Writing about a poem

Start with a clear claim about the poem as a whole, then explore evidence in a sensible order, which is often but not always the poem's own sequence. Quote briefly, name the technique, explain its effect and tie it back to your claim. Remember to write about the speaker, or persona, not simply the poet, unless you have a reason to connect them.

## Prose

For a novel or long prose work, the central question is how the writer makes meaning across a whole structure.

### A method for prose

1. **Narrative viewpoint.** First person, third person, omniscient or limited; how trustworthy is the narrator?
2. **Structure.** Opening, middle, ending, time shifts, repeated scenes, use of chapters or sections.
3. **Character and relationships.** What do speech, action and description reveal, and how does the writer manage our sympathy?
4. **Setting and symbolism.** How do places and objects carry meaning?
5. **Language and tone.** Irony, description, dialogue, sentence length and register.
6. **Context.** The social, cultural and historical circumstances that shape the text or the way it was received.

When writing about a passage, say where it comes in the novel and what it contributes to the whole. When writing a whole-text essay, choose moments from the beginning, the middle and the end, so that your answer shows how an idea develops rather than treating the text as a single block.

## Comparison

If your question asks you to compare, whether two poems, a poem and a prose text or two prose texts, organise by **points of comparison**, not by text. Instead of dealing with one text and then the other, build paragraphs around an idea, such as how each writer presents loss or how each uses a particular viewpoint, moving between the texts in the same paragraph. Comparative words and phrases such as "whereas", "similarly", "in contrast" and "both writers" signal your approach, but they only help if you also explain why the similarity or difference matters.

### Worked paragraph

Here is a short, invented comparison to show the technique, not to quote real set texts. Suppose you are comparing how two writers present a character's isolation, one in a poem and one in a novel.

"Both writers suggest isolation through the structure of what we read. In the poem, the speaker's thoughts are confined to short, end-stopped lines, so each idea seems to come to a halt rather than connect with the next, creating a sense of being cut off. By contrast, the novel's narrator is surrounded by other characters but is shown at the edge of each scene, so that isolation arises from social distance, not physical solitude. Whereas the poet makes the reader feel isolation through rhythm, the novelist makes us see it through the arrangement of people, which suggests that loneliness can be both an inner state and a social condition."

The paragraph makes a claim, uses a point of comparison (structure), discusses both texts with a method and an effect, and ends with a judgement. In your own answer, you would add short, accurate quotations from your texts.

## Planning and timing

Because the paper carries 50 marks over two hours, you have enough time to plan properly. Spend several minutes on each answer deciding your thesis, selecting evidence and ordering your points. Give each section time in proportion to its marks. If one section carries more marks, give it more time, but do not leave the shorter one rushed; a focused 20-mark answer can be written well in less time than a long one.

## Common mistakes

- Treating poetry and prose as separate subjects and using only technique spotting for poems and plot summary for novels.
- Writing about two texts one after the other with no real comparison.
- Quoting at length without analysis.
- Using context as a separate block of information.
- Writing about the poet instead of the speaker.
- Leaving the essay without a clear argument, so that each paragraph is a separate comment.

## Exam technique

Read the question twice and underline the key terms. Make sure that you answer the question set, for example about presentation, development or a particular idea, rather than the one you prepared. Use short quotations, vary your sentence structure, and keep a consistent line of argument. A short conclusion that makes a final judgement is better than a repeat of the introduction.

## Check your understanding

1. Name three things to examine when analysing the form of a poem. (For example stanza pattern, line length and punctuation, and rhyme or metre.)
2. What is the turn in a poem? (The point where the thought, tone or direction changes.)
3. Why should you refer to the speaker rather than the poet? (The voice may be a persona, not the writer's own view.)
4. Give two aspects of narrative viewpoint to comment on. (For example the person used and the narrator's reliability.)
5. How should a comparative essay be organised? (By points of comparison, moving between texts within paragraphs.)
6. Why can a comparative word such as "whereas" not guarantee marks? (You must also explain why the similarity or difference matters.)
7. When writing about a passage, what should you say about its place in the text? (Where it comes and what it contributes to the whole.)
8. How should you use time in a 50-mark, two-hour paper? (Plan properly and divide time in proportion to the marks for each section.)
