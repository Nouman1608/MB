## Work through the frameworks

| Framework | Examine |
|---|---|
| **Phonology** | Alliteration, sibilance, assonance, rhythm |
| **Lexis** | Formality, semantic fields, jargon, connotation, monosyllabic vs polysyllabic |
| **Semantics** | Metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole, euphemism, irony |
| **Grammar** | Sentence type and length, voice, modality, tense, pronouns |
| **Pragmatics** | Implied meaning, presupposition, politeness, face |
| **Discourse** | Structure, cohesion, openings and endings |
| **Graphology** | Layout, typeface, colour, images |

**The mark is for the effect, not the label.** Compare:

- Weak: *"The writer uses a rhetorical question."*
- Strong: *"The rhetorical question 'Who wouldn't want this?' presupposes agreement, making disagreement feel like an eccentric position rather than a reasonable one."*

**Quote → what it suggests → why the writer chose it.** That sequence is the whole skill.

## Grammar features that repay attention

- **Modality** — high modality (*must*, *will*) asserts authority and certainty; low modality (*might*, *could*) hedges and reduces the risk of being contradicted.
- **Voice** — the **passive can conceal the agent**. *"Errors were made"* omits who made them. This is a common institutional evasion, worth commenting on when the omission of the agent has a clear effect.
- **Pronouns** — inclusive *we* builds solidarity or spreads responsibility; direct *you* creates synthetic personalisation, addressing a mass audience as though individually.
- **Sentence variation** — a short sentence following long ones is emphatic. Comment on the **contrast**, not the length.
- **Presupposition** — what a sentence takes for granted. *"When did you stop worrying about this?"* smuggles in an assumption that resists challenge.

## Register and context

Register is analysed through **field** (subject), **tenor** (relationship between participants), and **mode** (spoken, written, or between).

**A register mismatch may be deliberate or accidental, but either way it is worth analysing its effect.** Informality in a formal context can build intimacy or humour; formality in a casual one can create distance, authority or irony. If a text's register feels wrong, that observation is the starting point for analysis, not the analysis itself -- the mark is still for explaining the effect the mismatch creates, not for labelling it as a mismatch.

## Spoken language

Features: fillers, false starts, repetition, repairs, overlaps, interruptions, adjacency pairs, turn-taking, discourse markers (*so*, *right*, *anyway*), deixis (*this*, *here*, *now*), back-channelling (*mm*, *yeah*).

**These are normal features of speech, not errors.** Marking them as mistakes is the standard misreading. Analyse what they *achieve* — buying planning time, holding the floor, signalling a topic change, or managing the relationship between speakers.

**Power in conversation** is visible in who interrupts, who controls topic, who asks and who answers questions, and who holds the floor longest. Unequal turn length is evidence, not a coincidence.

## Representation and language attitudes

**Representation** is how language constructs a version of people, groups or events — interrogate naming choices, modifiers, and whether **agency is expressed or obscured** (a passive construction can delete the actor entirely).

**Attitudes to variation** are examinable in themselves: **prescriptivism** treats change and non-standard forms as decline; **descriptivism** records variation without judgement. Standard English carries **overt prestige**; non-standard varieties can carry **covert prestige** within the group that uses them.

**Worked example.** Analyse: *"Mistakes were made, and lessons have been learned."*

```
passive voice x2   "were made", "have been learned" - no agent named in
                    either clause; nobody is identified as responsible
nominalisation      "mistakes", "lessons" turn actions into abstract
                    things, distancing them further from any actor
present perfect      "have been learned" implies completion, closing
                    the matter without offering evidence
parallel structure   balanced clauses give a formal, rehearsed quality,
                    marking it as institutional rather than personal
```

**Effect:** accountability is acknowledged in *form* while being grammatically avoided in *substance* — characteristic of institutional apology, and a strong contrast to comment on if a text elsewhere uses direct, agentive language.

## Directed writing

Section B's task is **transformation**, not summary: using some or all of Section A's material to
create a new text for a specified brief. There is no separate commentary on this unit -- credit goes
to **sustaining an appropriate style** for the task chosen, not to explaining your choices afterwards.

Fix **form, audience, purpose** before writing. Then:

- Adjust **register** for the new audience.
- **Select** relevant material; you need not use all of it.
- **Rephrase** — lifting from the source does not demonstrate the transformation this task requires.
- Follow the target form's conventions, and sustain them consistently for the whole response.

## Structuring the analysis

1. **Overview** — genre, audience, purpose, in one or two sentences.
2. **Body** — organised by effect or by framework. Short embedded quotations, precise analysis, always linked to purpose.
3. **Conclusion** — the overall effect and how the language achieves it.

Keep quotations **short**. A two-word quotation embedded in your own sentence demonstrates more control than a full copied line.

## Exam traps

- Feature-spotting without analysing effect.
- Treating spoken-language features as errors.
- Long quotations used as padding.
- Ignoring audience and purpose.
- Retelling content instead of analysing language.
- Lifting from the source in directed writing.
- Writing a commentary or explanation of your choices -- Unit 1 credits a sustained, consistent style in the writing itself, not a separate justification of it.

## Frequently asked questions

**What three-part sequence earns analysis marks in English Language?**
Quote the language, explain what it suggests, then explain why the writer chose it. The mark is for the effect of the feature, not for naming it.

**What is presupposition in language analysis?**
Presupposition is what a sentence takes for granted without stating it. A question such as "When did you stop worrying about this?" smuggles in an assumption that is hard to challenge.

**What is the difference between prescriptivism and descriptivism?**
Prescriptivism treats language change and non-standard forms as decline, while descriptivism records variation without judgement. Attitudes to language variation are examinable in their own right.

**Does OxfordAQA 9670 Unit 1 directed writing need a commentary?**
No. There is no separate commentary on this unit, so credit goes to sustaining an appropriate style for the task chosen, not to explaining your choices afterwards.

**How is power shown in conversation?**
Power is visible in who interrupts, who controls the topic, and who asks and who answers questions. Analysing these patterns shows the relationship between speakers.
