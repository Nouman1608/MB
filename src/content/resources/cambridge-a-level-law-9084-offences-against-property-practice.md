## Section A -- short answers and scenarios

**1.** State the maximum sentence for theft and the section that sets it. **[1]**

**2.** Identify the **three** situations in s2(1) Theft Act 1968 in which a person is not dishonest. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a "building" under s9(4) Theft Act 1968. **[2]**

**4.** Hana works in a bakery. Each evening she takes home unsold bread that is due to be thrown away, believing her manager would not mind. Explain whether Hana is dishonest. **[4]**

**5.** Ivo borrows his flatmate's concert ticket without asking, uses it to attend the concert, then puts it back. Explain whether Ivo has the intention to permanently deprive. **[4]**

**6.** Jas grabs a handbag from Kim's shoulder, pulling her arm back as the strap comes free, and runs off. Explain whether Jas is guilty of robbery. **[5]**

**7.** Lou is invited into his neighbour's house for a party. While there, he goes into an upstairs bedroom marked "Private" and takes a watch. Explain whether Lou is guilty of burglary. **[6]**

**8.** Mira finishes a taxi journey and, realising she has no cash, runs off, intending to send the driver the fare the next day. Explain whether Mira is guilty of making off without payment. **[4]**

## Section B -- short answer and essay

**9.** Distinguish fraud by false representation (s2 Fraud Act 2006) from obtaining services dishonestly (s11). **[5]**

**10.** Explain the actus reus and mens rea of handling stolen goods. **[6]**

**11.** Nadia writes "Pay me £500 or I will tell your employer you have been stealing from them" to a colleague she genuinely believes owes her £500. Explain whether Nadia is guilty of blackmail. **[6]**

**12.** Assess the extent to which the "without lawful excuse" defence in s5 Criminal Damage Act 1971 strikes a fair balance between defendants and property owners. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Seven years' imprisonment, under s7 Theft Act 1968 [1].

**2.** A belief that she has the legal right to deprive the other of the property [1]; a belief that the owner would consent if they knew of the appropriation and its circumstances [1]; a belief that the owner cannot be discovered by taking reasonable steps [1].

**3.** A building is a structure of some permanence, such as a house, shop or long-standing storage container (*B and S v Leathley* (1979)) [1]. Under s9(4) it includes an inhabited vehicle or vessel, such as a caravan or houseboat, whether or not the person living there is present [1].

**4.** Section 2(1)(b) provides that a person is not dishonest if she believes the owner would consent if it knew of the appropriation and its circumstances [1]. The belief need only be genuine, not reasonable [1]. Hana believed her manager would not mind, and the bread was due to be thrown away, which supports a genuine belief [1]. If the jury accepts her belief, she is not dishonest and is not guilty of theft; the *Ivey* test is not needed [1].

**5.** Under s6 a person who borrows property has the intention to permanently deprive if he intends to treat it as his own to dispose of regardless of the owner's rights [1]. Borrowing is enough where the property is returned with its "goodness, virtue and practical value" gone (*R v Lloyd* (1985)) [1]. Once the concert is over, the ticket has no remaining use or value [1]. Ivo therefore has the intention to permanently deprive, even though he returned the ticket [1].

**6.** Robbery under s8 requires theft plus force on a person immediately before or at the time of stealing, in order to steal [1]. Taking the bag is a dishonest appropriation of property belonging to Kim with intention to permanently deprive, so theft is made out [1]. Force applied to property can be force on the person where it is wrenched from them (*R v Clouden* (1987)) [1], and pulling her arm back as the strap comes free is force on Kim [1]. The force is used at the time of, and in order to complete, the theft, so Jas is guilty of robbery, which carries a maximum of life [1].

**7.** Lou entered the house with permission, so he did not enter the building as a trespasser [1]. However, burglary can be committed by entering **part of a building** as a trespasser (*R v Walkington* (1979)) [1]. The "Private" sign shows the bedroom was outside the scope of his invitation, so he entered that part as a trespasser [1], and he must know or be reckless that he was trespassing [1]. If he intended to steal when he entered the bedroom, s9(1)(a) applies; if not, s9(1)(b) applies because he stole having entered as a trespasser [1]. As the house is a dwelling, the maximum sentence is 14 years [1].

**8.** Mira knew that payment on the spot was expected at the end of a completed taxi journey [1], and she made off without paying [1]. However, s3 requires an intent to avoid payment permanently (*R v Allen* (1985)) [1]. As she intended to send the fare the next day, she lacks this intent and is not guilty of making off without payment [1].

**9.** Under s2 the defendant dishonestly makes a false representation, express or implied, knowing it is or might be untrue or misleading [1], intending to make a gain or cause a loss; nothing need actually be obtained [1]. Under s11 the defendant must actually obtain services [1] by a dishonest act, not an omission [1], knowing they are provided on the basis of payment and intending not to pay in full [1].

**10.** **Actus reus**: the goods must be stolen [1]; the defendant, otherwise than in the course of the stealing, receives them [1], or undertakes or assists in their retention, removal, disposal or realisation by or for the benefit of another, or arranges to do so [1]; concealing goods by lying to police is assisting in retention (*R v Kanwar* (1982)) [1]. **Mens rea**: knowing or believing the goods to be stolen, where suspicion is not enough (*R v Moys* (1984)) [1], and dishonesty [1].

**11.** **Actus reus**: the letter is a demand [1], made when it is posted (*Treacy v DPP* (1971)) [1]. A threat to tell the employer about stealing would influence an ordinary person of normal stability, so it is a menace (*R v Clear* (1968)) [1]. **Mens rea**: Nadia acts with a view to gain, the £500 [1]. The demand is unwarranted unless she believes both that she has reasonable grounds for the demand and that the menaces are a proper means of reinforcing it [1]. The test is subjective: her belief that the debt is owed may give reasonable grounds for the demand, but the jury must also accept that she believed threatening an accusation to her colleague's employer was a proper way to recover it; if it rejects that belief she is guilty, with a maximum of 14 years [1].

**12.** **Knowledge**: s5(2)(a) gives a lawful excuse where the defendant believes the owner consented or would have consented [1]; s5(2)(b) applies where the defendant acts to protect property believed to be in immediate need of protection by means believed reasonable [1]; s5(3) says an honestly held belief need not be justified [1]. **For defendants**: the subjective belief test protects those who act in good faith, even a drunken belief in consent (*Jaggard v Dickinson* (1981)) [1]. **For owners**: the courts add an objective requirement that the act was done to protect property, so protesters with remote aims fail (*R v Hill and Hall* (1989)) [1]; the defence does not apply to the aggravated offence involving danger to life [1]. **Criticisms**: allowing unreasonable or drunken beliefs can leave owners without protection [1], while the objective element can be inconsistent with the subjective wording of s5(3) [1]. **Balance**: the defence is limited to two defined situations, which prevents a general defence of good motives [1]. **Conclusion**: the balance is broadly fair, with the objective limit preventing abuse by those pursuing political aims [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Discussing the *Ivey* test where a s2(1) belief already resolves dishonesty.
- Forgetting "part of a building" when a defendant had permission to enter.
- Ignoring the intent to avoid payment permanently in making off.
- Omitting the maximum sentence where a question asks for it.
