## 1. What capital investment appraisal is

Capital expenditure buys or improves non-current assets used for more than one year, such as a new
machine or a shop extension. The money is spent now, but the benefits arrive over several years, so a
poor choice ties up funds and is hard to reverse.

**Capital investment appraisal** uses accounting techniques to judge whether a capital project is
worth undertaking, or which competing project to choose. The specification names two
methods: **payback** and **net present value (NPV)**. Both work with cash flows, not profit.

## 2. Calculating the cash flows

### Why cash, not profit

Profit uses accruals and includes non-cash items. Investment appraisal asks how much cash goes out,
how much comes back, and when. So you use the **net cash flow** for each year: cash received because
of the project minus cash paid because of the project.

### Which cash flows are relevant

Include only cash flows that are **future** and **incremental** (they happen only because the project
goes ahead):

- the **initial outlay**: purchase price, delivery and installation, paid at the start;
- **cash inflows**: extra sales receipts, or cost savings such as lower wages or lower running costs;
- **cash outflows**: extra materials, wages, maintenance and other running costs;
- the **residual value** (scrap or sale value) received when the asset is disposed of at the end.

Leave out:

- **depreciation**, a non-cash expense; the asset's cost is already in the initial outlay, so
  counting depreciation too would count the cost twice;
- **sunk costs**, money already spent (for example a feasibility study paid last month), because the
  decision cannot change it;
- **apportioned fixed overheads** that will be paid whether or not the project goes ahead.

If you are given a forecast **profit** that has been calculated after charging depreciation, convert it
back to cash flow by adding the depreciation back.

### Timing convention

The start of the project is called **year 0** ("now"). Unless a question says otherwise, NPV
calculations assume each year's cash flows arrive at the **end** of that year. Payback calculations
assume cash flows arrive **evenly through** the year, which is what allows a payback period in months.

### Worked example 1: from forecast profit to cash flows

Kestrel Print is considering a new press costing £120 000, paid now. It will last 4 years and then
be sold for £20 000. Depreciation is straight-line. The forecast profit from the press for each year
is shown below, after charging depreciation and a £5 000 share of existing head-office costs, which
will not change if the press is bought. Kestrel has already paid £3 000 for a market survey.

**Step 1: depreciation.** (120 000 − 20 000) ÷ 4 = **£25 000 a year**.

**Step 2: add back the non-cash and non-incremental items.**

| Year | Forecast profit | + Depreciation | + Apportioned head-office costs | Net cash flow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 000 | 25 000 | 5 000 | 40 000 |
| 2 | 16 000 | 25 000 | 5 000 | 46 000 |
| 3 | 22 000 | 25 000 | 5 000 | 52 000 |
| 4 | 12 000 | 25 000 | 5 000 | 42 000 |

**Step 3: add the residual value** to year 4: 42 000 + 20 000 = **£62 000**.

**Step 4: ignore the market survey.** The £3 000 is a sunk cost. Year 0 is the (120 000) outlay; brackets show an outflow.

## 3. Payback

The **payback period** is the time a project's net cash inflows take to repay the initial outlay.
Shorter is preferred, and a business may reject projects beyond a maximum (say three years).

**Equal annual cash inflows:** payback = initial outlay ÷ annual net cash inflow.

**Unequal cash inflows:** build a **cumulative** cash flow column. Payback falls in the year in which
the cumulative figure turns from negative to positive. Then:

`months into that year = (amount still to recover at the start of the year ÷ that year's cash inflow) × 12`

### Worked example 2: payback for Kestrel Print

| Year | Net cash flow (£) | Cumulative (£) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | (120 000) | (120 000) |
| 1 | 40 000 | (80 000) |
| 2 | 46 000 | (34 000) |
| 3 | 52 000 | 18 000 |
| 4 | 62 000 | 80 000 |

At the end of year 2, £34 000 is still to be recovered. Year 3 brings in £52 000.

(34 000 ÷ 52 000) × 12 = 7.8 months, so payback is **2 years 8 months** to the nearest month.

The year-4 residual value plays no part: payback ignores everything after the outlay is repaid.

## 4. Net present value

### The time value of money

£1 today is worth more than £1 in three years' time: it can be invested to earn a return, inflation
has not eroded it, and there is no risk it never arrives. So each future cash flow is converted to its
**present value**, its worth in today's money.

### Key terms

- **Cost of capital**: the rate of return a business must pay to the providers of its finance (interest
  on loans, the return expected by owners or shareholders). It is the minimum return a project must
  earn, and it is used as the discount rate.
- **Discount factor**: the number you multiply a future cash flow by to find its present value. For a
  cost of capital r (as a decimal) and a cash flow at the end of year n:
  `discount factor = 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ`. The year-0 factor is always 1.000. If a question supplies
  discount factors, use them exactly as given. This page rounds them to three decimal places.
- **Present value** = cash flow × discount factor.
- **Net present value (NPV)** = total present value of all the cash flows, with the initial outlay
  entered as a negative. Equivalently, total present value of inflows − initial outlay.

### Decision rule

- **Positive NPV**: the project earns more than the cost of capital and adds value. Accept on
  financial grounds.
- **Negative NPV**: the project earns less than the cost of capital. Reject on financial grounds.
- Choosing between projects: prefer the one with the **higher positive NPV**.

### Worked example 3: NPV for Kestrel Print at 10%

Discount factors at 10%: year 1 0.909, year 2 0.826, year 3 0.751, year 4 0.683.

| Year | Net cash flow (£) | Discount factor (10%) | Present value (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | (120 000) | 1.000 | (120 000) |
| 1 | 40 000 | 0.909 | 36 360 |
| 2 | 46 000 | 0.826 | 37 996 |
| 3 | 52 000 | 0.751 | 39 052 |
| 4 | 62 000 | 0.683 | 42 346 |
| | | **NPV** | **35 754** |

Total present value of inflows = 36 360 + 37 996 + 39 052 + 42 346 = £155 754.
NPV = 155 754 − 120 000 = **£35 754 positive**, so on financial grounds Kestrel should buy the press.

Note that the residual value is included in year 4 here, because NPV uses all the project's cash flows.

## 5. Benefits and limitations of each method

| | Payback | Net present value |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefits** | Simple to calculate and easy for non-accountants to understand | Allows for the time value of money |
| | Favours projects that return cash quickly, which helps liquidity | Uses all the cash flows over the whole life of the project |
| | Near-term forecasts are more reliable, so it reduces exposure to uncertain later years | Gives a clear decision rule linked to the cost of capital |
| | Useful when technology changes fast | Shows the money value a project adds |
| **Limitations** | Ignores all cash flows after the payback point | Harder to calculate and explain to non-accountants |
| | Ignores the time value of money | Depends on choosing the right cost of capital, which is hard to estimate |
| | Does not measure overall profitability or value added | Assumes cash flows arrive at year ends and that forecasts are accurate |
| | The maximum acceptable payback is a judgement | NPV alone does not show the return per £ invested |

Both methods are only as reliable as the cash flow forecasts, and later years are the least certain.

## 6. Using the measures to evaluate projects

A recommendation should combine the calculations with wider judgement.

**Financial factors** include: the NPV and payback figures; how reliable the forecasts are (what if
sales were 10% lower?); whether finance can be raised and at what cost; the effect on liquidity; and the
opportunity cost of the funds.

**Non-financial factors** include: effects on staff (training, redundancies, morale); product quality
and customer reaction; supplier reliability and servicing; environmental impact and reputation; legal
requirements such as health and safety; and fit with long-term aims.

### Worked example 4: choosing between two projects

Fenwick Packaging can afford one of two machines, each costing £90 000. Its cost of capital is 8%.
Discount factors: 0.926, 0.857, 0.794, 0.735, 0.681 for years 1 to 5.

| Year | Alder net cash flow (£) | Birch net cash flow (£) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 000 | 15 000 |
| 2 | 35 000 | 20 000 |
| 3 | 30 000 | 30 000 |
| 4 | 10 000 | 30 000 |
| 5 | 5 000 | 45 000 |

**Payback.** Alder cumulative: (50 000), (15 000), +15 000 in year 3, so 2 years + (15 000 ÷ 30 000) ×
12 = **2 years 6 months**. Birch cumulative: (75 000), (55 000), (25 000), +5 000 in year 4, so 3 years +
(25 000 ÷ 30 000) × 12 = **3 years 10 months**.

**NPV.** Alder present values: 37 040 + 29 995 + 23 820 + 7 350 + 3 405 = 101 610, NPV = **£11 610**.
Birch present values: 13 890 + 17 140 + 23 820 + 22 050 + 30 645 = 107 545, NPV = **£17 545**.

**Evaluation.** Both NPVs are positive, so both earn more than 8%. NPV favours Birch, which adds £5 935
more value; payback favours Alder, which pays back 16 months sooner. Birch relies on large inflows in
years 4 and 5, the least certain forecasts. If Fenwick is short of cash or its market changes quickly,
Alder's earlier return may matter more. If the later forecasts are reliable, Birch is better because it
adds more value. A strong answer adds a non-financial point, such as which supplier offers better
servicing, and ends with a clear recommendation.

## Common errors

- Including depreciation as a cash outflow, or forgetting to add it back to a profit figure.
- Including sunk costs or unchanged apportioned overheads.
- Leaving the residual value out of the NPV, or adding it to the wrong year.
- Converting a decimal of a year into months incorrectly (0.65 years is not 6.5 months).
- Using the cash inflow of the wrong year as the divisor in the months calculation.
- Discounting the year-0 outlay, or forgetting to subtract it.
- Saying "accept" for a negative NPV because total inflows exceed the outlay before discounting.
- Listing factors with no final decision.
