This guide covers quantitative skill **QS9** in section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: "interpret, apply and analyse information in written, graphical and numerical forms". It is the skill that data-response questions test most directly, and it can be assessed in any unit. All the countries, extracts and figures below are original and invented.

The broader [graphs and interpreting information guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-graphs-and-interpreting-written-graphical-and/) teaches how to construct and read graphs, spot misleading charts and handle a short extract. This page builds on that with a full data-response routine: describing a trend precisely, combining a written extract with a table, reading changes off a diagram, and judging how far the evidence supports a claim. Condensed recall is in the [QS9 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-revision-notes/), and the [QS9 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-practice/) give you sources to work on. The [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) and [printable checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) cover the rest of the course.

## Three verbs, three jobs

| Verb in QS9 | What it means in an answer | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Interpret | Say accurately what the information shows, with figures | "The price rose by 29% between 2020 and 2023, then fell by 4.5%" |
| Apply | Connect what it shows to an economic concept | "A fall in supply with inelastic demand raises price sharply" |
| Analyse | Build a chain of reasoning from the evidence to a conclusion, and test it | "The harvest fell by 22% in 2023 while imports rose, so total supply fell by 13%, which is consistent with the price rise" |

Most weak data answers interpret without applying, or apply theory without the data. Strong answers do all three, in that order.

## Describing a trend precisely

A trend description has four parts. Use figures and dates in each.

1. **Overall direction** between the first and last points.
2. **Size** of the change, as an absolute change, a percentage or both.
3. **Shape**: steady, accelerating, slowing, or with a turning point.
4. **Exceptions**: any year that breaks the pattern.

Weak: "The price of rice went up." Strong: "The price of rice rose from 1.20 to 1.48 a kilogram between 2020 and 2024, a rise of 23%. Almost all of it came in 2023, when the price jumped by 24%; in 2024 it fell back by 4.5%."

## Worked example: an extract with a table

**Extract (original, invented).** *Country K suffered a drought in 2023. "Our rice harvest was the worst in a decade," said the farm minister, "and that is the only reason prices rose." A consumer group replied that "prices are rising on everything, so rice is no different". The government raised rice imports to make up the shortfall.*

| Year | Rice price (per kg) | Harvest (m tonnes) | Imports (m tonnes) | Consumer price index (2020 = 100) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 1.20 | 8.0 | 1.0 | 100 |
| 2021 | 1.22 | 8.1 | 1.0 | 103 |
| 2022 | 1.25 | 7.9 | 1.1 | 107 |
| 2023 | 1.55 | 6.2 | 1.6 | 112 |
| 2024 | 1.48 | 7.4 | 1.3 | 116 |

**Task 1: Using the data, explain the change in the supply of rice in 2023.**

*Model answer:* Total supply is harvest plus imports. It fell from 9.0m tonnes in 2022 to 7.8m tonnes in 2023, a fall of 13.3%. The harvest fell by 1.7m tonnes (22%) because of the drought, and extra imports of 0.5m tonnes made up less than a third of the shortfall.

*Why it works:* it combines two columns to create a figure the table does not show, and links the numbers to the cause given in the extract.

**Task 2: Assess the consumer group's claim that rice is "no different" from other prices.**

*Model answer:* Between 2020 and 2022 rice rose by 4.2% while the general price index rose by 7%, so rice became cheaper in real terms (1.168 in 2020 prices). In 2023 rice rose by 24% against general inflation of 4.7%, so its real price jumped to 1.384. Over the whole period the real price of rice rose by 6.3%. The claim fits 2020 to 2022 but not 2023, when something specific to the rice market was at work.

*Why it works:* it converts money prices to real prices (QS7) to test a written claim against the numbers, and reaches a judgement that depends on the period.

**Task 3: Analyse whether the data support the minister's view that the drought was the only reason prices rose.**

*Model answer:* The timing fits: supply fell 13% and price rose 24% in the same year, and as the harvest recovered in 2024 the price fell back. A fall in supply with price-inelastic demand for a staple food would raise price by more than the fall in quantity. But "only reason" is too strong: general inflation of 4.7% in 2023 explains part of the money rise, and the price in 2024 is still well above 2022 even though supply is only 3% lower. Other factors, such as higher import or transport costs, may also have played a part, though the data do not show them.

*Why it works:* it supports the claim with evidence, then tests the word "only" with further evidence and points out what is missing.

## Reading information from a diagram

Diagrams in a question are information too. Suppose a diagram shows demand and an original supply curve meeting at a price of 40 and quantity of 300. After a rise in costs, supply shifts left and the new equilibrium is at 46 and 260.

- Price change: 6 / 40 = +15%. Quantity change: -40 / 300 = -13.3%.
- Consumer spending: 40 x 300 = 12,000 before; 46 x 260 = 11,960 after, almost unchanged.
- Along the demand curve, PED is about -13.3 / 15 = -0.89, just inelastic.

Writing the coordinates down first turns a picture into numbers you can use. Always state what the diagram does not show, for example how long the adjustment takes.

## Judging how strong the evidence is

| Question to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who said it, and why? | A minister or a lobby group may select favourable facts |
| Money or real values? | Inflation can make a rise look larger than it is |
| Levels or rates of change? | A falling growth rate is not a falling level |
| Average or distribution? | An average can hide how different groups are affected |
| How long a period? | Two years cannot establish a trend; the start year can change the story |
| Correlation or cause? | Two series moving together may share a third cause |
| What is missing? | Costs, quality, other countries, other years |

## Common mistakes

- Copying figures without saying what they show.
- Describing every year in turn instead of the overall pattern.
- Using theory with no reference to the data, or data with no theory.
- Quoting a figure without its unit or year.
- Accepting a claim in an extract without testing it against the table.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
- Forgetting to combine columns (harvest plus imports) when the question needs a total.

## Exam technique

- When a question says "using the data", quote at least two figures with units and dates.
- Calculate a percentage or real value when it makes the point sharper; examiners reward manipulation of data, not just copying.
- For "analyse", write a chain: evidence, concept, consequence. For "assess" or "evaluate", add a test of the evidence and a judgement.
- Keep calculations short and labelled so the reader can follow them.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many figures should I quote in a data answer?**
Enough to support each point, usually two or more, with units and dates. One precise figure used well beats a list of copied numbers.

**Should I bring in knowledge that is not in the extract?**
Yes, when it helps you apply a concept or explain a cause, but keep the data at the centre of the answer.

**What if the extract and the table disagree?**
Say so and explain which is more reliable, for example a time series over five years against a single quoted opinion.
