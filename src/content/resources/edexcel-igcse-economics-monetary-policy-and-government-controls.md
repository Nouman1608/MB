## Monetary policy and interest rates

**Monetary policy** is the use of changes in interest rates (and related tools) by the central bank to influence the economy. In this specification the focus is on interest rate changes.

An **interest rate** is the price of borrowing money, shown as a percentage of the amount borrowed. It is also the reward for saving. One rate therefore has two sides: borrowers pay it, savers receive it.

### The central bank's role

The central bank sets the **policy rate** (often called the base rate). Banks and other lenders use it as a starting point when they set their own rates for mortgages, loans, credit cards and savings accounts. The central bank usually has an inflation target and compares expected inflation with it:

- Inflation expected above target: it may raise the rate to slow spending.
- Inflation below target, or the economy weak: it may cut the rate to encourage spending.

Many central banks are independent of the government. An advantage is that decisions are less likely to be driven by the wish to win an election, for example by cutting rates before voting day. A disadvantage is that appointed officials make decisions that affect everyone.

## How a change in interest rates works through the economy

This chain is called the **transmission mechanism**: the route from the policy rate to spending, jobs and prices.

### Effect on consumers

When the rate rises:

1. Variable-rate loans and mortgages cost more each month.
2. Saving becomes more rewarding, so some people save instead of spend.
3. Borrowing for big purchases such as cars and furniture is less attractive.
4. Consumer spending falls, and confidence may fall too.

**Worked example (invented).** A household has a variable-rate loan of 150,000. The rate rises from 3% to 5%.

```
Interest at 3% = 150,000 x 0.03 = 4,500 a year
Interest at 5% = 150,000 x 0.05 = 7,500 a year
Extra cost     = 3,000 a year = 250 a month
```

If the household's yearly income after tax is 36,000, interest takes 12.5% of it at 3% (4,500 / 36,000) and 20.8% at 5% (7,500 / 36,000). Less is left for shops, so consumption falls.

A saver is affected the other way. With 20,000 saved, a rise from 2% to 4% lifts yearly interest from 400 to 800.

Households differ. A fixed-rate borrower feels the rise later, and a family with no debt may feel better off.

### Effect on businesses

Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, so fewer investment projects look worthwhile. A firm compares the profit a project earns with the interest it must pay.

**Worked example (invented).** A small bakery borrows 80,000 for a new oven. The oven is expected to add 5,600 a year to profit before interest, a return of 7% (5,600 / 80,000).

| Interest rate | Interest cost | Profit after interest |
|---|---|---|
| 3% | 2,400 | +3,200 |
| 5% | 4,000 | +1,600 |
| 8% | 6,400 | −800 |

At 8% the oven loses money, so the bakery would probably not buy it. Across many firms, fewer machines and buildings are bought, so **investment** falls. Firms with existing variable-rate debt also have higher costs, which cuts profit.

### Effect on exchange rates

A higher interest rate can attract foreign savers. They must buy Country A's currency to deposit money there, so its value tends to rise. A lower rate tends to have the opposite effect.

**Worked example (invented).** The central bank of Country A cuts its rate and the currency falls from 2.00 to 1.80 units of Country B's currency per A-unit.

```
Export priced 50 A-units: 50 x 2.00 = 100 B-units before; 50 x 1.80 = 90 B-units after (down 10%)
Import priced 72 B-units: 72 / 2.00 = 36 A-units before; 72 / 1.80 = 40 A-units after (up 11.1%)
```

Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. This links monetary policy to the current account.

## Impact on the macroeconomic objectives

Interest rates mainly change **aggregate demand (AD)**, which is total planned spending in the economy: consumption (C) + investment (I) + government spending (G) + exports minus imports (X − M).

**Worked example (invented).** Country A's AD, before and after a rate rise:

| Component | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Consumption | 600 | 570 |
| Investment | 150 | 135 |
| Government spending | 200 | 200 |
| Exports | 180 | 175 |
| Imports | 130 | 135 |
| **AD** | **1,000** | **945** |

AD falls by 55, which is 5.5% (55 / 1,000). Consumption and investment fall because borrowing is dearer. The currency rises, so exports fall slightly and imports rise.

How each objective is affected by a rate **rise** (a rate cut works in reverse):

| Objective | Likely effect of a rate rise | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | Falls or slows | Lower AD reduces demand-pull pressure; a stronger currency makes imported goods cheaper |
| Economic growth | Slows | Lower consumption and investment mean less output |
| Unemployment | Rises, usually after a delay | Firms facing weaker sales hire fewer workers |
| Current account | Tends to worsen | A stronger currency raises export prices and lowers import prices |

**Chain of reasoning for a rate cut:** interest rate falls, so borrowing is cheaper, so consumption and investment rise, so AD rises, so firms produce more and hire more, so growth rises and unemployment falls. If the economy is already near full capacity, firms raise prices instead and inflation rises.

### Why monetary policy can fail

- **Time lags.** Fixed-rate borrowers react slowly. Investment plans take months to change.
- **Confidence.** If people expect a bad future, cheap borrowing may not persuade them to spend.
- **Cause of inflation.** If inflation is cost-push (for example, dearer oil), higher rates do not cut costs and may just slow growth.
- **Trade-offs.** Fighting inflation can raise unemployment.

### Real interest rates

The real interest rate is roughly the nominal rate minus inflation. With a nominal rate of 6% and inflation of 4%, the real rate is about 2%. If inflation jumps to 7%, it is about −1%, so saving loses buying power.

## Awareness of asset purchasing

Cutting the policy rate has limits. If rates are already very low, there may be little room to cut further. A central bank can then use **asset purchasing** (often called quantitative easing). It creates new money electronically and uses it to buy assets, mainly government bonds, from banks, pension funds and other investors.

How it is meant to work:

1. The sellers receive money, so banks have more to lend.
2. Heavy buying pushes bond prices up. A bond's income is fixed, so a higher price means a lower **yield** (the return).
3. Lower yields push down other borrowing costs, so spending and investment may rise.

**Worked example (invented).** A bond pays 4 a year on a face value of 100. At a price of 100 the yield is 4%. When buying lifts its price to 104, the yield is 4 / 104 = 3.85%.

Advantages: it can support demand when the policy rate cannot fall. Disadvantages: if too much money is created it may cause inflation, it raises the price of assets such as shares and property (which benefits people who already own them), and the central bank may find it hard to reverse.

## Government controls

The specification asks for advantages and disadvantages of four controls. They are used for many problems, for example to reduce pollution.

### Regulation

**Regulation** means rules on how firms must behave, enforced by a public body: for example limits on the amount of waste a factory may release, safety standards and licensing.

- Advantages: sets clear standards; can stop the worst harm directly; flexible, as rules can be updated.
- Disadvantages: firms bear compliance costs; enforcement needs inspectors and money; one rule for all firms may not suit each firm.

**Worked example (invented).** A new safety rule costs every firm 12,000 a year. A small firm with sales of 240,000 spends 5.0% of its sales on it (12,000 / 240,000). A large firm with sales of 6,000,000 spends 0.2%. Small firms bear a heavier burden, and some may close.

### Legislation

**Legislation** means laws passed by the government that make certain acts legal or illegal, such as banning dumping waste in rivers.

- Advantages: applies to everyone, is clear and can ban the most harmful acts.
- Disadvantages: slow to pass; courts and police are costly; a ban can create a black market.

### Fines

A **fine** is a money penalty for breaking a law or rule.

- Advantages: raises revenue; makes polluting costly; punishes those who cause harm.
- Disadvantages: only works if the law-breaker is caught; a small fine can be treated as a business cost; firms may pass costs to customers.

**Worked example (invented).** A firm avoids 60,000 of treatment costs a year by dumping waste, and inspectors catch it one year in four (25%). If the fine is 100,000, the expected fine is 0.25 x 100,000 = 25,000, less than the saving, so the firm may keep dumping. To deter, the fine must exceed 60,000 / 0.25 = 240,000. A fine of 300,000 has an expected cost of 75,000, which is more than the saving.

### Pollution permits

A **pollution permit** allows its holder to release a set amount of pollution. The government sets a total limit and issues permits to match. Permits can usually be traded.

**Worked example (invented).** Firms K and L each emit 500 tonnes. The government issues permits for 800 tonnes in total, 400 each. Cutting a tonne costs K 30 and L 80.

```
No trading: K cuts 100 (cost 3,000); L cuts 100 (cost 8,000). Total cost = 11,000.
With trading: K cuts 200 (cost 6,000) and sells 100 permits to L at 55 each (5,500).
L buys 100 permits (cost 5,500) and keeps emitting 500.
Total emissions: 300 + 500 = 800 tonnes. Total cutting cost = 6,000.
K's net cost = 6,000 - 5,500 = 500 (saves 2,500). L pays 5,500 (saves 2,500).
```

The same limit is reached at a lower total cost. Any price between 30 and 80 makes both firms better off.

- Advantages: the total pollution is known; cuts happen where cheapest; firms have a reason to find cleaner methods.
- Disadvantages: the cap may be set too high; large firms may buy up permits; monitoring is needed; pollution may still be concentrated in one area; poorer firms may be priced out.

## Common errors

- Writing that a rate rise "increases spending". It reduces it.
- Describing only borrowers. Savers gain from higher rates.
- Calling asset purchasing a change in the interest rate. It is a separate tool, used when rates are already very low.
- Treating fines as always effective. The chance of being caught matters.
- Giving advantages without disadvantages. The specification asks for both, so a judgement needs both.
- Mixing up the exchange rate and the interest rate. They are linked but different.
