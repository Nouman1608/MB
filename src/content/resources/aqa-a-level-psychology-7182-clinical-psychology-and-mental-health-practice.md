This set follows AQA A-level Psychology (7182), Version 1.4 (1 September 2025), for teaching from September 2025. It covers Clinical Psychology and Mental Health, one of the four compulsory topics in Paper 1, Introductory Topics in Psychology, whose sections each contain multiple-choice, short-answer and extended-writing questions. The trial data and all people in the questions are fictional.

Learn the content first with the [clinical psychology and mental health study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-clinical-psychology-and-mental-health/) and the [clinical psychology revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-clinical-psychology-and-mental-health-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an emotional characteristic of OCD? Choose **one** letter.

A Repeatedly checking that the door is locked
B Recurrent intrusive thoughts about germs
C Strong feelings of disgust and guilt
D Avoiding public toilets

**[1]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following describes flooding? Choose **one** letter.

A Learning to relax while imagining a feared object
B Working step by step up a hierarchy of feared situations
C Immediate, prolonged exposure to the most feared situation
D Challenging irrational beliefs about a feared object

**[1]**

**3.** Outline the deviation from ideal mental health definition. **[3]**

**4.** Outline Ellis's ABC model as an explanation of depression. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** limitation of the genetic explanation of OCD. **[3]**

### Section B: applied and data questions

**6.** Dev washes his hands more than fifty times a day. He has stopped going to work because he cannot leave the house without completing his routines, and his friends say he no longer replies to messages.

Identify **two** definitions in the field of mental health that could be used to judge Dev's behaviour as a problem, and explain how each applies. **[4]**

**7.** Sasha says: "I know it's silly, but I keep picturing my house burning down. I feel sick with worry until I've checked every plug three times. Then I feel guilty for making us late."

Identify **one** behavioural, **one** emotional and **one** cognitive characteristic of OCD shown by Sasha. **[3]**

**8.** Gemma has a phobia of dogs. She is very anxious about therapy and has a learning difficulty that makes complex discussion hard for her.

Explain whether systematic desensitisation or flooding would be more suitable for Gemma. **[4]**

**9.** In a trial, three groups of adults with depression received CBT, an SSRI, or both together for twelve weeks. Mean scores on a depression scale (higher scores mean more severe depression) are shown.

| Group | Mean score before | Mean score after |
|---|---|---|
| CBT | 30 | 18 |
| SSRI | 31 | 19 |
| CBT and SSRI | 30 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reduction in the mean score for the combined group. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the combined treatment may have been the most effective. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** reason why the researchers should measure the scores again a year later. **[1]**

**10.** After failing his driving test, Tom says, "I failed, so I'm useless at everything, and I'll never pass anything." Explain how a therapist using CBT could challenge Tom's irrational thoughts. **[4]**

### Section C: extended writing

**11.** Evaluate the behavioural approach to treating phobias. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss the cognitive approach to explaining depression. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A and D are behavioural and B is cognitive; emotions are feelings.

**2.** C [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A and B describe systematic desensitisation, and D describes cognitive therapy.

**3.** Jahoda (1958) defined criteria that people need for ideal mental health [1], such as a positive attitude towards the self, self-actualisation, autonomy, resistance to stress, an accurate perception of reality and environmental mastery [1]. Someone who falls short of these criteria is judged to have a mental health problem [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two criteria named accurately are enough for the middle mark; a list of six is not needed.

**4.** Ellis argued depression comes from irrational thinking, not events themselves [1]. A is an activating event, such as being rejected [1]. B is the belief about it; if it is irrational, such as "I must be liked by everyone", it leads to C, the emotional and behavioural consequences, such as depression [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An example for each letter makes the answer clear and secure.

**5.** Concordance for OCD in identical (MZ) twins is well below 100%, for example 68% in the review by Nestadt et al. (2010) [1]. Because MZ twins share all their genes, environmental factors must also play a part [1]. So the genetic explanation is incomplete; a diathesis-stress view, with an inherited vulnerability triggered by experience, fits the evidence better [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Another valid limitation is that many genes are involved, so it is hard to identify which ones cause OCD; develop either point fully.

**6.** Failure to function adequately [1]: Dev cannot cope with ordinary daily demands, having stopped going to work and stopped replying to friends [1]. Statistical infrequency [1]: washing hands more than fifty times a day is very rare in the population [1]. (Deviation from social norms or from ideal mental health, applied to the scenario, are also creditworthy.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each application mark needs a detail from Dev's case.

**7.** Behavioural: checking every plug three times (compulsions) [1]. Emotional: feeling sick with worry, or guilt [1]. Cognitive: intrusive images of the house burning down, or insight ("I know it's silly") [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote Sasha's words; general characteristics not in the scenario do not earn marks.

**8.** Systematic desensitisation is more suitable [1]. Gemma is very anxious, and SD lets her move up a hierarchy at her own pace while relaxed, whereas flooding would be highly distressing and she might withdraw, strengthening her phobia [1]. SD does not depend on complex discussion, because it relies on relaxation and graded exposure rather than insight [1]. Flooding also needs fully informed consent to a traumatic procedure, which may be hard to obtain given her learning difficulty [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A justified choice needs reasons linked to both details given: her anxiety and her learning difficulty.

**9. (a)** Reduction = 30 − 14 = 16 [1]; 16/30 × 100 = 53.3% [1]
**(b)** The SSRI may reduce symptoms such as low mood and low energy quickly [1], which helps the person engage with CBT, which then tackles the irrational thinking behind the depression [1].
**(c)** To see whether improvements last after treatment ends, since symptoms may return when medication stops [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the starting score, 30; dividing by 14 is a common error.

**10.** The therapist helps Tom identify the irrational belief that failing one test makes him "useless at everything" [1]. Using Ellis's disputing, the therapist asks for evidence, such as things Tom does well, showing the belief is not supported [1]. The therapist challenges the logic: failing one test does not mean he will "never pass anything" [1]. Tom might be set homework, such as recording successes during the week, to replace the belief with a more rational, effective one [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification names "challenging irrational thoughts"; show the challenge applied to Tom's own words.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show the content a strong answer could use.

- Systematic desensitisation uses relaxation and an anxiety hierarchy, relying on reciprocal inhibition [1].
- Flooding uses immediate, prolonged exposure, so fear subsides through extinction [1].
- SD lets patients control the pace, so it suits anxious patients and those with learning difficulties [1].
- Flooding is quicker and so more cost-effective, but it is traumatic and some patients drop out [1].
- Flooding needs fully informed consent, raising ethical issues [1].
- Both treatments address avoidance, the maintaining factor in the two-process model, which gives them a clear rationale [1].
- They may be less effective for phobias with a strong cognitive element, such as social anxiety, where CBT may also be needed [1].
- Judgement: behavioural treatments are effective and practical for specific phobias, with SD the safer choice for most patients [1].

**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the two treatments with each other is the most efficient form of evaluation here.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. A strong answer might use this plan.

*AO1 (description)*

- The cognitive approach explains depression through irrational and negative thinking [1].
- Beck (1967): faulty information processing focuses on the negative and ignores the positive [1].
- Negative self-schemas form in childhood, for example through criticism [1].
- These lead to the negative triad: negative views of the self, the world and the future [1].
- Ellis (1962): the ABC model, an activating event, a belief and consequences [1].
- Irrational beliefs, such as "musturbation", turn events into depression [1].
- Ellis's model explains why two people react differently to the same event [1].
- The approach gives rise to CBT, which challenges irrational thoughts [1].

*AO3 (evaluation)*

- Research has found that depressed people show more negative thinking, supporting a link [1].
- The evidence is often correlational, so negative thinking may be a result of depression rather than its cause [1].
- The success of CBT supports the approach, since changing thinking reduces symptoms [1].
- But treatment success does not prove cause, as the treatment–aetiology fallacy shows [1].
- Ellis's model fits reactive depression better than depression with no clear trigger [1].
- It risks blaming the individual and overlooking real causes such as poverty or abuse [1].
- Biological explanations, such as low serotonin, offer an alternative, and drugs also reduce symptoms [1].
- Judgement: cognitive factors are an important part of depression, best combined with biological and social factors in an interactionist view [1].

**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* An essay that describes only Beck, or only Ellis, cannot cover the cognitive approach as the specification names it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling characteristics with the wrong type: behavioural, emotional or cognitive.
- Answering scenario questions without quoting the scenario.
- Confusing SD with flooding.
- Calculating percentage change from the wrong value.
- Describing CBT without showing how irrational thoughts are challenged.
- Using treatment success as proof of a cause.
- Ending extended answers without a judgement.
