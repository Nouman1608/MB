This guide teaches section **3.3.3.2 Elimination** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. This is the halogenoalkane elimination, not the separate elimination of water from alcohols in section 3.3.5.3. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level, and A-level Paper 3 can test it. The [Halogenoalkanes study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes/) covers the whole of 3.3.3. This page concentrates on the one idea that this sub-topic adds: the same reagent can behave as a nucleophile or as a base, and the two reactions happen at the same time. Recall is in the [Elimination revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-halogenoalkanes-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Elimination practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-halogenoalkanes-practice/). The reaction you need to compare it with is in the [Nucleophilic substitution study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nucleophilic-substitution/).

## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The concurrent substitution and elimination reactions of a halogenoalkane (for example 2-bromopropane with potassium hydroxide) | Recognise that both reactions happen together from the same reagents |
| | Explain the role of the reagent as both nucleophile and base |
| | Outline the mechanisms of these reactions |

## Two roles for one reagent

Hydroxide ions have a lone pair of electrons and a negative charge. They can do two different jobs.

| Role | What the hydroxide does | Where it attacks | Product from 2-bromopropane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleophile | Donates a lone pair to form a bond | The δ+ carbon bonded to Br | Propan-2-ol (substitution) |
| Base | Accepts a proton, H⁺ | A hydrogen on a carbon next to the C–Br carbon | Propene (elimination) |

A **base** is a proton acceptor. A **nucleophile** is a lone-pair donor that attacks carbon. The same ion is both, depending on which atom it attacks. Because both can happen, you get a **mixture** of products from one flask, and the proportion depends on conditions.

## Conditions that favour each pathway

| Condition | Favours substitution | Favours elimination |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | Water: aqueous hydroxide | Ethanol: ethanolic hydroxide |
| Temperature | Warm | Hotter (heat under reflux) |
| Structure | Primary halogenoalkane | Tertiary halogenoalkane |

In an exam answer, "ethanolic potassium hydroxide, heat under reflux" is the expected set of conditions for elimination, and "aqueous potassium hydroxide, warm or reflux" for substitution. State both the solvent and the heating. Substitution is never completely eliminated, so describe the conditions as "mainly elimination" if the question says so.

## The elimination mechanism

Take 2-bromopropane, CH₃–CHBr–CH₃, with hydroxide. Elimination needs a hydrogen on a carbon **adjacent** to the carbon bearing the bromine. Here both CH₃ groups qualify.

```
 HO⁻                    (one CH₃ group of 2-bromopropane shown)
   \
    H–CH₂–CH(Br)–CH₃
       
Arrow 1: lone pair on O of OH⁻ → the H on the adjacent carbon
Arrow 2: the C–H bond pair → between the two carbons, forming C=C
Arrow 3: the C–Br bond pair → Br, leaving as Br⁻

Products: CH₃–CH=CH₂ + H₂O + Br⁻
```

Three arrows, three electron pairs. Check the sequence: the hydroxide uses its lone pair to take H⁺, leaving the C–H bonding pair behind; that pair moves to make the double bond; the double bond forms only because the bromine takes the C–Br pair away. Water forms from OH⁻ and H⁺.

### Writing the mechanism as marking points

1. Dipole on the C–Br bond (δ+ on C, δ− on Br). Many mark schemes accept this, so include it.
2. Arrow from the lone pair of OH⁻ to the hydrogen.
3. Arrow from the C–H bond to the C–C bond region, forming the double bond.
4. Arrow from the C–Br bond to the bromine.
5. Products: the alkene, water and Br⁻.

## Predicting the products

Find the carbon bearing the halogen. Then look at **each** neighbouring carbon that has at least one hydrogen. Removing H from different neighbours can give different alkenes.

| Halogenoalkane | Neighbouring carbons with H | Alkene products |
|---|---|---|
| 1-bromopropane | One (C2) | Propene only |
| 2-bromopropane | Two, equivalent | Propene only |
| 2-bromobutane | C1 and C3 | But-1-ene, (E)-but-2-ene and (Z)-but-2-ene |
| 3-bromopentane | C2 and C4, equivalent | (E)-pent-2-ene and (Z)-pent-2-ene |
| 2-bromo-2-methylpropane | Three equivalent methyl groups | 2-methylpropene only |

For 2-bromobutane, removal of H from C3 gives but-2-ene, which has E/Z isomers because each carbon of the double bond carries two different groups. Removal of H from C1 gives but-1-ene, which has no E/Z isomerism. So three alkene isomers are possible. In practice the but-2-ene products are formed in larger amounts, but you are not asked to predict amounts.

## Worked example 1: mechanism for 2-bromopropane

**Question.** Outline the mechanism for the elimination reaction of 2-bromopropane with potassium hydroxide.

Answer in steps. Draw the molecule as CH₃–CHBr–CH₃. Draw one arrow from the lone pair on O of OH⁻ to one of the H atoms on a CH₃ group. Draw a second arrow from that C–H bond to the carbon–carbon bond. Draw a third arrow from the C–Br bond to Br. The products are propene, water and Br⁻.

## Worked example 2: how many alkenes?

**Question.** 2-bromo-3-methylbutane is heated with ethanolic potassium hydroxide. Name the possible alkene products.

The structure is CH₃–CHBr–CH(CH₃)–CH₃. The carbon bearing Br is C2. Its neighbours are C1 (a CH₃ group, so it has hydrogens) and C3 (a CH group with one hydrogen).

- Removing H from C1 gives CH₂=CH–CH(CH₃)₂, which is **3-methylbut-1-ene**.
- Removing H from C3 gives CH₃–CH=C(CH₃)₂, which is **2-methylbut-2-ene**.

Neither has E/Z isomers (3-methylbut-1-ene has two hydrogens on one carbon of the double bond, and 2-methylbut-2-ene has two methyl groups on one carbon), so there are **two** alkene products.

## Worked example 3: a competing-reaction calculation

12.29 g of 2-bromopropane (Mᵣ = 122.9) reacts with potassium hydroxide so that 60 % of the molecules undergo elimination and 40 % substitution. Calculate the volume of propene formed at room temperature and pressure (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹) and the mass of propan-2-ol (Mᵣ = 60.0) formed.

```
n(2-bromopropane) = 12.29 ÷ 122.9 = 0.1000 mol
n(propene)        = 0.60 × 0.1000 = 0.0600 mol
V(propene)        = 0.0600 × 24.0 = 1.44 dm³
n(propan-2-ol)    = 0.40 × 0.1000 = 0.0400 mol
mass              = 0.0400 × 60.0 = 2.40 g
```

The two pathways compete for the same halogenoalkane, so the amounts add up to the amount of starting material.

## Worked example 4: choosing conditions

A chemist wants to turn 2-bromobutane mainly into butene.

- Use ethanolic potassium hydroxide, not aqueous.
- Heat under reflux rather than warming gently.
- Note that some butan-2-ol is still formed.

To make butan-2-ol instead, use aqueous potassium hydroxide and warm, and accept that some butene forms.

## Linking to other specification content

- The alkenes made here can be made into polymers, as shown in the Addition polymers sub-topic.
- Elimination of water from alcohols (3.3.5.3) uses an acid catalyst, not a base. The mechanism is different, so do not mix them up.
- Elimination reduces the yield in substitution reactions, so it is a reason why percentage yields from nucleophilic substitution are below 100 %.

## Substitution and elimination side by side

| Feature | Substitution | Elimination |
|---|---|---|
| Role of OH⁻ | Nucleophile (lone-pair donor) | Base (proton acceptor) |
| Atom attacked by OH⁻ | The δ+ carbon bonded to the halogen | A hydrogen on the adjacent carbon |
| Curly arrows needed | Two | Three |
| Bond formed | C–O single bond | C=C double bond |
| Organic product | Alcohol | Alkene |
| Other products | Halide ion | Halide ion and water |
| Typical solvent | Water | Ethanol |
| Change in saturation | Stays saturated | Becomes unsaturated |

A quick test to tell the two products apart in the laboratory is to add bromine water to a sample of the organic product. The alkene decolourises it; the alcohol does not. This links to the Addition reactions of alkenes sub-topic.

## Why structure matters, in outline

Elimination needs a hydrogen on an adjacent carbon, and the more alkyl groups surround the C–X carbon, the more such hydrogens there are and the more crowded the carbon is for a nucleophile to approach. This is why tertiary halogenoalkanes lean towards elimination and primary ones towards substitution. The specification does not ask you to quantify this, but you can use it to justify a statement such as "more elimination product is expected from 2-bromo-2-methylpropane than from 1-bromopropane".

## Common mistakes

- Treating hydroxide only as a nucleophile and drawing the arrow to carbon, when the question asks about elimination.
- Writing the hydrogen as coming from the same carbon as the halogen. It must come from an **adjacent** carbon.
- Leaving out the arrow from the C–H bond, so the double bond has no source.
- Forgetting water as a product.
- Missing E/Z isomers when counting products.
- Naming the solvent as water, or not naming a solvent at all, when ethanol is required.
- Giving aqueous conditions for elimination.
- Defining a base as an electron-pair donor. A base is a proton acceptor here.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the role of the reagent**: name both roles, say what each attacks and give the product of each.
- **Outline the mechanism**: three arrows for elimination, two for substitution; use the lone pair on oxygen each time.
- **Name possible products**: check every adjacent carbon, then check for E/Z isomers.
- **Give conditions**: solvent and heating together.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does bromine leave when the hydroxide takes a proton?**
The electrons from the broken C–H bond form the C=C double bond. Carbon cannot hold five bonds, so the C–Br bond pair moves onto bromine as the double bond forms.

**Can 1-bromopropane undergo elimination?**
Yes. It has hydrogens on C2, so it gives propene. It mostly undergoes substitution under aqueous conditions.

**Is the elimination reversible?**
Not in this context. The bromide ion and water are stable products, and the alkene does not readily react back.
