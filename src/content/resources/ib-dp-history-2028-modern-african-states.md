**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 12, "Modern developments in Ethiopia, Niger, Somalia, Tunisia, Zambia and Zimbabwe (circa 1945 to 2020)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The guide says every examination question will require you to refer to **one of the prescribed countries** and recommends teaching a variety to cover all the inquiry topics. The six inquiry topics are: the reasons for the emergence of authoritarian rule; the move to multi-party democracy in the 1980s and 1990s; the reasons for economic growth and development; social challenges; the causes of coups and civil wars; and the impact of external involvement.

## The six countries at a glance

| Country | Independence or key break | Typical authoritarian leader or regime | Notable features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethiopia | Never fully colonised; revolution 1974 | Haile Selassie; Mengistu (the Derg); Meles Zenawi (EPRDF) | Famine; Cold War switch; ethnic federalism; rapid state-led growth |
| Niger | Independence 1960 from France | Diori; Kountché | Uranium; drought; repeated coups; young population |
| Somalia | Independence 1960; Siad Barre 1969 | Siad Barre | Ogaden War; state collapse 1991; piracy; Al-Shabaab |
| Tunisia | Independence 1956 | Bourguiba; Ben Ali | Women's rights; tourism; revolution 2011 |
| Zambia | Independence 1964 | Kaunda | One-party state 1972 to 1991; copper; liberation base |
| Zimbabwe | Independence 1980 | Mugabe | Land reform; hyperinflation; coup 2017 |

## 1. Reasons for the emergence of authoritarian rule

- **Impact of colonial legacies.** Colonial states were built for control and extraction, with few democratic habits. Borders joined or divided peoples. In Somalia, the union of the British and Italian territories in 1960 left deep clan divisions. In Zimbabwe, white-minority rule and the Rhodesian war left a culture of violence and mistrust. In Ethiopia, the empire's centralised monarchy ruled diverse peoples.
- **Results of independence and resistance movements.** Movements that had fought colonial rule often regarded opposition as a threat to unity. Bourguiba's Neo-Destour led Tunisia to independence in 1956, and he became president for life in 1975. Kaunda's UNIP led Zambia and banned other parties in 1972. ZANU-PF dominated Zimbabwe after 1980 and used force in Matabeleland (the Gukurahundi, 1983 to 1987), where thousands of civilians were killed.
- **Role of individuals, including personal ambition.** Siad Barre took power in a coup in 1969, Mengistu Haile Mariam emerged as leader of the Derg after 1974 and began the Red Terror in 1977, and Mugabe held power for 37 years.
- **Role of ideology.** Zambian Humanism and African socialism; scientific socialism in Somalia; Marxism-Leninism in Ethiopia after 1974; the one-party state justified as national unity.

**Argument to weigh:** "Authoritarian rule was mainly the result of colonial legacies." Support: weak institutions and divided societies. Challenge: leaders made choices, and Cold War patrons supported strongmen.

## 2. Move to multi-party democracy in the 1980s and 1990s

- **Foreign influence.** The end of the Cold War reduced support for allies of either superpower. Western donors and the IMF and World Bank linked aid and loans to economic reform and, increasingly, to political change. France's President Mitterrand told African leaders in 1990 that aid would favour democratisation.
- **Failures of single-party states.** Falling commodity prices, debt and corruption exposed the weakness of one-party economies. In Zambia, copper revenue fell, and shortages and unrest followed.
- **Ideological factors.** The collapse of Marxism-Leninism discredited socialist one-party systems, and liberal ideas gained ground.
- **Role of popular and elite support for reform.** In Zambia, the trade unions under Frederick Chiluba, the churches and business leaders formed the Movement for Multiparty Democracy, which won the 1991 election and ended Kaunda's rule. In Niger, a national conference in 1991 led to a multi-party election in 1993. In Ethiopia, rebels led by the EPRDF overthrew the Derg in 1991 and a new constitution in 1995 introduced ethnic federalism, though the ruling coalition dominated politics. In Tunisia, Ben Ali promised pluralism after 1987 but built another authoritarian system. In Somalia, the state collapsed in 1991 when Barre fled. In Zimbabwe, ZANU-PF kept power, and opposition grew only with the Movement for Democratic Change in 1999.

## 3. Reasons for economic growth and development (to 2020)

- **Political stability and reform.** Tunisia's stability and investment in education supported growth from the 1970s. Ethiopia under Meles Zenawi followed a state-led developmental model, with growth reported at high rates between about 2004 and 2018. Zambia's economy grew after copper prices rose in the 2000s.
- **Role of individuals and entrepreneurs.** Bourguiba's focus on education and women's legal status, Meles's planning, and, in Somalia, the traders and diaspora who kept the economy going without a functioning state through remittances.
- **Investment and development of infrastructure.** Chinese loans and projects in Zambia and Ethiopia, such as the Addis Ababa to Djibouti railway (2016) and the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (begun 2011), and earlier the TAZARA railway between Zambia and the Tanzanian coast (1970s). Niger's uranium mines attracted foreign investment.
- **Impact of technology.** Mobile phones and mobile money transformed trade in Somalia, Zambia and Ethiopia. In Tunisia, links with European manufacturers and call centres.

**Argument to weigh:** "Stable government is the main reason for development." Support: Tunisia and Ethiopia. Challenge: Somalia's trade grew without a state, and Zimbabwe's early gains were reversed despite a stable ruling party.

## 4. Social challenges (to 2020)

- **Health, including disease and famine.** HIV/AIDS devastated Zambia and Zimbabwe in the 1990s and 2000s. Ethiopia suffered severe famines in 1973 to 1974 and 1983 to 1985, worsened by war and government policy. Droughts caused food crises in Niger (2005, 2010) and Somalia (1992, 2011).
- **Education, including illiteracy.** Zimbabwe had expanded education rapidly after 1980. Niger had very low literacy and school attendance, and Somalia's school system collapsed. Tunisia invested heavily in schooling.
- **Demographics.** Niger has one of the youngest and fastest growing populations in the world, putting pressure on land, jobs and services.
- **Gender inequality and marginalised groups.** Tunisia's 1956 Personal Status Code gave women rights unusual in the region, such as the ban on polygamy. In Niger, early marriage remains common. Ethnic minorities, such as the Tuareg in Niger, the Ndebele in Zimbabwe, the Oromo and other peoples in Ethiopia, and smaller clans in Somalia, have faced exclusion.

## 5. Causes of coups d'état and civil wars

- **Ethnic tensions.** Clan divisions in Somalia; conflict between Ethiopian governments and Eritrean and other regional movements; Tuareg rebellions in northern Niger from the 1990s; Shona-Ndebele tension in Zimbabwe.
- **Economic problems, including inequality.** Poverty and unequal distribution fuelled discontent. In Ethiopia, famine and inflation helped the 1974 revolution against Haile Selassie.
- **Environmental factors.** Drought and desertification in the Sahel and Horn of Africa forced people to move and increased competition for land.
- **Failures of civilian governments.** Niger's first president, Diori, was overthrown by the army in 1974 during a famine, followed by further coups in 1996, 1999 and 2010. Somalia's civilian governments of the 1960s were criticised for corruption and clan politics before Barre's coup. Zimbabwe's army pushed Mugabe from office in November 2017.

## 6. Impact of external involvement

- **International rivalries and destabilisation by outside forces.** In the Cold War, Somalia was allied with the USSR until 1977, then Ethiopia and Somalia switched sides during the Ogaden War (1977 to 1978), when Soviet and Cuban forces helped Ethiopia. Rhodesia and South Africa attacked Zambia for hosting liberation movements. Libya supported rebels in Niger.
- **Neocolonialism.** Debate about whether economic dependence amounts to a continuing form of colonial control: French influence in Niger through uranium and the CFA franc; multinational mining companies and debt in Zambia; IMF programmes.
- **African Union.** It replaced the OAU in 2002, sent peacekeepers to Somalia (AMISOM from 2007), and can suspend members after coups. Its record is mixed.
- **UN.** The UN intervened in Somalia (UNOSOM and the US-led task force in 1992 to 1995), and imposed sanctions on Rhodesia from 1966. Its operations in Somalia withdrew after heavy losses.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "To what extent was economic failure the main reason for the end of single-party rule in one prescribed country?" Use Zambia.

1. Define "main reason" and list alternative factors.
2. Economic: collapse in copper prices after the 1970s, debt, shortages and the IMF reform programme.
3. Others: trade union and church organisation, external pressure after the Cold War, Kaunda's own decision to allow elections in 1991.
4. Perspectives: some historians stress structural economic decline; others emphasise the role of organised opposition and political choice.
5. Judgement: economic crisis created the conditions, but the MMD's organisation made change possible.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the six countries as one case.
- Giving only the Cold War as the cause of all conflict.
- Ignoring Tunisia and Zambia, which are examples of both reform and authoritarianism.
- Using casualty or growth figures without "about".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this study the same as the earlier independence study?** No. This study starts around 1945 but focuses on what happened after independence or revolution.

**Do I need all six countries?** No. Two or three with good depth can cover all six inquiry topics.
