This study guide teaches the Paper 2 comparative essay for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language
and Literature, at SL and HL. It draws on two of the course's three areas of exploration,
**Intertextuality: connecting texts** and **Time and space**, because Paper 2 asks you to connect two
literary works and to read them in their contexts. It follows the current IB subject brief (first
assessments for SL and HL 2021). Only the paper's weighting differs between levels.

## What Paper 2 is

The subject brief describes Paper 2 as a **comparative essay based on two literary works**, written in
response to **a choice of one out of four questions**.

| Fact from the subject brief | SL | HL |
|---|---|---|
| Time | 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) | 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) |
| Weighting of final grade | 35% | 25% |
| Works used | two literary works | two literary works |

So:

- **Only literary works count.** Advertisements, speeches and other non-literary texts belong to
  Paper 1 and the individual oral, not here.
- **Expect questions you have not seen.** The brief says only that you choose one of four questions;
  your teacher will show you specimen questions. Plan to fit the works you know to a new question.
- **The comparison is the task.** An essay that discusses two works well but separately has not done
  what the paper asks.

Plan to write from memory. Your teacher will confirm the exam rules and which of your studied works you
may use for Paper 2.

## Before the exam: preparing your works

### Build a pool, not a pair

If you prepare only two works, you are stuck when no question suits them. Prepare at least three
works well. Choose works that differ in form (a play,
a novel, a poetry collection) or in time and place. Differences give you more to compare.

### Make an evidence bank for each work

For every work in your pool, keep one page with:

1. **8–10 key moments** you can describe precisely: who, where, what happens, where it falls in the
   structure (opening, turning point, ending).
2. **5–6 short quotations** of a few words each, chosen because they carry an image, a shift in tone or
   a key idea. Short quotations are easier to recall exactly.
3. **Form and structure notes**: narrative voice, genre conventions, how the work opens and ends,
   any frame or repeated pattern.
4. **Context notes**: when and where it was written, and one way a reader today may read it
   differently from its first audience.
5. **Big concerns**: 4–6 human concerns the work explores (power, freedom, guilt, belonging).

### Pre-compare, don't pre-write

Do not memorise a whole essay; it rarely answers the question set. Instead, for each
pair in your pool, list three similarities and three differences in *method* (how the writers work),
not just in content. That list is what you adapt on the day.

## In the exam: reading the question

Whatever the question, unpack it in three moves.

1. **Underline the key term** (for example "silence", "a turning point", "a place").
2. **Find the double demand.** Many questions ask both *how* writers present something and *to what
   effect*, or ask for both similarity and difference. Your essay must meet both halves.
3. **Test the fit.** Before committing, name one moment from each work that answers the question. If
   you cannot find at least three comparable points, pick another question or another pair.

## Building a comparative thesis

A thesis for Paper 2 names **both works**, makes **one claim about the question**, and says **how the
works differ in method or effect**. A useful pattern:

```
Both [Work A] and [Work B] present [key term] as [shared claim],
but where [Writer A] uses [method A] to [effect A],
[Writer B] uses [method B] to [effect B], because [context or form].
```

Compare three versions for a question about how writers present home.

- *Weak (summary):* "Both works are about homes and the people in them."
- *Better (claim, no method):* "Both works show the home as a place of control."
- *Strong (claim + method + reason):* "Both works present the home as a place of control, but one
  writer shows it through a stage set the audience watches from outside, while the other traps the
  reader inside one character's diary, so the first asks us to judge and the second asks us to feel
  confined."

The strong version already tells you your paragraphs: control, the outside view, the inside view.

## Weaving comparison through the essay

### Point by point, not text by text

A **block** structure spends the first half on Work A and the second on Work B. It usually leaves the
real comparison to the final paragraph. A **point-by-point** structure makes every body paragraph
about both works. Use point by point.

### A paragraph pattern

1. **Comparative topic sentence** naming both works and the point.
2. **Evidence from Work A** with a short analysis of method.
3. **Evidence from Work B**, linked with a comparative connective.
4. **The comparison itself**: why the difference in method matters for the question.
5. **Link back** to the thesis.

Connectives that do real work: *whereas, while, by contrast, similarly, in the same way, unlike,
both ... but, where A ..., B ...*. A connective is not enough on its own; step 4 still has to say what
the difference shows.

## Using remembered evidence

When you write from memory, evidence takes several forms.

| Type of evidence | Example of the form (from the worked example below) |
|---|---|
| Short quotation | a line of a few words, recalled exactly |
| Precise reference to a moment | Nora hiding the macaroons in Act 1 |
| Structural placement | the final stage direction, the last entry of a diary |
| Recurring image or motif | the pattern of the wallpaper |
| Feature of form | first-person journal entries; a three-act play |

If you are unsure of the exact words, **paraphrase precisely** rather than misquote. "Near the end,
Nora tells Torvald they have never once talked seriously in eight years of marriage" is accurate
evidence. A misremembered quotation in inverted commas is not.

## Bringing in time and space and intertextuality

- **Time and space**: use context to explain a *difference* between the works, not as a separate
  history paragraph. "Written for a theatre audience in 1879" explains why Ibsen's challenge is public
  and argued. A context sentence that explains nothing can be cut.
- **Intertextuality**: compare conventions. How does each work use or break the expectations of its
  form? A play that ends with a door closing and a story that ends with a woman creeping round a room
  both refuse the neat ending their readers expected. That shared refusal is a strong comparative point.

## Worked example: from question to paragraph

**Original question written for Marlbridge:** *"In what ways, and with what effects, do two works you
have studied present a character who resists the role others have given them?"*

**Works chosen:** Henrik Ibsen, *A Doll's House* (play, first performed 1879); Charlotte Perkins
Gilman, "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (short story, 1892). Both are public domain.

**Fit test:** Nora leaves her husband and children at the end of the play; Gilman's narrator,
confined by her physician husband, tears down the wallpaper. Both resist; the forms of resistance
differ. Good fit.

**Thesis:** Both works present resistance to the role of the obedient wife, but Ibsen stages it as a
reasoned public argument that ends in Nora's departure, while Gilman locks the reader inside a
first-person journal, so that resistance appears as a breakdown the narrator experiences as freedom.

**Plan (point by point):**

| Paragraph | Point | Work A: *A Doll's House* | Work B: "The Yellow Wall-Paper" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | How the role is imposed | Torvald's pet names (a lark, a squirrel) | John, a physician, forbids her to write or work |
| 2 | Small, hidden resistance | Nora eats the forbidden macaroons in secret | the narrator hides her journal from John |
| 3 | Form shapes the resistance | three acts building to a debate | journal entries growing shorter and stranger |
| 4 | The ending and its effect on the audience | Nora leaves; the door closes offstage | she creeps over John, who has fainted |

**Model paragraph (point 2), original writing for Marlbridge:**

> Both writers show resistance beginning in secret, but the secrecy works differently in each. In Act 1
> of *A Doll's House*, Nora hides the macaroons Torvald has forbidden and denies eating them. The lie is
> small and almost comic, yet it shows that she already keeps a private self he does not see. Gilman's
> narrator also hides something forbidden: the journal itself. Early on she writes that "John laughs at
> me, of course, but one expects that in marriage", and she stops writing whenever he comes near. Where
> the audience of Ibsen's play watches Nora's deception from outside and can judge it, Gilman's reader
> is the only witness to the narrator's secret, so we become her accomplice. The difference in form
> therefore changes our role: Ibsen invites us to weigh Nora's choices, while Gilman makes us share the
> narrator's confinement.

The topic sentence names both works, each work gets one precise piece of evidence, and the last two
sentences say what the difference in method means for the question.

| Stage | Minutes |

| Introduction and four or five body paragraphs | 80 |
| Final paragraph and check | 10 |
| **Total** | **105** |

## Common errors

- **Two essays in one.** A block structure with a single comparative sentence at the end.
- **Retelling the plot.** Summary shows you read the work; analysis of method answers the question.
- **Ignoring half the question.** Writing about *how* without *to what effect*, or similarity without
  difference.
- **A thesis that could fit any question.** If your thesis does not contain the question's key term,
  rewrite it.
- **Uneven treatment.** One work gets three quotations per paragraph and the other gets none.
- **Context as decoration.** A paragraph of history with no link to either work's methods.
- **Misquoting.** An inaccurate quotation weakens trust in your evidence. Paraphrase instead.
- **Forcing a pair.** Choosing favourite works when another pair fits the question better.
