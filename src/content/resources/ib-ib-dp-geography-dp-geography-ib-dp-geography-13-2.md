## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how environmental risks arise from physical processes, human activity and their interaction.
- Examine how global interactions spread environmental risks between places.
- Analyse why some places and groups are more exposed and vulnerable than others.
- Evaluate attempts to manage transboundary environmental risks.

## Expressing risk as probability

Risk combines how likely an event is with how harmful it would be. Planners often describe the likelihood of floods or storms by a **return period**: a "1-in-100-year flood" has a 1 per cent chance of happening in any one year. People often misread this as "once a century", which hides how likely it is over a lifetime or a mortgage.

### Worked example: chances over time

The probability of at least one event in n years is 1 - (1 - p)^n, where p is the annual probability.

```
1-in-100-year flood: p = 0.01
  Over 30 years:  1 - 0.99^30 = 1 - 0.740 = 0.260, about 26%
  Over 50 years:  1 - 0.99^50 = 1 - 0.605 = 0.395, about 40%

1-in-50-year flood: p = 0.02
  Over 20 years:  1 - 0.98^20 = 1 - 0.668 = 0.332, about 33%
```

A household with a 30-year mortgage on a 1-in-100-year floodplain has roughly a one-in-four chance of being flooded at least once. Two further points lift an evaluation. First, return periods are calculated from past records, so climate change and land-use change can make them out of date: an event once rated 1-in-100 may become more frequent. Second, probability is only half of risk; the consequences depend on exposure and vulnerability.

## Global limits: two frameworks

### Planetary boundaries

In 2009 a group of scientists led by Johan Rockström proposed nine **planetary boundaries**, limits for Earth-system processes within which humanity can operate safely. They include climate change, biodiversity loss, changes to nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, land-system change, freshwater use, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, atmospheric aerosols and novel substances such as synthetic chemicals. The framework treats environmental risk as global and interconnected: crossing one boundary can increase pressure on others.

### Tipping points

A **tipping point** is a threshold beyond which a system shifts to a new state that is hard to reverse. Examples studied by scientists include the loss of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, dieback of parts of the Amazon rainforest, and the weakening of Atlantic ocean circulation. Tipping points matter for risk because they break the assumption that harm increases gradually: small additional changes could trigger large, lasting effects. Their exact thresholds are uncertain, which is itself part of the risk.

## Environmental risks spread by global interactions

Unit 4's flows can carry environmental risks as well as goods and people.

- **Invasive species.** Ships carry ballast water across oceans. Zebra mussels, native to the region around the Black and Caspian Seas, were discovered in North America's Great Lakes in 1988, probably introduced in ballast water. They clog water-intake pipes and alter ecosystems, at great cost to water users.
- **Food-system shocks.** In 2010, drought and a severe heatwave cut Russia's grain harvest, and the government banned grain exports. World wheat prices rose, affecting import-dependent countries far from Russia. An environmental event became a risk elsewhere through trade.
- **Disease.** Air travel allows pathogens to spread between continents within days, so a local outbreak can become a global risk.

In each case, the source of the risk is distant from many of the people affected, which links environmental risk to the geopolitical and economic risks of 13.1.

## Transboundary pollution and how it was governed

Two European cases show how governance responds to pollution that crosses borders.

- **Acid rain.** From the 1960s, Scandinavian scientists linked the acidification of lakes and forests to sulphur emissions carried from industrial areas elsewhere in Europe, including the UK and Germany. The 1979 Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution became the first international treaty on air pollution, and later protocols set limits on sulphur and other pollutants. Emissions fell substantially in the following decades.
- **The Rhine, 1986.** A fire at a chemical warehouse at Schweizerhalle near Basel in Switzerland in November 1986 washed toxic chemicals into the Rhine, killing large numbers of fish downstream through Germany, France and the Netherlands. The disaster strengthened cooperation between the Rhine states on monitoring and pollution control.

Both cases show that transboundary risks can be managed when affected states share scientific evidence, accept monitoring and agree enforceable limits. They contrast with issues such as climate change and plastic pollution, where many more states, sources and interests make agreement harder.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine why environmental risks are increasingly global.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Global trade and travel now carry environmental risks between distant places: ballast water introduced zebra mussels to the Great Lakes, and air travel spreads disease between continents within days **[mechanism with examples]**. Interdependence in food systems means that an environmental shock in one exporting country can raise prices elsewhere, as Russia's 2010 export ban showed **[a second mechanism]**. Some risks, such as climate change and ocean acidification, are global by nature, affecting Earth systems that no single state controls **[different type of global risk]**. Environmental risks are therefore global both because of the processes involved and because global interactions transmit local events **[examined conclusion]**."

## Common mistakes

- Reading a return period as a fixed interval between events.
- Treating environmental risks as purely physical, without the human factors that create exposure.
- Assuming harm always increases gradually, ignoring thresholds and tipping points.
- Describing transboundary pollution without explaining how governance responded.
- Forgetting that the source of a risk and the people affected may be far apart.

## Exam technique

- **Examine** questions reward explaining the mechanism that spreads or creates a risk.
- **Discuss** governance by comparing a case that succeeded with one that has been harder to manage.
- **Evaluate** a framework such as planetary boundaries by stating what it captures and what it leaves uncertain.
- **To what extent** answers should distinguish risks caused by global interactions from those that are global by nature.
- When data on probability are given, show the method and interpret the result for people.
