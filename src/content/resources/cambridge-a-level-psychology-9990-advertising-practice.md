**This practice set covers topic 2.5, Advertising, in Specialist Option 2 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All companies, products and people are fictional.

Related: [Advertising study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-advertising/) · [Advertising revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-advertising-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **three** features of the Yale model of communication. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how Lauterborn's 4 Cs differ from the traditional 4 Ps of marketing. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between the mere exposure and reminder explanations of product placement. **[4]**

**4.** Outline **two** functions of a slogan. **[2]**

**5.** Advertising aimed at young people raises concerns.

**(a)** Describe research into brand recognition in children and product placement. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate research into advertising–consumer interaction, including a discussion of the use of children in research. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** TrailPeak is launching a hiking boot. It plans one advert showing a famous climber at a stylish mountain lodge, and one listing the boot's grip, weight and waterproofing.

**(a)** Using Snyder and DeBono (1985), suggest which advert would suit high self-monitors and which would suit low self-monitors. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** weakness of measuring "willingness to pay" as the dependent variable. **[2]**

**7.** A researcher compares attention to the same advert in a magazine and on a smartphone, using eye-tracking.

**(a)** Suggest **one** quantitative measure she could take from the eye-tracker. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** strength of eye-tracking compared with asking participants what they noticed. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why her findings may not show whether the advert will increase sales. **[2]**

**8.** A juice company wants to create a new slogan.

**(a)** Using guidelines for effective slogans, suggest **two** pieces of advice for the company. **[2]**

**(b)** Distinguish between brand awareness and brand image, using the juice company as an example. **[2]**

**9.** A psychologist plans to replicate Auty and Lewis (2004) with a different film and a breakfast cereal.

**(a)** Identify the independent variable. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe how she could measure the dependent variable without relying on self-report. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **two** ethical guidelines she must follow because the participants are children. **[4]**

**10.** Fischer et al. (1991) used a logo-matching task. Explain **one** reason why this task may not be a valid measure of children's understanding of advertising. **[2]**

**11.** Using the Yale model, explain **two** ways a health charity could make its online advert more persuasive. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for any three, briefly outlined: source, who gives the message, e.g. its credibility (1); message, what is said and how (1); medium, the channel such as television or smartphone (1); audience, who receives it (1); effect, the change in attitude or behaviour (1).

**2.** Up to 4 marks. The 4 Ps are written from the seller's view; the 4 Cs are written from the consumer's view (1). Product becomes consumer wants and needs, so firms start with what customers want (1). Price becomes cost, the total cost including time and effort (1). Place becomes convenience and promotion becomes communication, a two-way exchange (1).

**3.** Up to 4 marks. Mere exposure: repeated exposure to a brand increases liking for it (1), even when the viewer does not consciously notice it, so prior knowledge is not needed (1). Reminder: seeing the brand in the film brings to mind a brand the viewer already knows (1), so the effect should be strongest for people who already have experience of the brand or film, as Auty and Lewis found for children who had seen the film before (1).

**4.** One mark each for any two: build brand awareness (1); create or reinforce a brand image (1); differentiate the brand from competitors (1); give continuity across campaigns (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Fischer et al. (1991) asked children aged 3 to 6 to match logos to pictures of products (1). Recognition increased with age (1), and many young children matched a cigarette brand's cartoon character to cigarettes (1). Auty and Lewis (2004) showed children a film clip with a Pepsi placement or a control clip (1); afterwards children who saw the placement were more likely to choose Pepsi (1), especially if they had seen the film before (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named issue, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Use of children*: research on children shows effects that matter for policy, but children cannot consent for themselves, so parental consent and child-friendly, non-distressing tasks are required; researchers must avoid encouraging unhealthy preferences.
- *Validity*: choosing a drink after a clip is a realistic behaviour; self-reports in Snyder and DeBono may not reflect real buying.
- *Generalisation*: student samples in Snyder and DeBono; specific films and brands in Auty and Lewis.
- *Determinism versus free will*: effects occur without awareness, yet many children did not choose the placed brand.
- *Application*: supports regulation of advertising to children and targeting of adverts to personality.
- *Conclusion*: consistent evidence of advertising effects, with ethical limits on how it can be studied.

**6.**

**(a)** One mark each. High self-monitors: the famous climber at the stylish lodge, an image appeal (1). Low self-monitors: the advert listing grip, weight and waterproofing, a quality claim (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It is hypothetical; saying what you would pay is not the same as spending money (1), so it may lack validity as a measure of real buying behaviour (1).

**7.**

**(a)** One mark for any quantitative measure, e.g. total time spent looking at the brand name (1); number of fixations on the product image (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It gives objective data on where participants actually looked (1), whereas participants may not remember, or may report what they think the researcher expects (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Looking at an advert shows attention, not liking or intention to buy (1); sales depend on other factors such as price and need, so attention may not lead to purchase (1).

**8.**

**(a)** One mark each for any two: link the slogan clearly to the brand name (1); use it to position the brand, e.g. as natural (1); keep it consistent and repeat it over time (1); treat it as a long-term investment (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Brand awareness is how readily consumers recognise or recall the juice brand, e.g. picking it out on the shelf (1). Brand image is what they associate with it, e.g. fresh, healthy and fun (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Whether children watch the clip with the cereal placement or the control clip without it (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Offer each child a choice of cereals afterwards, e.g. as a reward (1), and record which one they actually choose, an objective behavioural measure (1).

**(c)** Two marks per guideline. Consent: children cannot give fully informed consent, so parents must consent (1) and the child should still agree in an age-appropriate way (1). Protection from harm or right to withdraw: the procedure must not cause distress or encourage unhealthy eating (1), and the child must be able to stop at any time, with a debrief suitable for their age (1).

**10.** Up to 2 marks. Matching a logo to a product shows recognition of an association (1), but not whether the child understands that adverts are designed to persuade, so it may not measure understanding of advertising (1). Credit the possibility of guessing on a matching task, if explained.

**11.** Two marks for each way, linked to a Yale feature. Source: use a credible expert such as a doctor (1), because credible sources are more persuasive (1). Audience: tailor the message to the target age group (1), e.g. using the platforms and language young adults use, so the message is more likely to be attended to (1). Credit message (e.g. a clear, repeated message) or medium points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 2, listing the 4 Cs without matching them to the 4 Ps.
- In question 5(a), describing Snyder and DeBono, which is not about children.
- In question 9(c), naming "ethics" without saying what the researcher would do.
