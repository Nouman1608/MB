The values are rounded and meant for patterns. In an exam the data will be given, so practise reading trends and exceptions from them.

## Atomic radius

**Trend:** atomic radius decreases from sodium to chlorine.

**Explanation:**

- Across the period the number of protons increases by one each time, so nuclear charge increases.
- The extra electrons are added to the **same shell** (n = 3), so the shielding by inner electrons stays about the same.
- The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons increases, pulling the outer electrons closer to the nucleus.

The word "shielding" gets used loosely. In an answer, state that the outer electrons are in the same shell and the shielding is about the same (or "similar"), while the nuclear charge rises. Argon's radius is not usually compared with the others because it does not form bonds, so its value depends on how it is measured.

## First ionisation energy

The first ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.

**General trend:** it increases across the period from sodium to argon, because nuclear charge increases, atomic radius decreases and the outer electron is more strongly attracted. Argon is highest because its outer electrons are held most tightly in a full shell.

**Two exceptions** break the trend. Learn both and explain each in terms of the sub-level and the electron arrangement.

### Magnesium to aluminium (738 to 578 kJ mol⁻¹)

```
Mg  1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²
Al  1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹
```

- The outer electron of aluminium is in a **3p** orbital, and the outer electrons of magnesium are in the **3s** sub-level.
- The 3p sub-level is at a higher energy than 3s and, on average, further from the nucleus.
- The 3p electron is also shielded slightly by the 3s electrons.
- So it takes less energy to remove the 3p electron, even though aluminium has a larger nuclear charge.

### Phosphorus to sulfur (1012 to 1000 kJ mol⁻¹)

```
P  [Ne] 3s² 3p³   (three 3p electrons in separate orbitals)
S  [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴   (two electrons paired in one 3p orbital)
```

- In sulfur, two of the 3p electrons share the same orbital.
- The repulsion between paired electrons in the same orbital makes the electron easier to remove.
- So the first ionisation energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus.

**Model answer structure for any exception:** (1) state the electron configurations or the sub-level involved; (2) state what is different (higher energy sub-level, or paired electrons); (3) conclude that less energy is needed to remove the electron.

## Melting point

Melting point depends on the structure and the strength of the forces that must be overcome.

### Sodium, magnesium and aluminium: giant metallic lattices

A metal consists of positive ions in a lattice, surrounded by delocalised electrons. Melting requires enough energy to loosen the attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons.

| Metal | Ion | Delocalised electrons per atom | Ion size | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na | Na⁺ | 1 | Largest | 98 |
| Mg | Mg²⁺ | 2 | Smaller | 650 |
| Al | Al³⁺ | 3 | Smallest | 660 |

The melting point rises because the metal ions have a higher charge, more delocalised electrons, and a smaller size. The ions therefore attract the sea of delocalised electrons more strongly. Strong forces need more energy to overcome, so the melting point is higher.

### Silicon: giant covalent

Silicon has a macromolecular structure. Each silicon atom is bonded to four others by strong covalent bonds in a tetrahedral arrangement. Melting requires breaking many strong covalent bonds, so the melting point is very high (1410 °C). It is the highest in the period.

### Phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine: simple molecular

These are small molecules (P₄, S₈ and Cl₂). Melting overcomes only the weak London (induced dipole-induced dipole) forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds inside them. The melting points are low.

| Element | Molecule | Relative molecular mass | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | P₄ | 124 | 44 |
| S | S₈ | 256 | 115 |
| Cl | Cl₂ | 71 | –101 |

Sulfur melts at a higher temperature than phosphorus because S₈ has more electrons and is bigger than P₄, so the London forces are stronger. Chlorine, with the smallest molecule, has the weakest forces and the lowest melting point of the three.

### Argon: monatomic

Argon exists as separate atoms with very weak London forces between them. It has the lowest melting point in the period (–189 °C), because each atom has only 18 electrons and there is no bonding between atoms.

### The shape of the melting-point graph

The melting point rises from sodium to a maximum at silicon, then falls sharply to phosphorus, because the structure changes from giant to simple molecular. It is a clear case of "structure and bonding decide the property".

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: explain the trend in radius

Explain why the atomic radius of phosphorus is smaller than that of aluminium.

Phosphorus has more protons (15) than aluminium (13), so a larger nuclear charge. Both have electrons in the third shell and the shielding is similar. The outer electrons of phosphorus are attracted more strongly and pulled closer to the nucleus.

### Worked example 2: compare melting points

Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.

Magnesium ions have a 2+ charge and each atom contributes two delocalised electrons, whereas sodium ions have a 1+ charge and each contributes one. Magnesium ions are also smaller. The attraction between ions and delocalised electrons is stronger in magnesium, so more energy is needed to melt it.

### Worked example 3: compare two molecular elements

Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than chlorine.

Sulfur exists as S₈ molecules and chlorine as Cl₂ molecules. S₈ has more electrons, so stronger London forces act between molecules. More energy is needed to overcome them, so sulfur melts at a higher temperature. The covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "the covalent bonds break" when a simple molecular element melts.
- Writing "intermolecular bonds" for forces between molecules. Use "intermolecular forces" or "London forces".
- Comparing the bond strength of Na–Na with that of Mg–Mg. Use the strength of attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons.
- Not mentioning that the electrons are in the same shell when explaining atomic radius.
- Explaining the Mg to Al drop by "shielding by inner electrons" only. The key point is the 3p sub-level is higher in energy than 3s.
- Explaining the P to S drop by "half-filled sub-level stability" without stating electron pair repulsion.
- Giving silicon a molecular structure with London forces.
- Saying sodium atoms have fewer protons so there is less attraction without mentioning the radius or charge.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe the trend**: state the direction, and mention any exceptions using data.
- **Explain**: give a reason in terms of nuclear charge, shielding, sub-level or structure.
- **Explain why X has a higher melting point than Y**: name both structures, then the forces that must be overcome and compare their strengths.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the melting point of aluminium only slightly higher than magnesium?**
Aluminium ions have a 3+ charge and three delocalised electrons per atom, so the attraction is stronger, but the lattice arrangement also affects the melting point, so the rise is small.

**Why is argon's first ionisation energy the highest?**
It has the largest nuclear charge of the third-shell elements and a full outer shell, with a small radius, so the outer electron is held most tightly.

**Are all of Na to Al in the same type of structure?**
Yes. They are all giant metallic lattices, and the differences come from ionic charge, number of delocalised electrons and ion size.
