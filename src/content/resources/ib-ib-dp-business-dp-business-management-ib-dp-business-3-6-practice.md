These questions cover section 3.6 (Efficiency ratio analysis) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The whole section is HL only. Learn the method from the [3.6 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-3-6/) and the [3.6 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-3-6-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Give days and percentages to one decimal place.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between insolvency and bankruptcy. **[2]**

**2.** State the formula for the gearing ratio. **[1]**

**3.** Fernlea Florists has opening stock of 30,000, closing stock of 50,000 and cost of sales of 360,000.

**(a)** Calculate stock turnover in number of times. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate stock turnover in number of days. **[1]**

**4.** Calculate debtor days for a business with debtors of 45,000 and total sales revenue of 547,500. **[1]**

**5.** A company has non-current liabilities of 350,000 and equity of 150,000. Calculate its gearing ratio and explain one risk it faces. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why increasing creditor days may not always benefit a business. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

Questions 7 to 11 use the data below for Thornbury Electricals, a wholesaler of electrical goods to builders.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 2,000,000 | 2,100,000 |
| Cost of sales | 1,400,000 | 1,500,000 |
| Opening stock | 160,000 | 200,000 |
| Closing stock | 200,000 | 300,000 |
| Debtors | 250,000 | 345,000 |
| Creditors | 210,000 | 160,000 |
| Non-current liabilities | 300,000 | 700,000 |
| Equity | 900,000 | 920,000 |

In Year 2 Thornbury offered longer credit to win builder customers from a rival. Its main supplier shortened Thornbury's credit terms after two late payments. Thornbury took a 400,000 long-term loan to fund a new warehouse.

**7.** Calculate for both years:

**(a)** stock turnover in number of days. **[2]**

**(b)** debtor days. **[2]**

**(c)** creditor days. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the gearing ratio for both years. **[2]**

**9.** Analyse the changes in Thornbury's efficiency ratios between Year 1 and Year 2. **[6]**

**10.** Thornbury wants to bring debtor days back to 40 days at Year 2 sales revenue.

**(a)** Calculate the level of debtors this implies, to the nearest dollar. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the cash this would release compared with Year 2 debtors. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate whether Thornbury is at risk of insolvency. **[8]**

**12.** Kestrel Brewing has a gearing ratio of 62% and stable, rising profits. Discuss whether it should reduce its gearing by issuing new shares. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Insolvency is a financial position in which a business or person cannot pay debts as they fall due [1]; bankruptcy is a legal status or procedure that can follow insolvency, in which a court or official process deals with the debts [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (Non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed) × 100 [1] **[1]**

**3. (a)** Average stock = (30,000 + 50,000) ÷ 2 = 40,000 [1]; 360,000 ÷ 40,000 = **9 times** [1]

**(b)** 40,000 ÷ 360,000 × 365 = **40.6 days** [1] **[3]**

**4.** 45,000 ÷ 547,500 × 365 = **30.0 days** [1] **[1]**

**5.** Capital employed = 500,000; 350,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **70.0%** [1]. Risk: interest and repayments must be made whatever profit is [1], so a fall in sales could leave the company unable to meet them [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Paying suppliers later keeps cash in the business, but suppliers may react by withdrawing credit, charging higher prices or removing early payment discounts [1]; they may also give the business lower priority for deliveries, harming its operations [1]. **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Year 1: average stock 180,000; 180,000 ÷ 1,400,000 × 365 = **46.9 days** [1]. Year 2: average stock 250,000; 250,000 ÷ 1,500,000 × 365 = **60.8 days** [1]

**(b)** Year 1: 250,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 365 = **45.6 days** [1]. Year 2: 345,000 ÷ 2,100,000 × 365 = **60.0 days** [1]

**(c)** Year 1: 210,000 ÷ 1,400,000 × 365 = **54.8 days** [1]. Year 2: 160,000 ÷ 1,500,000 × 365 = **38.9 days** [1] **[6]**

**8.** Year 1: 300,000 ÷ 1,200,000 × 100 = **25.0%** [1]. Year 2: 700,000 ÷ 1,620,000 × 100 = **43.2%** [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Stock days rose by about 14 days, so stock is held longer and more cash is tied up, possibly because the new warehouse allowed more stock to be held [1]. Debtor days rose from 45.6 to 60.0 because Thornbury offered longer credit to win customers [1], so cash from sales arrives about two weeks later [1]. Creditor days fell from 54.8 to 38.9 because the supplier shortened terms after late payments [1], so cash leaves sooner [1]. Gearing rose from 25.0% to 43.2% because of the 400,000 loan, adding fixed interest payments at the same time as cash is being squeezed [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** 40 ÷ 365 × 2,100,000 = **230,137** [1]

**(b)** 345,000 − 230,137 = **114,863** [1] **[2]**

**11.** Signs of risk: all three day-counts worsened, so cash comes in more slowly and leaves more quickly [1]. Stock days plus debtor days minus creditor days rose from about 38 days (46.9 + 45.6 − 54.8) to about 82 days (60.8 + 60.0 − 38.9) [1], so Thornbury must fund more than twice as many days of trading itself [1]. The supplier has already shortened terms after late payments, which suggests cash pressure is real [1]. Gearing of 43.2% adds fixed interest at the same time [1]. Mitigating factors: sales are still rising, and the warehouse is an asset that may support future growth [1]; reducing debtor days to 40 would release about 114,863 of cash [1]. Judgement: Thornbury is not necessarily insolvent, but the risk is rising, and it depends on whether it can tighten credit control without losing the builder customers it has just won [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For issuing shares: lower gearing reduces fixed interest commitments and the risk if profits fall [1]; it may make future borrowing easier [1]. Against: profits are stable and rising, so Kestrel can probably meet its interest payments [1]; new shares dilute existing owners' control and share of future profits [1]; if borrowed funds earn more than the interest rate, high gearing increases returns to owners [1]. Judgement: with stable, rising profits, reducing gearing is not urgent; Kestrel should consider it only if interest rates rise or profits become less predictable [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

* Using sales revenue instead of cost of sales for stock or creditor days.
* Forgetting to average opening and closing stock.
* Calculating gearing on equity alone instead of capital employed.
* Describing ratio changes without linking them to case events.
* Treating rising risk of insolvency as proof of insolvency.
