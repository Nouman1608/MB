## 1. The model of comparative advantage

A country holds an **absolute advantage** in a good when a given set of resources yields more of it than the same resources would abroad. It holds a **comparative advantage** when it sacrifices less of its other output to make one more unit. That sacrifice is the **opportunity cost**.

**Worked example (invented).** In a week, 100 workers in Country A or Country B can make either pumps or baskets.

| | Pumps (all workers) | Baskets (all workers) | Opportunity cost of 1 pump | Opportunity cost of 1 basket |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Country A | 60 | 120 | 120 ÷ 60 = 2 baskets | 60 ÷ 120 = 0.5 pump |
| Country B | 20 | 100 | 100 ÷ 20 = 5 baskets | 20 ÷ 100 = 0.2 pump |

Country A has an absolute advantage in both goods (60 > 20 and 120 > 100). That does not mean it should make both. A gives up 2 baskets per pump, B gives up 5, so **A has the comparative advantage in pumps**. B gives up 0.2 pump per basket, A gives up 0.5, so **B has the comparative advantage in baskets**. It is impossible for one country to have the lower opportunity cost in both goods.

**Without trade**, suppose A makes 48 pumps (80% of its workers) and 24 baskets (20%), and B makes 6 pumps (30%) and 70 baskets (70%). World output is 54 pumps and 94 baskets.

**With specialisation**, A makes 60 pumps and B makes 100 baskets, so world output is 60 pumps and 100 baskets. That is 6 more of each, which is the sense in which specialisation and trade can raise total output.

**Sharing the gain.** Suppose they trade at 1 pump for 3 baskets. If A exports 9 pumps for 27 baskets, A consumes 51 pumps and 27 baskets (3 more of each than before). B consumes 9 pumps and 100 − 27 = 73 baskets (also 3 more of each).

**Terms of trade matter in the model.** Both countries gain only if the price of a pump lies between the two opportunity costs, here between 2 and 5 baskets. At exactly 2, A gains nothing and B takes all the gain. At exactly 5, the reverse. A price near a country's own opportunity cost leaves it with little of the gain.

**Assumptions to criticise:** constant opportunity costs, no transport costs or tariffs, full employment, mobile resources within each country, and two countries and two goods. Real opportunity costs often rise as a country specialises, which limits how far specialisation is worthwhile.

## 2. Causes of comparative advantage and why it changes

Causes include:

- **Factor endowments**: land, minerals, climate, size and skills of the labour force, stock of capital.
- **Productivity and technology**: better methods lower the cost of a good.
- **Relative costs**: wage levels, energy costs, taxes, and the exchange rate.
- **Economies of scale and learning**: firms that start early may keep a cost lead.

It changes over time through investment in education and capital, new technology, discovery or exhaustion of resources, rising wages in a country that has grown rich, and changes in the exchange rate. A country that once made basic goods cheaply can lose that advantage as wages rise, and can gain a new one in higher-value goods. This is why a comparative advantage is not fixed, and why a policy that protects an industry today may block the shift to a better one.

## 3. Other benefits and costs of trade

**Benefits beyond the model.**

- **Economies of scale**: a larger market lets firms produce more, so long-run average cost falls and prices can fall.
- **Competition**: foreign rivals push domestic firms to cut costs and innovate, and consumers get more choice.
- **Access** to goods, materials and technology a country cannot make at home.

**Costs.**

- **Structural unemployment** where an industry shrinks, because workers cannot move quickly to new jobs.
- **Over-specialisation** leaves a country exposed to a fall in the price of its main export.
- **Dependence on imports** of essentials, which raises security risks.
- **Environmental costs** of transport and of production in places with weak rules.
- **Unequal gains**: owners of the expanding industries gain more than workers in the shrinking ones.

A good answer weighs these. For a large, diverse economy with flexible labour markets the costs of adjustment are lower than for a small economy that depends on one product.

## 4. Terms of trade

The **terms of trade** compare what a country earns per unit of its exports with what it pays per unit of its imports.

Terms of trade = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100.

**Worked example (Country A, invented).** Year 1 is the base year.

| Period | Exports (price index) | Imports (price index) | Terms of trade (index) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| 2 | 106 | 101 | 106 ÷ 101 × 100 = 104.95 |
| 3 | 101 | 110 | 101 ÷ 110 × 100 = 91.82 |
| 4 | 96 | 120 | 96 ÷ 120 × 100 = 80.0 |

A figure above 100 is an **improvement**, below 100 a **deterioration**. By year 4 the terms of trade have fallen by 20% on the base year. To buy the same volume of imports the country must now export 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25 times as much, 25% more.

**Causes of a change.** A change in the price of key exports or imports (for example a raw material), a change in the exchange rate, which moves the prices of exports and imports relative to each other, different rates of inflation, shifts in world demand or supply, and trade barriers.

**Effects.** An improvement lets a country buy more imports for each export, which raises real income, and it may hurt exporters if the higher prices cut sales. A deterioration reduces the living standard that a given level of exports can support. What happens to the current account depends on how responsive the volumes of exports and imports are to price (their price elasticities): a fall in export prices raises export earnings only if demand for exports is elastic. Do not assume that an improvement is always good or a deterioration always bad.

## 5. Protectionism

**Protectionism** is the use of policies to shield domestic producers from foreign competition. The specification names three.

- **Tariff**: a tax on imports.
- **Quota**: a cap on the physical amount of a good allowed in.
- **Export subsidy**: a payment to domestic firms for each unit exported.

**Tariff and quota (invented market).** Country A is a small importer of cloth and takes the world price of 5 as given. Domestic demand is Qd = 120 − 4P and domestic supply is Qs = 20 + 4P.

| | Free trade | 2-unit tariff |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 5 | 7 |
| Quantity demanded | 100 | 92 |
| Domestic supply | 40 | 48 |
| Imports | 60 | 44 |

Diagram: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal, with the demand curve, the domestic supply curve and a horizontal line at the world price. The tariff moves that line up from 5 to 7, so demand contracts and domestic supply extends along their curves (no curve shifts). The effects are:

- Consumer surplus falls by (100 + 92) ÷ 2 × 2 = 192.
- Producer surplus rises by (40 + 48) ÷ 2 × 2 = 88.
- Government revenue is 2 × 44 = 88.
- Net welfare loss is 192 − 88 − 88 = 16, the two triangles: ½ × 2 × 8 for inefficient extra domestic production and ½ × 2 × 8 for lost consumption.

A **quota of 44 units** gives the same price rise, because price must reach 7 for domestic demand minus domestic supply to equal 44. The area 2 × 44 = 88 is not tax revenue. It goes to whoever holds the import licences, unless the government sells them.

**Export subsidy (invented market).** Country A exports tea at a world price of 10. Qd = 90 − 4P and Qs = 20 + 5P, so exports are 70 − 50 = 20. A subsidy of 2 per unit exported lifts the home price to 12, so Qd = 42 and Qs = 80, and exports rise to 38. Producers gain (70 + 80) ÷ 2 × 2 = 150, consumers lose (50 + 42) ÷ 2 × 2 = 92, and the subsidy costs 2 × 38 = 76. The net loss is 150 − 92 − 76 = −18.

**Reasons for protection.** Protecting an **infant industry** until it gains scale; shielding a declining industry to ease job losses; national security and food security; responding to **dumping** (selling below cost abroad); raising revenue; diversifying a narrow economy; and, in a weak balance of payments position, limiting imports.

**Consequences.** Higher prices and less choice for consumers; less pressure on protected firms to be efficient; higher costs for firms that use imported inputs; a fall in exports if other countries retaliate; possible disputes with trade partners. Evaluate by asking whether the protection is temporary, whether the country is large enough to affect world prices, and how well the targeted industry can in fact become competitive.

## 6. The changing pattern of world trade

In broad terms: the share of trade in manufactured goods and services has grown, emerging economies account for a rising share of world exports, trade within industries and within supply chains has grown, and falling transport and communication costs and lower tariffs have supported all of these. Country comparative advantages have shifted as capital, skills and technology have spread. Link each trend to a cause from section 2.

## 7. Trading blocs and the WTO

| Bloc | Members | Common external tariff | Free movement of labour and capital | Single currency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free trade area | Remove barriers to trade between themselves | No: each sets its own | No | No |
| Customs union | As above | Yes | No | No |
| Common market | As above | Yes | Yes | No |
| Monetary union | As above | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Each stage is deeper. Gains include a bigger home market, economies of scale and more competition. Costs include trade being steered towards members even where a non-member is cheaper, loss of independent policy-making, and in a monetary union the loss of the country's own exchange rate and interest rate.

The **World Trade Organisation (WTO)** is the body that sets rules for trade between its members. Its roles include providing a forum for negotiating lower trade barriers, administering agreements, monitoring members' trade policies and settling disputes between members. Its principle of non-discrimination means members should treat other members equally. Evaluate its power: it relies on members accepting its rulings, negotiations are slow, and developing countries may have less influence.

## Common errors

- Claiming that a country which is better at making everything has nothing to gain from trade.
- Reading comparative advantage from total output instead of from opportunity cost.
- Inverting the terms of trade fraction.
- Treating a quota's gain as government revenue.
- Writing "terms of trade improved so the current account improved" without elasticities.

## Frequently asked questions

**What causes comparative advantage?**
Comparative advantage comes from factor endowments such as land, labour and capital, productivity and technology, relative costs including wages and the exchange rate, and economies of scale and learning.

**Why does comparative advantage change over time?**
Investment in education and capital, new technology, discovery or exhaustion of resources, rising wages and exchange rate changes all shift relative costs. A policy that protects an industry today may block the shift to a better one.

**What are the costs of international trade?**
Costs include structural unemployment where industries shrink, over-specialisation that exposes a country to falls in its main export price, dependence on imported essentials, environmental costs and unequal gains between groups.

**What does the World Trade Organisation do?**
The WTO provides a forum for negotiating lower trade barriers, administers agreements, monitors members' trade policies and settles disputes. Its power is limited because it relies on members accepting its rulings and negotiations are slow.

**What are the limitations of the comparative advantage model?**
It assumes constant opportunity costs, no transport costs or tariffs, full employment, mobile resources within each country, and two countries and two goods. In reality opportunity costs often rise as a country specialises.
