## Section A

**1.** State the three components of urban population growth. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why natural increase is often the largest component of growth in fast-growing cities. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between backwash effects and spread effects. **[2]**

**4.** Describe two characteristics of the peri-urban fringe of a city in a lower-income country. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by right-sizing a shrinking city. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** An invented city grew from 7.5 million to 8.4 million in ten years. It recorded 1,100,000 births and 350,000 deaths. Boundary extension added 30,000 people.

**(a)** Calculate the natural increase. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the net migration. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the share of total growth due to net migration. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest one implication of your results for city planners. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows population change in an invented metropolitan region.

| Decade | Core city change (%) | Suburbs change (%) | Small towns beyond the suburbs change (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960s | +12 | +20 | +2 |
| 1980s | -8 | +6 | +10 |
| 2010s | +7 | +2 | +1 |

**(a)** Identify the stage of urban development shown in the 1980s, giving evidence. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons for the change in the core city between the 1980s and the 2010s. **[2]**

**8.** A regeneration scheme creates 12,000 jobs, of which 3,000 go to residents of the regenerated district. Before the scheme, 4,500 of the district's 25,000 working-age residents were unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate the district's unemployment rate before the scheme. **[1]**

**(b)** Assuming all 3,000 local hires were previously unemployed, calculate the new rate. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons why only a quarter of the jobs went to local residents. **[2]**

**9.** Using cumulative causation, explain how deindustrialisation can lead to long-term urban decline. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the success of regeneration strategies in one or more named cities. **[10]**

**11.** Examine the view that the processes changing cities in high-income countries are fundamentally different from those changing cities in lower-income countries. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Natural increase [1]; net migration [1]; reclassification of settlements or extension of city boundaries [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Many earlier migrants were young adults [1], who now have children in the city, so births greatly exceed deaths in a youthful population [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Backwash effects are the flows of people, capital and skills from weaker regions towards a growing core, which harm the periphery [1]; spread effects are benefits of core growth, such as demand for goods and investment, that eventually reach surrounding areas [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, for example: informal or unplanned housing on former farmland [1]; lack of piped water, sewers or paved roads [1]; factories and warehouses attracted by cheap land [1]; continuing farming on remaining plots [1]; unclear land ownership and weak planning [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**5.** Adapting a city's housing, services and infrastructure to a smaller population [1], for example by demolishing vacant housing and converting land to parks or urban farms [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 1,100,000 - 350,000 = **750,000** [1].

**(b)** Total growth = 8.4 - 7.5 = 0.9 million = 900,000 [1]. Net migration = 900,000 - 750,000 - 30,000 = **120,000** [1].

**(c)** 120,000 / 900,000 x 100 = **13.3%** [1].

**(d)** For example: since natural increase provides most growth, restricting migration would not stop the city growing [1], so planners must provide schools, health care and housing for a growing number of children born in the city [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Counter-urbanisation [1]: the core city lost 8% while small towns beyond the suburbs grew fastest, by 10% [1].

**(b)** Any two, for example: regeneration of the city centre attracting new residents [1]; growth of young professionals choosing city-centre living [1]; new jobs in services and knowledge industries in the centre [1]; conversion of old buildings to apartments [1]. Maximum [2]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** 4,500 / 25,000 x 100 = **18%** [1].

**(b)** (4,500 - 3,000) / 25,000 x 100 = 1,500 / 25,000 x 100 = **6%** [1].

**(c)** Any two, for example: many jobs needed skills or qualifications that local residents lacked [1]; firms recruited through existing networks outside the district [1]; new residents attracted to the area took jobs [1]; some jobs moved from elsewhere with their existing staff [1]. Maximum [2]. **[4]**

**9.** Factory closures remove jobs and reduce spending in local shops and services, causing further job losses through a negative multiplier [1]. Skilled and younger residents leave for work elsewhere, reducing the city's population and tax base [1]. Lower revenue means poorer services, maintenance and schools, and more vacant property [1]. The city becomes less attractive to new investors and residents, so decline continues unless intervention breaks the spiral [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Criteria:** jobs for existing residents, housing affordability, environmental quality, long-term economic change [1].
- **London Docklands:** large private investment, Canary Wharf and new transport such as the Docklands Light Railway [1]; but many jobs did not suit existing residents and housing costs rose [1].
- **Bilbao:** the Guggenheim Museum, opened in 1997, rebranded the city and attracted visitors [1]; success also relied on a new metro and riverside renewal [1].
- **Detroit:** downtown revival since bankruptcy in 2013, while outer neighbourhoods remain largely vacant [1].
- **Community-led schemes:** keep residents in place but are small in scale [1].
- **Gentrification:** a frequent outcome that can displace the people regeneration aimed to help [1].
- **Comparison:** schemes combining transport, housing and local employment do better than single flagship projects [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion about where and for whom regeneration has succeeded [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Similar processes:** suburbanisation and sprawl occur in both, driven by demand for space and new roads [1]; globalisation links cities everywhere through investment and trade [1].
- **Differences in growth:** many lower-income cities grow fast from migration and natural increase, while some high-income cities shrink [1] [1].
- **Differences in form:** peri-urban informal housing in lower-income countries, compared with planned suburbs in many high-income countries [1].
- **Deindustrialisation and regeneration:** mainly in older industrial cities of high-income countries, such as Detroit [1]; though some middle-income cities are now deindustrialising too [1].
- **Counter-urbanisation and re-urbanisation:** more common in high-income countries [1].
- **Stage, not type:** the stages model suggests the difference is one of stage of development rather than fundamental kind [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, disagree partly: the processes are similar, but their speed, scale and the governance capacity to manage them differ greatly [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* strong answers to Question 11 compare the same process in two named cities rather than describing each city separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to subtract reclassification when calculating net migration.
- Naming a stage of urban development without quoting data as evidence.
- Treating regeneration as automatically beneficial for existing residents.
- Writing about cities in general without naming any.
