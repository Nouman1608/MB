## The idea behind the policy

Monetary policy means the central bank steering the economy through the money supply and interest rates. It is a demand-side policy: it works by changing aggregate demand (AD), made up of consumption, investment, government spending and net exports. It is usually carried out by the central bank.

### The goals

- **Low and stable inflation.** Many central banks follow **inflation targeting**: the government or the bank announces a numerical target, for instance 2%, and interest rates are moved to steer inflation towards it. A published target helps firms and workers form expectations.
- **Low unemployment.** Higher AD raises demand for labour, so cyclical unemployment falls.
- **Reducing business cycle fluctuations.** Rates are cut in a downturn and raised in a boom, aiming for a smoother path of output.
- **A stable economic environment for long-term growth.** Predictable prices and borrowing costs support investment.
- **External balance.** Interest rates affect the exchange rate and the demand for exports and imports, so the current account is part of the picture.

Goals can conflict. Cutting rates to cut unemployment may push inflation above its target. State which goal is being traded for which.

## Real and nominal interest rates

The **nominal interest rate** is the rate quoted, with no adjustment for inflation. The **real interest rate** takes inflation into account:

real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate

**Worked example.** A Tavorian savings account pays 6.0% and inflation is 2.5%.

```
real rate = 6.0% - 2.5% = 3.5%
```

Savers gain 3.5% purchasing power a year. If the account paid 2.0% and inflation was 3.5%, the real rate would be 2.0% - 3.5% = -1.5%: savings lose purchasing power. Borrowers gain when real rates are negative. The real rate matters more than the nominal rate for decisions to save, borrow and invest.

## Expansionary and contractionary policy

### Transmission

When the central bank cuts its policy rate, commercial banks' lending and deposit rates tend to fall.

1. Borrowing is cheaper, so households take more loans for cars and homes.
2. Existing borrowers pay less interest, which leaves more income to spend. A household with a 150,000 tav variable-rate loan whose rate falls from 4% to 3% pays 6,000 - 4,500 = 1,500 tavs a year less.
3. Saving is less rewarding, so consumption is more attractive.
4. Investment projects with a lower expected return become worthwhile. A project expected to return 4.5% is rejected with borrowing at 5.0% but accepted at 3.5%.
5. A lower rate can reduce the demand for the currency, so the exchange rate may fall and net exports rise.

Consumption (C), investment (I) and net exports (X - M) rise, so AD shifts right. A rate rise works in the reverse direction.

### Closing a recessionary (deflationary) gap

Take a recession in Tavoria: potential output is 800 bn tavs, actual real GDP is 740 bn tavs. The gap is 740 - 800 = -60 bn, or -7.5% of potential.

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Vertical axis | Price level |
| Horizontal axis | Real GDP (bn tavs) |
| Full-employment output | Vertical LRAS at 800 |
| Starting point E1 | AD1 crosses AS at real GDP 740 |
| After the policy E2 | AD2 crosses AS at real GDP 800 |

In words: the economy starts at E1 where AD1 meets AS, with real GDP of 740 bn, below full employment. Expansionary monetary policy (lower interest rates, a bigger money supply) shifts AD right from AD1 to AD2. The new equilibrium E2 is at 800 bn, so output rises, unemployment falls and the price level rises a little. Label the axes (price level, real GDP), both AD curves, AS or LRAS, the gap and full-employment output.

### Closing an inflationary gap

Now potential output is 1,000 bn tavs and actual output is 1,060 bn: a positive gap of 60 bn, 6% of potential. Demand is pulling prices up. **Contractionary** policy raises interest rates, which reduces C and I and may raise the exchange rate. AD shifts left from AD1 to AD2, real GDP falls back to 1,000 bn, and the price level stops rising (or falls relative to where it would have been). The cost is lower output growth and possibly higher unemployment in the short run.

If your course teaches both Keynesian and monetarist or new classical AS, draw the version your teacher uses, and say what changes: with a vertical long-run AS the effect of a demand shift on output is only temporary.

## HL only: how commercial banks create money

Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest. Each loan becomes a new deposit somewhere in the banking system, and the process repeats.

Suppose the **minimum reserve requirement** is 20% and a customer deposits 1,000 tavs.

| Round | New deposit | Reserve kept (20%) | Lent out |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 200 | 800 |
| 2 | 800 | 160 | 640 |
| 3 | 640 | 128 | 512 |
| and so on | ... | ... | ... |

The deposits shrink by a factor of 0.8 each round. The maximum total of deposits is the original deposit multiplied by 1/(reserve ratio):

```
money multiplier = 1 / 0.20 = 5
total deposits   = 1,000 x 5 = 5,000 tavs
new money created = 5,000 - 1,000 = 4,000 tavs
```

This is the upper limit. It is smaller if banks hold more than the minimum, or if people hold cash instead of redepositing it. The key idea: the money supply is made up largely of bank deposits, so what banks lend matters as much as what the central bank prints.

## HL only: tools of monetary policy

| Tool | What the central bank does | Expansionary use | Contractionary use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open market operations | Buys or sells government bonds in the market | Buy bonds: banks gain reserves, lending rises | Sell bonds: reserves fall |
| Minimum reserve requirement | Changes the required reserve ratio | Lower the ratio: multiplier rises | Raise the ratio: multiplier falls |
| Minimum lending rate (base, discount or refinancing rate) | Changes the rate it charges banks | Cut it: market rates tend to fall | Raise it: market rates tend to rise |
| Quantitative easing | Creates reserves electronically to buy large amounts of assets | Used when rates are already very low | Reversed by selling assets |

Cutting the reserve ratio from 10% to 8% raises the multiplier from 10 to 12.5. On a 400 tav initial deposit the maximum deposits rise from 4,000 to 5,000.

## HL only: the money market and the equilibrium interest rate

The **demand for money** slopes downward: a high interest rate is a high opportunity cost of holding cash, so people hold less. The **supply of money** is vertical, since the central bank sets it. Axes: interest rate (vertical), quantity of money (horizontal). Equilibrium is where they cross.

Suppose Tavoria's money demand is Md = 500 - 40r (r in %, Md in bn tavs) and supply is 300 bn.

```
500 - 40r = 300
r = 200/40 = 5%
```

If the central bank buys bonds or uses quantitative easing and the supply becomes 340 bn:

```
500 - 40r = 340
r = 160/40 = 4%
```

The supply curve shifts right, the equilibrium rate falls from 5% to 4%, and cheaper borrowing raises AD. A fall in money supply to 260 bn raises r to 6%. A rise in incomes shifts demand for money right and raises the equilibrium rate unless supply changes.

## Effectiveness of monetary policy

### Constraints

- **Limited scope when rates are close to zero.** Rates cannot usefully go much below zero, so the central bank runs out of room in a deep recession.
- **Low consumer and business confidence.** If households fear for their jobs and firms see no demand, cheaper credit may not be used: "pushing on a string".

### Strengths

- **Incremental, flexible and easily reversible.** Rates can move in small steps and be reversed quickly if conditions change.
- **Short time lags.** Compared with some other policies, effects begin soon after a change, though the full effect takes time.

### Growth, unemployment and inflation

| Objective | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | A credible target anchors expectations; higher rates cool demand | Cost-push inflation from supply shocks is not cured by weaker demand without a loss of output |
| Unemployment | Lower rates raise AD and demand for labour | Does not tackle structural unemployment |
| Growth | Stable prices and low borrowing costs encourage investment | Affects demand, not the productive capacity of the economy |

To evaluate, weigh these against each other, ask which gap is being closed, how deep the recession is, and how confident households are. Reach a judgement.

## Common errors

- Writing real rate = nominal + inflation. It is nominal **minus** inflation.
- Drawing the money supply as upward sloping in the money market diagram.
- Shifting AS instead of AD when interest rates change.
- Stating that monetary policy is "fast" without noting that the full effect takes time.
- Describing the HL tools in an SL answer, which is not required.
