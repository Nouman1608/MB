This guide goes deeper on **2.3 Management**, the final subtopic of AS Level topic 2 (Human resource management) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus content for **2.3.1 Management and managers** is:

- traditional manager functions: planning, organising, directing, controlling;
- the role of managers: Fayol, Mintzberg;
- the contribution of managers to business performance;
- management styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, paternalistic;
- McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y managers.

The [combined guide to motivation and management](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-motivation-and-management/) defines each of these. This page shows how to *apply* them to a case and how to *evaluate* them, which is where most marks are. Revise with the [Management revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-management-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and practise with the [Management practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-management-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented. Leadership as a separate idea is studied at A Level in topic 7.3, so this page keeps to the management content of 2.3.

## The four functions applied to one decision

Learning the four words is easy; showing them in a context is the skill. Suppose **Marlow Fold Pharmacies** opens a new branch.

| Function | What the manager does at the new branch |
|---|---|
| Planning | Sets the opening date, the sales target for year one and the staffing budget |
| Organising | Recruits a pharmacist and three assistants, sets the rota, orders stock and equipment |
| Directing | Briefs staff on procedures, supports new assistants, deals with problems on opening day |
| Controlling | Compares weekly sales and waiting times with targets; changes the rota if queues build up |

The functions are linked in a cycle. Control produces information that feeds the next round of planning. A manager who plans carefully but never checks results has no way to correct mistakes.

## Fayol and Mintzberg compared

Both describe what managers do, but from opposite directions.

| | Fayol | Mintzberg |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Set out what management *should* involve, from his experience running a large company | Observed what managers *actually* do through studying them at work |
| Picture of the job | Orderly and logical: forecast and plan, organise, command, coordinate, control | Fragmented and fast: many short tasks, constant interruptions, much talking |
| Strength | Gives a clear checklist of responsibilities; useful for training and for judging whether a manager is covering all areas | Realistic; shows that managers spend much time on people and information, not just plans |
| Limitation | Underplays the messy, reactive reality of the job | Describes roles but gives less guidance on how to do them well |

A useful evaluation point: the two are not contradictory. Fayol describes the *purpose* of management; Mintzberg describes the *daily activity* through which those purposes are carried out.

### Classifying a manager's day using Mintzberg

Mintzberg grouped ten roles into three sets: **interpersonal** (figurehead, leader, liaison), **informational** (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson) and **decisional** (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator). Exam questions sometimes describe a manager's activities and ask you to classify them.

A hotel general manager's morning:

1. Welcomes a group of visiting travel agents and poses for a photograph: **figurehead** (interpersonal).
2. Reads the occupancy report and a competitor's new price list: **monitor** (informational).
3. Emails department heads about a change to check-in procedures: **disseminator** (informational).
4. Sorts out a burst pipe that has closed two rooms: **disturbance handler** (decisional).
5. Agrees a group rate with a conference organiser: **negotiator** (decisional).
6. Moves part of the marketing budget to repairs: **resource allocator** (decisional).

Notice how many roles involve talking to people rather than working alone. That is Mintzberg's central point.

## Judging a manager's contribution to performance

Managers contribute by setting direction, allocating resources, motivating staff, solving problems and controlling costs and quality. In a case, look for measurable evidence.

**Worked example.** After a new manager arrived at **Tolland Print**, the following changed over a year:

| Measure | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Orders delivered late | 18% | 7% |
| Labour turnover | 30% | 20% |
| Monthly costs | $96 000 | $90 000 |

- Late deliveries fell by 11 percentage points: (18 − 7) ÷ 18 = 61% fewer late orders in relative terms.
- Monthly costs fell by $6 000, a 6.25% cut, saving $72 000 a year.

Before crediting the manager, check other causes. Did demand fall, making deadlines easier? Was new equipment installed? Did a rival close? Strong answers say the evidence *suggests* a contribution and name what else would need to be checked.

## Choosing a management style

No style is always best. Use these factors to justify a choice:

- **Urgency**: in a crisis or safety emergency, an autocratic approach gives quick, clear instructions.
- **Skill and experience of staff**: experts may work best under a democratic or laissez-faire approach; new or unskilled staff may need closer direction.
- **Size and complexity of the decision**: complex decisions benefit from the knowledge of many people, favouring a democratic approach.
- **Staff expectations and culture**: some workforces expect consultation; others value security and clear guidance, which suits a paternalistic approach.
- **The manager's own beliefs**: links to McGregor.

**Worked comparison.** A restaurant manager faces two situations on the same day:

- A kitchen fire alarm sounds. The manager gives direct orders to evacuate: **autocratic**, appropriate because speed and safety matter.
- The manager wants a new seasonal menu. The chefs are consulted and their ideas shape the dishes: **democratic**, appropriate because the chefs have expertise and will be more committed to dishes they helped create.

The lesson for an evaluation answer: an effective manager adapts the style to the situation.

### Weaknesses of each style in context

- **Autocratic** in a creative business: ideas are lost and skilled staff may leave.
- **Democratic** when a decision is urgent: consultation causes delay.
- **Laissez-faire** with inexperienced staff: lack of direction, missed deadlines.
- **Paternalistic** with ambitious professionals: staff may feel their judgement is not trusted.

## McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y in depth

McGregor's theories describe **managers' assumptions** about workers, not types of worker.

- A **Theory X** manager assumes staff dislike work, avoid responsibility and need close control, so tends towards autocratic management, tight supervision and pay linked to output.
- A **Theory Y** manager assumes staff can enjoy work, accept responsibility and use initiative, so tends towards democratic management, delegation and empowerment.

**Self-fulfilling prophecy.** If a Theory X manager supervises staff closely and never gives them responsibility, staff have no chance to show initiative and may become passive, which seems to confirm the manager's view. A Theory Y manager who delegates may see staff rise to the challenge, again confirming the assumption.

**Links across the course.** Theory X fits Taylor's ideas on motivation; Theory Y fits Herzberg's motivators and Maslow's higher needs. Using these links in an answer shows understanding across topics.

**Limitations.** Real managers rarely hold one pure set of assumptions; they may treat different staff differently. Theory Y can fail if staff lack the skills or wish for more responsibility.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate whether a democratic management style would improve performance at a fast-food restaurant.*

> A democratic style involves staff in decisions, which may raise motivation and bring practical ideas, such as quicker ways to organise the counter at busy times. *(Application and analysis.)* However, fast-food work depends on speed and consistent procedures, and many staff are young and part-time, so long consultation could slow decisions and may not suit staff who want clear instructions. *(Counter-argument from the context.)* The manager might use a democratic style for issues such as rotas, where staff views matter, but keep set procedures for food safety and service standards. *(Balanced recommendation.)* Overall, a democratic style is likely to improve performance only for selected decisions; the outcome depends on staff experience and how much time is available. *(Judgement with conditions.)*

## Common mistakes

- Listing the four functions with no example from the case.
- Describing Theory X and Theory Y as types of worker.
- Saying one style is always best.
- Crediting a manager with every improvement without checking other causes.
- Confusing Fayol's elements with Mintzberg's roles.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify**: name a function, role or style; no explanation needed.
- **Explain**: show how a function or style works in the given business.
- **Analyse**: trace style → effect on staff → effect on performance.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: compare at least two styles or approaches for the situation and decide, stating what the judgement depends on.
- **Advise**: recommend a style or action for the named manager, using evidence from the case.
