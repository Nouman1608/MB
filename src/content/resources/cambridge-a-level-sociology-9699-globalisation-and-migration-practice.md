This set covers section 8.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: causes of
global migration, its consequences, and debates about who benefits. Section A checks concepts and
short explanations; Section B asks for developed explanations and full evaluative essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *net migration*. **[2]**

**2.** In one year a country records 180,000 immigrants and 205,000 emigrants. Calculate net
migration and state what the figure shows. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** intervening obstacles to migration in Lee's model. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between a refugee and an economic migrant. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which tourism can contribute to global migration. **[3]**

**6.** Outline what is meant by a *global care chain*. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **two** consequences of emigration for a sending country. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why net migration figures alone may give a misleading picture of population
movement. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** structural explanations of global migration. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why migration may produce negative perceptions in receiving
societies. **[8]**

**11.** "Migration is best explained by the choices individuals make." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Migrants themselves gain least from global migration." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of people entering a country to live (immigration) [1] minus the number leaving
it (emigration) over a stated period [1]. **[2]**

**2.** 180,000 − 205,000 = −25,000 [1]. The country lost 25,000 people through migration that
year, that is, net emigration, even though 180,000 people arrived [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: the cost of travel [1]; distance [1]; immigration laws and visa rules [1]; physical
barriers such as borders or difficult terrain [1]; lack of information about the destination [1].
**[2]**

**4.** A refugee has fled their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution or conflict
[1] and is protected under the 1951 UN Refugee Convention [1]; an economic migrant moves mainly to
find work or higher wages, so the move is usually treated as voluntary [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Identify a mechanism [1], explain it [1] and give an example or consequence [1]. For
example: tourist resorts create demand for seasonal hotel and restaurant workers [1], who move from
poorer regions or countries to fill these jobs [1]; some stay on, so temporary flows become
permanent settlement [1]. Also creditable: tourists returning as retirees or second-home owners;
tourism spreading images of a destination that become pull factors. **[3]**

**6.** A series of personal links across countries through which care work is passed on [1]: a
woman from a poorer country migrates to care for children or older people in a richer household [1],
while her own children are cared for by relatives or a lower-paid carer at home, so care flows from
poorer to richer families (Hochschild) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Two consequences, each identified [1] and explained [1]: **remittances** — money sent home
pays for schooling, health care or housing [1] [1]; **brain drain** — loss of trained doctors,
nurses or teachers weakens public services that the state paid to train [1] [1]; also creditable:
family separation and children left behind; reduced pressure on local job markets. **[4]**

**8.** Net migration is a single figure produced by subtracting emigration from immigration [1].
Large flows in both directions can cancel out, so a small net figure may hide rapid population
turnover [1]; it also says nothing about who is moving, for example skilled workers leaving while
others arrive [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per explanation: identify the theory [1], explain its mechanism [1], develop it
with a concept or study [1], and apply it to an example [1].
- **Dual labour market theory** [1]: Piore (1979) argued that rich economies contain a secondary
  labour market of insecure, low-paid jobs [1] that native workers avoid, so employers depend on
  recruiting migrants [1]; for example, agricultural and care sectors in many wealthy countries
  recruit abroad each year [1].
- **World-systems view** [1]: Sassen argued that investment from core economies disrupts work in
  the periphery [1], uprooting people and creating links that become migration routes [1]; flows
  often follow historical colonial and trade ties [1].
- Also creditable: network theory and chain migration; global labour patterns such as the kafala
  system. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a concept or perspective
[1], example or consequence [1].
- **Media framing** [1]: news coverage may describe migrants as a "flood" or a threat [1], which can
  create a moral panic and exaggerate the scale of the issue [1]; this shapes public opinion and
  can make hostile policies more popular [1].
- **Competition for scarce resources** [1]: residents may fear pressure on housing, schools,
  health care or jobs [1]; Marxists argue this hostility diverts attention from employers and
  governments who decide wages and spending [1]; pressure may be real in particular places where
  services have not grown with population [1].
- Also creditable: cultural defence by groups who feel their identity is threatened; political
  campaigning that uses migration as an issue. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level needs accurate knowledge of both individual and
structural explanations, applied to specific examples, with sustained evaluation and a supported
judgement. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Lee's push–pull model (1966) treats migration as a decision weighing origin,
  destination, intervening obstacles and personal factors; people facing the same conditions
  decide differently; migrants' own networks, plans and aspirations shape where they go;
  transnational views stress migrants as active agents.
- **Against the view:** Piore's dual labour market (1979) shows demand in receiving economies
  driving flows; Sassen on global cities and disruption of peripheral economies; forced migration
  of refugees removes meaningful choice; immigration law, visas and recruitment systems such as
  kafala decide who can move and on what terms; routes follow colonial and trade ties.
- **Global labour patterns and tourism:** sector-specific recruitment (care, construction,
  technology) and seasonal tourist economies show migration organised by markets.
- **Evaluation:** choice is exercised within structural limits; the balance differs between a
  skilled professional with many options and a refugee or contract worker; the poorest often
  cannot move at all, which shows resources matter as much as motives.
- **Judgement:** for example, individual choice explains *whether* a particular person moves, but
  structures explain the *size, direction and conditions* of global flows. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs gains and losses for migrants against those for
receiving and sending societies, distinguishes types of migrant and reaches a reasoned judgement.
Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Castles and Kosack (1973): migrants as the lowest stratum of the working class;
  exploitation, debt to recruiters and insecure legal status; loss of occupational status
  (qualified workers in unskilled jobs); global care chains place emotional costs on migrant
  women and their children; negative perceptions and racism.
- **Against the view:** migrants often earn far more than at home; remittances raise family living
  standards; refugees gain safety; children may gain education and opportunities; skilled
  migrants may gain career progression and later return with capital (brain circulation).
- **Other beneficiaries:** employers and consumers in receiving societies gain cheaper labour and
  services; sending states gain remittances but lose skills.
- **Evaluation:** outcomes vary by gender, skill, legal status and destination; short-term costs
  may produce long-term gains for the next generation; functionalist and Marxist views measure
  benefit differently (income versus power and exploitation).
- **Judgement:** for example, migrants often gain materially but have the least power over the
  terms of migration, so the view is partly accurate for low-paid and irregular migrants and less
  so for skilled ones. **[35]**
