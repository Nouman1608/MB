## Definitions

- **Exothermic:** a reaction that transfers energy **to** the surroundings. ΔH is **negative**.
- **Endothermic:** a reaction that takes in energy **from** the surroundings. ΔH is **positive**.
- **ΔH:** the enthalpy change, the energy change of the reacting chemicals, usually in kJ/mol.
- **Reversible reaction:** products can react to re-form the reactants, A + B ⇌ C + D.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| System | The reacting chemicals |
| Surroundings | Everything else: solvent, container, thermometer, air |
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | Energy change of the reacting chemicals, usually given per mole |
| Combustion | Burning a substance in oxygen |
| Oxidation | Gain of oxygen (and, in 3.8.4, loss of electrons) |
| Neutralisation | Acid + base → salt + water |
| Thermal decomposition | Breaking down a compound by heating |
| Activation energy | Minimum energy particles need to react |
| Hydrated / anhydrous | With / without water of crystallisation |

## Side-by-side

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Energy flow | System → surroundings | Surroundings → system |
| Temperature of surroundings | Rises | Falls |
| ΔH sign | − | + |
| Products vs reactants energy | Products lower | Products higher |
| Specification examples | Combustion, many oxidations, neutralisation | Thermal decompositions |
| Everyday uses | Self-heating cans, hand warmers | Some sports injury packs |

## Classifying from evidence

- Thermometer rises → exothermic → ΔH negative.
- Thermometer falls → endothermic → ΔH positive.
- Reaction only continues while heated → endothermic (for example, a thermal decomposition).
- Reaction keeps going once started → exothermic, even if a flame was needed to start it.

## Reactions to name in answers

**Exothermic**

- Burning methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
- Magnesium burning: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
- Neutralisation: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
- Magnesium with dilute acid: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

**Endothermic**

- Thermal decomposition of copper carbonate: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂
- Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
- Heating hydrated copper sulfate to drive off water

## Showing the type by experiment

1. Measure a known volume of one reactant solution into a polystyrene cup.
2. Record its starting temperature with a thermometer.
3. Add the second reactant and stir.
4. Record the highest or lowest temperature reached.
5. A rise means exothermic; a fall means endothermic.

The polystyrene cup insulates the mixture, so the temperature change reflects the reaction rather than energy exchanged with the air. Calculating the amount of energy from these readings belongs to 3.9.2.

## Reading ΔH values

| ΔH given | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| −57 kJ/mol | Exothermic: 57 kJ transferred to the surroundings per mole |
| +178 kJ/mol | Endothermic: 178 kJ taken in from the surroundings per mole |
| Larger negative number | More energy released per mole, if the reactions are compared per mole |

## Reversible reactions

hydrated copper sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper sulfate (white) + water

- Forward (heating blue crystals): **endothermic**.
- Reverse (adding water to white powder): **exothermic**; the mixture warms up.
- Same amount of energy each way; only the sign of ΔH changes.
- If forward ΔH = +x kJ/mol, reverse ΔH = −x kJ/mol.

## Uses: matching type to job

| Need | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Warm a drink without a stove | Exothermic | Energy released heats the drink |
| Warm hands outdoors | Exothermic | Slow, steady release over time |
| Cool a sports injury | Endothermic | Takes energy from the skin, which cools |

## Exam traps

- "Temperature went up, so ΔH is positive": wrong; ΔH is negative.
- "Needs a match, so endothermic": wrong; the match supplies activation energy only.
- "Endothermic reactions release cold": energy is taken in, not cold released.
- Swapping the copper sulfate colours.
- Saying energy is "used up" or "made"; it is transferred.
- Giving a type of reaction (combustion) when a specific example is asked for, or the reverse.

## Quick self-test

1. Define an exothermic reaction.
2. Give the sign of ΔH for an endothermic reaction.
3. Name three types of exothermic reaction listed in the specification.
4. Name the type of reaction that is endothermic in the specification.
5. A reaction makes the temperature of a solution fall from 21 °C to 16 °C. Classify it and give the sign of ΔH.
6. Why is the burning of a candle exothermic even though it must be lit with a match?
7. Give one everyday use of an exothermic reaction.
8. Give one everyday use of an endothermic reaction.
9. State the colour of hydrated and of anhydrous copper sulfate.
10. Is adding water to anhydrous copper sulfate exothermic or endothermic?
11. A reversible reaction has ΔH = −92 kJ/mol in the forward direction. Give ΔH for the reverse reaction.
12. Why does a thermal decomposition stop when the Bunsen burner is removed?

### Answers

1. A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings.
2. Positive.
3. Combustion, (many) oxidation reactions, neutralisation.
4. Thermal decomposition.
5. Endothermic; ΔH positive.
6. The match only provides the activation energy; once started, the reaction releases more energy than it took in.
7. Self-heating cans or hand warmers.
8. Sports injury (cold) packs.
9. Hydrated: blue. Anhydrous: white.
10. Exothermic.
11. +92 kJ/mol.
12. It is endothermic and needs a continuous supply of energy from the surroundings.
