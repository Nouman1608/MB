AQA GCSE History (8145) is assessed through two written papers, not tiered: Paper 1 (Understanding
the modern world -- a period study and a wider-world depth study, 2h, 84 marks, 50%) and Paper 2
(Shaping the nation -- a thematic study and a British depth study including the historic environment,
2h, 84 marks, 50%). These notes turn that structure into an exam-day plan, alongside the
[Course Structure and Study Approach guide](/resources/gcse-history-course-structure/) and the
topic-level [Understanding the Modern World](/resources/gcse-history-understanding-modern-world-revision-notes/)
and [Shaping the Nation](/resources/gcse-history-shaping-the-nation-revision-notes/) revision notes
already on the site.

## Confirm exactly which studies your school teaches before you revise

Schools choose their own period study, wider-world depth study, thematic study and British depth study
from AQA's published lists, and AQA specifies the historic environment site for each British depth study,
changing it annually -- two schools both entered for 8145 may be examined on genuinely different content.
**Exam-preparation priority**: confirm exactly which studies and which historic environment site apply to you before selecting past papers or revision material, since
revision guides and practice questions are only useful once matched to your specific course.

## AO1 to AO4: know which skill a question is testing

Across both papers, AQA GCSE History assesses four assessment objectives: AO1 (knowledge and
understanding of the key features and characteristics of the period, 35%), AO2 (explaining and
analysing events using second-order concepts such as causation, change, continuity, consequence and
significance, 35%), AO3 (analysing, evaluating and using contemporary sources to make substantiated
judgements, 15%) and AO4 (analysing, evaluating and making substantiated judgements about
interpretations, 15%). Sources (AO3) and interpretations (AO4) are separate skills, tested on separate
questions. **Exam-preparation priority**: each section moves from lower-tariff questions (such as a
4-mark AO1 "describe" question) to an extended essay that combines AO1 and AO2 and carries the
section's highest tariff (12 or 16 marks, with 4 further marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar on
two of the four essays) -- pairing every content-recall session with a timed extended-writing question
on that same material practises the highest-value marks, not just the easiest ones.

## Source evaluation is a distinct, practisable skill, not general knowledge

Source questions (AO3) sit in two places: Paper 1 Section B, the wider world depth study (a 4-mark
source-analysis question on one source, then a 12-mark question on how useful two sources are), and
Paper 2 Section A, the thematic study (an 8-mark question on how useful one source is). They ask
students to judge a source's usefulness from its content and provenance, using their own contextual
knowledge, not simply describe its content. Paper 2's historic environment is examined differently --
by a 16-mark essay on the specified site -- and the interpretation questions in Paper 1 Section A and
Paper 2 Section B test AO4, not source utility. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise source-utility questions
specifically -- what a source shows, why it was produced, and what that means for how it can be used
-- as a skill separate from period-content recall.

## The historic environment study needs its own dedicated revision block

One required element of the British depth study is a historic environment study of one specified
site, which AQA changes annually; it is examined by the final question of Paper 2 Section B, a 16-mark
essay (8 AO1, 8 AO2). **Exam-preparation priority**: confirm the current site for
your examination series with your teacher, then revise it as its own focused block, since this
content is tested specifically and is easy to under-revise relative to the rest of Paper 2's British
depth study.

## Worked practice scenario: assessing a source's usefulness

Question: "A government poster from wartime encourages citizens to conserve food. How useful is this
source for a historian studying the government's wartime priorities?"

```
Step 1 - origin and purpose:
The source is an official government poster, produced during wartime
to persuade the public to change behaviour (conserve food).

Step 2 - what it is directly useful for:
It is useful evidence of what the government considered a wartime
priority (food supply) and how it chose to communicate that priority
to the public.

Step 3 - its limitation, tied to a DIFFERENT question:
It is less useful as direct evidence of whether the public actually
complied, since a poster reflects the government's intention, not the
public's real behaviour.

Step 4 - conclusion:
The source is useful for the specific question asked (government
priorities and messaging) precisely because of its origin as an
official government product -- the same origin that limits its
usefulness for a related but different question about public
behaviour.
```

Notice the answer is tied to the *specific* question asked (government priorities) rather than a
generic comment on posters or propaganda in the abstract -- exactly the distinction that separates a
strong source-utility answer from a weak one.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: confirm exactly which period, depth, thematic and British studies your school
  teaches, and the historic environment site specified for your series; pair every content-recall revision session with a
  timed extended-writing question on the same material; practise source-utility questions
  specifically, not just period-content recall; give the historic environment study its own dedicated
  revision block.
- **During Paper 1**: identify which specific period study and depth study a question is drawing on
  before answering; in Section B, apply provenance-and-purpose reasoning to both source questions, and
  in Section A, treat the interpretation questions as AO4, not source evaluation.
- **During Paper 2**: tie the Section A source-utility question to the specific question asked, not a
  generic comment on the source type, and build the Section B historic environment essay from specific
  features of the specified site linked to the wider depth study.
- **On every paper**: match your answer's depth to the command word and question tariff -- a
  low-tariff AO1 question does not need extended analysis, and a high-tariff question needs
  more than recall.

## Self-test

1. Why might two schools both entered for AQA GCSE History be examined on different content?
2. What four assessment objectives does the specification test across both papers?
3. Why is source evaluation a distinct skill from period-content recall?
4. In the worked scenario, why is the poster useful for one question but limited for a related one?

**Answers:** 1. Because schools choose their own period study, wider-world depth study, thematic
study and British depth study from AQA's published lists (and AQA changes the specified historic
environment site annually), so the exact content examined depends on those choices. 2. AO1 (knowledge
and understanding), AO2 (explaining and analysing causation, change, continuity, consequence and
significance), AO3 (analysing, evaluating and using contemporary sources) and AO4 (analysing and
evaluating historical interpretations). 3. Because source-based questions ask for
a judgement about a source's usefulness or reliability given its origin and purpose, not simply a
description of what the source shows -- a genuinely separate skill from recalling period content. 4.
Because the poster's origin (an official government product) makes it strong direct evidence of what
the government considered a priority and how it communicated that, but weak evidence of whether the
public actually complied, since a poster reflects intention, not real behaviour.
