This guide covers subtopic **15.1 Coordination and response in plants** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe gravitropism and phototropism as responses in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from gravity or light; to explain the role of auxin in controlling shoot growth (made in the shoot tip, spreads through the plant, is unequally distributed in response to light and gravity, and stimulates cell elongation); and to investigate gravitropism and phototropism in shoots and roots.

## Plants respond to stimuli

Plants have no nervous system, but they detect changes in their surroundings and respond to them. A plant cannot move from place to place, so its main response is to change the direction in which parts of it grow. A **tropism** is a directional growth response to a stimulus. The direction of growth depends on the direction of the stimulus. The two tropisms in the syllabus are phototropism (light) and gravitropism (gravity).

A response is described as **positive** if the part grows towards the stimulus and **negative** if it grows away.

## Phototropism

**Phototropism is a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from light.**

- Shoots are **positively phototropic**: they grow towards the light. This helps the leaves to absorb more light for photosynthesis.
- Roots are usually not strongly affected by light, and where they respond they grow away from it, so they are described as negatively phototropic. They grow down into the soil, where they take up water and ions.

## Gravitropism

**Gravitropism is a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from gravity.**

- Roots are **positively gravitropic**: they grow downwards, towards gravity. This anchors the plant and reaches water in the soil.
- Shoots are **negatively gravitropic**: they grow upwards, away from gravity. This lifts the leaves into the light and the flowers into position for pollination.

| Part | Response to light | Response to gravity |
|---|---|---|
| Shoot | Grows towards (positive phototropism) | Grows away (negative gravitropism) |
| Root | Grows away or little response | Grows towards (positive gravitropism) |

The two stimuli can act together. A seed that germinates in the dark underground uses gravity to grow its shoot upwards until it reaches the light, and then light takes over.

## Auxin and shoot growth

Plant responses are coordinated by chemicals. The syllabus names one, **auxin**, and asks about its role in shoots. Four points are required.

1. Auxin is **made in the shoot tip**.
2. Auxin **spreads through the plant** from the shoot tip, moving down the shoot.
3. Auxin is **unequally distributed** in response to light and gravity.
4. Auxin **stimulates cell elongation** (the cells get longer).

The key idea follows from point 4. Where there is more auxin in a shoot, the cells on that side elongate more. If the two sides of a shoot grow at different rates, the shoot bends towards the side that is growing more slowly.

### How phototropism works in a shoot

1. Light shines on a shoot from one side.
2. Auxin made at the tip moves to the **shaded side** of the shoot.
3. The cells on the shaded side receive more auxin, so they **elongate more** than the cells on the lit side.
4. The shaded side grows faster, so the shoot **bends towards the light**.

### How gravitropism works in a shoot

1. A shoot is placed on its side.
2. Auxin collects on the **lower side** because of gravity.
3. The cells on the lower side elongate more than those on the upper side.
4. The lower side grows faster, so the shoot **bends upwards**, away from gravity.

In both cases, the sequence of "unequal auxin, unequal elongation, bending" is what earns the marks. A frequent error is to say that auxin makes cells divide. At this level, auxin stimulates cell **elongation**.

### Roots (extension, not required)

The syllabus asks you to explain auxin in shoots only. For interest, roots respond to unequal auxin in the opposite way: a high concentration of auxin on the lower side of a root slows the growth of that side, so the root bends downwards. If a question asks about roots you can describe the response, but the explanation required at this level concerns shoots.

## Role of the shoot tip

The tip is the source of auxin. Experiments show this.

| Treatment | Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Normal shoot, light from one side | Bends towards the light | Normal response |
| Tip removed, light from one side | No bending, little growth | The tip makes something needed for growth |
| Tip covered with an opaque cap | No bending | The tip detects the light |
| Tip left uncovered but lower part covered | Bends towards the light | The tip detects the light; the lower part is not the detector |

These results show that the tip both detects the stimulus and produces a growth substance that moves down to the region where the cells elongate.

## Investigating tropisms

The syllabus asks you to investigate gravitropism and phototropism in shoots and roots. Use these designs.

### Phototropism in shoots

1. Grow several seedlings (for example cress or oat) in identical pots.
2. Put one pot in a box with a slit that lets in light from one side. Put a second, control pot in a box with light from all sides or in a rotating clinostat.
3. Leave the seedlings for several days under the same conditions.
4. Observe the direction of the shoots, or measure the **angle of curvature** with a protractor.

The test seedlings bend towards the slit. The control seedlings grow straight up. Keep the temperature, water supply and type of seedling the same.

### Gravitropism in shoots and roots

1. Germinate seeds on damp cotton wool or filter paper in a transparent container, such as a Petri dish.
2. Stand the dish upright so the seeds are in different positions, or turn it on its side.
3. Keep the dish in the dark, so that light cannot be a stimulus.
4. Observe after 2 to 3 days. The roots curve downwards and the shoots curve upwards whatever the orientation of the seed.

A **clinostat** rotates the plants slowly so that gravity acts equally on all sides, and so the seedlings grow straight. It makes a good control.

### Worked example: processing results

*Five seedlings were grown with light from the right. After three days the angles of curvature of the shoots towards the light were 32, 38, 35, 29 and 41 degrees. Seedlings on a clinostat showed 3, 2, 4, 1 and 5 degrees.*

1. Mean for the test = (32 + 38 + 35 + 29 + 41) divided by 5 = 175 divided by 5 = 35 degrees.
2. Mean for the control = (3 + 2 + 4 + 1 + 5) divided by 5 = 15 divided by 5 = 3 degrees.
3. Difference = 35 - 3 = 32 degrees. The shoots with one-sided light bent towards it, which supports positive phototropism.

## Worked example: explaining a result

*A shoot tip is cut off and placed on a small block of agar for an hour. The agar block is then placed on one side of a decapitated shoot in the dark. The shoot bends away from the side with the block. Explain.*

- Auxin made in the tip diffused into the agar block.
- Auxin passed from the block into the one side of the shoot stump.
- The cells on that side elongated more than the cells on the other side.
- The shoot therefore bent towards the opposite side.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the shoot "knows" where the light is without mentioning auxin.
- Saying that auxin moves to the side with more light. It moves to the shaded side.
- Saying that auxin makes cells divide or grow wider. It stimulates elongation.
- Mixing up positive and negative: shoots are negatively gravitropic and roots are positively gravitropic.
- Forgetting that the tip makes the auxin.
- Leaving out controls, or leaving the plants in a light that can affect gravitropism results.
- Writing that the plant "moves" towards the light. It grows.

## Command words

- **Describe**: define a tropism or the result of an experiment.
- **Explain**: link unequal auxin to unequal elongation and bending.
- **Investigate**: plan a test with a control and a measured result.
