> "The origins and role of the major law schools in Islam including: Hanafi, Shaf'i, Maliki, Hanbali

## Answers

**How the parts are marked.** One mark for each correct answer in the 1- and 2-mark questions. In
the 4-mark questions, one mark for each point identified and one for explaining it, up to two marks
for each of the two. The 6-mark questions give four AO1 marks on that pattern plus up to two AO2
marks: **one** for a general, accurate use of a source of authority, **two** for a specific use
applied to the question. The 12-mark questions are marked by levels:

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Thorough examination of a range of points; logical reasoning with detailed evidence; excellent, direct use of the passage |
| 3 | 7–9 | Clear examination of more than one point; reasoning with some detail; good use of the passage |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some accurate examination; reasoning not always supported; general use of the passage. **Maximum Level 2 if only one point is made** |
| 1 | 1–3 | Limited examination with little reasoning. **Maximum Level 1 if the passage is not used** |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit |

### Section A

**1.** Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal [1].

**2.** Madinah [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: the Qur'an; the Sunnah or Hadith; consensus (Ijma). (Qiyas is used by the four Sunni schools but not in the same way by the Ja'fari school, so credit it only with that qualification.)

**4.** Any two, [1] each: South Asia (Pakistan, India, Bangladesh); Turkey; Central Asia; the Balkans; Afghanistan.

**5.** For each of two reasons: reason identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Expansion of Islam** [1]: Muslims in new lands faced questions not answered directly in Madinah [1].
- **Spread of knowledge** [1]: companions settled in different cities, so different Hadith were known in different places [1].
- **Different methods** [1]: some scholars stressed Hadith and practice, others reasoning by analogy [1].
- **Need for consistency** [1]: judges and communities needed organised, reliable rulings [1].

**6.** For each of two features: feature identified [1] and explained [1].

- **al-Risala** [1]: al-Shafi'i set out the principles of jurisprudence systematically for the first time [1].
- **Order of sources** [1]: Qur'an, Sunnah, Ijma, Qiyas, which became the standard Sunni account [1].
- **Authentic Hadith above custom** [1]: a sound Hadith outweighs local practice or personal opinion [1].
- **A bridge between schools** [1]: al-Shafi'i studied with Malik and with Hanafi scholars, combining their approaches [1].

**7.** For each of two features: feature identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Founded on Ja'far al-Sadiq's teaching** [1]: the sixth Imam in Twelver Shi'a belief, a descendant of the Prophet (pbuh) [1].
- **Teachings of the Ahl al-Bayt** [1]: the sayings of the Imams are part of the Sunnah it relies on [1].
- **Reason instead of qiyas** [1]: it uses 'aql where the texts are silent [1].
- **Living ijtihad** [1]: qualified scholars continue to give rulings, and followers choose a living marja' [1].

**8.** For each of two differences: difference identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Hands in prayer** [1]: Hanafis place them below the navel, Shafi'is above it, many Malikis at the sides [1].
- **Saying "Amin"** [1]: Shafi'is and Hanbalis say it aloud after al-Fatiha, Hanafis quietly [1].
- **What breaks Wudu** [1]: Hanafis hold that flowing blood breaks it, Shafi'is that touching a non-mahram of the opposite sex does [1].
- **Combining prayers** [1]: Ja'faris combine regularly; Hanafis only at Arafat and Muzdalifah [1].

### Section B

**9.** AO1 **[4]**: for each of two reasons, the reason identified [1] and explained [1]. AO2 **[2]**: one source of authority used generally [1] or specifically and applied [2].

Reasons (AO1):

- **Practical guidance** [1]: Muslims know how to pray, fast and trade correctly without studying the sources for themselves [1].
- **Flexibility** [1]: different methods help Islamic law respond to new situations and cultures [1].
- **Continuity** [1]: the schools link Muslims today to the scholarship of the earliest generations [1].
- **Protection from error** [1]: rulings come from trained scholars, not personal guesswork [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you" (Qur'an 4:59), which many scholars understand to include the learned [2].
- Mu'adh ibn Jabal (RA) told the Prophet (pbuh) he would judge by the Qur'an, then the Sunnah, then his own reasoning, and the Prophet approved (Abu Dawud): the schools follow the same order [2].

**10.** AO1 **[4]**: as in question 9. AO2 **[2]**: as in question 9.

Reasons (AO1):

- **Ijtihad needs expertise** [1]: Arabic, the Qur'an, thousands of Hadith and the principles of law must all be mastered [1].
- **Avoiding mistakes** [1]: an untrained person might misread a verse or use a weak Hadith [1].
- **Unity of practice** [1]: following a school keeps communities consistent in worship [1].
- **Trust in scholarship** [1]: the schools' rulings have been tested by generations of scholars [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- "Ask the people of the message if you do not know" (Qur'an 16:43) [2].
- "Refer it to Allah and the Messenger" (Qur'an 4:59): the schools exist to do this carefully on behalf of Muslims [2].

**11.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **For the statement:** differences in practice can cause arguments in mosques and communities; some Muslims become attached to their school above the wider Ummah; differences have at times been used to divide people.
- **Against the statement:** the schools agree on all the fundamentals; the Sunni schools accept one another; the Amman Message recognised the Sunni and Ja'fari schools together; diversity gives flexibility for different cultures; the imams themselves were teachers and students of one another.
- **Balanced view:** differences weaken unity only when Muslims treat them as divisions of faith rather than of method.

*Use of the passage*

- "If you disagree over anything" shows that the Qur'an expects disagreement, so it is not in itself a failure.
- "Refer it to Allah and the Messenger" gives a shared method that unites all the schools.
- "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger" is the common foundation every school accepts.
- "That is the best [way] and best in result" suggests that handling disagreement through the sources leads to good results, not division.

**12.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **For the statement:** scholars have the knowledge to interpret the sources; following them avoids error and division; Qur'an 16:43 tells people to ask those who know.
- **Against the statement:** loyalty is to Allah and the Messenger first; scholars can make mistakes; some Muslims follow the strongest evidence on an issue rather than one school; in the Ja'fari school followers choose a living marja' and may change; new situations may need fresh ijtihad.
- **Balanced view:** following qualified scholars is wise for most Muslims, but scholars are followed because they explain the sources, not instead of them.

*Use of the passage*

- "Whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear" shows that the final authority is the Prophet's (pbuh) guidance, not any scholar.
- "Follows other than the way of the believers" is used by scholars to support consensus, which suggests that individuals should not go against the agreed views of the community's scholars.
- The warning "drive him into Hell" shows how seriously the Qur'an treats leaving the guidance shared by the believers.

An answer that explains a range of points, reasons about their implications and uses the passage directly reaches Level 4.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming only four schools when the question allows any of the five.
- Mixing up founders and cities.
- Calling the schools separate religions or sects.
- Giving a difference in creed rather than in practice.
- In the 12-mark questions, ignoring the counter-view or not using the passage.
