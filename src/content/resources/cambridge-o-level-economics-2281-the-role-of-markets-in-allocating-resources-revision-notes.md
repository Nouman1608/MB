These are condensed notes for section 2.2, *The role of markets in allocating resources* (2.2.1 the market system; 2.2.2 key resource allocation decisions; 2.2.3 introduction to the price mechanism), of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on the role of markets](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-markets-in-allocating-resources/). Then try the [practice questions on the role of markets](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-markets-in-allocating-resources-practice/). All examples are invented.

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Market | Any arrangement in which buyers and sellers exchange goods, services or factors |
| Market system | An economic system in which resources are allocated through markets and prices |
| Market equilibrium | Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure for price to change |
| Equilibrium price | The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Market disequilibrium | Quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal |
| Excess demand (shortage) | Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied, at a price below equilibrium |
| Excess supply (surplus) | Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, at a price above equilibrium |
| Price mechanism | The way price changes, caused by changes in demand and supply, allocate resources |

## Buyers and sellers in two kinds of market

| Market | Buyers | Sellers | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods and services | Households (consumers) | Firms | Price of the product |
| Factors of production (e.g. labour) | Firms | Households (workers) | Wage, rent, interest |

## Disequilibrium and how it is removed

**Worked reminder (invented): rice in Kemar.**

| Price per kg (cu) | Demanded (tonnes) | Supplied (tonnes) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.80 | 70 | 30 | Shortage of 40 |
| 1.00 | 60 | 40 | Shortage of 20 |
| 1.20 | 50 | 50 | Equilibrium |
| 1.40 | 40 | 60 | Surplus of 20 |

- **At 1.40 cu (surplus):** unsold rice → sellers cut price → quantity demanded extends, quantity supplied contracts → gap closes at 1.20 cu.
- **At 1.00 cu (shortage):** buyers compete → price rises → quantity demanded contracts, quantity supplied extends → gap closes at 1.20 cu.
- Excess demand or supply = quantity demanded − quantity supplied (positive = shortage, negative = surplus).

## The three key questions

Scarcity → every economy must decide:

- **What** to produce, and how much of each good.
- **How** to produce: which factors and methods.
- **For whom** to produce: who gets the output.

## Three jobs of price

| Job | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| Signalling | Price changes carry information about changes in demand or supply |
| Incentive | Higher prices and profits encourage more supply; lower prices encourage more buying |
| Rationing | A higher price limits a scarce good to those willing and able to pay |

## How the price mechanism answers each question

| Question | The answer in a market system |
|---|---|
| What | Goods with rising demand rise in price and profit, so firms make more; resources leave goods with falling demand |
| How | Firms choose the lowest-cost method at current factor prices; if wages rise relative to machine costs, they switch to machines |
| For whom | Those willing and able to pay; income decides ability to pay |

**Chain to write.** Demand for a good rises → price rises (signal) → profit rises, firms expand (incentive) → firms hire more factors, so resources move into the industry → higher price limits the good to those willing and able to pay (rationing).

**Worked reminder: "how".** Method X: 8 workers + 30 cu equipment. Method Y: 3 workers + 130 cu equipment. Equal cost when 8w + 30 = 3w + 130, so 5w = 100 and w = 20 cu. Above a wage of 20 cu, method Y (more capital) is cheaper.

## Strengths and limits

**Strengths**

- No central planner needed; millions of decisions are coordinated by prices.
- Responds to changes in what consumers want.
- Profit encourages efficiency and lowest-cost methods.

**Limits**

- "For whom" favours those with higher incomes; people with low incomes may lack essentials.
- Some goods may be under- or over-provided (market failure, section 2.10).
- Adjustment takes time.

## Must-know distinctions

- Shortage (excess demand) is below the equilibrium price; surplus (excess supply) is above it.
- Equilibrium means quantities match, not that everyone is satisfied.
- Willing and able to pay, not simply wanting the good.
- Goods markets versus factor markets: households are buyers in one and sellers in the other.

## Exam traps

- Writing "demand rises" when the price falls in a surplus. Say quantity demanded **extends**.
- Mixing up which side of equilibrium a shortage is on.
- Listing the three questions with no link to scarcity.
- Describing the price mechanism without the response of firms and the movement of resources.
- One-sided evaluation of the price mechanism.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a market.
2. Define market equilibrium.
3. At a price below equilibrium, is there a shortage or a surplus?
4. Using the rice table, what is the excess demand at 0.80 cu?
5. Explain what happens to price when there is a surplus.
6. State the three key questions every economy must answer.
7. Why do the three questions exist?
8. Name the three jobs of price.
9. How does the price mechanism decide "for whom"?
10. Who are the sellers in the labour market?
11. Method A uses 6 workers and 40 cu of equipment; method B uses 2 workers and 120 cu. Above what wage is method B cheaper?
12. Give **one** limitation of the price mechanism.

### Answers

1. Any arrangement in which buyers and sellers exchange goods, services or factors.
2. The situation where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure for the price to change.
3. A shortage.
4. 70 − 30 = 40 tonnes.
5. Sellers cut the price to sell unsold stock; quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until equilibrium is reached.
6. What to produce, how to produce, for whom to produce.
7. Resources are finite and wants unlimited, so choices must be made.
8. Signalling, incentive, rationing.
9. Goods go to those willing and able to pay the price.
10. Households (workers).
11. 6w + 40 = 2w + 120, so 4w = 80 and w = 20 cu; above 20 cu, method B is cheaper.
12. Any one: low-income households may go without; some goods are under- or over-provided; adjustment takes time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does disequilibrium not last in a free market?**
A surplus pushes sellers to cut prices and a shortage lets them raise prices. Each price change moves quantity demanded and supplied towards each other until they are equal.

**How does a market system decide what to produce?**
Through prices and profit. Goods that consumers want more of rise in price and become more profitable, so firms move resources into producing them.
