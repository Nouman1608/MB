## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Respiration | Transfer of energy stored in complex organic molecules to ATP |
| Phosphorylation | Adding a phosphate group |
| Oxidation | Losing electrons; in these pathways, usually losing hydrogen |
| Reduction | Gain of electrons, or in these pathways usually gain of hydrogen |
| Decarboxylation | Removal of carbon dioxide from a substrate |
| Dehydrogenation | Taking hydrogen off a substrate molecule |
| Substrate-level phosphorylation | Making ATP by passing a phosphate group directly from a substrate to ADP |
| Coenzyme | Non-protein organic molecule that helps an enzyme by carrying a group or hydrogen |

## 5.2.2(a) Why respire

- ATP is the immediate energy source. Cells store little, so respiration must keep making it.
- Animals: active transport (sodium–potassium pumps), muscle contraction, synthesis of proteins.
- Plants: active transport of ions into root hair cells, active loading of sugars, making cellulose and other macromolecules.
- Microorganisms: growth, division, synthesis, movement.
- Named reaction link: glycolysis starts by using ATP to phosphorylate glucose.
- Plants respire all the time, not only at night.

## Where each stage happens

| Stage | Site | Oxygen needed directly? |
|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | Cytoplasm | No |
| Link reaction | Matrix | No |
| Krebs cycle | Matrix | No |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Inner membrane and cristae | Yes, final electron acceptor |

## Method in steps: glycolysis (cytoplasm)

1. Two ATP phosphorylate glucose, giving hexose bisphosphate (6C).
2. Hexose bisphosphate splits into two triose phosphate (3C).
3. Each triose phosphate is oxidised: hydrogen to NAD gives reduced NAD, and two ATP form per triose phosphate.
4. Two pyruvate result.

Net for one glucose: two ATP (four formed, two spent), two reduced NAD and two pyruvate.

## Method in steps: link reaction (matrix)

1. Pyruvate (3C) is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂.
2. The remaining part is dehydrogenated: NAD becomes reduced NAD.
3. The two-carbon acetyl group joins coenzyme A to form acetyl CoA.

Per glucose (×2): 2 CO₂, 2 reduced NAD, 2 acetyl CoA, no ATP.

## Method in steps: Krebs cycle (matrix)

1. Acetyl CoA (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) to form citrate (6C); CoA is released.
2. Citrate is decarboxylated (two CO₂) and dehydrogenated, in enzyme-controlled steps.
3. ATP forms by substrate-level phosphorylation.
4. Oxaloacetate is regenerated, so the cycle continues.

Each turn gives two CO₂, three reduced NAD, one reduced FAD and one ATP. One glucose means two turns.

## Worked reminders

**Magnification.** A mitochondrion is 27 mm long on a micrograph at ×15 000. Real length = 27 / 15 000 = 0.0018 mm = 1.8 µm.

**Glycolysis in moles.** 0.25 mol glucose gives 0.25 × 4 = 1.00 mol ATP made, 0.25 × 2 = 0.50 mol used, so 0.50 mol net. Reduced NAD is 0.25 × 2 = 0.50 mol and pyruvate is 0.50 mol.

**After glycolysis.** Twelve pyruvate molecules give 12 acetyl CoA and 12 CO₂ in the link reaction. They then give 12 turns of the cycle: 24 CO₂, 36 reduced NAD, 12 reduced FAD and 12 ATP. The total CO₂ is 36, which equals 6 glucose × 6 carbon atoms.

**Rule of thumb.** Count per pyruvate first, then double for each glucose. This avoids the commonest slip.

## Linking the stages together

- Glycolysis feeds pyruvate to the link reaction, which feeds acetyl CoA to the Krebs cycle.
- The carbon atoms leave as CO₂ in the link reaction and Krebs cycle, not in glycolysis.
- The hydrogen atoms end up on reduced NAD and reduced FAD, which are used in oxidative phosphorylation.
- Direct ATP formation (substrate-level phosphorylation) happens in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle only.
- Each stage depends on oxidised NAD or FAD being available, so a shortage of oxygen has effects on all of them.

## Coenzymes

| Coenzyme | Carries | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| NAD | Hydrogen | Glycolysis, link reaction, Krebs cycle |
| FAD | Hydrogen | Krebs cycle |
| Coenzyme A | Acetyl group | Link reaction and delivery to the Krebs cycle |

Reduced NAD and reduced FAD deliver hydrogen and electrons to oxidative phosphorylation. They are recycled: oxidised NAD and FAD must be available again for the earlier stages to carry on.

## 5.2.2(g) Oxidative phosphorylation: what to say

- Site: inner mitochondrial membrane, including cristae.
- Reduced NAD and reduced FAD give up hydrogen, and electrons pass along electron carriers.
- Energy is released step by step along the chain.
- Oxygen takes up the electrons at the end of the chain and, with protons, becomes water.
- No oxygen: chain stops, reduced coenzymes build up, link reaction and Krebs cycle slow.
- Cristae give a large surface area for carriers and ATP synthase.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Decarboxylation and dehydrogenation | CO₂ removed, compared with hydrogen removed |
| Oxidation and reduction of NAD | Losing hydrogen turns reduced NAD into NAD; gaining hydrogen turns NAD into reduced NAD |
| Gross and net ATP in glycolysis | 4 made, 2 used, net 2 |
| Substrate-level and oxidative phosphorylation | Direct phosphate transfer, compared with ATP made using the electron carriers on the inner membrane |
| Acetyl group and acetyl CoA | Two-carbon group, compared with that group attached to coenzyme A |
| Citrate and oxaloacetate | 6C product, compared with the 4C compound regenerated |

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing glycolysis in the mitochondrion instead of the cytoplasm.
- Writing "makes ATP" for the link reaction, which makes none directly.
- Stating 2 ATP for glycolysis without showing that 2 are used and 4 made.
- Forgetting to double the link reaction and Krebs cycle numbers per glucose.
- Writing "hydrogen" and "oxygen" interchangeably in oxidation and reduction answers.
- Describing the Krebs cycle as a straight chain, with no regeneration of oxaloacetate.
- Saying oxygen is "used in the Krebs cycle" instead of explaining its role at the end of the electron carriers.
- Giving cristae as "where respiration happens" without saying they increase the surface area.
- Not converting mm to µm in a magnification calculation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much ATP does glycolysis make?**
Glycolysis makes 4 ATP but uses 2 ATP in the phosphorylation of glucose, giving a net yield of 2 ATP per glucose. Show both figures, because stating "2 ATP" without the 4 made and 2 used can lose marks.

**Is oxygen used in the Krebs cycle?**
No. Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain. Without it, reduced NAD and FAD are not reoxidised, so the Krebs cycle stops indirectly because it runs out of NAD and FAD.

**Why is the Krebs cycle a cycle?**
Oxaloacetate combines with acetyl CoA to form citrate, and oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of each turn so that it can accept another acetyl group. Describing it as a straight chain misses this regeneration.

**Why are the cristae important?**
The cristae are folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane that increase its surface area for electron carriers and ATP synthase. Saying only that they are "where respiration happens" does not earn the mark.

**Do I need to learn every Krebs cycle intermediate for OCR?**
No. The OCR A Level Biology A (H420) specification does not require the names of the intermediate compounds; you need citrate and oxaloacetate only.
