## 1. Measuring capacity utilisation

**Capacity** is the maximum output a business can produce in a given period with its existing resources (premises, machines, labour and time). **Capacity utilisation** shows how much of that maximum is actually being used. The standard form is:

capacity utilisation (%) = (actual output ÷ maximum possible output) × 100

Both figures must cover the same period and use the same unit, for example kayaks per year or customers per week. You can rearrange the formula:

- actual output = utilisation ÷ 100 × maximum output
- maximum output = actual output ÷ utilisation × 100

**Worked example 1.** Ardmoor Kayaks can build 2,400 kayaks a year and built 1,740 last year.

- Utilisation = 1,740 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **72.5%**.
- The owner wants 85%. Required output = 0.85 × 2,400 = 2,040 kayaks, which is 300 more than now.

Utilisation is easiest to judge when compared: with last year, with a target, or with a rival in the same industry. A figure on its own says little. It also describes capacity, not demand: a business can be at 72.5% because customers are not buying, because stock is short, or because a machine is down.

## 2. Operating under maximum capacity

Running well below capacity means the business pays for resources it does not fully use. Likely impacts:

- **Higher unit costs.** Fixed costs (rent, machinery, salaried staff) are spread over fewer units.
- **Lower profit**, because the same overheads are covered by less revenue.
- **Waste of resources**: idle machines, underemployed workers and unused space.
- **Risk to survival** if the position lasts, because costs may stay above the selling price.
- **Possible gains**: spare capacity can absorb a sudden rise in orders, and gives time for maintenance and training.

**Worked example 2.** Ardmoor Kayaks has fixed costs of 360,000 a year and variable costs of 150 a kayak.

| | 1,740 kayaks (72.5%) | 2,100 kayaks (87.5%) |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost per kayak | 206.90 | 171.43 |
| Variable cost per kayak | 150.00 | 150.00 |
| Total cost | 621,000 | 675,000 |
| Unit cost | 356.90 | 321.43 |

Total costs rise by 54,000, but each kayak costs 35.47 less to make. That fall comes entirely from fixed costs being shared out. It only helps if the extra kayaks can be sold.

## 3. Operating over maximum capacity

Output can exceed the planned maximum for a while: overtime, extra shifts, skipped maintenance, faster machine speeds. On the formula this gives utilisation above 100%.

**Worked example 3.** Oakshott Furniture is designed to make 4,800 chairs a month and has been making 5,400. Utilisation = 5,400 ÷ 4,800 × 100 = **112.5%**.

Impacts to consider:

- **Costs**: overtime premiums, wear and repair bills, and emergency hiring.
- **Quality**: rushed work raises defects, rework and complaints.
- **Reliability**: breakdowns become more likely when maintenance is squeezed, so deadlines may be missed.
- **Employees**: fatigue, stress, absence and resignations.
- **Sales**: orders may be refused or delayed, risking customer loyalty.
- **Gains**: extra sales and revenue now, and fixed costs per unit fall further.

Near-full use is often a good sign, but a business at or beyond its limit has no room to cope with a surge or a breakdown. Many firms aim for high but not total use.

## 4. Methods of improving capacity utilisation

The syllabus does not name specific methods, so the groups below are the usual ones. Which applies depends on whether the problem is **spare capacity** (under) or **excess demand** (over).

**If utilisation is too low, raise output (the numerator):**

- Increase demand through promotion, new markets, new products or off-peak offers.
- Use spare capacity to produce for other businesses, such as own-label or contract work.
- Spread demand more evenly, for example with discounts in quiet seasons.

**If utilisation is too low, reduce capacity (the denominator), which is sometimes called rationalisation:**

- Close or sell a production line, rent out unused space, or move to smaller premises.
- Move to flexible or part-time contracts, so that labour capacity follows demand.

**If utilisation is too high:**

- Add capacity through new machines, extra shifts or bigger premises.
- Outsource some work to another business (see section 5).
- Manage demand by lengthening order lead times or raising prices.

**Worked example 4.** Wyndmoor Printing can complete 8,000 print runs a year and completes 5,000 (62.5%). It could close one of its presses, cutting maximum capacity to 6,000 runs. Utilisation becomes 5,000 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **83.3%** without selling a single extra run. The cost is redundancy payments and a lower ceiling if demand later recovers. A promotion that lifted output to 6,000 runs would also reach 75%, but only if customers respond.

## 5. Outsourcing

**Outsourcing** means using another business to carry out an activity or process that the firm could do itself, such as payroll, packaging, deliveries, IT support or manufacturing a component. It is a decision about what to do in-house and what to buy in. The syllabus does not define the term, so this is the standard meaning.

**Possible positive impacts:**

- **Lower or more predictable cost**, if the specialist works at greater scale or the firm avoids buying equipment.
- **Access to expertise** and technology the firm lacks.
- **Focus on core activities**, freeing management time and money.
- **Flexibility and capacity**: the contractor can absorb peaks, so the firm avoids over-investing in its own capacity and may improve its own utilisation.

**Possible negative impacts:**

- **Less control** over quality, speed and confidential information.
- **Dependence** on a supplier whose failures become the firm's failures and whose prices may rise at renewal.
- **Hidden costs** of finding, contracting and monitoring the supplier.
- **Employees**: redundancies, loss of skills and lower morale among those who stay.
- **Reputation**: customers blame the brand, not the contractor, when something goes wrong.

**Worked example 5.** Daleway Bakery packs its own loaves. In-house packing has fixed costs of 48,000 a year and variable cost of 0.30 a pack. A contractor charges 0.62 a pack.

- Break-even volume = 48,000 ÷ (0.62 − 0.30) = **150,000 packs**.

| Packs a year | In-house cost | Outsourced cost | Cheaper option |
|---|---|---|---|
| 120,000 | 84,000 | 74,400 | Outsource |
| 150,000 | 93,000 | 93,000 | Equal |
| 200,000 | 108,000 | 124,000 | In-house |

Cost alone says outsource below 150,000 packs and pack in-house above it. A good answer then adds non-cost factors: Is demand steady? Does the contractor protect freshness and food safety? Is there spare in-house equipment already paid for?

## 6. Writing impact and evaluation answers

The syllabus lists these command words and meanings: **analyse** is to examine in detail to show meaning, identify elements and the relationship between them; **evaluate** is to judge or calculate the quality, importance, amount or value of something; **justify** is to support a case with evidence or argument. For this section:

- **Point**: name the impact (for example, higher unit costs).
- **Application**: use the figures and facts in the stem.
- **Analysis**: show the chain (spare capacity, so fixed costs per unit are higher, so profit margins are squeezed unless prices rise).
- **Evaluation**: weigh it. Useful factors include how long the position lasts, how large the gap is, the nature of the product, the firm's finances, how reliable the contractor is and whether the activity is core.

End an evaluation with a judgement that answers the question, not a summary.

## Common errors

- Dividing maximum output by actual output, which reverses the ratio.
- Mixing periods or units, such as a monthly output against a yearly maximum.
- Saying unit costs fall at higher output without linking this to fixed costs.
- Treating over-capacity as only good news.
- Listing outsourcing advantages with no reference to the business.
- Calling outsourcing "cheaper" without comparing costs at the business's actual volume.
- Confusing outsourcing with simply buying raw materials.
