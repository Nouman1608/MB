## 3.1.1 Layout of the table

| Block | Groups | Sublevel being filled |
|---|---|---|
| s | 1, 2 (and He) | s |
| p | 13 to 18 | p |
| d | 3 to 12 | d |
| f | Lanthanoids, actinoids | f |

- Metals: left and centre. Non-metals: upper right, plus hydrogen.
- Metalloids lie along the stepped boundary: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
- The guide notes that the data booklet includes a periodic table.

## 3.1.2 Position and configuration

- **Period** = outer occupied main level (n).
- **Group** = shared number of valence electrons. Groups 1 and 2: same as group number. Groups 13 to 18: group number minus 10.
- Names to know: alkali metals (group 1), halogens (group 17), noble gases (group 18), transition elements (d-block).

**Method in steps: position to configuration**

1. Read the period: this gives the outer n.
2. Read the block: this tells you which sublevel the last electron enters.
3. Count across the block to place the last electron.
4. Fill in order 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p, and check the total equals Z.

Reminder: calcium (period 4, group 2, s-block) is [Ar] 4s². Krypton (period 4, group 18) is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶. Exceptions up to Z = 36: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

## 3.1.3 Periodic trends

Two explanations do nearly all the work:

- **Across**: more protons, same main level, similar shielding → outer electrons pulled harder.
- **Down**: extra main level, more shielding → outer electrons further away and held less tightly.

| Property | Across a period | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | ↓ | ↑ |
| Ionic radius | Falls along cations, jumps up at the first anion, then falls | ↑ |
| First ionization energy | ↑ (with dips) | ↓ |
| Electron affinity | Generally more exothermic | Generally less exothermic |
| Electronegativity | ↑ | ↓ |

**Definitions**

- **First ionization energy**: the energy, per mole, to take the outermost electron from each atom of a gaseous element in its ground state, giving gaseous 1+ ions.
- **Electron affinity**: the energy change, per mole, as each gaseous atom picks up one electron to become a gaseous 1− ion.
- **Electronegativity**: an atom's ability to attract a bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

**Ions**

- Cation smaller than its atom: one fewer occupied main level.
- Anion larger than its atom: more electron repulsion, same nuclear charge.
- Isoelectronic ions: more protons → smaller. Example: N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ (all 10 electrons).

## 3.1.4 Group 1 and group 17

**Group 1 + water**: 2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g). Alkaline solution; fizzing. Reactivity **increases** down the group as the outer electron is further away, more shielded and lost more easily.

**Group 17 + halide ions**: a halogen oxidizes halide ions of halogens **below** it.

| Halogen added | Cl⁻(aq) | Br⁻(aq) | I⁻(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂(aq) | -- | Orange (Br₂ forms) | Brown (I₂ forms) |
| Br₂(aq) | No reaction | -- | Brown (I₂ forms) |
| I₂(aq) | No reaction | No reaction | -- |

Reactivity **decreases** down the group: a bigger atom attracts an incoming electron less strongly.

Small reminder: Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq).

## 3.1.5 Oxides

Continuum across a period: basic → amphoteric (Al₂O₃) → acidic.

| Oxide | With water |
|---|---|
| Li₂O | Li₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) |
| MgO | MgO(s) + H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(s) (slightly soluble, weakly alkaline) |
| BaO | BaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ba(OH)₂(aq) |
| CO₂ | CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) |
| SO₂ | SO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₃(aq) |
| SO₃ | SO₃(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₄(aq) |

- **Acid rain**: SO₂ and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels dissolve in rain to form acids.
- **Ocean acidification**: extra CO₂ dissolves → H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → pH falls; H⁺ removes CO₃²⁻ as HCO₃⁻, so less carbonate is available for shells.

## 3.1.6 Oxidation states

**Method in steps**

1. Give elements 0.
2. Fix the "certain" atoms: group 1 +1, group 2 +2, F −1, H +1, O −2.
3. Override for exceptions: H −1 in metal hydrides; O −1 in peroxides.
4. Set the sum equal to the overall charge and solve.

Small reminders:

```
I in HIO₃:    (+1) + x + 3(−2) = 0    -> x = +5
N in HNO₂:    (+1) + x + 2(−2) = 0    -> x = +3
```

- Write the sign before the number: +3, not 3+ (3+ is an ion charge).
- An element's oxidation state is zero because bonding between identical atoms involves no electron transfer.
- Oxyanion names: NO₃⁻ nitrate(V)/nitrate, NO₂⁻ nitrate(III)/nitrite, SO₄²⁻ sulfate(VI)/sulfate, SO₃²⁻ sulfate(IV)/sulfite.

## HL only: 3.1.7 Dips in first ionization energy

- Group 2 → 13 (Be→B, Mg→Al): the electron removed is in a p sublevel, higher in energy than the s sublevel, so it is easier to remove.
- Group 15 → 16 (N→O, P→S): the electron removed is paired; repulsion raises its energy, so it is easier to remove.
- Base the answer on the **energy of the electron removed**. Do not rely on "half-filled stability".

## HL only: 3.1.8 Transition element properties

Variable oxidation states · high melting points · magnetic properties · catalytic properties · coloured compounds · complex ions with ligands.

- Cause: an incomplete d-sublevel.
- Zn excluded: 3d¹⁰ in the atom and in Zn²⁺.
- Scandium: debated (Sc³⁺ has no d electrons).
- Ligand: a molecule or ion with a lone pair that it donates to the central ion.

## HL only: 3.1.9 Variable oxidation states

- 4s and 3d are close in energy → successive ionization energies rise steadily, without the large jump a group 2 element shows after two electrons.
- **Ions: 4s out first.**

| Ion | Configuration |
|---|---|
| Ti²⁺ | [Ar] 3d² |
| Mn³⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁴ |
| Co³⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁶ |
| Cu²⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁹ |

## HL only: 3.1.10 Colour

1. Ligands split the d orbitals into two sets.
2. An electron absorbs visible light of energy equal to the gap and is promoted.
3. The colour seen is the **complementary** colour, opposite on the colour wheel.
4. Use c = λf to convert. Put λ in metres.

The guide says the data booklet supplies both the colour wheel and c = λf. Approximate pairs: red ↔ green, orange ↔ blue, yellow ↔ violet.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Group** (column, valence electrons) vs **period** (row, outer main level).
- **Electron affinity** (energy change when gaseous atoms gain electrons, kJ mol⁻¹) vs **electronegativity** (pull on a shared pair; no units).
- **Halogen** (the element, Cl₂) vs **halide** (the ion, Cl⁻).
- **Oxidation state** (+2, sign first) vs **ion charge** (2+, number first).
- (HL) **Colour absorbed** vs **colour seen**.

## Quick self-test

1. State the period, group and block of the element with configuration [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁴, and name it.
2. How many valence electrons does the element in period 3, group 13 have?
3. Which is larger, a chlorine atom or a chloride ion? Give a reason.
4. Write an equation for lithium reacting with water.
5. Aqueous chlorine is added to potassium bromide solution. State the colour change and write the ionic equation.
6. Write an equation for sulfur trioxide reacting with water.
7. Deduce the oxidation state of phosphorus in P₂O₇⁴⁻ and of nitrogen in N₂F₄.
8. Give the systematic name of NaClO.
9. (HL) Why is the first ionization energy of aluminium lower than that of magnesium?
10. (HL) Write the configurations of Ni²⁺ and Cr³⁺.
11. (HL) A complex absorbs at 440 nm. State the colour seen and calculate the frequency absorbed (c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹).
12. (HL) A complex absorbs light of frequency 6.00 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹. Find the wavelength and the colour seen.

### Answers

1. Period 4, group 16, p-block: selenium (Z = 34).
2. Three (13 − 10).
3. The chloride ion: one extra electron adds repulsion with the same 17 protons.
4. 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g)
5. Pale green/colourless to orange; Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq).
6. SO₃(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₄(aq)
7. P: 2x + 7(−2) = −4, so +5. N: 2x + 4(−1) = 0, so +2.
8. Sodium chlorate(I).
9. Aluminium's electron is removed from 3p, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s electron.
10. Ni²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁸; Cr³⁺ [Ar] 3d³.
11. 440 nm is violet, so yellow is seen. f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / (440 × 10⁻⁹) = 6.82 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹.
12. λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 6.00 × 10¹⁴ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm, green absorbed, so red is seen.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a group 15 element has 15 valence electrons (it has 5).
- Explaining a trend with "more protons" alone, without the same-level or extra-level shielding point.
- Describing the halogen colour you add instead of the colour of the halogen that forms.
- Missing state symbols, or not balancing group 1 + water equations as 2 : 2 → 2 : 1.
- Showing the CO₂ and water reaction as going to completion, or leaving out the carbonate step in ocean acidification.
- Forgetting that metal hydrides put hydrogen at −1 and peroxides put oxygen at −1.
- Writing an oxidation state as "2+" rather than "+2".
- (HL) Using "half-filled stability" for the group 15 to 16 dip.
- (HL) Taking 3d electrons out before 4s.
- (HL) Leaving λ in nanometres in c = λf.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between electron affinity and electronegativity?**
Electron affinity is the energy change, per mole, when gaseous atoms each gain one electron. Electronegativity is an atom's ability to attract a bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

**Why is aluminium's first ionization energy lower than magnesium's?**
Aluminium's outer electron is in a 3p sublevel, which is higher in energy than magnesium's 3s sublevel, so it is easier to remove. This is HL content in Structure 3.1.7.

**How do you work out an element's group from its valence electrons?**
For groups 1 and 2 the number of valence electrons equals the group number. For groups 13 to 18 it is the group number minus 10, so a group 15 element has 5 valence electrons.

**Why are isoelectronic ions different sizes?**
Isoelectronic ions have the same number of electrons, so the ion with more protons pulls them in more strongly and is smaller. For example, N³⁻ is larger than O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺.
