## Questions

**1.** A customer rating is recorded as poor, fair, good or excellent. P(poor) = 0.08, P(fair) = 0.22 and P(good) = 0.45.

**(a)** Find P(excellent). **[1]**
**(b)** Find the probability that a rating is fair or good, and explain why you may add. **[2]**

**2.** A and B are independent events with P(A) = 0.35 and P(B) = 0.6.

**(a)** Find P(A ∩ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[2]**
**(c)** Find the probability that A and B both fail to occur. **[1]**

**3.** In a sample of 160 adults, 96 use a fitness app (A), 40 are aged over 50 (B) and 24 are both.

**(a)** Show that A and B are independent. **[3]**
**(b)** In a second sample of 160 adults, the counts for A and B are the same, but 30 are both. Explain whether A and B are independent in this sample. **[2]**

**4.** Events C and D are mutually exclusive with P(C) = 0.5 and P(D) = 0.2.

**(a)** Find P(C ∪ D). **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why C and D cannot be independent. **[2]**

**5.** A gym has 90 members. The table shows their usual session time and type of membership.

| | Adult | Student |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | 24 | 11 |
| Evening | 31 | 24 |

A member is chosen at random.

**(a)** Find the probability that the member is a student. **[1]**
**(b)** Given that the member attends in the evening, find the probability they are a student. **[2]**
**(c)** Given that the member is a student, find the probability they attend in the evening. **[2]**

**6.** A box holds 15 chocolates: 6 dark, 5 milk and 4 white. Chocolates are taken at random and not replaced. The first chocolate taken is dark.

**(a)** Find the probability that the second chocolate is also dark. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that the second chocolate is not milk. **[2]**

**7.** To gain a licence, a candidate takes a theory test and a practical test. The probability of passing the theory test is 0.75 and of passing the practical test is 0.6. The results are independent.

**(a)** Draw a tree diagram showing all four outcomes and their probabilities. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability of passing both tests. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the probability of passing exactly one test. **[2]**
**(d)** Find the probability of passing at least one test. **[1]**

**8.** A bag contains 8 tiles: 3 show a vowel and 5 show a consonant. Two tiles are taken at random without replacement.

**(a)** Draw a tree diagram with the probability on every branch. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that both tiles show consonants. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the probability that one shows a vowel and one a consonant. **[2]**
**(d)** Find the probability that at least one shows a vowel. **[1]**

**9.** Machine A makes 60% of the phones in a factory and machine B makes the rest. 3% of phones from A are defective and 8% of phones from B are defective. One phone is chosen at random.

**(a)** Draw a tree diagram for this information. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that the phone is defective. **[2]**
**(c)** The phone is found to be defective. Find the probability it came from machine B. **[3]**

**10.** In each of three rounds of a quiz game, a player answers correctly with probability 0.6, independently of the other rounds.

**(a)** Find the probability that she answers all three rounds correctly. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the probability that she answers exactly two rounds correctly. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the probability that she answers at least one round correctly. **[2]**
**(d)** To qualify she needs at least two correct answers. Given that she qualifies, find the probability that she answered all three correctly. **[3]**

**11.** Bag P holds 4 red and 6 white balls. Bag Q holds 5 red and 3 white balls. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, a ball is taken at random from bag P. If it lands tails, a ball is taken at random from bag Q.

**(a)** Draw a tree diagram for this experiment. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that the ball is red. **[2]**
**(c)** The ball is red. Find the probability that it came from bag P. **[3]**
**(d)** By comparing P(heads and red) with P(heads) × P(red), state whether "the coin lands heads" and "the ball is red" are independent. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 1 - 0.08 - 0.22 - 0.45 = **0.25** [1]
**(b)** The ratings fair and good cannot both be recorded for one customer, so they are mutually exclusive and the probabilities add. [1] 0.22 + 0.45 = **0.67** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming the reason for adding (a customer cannot give two ratings) is advice worth following whenever a question says "explain why", because the answer then shows the idea and not just the sum.

**2. (a)** P(A ∩ B) = 0.35 × 0.6 = **0.21** [1]
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.35 + 0.6 - 0.21 [1] = **0.74** [1]
**(c)** 1 - 0.74 = **0.26** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Writing the subtraction of the overlap as a separate line makes the method clear.

**3. (a)** P(A) = 96/160 = 0.6 and P(B) = 40/160 = 0.25 [1] P(A ∩ B) = 24/160 = 0.15 [1] P(A) × P(B) = 0.6 × 0.25 = 0.15, which equals P(A ∩ B), so A and B are independent [1]
**(b)** P(A ∩ B) = 30/160 = 0.1875 [1] This differs from 0.6 × 0.25 = 0.15, so A and B are not independent in this sample [1]
*Examiner insight:* For a "show that" question, state all three probabilities, the product, and the comparison. Stopping at the numbers without a conclusion leaves the argument unfinished.

**4. (a)** 0.5 + 0.2 = **0.7** [1]
**(b)** Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen, so P(C ∩ D) = 0 [1] For independence it would need to be 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1, which is not 0, so C and D are not independent [1]
*Examiner insight:* A convincing answer gives both numbers, 0 and 0.1, and compares them.

**5. (a)** 35/90 = **7/18** [1]
**(b)** The evening group has 31 + 24 = 55 members [1] P(student | evening) = 24/55 [1] So **24/55**
**(c)** The student group has 11 + 24 = 35 members [1] P(evening | student) = 24/35 [1] So **24/35**
*Examiner insight:* Underline the word after "given" and use that row or column total as the denominator. Using 90 for every part is the usual loss in these questions.

**6. (a)** After one dark chocolate is removed, 14 remain, 5 of them dark [1] P(dark) = **5/14** [1]
**(b)** 5 milk of 14 remain, so not milk is 14 - 5 = 9 out of 14 [1] **9/14** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Stating the new total and the new count (14 and 5) shows the idea of conditional probability and is the working worth setting out clearly.

**7. (a)** Theory branches 0.75 pass and 0.25 fail [1] From each, practical branches 0.6 pass and 0.4 fail, with the four products 0.45, 0.30, 0.15 and 0.10 [1]

```
Theory         Practical       Outcome        Probability
               0.6 -- Pass     Pass, Pass     0.45
   0.75 -- Pass
               0.4 -- Fail     Pass, Fail     0.30
Start
               0.6 -- Pass     Fail, Pass     0.15
   0.25 -- Fail
               0.4 -- Fail     Fail, Fail     0.10
```

**(b)** 0.75 × 0.6 = **0.45** [1]
**(c)** 0.75 × 0.4 + 0.25 × 0.6 [1] = 0.30 + 0.15 = **0.45** [1]
**(d)** 1 - 0.25 × 0.4 = 1 - 0.1 = **0.9** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Label every branch with its probability and check that each pair adds to 1. A clearly labelled tree also makes your later working easier to follow.

**8. (a)** First stage: vowel 3/8, consonant 5/8 [1] Second stage: after a vowel, vowel 2/7 and consonant 5/7; after a consonant, vowel 3/7 and consonant 4/7 [1]
**(b)** 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 = **5/14** [1]
**(c)** 3/8 × 5/7 + 5/8 × 3/7 [1] = 15/56 + 15/56 = 30/56 = **15/28** [1]
**(d)** 1 - 5/14 = **9/14** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The denominators at the second stage must be 7. Check that the four outcomes sum to 1: 6/56 + 15/56 + 15/56 + 20/56 = 56/56.

**9. (a)** First stage: A with 0.6 and B with 0.4 [1] Second stage: A then defective 0.03 and not defective 0.97; B then defective 0.08 and not defective 0.92 [1]
**(b)** 0.6 × 0.03 + 0.4 × 0.08 [1] = 0.018 + 0.032 = **0.05** [1]
**(c)** Only the defective paths are possible, with probabilities 0.018 (machine A) and 0.032 (machine B) out of 0.05 in total [1] P(B | defective) = 0.032/0.05 [1] = **0.64** (or 16/25) [1]
*Examiner insight:* In a "given" question, say which paths remain and divide by their combined probability.

**10. (a)** 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 = **0.216** [1]
**(b)** One wrong answer in each of three positions, so 3 × 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.4 [1] = **0.432** [1]
**(c)** 1 - P(none) = 1 - 0.4³ [1] = 1 - 0.064 = **0.936** [1]
**(d)** P(qualifies) = 0.432 + 0.216 = 0.648 [1] Only these paths are possible, so P(all three | qualifies) = 0.216/0.648 [1] = **1/3** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Counting the three orders for "exactly two" is a step that is easy to forget. Listing the three orders takes seconds.

**11. (a)** Heads 1/2 and tails 1/2 [1] From heads: red 4/10, white 6/10. From tails: red 5/8, white 3/8 [1]
**(b)** 1/2 × 4/10 + 1/2 × 5/8 [1] = 0.2 + 0.3125 = 0.5125 = **41/80** [1]
**(c)** P(heads and red) = 0.2 [1] P(from P | red) = 0.2/0.5125 [1] = **16/41** (about 0.390) [1]
**(d)** P(heads) × P(red) = 0.5 × 0.5125 = 0.25625 [1] This is not equal to P(heads and red) = 0.2, so the events are **not independent** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A written conclusion that links the two numbers to the word "independent" is better practice than two numbers left side by side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding the probabilities of events that can occur together.
- Multiplying for events that are linked, such as draws without replacement.
- Keeping the denominator at the original total after items are removed.
- Dividing by 90 or 120 when the question names a smaller group.
- Not stating a conclusion after an independence test.
- Forgetting the mixed paths when finding "exactly one" or "exactly two".
- Leaving branches that do not sum to 1.
