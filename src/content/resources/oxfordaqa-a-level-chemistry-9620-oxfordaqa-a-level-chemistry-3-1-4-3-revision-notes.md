These notes condense **3.1.4.3 Applications of Hess's law** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. For cycles drawn out in full, read the [Hess's law study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-3/); for exam-style questions, use the [Hess's law practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-3-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **Hess's law**: the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
- It is a consequence of the **conservation of energy**.
- It lets you calculate enthalpy changes that **cannot be measured directly**.
- The specification expects calculations from **enthalpies of formation** and from **enthalpies of combustion**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hess's law | ΔH for a reaction is independent of route, for the same initial and final conditions |
| Energy (Hess) cycle | A diagram showing a direct route and an indirect route between the same reactants and products |
| ΔfH° | Enthalpy change forming one mole of a compound from its elements, standard conditions and states |
| ΔcH° | Enthalpy change when one mole of a substance burns completely in oxygen, standard conditions and states |
| Standard state | Physical state under standard conditions (100 kPa, stated temperature) |

## The two formulae

| Data type | Bottom of the cycle | Arrow direction | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formation | Elements | Up | ΔrH = ΣΔfH(products) − ΣΔfH(reactants) |
| Combustion | CO₂ and H₂O | Down | ΔrH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΣΔcH(products) |

Memory aid: formation is "products minus reactants"; combustion is the reverse.

## Rules for every calculation

1. Multiply each ΔH by the **balancing number** in the target equation.
2. ΔfH of an **element** in its standard state = **0**.
3. Reversing a step **changes the sign**.
4. Match **state symbols**: H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) have different values.
5. Put negative numbers in brackets when subtracting.

## Combining equations

- Write the target equation.
- Arrange the given equations so each substance is on the correct side and in the correct amount.
- Reverse (change sign) or multiply (multiply ΔH) as needed.
- Add the ΔH values; check that unwanted species cancel.

## Worked reminders (data supplied)

- **Formation data.** 2H₂S(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2SO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l); ΔfH: H₂S −21, SO₂ −297, H₂O(l) −286. ΔrH = [2(−297) + 2(−286)] − [2(−21)] = −1166 + 42 = **−1124 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- **Combustion data.** ΔfH of ethanol from ΔcH: C −394, H₂ −286, C₂H₅OH −1367. ΔfH = [2(−394) + 3(−286)] − (−1367) = −1646 + 1367 = **−279 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- **Equations.** S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g), −297; 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g), −198. ΔfH(SO₃) = −297 + (−198 ÷ 2) = **−396 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## Drawing a cycle step by step

**Formation cycle**

1. Write the target equation across the top, with state symbols.
2. Below it, write the elements in their standard states, balanced to match both sides.
3. Draw arrows **up** from the elements to the reactants and to the products.
4. Label each arrow with the sum of ΔfH values, including multipliers.

**Combustion cycle**

1. Write the target equation across the top.
2. Below it, write the combustion products (CO₂(g) and H₂O(l)) with the O₂ needed.
3. Draw arrows **down** from the reactants and from the products.
4. Label each arrow with the sum of ΔcH values, including multipliers.

## Sense checks on an answer

- Combustion enthalpies are always negative; a calculated ΔcH that comes out positive signals a slip.
- If the target reaction is a formation reaction, the answer must be per **one mole** of the compound.
- Re-adding the steps around the cycle should return zero: direct route = indirect route.
- An answer much larger than any data value usually means a missed bracket in a double negative.

## Exam traps

- Using "products − reactants" with combustion data.
- Forgetting to multiply (for example 3 × ΔcH(H₂)).
- Including O₂ with a non-zero formation value.
- Not halving a ΔH when only half the equation is needed.
- Sign errors from subtracting a negative.
- A cycle with arrows unlabelled or pointing the wrong way.

## Self-test

1. State Hess's law.
2. Which physical law does Hess's law follow from?
3. Give the formula for ΔrH from enthalpies of formation.
4. Give the formula for ΔrH from enthalpies of combustion.
5. What is ΔfH° for O₂(g)?
6. Reaction A has ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔH for the reverse reaction?
7. Calculate ΔH for CaO(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s). ΔfH: CaO −635, CO₂ −394, CaCO₃ −1207 kJ mol⁻¹.
8. Calculate ΔfH of methane. ΔcH: C −394, H₂ −286, CH₄ −890 kJ mol⁻¹.
9. Why can ΔfH of methane not be measured directly?
10. Calculate ΔH for 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). ΔfH: NO +90, NO₂ +33 kJ mol⁻¹.
11. C(graphite) → C(diamond). ΔcH: graphite −394, diamond −396 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH.
12. In a combustion cycle, where do the arrows point?

### Answers

1. The enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
2. The law of conservation of energy.
3. ΔrH = ΣΔfH(products) − ΣΔfH(reactants).
4. ΔrH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΣΔcH(products).
5. Zero.
6. +120 kJ mol⁻¹.
7. −1207 − (−635 + −394) = −1207 + 1029 = **−178 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. [−394 + 2(−286)] − (−890) = −966 + 890 = **−76 kJ mol⁻¹**.
9. Carbon and hydrogen do not react directly to give methane under standard conditions.
10. 2(+33) − 2(+90) = 66 − 180 = **−114 kJ mol⁻¹**.
11. −394 − (−396) = **+2 kJ mol⁻¹**.
12. Down, from reactants and products to the combustion products (CO₂ and H₂O).
