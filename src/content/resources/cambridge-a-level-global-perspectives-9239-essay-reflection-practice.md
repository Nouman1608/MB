These questions practise the two AO2 points in Component 2, the Essay, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028: "reflect on the impact of alternative perspectives on their viewpoint" and "suggest further relevant research". For the essay as a whole, see the [Component 2 Essay study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-essay/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Essay is coursework, so these questions test the reflective skills behind it rather than imitating an exam paper. All essay extracts below are invented for practice. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [essay reflection study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-reflection/) and the [essay reflection revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-reflection-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the two AO2 assessment points in the Essay. **[2]**

**2.** State the AO2 weighting in Component 2 and in Component 1. **[2]**

**3.** Name the three ways a candidate's view can move as a result of research. **[3]**

**4.** Give **two** of the Critical Path's reflection questions. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A candidate ends her essay on "Should wealthy countries cancel the debts of the poorest countries?" with: "Doing this essay was really interesting and I learned a lot about debt. Now I can see both sides of the argument. More research is needed."

**(a)** Identify **two** weaknesses in this reflection. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite it as effective reflection, inventing reasonable details about her research. **[4]**

**6.** For each extract, state whether the writer's view was strengthened, qualified or reversed, and explain how you can tell. **[3]**

- **Extract A:** "I expected the economic case for tourist taxes to be weak, but the evidence on funding for local conservation persuaded me that they are justified."
- **Extract B:** "I still believe social media companies should remove harmful content, but the free-expression argument convinced me this should apply only to content that is illegal."
- **Extract C:** "The strongest argument against space funding was the opportunity cost, but it assumed the money would otherwise go to health care, which my sources showed it rarely does, so my support is unchanged."

**7.** A candidate writes: "A limit of my research was that I did not have time to read everything." Explain why this is a weak statement of a limit, and rewrite it. **[3]**

**8.** Evaluate these two suggestions for further research for the essay question "Should cities ban new fossil-fuel cars by 2035?" **[4]**

- **Suggestion A:** "I would like to interview more people about this."
- **Suggestion B:** "My sources came mainly from European cities with strong public transport. Surveys of commuters in cities with limited public transport would show whether a ban would leave people without a practical alternative; if so, my conclusion should apply only where good public transport exists."

**9.** A candidate's essay asks "Should international sports bodies refuse to award events to countries with poor labour standards?" Her sources were mostly reports by the sports bodies themselves. Write one suggestion for further relevant research that addresses this limit. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why an invented change of mind is weaker reflection than an honest account of a view that was strengthened. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate puts all her reflection in the final sentence of the essay. Explain **two** benefits of placing some reflection earlier. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the following conclusion as reflection. **[6]**

> "At the start of my research I thought fast-food advertising aimed at children should be banned completely. The industry perspective, that parents not advertisers are responsible for children's diets, did not change my view, because the studies I read showed advertising influences children's food requests even when parents set rules. However, I found little evidence from lower-income countries. Therefore, a complete ban is the right answer everywhere."

## Answers

**1.** "Reflect on the impact of alternative perspectives on their viewpoint" [1]; "suggest further relevant research" [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Component 2: 22% [1]. Component 1: 0% [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Strengthened [1]; qualified [1]; reversed [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any two: "What was your personal viewpoint before carrying out research?"; "How does your own viewpoint relate to the perspectives identified in the research?"; "What impact has the research had on any views you had before?"; "Why has the research had an effect on your previous views?"; "What additional research might be useful?" [1] [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Any two: it describes feelings rather than the impact of a perspective [1]; it gives no starting view, so no change can be seen [1]; "both sides" replaces a personal, supported view [1]; "more research is needed" is not specific or relevant [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Credit a rewrite that includes: a starting view [1]; the perspective that challenged it and the reason [1]; the resulting view, stated clearly [1]; a specific, relevant suggestion for further research [1]. For example: "I began by thinking debt cancellation was simply fair. The argument that cancellation without conditions can reward poor governance made me reconsider, because two of my sources showed money freed by earlier relief being spent unevenly. I now support cancellation linked to transparent spending plans. Studies of spending in countries that received relief would test whether such conditions work." **[4]**

**6.** Extract A: reversed, because the writer expected one view and was persuaded of the opposite by named evidence [1]. Extract B: qualified, because the view is kept but limited to a narrower condition [1]. Extract C: strengthened, because the writer met the strongest opposing argument and explains why it failed [1]. **[3]**

**7.** It describes the writer's circumstances, not a gap in the evidence, so it does not tell the reader how far to trust the conclusion [1]. Rewrite, for example: "My sources were mostly from high-income countries [1], so my conclusion that the policy reduces inequality is more secure for those countries than for lower-income ones [1]." **[3]**

**8.** Suggestion A is weak: it does not say who would be interviewed, what gap this fills, or how the results could change the judgement [1] [1]. Suggestion B is strong: it names a specific kind of evidence and the limit it addresses, and explains how a result would qualify the conclusion [1]. Judgement: B meets the what-why-change test and is relevant to the question; A does not [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Credit a suggestion that names specific evidence [1], says it fills the gap left by relying on the sports bodies' own reports [1], and explains how it could change the judgement [1]. For example: "Reports by independent labour-rights organisations and interviews with construction workers at past venues would test the sports bodies' claims about improved conditions; if conditions did not improve after events were awarded, the case for refusing awards would be stronger." **[3]**

**10.** Reflection is assessed on evaluating the impact of perspectives, not on how much the view moved [1]. An invented reversal usually lacks a convincing reason, which is the part of reflection that earns most [1]. An honest strengthened view explains why the strongest opposing argument failed, which shows real engagement with it [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Any two, 2 marks each: a starting view in the introduction gives a baseline against which change can be judged [1] [1]; reflective sentences within the evaluation show the effect of a perspective at the point it is weighed [1] [1]; spreading reflection allows it to be developed in more depth than one sentence permits [1] [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Strength: a clear starting view is stated [1].
- Strength: it names the alternative perspective and gives a specific evidence-based reason why it did not change the view [1].
- Strength: it identifies a real limit, the lack of evidence from lower-income countries [1].
- Weakness: the conclusion ignores that limit; "everywhere" claims more than the evidence supports [1].
- Weakness: no further research is suggested, although the limit points directly to one [1].
- Judgement: good reflection on the impact of a perspective, but the final judgement is not adjusted for the stated limit, so the reflection on limits is incomplete [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Rewrites that add detail but still lack the reason a view moved.
- Calling a narrowed view "reversed" because the writer sounds less certain.
- Limits that describe effort or time rather than gaps in evidence.
- Further research that is specific but not relevant to the essay question.
- Spotting a limit and then concluding as if it did not exist.
