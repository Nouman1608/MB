These questions test **Structure 2.4, From models to materials**, understandings 2.4.1 to 2.4.6, from the IB *Chemistry guide* for the Diploma Programme (issued February 2023; first assessment 2025). Questions 1 to 8 and 11 are for SL and HL. Questions 9, 10 and 12 test Structure 2.4.6, which is additional higher level, so they are labelled **HL only**.

Work through the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-chemistry-from-models-to-materials/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-chemistry-from-models-to-materials-revision-notes/) first. Questions 11 and 12 combine several understandings. Other units sit on the [course page](/boards/ib/ib-dp/chemistry/); log progress on the [syllabus checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/chemistry/).

Electronegativities are Pauling values given in each question. For masses, take C = 12.01, H = 1.01, O = 16.00, N = 14.01 and Cl = 35.45.

## Questions

**1.** Aluminium oxide is used to make the insulating body of spark plugs. Using a bonding model, explain why this oxide melts only at a very high temperature and is a non-conductor in the solid state. **[3]**

**2.** Use these electronegativity values: Ca 1.00, O 3.44, C 2.55, S 2.58, Ni 1.91, Al 1.61.

**(a)** Calculate the electronegativity difference and the average electronegativity for CaO, CS₂ and NiAl. **[3]**
**(b)** State the region of the bonding triangle in which each compound lies. **[2]**

**3.** Use these electronegativity values: Si 1.90, Cl 3.16, Mg 1.31, F 3.98.

**(a)** Calculate the electronegativity difference and the average electronegativity for SiCl₄ and MgF₂. **[2]**
**(b)** Use their positions in the bonding triangle to predict which has the higher melting point and which conducts when molten. **[3]**

**4.** Cupronickel, an alloy of copper and nickel, is used for coins.

**(a)** Define the term alloy. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why cupronickel is described as a mixture rather than a compound. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of non-directional bonding, why cupronickel is harder than pure copper but still conducts electricity. **[3]**

**5.** Poly(ethene) is a plastic made of long hydrocarbon chains. Explain, in terms of its structure, why poly(ethene):

**(a)** is an electrical insulator **[1]**
**(b)** softens when heated **[2]**
**(c)** persists for a long time in the environment **[1]**

**6.** Classify each of these polymers as natural or synthetic: cellulose, nylon, DNA, poly(chloroethene), natural rubber, a polyester. **[2]**

**7.** 1-Chloropropene, CH₃CH=CHCl, undergoes addition polymerization.

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit. **[2]**
**(b)** State the atom economy of the polymerization and explain your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** A sample of the polymer has an average molar mass of 2.68 × 10⁵ g mol⁻¹. Calculate the average number of monomer units per chain. **[2]**

**8.** A section of an addition polymer is shown.

–CH₂–C(CH₃)(CN)–CH₂–C(CH₃)(CN)–CH₂–C(CH₃)(CN)–

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce the structural formula of the monomer and name it. **[2]**

**9.** (HL only) 5-Aminopentanoic acid, H₂N(CH₂)₄COOH, polymerizes by condensation with itself.

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit formed. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the type of link formed and the small molecule released. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the atom economy for the formation of the polymer. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only)

**(a)** Hydroxyethanoic acid, HOCH₂COOH, forms a polyester. Draw its repeating unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Another polyester has the repeating unit –[–O(CH₂)₃O–CO(CH₂)₂CO–]–ₙ. Deduce the structural formulas of its two monomers. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why polyesters can be broken down by hydrolysis but poly(ethene) cannot. **[2]**

**11.** A kettle has a stainless steel body, a plastic handle and an aluminium oxide insulator round its heating element. Use these electronegativity values: Al 1.61, O 3.44, Ti 1.54, Cl 3.16.

**(a)** Calculate the electronegativity difference and the average electronegativity for Al₂O₃. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the position of Al₂O₃ in the bonding triangle and predict two of its properties that suit it to this use. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why stainless steel is chosen for the body rather than pure iron, and why it still conducts heat. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the handle is an electrical insulator, and why it could soften if it became too hot. **[2]**
**(e)** TiCl₄ has an electronegativity difference only a little smaller than that of Al₂O₃, yet it is a liquid at room temperature. Suggest what this shows about discrete bonding categories. **[1]**

**12.** (HL only)

**(a)** State the type of reaction by which all biological macromolecules form. **[1]**
**(b)** A polypeptide contains 85 amino acid residues. Deduce the number of water molecules released when it forms, and calculate the mass of water released when 1 mol of these chains forms. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of glucose formed by complete hydrolysis of 4.86 g of cellulose, taking each unit in the chain as C₆H₁₀O₅. **[3]**
**(d)** State two differences between addition and condensation polymerization. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Ionic lattice of Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions [1]. Highly charged ions attract strongly, so much energy is needed to separate them: **very high melting point** [1]. Ions are fixed in the lattice with no mobile charges: **no conduction as a solid** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Strong bonds" alone earns little; credit comes from naming the ions and their electrostatic attraction.

**2. (a)** CaO: Δχ = **2.44**, average = **2.22** [1]. CS₂: Δχ = **0.03**, average = **2.565** [1]. NiAl: Δχ = **0.30**, average = **1.76** [1].
**(b)** CaO **ionic**; CS₂ **covalent** [1]; NiAl **metallic** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use one value per element. Multiplying the sulfur value by two for CS₂ gives a wrong average and loses the mark.

**3. (a)** SiCl₄: Δχ = **1.26**, average = **2.53** [1]. MgF₂: Δχ = **2.67**, average = **2.645** [1].
**(b)** MgF₂ lies much higher, in the ionic region: a lattice of ions with strong attraction [1]. **MgF₂ has the higher melting point** [1]. SiCl₄ is lower, polar covalent and molecular; **only MgF₂ conducts when molten**, as its ions become mobile [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each prediction to the triangle position; "MgF₂ is ionic" with no reference to Δχ is incomplete.

**4. (a)** A metal mixed with one or more further elements, metallic or non-metallic [1].
**(b)** Its composition can vary; there is no fixed formula [1].
**(c)** Non-directional metallic bonding lets nickel atoms take places in the copper lattice [1]. Being a different size, they distort the layers, which slide less easily: **harder** [1]. Mobile delocalized electrons remain: **still conducts** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "The alloy has stronger bonds" does not explain hardness; credit goes to the idea that layers of cations can no longer slide easily.

**5. (a)** All electrons are in localized covalent bonds; there are no mobile ions or delocalized electrons [1].
**(b)** Chains are held together only by London dispersion forces [1]. Heating overcomes these so chains move past each other; covalent bonds in the chains do not break [1].
**(c)** The C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar, so the chains are unreactive and break down very slowly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying the plastic softens because "bonds break" is wrong unless you specify intermolecular forces, not covalent bonds.

**6.** Natural: **cellulose, DNA, natural rubber**. Synthetic: **nylon, poly(chloroethene), polyester**. [1] for four or five correct; [1] for all six correct. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Where a mark is shared across a list, every item usually needs to be correct for full credit, so check each one.

**7. (a)** Two-carbon backbone, single bond, each carbon keeping its groups: –CH(CH₃)–CHCl– [1]; brackets, continuation bonds and n: **–[–CH(CH₃)–CHCl–]–ₙ** [1].
**(b)** **100%** [1]. The polymer is the only product; every atom of the monomer is in the polymer [1].
**(c)** M(C₃H₅Cl) = 3(12.01) + 5(1.01) + 35.45 = 76.53 g mol⁻¹ [1]. n = 2.68 × 10⁵ / 76.53 = **3.50 × 10³** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Both methyl and chlorine are side groups here; drawing CH₃ as part of the backbone gives a chain that does not match the monomer.

**8. (a)** **–[–CH₂–C(CH₃)(CN)–]–ₙ** [1].
**(b)** **CH₂=C(CH₃)CN** [1]; **2-methylpropenenitrile** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A monomer is a molecule, so continuation bonds must not appear in its formula.

**9. (a)** Remove H from –NH₂ and OH from –COOH and join the N to the C=O [1]: **–[–NH(CH₂)₄CO–]–ₙ** [1].
**(b)** **Amide** link [1]; **water** [1].
**(c)** M(monomer, C₅H₁₁NO₂) = 117.17 g mol⁻¹; M(repeating unit) = 117.17 − 18.02 = 99.15 g mol⁻¹ [1]. Atom economy = 99.15 / 117.17 × 100 = **84.6%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* One monomer with both groups gives one residue per repeating unit; two residues is not the simplest unit.

**10. (a)** H from –OH and OH from –COOH lost as water; ester link formed [1]: **–[–OCH₂CO–]–ₙ** [1].
**(b)** **HO(CH₂)₃OH** (propane-1,3-diol) [1]; **HOOC(CH₂)₂COOH** (butanedioic acid) [1].
**(c)** Polyesters contain polar ester links that water can attack, splitting the chain [1]. Poly(ethene) offers water no polar link to attack: its bonds are all C–C or C–H [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* When deducing monomers, add H₂O back at every link: each –O– gains H and each C=O gains OH.

**11. (a)** Δχ = 3.44 − 1.61 = **1.83** [1]; average = (1.61 + 3.44) / 2 = **2.525** [1].
**(b)** High in the triangle, in or near the ionic region, with some covalent character [1]. Predicted high melting point and no electrical conduction as a solid [1].
**(c)** Other atoms distort the iron layers so they slide less easily (stronger, harder), and it resists rusting [1]. Delocalized electrons remain, so it still conducts heat [1].
**(d)** No mobile charged particles in the plastic [1]. Heating overcomes the intermolecular forces between chains, so they can slide [1].
**(e)** TiCl₄ (Δχ = 1.62, average 2.35) sits close to Al₂O₃ yet is molecular, so bonding changes gradually and discrete categories cannot describe every compound [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Each property needs a structural reason; a bare list earns little.

**12. (a)** **Condensation** [1].
**(b)** Number of links = 85 − 1 = **84** water molecules [1]; mass = 84 × 18.02 = **1.51 × 10³ g** [1].
**(c)** M(C₆H₁₀O₅) = 162.16 g mol⁻¹; moles of units = 4.86 / 162.16 = 0.02997 mol [1]. Each unit adds one H₂O to give glucose, M = 180.18 g mol⁻¹ [1]. Mass of glucose = 0.02997 × 180.18 = **5.40 g** [1].
**(d)** Addition gives no other product; condensation releases a small molecule such as water [1]. Addition needs a C=C in the monomer; condensation needs two functional groups per monomer [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Hydrolysis adds water, so the glucose mass must exceed the cellulose mass; a smaller answer means the ratio is upside down.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying an electronegativity by a subscript when finding Δχ or the average.
- Explaining alloy hardness through "stronger bonds" instead of layers that cannot slide.
- Saying covalent bonds break when a plastic softens.
- Drawing a side group of an addition polymer in the main chain.
- Leaving continuation bonds on a monomer, or leaving them off a repeating unit.
- (HL only) Keeping the H or OH that should have been lost at each condensation link.
- (HL only) Counting N water molecules instead of N − 1 for a chain of N units.
