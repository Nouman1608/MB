**This guide covers one requirement of Paper 1 Section A in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Language (9270).** The specification says that students "will be given a single text for close
study. The text will be divided into sections with questions. At least one question will relate to
the whole text." The [Section A literary non-fiction study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-literary-non-fiction/)
gives one line of advice on this question; this page explains what makes a whole-text question
different from a section question, how to prepare for it while reading, and how to build an answer
that covers the text without retelling it.

## How a whole-text question differs

Most Section A questions point you to one section. The whole-text question removes that boundary,
and that changes what is being tested.

| Section question | Whole-text question |
|---|---|
| Close analysis of a short stretch of text | Selection from the full text |
| Evidence from one place | Evidence from beginning, middle and end |
| Effects of particular words and sentences | How ideas, attitudes or impressions **develop** |
| Mainly R1 and R4 | R2 (synthesis), R3 (sustained interpretation) and R4 (structure and evaluation) |

R2 asks you to "collate and synthesise detail"; R3 asks you to "develop and sustain interpretations";
R4 includes the word "evaluate". The whole-text question is where all three come together.

## Common shapes of whole-text question

Wording varies, but these shapes are common in reading papers:

- **Presentation across the text**: "How does the writer present [a person, place or feeling] across
  the whole text?"
- **Structure**: "How has the writer structured the text to interest the reader?"
- **Change**: "How do the writer's feelings about [something] change?"
- **Statement to evaluate**: "A student said: '…'. To what extent do you agree?"

Each needs evidence from more than one section, and each rewards an answer that shows movement.

## Preparing while you read

You will usually answer section questions before the whole-text question. Use that reading time.

1. **On the first quick read, mark three points**: the opening, a turning point and the ending.
2. **As you answer each section question, jot one word in the margin** that sums up the writer's
   attitude or the impression in that section: *nervous*, *amused*, *proud*, *resentful*.
3. **When you reach the whole-text question, read your margin words in order.** They show the
   journey of the text, and your answer can be built on it.

This takes almost no extra time, and it stops you having to reread the whole text at the end.

## Building the answer

**Start with an overview sentence** that names the movement: "The writer's view of her grandfather
moves from fear to admiration, and the final section suggests that admiration is mixed with
regret." This is your argument, and it tells the examiner you have understood the whole text.

**Organise by stages, not by section number.** Three paragraphs are usually enough: how the text
begins, how and where it changes, and how it ends. Each paragraph should contain:

- a point about the impression or attitude at that stage;
- one or two short quotations;
- analysis of a method (language or structure);
- a link to the overall movement.

**Use structural vocabulary** where it is accurate: *opening*, *shift*, *turning point*, *contrast*,
*echo*, *return*, *cyclical*, *build-up*, *final reflection*.

**Finish with a judgement**, especially if the question asks "to what extent". Say which reading the
whole text supports most strongly, and why.

## Synthesising within one text

R2's wording mentions "one or more sources". On Paper 1 there is only one, so synthesis means
gathering details from different sections into a single point. For example:

> Across the text, the writer repeatedly associates her grandfather with silence: in the first section
> he "said nothing" at the gate; in the second she notices that "he never asked" about school; and in
> the last she realises that his silence was a kind of attention. Gathering these moments shows that
> silence is the thread the writer uses to rethink him.

That is stronger than three separate points, because it shows a pattern the reader would only see by
reading the whole text.

## Worked example

The following is an **original passage written for this guide**, divided into sections as the exam
text is.

> **Section 1.** *My grandfather kept bees, and I was afraid of them and of him. He moved slowly in his
> white veil, and when I asked a question he answered with a nod, as though words cost money.*
>
> **Section 2.** *The summer I was twelve, he let me hold the smoker. My hands shook. He put his hand
> over mine, not to take it, but to steady it, and said the only long sentence I ever heard from
> him: "They know when you're frightened, so be calm for their sake, not yours."*
>
> **Section 3.** *He died the next spring. Nobody wanted the hives. I took one, and I keep it still,
> and every summer I put on the veil and move slowly, and answer my own questions with a nod.*

**Question:** How does the writer present her grandfather across the whole text?

**Model answer (extract):**
The writer's presentation of her grandfather moves from distant figure to model, and by the end she
has become like him. In Section 1 he seems remote and slightly frightening: the "white veil" hides
him, and the simile "as though words cost money" suggests he is mean with speech, so the child reads
his silence as coldness. Section 2 is the turning point. The physical gesture of putting his hand
over hers "not to take it, but to steady it" shows patience rather than control, and the contrast
with his usual silence makes his single long sentence memorable; its advice to be calm "for their
sake, not yours" reveals a care for others that the child had not seen. Section 3 returns to the
details of the opening, the veil, slow movement and the nod, but now they describe the writer
herself. This cyclical structure shows that what once frightened her has become her own way of
living, so the final impression is of a grandfather understood too late but carried on in her.

*Why it works:* it opens with the overall movement, uses evidence from every section, analyses
language and structure, and identifies the cyclical ending as the key to the writer's perspective.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the text in order** with no argument about change.
- **Writing about only one section**, usually the most vivid one.
- **Repeating section answers** instead of building a pattern across them.
- **Ignoring the ending**, which often holds the writer's final perspective.
- **No overall judgement** when the question asks "to what extent".
- **Running out of time**: a whole-text answer needs evidence from every section, so it takes
  longer to plan than a section answer.

## Exam technique

Divide your Section A time by the marks shown, and protect the time for the whole-text question. Use
your margin words to plan in a minute. Aim for three stages, each with brief evidence, and a final
sentence of judgement.
