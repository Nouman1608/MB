## Withdrawal and consumption

Two measures of water use are easily confused:

- **Withdrawal**: water taken from a river, lake or aquifer, whether or not it is returned.
- **Consumption**: the part of the withdrawal that is not returned to the same source, because it evaporates, is transpired by crops, or is built into a product.

A power station may withdraw a large volume for cooling but return most of it, warmer, to the river: high withdrawal, low consumption. Irrigation consumes a large share of what it withdraws, because crops transpire it. Globally, agriculture accounts for about 70% of freshwater withdrawals, followed by industry and then domestic use. The balance differs by country: industry takes a larger share in many high-income countries.

## Measuring pressure on supply

The per-person indicator in the Topic 4 guide shows how much water exists for each person. A second indicator shows how hard the supply is being worked:

```
water-stress ratio = total withdrawals ÷ renewable freshwater × 100
```

A high ratio means that users compete for a large share of what is available, leaving little for ecosystems or for dry years.

### Worked example: a stress ratio and a demand strategy

The fictional country of Sorvia has 60 km³ of renewable freshwater a year. It withdraws 28 km³ for agriculture, 9 km³ for industry and 5 km³ for domestic use.

```
total withdrawals = 28 + 9 + 5 = 42 km³
stress ratio      = 42 ÷ 60 × 100 = 70 %
agriculture share = 28 ÷ 42 × 100 = 66.7 %

if drip irrigation cuts agricultural withdrawals by 25 %:
new agriculture   = 28 × 0.75 = 21 km³
new total         = 21 + 9 + 5 = 35 km³
new stress ratio  = 35 ÷ 60 × 100 = 58.3 %
```

**Interpretation.** Because agriculture dominates withdrawals, a moderate efficiency gain on farms lowers national stress far more than a large saving in homes would. A 25% cut in domestic use would save only 1.25 km³. The indicator still hides seasonal shortages, regional differences within the country and water quality.

## Water footprints and virtual water

A **water footprint** measures all the freshwater used to produce the goods and services a person, business or country consumes. It has three components:

- **Blue water footprint**: surface and groundwater consumed (mainly irrigation).
- **Green water footprint**: rainwater stored in soil and used by crops.
- **Grey water footprint**: the volume of freshwater needed to dilute pollution from production to acceptable quality.

Footprints show that much of a country's water use is hidden in imports. A country that imports grain imports the **virtual water** used to grow it, sparing its own supply.

### Worked example: virtual water in grain imports

A dry fictional country, Tamesh, imports 2.0 million tonnes of wheat a year. Assume, for this example, that each tonne of wheat needed 1300 m³ of water to grow. Tamesh's own renewable freshwater is 4.0 km³ a year.

```
virtual water imported = 2.0 × 10⁶ t × 1300 m³ per t
                       = 2.6 × 10⁹ m³
                       = 2.6 km³   (1 km³ = 10⁹ m³)
as a share of domestic supply = 2.6 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = 65 %
```

**Interpretation.** Growing the wheat at home would need water equal to 65% of Tamesh's entire renewable supply. Importing it improves Tamesh's water security, but makes its **food security** depend on world prices and on exporting countries' harvests. It also moves pressure to exporting regions, which may themselves be over-using aquifers.

## Water as a right

In 2010 the United Nations General Assembly recognised access to safe drinking water and sanitation as a human right. Sustainable Development Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation) aims for universal access by 2030. Where water is not piped to homes, the work of collecting it falls most often on women and girls, taking time from school and paid work. Unsafe water and poor sanitation spread diseases such as cholera. These are reasons why water security is a social issue as well as an environmental one.

A tension follows: if water is a right, should it be free? Pricing by volume encourages saving and pays for infrastructure, but can exclude poor households. A common compromise is a **rising block tariff**: a basic amount for each household at low cost, with higher prices for heavier use.

## Shared water and conflict

Many rivers and aquifers cross borders. Upstream states can build dams or divert water, affecting those downstream.

**The Nile.** Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, built for hydroelectricity, has caused long-running tension with Egypt and Sudan downstream. Egypt depends heavily on Nile water and fears reduced flow, especially while the reservoir fills during dry years. Ethiopia argues it needs the electricity for development. The case shows competing claims of equity, development and historical use.

**The Aral Sea.** From the 1960s, the Soviet Union diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton in Central Asia. With much less inflow, the Aral Sea, once one of the world's largest lakes, shrank dramatically and split into separate parts. Salinity rose, the fishing industry collapsed, and wind blew salt and dust laced with farm chemicals from the exposed seabed, harming health and crops. A dam completed in 2005 has partly restored the northern part of the sea, but most of the southern part has not recovered. The case shows how gains for one use (irrigated cotton) can destroy a whole water system and the livelihoods that depended on it.

## Integrated management

**Integrated water resources management** (IWRM) treats a whole drainage basin as one system and brings all users (farmers, cities, industry, ecosystems and neighbouring states) into decisions. Typical elements are basin-wide agreements, monitoring of withdrawals, allocations for environmental flows (water left in rivers for ecosystems) and combining supply measures with demand reduction. Further supply options include **managed aquifer recharge**, where surplus wet-season water or treated wastewater is deliberately infiltrated into aquifers for later use.

## Model answer: evaluate

**Question.** Evaluate the use of virtual water imports as a strategy for water security in a dry country. **[4]**

**Answer.** Importing water-intensive goods such as grain saves the country's own scarce water, which can then be used for drinking and industry [1]. It may be cheaper than desalination or large transfer schemes [1]. However, it makes food supply depend on world prices and on harvests in exporting countries, so a global price rise threatens food security [1]. Overall it is effective for water security only if the country can afford imports and diversifies its suppliers [1].

## Common mistakes

- Using "withdrawal" and "consumption" as if they mean the same thing.
- Calculating a stress ratio without converting all volumes to the same unit.
- Defining grey water footprint as wastewater; it is the dilution volume needed for pollution.
- Treating virtual water as a free gain, without its effect on food security or exporting regions.
- Describing the Aral Sea as natural drying; the cause was irrigation diversions.
- Recommending one strategy for all places without considering cost, energy and local conditions.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, keep units consistent and give the answer to sensible significant figures.
- **Explain** inequality of access: link economic, physical and social causes to effects on health and livelihoods.
- **Evaluate** a strategy: benefit, cost, side effect, who gains or loses, judgement.
- **Discuss** a transboundary case: present each state's position, then judge what kind of agreement could balance them.
