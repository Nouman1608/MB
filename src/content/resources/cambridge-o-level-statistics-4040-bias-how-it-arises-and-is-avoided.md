This guide covers section 1.3 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "Bias: how it arises and is avoided". Questions on bias usually describe a real data collection and ask why the results may be biased, which way they are pushed, and what change would put it right.

## What bias is

**Bias** is a systematic error: the method of collecting data tends to push the result the same way every time it is used. It is different from **sampling variation**, the chance difference between one random sample and another. Sampling variation makes estimates scatter on both sides of the true value and gets smaller as the sample grows. Bias moves the centre of that scatter away from the true value, and no increase in sample size moves it back.

A useful test: imagine repeating the survey many times in exactly the same way. If the results would cluster around the true value, the method is unbiased, even though any one result may miss. If they would cluster around the wrong value, it is biased.

## How bias arises

Bias can enter at four stages. Naming the stage helps you find the cause in a long question.

### 1. Choosing who or what is measured (selection bias)

- **An incomplete sampling frame.** Some members of the population are not on the list, so they can never be chosen. A list of registered library members used to study reading habits in a town leaves out everyone who never uses the library.
- **An out-of-date frame.** A school roll from last year omits new pupils and includes pupils who have left.
- **Non-random choice.** In convenience sampling the investigator asks whoever is easiest to reach, and in quota sampling the interviewer picks who fills each quota. People who are approachable, nearby or available at that hour are over-represented.
- **Self-selection.** When people choose to take part (a phone-in vote, an online poll, a box of forms left at a counter), those with strong opinions are far more likely to reply.
- **Periodic lists in systematic sampling.** If every 10th flat on a list is a corner flat with an extra room, taking every 10th flat from a fixed start can pick corner flats only.

### 2. Who answers (non-response bias)

Non-response becomes bias when the people who do not reply differ from those who do. A postal survey about working hours is least likely to be returned by people who work the longest hours, so the average reported is too low.

### 3. How the question is asked (response bias)

- **Leading questions** suggest the answer wanted: "Don't you agree that the new car park is a waste of money?"
- **Loaded words** carry an opinion: "reckless spending", "generous offer".
- **Sensitive questions** asked face to face, or on a form with a name on it, produce socially acceptable answers rather than true ones.
- **Response options that are not balanced**, for example Excellent / Very good / Good / Fair, with no option below "Fair".

### 4. How the answer is recorded (interviewer and measurement bias)

- **Interviewer bias.** The tone, appearance or reactions of an interviewer influence replies, or the interviewer records vague answers in the way they expect.
- **Measurement bias.** A faulty instrument reads wrongly in one direction: kitchen scales that read 50 g with nothing on them make every mass 50 g too high. Rounding every measurement down has the same effect.

## How bias is avoided

Each cause has a matching cure. Exam answers gain credit when the cure is specific to the cause, not a general wish for a "better survey".

| Cause | Change that avoids it |
|---|---|
| Incomplete or out-of-date frame | Use a full, current list of the population, or combine lists so no group is missing |
| Non-random choice by the investigator | Select with random numbers from the frame (simple random), or use a random start for a systematic sample |
| Important subgroups under-represented | Use stratified sampling, with each stratum sampled in proportion and at random |
| Self-selection | Choose the sample yourself, at random, and contact those chosen |
| Non-response | Send reminders, call back at different times, keep the form short, and report the response rate |
| Leading or loaded questions | Use neutral wording and balanced options; test the wording in a pilot survey |
| Sensitive questions | Make replies anonymous, for example a sealed form with no name |
| Interviewer influence | Train interviewers to read questions exactly as written; use a self-completed form |
| Faulty instrument | Check and calibrate the instrument before use; record to the stated accuracy |
| Time or place of collection | Collect on several days, at several times and in several places |

A **pilot survey**, a small trial run on a few members of the population, is the main way to find unclear or leading questions before the main survey. It does not fix a biased sampling frame, so do not offer it as a cure for selection bias.

## Worked example 1: diagnosing a survey

A council wants to know the average number of hours per week that adults in a town spend travelling to work. An officer stands at the main bus station from 8 a.m. to 9 a.m. on a Monday and asks 200 people as they leave a bus.

(a) Give two reasons why the results may be biased. (b) State the likely direction of the bias for one of them. (c) Suggest an improved method.

**Model answer.**

(a) Only bus users are asked, so adults who drive, walk or work from home cannot be included. Only people travelling between 8 and 9 a.m. on a Monday are asked, so shift workers and part-time workers who travel at other times are missed.

(b) Adults who work from home (travel time zero) or live close enough to walk can never be in the sample, while everyone asked has made at least one bus journey. The mean travel time is therefore likely to be too high. Because the question asks for a direction, the answer gives the reason that leads to it.

(c) Take a simple random sample of adults from the town's electoral register, or another complete list of adults, and contact each one, with reminders for those who do not reply. Ask about a typical week so that the day of contact does not matter.

The structure is: cause, link to the population, effect on the result, specific remedy.

## Worked example 2: rewriting a biased question

Original: "Most parents think homework is too long. How long do you think homework should be each evening?"

The opening sentence tells the respondent what others think, which pushes answers towards shorter times. A neutral version removes the opinion and gives balanced, non-overlapping options:

"How long do you think homework should take each evening?"
Under 30 minutes / 30 minutes to under 1 hour / 1 hour to under 1.5 hours / 1.5 hours or more

## Worked example 3: bias or sampling variation?

Two pupils estimate the mean mass of apples in a crate of 500. Pupil A picks the 20 apples on top of the crate. Pupil B numbers the apples and picks 20 using random numbers. Both estimates differ from the true mean.

Pupil B's error is sampling variation; repeated random samples would scatter around the true mean, and a sample of 50 would usually be closer. Pupil A's method is biased if larger apples settle on top (or are placed there for display): repeated samples from the top would keep giving too high a mean, and taking 50 from the top would not help.

## Common errors

- Writing "the sample is too small" as a source of bias. Small samples give imprecise results, not biased ones.
- Naming a cause without saying how it changes the result.
- Offering "use a bigger sample" or "ask more people" as the remedy.
- Suggesting a pilot survey to fix a sampling frame problem.
- Describing a remedy too vaguely: "make it random" earns less than "number the frame and choose with random numbers".

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **state**: one clear cause, tied to the context ("only people at the bus station").
- **Explain**: cause, then why that group differs from the population, then the effect on the result.
- **Suggest an improvement**: name the method and how it is carried out, for example "a simple random sample from the electoral register using random numbers".
- **Comment on a claim**: decide whether the claim is supported and say which feature of the method limits it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a larger sample reduce bias?**
No. A larger sample reduces sampling variation, so the estimate is more precise, but a biased method stays biased however many people are asked.

**Is quota sampling biased?**
It can be. The quotas make the sample match the population on chosen features, but the interviewer picks the individuals, so people who are easy to approach are over-represented.

**What is the difference between non-response and self-selection?**
In non-response, the investigator chooses the sample and some chosen people do not reply. In self-selection, the people decide for themselves whether to take part, so the investigator never chooses a sample at all.
