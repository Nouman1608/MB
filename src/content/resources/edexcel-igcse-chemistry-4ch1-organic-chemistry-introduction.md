## Organic chemistry and hydrocarbons (4.1)

Organic chemistry is the study of compounds of carbon. Carbon atoms form four covalent bonds and can join to each other in long chains, branched chains and rings, so there are millions of organic compounds. A **hydrocarbon** is a compound containing **hydrogen and carbon only**. Methane (CH₄), ethene (C₂H₄) and hexane (C₆H₁₄) are hydrocarbons. Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is not, because it contains oxygen, and bromoethane is not, because it contains bromine.

In organic molecules, each carbon makes **four bonds**, each hydrogen makes one, each oxygen makes two, and each halogen makes one. Checking these numbers is the best way to catch a drawing mistake.

## The five types of formula (4.2)

Take butane as the example.

| Type | What it shows | Butane |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical formula | The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element | C₂H₅ |
| Molecular formula | The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule | C₄H₁₀ |
| General formula | The formula for all members of a homologous series, using n | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkanes) |
| Structural formula | The atoms grouped to show how they are arranged, without drawing every bond | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| Displayed formula | Every atom and every bond shown | See below |

Displayed formula of butane:

```
    H   H   H   H
    |   |   |   |
H - C - C - C - C - H
    |   |   |   |
    H   H   H   H
```

**Finding an empirical formula from a molecular formula.** Divide all the numbers by their highest common factor. C₆H₁₂ becomes CH₂; C₄H₁₀ becomes C₂H₅; C₂H₆ becomes CH₃; C₃H₈ cannot be simplified, so it is already empirical.

**Worked example 1: empirical formula from percentage composition.** A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass.

| | C | H |
|---|---|---|
| Mass in 100 g / g | 85.7 | 14.3 |
| Moles = mass / Ar | 85.7 / 12 = 7.14 | 14.3 / 1 = 14.3 |
| Divide by the smallest | 7.14 / 7.14 = 1 | 14.3 / 7.14 = 2.0 |

The empirical formula is **CH₂**. If the relative molecular mass is 56, then the molecular formula is found by dividing: the empirical formula mass is 12 + 2 = 14, and 56 / 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is (CH₂)₄ = **C₄H₈**.

## Homologous series, functional groups and isomerism (4.3)

A **homologous series** is a family of organic compounds with the **same functional group** and the **same general formula**, with similar chemical properties, and a gradual trend in physical properties (such as boiling point). Neighbouring members differ by one CH₂.

A **functional group** is an atom or group of atoms that is responsible for the characteristic reactions of a compound.

| Homologous series | Functional group | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkanes | C-C single bonds only | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CH₃CH₃ ethane |
| Alkenes | C=C double bond | CₙH₂ₙ | CH₂=CH₂ ethene |
| Alcohols (Paper 2 only) | -OH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | CH₃CH₂OH ethanol |
| Carboxylic acids (Paper 2 only) | -COOH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH | CH₃COOH ethanoic acid |

**Isomers** are compounds with the **same molecular formula but different structural formulae**. They have different structures and so different physical properties (and sometimes chemical ones). For example, butane and 2-methylbutane have different formulae, so they are not isomers, but butane and 2-methylpropane are both C₄H₁₀, so they are.

## IUPAC naming (4.4)

Names are built from a **stem** (the number of carbons in the longest chain), an **ending** (the homologous series) and sometimes **prefixes** (branches or halogens).

| Carbons | Stem | Carbons | Stem |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | meth- | 4 | but- |
| 2 | eth- | 5 | pent- |
| 3 | prop- | 6 | hex- |

| Series | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane | -ane | propane |
| Alkene | -ene | but-1-ene |
| Alcohol (Paper 2 only) | -ol | ethanol |
| Carboxylic acid (Paper 2 only) | -oic acid | propanoic acid |

**Rules.**

1. Find the **longest continuous carbon chain** that includes the functional group. This gives the stem.
2. Add the ending.
3. For a **branch** (methyl, CH₃, one carbon; ethyl, C₂H₅, two carbons), put the name before the stem, with a number for the carbon it is attached to, counting from the end that gives the **lower** number. 2-methylpropane has a methyl group on carbon 2 of a three-carbon chain.
4. For a **halogen**, use the prefix chloro-, bromo- or iodo-, with a number if needed, for example bromoethane or 1,2-dibromoethane.
5. For a **double bond or -OH group**, put the position number of the first carbon involved before the ending: but-1-ene has C=C between carbons 1 and 2; but-2-ene has it between 2 and 3.
6. Use **di-** for two identical groups, with a number for each, separated by commas, for example 1,2-dibromoethane.

## Writing structural and displayed formulae from a molecular formula (4.5)

**Method for alkane isomers.** Start with the longest straight chain. Then shorten the chain by one carbon and place that carbon as a branch on every different inner carbon. Do not count the same structure twice. A branch on an end carbon just makes a longer chain, and a bent drawing of the same chain is not a new isomer.

**Worked example 2: isomers of C₅H₁₂.**

1. Straight chain, 5 carbons: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ is **pentane**.
2. Longest chain 4 carbons with a methyl branch on carbon 2: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ is **2-methylbutane**. A methyl group on carbon 3 gives the same compound from the other end.
3. Longest chain 3 carbons with two methyl branches on carbon 2: CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₃ is **2,2-dimethylpropane**.

There are **three isomers**, and each has Mr = 72.

**Worked example 3: isomers of C₄H₈ that are alkenes.** Put the double bond in each possible place. Chain of 4 carbons, double bond between carbons 1 and 2: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ is **but-1-ene**. Double bond between carbons 2 and 3: CH₃CH=CHCH₃ is **but-2-ene**. With a branched 3-carbon chain: CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃ is **2-methylpropene**. The first two are position isomers of each other. (Cis/trans naming is not required.)

**Worked example 4: dibromoethane.** C₂H₄Br₂ has two isomers: 1,2-dibromoethane (BrCH₂CH₂Br, one bromine on each carbon) and 1,1-dibromoethane (CH₃CHBr₂, both on the same carbon).

After drawing, check each carbon has four bonds and the formula matches.

## Types of reaction (4.6)

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution | One atom or group is replaced by another | CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (UV light) |
| Addition | Atoms add across a double bond, giving a single product | C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ |
| Combustion | Burning in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water (complete) | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O |

Substitution produces **two products**, while addition produces **one**. A reaction mechanism is not required.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a compound with oxygen or a halogen as a hydrocarbon.
- Drawing a carbon with five bonds, or a hydrogen with two.
- Counting a flipped or bent version of a chain as a new isomer.
- Not choosing the longest chain when naming: "2-ethylbutane" is really 3-methylpentane.
- Numbering from the wrong end of the chain.
- Confusing the empirical formula with the molecular formula, for example giving CH₂ when asked for the formula of a molecule of C₄H₈.
- Writing the general formula of alkenes as CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
- Labelling a reaction as substitution because "something changed". Substitution replaces an atom, while addition joins across a double bond.

## Exam technique

For a naming question, identify the functional group, count the longest chain, then add any branches and numbers. For a drawing question, always check the bond count. For "how many isomers", work systematically from the longest chain downwards and name each one as you go; it is easier to see duplicates. Write displayed formulae with every bond as a line, and structural formulae with the atoms grouped.
