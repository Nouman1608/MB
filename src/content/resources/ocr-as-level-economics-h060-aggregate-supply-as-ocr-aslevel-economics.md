## 1. What aggregate supply is

**Aggregate supply** is the total output of goods and services that firms in an economy plan to produce and sell at each possible price level, over a period. It is drawn on the same axes as aggregate demand: **price level** on the vertical axis and **real output** on the horizontal.

The key to AS is the distinction between the **short run** and the **long run**. In macroeconomics the short run is the period in which some input prices, especially money wages, are fixed by contracts and agreements. In the long run all input prices can adjust, and output is limited only by the economy's productive capacity.

## 2. Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)

The SRAS curve slopes **upwards**. Two linked reasons explain why.

1. **Sticky costs.** If the price level rises while money wages and other contracted costs stay the same, each unit sold earns more revenue relative to its cost. Producing more becomes profitable, so firms expand output.
2. **Rising costs as output expands.** To produce more in the short run, firms pay overtime, hire less experienced workers, run machines harder and buy inputs at higher prices. Costs per unit rise, so firms supply more only at a higher price level.

The SRAS curve is drawn for a given set of input costs. If those costs change, the whole curve shifts.

## 3. Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)

Economists disagree about the shape of the long-run curve. The specification does not name either school, but you should know both views, because they give different answers about the long run.

### The neo-classical (free-market) view

LRAS is **vertical** at the full-capacity level of output, sometimes called potential output. In the long run wages and prices are fully flexible, so any rise in the price level is matched by rising costs and the profit incentive to expand disappears. Output is set by the quantity and quality of factors of production and by technology, not by the price level.

### The Keynesian view

The Keynesian LRAS has three sections.

- **Horizontal** at low levels of output. There is plenty of spare capacity: idle machines and unemployed workers. Output can rise without bidding up costs, so the price level does not rise.
- **Upward-sloping** in the middle. Bottlenecks appear in some industries and skills, so costs start to rise as output expands.
- **Vertical** at full capacity. No more output can be produced, so extra demand only raises prices.

The two views differ mainly about the economy below full capacity. Keynesians argue that wages are sticky downwards, so unemployment can persist for a long time. Neo-classical economists argue that markets clear, so the economy returns to full capacity on its own.

## 4. Shifts in SRAS

SRAS shifts when the **costs of production** change for a reason other than the price level.

| SRAS shifts left (costs rise) | SRAS shifts right (costs fall) |
|---|---|
| Money wages rise faster than productivity | Productivity rises faster than wages |
| Higher prices of raw materials and energy | Cheaper commodities or energy |
| Exchange rate depreciation, raising import prices | Exchange rate appreciation, lowering import prices |
| Higher indirect taxes, such as VAT or fuel duty | Lower indirect taxes or new subsidies to producers |
| New regulations that add to compliance costs | Removal of costly regulations |

**Worked example 1: unit labour costs.** What matters for firms is the wage cost of each unit produced, not the wage alone.

Unit labour cost = wage per hour / output per hour

In Country U, hourly wages rise by 6% while output per hour rises by 2%. Unit labour costs change by 1.06 / 1.02 - 1 = **+3.9%**. SRAS shifts left. If productivity had risen by 6% as well, unit labour costs would be unchanged and SRAS would not shift, even though wages rose.

**Worked example 2: an energy shock.** A typical firm's cost per unit is made up of labour 50, materials 30 and energy 20, a total of 100. Energy prices rise by 40%, adding 8 to the cost per unit, a rise of **8%**. Firms will supply the same output only at a higher price, so SRAS shifts left. The size of the shift depends on how energy-intensive production is: a firm where energy is 5% of costs faces only a 2% rise.

## 5. Shifts in LRAS

LRAS shifts when the economy's **productive capacity** changes. The causes fall into three groups.

**Quantity of factors of production**

- A larger labour force, from population growth, net migration, higher participation (for example more parents returning to work) or a later retirement age.
- A larger capital stock, from net investment in machinery, buildings and infrastructure.
- Discovery of new natural resources.

**Quality of factors of production**

- Better education, training and skills, which raise labour productivity.
- Improved health, so fewer working days are lost.
- More modern capital that embodies new technology.

**Technology and institutions**

- New technology and innovation, from research and development.
- Stronger competition and well-functioning markets, which push firms to use resources efficiently.
- Clear property rights, stable laws and lower regulatory barriers.

**Worked example 3: growth in potential output.** In Country V the labour force grows by 0.5% a year and output per worker grows by 1.5% a year. Potential output grows by about 1.005 x 1.015 - 1 = **2.0%** a year, so LRAS shifts right by about 2% each year. If productivity growth halves to 0.75%, potential growth falls to about 1.25%, showing how important productivity is to long-run living standards.

LRAS shifts are linked to the production possibility curve. An outward shift of the PPC and a rightward shift of LRAS both show an increase in the economy's capacity to produce.

## 6. When one change shifts both curves

Some changes affect both SRAS and LRAS.

- A **permanent rise in productivity**, for example from new technology, lowers unit costs (SRAS right) and raises capacity (LRAS right).
- A **temporary rise in oil prices** raises costs (SRAS left) but does not change capacity, so LRAS stays where it is.
- A **long-lasting energy shortage** could reduce capacity too, if energy-intensive industries close for good.

The test is whether the change alters costs at existing capacity (SRAS only) or the capacity itself (LRAS, and usually SRAS too).

## Common mistakes

- Shifting SRAS because the price level changed: a change in the price level is a movement along the curve.
- Saying higher wages always shift SRAS left without checking productivity.
- Treating LRAS shifts as quick: investment, education and migration take years to change capacity.
- Drawing a vertical LRAS and then saying demand can raise long-run output. With a vertical LRAS, it cannot.
- Labelling the vertical axis "price" instead of "price level".
- Mixing up the two views without saying which you are using.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** the slope of SRAS as a chain: price level rises, costs fixed in the short run, profit per unit rises, firms supply more.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw the curve, label axes and curves (SRAS1, SRAS2 or LRAS1, LRAS2), show the direction of the shift with an arrow and name its cause in the text.
- State which LRAS model you are using. The Keynesian curve is the better choice when the question mentions spare capacity or high unemployment.
- In data questions, compare wage growth with productivity growth before deciding which way SRAS moves.
