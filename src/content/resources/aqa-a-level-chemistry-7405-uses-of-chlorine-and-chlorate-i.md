## Oxidation states and disproportionation

Chlorine is an element, so its oxidation state in Cl₂ is 0. In its compounds it can be –1, +1 and other values.

| Species | Oxidation state of chlorine |
|---|---|
| Cl₂ | 0 |
| Cl⁻ (chloride) | –1 |
| ClO⁻ (chlorate(I)) | +1 |
| HClO (chloric(I) acid) | +1 |

In ClO⁻ the oxygen is –2 and the overall charge is –1, so chlorine is +1. The (I) in the name means oxidation state +1.

**Disproportionation** is a reaction in which the same element is both oxidised and reduced. Several reactions of chlorine in this section are disproportionation reactions.

## Chlorine and water

When chlorine dissolves in water, an equilibrium is established:

```
Cl₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HClO(aq) + HCl(aq)
Cl₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ 2H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + ClO⁻(aq)
```

Both forms are correct. The second shows the ions the specification names: **chloride ions and chlorate(I) ions**. The solution is acidic, because HCl is a strong acid, and it is pale green because of dissolved chlorine.

This is disproportionation. One chlorine atom goes from 0 to –1 (reduction) to form chloride, and the other goes from 0 to +1 (oxidation) to form chlorate(I). Both happen to atoms of the same element in the same reaction.

```
Cl₂ → Cl⁻     0 to –1   reduced
Cl₂ → ClO⁻    0 to +1   oxidised
```

The ClO⁻ ion (and HClO) is a strong oxidising agent. It is the species that kills bacteria in water.

### Chlorine and water in sunlight

In bright sunlight, chlorine water decomposes and releases oxygen:

```
2Cl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 4H⁺(aq) + 4Cl⁻(aq) + O₂(g)
```

Here chlorine is reduced from 0 to –1, and oxygen in water is oxidised from –2 to 0. This reaction is the reason a bottle of chlorine water is kept in the dark and loses its bleaching power if left in the light. It is not disproportionation, since chlorine is only reduced.

## Chlorine in water treatment

Chlorine is added to drinking water and swimming pools to kill microorganisms. Dissolved chlorine forms chlorate(I) ions and chloric(I) acid, which are oxidising agents and destroy bacteria and viruses. This has prevented outbreaks of waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid.

### Benefits and risks

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Kills disease-causing microorganisms | Chlorine gas is toxic and corrosive, so it must be stored and handled with care |
| Cheap and effective in small amounts | Chlorine can react with organic matter in water to form chlorinated hydrocarbons, some of which are suspected to be carcinogenic |
| A residual amount protects water as it travels through pipes | It can affect taste and smell, and irritate eyes and skin in swimming pools |

The specification asks you to appreciate that the **benefits to health of water treatment by chlorine outweigh its toxic effects**. In practice, the quantities used are very small compared with the risk from untreated water.

### Society and water supplies

The specification also asks you to appreciate that society assesses the advantages and disadvantages when deciding whether chemicals should be added to water supplies. Fluoride is a common example of this debate. Fluoridation reduces tooth decay, and people argue about whether it is right to add a chemical to water that everyone drinks, whether excess fluoride can harm teeth, and who should decide. A good answer weighs a benefit against a risk and says who is affected. Avoid presenting one side as the only correct view.

## Chlorine and cold dilute sodium hydroxide

When chlorine reacts with **cold, dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide**, it forms sodium chloride and sodium chlorate(I):

```
Cl₂(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H₂O(l)
Cl₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cl⁻(aq) + ClO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
```

This is another disproportionation: chlorine goes from 0 to –1 in NaCl and from 0 to +1 in NaClO. The solution formed contains sodium chlorate(I) and is sold as bleach. The temperature must be kept low (cold) and the alkali dilute, because hot concentrated alkali gives a different product with chlorine in a higher oxidation state.

### Uses of the solution

Sodium chlorate(I) is an oxidising agent. It is used:

- as household **bleach** to remove stains and whiten fabrics and paper;
- as a **disinfectant** and cleaner for kitchens and bathrooms;
- to treat **swimming pool and drinking water**, as ClO⁻ kills bacteria.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: oxidation states in the water reaction

Show that Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HClO + HCl is disproportionation.

- In Cl₂, chlorine is 0.
- In HClO, chlorine is +1 (H is +1, O is –2, so Cl is +1). It has been oxidised.
- In HCl, chlorine is –1. It has been reduced.
- The same element is both oxidised and reduced, so it is disproportionation.

### Worked example 2: mass of bleach

7.10 g of chlorine reacts with excess cold dilute sodium hydroxide. Calculate the mass of sodium chlorate(I), NaClO (Mᵣ = 74.5), formed. Mᵣ(Cl₂) = 71.0.

```
n(Cl₂) = 7.10 ÷ 71.0 = 0.100 mol
n(NaClO) = 0.100 mol (1:1)
mass = 0.100 × 74.5 = 7.45 g
```

If 6.20 g is actually obtained, the percentage yield is 6.20 ÷ 7.45 × 100 = 83.2%.

### Worked example 3: sodium hydroxide needed

What volume of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is needed to react with 0.0100 mol of chlorine?

Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O. The ratio is 1 : 2, so n(NaOH) = 0.0200 mol. Volume = 0.0200 ÷ 0.200 = 0.100 dm³ = 100 cm³.

### Worked example 4: dosing a water supply

A water company dosing rate is 0.50 mg of chlorine per dm³. Calculate the mass of chlorine for 5.00 × 10⁴ dm³ of water.

```
mass = 5.00 × 10⁴ × 0.50 mg = 2.50 × 10⁴ mg = 25.0 g
```

That is 25.0 ÷ 71.0 = 0.352 mol of Cl₂. It is a very small concentration, but enough to kill bacteria.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the reaction of chlorine with water as giving only HCl, or forgetting the chlorate(I) ion.
- Writing a single arrow for the reaction of chlorine with water. It is an equilibrium.
- Calling chlorate(I) "chlorine oxide" or confusing it with chlorate(V).
- Not giving oxidation states when asked to show disproportionation.
- Using hot or concentrated sodium hydroxide in the equation for bleach.
- Forgetting that sunlight gives oxygen, not chlorate(I).
- Claiming chlorine is harmless. Both sides need to be stated in a "discuss" question.
- Saying chlorine "removes" bacteria. It kills them by oxidation.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write an equation**: balance charges as well as atoms in ionic equations.
- **Show that a reaction is disproportionation**: give the oxidation state before and after for each product, and identify oxidation and reduction.
- **Discuss / evaluate**: give a benefit, a risk and a judgement.
- **State a use**: be specific, for example "bleach for fabrics".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the solution of chlorine in water acidic?**
It produces HCl (a strong acid), so the solution contains H⁺ ions.

**Why is the temperature of the sodium hydroxide reaction important?**
Cold dilute alkali gives chlorate(I) (+1). Hot concentrated alkali gives a different oxidation state of chlorine and the product is not bleach.

**Is chlorate(I) the same as chlorate(V)?**
No. Chlorate(I) is ClO⁻ and chlorate(V) is ClO₃⁻. They differ in the oxidation state of chlorine.
