## Answers

**1. (a)** Pale yellow-green gas [1].

**(b)** Shiny grey-black solid [1].

**2.** Purple (violet) [1].

**3.** Volatility decreases (boiling point increases) down the group [1].

**4.** Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id, London) forces [1].

**5.** It decreases [1].

**6.** Iodine [1].

**7.** Both are simple molecular, so only intermolecular forces are overcome on boiling [1]. These are id-id forces [1]. Br₂ has more electrons than Cl₂ (70 compared with 34), so its electron cloud is more polarisable and the id-id forces are stronger [1]. More energy is needed to separate Br₂ molecules, so bromine has a higher boiling point and is a liquid at room temperature [1].

**8.** The atoms get larger, so the bond length increases [1]. The bonding pair of electrons is further from the nuclei and more shielded by inner electrons [1]. The attraction between the nuclei and the bonding pair is weaker, so less energy is needed to break the bond [1].

**9. (a)** F₂ (F–F) [1].

**(b)** Fluorine atoms are very small, so in F₂ the lone pairs on the two atoms are very close together [1]. The repulsion between these lone pairs weakens the F–F bond [1].

**10. (a)** The I–I bond is weaker, not stronger, than the Cl–Cl bond [1]. Boiling does not break covalent bonds within the molecules; it overcomes the forces between molecules [1].

**(b)** I₂ molecules have more electrons than Cl₂ molecules, so the id-id forces between I₂ molecules are stronger [1]; more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1].

**11. (a)** A solid [1]: At₂ molecules would have more electrons than I₂, so stronger id-id forces and a higher melting point than iodine [1].

**(b)** Weaker than I–I [1]: astatine atoms are larger, so the bonding pair is further from the nuclei and more shielded, and is held less strongly [1].

**12.** Electrons in one Br₂ molecule move, and at an instant are unevenly distributed, giving an instantaneous dipole [1]. This distorts the electron cloud of a neighbouring molecule, inducing a dipole, and the two dipoles attract [1]. I₂ molecules have more electrons [1], so their electron clouds are more polarisable and larger dipoles form, giving stronger id-id forces [1].

**13. (a)** Sublimation (solid to gas) [1].

**(b)** At the bottom, the energy supplied overcomes the id-id forces between I₂ molecules, so they separate into the vapour without the I–I bonds breaking [1]. At the cooler top, the molecules lose energy and the id-id forces re-form, so the molecules attract each other into a solid lattice again [1].

**14.** Boiling only overcomes the id-id forces between Br₂ molecules [1]. These intermolecular forces are much weaker than the covalent Br–Br bond within each molecule, which is not broken on boiling [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining volatility with the strength of the X–X bond.
- Saying "bigger molecules" without mentioning more electrons or polarisability.
- Stating colours vaguely, such as "brown" for both bromine and iodine.
- Missing the fluorine anomaly in bond energy data.
- Writing halogens as single atoms rather than diatomic molecules.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do halogen boiling points rise down the group if the bonds get weaker?**
Boiling separates whole molecules and does not break the X–X bond. The forces between molecules, id-id forces, get stronger as the number of electrons increases, so the boiling point rises even though the bond inside each molecule gets weaker.

**Do I need to know the colours of the halogens in solution?**
The outcome asks for the colours of the elements. Solution colours are used in displacement reactions in the next subtopic, so they are worth learning alongside.
