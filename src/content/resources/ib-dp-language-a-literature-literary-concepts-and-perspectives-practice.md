## Questions

**1.** Explain the difference between a critical perspective and a personal opinion. **[2]**

**2.** The subject brief says students "negotiate meanings for texts". Explain what this asks you to
do when two interpretations of a text conflict. **[2]**

### Passage A -- public-domain poem

Christina Rossetti, "In an Artist's Studio" (written 1856, first published 1896).

> One face looks out from all his canvases,\
> One selfsame figure sits or walks or leans:\
> We found her hidden just behind those screens,\
> That mirror gave back all her loveliness.\
> A queen in opal or in ruby dress,\
> A nameless girl in freshest summer-greens,\
> A saint, an angel – every canvas means\
> The same one meaning, neither more nor less.\
> He feeds upon her face by day and night,\
> And she with true kind eyes looks back on him,\
> Fair as the moon and joyful as the light:\
> Not wan with waiting, not with sorrow dim;\
> Not as she is, but was when hope shone bright;\
> Not as she is, but as she fills his dream.

**3.** Identify two ways Passage A presents the woman as someone looked at rather than someone who
acts. Support each with a quotation. **[2]**

**4.** Read Passage A through a feminist (gender) lens. Explain two points, each with evidence.
**[4]**

**5.** Explain how the concept of creativity works in Passage A, for both the painter and the poet.
**[4]**

### Passage B -- prose (original text written for Marlbridge; people, places and the company are invented)

> **Plot 14**
>
> Nan's allotment sat in the shadow of the old rolling mill: plot 14 of 40. The mill shut the year my
> mother was born, and the council let the land to anyone who would dig it. Nan dug it for thirty-one
> years. She grew what the shop charged too much for: runner beans, onions, rhubarb for half the
> street. The men from the mill kept the plots by the gate. Nan's was at the far end, by the fence,
> where the soil was full of cinders and she barrowed in manure from Oates's farm.
>
> Last spring a letter came. The site had been sold to Brindlemoor Homes as "a regeneration
> opportunity". Plot-holders would be offered "alternative provision".
>
> On her last Sunday Nan did not dig. She sat on an upturned bucket and ate a raw pea-pod, slowly,
> the way you eat something you have paid for. Behind her the new hoarding said FUTURE LIVING.
> Beside her a blackbird worked the turned earth as if nothing had been sold at all.

**6.** Read Passage B through a class (Marxist) lens. Explain two points, each with evidence. **[4]**

**7.** Make one ecocritical point about Passage B, with evidence, and name one detail that lens
cannot explain. **[3]**

### Passage C -- two student interpretations of Passage A (original texts written for Marlbridge)

> **Interpretation 1.** "This is a love poem. The painter is loyal to one woman: he paints her 'by
> day and night', and she looks back 'with true kind eyes'. Rossetti admires his devotion."
>
> **Interpretation 2.** "The poem is only about Christina Rossetti's brother, the painter Dante
> Gabriel Rossetti, and the woman he painted. It is a record of her family and tells us nothing
> beyond that."

**8.** Evaluate Interpretation 1, using at least two tests (evidence, coverage, coherence, context,
counter-reading). **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate Interpretation 2, and suggest how its writer could improve it. **[4]**

**10.** Guided analysis plan: *How does the writer of Passage B present the allotment as a place that
means different things to different people?* Give a thesis, three analytical points with evidence,
and one counter-reading you would address. **[8]**

**11.** Comparison plan: *Compare how Passage A and Passage B show one viewer's perspective shaping
how another person or place is seen.* Give a thesis, three points of comparison with evidence from
both passages, and a sentence on what the comparison reveals. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A critical perspective is a consistent set of questions a reader brings to a text; a gender
lens asks who is looked at and who decides. [1] A personal opinion is an untested reaction; a
perspective produces an interpretation that can be checked against the text. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "A perspective is a point of view" earns nothing; the credit is for the
difference, which is testability.

**2.** Test each reading against the text, for evidence and coverage, instead of choosing a favourite.
[1] Reach a reasoned position that keeps what each reading explains well, drops what fails,
and says why. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers describe a process; "pick the best one" gives no criterion.

**3.** Any two, each with a quotation: she is reduced to a repeated image, "One face" or "One
selfsame figure" [1]; she is hidden or only reflected, "hidden just behind those screens", or
consumed by his gaze, "He feeds upon her face" [1].
*Examiner insight:* Two quotations for the same way earn one mark.

**4.** The painter turns one woman into roles he chooses: "A queen", "A nameless girl", "A saint, an
angel". "Nameless" erases her. [1] Every canvas "means / The same one meaning", so her
variety is flattened into his single idea of her. [1] "He feeds upon her face by day and night" makes
looking a kind of consuming: he takes, she is used. [1] The last line, "Not as she is, but as she
fills his dream", shows her present self replaced by his fantasy. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The lens must lead to analysis of words ("feeds", "fills"), not a summary.

**5.** The painter's creativity is repetitive and possessive: "every canvas means / The same one
meaning, neither more nor less". [1] His art fixes her in an idealised past: "Fair as the moon and
joyful as the light". [1] The poet's creativity corrects his: the repeated "Not as she is" in the last
two lines reveals what the paintings leave out. [1] So the poem is art that criticises a way of making
art, and asks the reader to see the woman the canvases hide. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Treating creativity as the painter's skill only misses half the question.

**6.** Institutions control the land: "the council let the land", then it was "sold to Brindlemoor
Homes". [1] Nan works it for "thirty-one years" but never owns it, so her labour gives her no legal
claim. [1] The developer's euphemisms, "a regeneration opportunity" and "alternative provision", sit in
quotation marks that show the narrator's distrust. [1] "FUTURE LIVING" sells a future to buyers, not
to those who kept the land alive. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Credit comes from details of ownership and language; "Nan is poor" is assertion.

**7.** The blackbird "worked the turned earth as if nothing had been sold at all": nature does not
recognise human ownership. [1] Soil "full of cinders", made fertile by manure, shows land renewed
after industry. [1] The lens cannot fully explain "the way you eat
something you have paid for", which is about money and human feeling. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming what a lens leaves out is evaluation, and earns its own mark.

**8.** Evidence: the quotations are accurate and show constancy. [1] But "feeds upon" is a
consuming verb, not a devoted one, so the evidence is partly misread. [1] Coverage: it ignores "Not
as she is, but was when hope shone bright", which says the paintings show her past, not her present.
[1] He is loyal to an image, not the woman. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "I disagree, it is not a love poem" is a counter-opinion, not an evaluation.

**9.** Biography can be a valid lens if it is supported and used alongside the text. [1] But the poem names no one: the painter is only "he", and the woman is "A nameless girl". [1]
"Only" and "nothing beyond that" shut out the general pattern the poem builds, "all his canvases" and
its list of types, which could apply to anyone who idealises another person. [1] Improvement: keep
the biography as one tentative lens beside a wider, text-based reading. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Context earns credit when tied to specific lines, not when it replaces them.

**10.** Model plan (indicative points):
Thesis: to the council and developer the plot is property; to Nan it is labour and food; the ending
lets nature claim it too, asking who has a right to a place. [1]
Point 1: "plot 14 of 40", "the council let the land" [1]; the official view reduces the place to a
number and a transaction. [1]
Point 2: "Nan dug it for thirty-one years", "she barrowed in manure" [1]; her claim is made by work,
turning cinders into soil. [1]
Point 3: "Behind her the new hoarding"; "Beside her a blackbird" [1]; the parallel sentences place
Nan between the developer's future and nature's indifference. [1]
Counter-reading: the blackbird may seem consoling, but "as if" suggests illusion, so the ending stays
uneasy. [1]
The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point.
*Examiner insight:* Each point must say whose meaning it shows, or the plan loses the question's focus.

**11.** Model plan (indicative points):
Thesis: in both passages a viewer with power fixes another person or place in a single meaning, and
the text restores what that view leaves out. [1]
Comparison 1: A's "every canvas means / The same one meaning" and B's "a regeneration opportunity"
both reduce something living to one idea [1]; the painter's view is private desire, the developer's
is public commerce. [1]
Comparison 2: A's paintings hide her present sorrow, "Not wan with waiting, not with sorrow dim"; B's
"alternative provision" hides the labour in soil "full of cinders" [1]; each shows a cost the powerful
view ignores. [1]
Comparison 3: A corrects the view directly with the repeated "Not as she is" [1]; B corrects it
through a grandchild narrator ("my mother") and a quiet closing image [1]; the poem negates, the prose
shows. [1]
Lens link: gender suits A, class suits B; both ask who decides how something is seen. [1]
Reveals: literature gives back the meaning that ownership and desire take away. [1]
The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point.
*Examiner insight:* Weave both passages into each point; all of A, then all of B, is two analyses.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a lens and then retelling what happens, with no analysis of a word or image.
- Treating another reader's interpretation as fact instead of testing it.
- Evaluating by disagreement ("I think it is not a love poem") instead of applying criteria.
- Using biography or history in place of the text rather than alongside it.
- Comparison plans that give each passage its own block.
- A thesis that states a topic ("both are about power") instead of an arguable claim.
