These questions cover 3.2.1.2 World cities – London and New York in
Section A Urban Issues and Challenges of the OxfordAQA International GCSE
Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The two districts in
question 5 and their data are fictional and illustrative; every other
question asks you to use London or New York.

Revise first with the [World cities study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-world-cities-london-new-york/)
and the [World cities revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-world-cities-london-new-york-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** What is meant by a *world city*? **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between national and international
migration. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** ways in which London or New York is important for
global finance and business. **[2]**

**4.** Define the terms *brownfield site* and *greenfield site*. **[2]**

**5.** Table 1 shows illustrative data for two fictional districts in
the same world city.

| Indicator | District P | District Q |
|---|---|---|
| Average annual household income (thousand dollars) | 32 | 120 |
| Children living in poverty (%) | 41 | 9 |
| Adults with a university degree (%) | 24 | 68 |
| Life expectancy (years) | 78 | 84 |

**(a)** How many times greater is average household income in District
Q than in District P? Give your answer to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(b)** Using Table 1, describe the inequalities between the two
districts. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** reason why life expectancy is lower in District
P. **[2]**

### Section B

**6.** Explain how international migration has influenced the growth
and character of London or New York. **[4]**

**7.** Describe **two** environmental opportunities in London or New
York created by urban greening. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why traffic congestion is a challenge in London or New
York, and how the city has tried to reduce it. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **one** challenge of managing either waste or water supply
in London or New York. **[3]**

**10.** For a flagship urban regeneration project you have studied,
assess the extent to which it has benefited the people who lived in the
area before regeneration. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A city with influence beyond its own country [1], as a global
centre of finance, business, trade, culture or politics, for example
London or New York [1].
*Examiner insight:* "a very big city" earns nothing; world cities are
defined by global influence, not population size alone.

**2.** National migration is movement within the same country, for
example from northern England to London [1]. International migration is
movement between countries, for example from the Caribbean to London
[1].

**3.** Any two, with named detail for credit, for example: home of a
major stock exchange (London Stock Exchange; New York Stock Exchange or
Nasdaq) [1]; headquarters of many global banks and companies in the
City of London, Canary Wharf or Wall Street [1]; a time zone that
links Asian and American markets (London) [1].

**4.** Brownfield: land that has been built on before and is available
for reuse, such as old docks or factory sites [1]. Greenfield: land not
previously built on, often farmland at the edge of a city [1].

**5. (a)** 120 / 32 = **3.8 times** [1].
**(b)** District Q is much wealthier, with average income of 120
thousand dollars compared with 32 thousand in P [1]. Child poverty is
much higher in P (41%) than Q (9%) [1]. Education and health are also
unequal: 68% of adults in Q have a degree compared with 24% in P, and
life expectancy is 6 years lower in P (78 compared with 84) [1].
**(c)** Any one, developed, for example: lower incomes mean poorer diet
and housing conditions [1], leading to more illness such as respiratory
disease from damp or overcrowded homes [1]; or less access to good
healthcare [1] because of cost or fewer local services [1].
*Examiner insight:* in describe questions, compare paired figures from
the table; explanation is not needed for (b).

**6.** Up to 4 marks for linked points with specific detail, for
example (London): migrants from the Caribbean arrived from 1948, followed
by South Asian, African and, after 2004, Eastern European migrants [1];
this added to population growth, and around four in ten Londoners were
born outside the UK [1]; it created distinctive neighbourhoods such as
Brick Lane's Bangladeshi community [1] and cultural events such as the
Notting Hill Carnival, making London highly multicultural [1].
For New York, credit Ellis Island (1892–1954), later Caribbean, Latin
American and Asian migration, more than a third born abroad, and
neighbourhoods such as Chinatown and Little Italy.

**7.** Two opportunities, 2 marks each (identify and develop), for
example:
- Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park in London [1] turned former industrial
  land into parkland and wetland habitat for wildlife and recreation [1].
- London becoming a National Park City in 2019 [1] encourages more
  green space, tree planting and outdoor activity across the city [1].
- The High Line in New York [1] turned a disused elevated railway into a
  planted walkway, adding green space in a dense area [1].
- Freshkills Park in New York [1] is turning a former landfill site into
  a large park [1].

**8.** Levels-style marking.
- Level 1 (1–2): simple statements, such as "too many cars".
- Level 2 (3–4): explains causes, such as high population, commuters
  from suburbs and delivery traffic on old, narrow streets, with some
  detail of one response.
- Level 3 (5–6): clear explanation of why congestion is a problem
  (delays, air pollution, lost working time) and specific responses,
  for example London's Congestion Charge (2003), ULEZ (2019), the
  Underground and the Elizabeth line, or New York's subway and its
  congestion pricing for vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th
  Street (from January 2025), with some comment on their effect.

**9.** For example (London water): Victorian sewers overflow into the
River Thames during heavy rain [1], polluting the river and harming
wildlife [1]; the Thames Tideway Tunnel was built to capture these
overflows [1]. Or (New York waste): since the Fresh Kills landfill closed
in 2001 [1], the city has had to send much of its rubbish to other
states [1], which is costly and creates transport emissions [1].

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response.
Indicative content (London Docklands):
- Why regeneration was needed: docks closed from the late 1960s to 1981
  as ships grew larger and container trade moved to Tilbury; derelict
  land and high unemployment.
- Features: London Docklands Development Corporation (1981–1998);
  Canary Wharf; Docklands Light Railway (1987); London City Airport
  (1987); Jubilee line extension (1999); housing and the ExCeL centre.
- Benefits to existing residents: better transport links; cleaned-up
  environment; some new services and jobs in construction and
  services.
- Limits: many office jobs went to commuters with financial skills
  rather than former dock workers; new housing was often too expensive;
  some residents felt excluded from decisions.
- Judgement: for example, "the project brought large economic gains to
  the area, but original residents benefited less than incomers, so it
  helped them only to a limited extent".
Top-level answers use specific detail throughout and finish with a
supported judgement. Credit the High Line or another flagship project
with equivalent detail.
*Examiner insight:* "assess the extent" needs both sides and a final
judgement on how far, not a description of the project.
