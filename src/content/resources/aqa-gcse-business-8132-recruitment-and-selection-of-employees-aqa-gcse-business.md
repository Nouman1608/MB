Two words are often mixed up. **Recruitment** is attracting suitable applicants to a vacancy. **Selection** is choosing the best of those applicants.

## Why a business recruits

A vacancy appears for one of three broad reasons, and the reason affects how the business should fill it.

- **Growth.** A new branch, a second shift or rising orders means more people are needed. The business may need several people at once, so a wide external search is likely.
- **Replacing a leaver.** Someone resigns, retires or is dismissed. The job already exists, so the existing job description can be updated rather than written from scratch.
- **New skills.** A business adopting new technology or entering a new market may have nobody who can do the work. This usually points towards external recruitment.

## Internal or external recruitment

Internal recruitment fills a vacancy from people already employed by the business, usually through promotion or a transfer. External recruitment looks outside the business.

The topic guide lists the standard advantages and disadvantages. In an exam, the skill is to decide which ones matter for the business in the case. Ask three questions:

1. **Does anybody inside have the skills?** If the vacancy needs a skill the business lacks, internal recruitment cannot work, however cheap it is.
2. **How urgent is it?** Internal recruitment is usually quicker, because there is no external advert and the candidate needs little induction.
3. **What does the business need besides skills?** A business that wants fresh ideas, perhaps because sales have stalled, gains more from an outsider. A business that wants to reward loyalty and show staff that promotion is possible gains more from an internal appointment.

Remember the hidden cost of internal recruitment: the promoted worker leaves a gap, so the business often has to recruit again for the old job.

### Ways of advertising a vacancy

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| Staff noticeboard or intranet | The business's own website and social media |
| Email to all staff | Job websites |
| Managers suggesting suitable staff | Local or specialist printed press |
| | Jobcentres, recruitment agencies and word of mouth |

An agency finds and screens candidates, saving management time, but charges a fee. A specialist magazine reaches people with a particular skill.

### Worked example: comparing the cost of each route

Fenwold Garden Centre needs a deputy manager on a salary of £26,000. The owner compares two options.

**External:** a job website advert costs £450 and an agency charges 15% of the first year's salary. Interviewing takes 12 hours of the owner's time, which the owner values at £20 an hour.

- Agency fee: 15% × £26,000 = £3,900
- Interview time: 12 × £20 = £240
- Total: £450 + £3,900 + £240 = **£4,590**

**Internal:** promote a senior sales assistant. Interviewing takes 6 hours (£120). The assistant's old job must then be filled with a local advert (£150) and 4 hours of interviews (£80).

- Total: £120 + £150 + £80 = **£350**

The internal route is £4,590 − £350 = £4,240 cheaper. That is not the end of the answer. If the garden centre wants to open a café and nobody inside has catering experience, an external deputy may be worth the extra cost. A strong answer uses the figures and then weighs them against the skills the business needs.

## From vacancy to appointment

### Job analysis

Job analysis is studying what the job involves: the tasks, the skills needed and how the post fits the structure. It is the first step because every later document depends on it.

### Job description

A job description describes the job: the title, the main duties and responsibilities, where the job is based and who the post holder reports to. It tells applicants what they would do.

### Person specification

A person specification describes the person: the qualifications, skills, experience and qualities needed. Many businesses split it into **essential** criteria (an applicant without them is rejected) and **desirable** criteria (which help choose between strong applicants).

The classic exam mistake is swapping these two. Job description is about the job; person specification is about the person.

### Selection methods

After applications arrive as application forms or CVs with a covering letter, the business shortlists those who best match the person specification and then uses one or more selection methods.

| Method | What it shows | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Interview | Communication, attitude, how the applicant explains past experience | Candidates can rehearse answers; interviewers can be swayed by first impressions |
| Skills or aptitude test | Whether the applicant can actually do a task, such as typing speed or a practical test | Takes time to design and run |
| Group exercise or assessment day | Teamwork and leadership | Expensive, so usually kept for senior or graduate roles |
| References | A previous employer's view of reliability | Referees may be reluctant to criticise |

Choose methods that test what the job needs. A trial shift suits a barista. A written test suits an accounts assistant. An interview alone suits neither, because talking well is not the same as doing the job well.

## Why getting it right pays

The specification names three benefits of an effective recruitment process. Link each to a consequence for the business.

- **High productivity.** The right person has the skills, so output per worker is higher and less supervision is needed.
- **High-quality production or customer service.** Fewer errors, fewer complaints and more repeat customers.
- **Staff retention.** Someone who suits the job is more likely to stay, so the business avoids repeating the cost of recruitment and induction.

### Worked example: the cost of poor selection

Ashcombe Café recruits and trains 10 new staff a year, because many leave within months. Each appointment costs about £1,200 in advertising, interviews and training. The manager adds a trial shift to selection and the number leaving each year falls to 4.

Appointments avoided: 10 − 4 = 6. Saving: 6 × £1,200 = **£7,200 a year**, before counting the better service from experienced staff. The trial shift costs some manager time, so a full answer weighs that against the saving.

## Contracts of employment

A contract of employment is a legal agreement between employer and employee that sets out the terms of the job, such as hours, pay and holiday.

| Contract | Key feature | Suits the business when |
|---|---|---|
| Full-time | Works the business's standard full week | Work is steady and the business wants commitment and continuity |
| Part-time | Works fewer hours than full-time, usually set hours | Demand peaks at certain times, such as lunchtimes or weekends |
| Job share | Two people share one full-time job | The business wants to keep skilled staff who cannot work full-time |
| Zero-hour | No guaranteed hours; called in when needed | Demand is unpredictable, as in events catering |

**Benefits of full-time employment.** The business gets staff who are available throughout the week, build experience quickly and are more likely to stay. Workers gain a regular, predictable income.

**Benefits of part-time employment.** The business can match staff to busy periods and pay only for the hours needed. It can also recruit from people who cannot work a full week, such as students or parents, which widens the pool of applicants.

Applied example: a sandwich shop that is busy from 11:30 to 14:00 gains little from a full-time worker standing idle at 16:00. Two part-timers covering the lunch rush give more cover for the same wage cost.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the job description and the person specification.
- Saying internal recruitment is always cheaper without mentioning the vacancy it leaves behind.
- Treating recruitment and selection as one word with one meaning.
- Listing selection methods without saying why one suits the job in the case.
- Describing a zero-hour contract as a part-time contract. Part-time staff normally have set hours.
- Giving "staff retention" as a benefit with no consequence, such as lower recruitment costs.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State or identify:** a short answer, such as one item on a person specification.
- **Explain:** a point developed with a reason, for example "an internal candidate already knows the firm's systems, so induction time is shorter."
- **Analyse:** a chain of two or three linked consequences applied to the case.
- **Evaluate or justify:** compare the options, use any figures given, and give a decision with the factor that most affected it.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between recruitment and selection?**
Recruitment is attracting suitable applicants to a vacancy, for example by advertising it. Selection is choosing the best applicant, using methods such as interviews, tests and references.

**When is external recruitment better than internal recruitment?**
When nobody inside the business has the skills needed, when the business wants new ideas, or when it needs many staff at once. It costs more and takes longer.

**Why is staff retention a benefit of effective recruitment?**
A worker who suits the job is more likely to stay, so the business avoids paying again to advertise, select and train a replacement.
