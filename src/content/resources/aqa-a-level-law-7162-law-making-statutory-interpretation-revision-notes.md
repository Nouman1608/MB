Condensed notes on **Law making: statutory interpretation** in AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1: the literal, golden and mischief rules and the purposive approach; intrinsic and extrinsic aids; the impact of EU law and the Human Rights Act 1998; and advantages and disadvantages. For full explanation and a worked application, see the [Statutory Interpretation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-statutory-interpretation/).

## Why interpretation is needed

Broad terms; ambiguity; drafting errors; new technology or social change; unforeseen situations. General help: **Interpretation Act 1978** (masculine includes feminine; singular includes plural, unless contrary intention).

## Case bank by rule

### Literal rule: plain meaning, even if absurd

| Case | Words | Result |
|---|---|---|
| *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868) | "any person entitled to vote" | Impersonating a dead voter not an offence |
| *Fisher v Bell* (1960) | "offer for sale" | Knife in window is an invitation to treat; not guilty |
| *LNER v Berriman* (1946) | "relaying or repairing" | Oiling points was maintenance; no compensation |

### Golden rule: modify to avoid absurdity

| Case | Approach | Result |
|---|---|---|
| *R v Allen* (1872) | Narrow | "Marry" read as go through a ceremony of marriage |
| *Adler v George* (1964) | Narrow | "In the vicinity of" read as "in or in the vicinity of" |
| *Re Sigsworth* (1935) | Broad | Murderer son could not inherit as "issue" |
| *R v Registrar General, ex p Smith* (1991) | Broad | Birth certificate refused because of risk to birth mother |

  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-statutory-interpretation--02-golden-rule.webp" width="900" height="613" alt="The golden rule has a narrow approach, choosing between meanings to avoid absurdity, as in R v Allen (“shall marry” meaning a ceremony), and a broad approach, modifying a single meaning to avoid repugnance, as in Re Sigsworth and ex p Smith. In Adler v George “in the vicinity of” was read as “in or in the vicinity of”." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Narrow: pick the sensible meaning. Broad: change the only meaning to avoid a repugnant result.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Mischief rule: *Heydon's Case* (1584)

Four questions: the common law before the Act; the mischief; the remedy; the reason for the remedy.

- *Royal College of Nursing v DHSS* (1981): nurses' role lawful; Act aimed at unsafe abortions (3–2).
- *Elliott v Grey* (1960): jacked-up car without a battery was "used on a road".

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-statutory-interpretation--03-mischief-rule.webp" width="900" height="553" alt="Heydon’s Case asks four questions: the common law before the Act, the mischief or defect, the remedy Parliament chose, and the true reason for it; the court then interprets the Act to suppress the mischief. In RCN v DHSS nurses’ involvement was lawful because the mischief was backstreet abortions; in Elliott v Grey a car with no battery was “used on a road”." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Find the defect the Act was passed to cure, then read the words to cure it.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Purposive approach: purpose of the Act as a whole

- *Jones v Tower Boot* (1997): "in the course of employment" read broadly to fulfil anti-discrimination purpose.
- *R (Quintavalle) v Secretary of State for Health* (2003): cloned embryos regulated.

## Aids

| Intrinsic | Extrinsic |
|---|---|
| Long and short title | Dictionaries |
| Preamble | Earlier Acts, historical setting |
| Headings | *Hansard* (*Pepper v Hart*, 1993): ambiguity, minister or promoter, clear statement |
| Interpretation sections | Law Commission and committee reports (*Black-Clawson*, 1975) |
| Schedules | Explanatory notes (*Westminster CC v NASS*, 2002) |
| | Treaties |

## Rules of language

| Rule | Meaning | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Ejusdem generis | General words after a list take the same kind | *Powell v Kempton Park* (1899): indoor places only |
| Expressio unius | Listing some excludes others | *R v Inhabitants of Sedgley* (1831): coal mines only |
| Noscitur a sociis | Words read in context | *IRC v Frere* (1965): annual interest only |

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-statutory-interpretation--05-rules-of-language.webp" width="900" height="598" alt="Three rules of language. Ejusdem generis: “house, office, room or other place” covered indoor places only (Powell v Kempton Park). Noscitur a sociis: “interest” among annual payments meant annual interest only (IRC v Frere)." loading="lazy">

</figure>

## EU law and the Human Rights Act

- **EU law (membership):** duty to interpret national law, as far as possible, to give effect to EU law; encouraged purposive interpretation. Largely historical since withdrawal.
- **Human Rights Act 1998 section 3:** read compatibly "so far as it is possible to do so", even without ambiguity.
- **Section 4:** declaration of incompatibility if impossible; the Act stays in force.

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-statutory-interpretation--06-eu-hra-impact.webp" width="900" height="546" alt="EU law, while the UK was a member, required national law to be read so far as possible to give effect to EU law, encouraging purposive reasoning. Section 3 of the Human Rights Act requires a Convention-compatible reading so far as possible, even without ambiguity; where impossible, higher courts may make a section 4 declaration of incompatibility, but cannot strike the Act down." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Section 3 reaches further than the ordinary rules; section 4 is the fallback, not a strike-down power.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Evaluation

| Approach | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | Supremacy; certainty | Absurd or unjust results |
| Golden | Avoids absurdity | "Absurd" undefined |
| Mischief | Fills gaps; Parliament's aim | Dated; looks like judicial law-making |
| Purposive | Flexible; modern | Purpose hard to find; less predictable |

**Central tension:** respect for Parliament's words versus achieving Parliament's purpose and justice.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Literal rule | Plain ordinary meaning of words |
| Golden rule | Literal meaning modified to avoid absurdity |
| Mischief rule | Interpretation to suppress the defect the Act targeted |
| Purposive approach | Interpretation to achieve the purpose of the Act |
| Intrinsic aid | Help found within the Act itself |
| Extrinsic aid | Help found outside the Act |
| Ejusdem generis | General words limited to the same kind as specific words before them |
| Noscitur a sociis | A word known by its context |
| Expressio unius | Express mention of one thing excludes others |

## Exam traps

- Defining rules with no application to the facts.
- Golden rule confused with mischief rule.
- *Hansard* treated as always usable.
- Section 3 described as striking down Acts.
- EU interpretive duty treated as still applying in full.

## Self-test

1. Why was the defendant in *Whiteley v Chappell* not guilty?
2. Which rule was used in *Fisher v Bell*, and why was the result criticised?
3. Distinguish the narrow and broad golden rule with one case each.
4. State the four questions in *Heydon's Case*.
5. How did the mischief rule apply in *RCN v DHSS*?
6. How does the purposive approach differ from the mischief rule?
7. Give three intrinsic aids.
8. State the three conditions in *Pepper v Hart*.
9. Explain ejusdem generis with a case.
10. Explain noscitur a sociis with a case.
11. How does section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 go further than the ordinary rules?
12. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of the literal rule.

**Answers:** 1. A dead person is not "entitled to vote", so impersonating one was not literally within the offence. 2. The literal rule; the shopkeeper escaped although the Act was clearly aimed at stopping the sale of such knives. 3. Narrow: choose between meanings, *R v Allen*; broad: modify a single meaning to avoid a repugnant result, *Re Sigsworth*. 4. What was the common law before the Act; what was the mischief; what remedy did Parliament provide; what was the reason for the remedy. 5. The Act aimed to end unsafe backstreet abortions, so nurses acting under a doctor's supervision were within it. 6. The purposive approach considers the purpose of the Act as a whole and a wider range of material, not just the defect in earlier law. 7. Any three: long title, short title, preamble, headings, interpretation sections, schedules. 8. The legislation is ambiguous or obscure or leads to absurdity; the statement relied on was made by a minister or other promoter; the statement is clear. 9. General words after a list are limited to the same kind: *Powell v Kempton Park* (1899), "other place" meant indoor places. 10. A word is read in the light of the words around it: *IRC v Frere* (1965), "other annual payments" read with "interest" and "annuities". 11. It requires a compatible reading so far as possible even where the words are not ambiguous. 12. Advantage: respects parliamentary supremacy and gives certainty. Disadvantage: can produce absurd or unjust results, as in *Berriman*.
