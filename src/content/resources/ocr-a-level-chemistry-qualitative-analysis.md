## What 3.1.4 asks of you

| Statement | Skill | Additional guidance (specification) |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1.4(a)(i) | Identify the anions CO₃²⁻ (reaction with H⁺(aq) to form CO₂ gas), SO₄²⁻ (precipitation with Ba²⁺(aq)) and Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ (see 3.1.3(g)) in an unknown compound, on a test-tube scale | The sequence of tests is carbonate, then sulfate, then halide; the specification notes that BaCO₃ and Ag₂SO₄ are both insoluble. Cross-reference to 2.1.4(c). PAG4; HSW4 |
| 3.1.4(a)(ii) | Identify the cation NH₄⁺ by reaction with warm NaOH(aq), forming NH₃ | PAG4; HSW4 |

PAG4 is "Qualitative analysis of ions". The specification lists its skills as using apparatus for qualitative tests for ions and making and recording qualitative observations. Its suggested activity is identifying the anions and cations in a mixture of Group 2 salts. Later, in 5.3.2, the same tests return alongside tests for transition metal ions.

The outcome asks for "processes and techniques", so you need more than a list of colours. You need to know which reagent to add, in what order, what you see, what equation explains it, and why the order matters.

## Working on a test-tube scale

Each test uses small amounts: a spatula tip of solid dissolved in about 2 cm³ of distilled water, with reagents added a few drops at a time from a dropping pipette.

- Use distilled or deionised water. Tap water can contain chloride and sulfate ions, so it can give a false positive.
- Take a fresh portion of the unknown for each separate test unless the plan says to carry on with the same solution.
- Run a blank (distilled water plus the same reagents) if a result is faint or unclear.
- Warm with a water bath rather than a direct flame when only gentle warming is needed.
- Record what you see in precise words: "white precipitate", "effervescence", "colourless gas", "no visible change". Write the observation first and the conclusion second.

## 3.1.4(a)(i): Testing for carbonate ions

**Reagent:** any dilute acid supplies H⁺(aq). When further tests will follow on the same solution, use **dilute nitric acid**, for the reasons explained in the sequence section below.

**Observation:** effervescence (fizzing). Bubble the gas through limewater, a solution of calcium hydroxide, and it turns milky as a white precipitate of calcium carbonate forms. That confirms the gas is carbon dioxide.

**Equations:**

- Ionic: CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)
- Full, for potassium carbonate: K₂CO₃(aq) + 2HNO₃(aq) → 2KNO₃(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)
- Limewater: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

This is the neutralisation of a carbonate by an acid from 2.1.4(c), now used as a test. Fizzing alone is not proof. Other reactions also release gases, so the limewater result is what identifies CO₂.

### Worked example 1: a carbonate and a gas volume

A student adds excess dilute nitric acid to 0.691 g of potassium carbonate. Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide formed at room temperature and pressure. Use Ar values K = 39.1, C = 12.0, O = 16.0. Take the molar gas volume at RTP as 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. Mr(K₂CO₃) = 2(39.1) + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 138.2
2. n(K₂CO₃) = 0.691 ÷ 138.2 = 0.00500 mol
3. One mole of carbonate gives one mole of CO₂, so n(CO₂) = 0.00500 mol.
4. Volume = 0.00500 × 24.0 = 0.120 dm³ = **120 cm³**

## 3.1.4(a)(i): Testing for sulfate ions

**Reagent:** aqueous barium ions, Ba²⁺(aq), as barium nitrate or barium chloride solution. Add it to the solution after it has been acidified with dilute nitric acid.

**Observation:** a white precipitate of barium sulfate.

**Equation:** Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

Why acidify first? Barium carbonate is also a white solid that does not dissolve in water. If carbonate ions are still in the solution, Ba²⁺ precipitates them too and you cannot tell the two precipitates apart. Adding acid first destroys any carbonate as CO₂, so a white precipitate that forms afterwards must be barium sulfate.

### Worked example 2: how much sulfate?

Because BaSO₄ is insoluble, the precipitate can be filtered, dried and weighed. A 0.800 g sample of lawn feed contains ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄, and no other sulfate. It is dissolved, acidified and treated with excess barium nitrate. The dry precipitate has a mass of 1.272 g. Calculate the percentage by mass of ammonium sulfate. Use Ar values Ba = 137.3, S = 32.1, O = 16.0, N = 14.0, H = 1.0.

1. Mr(BaSO₄) = 137.3 + 32.1 + 4(16.0) = 233.4
2. n(BaSO₄) = 1.272 ÷ 233.4 = 0.00545 mol
3. One (NH₄)₂SO₄ gives one SO₄²⁻, which gives one BaSO₄. So n((NH₄)₂SO₄) = 0.00545 mol.
4. Mr((NH₄)₂SO₄) = 2(14.0) + 8(1.0) + 32.1 + 4(16.0) = 132.1
5. Mass = 0.00545 × 132.1 = 0.720 g
6. Percentage = 0.720 ÷ 0.800 × 100 = **90.0%**

Carry unrounded values through the working. Round only the final answer.

## 3.1.4(a)(i): Testing for halide ions

This test is set out in 3.1.3(g), and 3.1.4(a) brings it into the analysis sequence.

**Reagent:** first make the solution acidic with dilute nitric acid; then add AgNO₃(aq), silver nitrate solution.

**Observations and follow-up with aqueous ammonia:**

| Ion in the unknown | Solid formed with Ag⁺(aq) | Its colour | Then dilute NH₃(aq) | Then concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chloride | AgCl | white | precipitate disappears | precipitate disappears |
| bromide | AgBr | cream | precipitate remains | precipitate disappears |
| iodide | AgI | yellow | precipitate remains | precipitate remains |

**Ionic equations:**

- Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
- Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s)
- Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s)

White, cream and yellow can look alike in a test tube, especially in small amounts. The ammonia step separates them. The specification says complexes with ammonia are not required other than observations, so you need to know what dissolves, not the formula of what forms.

The nitric acid has the same job as before. Silver carbonate is insoluble, so carbonate ions left in the solution would give a precipitate with Ag⁺ and confuse the result.

## 3.1.4(a)(ii): Testing for ammonium ions

**Method:** add aqueous sodium hydroxide to the solid or solution and warm gently. Hold a piece of damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the test tube, without touching the glass.

**Observation:** the litmus paper turns blue. You may also notice the sharp smell of ammonia, but do not sniff the tube directly.

**Equations:**

- Ionic: NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)
- Full, for ammonium chloride: NH₄Cl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)

Two details carry the explanation. **Warming** matters because ammonia is very soluble in water; at room temperature most of it stays dissolved, and heat drives the gas out. The litmus must be **damp** because ammonia only shows as an alkali when it dissolves in water and releases OH⁻ ions. Dry paper may not change. If the paper touches the tube, sodium hydroxide on the glass turns it blue and gives a false positive.

## The sequence: carbonate, then sulfate, then halide

When one solution may contain several anions, the specification sets the order of tests as carbonate, sulfate, then halide. The reason is that each later reagent also precipitates an ion tested earlier.

| Step | Reagent | Positive result | Why it must come at this point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dilute HNO₃(aq), until fizzing stops | Effervescence; gas turns limewater milky | Removes CO₃²⁻, which would otherwise precipitate with both Ba²⁺ and Ag⁺ |
| 2 | Ba(NO₃)₂(aq), in excess | White precipitate (BaSO₄) | BaCO₃ is insoluble, so carbonate must already be gone. Ag₂SO₄ is insoluble too, so sulfate must be removed before step 3 |
| 3 | AgNO₃(aq), then NH₃(aq) | White, cream or yellow precipitate | Only halide ions are left to precipitate with Ag⁺ |

If step 2 gives a precipitate, filter it off and carry on with the filtrate. That removes the sulfate before silver ions are added.

**Choice of acid.** Hydrochloric acid would add Cl⁻ ions, so step 3 would always show chloride. Sulfuric acid would add SO₄²⁻ ions, so step 2 would always show sulfate. Nitric acid adds only nitrate ions, which none of the reagents precipitates.

**Choice of barium salt.** Barium chloride would also add Cl⁻ ions and spoil the halide test. Barium nitrate does not. When you only need to test for sulfate and no halide test follows, either barium salt works.

### Worked example 3: identifying an unknown salt

A white solid, W, is a single compound. A student records these observations.

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| W dissolved in distilled water, then dilute HNO₃(aq) added | no effervescence |
| Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) added to the acidified solution | no visible change |
| AgNO₃(aq) added to the same solution | yellow precipitate |
| Concentrated NH₃(aq) added to the precipitate | precipitate does not dissolve |
| Fresh W warmed with NaOH(aq), damp red litmus held at the mouth | litmus turns blue |

Reasoning, step by step:

1. No effervescence with acid: no carbonate.
2. No precipitate with Ba²⁺ in acid: no sulfate.
3. Yellow precipitate with Ag⁺, insoluble in concentrated ammonia: iodide, I⁻.
4. Alkaline gas on warming with NaOH: ammonia, so the cation is NH₄⁺.
5. W is **ammonium iodide, NH₄I**.

Equations: Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s), and NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l).

## Writing observations that earn credit

- Name the state and the colour: "white precipitate", not "goes white" or "goes cloudy".
- For a gas, describe what you see (effervescence, bubbles) and then the test result, such as "limewater turns milky".
- "No visible change" is an observation. "Nothing happens" is weaker, because something may happen that you cannot see.
- Keep observation and inference apart. "Cream precipitate" is what you see; "bromide present" is what you conclude.

## Common errors

- Using hydrochloric acid to acidify before a halide test, then reporting chloride that came from the acid.
- Using barium chloride for the sulfate test and then testing the same solution for halides.
- Adding barium ions before checking for carbonate, then reading a white BaCO₃ precipitate as sulfate.
- Saying that AgBr dissolves in dilute ammonia, or that AgI dissolves in concentrated ammonia.
- Using dry litmus, blue litmus, or forgetting to warm in the ammonium test.
- Writing BaSO₄ or the silver halides with (aq) instead of (s) in the ionic equation.
- Writing the carbonate ionic equation with only one H⁺.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the order of anion tests in OCR A Level Chemistry?**
The OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432) specification sets the order as carbonate, then sulfate, then halide. Each later reagent would also precipitate an ion tested earlier, because BaCO₃, silver carbonate and Ag₂SO₄ are all insoluble.

**Why is nitric acid used to acidify a solution before ion tests?**
Hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions and sulfuric acid would add sulfate ions, giving false positives. Nitric acid adds only nitrate ions, which none of the test reagents precipitate.

**Is fizzing with acid enough to prove a carbonate is present?**
No. Other reactions also release gases, so the gas must be bubbled through limewater; it turns milky only if the gas is carbon dioxide, confirming carbonate.

**Do I need to know the formulae of silver–ammonia complexes for OCR?**
No. The specification says complexes with ammonia are not required other than observations, so you need to know which silver halide precipitates dissolve in dilute and concentrated ammonia, not the formula of the complex.
