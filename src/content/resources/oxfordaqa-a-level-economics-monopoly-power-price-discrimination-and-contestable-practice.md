## Questions

**1.** Explain two factors that influence the monopoly power of a firm. **[4]**

**2.** A sole toll-bridge operator in Country E faces demand P = 200 − 8Q, where Q is in thousand crossings and P is the toll. Average cost equals marginal cost at 56.

**(a)** Derive the marginal revenue equation. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the profit-maximising output and toll. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the supernormal profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate price elasticity of demand at this point. **[2]**
**(e)** State the output and toll in a competitive market with the same costs, and compare. **[2]**

**3.** A gas pipeline in Country F has total cost TC = 800 + 5Q.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by a natural monopoly. **[2]**
**(b)** Market demand is 40 units. Calculate average cost if one firm supplies it, and if two equal firms share it. **[3]**

**4.** Evaluate whether a monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. **[8]**

**5.** A swimming pool charges adults and students different entry prices for the same session. Explain four conditions needed for this price discrimination to work. **[4]**

**6.** A zoo has marginal cost of 24 per visitor. Weekend demand is P = 108 − 6Q and weekday demand is P = 48 − 2Q (Q in hundreds of visitors).

**(a)** Find the profit-maximising price and quantity at the weekend. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the profit-maximising price and quantity on weekdays. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate total profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate PED at each point and explain which market pays the higher price. **[3]**

**7.** A bike-hire scheme sold 500 hires at a single charge of 20 (300 to office workers, 200 to tourists). It then charged office workers 26 and tourists 14, selling 270 and 290 hires.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in total revenue. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate PED for each group. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why revenue rose. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how sunk costs affect the contestability of a market. **[4]**

**9.** A freight depot sells 70 loads a month at 18, with average cost of 13. An entrant could charge 16 and take all the trade for 4 months before the depot responds.

**(a)** Calculate the incumbent's monthly supernormal profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the entrant's profit over 4 months. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the net gain if sunk costs are 250, and if they are 1,000. **[2]**
**(d)** State which case deters entry. **[1]**

**10.** "Because the market for flights on a single route is contestable, a dominant airline's high market share is no cause for concern." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Barriers to entry: legal protection or large economies of scale keep rivals out [1], so supernormal profit lasts and the firm keeps control of price [1]. Product differentiation or advertising: a branded product with few close substitutes has a lower PED [1], so a price rise loses fewer sales [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each factor to pricing power. Naming a factor without saying how it changes the firm's control of price usually leaves the explanation incomplete.

**2. (a)** MR = 200 − 16Q [1].
**(b)** MR = MC: 200 − 16Q = 56 [1]. Q = 9 [1]. P = 200 − 8 × 9 = 128 [1].
**(c)** Profit = (128 − 56) × 9 [1] = 648 [1].
**(d)** PED = −(1/8) × 128/9 [1] = −1.78 [1].
**(e)** Competitive: P = MC = 56, Q = 18 [1]. The monopolist charges more than double the toll and sells half the output [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution that gives the price. Reading the toll from the MR equation is an easy slip; the price comes from demand.

**3. (a)** A market where long-run average cost is still falling when output reaches market demand [1], so one firm can supply at lower unit cost than several [1].
**(b)** One firm: AC = 800/40 + 5 = 25 [1]. Two firms with 20 each: AC = 800/20 + 5 = 45 [1], which is 80% higher because the fixed cost is paid twice [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the result to the duplicated fixed cost shows why one firm is cheapest. Bare figures say less.

**4.** Against: price above marginal cost and lower output than competition [1], for example 128 against 56 in question 2 [1]; allocative inefficiency and a welfare loss [1]. For: economies of scale can lower average cost [1]; in a natural monopoly one firm is cheapest [1]; supernormal profit can fund innovation [1]. Evaluation: consumers only gain if savings are passed on, which needs rivals or a credible threat of entry [1]; so monopoly is not always against consumers, but is more likely to be where barriers are strong and the good is essential [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation reads best when tied to a condition (pass-through, barriers, type of good) and ends in a judgement, rather than restating both lists.

**5.** The pool needs market power so that it can set price [1]. It must be able to separate adults from students, for example by checking student cards [1]. Students must not be able to resell entry to adults, which is arbitrage [1]. The groups need different PEDs, or there is no extra profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each condition to the pool. Four bare terms with no explanation tend to read as thin.

**6. (a)** Weekend: MR = 108 − 12Q = 24 [1]; Q = 7, P = 66 [1].
**(b)** Weekday: MR = 48 − 4Q = 24, so Q = 6 [1]; P = 36 [1].
**(c)** Profit = (66 − 24) × 7 + (36 − 24) × 6 = 294 + 72 [1] = 366 [1].
**(d)** Weekend PED = −(1/6) × 66/7 = −1.57 [1]; weekday PED = −(1/2) × 36/6 = −3 [1]. Weekend demand is less elastic, so it pays the higher price [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply MR = MC separately in each market. Using one combined MR curve is a different method and gives different answers.

**7. (a)** Revenue before = 20 × 500 = 10,000 [1]. After = 26 × 270 + 14 × 290 = 7,020 + 4,060 = 11,080 [1]. Change = +10.8% [1].
**(b)** Office workers: −10% ÷ +30% = −0.33 [1]. Tourists: +45% ÷ −30% = −1.5 [1].
**(c)** Office-worker demand is inelastic, so the higher charge raised revenue (6,000 to 7,020) [1]. Tourist demand is elastic, so the lower charge also raised revenue (4,000 to 4,060) [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out each percentage change before dividing, and keep the sign on PED, interpreting it rather than just quoting it.

**8.** Sunk costs cannot be recovered on leaving [1]. High sunk costs make entry risky [1], because an entrant loses them if the incumbent reacts [1]. That weakens hit-and-run entry and makes the market less contestable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep sunk costs distinct from fixed costs. Equipment that can be sold on exit does not hurt contestability.

**9. (a)** (18 − 13) × 70 = 350 [1].
**(b)** (16 − 13) × 70 = 210 a month [1]; over 4 months = 840 [1].
**(c)** 840 − 250 = 590 [1]; 840 − 1,000 = −160 [1].
**(d)** Sunk costs of 1,000 deter entry [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Deduct sunk costs once from the total earned, not from every month.

**10.** For: if entry and exit are cheap, the airline must hold price near average cost to deter entrants [1]; hit-and-run entry is possible when sunk costs are low [1]; so a high share gives little pricing power and profit stays normal [1]. Against: contestability depends on real barriers, such as scarce airport slots or large marketing sunk costs [1]; the incumbent can react with price cuts or loyalty schemes, so entry may not pay [1]; few markets are fully contestable [1]; a share figure alone says little about behaviour, so prices persistently above cost would count as evidence against contestability [1]; the airline may also be large because it is efficient, which could benefit passengers [1]. Judgement: the concern is smaller where sunk costs are low and entry is credible, and larger where barriers exist [1], so the claim holds only on conditions that need evidence, such as past entry and price levels [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the evidence that would settle the question (entry history, sunk costs, price against cost) tends to strengthen a conclusion more than a general opinion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the monopoly price from MR rather than demand.
- Using the wrong slope for MR, which is twice as steep as a linear demand curve.
- Leaving out units or the sign in PED and revenue answers.
- Listing price discrimination conditions with no application.
- Forgetting that discrimination needs MR = MC in each segment separately.
- Treating sunk costs as fixed costs.
- Equating contestable with many firms.
- Evaluating by repeating the lists with no judgement.
