## Definitions to learn

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Mixed economic system | Resources are allocated by both the private sector (price mechanism) and the government (public sector) |
| Maximum price | A legal limit above which a price cannot be charged |
| Minimum price | A legal limit below which a price cannot fall |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending on goods and services, paid to the government by sellers |
| Subsidy | A payment from the government that lowers the cost of producing or buying a good |
| Regulation | Laws and rules that control how firms and consumers behave |
| Privatisation | Sale of state-owned assets to the private sector |
| Nationalisation | Transfer of private-sector firms to government ownership |
| Direct provision | The government supplies goods and services itself |

## The three price diagrams in steps

1. Draw demand and supply, mark equilibrium price and quantity.
2. Draw the controlled price as a horizontal line. A **maximum** price must be below equilibrium. A **minimum** price must be above it.
3. Read quantity demanded where the line meets demand, and quantity supplied where it meets supply.
4. Larger quantity demanded than supplied is a **shortage**. Larger quantity supplied than demanded is a **surplus**.
5. Say who gains, who loses and what the government must do next (ration, buy up, enforce).

| Market | Price is | Maximum price creates | Minimum price creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Price of the good | Shortage, black market | Surplus, government buying |
| Labour | Wage | Labour shortage (rare) | Unemployment |
| Foreign exchange | Exchange rate | Excess demand for the currency, so the central bank sells it | Excess supply of the currency, so the central bank buys it |

**Worked reminder (maximum price, illustrative).** Qd = 240 − 8P, Qs = −48 + 16P.
- Equilibrium: 288 = 24P, so P = 12, Q = 144.
- Maximum price 9: Qd = 168, Qs = 96, shortage = 72.
- Buyers would pay up to P where 240 − 8P = 96, so P = 18, which is the likely black-market price.

**Worked reminder (minimum price, illustrative).** Qd = 300 − 6P, Qs = −60 + 12P.
- Equilibrium: 360 = 18P, so P = 20, Q = 180.
- Minimum price 25: Qd = 150, Qs = 240, surplus = 90.
- If the government buys the surplus, cost = 25 × 90 = 2,250.

## Taxes and subsidies in steps

| | Indirect tax | Subsidy |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on supply | Shifts up (left) by the tax | Shifts down (right) by the subsidy |
| Market price | Rises | Falls |
| Quantity | Falls | Rises |
| Government | Gains revenue = tax × new quantity | Pays cost = subsidy × new quantity |
| Burden or gain | Split between consumers and producers | Split between consumers and producers |
| Usually aimed at | Demerit goods, external costs, revenue | Merit goods, external benefits |

Key rules:
- The vertical gap between the old and new supply curve equals the tax or subsidy.
- Consumer burden per unit = new price − old price.
- Producer burden per unit = tax − consumer burden.
- The more **inelastic** side bears more of a tax and gains more from a subsidy.

**Worked reminder (tax, illustrative).** Qd = 500 − 10P, Qs = −100 + 15P.
- Equilibrium: 600 = 25P, so P = 24, Q = 260.
- Tax 5: Qs = −100 + 15(P − 5) = −175 + 15P. Then 675 = 25P, so P = 27, Q = 230.
- Consumers pay 3 more, producers bear 5 − 3 = 2. Revenue = 5 × 230 = 1,150.

**Worked reminder (subsidy, illustrative).** Qd = 400 − 10P, Qs = −50 + 20P.
- Equilibrium: 450 = 30P, so P = 15, Q = 250.
- Subsidy 3: Qs = −50 + 20(P + 3) = 10 + 20P. Then 390 = 30P, so P = 13, Q = 270.
- Consumers pay 2 less. Producers receive 13 + 3 = 16, which is 1 more. Cost = 3 × 270 = 810.

## Other measures

| Measure | Reason | Main advantage | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulation | Limit harm, protect consumers, curb monopoly | Direct control | Cost of enforcing, loopholes, higher costs for firms |
| Privatisation | Raise efficiency, raise funds | Profit motive, lower government spending | Social aims lost, possible private monopoly |
| Nationalisation | Control essential or monopoly industries | Public interest first | Weak incentives, taxpayer funds losses |
| Direct provision | Public and merit goods are under-provided | Reaches everyone | Opportunity cost, possible waste |

**Opportunity cost reminder.** A budget of 200 buys hospitals at 50 or colleges at 20. Three hospitals cost 150, leaving 50, which buys 2 colleges (50 / 20 is 2.5, so 2 whole). One extra hospital costs 50 / 20 = 2.5 colleges.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Tax versus subsidy:** opposite shifts of supply, opposite effects on price and quantity.
- **Shortage versus surplus:** shortage comes from a maximum price, surplus from a minimum price.
- **Privatisation versus nationalisation:** ownership moves out of, or into, the state.
- **Regulation versus direct provision:** rules for private suppliers versus the government supplying the good.
- **Market failure versus government failure:** the first is the reason to intervene, the second is intervention that makes matters worse or costs too much.

## Evaluating effectiveness

Use a short chain: aim, mechanism, result, drawback, judgement.
- *Tax on a demerit good:* price up, quantity down. It works best if demand is elastic and substitutes exist. If demand is inelastic, consumption barely falls but revenue is high.
- *Subsidy for a merit good:* price down, use rises. It works if it reaches consumers, but it costs taxpayers.
- *Maximum price:* helps those who get the good. Fails if the shortage is large and enforcement is weak.
- *Minimum wage:* helps those who stay employed. The loss of jobs is larger if labour demand is elastic.
- *Regulation:* targets the harm directly, but monitoring costs money and firms may pass higher costs on to buyers.
- *Privatisation:* may raise efficiency and bring in funds, but social aims can be lost and a private monopoly may form.
- *Direct provision:* reaches everyone, including those who could not pay, but every unit built has an opportunity cost.
- Judge by context: size of the gap, elasticity, cost, time lag and side effects. Do not simply list advantages and disadvantages.

**Evaluation sentence frame:** "This policy should work because ..., but it is limited by ..., so overall it is most effective when ...".

## Quick self-test

1. Define a mixed economic system.
2. Qd = 120 − 4P and Qs = 6P. Find equilibrium price and quantity.
3. Using question 2, a minimum price of 15 is set. Find the surplus.
4. Using question 2, a maximum price of 10 is set. Find the shortage.
5. Labour: Ld = 500 − 25W, Ls = 100 + 15W. Find equilibrium and the unemployment created by a minimum wage of 12.
6. A tax of 4 per unit raises the market price from 25 to 27 and 60 units are sold. How much of the revenue do consumers bear?
7. A subsidy of 4 per unit cuts price from 30 to 27 and raises sales from 100 to 118. Work out what the subsidy costs taxpayers and what fraction of it consumers receive as a lower price.
8. A central bank fixes its currency at 2.5 foreign units per unit while equilibrium is 2.0, creating excess supply of 40 million. What must it spend?
9. State two drawbacks of nationalisation.
10. Give one reason a tax may fail to reduce consumption of a demerit good.

### Answers

1. A system where resources are shared out through both private markets and government decisions.
2. 120 − 4P = 6P, so 120 = 10P, **P = 12** and **Q = 72**.
3. Qd = 120 − 60 = 60, Qs = 90. **Surplus = 30.**
4. Qd = 120 − 40 = 80, Qs = 60. **Shortage = 20.**
5. 400 = 40W, so **W = 10** and **L = 250**. At 12: Ld = 200, Ls = 280, so **unemployment = 80.**
6. Consumers pay 27 − 25 = 2 more per unit, so they bear 2 × 60 = **120** of the 240 revenue. Producers bear 120.
7. Cost = 4 × 118 = **472**. Consumers gain 3 of the 4, so **three quarters** (3/4).
8. 40 million × 2.5 = **100 million** foreign units, taken from reserves.
9. Any two from: weak incentives to be efficient, losses paid by taxpayers, political interference, less choice.
10. Demand is price inelastic, or there are no close substitutes, or the good is habit-forming.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Setting a ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it, which changes nothing.
- Shifting supply left for a subsidy or right for a tax.
- Saying a minimum wage creates a labour shortage: it creates unemployment, because the wage is above equilibrium.
- Giving the tax revenue as tax × old quantity instead of tax × new quantity.
- Stating the price rises by the whole tax when the burden is shared.
- Forgetting the cost to the government or taxpayers in an evaluation.
- Giving only advantages of privatisation or only drawbacks of nationalisation.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement tied to the case.
