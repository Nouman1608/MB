## Equilibrium and disequilibrium

**Market equilibrium** is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure for price to change. **Disequilibrium** is any situation in which quantity demanded and quantity supplied differ at the current price.

- **Excess demand (shortage):** at a price below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
- **Excess supply (surplus):** at a price above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

### Worked example 1: finding equilibrium and the gaps

In an invented market for school backpacks, Qd = 300 - 10P and Qs = 20P - 60, with P in cu and Q in hundreds a month.

```
Equilibrium: 300 - 10P = 20P - 60  ->  360 = 30P  ->  P = 12
             Q = 300 - 120 = 180

At P = 10:  Qd = 200, Qs = 140  -> excess demand of 60
At P = 15:  Qd = 150, Qs = 240  -> excess supply of 90
```

At a price of 10, some buyers cannot find backpacks. Sellers notice empty shelves and raise the price. As price rises, quantity demanded **contracts** and quantity supplied **extends** until both equal 180 at a price of 12. At 15, unsold stock builds up, so sellers cut the price; quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until the gap closes. Notice that the curves do not shift during this adjustment: only movements along them occur.

## Effects of shifts on equilibrium

| Change | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases | Rises | Falls |

### Worked example 2: one shift, then two

Return to the backpack market.

```
Demand rises by 60 at every price: Qd = 360 - 10P
  360 - 10P = 20P - 60 -> P = 14, Q = 220

Supply falls by 30 at every price: Qs = 20P - 90
  300 - 10P = 20P - 90 -> P = 13, Q = 170

Both shifts together:
  360 - 10P = 20P - 90 -> P = 15, Q = 210
```

When both curves shift, one variable is certain and the other depends on the size of the shifts. Here demand rises and supply falls, so **price must rise**. Quantity rises in this case (180 to 210) only because the demand shift is larger than the supply shift. Had supply fallen by more, quantity could have fallen.

| Combined shift | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| D up, S up | Uncertain | Rises |
| D down, S down | Uncertain | Falls |
| D up, S down | Rises | Uncertain |
| D down, S up | Falls | Uncertain |

In an exam answer, say which variable is uncertain and what it depends on: "the final effect on quantity depends on the relative size of the shifts".

## Relationships between markets

Section 2.4.3 names four links. In each case a change in one market shifts a curve in another.

| Relationship | Meaning | Example chain |
|---|---|---|
| Joint demand (complements) | Goods used together | A price cut for games consoles extends quantity demanded of consoles, so **demand for games shifts right** |
| Alternative demand (substitutes) | Goods that meet the same want | Higher train fares make bus travel relatively cheaper, so **demand for bus travel shifts right** |
| Derived demand | Demand for an input that comes from demand for the final product | Growth in online shopping shifts **demand for delivery drivers right** |
| Joint supply | One process yields two or more products | More crude oil refined to make petrol also produces more diesel, so the **supply of diesel shifts right** |

A common slip is to describe the substitute case as a movement. A change in the price of one good never moves along the other good's demand curve; it shifts that curve.

### Working through a joint supply chain

Suppose demand for petrol rises sharply. Petrol's price rises and refineries process more crude oil. Because refining yields diesel and other fuels together with petrol, more diesel is produced: **supply of diesel shifts right**. If demand for diesel is unchanged, diesel's equilibrium price falls and its quantity rises. The strength of the link depends on how far refineries can change the mix of products. If they can raise the share of petrol in their output, the effect on diesel is smaller.

### Evaluating links

- **Strength of substitution or complementarity.** A weak substitute shifts demand only a little.
- **Share of cost.** Derived demand for an input that is a small part of the final price responds little to the final market.
- **Time.** Buyers need time to switch between substitutes; producers need time to change joint output.

## Functions of price

Section 2.4.4 names three functions. They are easiest to explain in one connected story.

### Worked scenario: a rise in demand for electric bicycles

1. **Signalling (transmission of preferences).** More consumers want electric bicycles, so at the old price there is excess demand. Sellers raise the price. The higher price signals to producers that consumers value this product more, and it tells consumers the product has become scarcer.
2. **Incentivising.** The higher price raises expected profit. Existing firms extend output, buy more batteries and motors, and hire workers; new firms may enter. Resources move away from less profitable products. On the demand side, the higher price gives some buyers a reason to choose an ordinary bicycle instead.
3. **Rationing.** Until supply catches up, the limited number of electric bicycles goes to the buyers willing and able to pay the higher price. Price rations the scarce stock without queues or waiting lists.

If demand later falls, the same functions run in reverse: a lower price signals reduced preference, profit falls, and resources leave the industry.

The mechanism has limits that make good evaluation. Rationing by price allocates according to **ability to pay**, which may not match need. Signals are weak or slow when supply is inelastic, and the incentive may arrive after the shortage has already done harm.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how the price mechanism reallocates resources when demand for a product falls. **[4]**

> At the original price there is now **excess supply**, so sellers cut the price to clear unsold stock *(1: disequilibrium and adjustment)*. The lower price **signals** that consumers value the product less *(1: signalling)*. Lower profits reduce the **incentive** to produce it, so firms cut output and some leave the market *(1: incentive)*. The labour and capital released move to products whose prices and profits are higher, so resources are **reallocated** towards what consumers prefer *(1: reallocation)*.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting a curve during the adjustment back to equilibrium. Excess demand or supply is removed by movements along the curves.
- Claiming a definite change in both price and quantity when both curves shift.
- Writing that a substitute's price change causes a "movement" in the other market.
- Confusing joint supply with joint demand.
- Naming the functions of price without linking them to the specific market in the question.
- Defining equilibrium as "where the curves cross" without saying quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

## Exam technique

- **Define** equilibrium in words: quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, with no tendency for price to change.
- **Analyse** a shift with a labelled diagram: original curves, new curve, original and new equilibrium price and quantity, and an arrow showing the shift.
- In a Section B essay on a market change, follow the sequence: cause, which curve, direction, disequilibrium at the old price, adjustment, new equilibrium.
- In Paper 1, combined-shift items reward working out which variable is certain before you read the options.
- For **Assess** questions on the price mechanism, weigh its speed and efficiency against equity and the time firms need to respond.
