This guide covers the full framework of **IB Middle Years Programme
Individuals and Societies**, from 2014. It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-subject-guide/)
and the
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by setting out the discipline options schools
choose from and the four assessment criteria used to judge student
work -- the actual scope of the subject group, not just its aims.

The MYP requires at least 50 hours of teaching time for individuals and
societies in each year of the programme, rising to a recommended 70
hours of guided learning per year in MYP years 4 and 5 for students
pursuing MYP eAssessment.

## What "individuals and societies" actually covers

This subject group incorporates disciplines traditionally studied under
humanities and social sciences, and its stated purpose is to encourage
learners to respect and understand the world around them while
equipping them with skills to inquire into the historical, geographical,
political, social, economic and cultural factors that affect
individuals, societies and environments. For MYP individuals and societies,
schools develop courses in **integrated humanities, history,
economics, geography, philosophy, sociology/anthropology, business
management, psychology, and world religions**. Which of these
disciplines a given school actually teaches is a school-level decision,
so the specific content a student studies can vary considerably between
schools even though the subject group name and assessment criteria stay
the same.

## Key and related concepts

The framework is organised around key concepts of **change, global
interactions, time, place and space, and systems**, explored through
related concepts such as causality, globalization, culture and
sustainability. As in every MYP subject group, students explore these
through the same six global contexts -- identities and relationships,
orientation in space and time, personal and cultural expression,
scientific and technical innovation, globalization and sustainability,
and fairness and development.

## The four assessment criteria

- **Criterion A: Knowing and understanding** -- developing factual and
  conceptual knowledge about individuals and societies.
- **Criterion B: Investigating** -- developing systematic research
  skills and processes associated with humanities and social science
  disciplines, working independently and collaboratively.
- **Criterion C: Communicating** -- organising, documenting and
  communicating learning using a variety of media and presentation
  formats.
- **Criterion D: Thinking critically** -- using critical-thinking
  skills to develop and apply understanding of individuals and
  societies and the process of investigation.

Each criterion carries equal weight and is scored on eight achievement
levels (1-8), grouped into four descriptor bands -- the same
level-and-band structure used across every MYP subject group, which
means students moving between individuals and societies and, say, MYP
Sciences encounter a familiar assessment logic even though the
criterion names and content differ.

## MYP eAssessment

Students seeking IB MYP course results or the MYP certificate in this
subject group complete a 2-hour on-screen examination -- available
specifically in **history, geography and integrated humanities**, made
up of three tasks:

- **Investigating** (criteria A and B, 26 marks) -- evaluating an
  investigation and planning one of the student's own, alongside
  demonstrating knowledge and understanding either from the course or
  from source material presented in the exam itself.
- **Communicating** (criteria A and C, 18 marks) -- engaging creatively
  with a given topic or context, presenting information effectively in
  a style and format appropriate to the audience and purpose.
- **Thinking critically** (criteria A, C and D, 36 marks) -- discussing
  issues, arguments and perspectives through structured questions,
  culminating in an extended piece of writing. This is the largest of
  the three tasks by mark allocation.

Sample topics the on-screen examinations draw on include demographics
and human movement, settlement and urban morphology, superpowers and
supra-national alliances, significant individuals, warfare and
peacekeeping, rights and social protest, trade and economic exchange,
and industrialization and technological development.

## How to approach it

Because the thinking-critically task carries the largest share of marks
(36 of 80) and culminates in extended writing, practising sustained,
structured argument -- not just short-answer recall -- is the single
highest-value use of exam preparation time in this subject. Because the
investigating task explicitly asks students to evaluate source
material's value and limitations (not just summarise it), build the
habit of asking what a source can and cannot tell you about a topic
every time you work with primary or secondary material in class, since
this is tested directly and is a distinct skill from simply extracting
facts from a source. Because the specific discipline mix (history,
geography, economics, and so on) varies by school, confirm early which
of the on-screen examination subjects -- history, geography or
integrated humanities -- your own course actually prepares you for,
since revising the wrong one wastes significant effort in a subject
group already broader than most others.

One sample question from the official brief's history eAssessment
illustrates the investigating task's source-evaluation demand directly:
"Explain one value or one limitation of source B for a student
researching protests." A further question -- "Compare and contrast
source C and source D in terms of their usefulness in studying protest
movements" -- shows that source evaluation is not a one-off skill but
one applied comparatively across multiple sources. A third sample
question, "To what extent do you agree that gaining support from
different groups in society is the most important factor in making
sure a protest movement is successful?", models the kind of
extended-writing, multi-perspective argument the thinking-critically
task expects: taking a position, weighing it against alternatives, and
reaching a reasoned judgement rather than simply describing events.
