These questions cover **4.3.2 Internal energy and energy transfers** (4.3.2.1 to 4.3.2.3) of AQA GCSE Physics
(8463), assessed on Paper 1 at both tiers. ΔE = m c Δθ and E = m L are given on the equation sheet; you must choose
and apply them.

Revise first with the [internal energy and energy transfers study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-internal-energy-and-energy-transfers/)
and the [internal energy and energy transfers revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-internal-energy-and-energy-transfers-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define internal energy. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 3.0 kg of water from 15 °C to 35 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C. **[3]**

**3.** Calculate the energy needed to melt 0.15 kg of ice at 0 °C. The specific latent heat of fusion of ice is 334 000 J/kg. **[2]**

**4.** State the difference between the specific latent heat of fusion and the specific latent heat of vaporisation. **[2]**

**5.** A 2.0 kg block of concrete absorbs 44 000 J and its temperature rises by 25 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of concrete. **[3]**

**6.** State the unit of specific latent heat. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A beaker of a solid wax is heated at a steady rate. Its temperature rises from 20 °C to 54 °C, stays at 54 °C for 6 minutes, then rises again.

**(a)** State the melting point of the wax. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the temperature stays constant for 6 minutes even though the wax is still being heated. **[3]**
**(c)** The graph slopes more steeply after the flat part than before it. Suggest what this tells you about the liquid wax compared with the solid wax. **[1]**

**8.** Explain why the temperature rise of a substance depends on its mass, the type of material and the energy supplied. Use ideas about specific heat capacity in your answer. **[3]**

**9.** 0.50 kg of water at 20 °C is heated in a kettle until it boils at 100 °C, and then 0.10 kg of it is boiled away. Use c = 4200 J/kg °C and L = 2 260 000 J/kg.

**(a)** Calculate the energy to heat the water to 100 °C. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy to boil away 0.10 kg. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the total energy transferred to the water. **[1]**

**10.** A student measures the specific latent heat of fusion of water using two funnels of crushed ice, one with a 50 W immersion heater and one without.

**(a)** Explain why the second funnel is needed. **[2]**
**(b)** The heater is on for 400 s. The heated funnel collects 0.066 kg of water and the other funnel collects 0.012 kg. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion. **[4]**
**(c)** The accepted value, about 334 000 J/kg, is lower than the student's result. Suggest one reason. **[1]**

**11.** Steam at 100 °C causes a worse burn than the same mass of water at 100 °C. Explain why, using the idea of latent heat. **[3]**

**12.** 0.80 kg of liquid paraffin cools from 70 °C to 25 °C and releases 79 200 J.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of paraffin. **[3]**
**(b)** State what happens to the internal energy of the paraffin as it cools. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The total kinetic energy and potential energy [1]; of all the particles (atoms and molecules) that make up a system [1]

**2.** Δθ = 35 − 15 = 20 °C [1]; ΔE = 3.0 × 4200 × 20 [1]; ΔE = **252 000 J** [1]

**3.** E = m L = 0.15 × 334 000 [1]; E = **50 100 J** [1]

**4.** Fusion is the energy per kilogram for a change between solid and liquid [1]; vaporisation is the energy per kilogram for a change between liquid and vapour [1]

**5.** c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ) [1] = 44 000 ÷ (2.0 × 25) [1]; c = **880 J/kg °C** [1]

**6.** J/kg (joules per kilogram) [1]

**7. (a)** **54 °C** [1]
**(b)** The wax is changing state (melting) [1]; the energy supplied increases the internal energy by increasing the potential energy of the particles [1]; it does not increase their kinetic energy, so the temperature does not change [1]
**(c)** The liquid wax has a smaller specific heat capacity than the solid wax [1]

**8.** A larger mass needs more energy for the same temperature rise, so for a given energy input it warms less [1]; different materials have different specific heat capacities, so they need different amounts of energy for each kilogram to rise by 1 °C [1]; more energy supplied to the same mass of the same material gives a larger temperature rise [1]

**9. (a)** ΔE = 0.50 × 4200 × 80 [1]; ΔE = **168 000 J** [1]
**(b)** E = 0.10 × 2 260 000 [1]; E = **226 000 J** [1]
**(c)** 168 000 + 226 000 = **394 000 J** [1]

**10. (a)** Some ice melts because of energy from the warm room, not the heater [1]; the control funnel measures this, so it can be subtracted to find the mass melted by the heater alone [1]
**(b)** E = P t = 50 × 400 = 20 000 J [1]; m = 0.066 − 0.012 = 0.054 kg [1]; L = E ÷ m = 20 000 ÷ 0.054 [1]; L = **370 000 J/kg** (3.7 × 10⁵ J/kg, 2 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** Some energy from the heater was transferred to the surroundings (or the funnel) rather than melting ice, so less ice melted than the energy supplied would suggest [1]

**11.** When steam touches the skin it condenses to water [1]; condensing releases the specific latent heat of vaporisation, a large amount of energy per kilogram, with no fall in temperature [1]; this is in addition to the energy released as the water then cools, so more energy is transferred to the skin than from water alone [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the final temperature instead of the temperature change.
- Mixing up which equation applies to a slope and which to a flat part of a heating graph.
- Forgetting to subtract the control mass in the latent heat experiment.
- Explaining a flat part with "the energy is used up" instead of the change in potential energy.
- Writing c in J/kg or L in J/kg °C.
