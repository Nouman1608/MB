## Method: a five-step model

1. Ask a question (does a fare cut reduce car use?).
2. Build a simplified model.
3. Assume **ceteris paribus**.
4. State a testable hypothesis.
5. Test against data; accept, reject or refine.

**Why economics differs from a natural science:** controlled experiments are rarely possible; many things change at
once; people respond to incentives, expectations and the study itself; results are tendencies; values enter when
findings guide policy.

## Positive or normative?

| Signal | Usually |
|---|---|
| Figures, "rose", "fell", "will lead to", "caused" | positive |
| "Should", "ought", "fair", "unfair", "too much", "well spent", "better" | normative |

- The test is whether **evidence could settle it**, not whether it is true.
- A forecast ("the tax will cut smoking by 5%") is positive.
- A report of opinion ("many voters say the tax is unfair") is positive.

## Value judgements

- Decisions rest on **predicted consequences** (positive) and **moral and political judgements** (normative).
- Agreement on facts does not guarantee agreement on policy.

## The economic problem

- **Purpose of economic activity:** produce goods and services to satisfy needs and wants.
- **Needs:** essential for survival (food, water, shelter). **Wants:** desires beyond these.
- **Three decisions:** what to produce; how to produce; who benefits.

## Factors of production

| Factor | Definition | Café example |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources | The seafront site |
| Labour | Human effort | Baristas |
| Capital | Man-made goods used in production | Coffee machine |
| Enterprise | Organising factors and taking risk | The owner |

- Money is **not** a factor; it buys factors.
- **Environment is scarce:** non-renewable resources run out; renewable ones (groundwater, fish) can be overused.

## Scarcity and opportunity cost

- Scarcity → choice → opportunity cost.
- **Opportunity cost** = the next best alternative forgone.
- Applies to households, firms and governments.

## Production possibility diagrams

- **Boundary:** maximum combinations of two goods with all resources fully and efficiently used.
- **Straight line:** constant opportunity cost (resources equally suited to both goods).
- **Concave curve:** rising opportunity cost (resources specialised).
- **Inside:** unemployed or inefficiently used resources.
- **Outward shift:** economic growth (more or better resources, technology).
- **Pivot:** improvement affecting one good only.
- **Inward shift:** permanent loss of resources.
- **Productively efficient:** all points on the boundary.
- **Allocatively efficient:** only the mix that best matches society's wants.

## Opportunity cost calculation

Opportunity cost of 1 unit of good A = units of good B given up ÷ units of A gained.

**Worked reminder (invented).** A workshop can make 40 tables or 160 chairs a week, on a straight line.

- 1 table costs 160 ÷ 40 = **4 chairs**.
- 1 chair costs 40 ÷ 160 = **0.25 tables**.
- Moving from 10 to 15 tables costs **20 chairs**.

## Exam traps

- Copying a statement from an extract without a reason for its classification.
- Treating "positive" as "good" or "true".
- Listing the factors without applying them to the business in the question.
- Calling money capital.
- Dividing the wrong way round in opportunity cost questions.
- Showing a temporary rise in unemployment as an inward shift.
- Assuming every point on the boundary is the best one for society.

## How to write a two-mark classification answer

1. **Quote** the exact words from the extract.
2. **Classify** them: positive or normative.
3. **Justify** with the reason: "it can be checked against data" or "'should' expresses a value judgement".

## Self-test

1. Why is economics called a social science?
2. What does ceteris paribus mean, and why do economists use it?
3. Classify: "The minimum wage rose by 9% last year."
4. Classify: "The minimum wage should rise faster than prices."
5. Classify: "A higher minimum wage will reduce employment of young workers."
6. Name the three key economic decisions.
7. A bakery buys a new oven with a bank loan. Which factor of production is the oven?
8. Give one reason why groundwater is a scarce resource.
9. A student chooses revision over a shift at work paying 60 and an evening with friends she values at 20.
   What is the opportunity cost?
10. Using the workshop reminder, what is the opportunity cost of producing 8 more chairs?
11. What does a point inside a boundary show?
12. Why might a point on the boundary not be allocatively efficient?

### Answers

1. It studies human behaviour using scientific methods such as models, hypotheses and evidence.
2. "All other things being equal"; it isolates the effect of one change by assuming other influences stay the same.
3. **Positive**: it can be checked.
4. **Normative**: "should" expresses a value judgement.
5. **Positive**: it is a testable prediction, whether or not it proves correct.
6. What to produce, how to produce it, and who benefits.
7. **Capital**: a man-made good used to produce other goods (the loan is finance, not a factor).
8. It is limited, and pumping it faster than rainfall refills it leaves less for future users.
9. The **shift at work worth 60**, the next best alternative.
10. 8 × 0.25 = **2 tables**.
11. Unemployed or inefficiently used resources; more of both goods could be made.
12. The output mix may not match what society wants most, even though no resources are wasted.
