## Section A: definitions

**1.** Define the term **cyber security**. **[2]**

**2.** Define **social engineering** and **malware**. **[2]**

## Section B: threats

**3.** For each threat, explain why it puts a system at risk:
(a) weak and default passwords (b) misconfigured access rights (c) removable media (d) unpatched software. **[4]**

**4.** Identify the form of social engineering in each case and suggest one way to protect against it:
**(a)** A caller claiming to be from the IT department asks an employee for their password to "fix an urgent fault".
**[2]**
**(b)** A customer types the correct address of their bank's website but is taken to a fake copy of the site. **[2]**
**(c)** Someone in a queue watches a shopper enter a PIN at a card machine. **[2]**
**(d)** A text message says a parcel could not be delivered and asks the recipient to enter card details at a link.
**[2]**

**5.** Describe the difference between a computer virus and a trojan. **[2]**

**6.** Describe what **spyware** and **adware** do. **[2]**

**7.** Give three ways a user can protect a computer against malware. **[3]**

## Section C: penetration testing

**8.** **(a)** Explain what penetration testing is and why an organisation would pay for it. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the two types of penetration testing and the kind of attack each simulates. **[4]**

## Section D: detection and prevention

**9.** Explain why biometric measures are particularly suitable for mobile devices. **[2]**

**10.** Describe three features of a password system that improve security. **[3]**

**11.** A website asks new users to complete a CAPTCHA and then click a link in a confirmation email.
**(a)** Explain the purpose of the CAPTCHA. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the purpose of the confirmation email. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how automatic software updates reduce the risk of a cyber attack. **[2]**

**13.** A small accounts firm stores clients' financial records on a network. Staff often use USB sticks to take work
home, several staff share one login, and the computers have not been updated for a year. Identify three threats in
this scenario and recommend a measure to reduce each. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The processes, practices and technologies [1] designed to protect networks, computers, programs and data from
attack, damage or unauthorised access [1].

**2.** Social engineering: the art of manipulating people so they give up confidential information [1]. Malware: an
umbrella term for a variety of forms of hostile or intrusive software [1].

**3.** (a) They can be guessed easily or found in lists of factory settings, letting an attacker log in [1].
(b) Users may be able to see, change or delete data that they should not have access to [1].
(c) A USB stick can bring malware into the system, or allow data to be copied and lost or stolen [1].
(d) Known vulnerabilities have not been fixed, so attackers can exploit them [1].

**4. (a)** Blagging (pretexting) [1]; staff training never to reveal passwords and to verify callers by ringing back on a
known number [1].
**(b)** Pharming [1]; check the site uses HTTPS and the address is correct, and keep security software up to date [1].
**(c)** Shouldering (shoulder surfing) [1]; shield the keypad with a hand when entering a PIN [1].
**(d)** Phishing [1]; do not follow links in unexpected messages -- contact the delivery company directly using its
official website [1].

**5.** A virus attaches itself to files or programs and copies itself to other files when it is run [1]; a trojan
disguises itself as legitimate software, so the user installs it themselves [1].

**6.** Spyware secretly monitors the user's activity, such as keystrokes, and sends the information to a third party
[1]. Adware displays unwanted advertisements, sometimes based on tracking the user's browsing [1].

**7.** Any three: install anti-malware software and keep it up to date; turn on automatic updates; only download
software from trusted sources; do not open unexpected email attachments; scan removable media before use [3].

**8. (a)** Penetration testing is an authorised attempt to gain access to resources without knowing usernames,
passwords or other normal means of access [1], to find weaknesses so they can be fixed before a real attacker
exploits them [1].
**(b)** Internal test: the tester has knowledge of, and possibly basic credentials for, the target system [1],
simulating an attack by a malicious insider [1]. External test: the tester has no knowledge of any credentials [1],
simulating an attack from outside the organisation [1].

**9.** Mobile devices are easily lost or stolen, and a fingerprint or face scan is unique to the owner and hard to copy
[1]; it is also quick and convenient, so users are more likely to keep the device locked [1].

**10.** Any three: minimum password length; a mix of upper case, lower case, digits and symbols; regular changes and
no reuse of old passwords; locking the account after several failed attempts [3].

**11. (a)** A CAPTCHA is easy for a human but hard for an automated program to solve [1], so it stops bots creating
large numbers of fake accounts or guessing passwords automatically [1].
**(b)** It confirms the user's identity by proving they control the email address given [1], so accounts cannot be
created with someone else's address [1].

**12.** Updates often include patches that fix known security vulnerabilities [1]; installing them automatically means
the holes are closed as soon as possible, before attackers can exploit them [1].

**13.** Any three threats, each with a matching measure [2 each]:
- **Removable media**: USB sticks could carry malware in or lose client data -> ban personal USB sticks, use encrypted
  approved devices, or scan media automatically.
- **Shared login (misconfigured access rights / weak password practice)**: no individual accountability, and everyone
  can access all records -> give each person their own account and strong password, with access only to the files
  they need.
- **Unpatched/outdated software**: known vulnerabilities remain open -> turn on automatic software updates.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a threat correctly but giving a protection that does not match it.
- Defining phishing as "a fake website" -- that is closer to pharming; phishing uses fraudulent messages.
- Describing penetration testing without saying it is done **without** the normal means of access.
- Using vague terms such as "hacking" instead of the specification's named threats.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know about ransomware or worms for 9210?**
The specification lists computer virus, trojan, spyware and adware as the forms of malware to describe. Learn those
four precisely.

**How should I answer a scenario question on threats?**
Quote the detail from the scenario that shows the threat, name it using the specification's term, and give a
protection that directly addresses it.
