## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **structural differentiation**. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what Parsons meant by the **stabilisation of adult personalities**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by describing the family as a **unit of consumption**. **[2]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** of the functions of the family identified by Murdock.
**[4]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons why Parsons believed the nuclear family "fits"
industrial society. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** differences between Marxist feminist and radical feminist
views of the family. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** Explain **two** criticisms of the functionalist view that the nuclear family fits the needs of
industrial society. **[8]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which families may act as agents of social control, according to
Marxists. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which families may shape the position of women in society. **[8]**

**10.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that the family benefits all its
members equally. **[20]**

**11.** Evaluate the Marxist view of the relationship between families and society. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The process by which new, specialised institutions develop to take over functions previously
performed by one institution [1], for example schools and hospitals taking over education and
health care from the family [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The family provides emotional support and relaxation for adults [1], relieving the stresses of
working life so that adults can return to work and society stays stable [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Families buy goods and services such as housing, food and technology [1], which generates
profit for capitalist businesses, often encouraged by advertising aimed at parents and children [1].
**[2]**

**4.** Two functions, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Sexual [1]: regulates sexual relationships, reducing conflict in society [1].
- Reproductive [1]: produces the next generation, so society continues [1].
- Economic [1]: shares resources such as food and shelter among members [1].
- Educational [1]: socialises children into shared norms and values [1].
**[4]**

**5.** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- Geographical mobility [1]: a small nuclear unit can move easily to where industry needs labour [1].
- Social mobility [1]: achieved status at work does not cause conflict, as it might in an extended
  household where the father's ascribed status is higher than a successful son's [1].
- Division of roles [1]: instrumental and expressive roles let one adult concentrate on paid work
  while the other provides care [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Root cause [1]: Marxist feminists see capitalism as the main cause of women's oppression, while
  radical feminists see patriarchy as the root [1].
- Beneficiary [1]: Marxist feminists say women's domestic labour mainly benefits capitalism by
  reproducing workers, while radical feminists say it benefits men directly [1].
- Solution [1]: Marxist feminists link women's liberation to ending capitalism; radical feminists
  argue patriarchy must be challenged directly, with some advocating separatism [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two criticisms, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence [1]):
- **The nuclear family pre-dated industrialisation** [1]: if it was already common, it cannot have
  developed to fit industry [1]; this suggests the causal link may run the other way, with nuclear
  households helping industrialisation start [1]; Laslett's parish-record research found nuclear
  households common in pre-industrial England [1].
- **Extended families in industrial towns** [1]: early industrialisation may have strengthened
  extended kin ties [1], because kin helped each other find work and housing when there was no
  welfare state [1]; Anderson's study of Preston using the 1851 census supports this [1].
- Credit: ignores diversity; ignores conflict and gender inequality; based on middle-class American
  families.
**[8]**

**8.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Ideological conditioning** [1]: families teach children to accept the authority of parents [1],
  which prepares them to accept the authority of employers [1]; Althusser called the family an
  ideological state apparatus [1].
- **Reproducing inequality through inheritance** [1]: wealth passes down family lines [1], so the
  class structure is maintained across generations without obvious coercion [1], as Engels argued
  when linking monogamy to private property [1].
- Credit: unit of consumption; a haven that makes exploitation bearable (Zaretsky); cushioning male
  workers' frustration.
**[8]**

**9.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Domestic labour** [1]: women usually carry the main burden of unpaid housework and childcare [1],
  which limits time for paid work and promotion and creates economic dependence [1]; Oakley's
  research showed housework as unpaid, low-status work that the housewife role made seem natural [1].
- **Gender socialisation** [1]: families socialise girls and boys differently [1], reproducing
  expectations that women take caring roles in society, such as nursing or teaching young children [1];
  feminists see this as a key way patriarchy is passed on [1].
- Credit: male control of decision-making and money; domestic violence; restrictions on women's
  movement in patriarchal families.
**[8]**

**10.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge of several perspectives; sustained application to a named society;
  balanced, analytical evaluation; justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation or application.
- 5–8: basic, mainly descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For the view:* functionalists (Murdock, Parsons) see the family meeting the
needs of all, socialising children and stabilising adults; complementary roles benefit both
partners; emotional support and care benefit children and older people. *Against the view:*
liberal feminists (Oakley) show women do more unpaid labour; radical feminists (Delphy and Leonard)
argue men exploit women's work; violence against women and children shows the family can harm
members; Marxists argue the family mainly benefits capitalism rather than members; children may lack
power; older people's experiences vary. *Application:* reward evidence from a named society, such as
laws on inheritance or patterns of domestic labour. *Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example
that benefits are real but unequally distributed by gender and age. **[20]**

**11.** Levels of response as for Question 10.

Indicative content. *Exposition:* Engels on property and monogamy; Althusser's ideological state
apparatus; family as unit of consumption; Zaretsky's haven argument; families as agents of social
control. *Strengths:* links private family life to economic structure; explains inheritance of
class advantage; explains why families are targeted by advertising. *Weaknesses:* functionalists
argue families meet genuine needs; feminists say Marxism ignores patriarchy (Benston, a Marxist
feminist, partly bridges this); assumes one nuclear form and ignores diversity, such as single-parent
and reconstituted families; underplays agency, since families can resist ideology; families
persisted in non-capitalist societies. *Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that Marxism
explains the class side of the relationship but must be combined with feminist insight. **[20]**
