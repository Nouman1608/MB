Content tested: "American people and the 'Boom' of the 1920s"; "Bust - Americans' experiences of the Depression and New Deal, 1929-1945"; "Post-war America, 1945-68".

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three reasons for the economic boom of the 1920s. **[3]**

**2.** Give three features of the Ku Klux Klan in the 1920s. **[3]**

**3.** Give three features of the New Deal. **[3]**

**4.** Give three features of the Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955-56. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which hire purchase affected life in the USA in the 1920s. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which Prohibition affected American society. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the Second World War changed life for African Americans. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the Depression affected the lives of Americans in the early 1930s. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which television and the media affected the civil rights movement. **[9]**

**10.** "The New Deal was a success for all Americans." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "The 1920s were a decade of prosperity for all Americans." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**12.** "Federal government action was the main reason for progress in civil rights in the years 1954-68." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation of how it affected the development [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: the USA had been enriched by the First World War; Republican governments followed laissez-faire policies and low taxes; high tariffs protected American industry; mass production, as in Ford's factories, lowered prices; new industries such as cars and electrical goods grew; advertising and credit encouraged people to buy; rising share prices encouraged investment.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: it was revived in 1915 and grew to several million members in the early 1920s; it attacked African Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants; it used violence, lynching and intimidation; members wore white robes and hoods; it was strongest in the South but spread to the Midwest; its power declined after scandals in the later 1920s.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: Roosevelt introduced it after being elected in 1932; there were "alphabet agencies" such as the CCC, AAA, TVA and WPA; the Social Security Act of 1935 provided pensions and unemployment insurance; the Wagner Act protected unions; it increased the role of the federal government; some measures were declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: it began in December 1955 after Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat; African Americans boycotted the city buses for over a year; Martin Luther King led it; car-pools and walking replaced the buses; the bus company lost income; the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional.

**5.** Way identified [1]: it increased consumer spending and debt. Precise knowledge [2]: many families bought cars, radios and appliances by paying in instalments, and an increasing share of sales were on credit. Explanation [2]: people could own goods they could not afford in cash, which boosted demand and factory jobs, but it also left families in debt and dependent on steady wages. Further development [1]: when the Depression began, people could no longer keep up payments, which worsened the crisis.

**6.** Way identified [1]: it increased crime and corruption. Precise knowledge [2]: from 1920 the 18th Amendment banned alcohol, and illegal speakeasies and bootlegging developed; gangsters such as Al Capone in Chicago made huge profits. Explanation [2]: gang violence, bribery of police and officials, and widespread disregard for the law followed, so many Americans lost respect for the law. Further development [1]: Prohibition was repealed in 1933.

**7.** Way identified [1]: it increased opportunities in the North and in industry. Precise knowledge [2]: many African Americans migrated from the South to northern and western cities to take defence jobs, and in 1941 Roosevelt banned discrimination in defence industries after pressure from civil rights leaders. Explanation [2]: they earned higher wages and experienced less segregation than in the South, but they met discrimination and overcrowded housing, and the armed forces remained segregated. Further development [1]: this raised expectations of equality and led to the growth of the civil rights movement, although riots broke out in Detroit in 1943.

**8.** Way one [4]: unemployment [1]; by 1933 about a quarter of the workforce had no jobs, and wages fell [1]; so many families lost their homes and relied on charity, soup kitchens and Hoovervilles [2]. Way two [4]: farming communities [1]; falling prices and bank failures meant that thousands of farmers lost their land, and drought created the Dust Bowl [1]; so families left for California or became tenant farmers and migrant workers [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the Crash of October 1929 or the Bonus Army.

**9.** Way one [4]: television showed the violence used against peaceful protesters [1]; in 1963 and 1965 television viewers saw police using dogs, hoses and batons in Birmingham and Selma [1]; so public opinion across the country turned in favour of civil rights and put pressure on politicians to act [2]. Way two [4]: it brought civil rights leaders to a national audience [1]; television covered the March on Washington in 1963 and King's "I have a dream" speech [1]; so the movement gained supporters, including white Americans, and helped to pass the Civil Rights Act [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example Emmett Till or Little Rock.

**10.** Given factor [3]: the New Deal created jobs through the CCC and WPA; the Social Security Act created pensions and unemployment insurance; banks were made safer; farmers received help; unions were protected. Other factors [4]: unemployment was still high in 1938; African Americans were often excluded or paid less; women received lower pay and fewer jobs; Indigenous peoples and tenant farmers gained little; opposition from the Supreme Court and the wealthy. Comparison [3]: the New Deal helped many groups, but the benefits were uneven, and the real recovery came from war production after 1941. Judgement [2]: for example, the New Deal was a success in giving relief and hope to many, but it was not a success for all, because some groups gained little and the Depression continued until the war.

**11.** Given factor [3]: car ownership and electrical goods spread; wages rose in new industries; advertising and credit gave many families new goods; the stock market boomed; cinema and radio became popular. Other factors [4]: farmers suffered from falling prices; coal and textile workers had low wages; African Americans faced segregation and poverty in the South; immigrants faced restrictions and prejudice; inequality of wealth. Comparison [3]: the boom brought a new standard of living to many city workers and the middle class, but millions missed out, and the reliance on credit and share speculation made the prosperity fragile. Judgement [2]: for example, the 1920s was a decade of prosperity for many but not for all, so the statement is only partly true.

**12.** Given factor [3]: the Supreme Court's decision in Brown (1954); Eisenhower sent troops to Little Rock in 1957; Kennedy supported a civil rights bill in 1963; Johnson passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Other factors [4]: protests such as the Montgomery Bus Boycott and the March on Washington; the leadership of Martin Luther King and the NAACP; the media; young activists in sit-ins and Freedom Rides; Black Power and riots. Comparison [3]: federal action turned demands into law, but it was a response to protest and media pressure, and the federal government often acted slowly, so the movement and the government were linked. Judgement [2]: for example, federal laws were essential for lasting change, but the pressure of the civil rights movement was the main reason that the government acted.
