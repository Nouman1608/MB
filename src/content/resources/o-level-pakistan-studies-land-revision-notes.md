## Location — map facts

Tropic of Cancer; latitudes **30°N, 36°N**; longitudes **64°E, 70°E, 76°E**; Arabian Sea to the south;
Pakistan's position in South and Central Asia. This section is tested **visually as often as in
writing** — practise locating everything on a blank outline map, not just recalling facts in words.

## Cities and administrative areas — the 15 to place

Islamabad, Murree, Rawalpindi, Gujranwala, Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan, Sialkot, Peshawar, Chitral,
Gilgit, Hyderabad, Karachi, Quetta, Gwadar.

## Topography — no formation, only identification and influence

**Not required**: how any landform was geologically formed. **Required**: identifying it and
explaining its influence on human activity.

| Feature type | Named examples |
|---|---|
| Mountain ranges | Hindu Kush, Karakoram, Himalaya, Sulaiman Range, Safed Koh |
| Plateaux | Balochistan Plateau, Potwar Plateau, Salt Range |
| Rivers | Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej, Kabul, Hab, Dasht |
| Deserts | Thar, Thal, Kharan |

**Influence to always explain**: steep, high-altitude terrain makes road/rail construction difficult
and expensive → concentrates transport routes along valleys and passes rather than a dense, even
network. This exact chain — named feature → physical constraint → human consequence — is the shape a
strong topography answer takes.

## Climate — describe AND explain, always paired

Four zones: **arid, semi-arid, humid, highland**. The **monsoon** (seasonal reversal of wind
direction and pressure systems) brings most of the country's annual rainfall and is the single most
consequential climatic feature for agriculture and water supply.

**Always pair a physical pattern with its human effect**: highland regions face low temperatures, ice
and snow shaping mountain-community life; lowland regions face both drought/water shortage AND
flooding from heavy rain storms, depending on season and region.

## Worked chain — the answer shape examiners want

Named mountain range (e.g. Karakoram/Himalaya) → steep, high-altitude terrain → difficult, expensive
road/rail construction → transport concentrated along valleys/passes rather than spread evenly.
Practise this exact four-step shape for any topography-to-consequence question.

## Why this section is foundational to the rest of Paper 2

Paper 1 covers The History and Culture of Pakistan; Paper 2 is The Environment of Pakistan, and Section
1 lays the physical foundation for everything that follows in that paper. Location, administrative
geography, topography and climate all recur as context in later Paper 2 sections on natural resources,
agriculture, industry and population — a candidate who has not secured the Indus tributaries, the
western plateaux, or the four climate zones here will lose time re-deriving that context mid-answer
when a later section assumes it. Memorising this section thoroughly is one of the most efficient uses
of Paper 2 revision time precisely because it underpins so much of what comes after it.

## Distinguishing administrative geography from physical geography

A frequent source of lost precision is blending 1(b)'s administrative map (provinces, territories,
cities) with 1(c)'s physical landforms (mountain ranges, plateaux, deserts) as though they were one and
the same. They are assessed as two distinct layers: a city like Quetta belongs to 1(b) as an
administrative location, while the Balochistan Plateau it sits within belongs to 1(c) as a physical
feature. An answer that treats a city as though it were itself a landform, or vice versa, loses marks
for imprecision even when the underlying facts are broadly correct.

## Connecting climate zones to the regions that produce them

Rather than learning arid, semi-arid, humid and highland climate as four abstract categories, attach
each to a region already introduced in this section: highland climate to the northern mountain ranges,
arid and semi-arid conditions to the western plateaux and southern deserts, and humid conditions to the
areas most affected by monsoon rainfall. This regional attachment turns "which climate zone does this
location belong to" questions into a lookup against the map you have already built, rather than a
separate piece of recall to hold in memory independently.

## Exam traps

- Naming landforms, rivers or deserts without being able to locate them on a map.
- Describing how a landform was geologically formed — not required.
- Treating climate and topography as unconnected rather than explaining how one influences the other.
- Giving a vague "harsh climate" answer instead of naming the specific zone, season, and human effect.

## A revision checklist to work through in order

Confirm you can, in sequence: locate the Tropic of Cancer, the specified latitudes and longitudes, and
Pakistan's regional position on a blank map; name and locate every administrative area and city listed
above; name and locate the major mountain ranges, plateaux, rivers and deserts; explain how topography
has influenced land use and transport networks; describe the causes of the monsoon and Pakistan's main
climate zones; and explain the effects of climate on Pakistan's economy and people. Working through
this checklist in order, rather than jumping between sub-sections, mirrors how the section itself
builds — each later item assumes the earlier ones are already secure.

## Self-test

1. Name the three longitude lines specified for Pakistan's location.
2. List the four Section 1 climate zones.
3. What is the single most consequential climatic feature for agriculture and water supply?
4. Why does mountainous terrain concentrate transport along valleys and passes?
5. What is explicitly NOT required about Pakistan's landforms?

**Answers:** 1. 64°E, 70°E, 76°E. 2. Arid, semi-arid, humid, highland. 3. The monsoon. 4. Steep,
high-altitude terrain makes road and rail construction difficult and expensive. 5. How the landforms
were geologically formed.
