## Part A: Why countries trade (3.2.4.1)

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exports | Goods and services produced in the UK and sold to buyers abroad (money flows in) |
| Imports | Goods and services produced abroad and bought by UK buyers (money flows out) |
| Specialisation | A country concentrating on producing what it is relatively good at, and trading for the rest |
| Global interdependence | Countries relying on each other for goods, services, markets, investment and supply chains |

### Why trade matters to economies

- **Different resources:** countries have different climates, natural resources, skills and capital. The UK cannot grow bananas or mine much of the metal it uses, so it imports them.
- **Specialisation:** each country can focus on what it produces most efficiently, so total world output rises.
- **Lower prices and more choice** for consumers.
- **Bigger markets** for firms, which allow **economies of scale** and lower average costs.
- **Competition** from foreign firms pushes domestic firms to become more efficient and innovative.
- **Jobs and income** in exporting industries, and tax revenue for the government.

### The UK's exports and imports

| UK exports | UK imports |
|---|---|
| Services, especially financial, insurance and other business services, which make up about half of UK exports | Machinery and electrical goods |
| Machinery, including aircraft engines | Cars and other vehicles |
| Cars | Fuels such as gas and oil |
| Medicines and other chemicals | Food |
| Precious metals and fuels | Clothing and consumer electronics |

- The UK usually has a **deficit on goods** and a **surplus on services**.
- The European Union, as a group, is the UK's largest trading partner. The USA is the largest single country for UK exports.

### Consequences of global interdependence for the UK

| Advantages | Risks |
|---|---|
| Access to cheaper goods and inputs | A recession abroad cuts demand for UK exports, so output and jobs fall |
| Large export markets for UK firms | Problems in supply chains abroad can cause shortages and price rises (as in 2021–22) |
| Foreign investment and jobs in the UK | Rises in world energy and food prices feed into UK inflation |
| Shared technology and ideas | UK firms can lose out to cheaper foreign producers |

### Calculations and data

**1. Shares.** Total UK exports are £800bn, of which services are £420bn. Services' share = 420 ÷ 800 × 100 = **52.5%**.

**2. Openness to trade.** Exports £800bn and imports £850bn in an economy with GDP of £2,500bn. Trade as a share of GDP = (800 + 850) ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **66%**.

**3. Change over time.** Exports to a country rise from £40bn to £46bn: (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **15%** growth.

## Part B: Exchange rates (3.2.4.2)

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another, for example £1 = $1.25 |
| Floating exchange rate | A rate set by the demand for and supply of the currency, as for the pound |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency: each pound buys more foreign currency |
| Depreciation | A fall in the value of a currency: each pound buys less foreign currency |

### How the rate is determined: demand and supply of pounds

| Demand for pounds comes from | Supply of pounds comes from |
|---|---|
| Foreign buyers of UK exports | UK buyers of imports |
| Foreign tourists visiting the UK | UK tourists going abroad |
| Foreign firms investing in the UK | UK firms investing abroad |
| Foreign savers attracted by higher UK interest rates | UK savers moving money abroad |
| Speculators who expect the pound to rise | Speculators who expect the pound to fall |

- On a diagram, the price is the exchange rate (for example $ per £) and the quantity is pounds traded.
- **More demand for pounds** (demand shifts right) → the pound **appreciates**.
- **More supply of pounds** (supply shifts right) → the pound **depreciates**.
- Example: a rise in UK interest rates attracts foreign savers, demand for pounds rises, and the pound appreciates.

**UK examples.** After the EU referendum in June 2016 the pound fell by more than 10% against the US dollar within weeks. In September 2022, after a mini-budget of unfunded tax cuts, it fell to a record low against the dollar.

### Effects of a change in the exchange rate

Memory aid: **SPICED**, Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. A weak pound does the opposite.

| Group | Pound appreciates (strong) | Pound depreciates (weak) |
|---|---|---|
| UK consumers | Imported goods and holidays abroad are cheaper | Imports and holidays abroad dearer |
| UK exporters | Goods dearer abroad, so they may sell less | Goods cheaper abroad, so sales may rise |
| UK firms using imported materials | Costs fall | Costs rise, which may push up prices |
| UK firms competing with imports | Face cheaper foreign rivals | Imports dearer, so they gain sales |
| Inflation | Tends to fall (cheaper imports) | Tends to rise (dearer imports) |
| Current account | May worsen | May improve, if demand responds |

### Calculations you must be able to do

**1. Converting prices.** At £1 = $1.25:
- A UK good priced at £40 costs 40 × 1.25 = **$50** in the USA.
- A US good priced at $500 costs 500 ÷ 1.25 = **£400** in the UK.

**2. After a depreciation to £1 = $1.10:**
- The £40 UK good now costs 40 × 1.10 = **$44**: cheaper for US buyers.
- The $500 US good now costs 500 ÷ 1.10 = **£454.55**: dearer for UK buyers.

**3. Size of the change.** From $1.25 to $1.10 is a fall of 0.15 ÷ 1.25 × 100 = **12%** in the value of the pound.

Rule: going from pounds to dollars, **multiply** by the rate; going from dollars to pounds, **divide**.

### Exam traps

- A "strong" pound is not always good: it hurts exporters and firms competing with imports.
- A depreciation helps exporters only if foreign buyers respond to lower prices, and it can raise UK inflation.
- When the pound rises, foreign currencies fall in pound terms. Check which way round the rate is written.
- UK tourists going abroad **supply** pounds; foreign tourists coming to the UK **demand** pounds.

## Quick self-test

1. Give three benefits of international trade.
2. Name two main types of UK export.
3. What is global interdependence? Give one risk it brings the UK.
4. Define an exchange rate.
5. Give two sources of demand for pounds.
6. What happens to the pound if UK interest rates rise? Why?
7. At £1 = €1.20, how much does a £30 UK good cost in euros?
8. At £1 = €1.20, how much does a €90 French good cost in pounds?
9. The pound depreciates. What happens to the price of UK exports abroad?
10. Who loses when the pound appreciates?

### Answers

1. Any three: access to resources not found at home, specialisation, lower prices, more choice, bigger markets and economies of scale, more competition.
2. Any two: financial and business services, machinery (including aircraft engines), cars, medicines and chemicals.
3. Countries relying on each other for goods, markets and supply chains. Risk: for example, a recession abroad cuts UK exports and jobs.
4. The price of one currency in terms of another.
5. Any two: foreign buyers of UK exports, foreign tourists, foreign investors, foreign savers, speculators.
6. It tends to appreciate, because higher returns attract foreign savings, raising demand for pounds.
7. 30 × 1.20 = €36.
8. 90 ÷ 1.20 = £75.
9. They fall in foreign currency, so UK exports become more competitive.
10. Exporters, and UK firms competing with imports; foreign tourists visiting the UK also pay more.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying when you should divide in a currency conversion.
- Saying a strong pound "is good for the economy" without saying for whom.
- Forgetting that the pound's value is set by demand and supply, then giving no shift.
- Naming UK exports as only goods: services are about half.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does SPICED stand for?**
Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. A weak pound does the opposite: imports become dearer and exports cheaper.

**What determines the value of the pound?**
The demand for and supply of pounds. Demand comes from foreign buyers of UK exports, tourists, investors, savers and speculators; supply comes from UK buyers of imports, tourists and investors going abroad.

**What happens to the pound if UK interest rates rise?**
It tends to appreciate, because higher returns attract foreign savers, which raises demand for pounds.

**What is global interdependence?**
Global interdependence is countries relying on each other for goods, services, markets, investment and supply chains, so problems abroad, such as a recession, can affect the UK.

**What does the UK mainly export?**
Services, especially financial, insurance and other business services, which make up about half of UK exports, plus machinery, cars and medicines. The UK usually has a deficit on goods and a surplus on services.
