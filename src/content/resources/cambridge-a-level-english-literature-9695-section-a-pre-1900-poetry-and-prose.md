Section A of Paper 4 asks for one essay on a pre-1900 set text, which may be poetry or prose. It is
worth 25 of the paper's 50 marks and is assessed on **AO1 to AO5**, each 20%. This guide concentrates on
what is distinctive about older texts: their language, their conventions, and the long critical
conversation around them that feeds AO5. For the paper as a whole, see the
[Paper 4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-english-literature-paper-4-pre-and-post-1900-poetry-and-prose-study-guide/).

## What the section asks of you

On each set text you choose an **(a) question**, an essay on the whole text, or a **(b) question** on a
passage or poem from the text printed on the paper. Texts are not allowed in the exam room.

**The form rule matters here.** Across Paper 4 you must write **one essay on a poetry text and one on a
prose text**, one from each section. Your choice of pre-1900 text therefore decides the form of your
post-1900 text.

| 2026 Section A text | Form |
|---|---|
| Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* | Prose |
| Chaucer, *The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale* | Poetry |
| Eliot, *Middlemarch* | Prose |
| Hardy, *Jude the Obscure* | Prose |
| Shakespeare, *Selected Sonnets* | Poetry |
| Whitman, selected poems from *Leaves of Grass* | Poetry |

For 2027, Austen's *Mansfield Park* and a selection of Byron's poetry replace *Pride and Prejudice* and
Whitman. Check the list for your examination year.

## The key idea: read the text in its own terms first

Older texts use conventions that a modern reader may mistake for faults or oddities. A long narrative
digression, a moralising narrator, a strict rhyme scheme or an archaic word is usually a deliberate
choice within the conventions of its time. Strong answers recognise the convention, then analyse how the
writer uses or bends it.

## Pre-1900 prose: the narrator is the method

### The intrusive and the ironic narrator

- **Authorial commentary.** Eliot's narrator steps out of the story to reflect, generalise and address
  the reader. Treat these passages as part of the novel's argument: they widen a single character's
  experience into a claim about human nature or society.
- **Irony and free indirect style.** Austen often presents a character's thoughts in the narrator's
  voice, so the reader shares a view and judges it at once. Ask whose opinion a sentence really carries.
- **Tragic narration.** Hardy's narrator frequently places characters against forces larger than
  themselves: social institutions, chance and coincidence. Look for moments where the narration widens
  from the individual to the system.

### Structure in long novels

These are large books, often first published in parts or volumes. Their structure works through
**parallel plots** (Middlemarch's several marriages), **turning points** placed at the centre or end of a
volume, and **endings** that weigh what has been achieved. For an (a) essay, map the focus across the
whole book, not only the famous scenes.

### Language and register

Pre-1900 prose may use long, periodic sentences that delay their main clause, formal diction, and
dialogue that reveals class and education. Notice when a character's speech differs from the
narrator's: the gap is often where judgement lies.

## Pre-1900 poetry: form and voice

### Chaucer

*The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale* is examined in Middle English, using the edition the syllabus
specifies. Learn to read it aloud; the rhyming couplets and rhythm become clear quickly. The Prologue is
an extended self-presentation in which the Wife sets her own **experience** of five marriages against
the written **authority** of clerical writers on women. The Tale, an Arthurian romance about a knight who
must discover what women most desire, both supports and complicates her Prologue. Ask how the
relationship between teller and tale shapes meaning.

### Shakespeare's sonnets

The Shakespearean sonnet has three quatrains and a closing couplet. Analyse **how argument moves
through the form**: where the turn falls, how each quatrain develops or contradicts the last, and
whether the couplet resolves or undercuts the poem. Because the selection is a sequence, poems can be
read in relation to one another: a later poem may answer an earlier one.

### Whitman

Whitman's poetry is pre-1900 but formally radical: **free verse**, long lines, **catalogues** (lists of
people, places and activities) and **anaphora**. The speaking "I" often claims to contain or speak for
others. Analyse what the cataloguing does: how it builds inclusiveness, energy and a democratic vision,
and where it strains.

## Context and AO5 for older texts

Context is especially useful for pre-1900 texts, because conventions of marriage, property, religion,
class and gender may differ sharply from the reader's own. Use context to explain a choice: why
marriage is the structural goal of a novel, why a narrator addresses the reader, why a speaker defends
herself against written authority.

AO5 draws naturally on **critical history**. Older texts have been read differently over time:

- Is the Wife of Bath a satire of a talkative woman, or a voice challenging the male authorities who
  wrote about women?
- Is the marriage plot conservative, rewarding conformity, or ironic about the economic pressures behind
  it?
- Does a tragic narrator blame individuals, society or fate?

### Annotated model paragraph

> The Wife of Bath's Prologue has been read in two opposed ways, and the poem's structure keeps both in
> play. One reading treats her as an anti-feminist caricature: a woman whose talk confirms the clerical
> stereotypes she quotes. Feminist readings instead see her setting "experience" against "auctoritee" to
> expose those stereotypes as one-sided. The second reading is stronger because the Prologue lets her
> occupy the authorities' own territory: she argues from scripture and turns their examples against
> them. Yet Chaucer never lets her escape their terms entirely, since her defence depends on the texts she
> resists. The poem is most convincingly read as staging an argument about who has the right to speak
> about women, rather than settling it.

The paragraph sets out two readings, tests them against the poem's **method**, and reaches a qualified
**judgement**.

## The (b) answer on a passage or poem

1. **Locate** the passage or poem in the text or sequence.
2. **Thesis** in the question's terms.
3. **Analyse closely**: voice, syntax, form, imagery; for poetry, metre and rhyme.
4. **Link outward** to the whole novel or to other poems in the selection.
5. **AO5**: one or two evaluated alternative readings.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking convention for weakness**: criticising a moralising narrator instead of analysing what it
  does.
- **Modern assumptions**: judging characters by present-day norms without considering the period.
- **Context as history essay**: background with no link to a passage or choice.
- **Avoiding the language**: paraphrasing Middle English or older syntax rather than analysing it.
- **Breaking the form rule**: choosing two prose or two poetry texts across Paper 4.
