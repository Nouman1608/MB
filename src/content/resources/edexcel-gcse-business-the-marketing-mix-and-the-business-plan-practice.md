## Questions

**1.** Define the term "marketing mix". **[2]**

**2.** A café adds an online page where customers order food for collection or delivery. State which element of the marketing mix this mainly changes. **[1]**

**3.** Explain one reason why promotion is important to a new business. **[3]**

**4.** Brindle & Oak makes candles. It expects to sell 500 candles at £14 each. Total fixed costs are £2,100 and variable cost is £5 per candle. Calculate the forecast revenue, total cost and profit. **[4]**

**5.** Brindle & Oak starts with £1,200 in the bank. Month 1: cash inflows £6,300, cash outflows £7,150. Month 2: cash inflows £7,400, cash outflows £6,100.

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for each month. **[4]**
**(b)** State which month has the lower closing balance. **[1]**

**6.** Marrow Street Kitchen is a street-food stall that has opened beside two stalls selling similar meals at lower prices. The owner thinks the target market is office workers who want a quick, good-quality lunch. Analyse how the competitive environment could affect Marrow Street Kitchen's marketing mix. **[4]**

**7.** Explain one way in which changing consumer needs could lead a business to change its product. **[3]**

**8.** Hollin Books is a small bookshop in a market town. Its owner is considering selling through a website as well as the shop. Analyse the impact of e-commerce on Hollin Books' marketing mix. **[6]**

**9.** Give two sections of a business plan that a bank would find useful when deciding whether to lend. **[2]**

**10.** Outline two ways in which writing a business plan can help to minimise risk. **[4]**

**11.** Northgate Fitness plans a small studio offering classes. Total fixed costs are £2,400 a month and variable cost is £4 per customer visit. The owner is choosing between a price of £18, expecting 260 visits a month, and a price of £22, expecting 200 visits a month.

**(a)** Calculate the forecast profit at each price. **[4]**
**(b)** Justify which price the owner should choose. **[6]**

**12.** Rowan Print Studio needs £4,800 for equipment, £2,200 for a deposit on premises and £1,500 for opening stock. The owner has £5,000 of savings.

**(a)** Calculate how much extra finance is needed. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate the importance of a business plan in helping the owner obtain this finance. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A set of choices a business makes [1] about product, price, promotion and place [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition tends to gain credit when it names all four elements. A statement such as "how a business sells" is too vague to show the elements.

**2.** **Place** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-word answer is enough for an identify question. Naming two elements risks giving the wrong one alongside the right one.

**3.** Promotion makes customers aware that the business exists [1]. A new business has no reputation, so people will not buy from it if they do not know about it [1]. Without sales the business earns no revenue and may not survive its first months [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain answers tend to gain credit when a statement is followed by two linked developments. A repeated statement with no development stays at one point.

**4.** Revenue = 14 × 500 = **£7,000** [1]. Total variable cost = 5 × 500 = £2,500 [1]. Total cost = 2,100 + 2,500 = **£4,600** [1]. Profit = 7,000 − 4,600 = **£2,400** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing each step lets a slip in one line be seen without losing the later working. Stating the formula before using it makes the method clear.

**5. (a)** Month 1 net cash-flow = 6,300 − 7,150 = −£850 [1]. Closing balance = 1,200 − 850 = **£350** [1]. Month 2 net cash-flow = 7,400 − 6,100 = £1,300 [1]. Month 2 opening balance is £350, so closing balance = 350 + 1,300 = **£1,650** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** **Month 1** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The second month's opening balance must be the first month's closing balance. Using the original £1,200 again is a common reason for losing the later marks.

**6.** The two rivals sell similar meals at lower prices, so the stall cannot easily win on price alone [1]. Kestrel could stress product quality, such as fresh ingredients and speed, which suits office workers with little time [1]. It could use promotion to tell its target market about this, for example social media posts near the offices at lunchtime [1]. Keeping a higher price fits the quality message but risks losing buyers who compare, so the mix must stay consistent [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers tend to gain credit when each point uses the stem, here the lower-priced rivals and the office workers. A general list of mix elements with no link to the stall stays generic.

**7.** Consumer needs can change, for example more customers wanting healthier food [1]. A business that keeps selling the same product would lose sales to rivals who respond [1]. So it might add healthier options or change ingredients to keep its target market buying [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links the change in need to the change in the product and then to the effect on sales. Naming a change in need without saying what the business alters misses the question.

**8.** Place: a website lets the shop sell to customers outside the town without opening more premises [1]. The business must arrange delivery and returns, which adds cost [1]. Price: customers can compare online prices easily, so the shop may need to review its prices or offer something extra [1]. Product: the website can show a wider range than the shelves hold [1]. Promotion: the shop can use email and social media to reach readers at low cost [1]. Overall, the changes must fit together: if the website stresses low prices, the shop's service-based positioning may not match [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers tend to gain credit when each element named is tied to the bookshop and developed. Writing only "reach more customers" without saying which element it affects is a narrower answer.

**9.** Any two from: forecast revenue, cost and profit; cash-flow forecast; target market (market research); sources of finance. One for each correct section [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct sections are needed. Two different names for the same section, such as "profit forecast" and "forecast profit", stay at one point.

**10.** Market research in the plan tests whether customers want the product before money is spent [1], so the owner avoids committing to an idea with no demand [1]. The cash-flow forecast shows months when cash could run out [1], so the owner can arrange extra finance or reduce spending before a shortage occurs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs an explained link to risk. Naming a section without saying how it lowers risk gains little.

**11. (a)** At £18: revenue = 18 × 260 = £4,680 [1]. Total cost = 2,400 + (4 × 260) = 2,400 + 1,040 = £3,440, so profit = **£1,240** [1]. At £22: revenue = 22 × 200 = £4,400 [1]. Total cost = 2,400 + 800 = £3,200, so profit = **£1,200** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The £18 price gives £40 more profit a month than £22 [1]. It also brings 60 more visits, which builds awareness and word of mouth for a new studio [1]. The £22 price gives almost the same profit with fewer customers to handle, and may suggest higher quality [1]. However, the demand figures are forecasts, and a higher price could lose more customers than expected [1]. The difference in profit is small, so the decision depends on the aim: building a customer base or keeping a premium image [1]. I would choose £18, because the studio is new and the profit is not lower, though the owner should review it once real attendance is known [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify answers tend to gain credit when they use the figures provided and end with a clear recommendation. Calculating profit but not using it in the decision leaves the justification unsupported.

**12. (a)** Total needed = 4,800 + 2,200 + 1,500 = £8,500 [1]. Extra finance = 8,500 − 5,000 = **£3,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The plan shows a lender the idea, the target market and research evidence, which suggests demand exists [1]. Forecast revenue, cost and profit show whether the business could repay a loan [1]. A cash-flow forecast shows whether there is enough cash each month to meet repayments [1]. Stating exactly what the £3,500 is for shows the lender how it will be used [1]. However, a plan is only as good as its forecasts, and a lender may still refuse if the figures look over-optimistic or the owner has little experience [1]. Overall, the plan is very important because most lenders expect evidence, but it supports the request and does not guarantee finance [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers tend to gain credit when they weigh the plan's usefulness against its limits and reach a judgement. Listing sections of the plan with no judgement stays descriptive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining the marketing mix without naming all four elements.
- Giving a one-word reason in an explain question, with no development.
- Writing about the mix in general instead of using the business in the stem.
- Treating price, product, promotion and place as separate lists instead of showing how they fit together.
- Using the starting balance again in month 2 of a cash-flow forecast.
- Showing only a final figure in a calculation.
- Mixing up revenue, total cost and profit.
- Making a recommendation with no reason, or no weighing of both sides, in a justify or evaluate question.
- Claiming that a business plan guarantees success.
