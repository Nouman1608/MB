## Questions

**1.** Define the term *grapevine*. **[2]**

**2.** A food factory runs day, evening and night shifts. Identify two barriers to communication that could arise. **[2]**

**3.** (HL only) Distinguish between a strike and a work-to-rule. **[4]**

**4.** Tolvaney Bakeries has six outlets with 8 staff in each. The owner wants everyone at one 1.5-hour meeting to explain a new allergen-labeling routine.

**(a)** Calculate the total staff-hours the meeting uses. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain one advantage and one limitation of using the meeting. **[4]**

**5.** At Ilsmere Care, 45 of 75 staff say they heard about a rota change from colleagues before the official notice.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who heard it informally first. **[1]**
**(b)** Analyse the possible effects of this on the business. **[4]**

**6.** (HL only) State two sources of conflict in the workplace. **[2]**

**7.** (HL only) Distinguish between conciliation and arbitration. **[4]**

**8.** (HL only) Explain two risks to an employer of threatening redundancies during a pay dispute. **[4]**

**9.** (HL only) Caldervane Engineering has 200 staff on fixed 40-hour weeks. It plans a change of contract to annualized hours so that hours rise and fall with orders. The union opposes the change. Analyse the possible effects on employee relations. **[5]**

**10.** (HL only) Merriott Textiles employs 250 people. Evaluate employee participation and industrial democracy as a way of reducing conflict there. **[8]**

**11.** (HL only) Merriott Textiles has a payroll of 250 staff averaging 31,000 each. The union claims a 4% pay rise and the company offers 1.5%. Of the 90 union members, 81 voted to strike. A stoppage would lose sales of 64,000 a day.

**(a)** Calculate the difference between the cost of the claim and the cost of the offer. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the sales lost in a three-day strike. **[1]**
**(c)** Recommend whether Merriott should raise its offer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The unofficial network of staff communication [1] through which news or rumour passes outside official channels [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition is stronger when it says what makes the grapevine different, namely that it is unofficial, rather than only saying it is "talking".

**2.** Any two, one mark each: staff on the night shift have little contact with managers, who work days [1]; messages are posted where only some shifts can see them [1]. Other valid barriers include language, noise or hand-over gaps. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Identify" asks only for a choice, so extra explanation is not needed. Two distinct barriers are needed, not one barrier worded twice.

**3.** In a strike staff stop work completely [1] and normally lose pay [1]. In a work-to-rule staff stay at work but do only what the contract and rules require [1], so output slows while pay continues [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinguishing answer needs the difference stated, such as stopping versus working slowly. Two separate definitions with no contrast can leave the comparison unclear.

**4. (a)** 6 × 8 = 48 staff; 48 × 1.5 = **72 staff-hours** [1]
**(b)** Advantage: a formal meeting lets the owner explain a detailed routine and take questions [1], so misunderstanding of allergen rules is less likely [1]. Limitation: gathering 48 staff means outlets close or lose service while 72 staff-hours are used [1], which costs sales and may annoy staff on days off [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each side needs a reason and a consequence tied to the bakery. A generic "meetings are slow" is thin without the closing outlets.

**5. (a)** 45 ÷ 75 × 100 = **60%** [1]
**(b)** Staff may hear an incomplete or distorted version [1], which creates worry about their hours and may raise absence [1]. Managers lose control of how the message is received [1], so trust in official notices falls, and staff may stop reading them [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain credit when the effect is placed on a business outcome, such as absence or trust, and not left as "rumours spread".

**6.** Any two, one mark each: pay [1]; working conditions or hours [1]. Others include job security, change and management style. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A source of conflict is a cause. Naming a method such as a strike is not a source.

**7.** Conciliation: a neutral third party helps the two sides to reach their own agreement [1], and cannot impose a settlement [1]. Arbitration: a neutral third party hears both sides [1] and makes a ruling [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The contrast is who decides. Answers that only say both involve a third party do not show the difference.

**8.** Risk 1: skilled staff may leave early for other jobs [1], so the firm loses expertise and output [1]. Risk 2: the threat may harden the union's stance and provoke a strike [1], which stops production and costs sales [1]. Other valid risks: lower morale, damaged reputation as an employer. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each risk needs a developed consequence. Two risks listed with no development leave much of the question unanswered.

**9.** Possible negative effect: staff face unpredictable hours [1], which can hurt trust and lead to a work-to-rule or strike [1]. Possible positive effect: the firm can match labor to orders, cutting idle time and giving a stronger case for job security [1], if the firm consults staff first [1]. Judgement: the outcome depends on how much say staff have over the schedules and how the change is explained [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An "analyse" answer breaks the effect into parts and links each to the case. A balanced view with a short judgement is the stronger answer.

**10.** Definition: staff take part in decisions through consultation, councils or representation [1]. Strength: staff with a voice feel heard, so grievances are raised before they become disputes [1], and managers get practical ideas from a 250-strong workforce [1]. Limitation: decisions take longer [1], and participation may be token if managers ignore the views, which can increase distrust [1]. Application: with 250 staff, representatives would be needed, so how they are chosen matters [1]. Judgement: it is likely to reduce conflict over time if the company acts on staff views [1], but it is not enough alone for a live pay dispute, where conciliation or arbitration may still be needed [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers tend to gain credit when strengths and limitations both appear and the judgement is conditional on the case, not a repeated summary.

**11. (a)** Payroll = 250 × 31,000 = 7,750,000 [1]. Claim = 4% = 310,000 and offer = 1.5% = 116,250 [1]. Difference = **193,750** [1]
**(b)** 3 × 64,000 = **192,000** [1]
**(c)** Recommendation: raise the offer, and use conciliation to settle [1]. A three-day strike loses 192,000, almost the whole 193,750 gap [1], so refusing is poor value [1]. A midpoint offer of 2.75% costs 213,125, only 96,875 more than the current offer, which is about half the cost of the strike [1]. 90% of the union members voted to strike, so the threat is real [1]. Counter-point: only 90 of 250 staff are union members, and a higher offer sets a precedent, so arbitration remains a fallback if conciliation fails [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation is stronger when it uses the figures from (a) and (b) as evidence and states its own limit. A calculation with no link to the decision gains little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining grapevine or informal communication as "gossip" without saying it sits outside official channels.
- Listing barriers with no link to the business in the stem.
- Confusing conciliation with arbitration, or describing both as "talking".
- Calling a work-to-rule a strike, or a lockout a union action.
- Giving a calculation result with no working shown on a multi-step question.
- Stopping at the cost gap and not using it to support a decision.
- Writing one-sided answers to evaluate and recommend questions.
- Ending with a summary that repeats points instead of a judgement.
