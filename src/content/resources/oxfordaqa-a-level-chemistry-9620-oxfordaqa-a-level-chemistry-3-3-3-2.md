## What the specification asks for

The specification's content statement is "the concurrent substitution and elimination reactions of a halogenoalkane (eg 2-bromopropane with potassium hydroxide)". You should be able to:

- "explain the role of the reagent as both nucleophile and base"
- "outline the mechanisms of these reactions".

So the subtopic is really about **one reagent, two pathways, one flask**. Questions typically give a halogenoalkane and potassium hydroxide, then ask for both products, both mechanisms, or the role of the hydroxide ion in each.

## One ion, two roles

The hydroxide ion carries three lone pairs on oxygen. What it does with a lone pair decides the reaction.

| Role | What OH⁻ does with its lone pair | Bond formed | Reaction | Organic product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nucleophile** | Donates it to the δ+ **carbon** bonded to the halogen | C–O | Substitution | Alcohol |
| **Base** | Donates it to a **hydrogen** on a carbon adjacent to the C–X carbon, removing H⁺ | O–H (water forms) | Elimination | Alkene |

A **base** here means a proton acceptor. Both roles involve donating an electron pair; the difference is the atom that receives it. That is the precise answer to "explain the role of the reagent".

## The specification's example: 2-bromopropane

When 2-bromopropane is heated with potassium hydroxide, both reactions happen at the same time.

**Substitution (OH⁻ as nucleophile):**

CH₃CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + Br⁻  (propan-2-ol)

**Elimination (OH⁻ as base):**

CH₃CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₂=CHCH₃ + H₂O + Br⁻  (propene)

With KOH as the formula: CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH → CH₂=CHCH₃ + H₂O + KBr. The overall change is the loss of HBr from adjacent carbons, which is why it is called elimination.

### Outlining the elimination mechanism

Three curly arrows, drawn in one step:

```
        H     Br
        |     |
   H — C1 —— C2 — CH₃              arrow 1: lone pair on O of :OH⁻ → H on C1
        |     |                    arrow 2: C1–H bond → between C1 and C2
        H     H                    arrow 3: C2–Br bond → Br
   ⟹  CH₂=CH–CH₃  +  H₂O  +  :Br⁻
```

Marking points to secure:

1. Arrow 1 starts on a **lone pair** of OH⁻ and ends at a **hydrogen on an adjacent carbon**, not at the C–Br carbon.
2. Arrow 2 starts on the **C–H bond** and ends between the two carbons, where the pair becomes the second bond of C=C. Draw it clearly into the C1–C2 region.
3. Arrow 3 starts on the **C–Br bond** and ends on Br.
4. Products: the alkene, water and Br⁻.

Put the substitution mechanism beside it for comparison: two arrows only, the first going to carbon C2, the second from C–Br to Br. Being able to draw both from the same starting structure is exactly what "concurrent" reactions require.

## Steering the balance with conditions

Both reactions always compete, but conditions change which product dominates:

| Conditions | Main role of OH⁻ | Main product |
|---|---|---|
| KOH or NaOH in **water**, warm | nucleophile | alcohol |
| KOH dissolved in **ethanol**, heated (reflux) | base | alkene |

When a question says "aqueous" expect the alcohol as major product; when it says "ethanolic" or "in ethanol", expect the alkene. Higher temperature also favours elimination.

## Counting elimination products

A halogenoalkane can give more than one alkene when the C–X carbon has **non-equivalent** neighbours carrying hydrogen. Use the same four-step check every time:

1. Mark the C–X carbon.
2. List each adjacent carbon that has at least one H.
3. Equivalent neighbours give the same alkene; different neighbours give different (positional) isomers.
4. For each alkene, check both C=C carbons: if each carries two different groups, E and Z isomers both form.

### Worked example 1: 2-bromobutane

*2-Bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃, is heated with ethanolic potassium hydroxide. Name every alkene formed.*

- The C–Br carbon is C2. Its neighbours are C1 (three H) and C3 (two H). They are not equivalent.
- Loss of H from C1: **but-1-ene**, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃. C1 carries two H atoms, so no E/Z isomers.
- Loss of H from C3: **but-2-ene**, CH₃CH=CHCH₃. Each alkene carbon carries H and CH₃, so **(E)-but-2-ene** and **(Z)-but-2-ene** both form.
- Answer: **three alkenes**. The substitution product, butan-2-ol, also forms in smaller amounts.

### Worked example 2: neighbours with no hydrogen

*Explain why 1-bromo-2,2-dimethylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCH₂Br, cannot form an alkene by this mechanism.*

The only carbon adjacent to the C–Br carbon is the central quaternary carbon, which carries **no hydrogen atom**. There is no H for the base to remove from an adjacent carbon, so this elimination cannot occur; only substitution is possible.

### Worked example 3: how much went each way?

*6.15 g of 2-bromopropane (Mr 122.9) reacts completely with potassium hydroxide. 1.80 g of propan-2-ol (Mr 60.0) is formed and the rest of the 2-bromopropane is converted into propene. Calculate the percentage of the 2-bromopropane that underwent elimination.*

```
n(2-bromopropane) = 6.15 ÷ 122.9 = 0.0500 mol
n(propan-2-ol)    = 1.80 ÷ 60.0  = 0.0300 mol   (substitution, 1 : 1)
n(eliminated)     = 0.0500 − 0.0300 = 0.0200 mol
percentage        = 0.0200 ÷ 0.0500 × 100 = 40.0 %
```

The calculation itself is simple; the chemistry it tests is that both products come from the same reactant at the same time.

## Why it matters

Elimination is the AS route from a halogenoalkane to an alkene, and so links section 3.3.3 to [alkenes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-alkenes/). It has a low atom economy: for 2-bromopropane with KOH, only 42.0 of 179.0 mass units of reactants end up as propene (23.5 %), because water and potassium bromide are by-products. Alcohols give alkenes by a different, acid-catalysed elimination in [3.3.5.2](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-5-2/); do not mix up the two reagents.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the OH⁻ arrow to the C–Br carbon in an elimination mechanism.
- Removing H from the C–Br carbon itself rather than from an adjacent carbon.
- Missing the arrow from the C–H bond into the C–C bond, so C=C has no origin.
- Saying OH⁻ "acts as an acid" or "acts as an electrophile". It acts as a **base**.
- Giving only one alkene from 2-bromobutane, or forgetting E/Z isomers of but-2-ene.
- Swapping the conditions: aqueous favours substitution; ethanolic favours elimination.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain the role of the reagent**: say *where* the lone pair goes in each case: to the δ+ carbon (nucleophile) or to an adjacent H, removing a proton (base).
- **Outline the mechanism**: three arrows for elimination, starting from a lone pair and from two bonds; include products.
- **Name all the products**: work through the four-step check and write full names, including E/Z labels.
- **State the conditions**: name the solvent, because it is the deciding factor.
- **Suggest**: if asked why a mixture forms, link it to the two roles happening concurrently.
