This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 12.1 of Unit 5, Human development and diversity, part of the Higher Level extension of IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019), examined in Paper 3. The [12.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-development-opportunities-revision-notes/) already list measures of development, the development goals, and examples of trade, investment, aid, debt relief, microfinance and remittances. This page adds two contrasting theories that frame those routes, the idea of the middle-income trap, technological leapfrogging, the role of gender, and a calculation of the Gini coefficient from income shares. Practise with the [12.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-12-1-practice/); the [Human development and diversity study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-human-development-diversity-study-guide/) gives the unit overview, including a worked HDI example.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Evaluate measures of development and what they reveal or hide.
- Examine how global interactions create, or limit, opportunities for development.
- Analyse who is able to take those opportunities, by place and by social group.
- Evaluate the effectiveness of different routes to development, using located examples.

## Two contrasting views of development

Many arguments in this sub-topic rest on two older but influential frameworks. Knowing them lets you explain *why* commentators disagree about trade, investment and aid.

| | Modernisation (Rostow) | Dependency theory |
|---|---|---|
| Key thinker | W. W. Rostow, *The Stages of Economic Growth* (1960) | Associated with Andre Gunder Frank and Latin American economists of the 1960s |
| Core idea | Countries pass through stages, from traditional society through take-off to high mass consumption | Global trade and investment keep peripheral countries supplying raw materials to the core, so integration can cause underdevelopment |
| View of global interaction | An opportunity: investment and trade help a country reach take-off | A trap: unequal exchange drains value from the periphery |
| Criticism | Assumes one Western path suits every country | Struggles to explain the rapid growth of East Asian economies that integrated into world trade |

Modern answers treat both as partial: global interactions can be an opportunity or a trap depending on terms of trade, governance and what a country produces.

## The middle-income trap

Some countries grow quickly from low to middle income, using cheap labour and imported technology, then stall. Wages rise, so they lose their cost advantage over poorer competitors, but they lack the skills, research and institutions to compete with high-income economies on innovation. This is called the **middle-income trap**. South Korea and Taiwan are often cited as economies that escaped it through heavy investment in education, research and high-value manufacturing; several Latin American economies are often described as having stalled at middle income for long periods. The idea is useful for questions that ask whether a development route can be sustained, not just started.

## Leapfrogging: skipping a stage

Global technology flows can allow places to **leapfrog**, adopting a newer technology without passing through the older one. Many people in sub-Saharan Africa gained mobile phones without ever having a landline. In Kenya, the mobile money service M-Pesa, launched by Safaricom in 2007, let people send and store money by text message without a bank account, widening access to financial services, especially in rural areas. Similar leapfrogging is possible with off-grid solar power where national grids are absent.

Evaluation: leapfrogging can spread opportunity quickly and cheaply, but it depends on network coverage, affordable devices and digital skills, so it can widen gaps between connected and unconnected groups.

## Where opportunity spreads: cumulative causation

Opportunity also has a geography within countries. The economist Gunnar Myrdal, in work published in 1957, argued that growth tends to concentrate where it starts. A thriving core region attracts investment, skilled migrants and capital from elsewhere (**backwash effects**), which can drain peripheral regions. Over time, demand from the core for food, raw materials and services may spread some benefits outward (**spread effects**). Whether spread effects outweigh backwash depends largely on policy: infrastructure, regional investment and services in lagging areas. The model helps explain why national growth can coexist with stubborn regional inequality.

## Who can take opportunities: gender

Development opportunities are not open to everyone equally. Gender is one of the clearest dividing lines.

- **Education.** Girls' schooling is associated with later marriage, smaller families, better child health and higher earnings, so it is one of the most widely cited routes to wider development.
- **Work.** Export industries such as garment manufacturing have created paid jobs for many women, increasing their independence, though often with low pay and poor conditions.
- **Assets and finance.** Where women cannot easily own land or open accounts, they are less able to invest or borrow.
- **Measurement.** The Gender Development Index compares HDI values for women and men, while the Gender Inequality Index captures gaps in health, empowerment and the labour market.

A strong answer shows that a single development route can widen some gender gaps while narrowing others.

## Worked example: a Gini coefficient from income shares

The Gini coefficient can be estimated from the share of income received by each fifth (quintile) of households. Figures are invented.

```
Quintile (poorest to richest)    1      2      3      4      5
Share of income (%)              5      9     14     22     50
Cumulative share (Lorenz curve)  0.05   0.14   0.28   0.50   1.00

Each quintile is 0.2 of the population. The area under the Lorenz curve is
  0.2 x 0.5 x [(0 + 0.05) + (0.05 + 0.14) + (0.14 + 0.28) + (0.28 + 0.50) + (0.50 + 1.00)]
  = 0.1 x (0.05 + 0.19 + 0.42 + 0.78 + 1.50)
  = 0.1 x 2.94 = 0.294

Gini = 1 - 2 x (area under the Lorenz curve) = 1 - 0.588 = 0.412
```

A Gini of about 0.41 shows substantial inequality: the richest fifth receives ten times the share of the poorest fifth (50 / 5 = 10). The evaluation: grouping households into fifths hides inequality *within* each group, so this method slightly underestimates the true Gini, and the figure says nothing about whether inequality is rising or falling unless it is compared over time.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the view that global interactions offer more opportunities than threats to development.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Global technology flows have created opportunities that bypass weak infrastructure: Kenya's M-Pesa, launched in 2007, extended financial services to people without bank accounts **[evidence of opportunity, located and dated]**. Yet dependency theorists argue that integration can lock countries into supplying low-value goods, and economies that rely on assembly work risk stalling in a middle-income trap **[threat, framed by theory]**. Who benefits also differs: connected urban groups gain first, while rural women may lack devices, skills or assets **[distribution by place and group]**. Opportunities therefore outweigh threats where governments invest in skills and access, but not automatically **[conditional judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Using Rostow or dependency theory as if either is simply correct.
- Treating rising national income as proof that everyone has gained.
- Describing leapfrogging without its conditions: coverage, cost and skills.
- Ignoring gender, age and location when discussing who takes opportunities.
- Calculating a Gini coefficient without cumulating income shares first.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** routes to development against criteria such as speed, sustainability and fairness.
- **Discuss** needs at least two perspectives; theories provide them ready-made.
- **Examine** questions reward separating who gains from who loses, by place and group.
- **To what extent** needs a conditional judgement where the evidence is mixed, with the conditions stated.
- Use one detailed, dated example per route rather than many shallow ones.
