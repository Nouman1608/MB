Section 12.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks one question from four angles:
is religion declining in modern societies? The syllabus lists four content points: the
secularisation thesis; evidence for a decline in religious belief; discussions about whether
rationalisation is leading to a decline in religious belief; and debates about whether religion
has lost its social significance. It links the section to the key concept of *Social change and
development*, so every answer should treat secularisation as a claim about change over time that
needs a clear starting point and reliable evidence.

The broader [secularisation, feminism and post-modern religion guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-influence-of-religion/)
gives the core evidence for and against. This page goes further into how the concept is defined,
why the evidence is contested, and how to turn the debate into a Paper 4 essay.

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section C (Religion),
heading 12 *The influence of religion*, sub-topic 12.1:

- The secularisation thesis.
- Evidence for a decline in religious belief.
- Whether rationalisation is leading to a decline in religious belief.
- Whether religion has lost its social significance.

Paper 4 has two essay questions in each of its three sections; candidates answer two, from
different sections, and each essay carries 35 marks. Essays must explore contrasting views or
evidence.

## What exactly is being claimed?

Much of the disagreement about secularisation comes from people measuring different things. Karel
Dobbelaere (1981) argued that secularisation is a multi-dimensional concept and separated three
levels:

| Level | Dobbelaere's term | What would count as secularisation |
|---|---|---|
| Society | Laicisation | Institutions such as law, education and welfare run without religious authority |
| Organisations | Religious change | Churches adapt their teaching and practice to fit secular culture |
| Individuals | Religious involvement | Fewer people attend, belong, pray or believe |

The levels can move independently. A society can be highly laicised while many individuals still
pray, or a state can have an established church while attendance is low. A strong essay names the
level it is discussing before it judges the evidence.

Bryan Wilson's definition, that religion loses *social significance*, points mainly at the societal
level. Steve Bruce's version places more weight on individuals: each generation is less religious
than the one before. These are related but separate claims, so evidence against one does not
automatically refute the other.

## Why modernisation was expected to weaken religion

The thesis combines several processes. Students often list them without explaining the mechanism,
so learn each as a cause and an effect:

- **Structural differentiation.** Talcott Parsons argued that, as societies industrialise,
  specialised institutions take over functions once performed by religion. Schools educate, the
  state provides welfare and courts settle disputes. Religion survives but becomes one
  institution among many, with a narrower role.
- **Social and cultural diversity.** Bruce argues that industrial societies break up close-knit
  communities in which shared religion was reinforced daily. When people live among others with
  different beliefs, faith becomes a personal option rather than a taken-for-granted truth.
- **Religious diversity.** Peter Berger's argument in *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) was that
  competing faiths undermine each other's claim to certainty, creating a "crisis of credibility".
- **Rationalisation.** Covered below as a separate syllabus point.

## Evidence for decline, and the problems with it

The syllabus says "evidence for a decline in religious belief", so examiners reward answers that
evaluate the quality of evidence, not just its direction.

**Types of evidence and their limits**

| Evidence | What it seems to show | Problem to raise |
|---|---|---|
| Attendance counts | Falling participation in many Western European churches | Attendance is practice, not belief; people may attend for social reasons or believe without attending |
| Membership records | Declining enrolment in established denominations | Organisations count members differently; some keep names on rolls, others remove inactive members |
| Census religious identity | Growth in "no religion" responses in some countries | A census question about identity may capture cultural or ethnic belonging, not faith |
| Belief surveys | Fewer people affirming a personal God | Results depend on question wording, such as "God" versus "a higher power" |
| Rites of passage | Fewer religious weddings and baptisms | Civil alternatives became easier and more acceptable; the choice may reflect convenience |

**The golden-age problem.** Decline must be measured from a starting point. Historians of religion
have questioned whether pre-industrial Europe was as devout as the thesis assumes; the 1851 Census
of Religious Worship in England and Wales showed that a large part of the population did not
attend on census Sunday. If past religiosity is overstated, measured decline is exaggerated.

**Definitions shape findings.** An *exclusive* definition (belief in the supernatural or God) makes
decline easier to show. An *inclusive* definition (anything that performs religious functions,
such as national rituals or sport) makes religion look ever-present. Linking back to section 11.1,
on defining and measuring religion, is valid and credited.

**Debates about believing and belonging.** Grace Davie's "believing without belonging" is one
reading of the gap between belief and attendance. David Voas and Alasdair Crockett (2005) replied
that British survey data showed belief falling alongside attendance, which they summarised as
"neither believing nor belonging". Davie later proposed **vicarious religion**: a small active
minority practises on behalf of a larger number who approve of it and turn to it at moments such
as funerals or national tragedies. Bruce and Voas questioned how far vicarious religion can be
demonstrated with evidence.

## Is rationalisation leading to decline?

Max Weber used the phrase "the disenchantment of the world" for the spread of rational,
calculable thinking: events are explained by natural causes, and the world is treated as
something that can be understood and controlled. Two points push this further than the basic
account:

- **Religion as the source of its own decline.** Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of
  Capitalism* (1905) showed how Protestantism encouraged a disciplined, rational way of life.
  Berger and Bruce drew out the implication that, by removing magic, saints and sacraments from
  everyday faith, Protestantism opened the way to a world explained without God.
- **Technology and practical control.** Bruce argues that when people can deal with illness,
  harvest failure or danger through medicine and technology, they turn to prayer less often in
  daily life.

**Evaluating the rationalisation argument**

- Belief in the supernatural persists among people who use science and technology daily, and
  scientists hold a range of religious views; rational thinking and faith can coexist.
- New forms of spirituality (see section 12.3) suggest rationalisation may change religion rather
  than remove it.
- Rationalisation may explain declining *institutional* authority better than declining *private*
  belief.
- The argument is built on Western European history and may not transfer to societies with
  different religious traditions.

## Has religion lost its social significance?

This is the societal-level question. Wilson argued that even if individuals keep private beliefs,
religion no longer shapes how society works. Evidence for the claim includes laicisation (state
control of education, welfare and law) and **secularisation from within**: Will Herberg's
*Protestant, Catholic, Jew* (1955) argued that American churches remained full partly because
they had absorbed secular values, celebrating "the American way of life" more than distinctive
doctrine.

Evidence against includes the public role religion plays in politics and identity (Casanova's
deprivatisation thesis, and the case studies in section 11.3), the persistence of religious
schools and charities, and religion's role in national ceremonies.

**David Martin** (*A General Theory of Secularization*, 1978) offers a useful middle position:
secularisation is not one universal process, and its pattern depends on each country's history,
for example whether a single church was tied to the state or many denominations competed.

## Worked example: annotated essay plan

*Original practice question:* "The evidence for secularisation is too unreliable to support the
claim that religion is declining." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

1. **Introduction.** Define secularisation (Wilson) and state that its truth depends on what is
   measured; name Dobbelaere's three levels. *Shows the debate is about evidence, not opinion.*
2. **Evidence appears strong.** Long-term falls in attendance, membership and religious rites in
   Western Europe; Bruce's generational decline. *Gives the view a fair hearing.*
3. **Reliability problems.** Counting methods, survey wording, census identity questions, the
   golden-age problem. *This is the core of the question; spend most time here.*
4. **Validity problems.** Practice is not belief; Davie's believing without belonging and
   vicarious religion. *Moves from "is it accurate?" to "does it measure religion?"*
5. **Counter-evaluation.** Voas and Crockett show belief declining too, so the trend is
   consistent across several types of data. *Several imperfect measures pointing the same way
   strengthen the case.*
6. **Global perspective.** Data from Western Europe cannot support a universal claim; Martin's
   varied patterns.
7. **Conclusion.** The evidence is imperfect but consistent for institutional decline in Western
   Europe; it is weaker for private belief and weakest as a global claim.

## Common mistakes

- Treating secularisation as one thing; always say which level you mean.
- Listing statistics you cannot source. Named studies and types of evidence are safer.
- Using post-modern arguments (section 12.3) without linking them back to the 12.1 question.
- Describing Weber's theory of capitalism (section 11.3) at length instead of applying the idea of
  rationalisation to belief.
- Concluding "secularisation is true" or "false" without specifying where and in what sense.

## Command words in Paper 4

Paper 4 essays use **Evaluate**: judge the quality, importance or value of something. Evaluation
here means weighing types of evidence, testing a theory against cases outside Western Europe, and
reaching a qualified judgement. A conclusion that only repeats points scores less than one that
explains which arguments carry most weight and why.
