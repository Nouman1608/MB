**This guide covers content area 3.2.2.5 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiat (9237), The different schools of thought in Islam: "The origins and role of the major law schools in Islam including: Hanafi, Shaf'i, Maliki, Hanbali and Ja'fari."** The prescribed passage is Qur'an 4:59.

The earlier [schools of thought study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-islamiyat-9237-schools-of-thought/) introduces each founder. This guide takes a different approach. It asks **why legal differences were bound to arise**, fixes the dates so that you can place the founders in order, shows how a single verse can lead to two rulings, and trains you to compare the schools by method.

## A school is a method

A **madhhab** is a school of Islamic law (*fiqh*). The word means "a way to go". Each school gathered the rulings of its leading scholars and, more importantly, the method used to reach them. The five schools agree on belief and on the Five Pillars. They differ on some details of practice.

Qualified scholars who work out rulings from the sources perform **ijtihad**. Other Muslims follow a school or a scholar, which is called **taqlid**. This works like any expert knowledge: most people follow a doctor's advice rather than learning medicine for themselves.

## Why did differences arise?

Differences of opinion (*ikhtilaf*) began in the time of the companions and grew as the Muslim world grew. Four causes explain most of them.

1. **Wording that can be read in more than one way.** The best-known example is Qur'an 2:228, which says that divorced women wait "three *quru'*". The Arabic word can mean *menstrual periods* or *periods of purity between them*. The Hanafi and Hanbali schools understand it as menstrual periods; the Maliki and Shafi'i schools as periods of purity. Both are reading the same verse, and both rely on the language.
2. **Different Hadith known in different places.** The companions settled in cities such as Madinah, Kufa, Basra and Damascus. A scholar might know a Hadith that another had never heard, or might grade one differently.
3. **Different methods of reasoning.** Some scholars gave great weight to the practice of Madinah, some to analogy and public interest, and some to the text alone.
4. **Different circumstances.** Customs, trade and climate differed from Iraq to the Hijaz to Egypt, so scholars applied the same principles to different situations.

Seen this way, the schools do not mean that Islam is divided. They show how a living law was applied by careful scholars, each trying to follow the Qur'an and Sunnah. The Qur'an itself accepts that people may differ and tells them how to settle it (4:59).

## The founders in order

| School | Founder | Dates (approx.) | Centre |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ja'fari | Ja'far al-Sadiq | 83-148 AH (702-765 CE) | Madinah |
| Hanafi | Abu Hanifa (al-Nu'man ibn Thabit) | 80-150 AH (699-767 CE) | Kufa |
| Maliki | Malik ibn Anas | 93-179 AH (711-795 CE) | Madinah |
| Shafi'i | Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i | 150-204 AH (767-820 CE) | Madinah, Iraq, then Egypt |
| Hanbali | Ahmad ibn Hanbal | 164-241 AH (780-855 CE) | Baghdad |

Notice the overlap. The first three imams lived in the same period, and al-Shafi'i and Ahmad were of the next generation. Al-Shafi'i studied under Malik, and Ahmad studied under al-Shafi'i. The schools were formed by teachers and students who knew and respected one another, and the names we use today were attached by their students afterwards.

## What marks each school

**Hanafi.** Abu Hanifa taught in Kufa, where there were few Hadith and many new legal questions. His school makes full use of reasoning: qiyas, *istihsan* (preferring a ruling that is fairer in the case), and local custom. His students, Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani, wrote down his teaching, and Abu Yusuf served as a chief judge under the Abbasids, which helped the school spread. It later became the official school of the Ottoman Empire. Today it has the largest following, especially in South Asia, Turkey and Central Asia.

**Maliki.** Malik taught in Madinah, the city of the Prophet (pbuh). He treated the **practice of the people of Madinah** as strong evidence of the Sunnah, since it passed from generation to generation. His book *al-Muwatta* combines Hadith, the opinions of companions and the practice of Madinah. The school spread to North and West Africa and, in the past, to Muslim Spain. It also gives weight to public interest (*maslahah*).

**Shafi'i.** Al-Shafi'i learned from Malik and from Hanafi scholars, so he knew both approaches. In his book *al-Risala* he set out the principles of jurisprudence in an orderly way: Qur'an, Sunnah, Ijma, Qiyas. He argued that an authentic Hadith should always outweigh local practice or personal opinion. His school is followed in Egypt, East Africa, Yemen and South-East Asia.

**Hanbali.** Ahmad ibn Hanbal was an expert in Hadith and compiled the large collection called *al-Musnad*. His method gave priority to the texts, including the opinions of the companions, over analogy. He suffered imprisonment during the *mihna* because he would not say that the Qur'an was created, and he became a symbol of principle. His school is followed mainly in Saudi Arabia and Qatar.

**Ja'fari.** The Ja'fari school is named after Ja'far al-Sadiq, a descendant of the Prophet (pbuh) through Hazrat Ali (RA) and Hazrat Fatima (RA), who taught in Madinah. Twelver Shi'a Muslims regard him as the sixth Imam. It is followed in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain, Lebanon and Azerbaijan. In addition to the Qur'an and Sunnah, it recognises the guidance of the Imams of the Prophet's family (the *Ahl al-Bayt*). It uses reason (*'aql*) rather than Sunni qiyas, and living senior scholars continue to give rulings by ijtihad.

## Comparing the methods

| School | Distinctive source or principle | Attitude to reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Hanafi | Istihsan; custom | Wide use of reasoned opinion |
| Maliki | Practice of Madinah; public interest | Moderate; ties reasoning to Madinah's practice |
| Shafi'i | Fixed order of sources in al-Risala | Qiyas allowed, but under strict rules |
| Hanbali | Hadith and companions' opinions first | Narrow use of analogy |
| Ja'fari | Teachings of the Imams; reason | Reason ('aql) in place of qiyas |

## Examples of differences in practice

These are examples of the kind you can quote, and in each case the school is following a position it holds on the sources.

| Matter | Differences |
|---|---|
| Hands in prayer | Hanafi: below the navel; Shafi'i: above the navel; Ja'fari: arms at the sides; Maliki: commonly at the sides, though folding the hands is also permitted |
| Witr prayer | Hanafi: required (wajib); most others: strongly recommended |
| Combining prayers | Ja'fari: Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha is regularly allowed; other schools: only in particular situations, such as travel |
| Prostration | Ja'fari: on natural earth or a clay tablet; others: on any clean surface |

None of these changes the fact that all five schools pray five times a day, fast in Ramadhan, pay Zakah and make the pilgrimage.

## The role of the schools today

- **Guidance.** They give ordinary Muslims clear, tested answers.
- **Consistency.** Courts and communities use a settled body of law.
- **Flexibility.** The variety of methods lets the law respond to new questions.
- **Respect for the sources.** Rulings must be justified from the Qur'an and Sunnah, not personal taste.
- **Mutual recognition.** In 2005 the Amman Message, supported by scholars from around the Muslim world, recognised the four Sunni schools and the Ja'fari school, among others, as valid.

## The prescribed passage: Qur'an 4:59

The specification gives this verse as: "O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger, if you should believe in Allah and the Last Day. That is the best [way] and best in result."

- **"Obey Allah and obey the Messenger":** every school starts with the Qur'an and Sunnah.
- **"Those in authority among you":** understood by many to include scholars, which supports following trained jurists.
- **"If you disagree over anything":** disagreement is expected.
- **"Refer it to Allah and the Messenger":** the method to settle disagreement is to return to the sources.
- **"Best [way] and best in result":** this approach gives good outcomes, including unity.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain **two** reasons why different schools of law developed. Use a source of authority. **[6]**

> One reason was that the sources could be read in more than one way [1]. For example, the word *quru'* in the verse on waiting after divorce can mean periods of menstruation or periods of purity, so the Hanafi and Maliki schools reached different rulings [1]. A second reason was that knowledge of Hadith was spread over different cities [1], so a scholar in Madinah might know a report that a scholar in Kufa had not heard, and each reasoned from what he knew [1]. Qur'an 4:59 says "if you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger", which accepts that disagreement will occur and shows that the schools are different attempts to follow the same sources [2].

## Common mistakes

- **Calling the schools sects.** They are schools of law; the Sunni schools share one creed.
- **Saying the schools disagree about the pillars.** They disagree about details.
- **Leaving out the Ja'fari school.** The specification names five.
- **Getting the order wrong.** Abu Hanifa and Ja'far al-Sadiq were the earliest; Ahmad ibn Hanbal was the latest.
- **Not saying why.** "Origins and role" means causes and purposes, not just names.
