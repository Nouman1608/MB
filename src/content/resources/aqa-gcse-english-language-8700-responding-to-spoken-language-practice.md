The spoken language endorsement is graded holistically, with no marks. The marks below are for these practice tasks
only. Every exchange on this page is original. Several questions refer to the same imagined talk: *"Should our school
replace printed textbooks with tablets?"*, in which the speaker argued in favour.

---

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** State the assessment objective for this strand and summarise what it requires. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the **type** of each question asked after the talk on tablets: (a) *"What do you mean by 'digital textbook'?"* (b) *"Wouldn't screens make it harder for some students to concentrate?"* (c) *"What would happen if the Wi-Fi failed during a lesson?"* **[3]**

**3.** Write **one** question the speaker could ask to elicit clarification after being asked: *"But is it fair?"* **[1]**

**4.** Explain the difference between the Merit and Distinction criteria for responding. **[3]**

**5.** A student answers every question with "Yes" or "No, not really." Which criterion would this match most closely, and why might it not even meet that criterion? **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** formal holding phrases a speaker could use to gain thinking time. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why answering before the questioner has finished can lead to a weak response. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** After the talk on tablets, an audience member asks: *"How much would all this cost?"* The speaker had not researched cost. Write a response that stays formal and honest and still adds something useful. **[4]**

**9.** An audience member challenges: *"Isn't this unfair on families who can't afford good internet at home?"* Write a response using the shape *acknowledge, concede, respond, restate*, and label each part. **[4]**

**10.** Read these three original responses to the question *"Why do you think tablets are better than books?"*

> *Response A: "Because they're better. They just are."*
>
> *Response B: "Tablets are lighter to carry and can hold every textbook at once, so students wouldn't need to bring
> heavy bags. For example, Year 9 currently carry five books on some days."*
>
> *Response C: "I'd say 'better' depends on what we want textbooks to do. If the aim is access, tablets win: they're
> lighter, always up to date, and can read text aloud for students who struggle with print. But if the aim is
> concentration, there's research suggesting some readers remember more from paper. So my real argument is for
> tablets alongside some printed books, not instead of them."*

For each response, say which grade description it is closest to and explain why. **[6]**

**11.** Feedback from the audience: *"Your point about the environment didn't have any evidence."* Write a response that shows you have listened and engages constructively. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how a speaker can prepare for questions they cannot predict. Give **three** methods. **[3]**

**13.** Explain what "perceptively" means in the Distinction criterion, using an example question of your own about any presentation topic. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** AO8 [1]: listen and respond appropriately to spoken language, including to questions and feedback on presentations [1].

**2.** (a) clarification [1]; (b) challenge [1]; (c) hypothetical [1].

**3.** A question that narrows the meaning, for example: "Do you mean fair on students, or fair on teachers who would have to change how they teach?" [1].

**4.** Merit requires responding formally and in some detail [1]. Distinction requires responding perceptively, seeing what lies behind the question [1], and, where appropriate, elaborating with further ideas and information beyond the presentation [1].

**5.** It is closest to the Pass description, a response "in a straightforward manner" [1]; but a bare "Yes" or "No, not really" may not count as an appropriate response at all, and the informal register may fall short of language appropriate to the formal setting [1].

**6.** Any two formal phrases, one mark each (2 × [1]). For example: "That's an important question; let me think about it carefully." "There are a couple of sides to that, so I'll take them in turn."

**7.** The speaker may answer a question that was not asked, or miss its key word [1], so the answer is not appropriate to the question and cannot be perceptive [1].

**8.** Award 4 marks:
- an honest acknowledgement that cost was not covered, in formal register [1];
- a reasoned point about how cost would affect the proposal, for example comparing it with replacing printed books [1];
- an elaboration that adds something useful, such as phasing the change in one year group first [1];
- a link back to the main argument [1].

For example: "That's a fair question, and I haven't researched exact figures. What I'd point out is that schools already spend money replacing worn-out textbooks, so the real comparison is between those two costs. A sensible approach might be to trial tablets in one year group first and compare. Either way, I think the benefits for access make it worth investigating."

**9.** One mark for each labelled part, correctly used (4 × [1]). For example: (acknowledge) "That's a really important point." (concede) "Not every family has reliable internet, and a plan that ignored that would be unfair." (respond) "The tablets could download books in school, so students could read them at home without a connection." (restate) "So I still think tablets would help, as long as the scheme is designed for everyone."

**10.** Two marks for each response: an appropriate grade judgement [1] and a reason [1].
- Response A: below or at the very edge of Pass [1]; it is circular, gives no reason, and its casual tone ("They just are") hardly suits the formal setting [1].
- Response B: Merit [1]; it is formal and gives a reason with a specific example, which is "in some detail", but it does not go beyond the obvious [1].
- Response C: Distinction [1]; it questions the assumption in "better", weighs access against concentration and elaborates with new information, refining the speaker's own position perceptively [1].

**11.** Thanks or acknowledges the feedback [1]; shows understanding by accepting that the point needed support [1]; responds constructively, for example by offering the evidence now or explaining how the point would be strengthened [1].

**12.** Any three methods, one mark each (3 × [1]): knowing more than is presented and keeping reserve material; listing likely challenges and drafting first sentences of replies; practising with a partner who asks unexpected or hostile questions; preparing holding and clarification phrases.

**13.** "Perceptively" means recognising the concern, assumption or implication behind a question rather than only its surface [1]. An example question, such as "Do you really think people will give up their cars?", after a talk on public transport [1], with an explanation that a perceptive answer addresses the underlying doubt about whether people change habits, for example by discussing what would make the change easier [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- One-word answers with no reason or example.
- Losing the formal register in the question session.
- Guessing at unclear questions instead of asking for clarification.
- Responding defensively to challenges.
- Repeating the presentation instead of adding new ideas.
