## Questions

**1.** A small bakery sells sourdough loaves. State whether each event causes a movement along the demand curve or a shift of it, and give the direction. (i) The bakery cuts its own price. (ii) A rival bakery cuts its price. (iii) Local house prices rise, increasing residents' wealth. **[3]**

**2.** Explain two social or emotional influences on the demand for fashion clothing. **[4]**

**3.** In a town, a gym raises its monthly fee from 30 to 33. The number of members falls from 1,000 to 940.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** Classify demand as elastic or inelastic. **[1]**

**4.** A cafe sells 400 cups of coffee a day at 3.00 each. The price elasticity of demand is −2.2. The owner cuts the price by 5%. Calculate the new daily quantity, the new price and the percentage change in total revenue. Explain the result. **[4]**

**5.** In Country C, household income rises by 6%. Demand for a budget packaged meal falls by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the income elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(b)** What does the value tell you about the type of good? **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** The price of Brand P tablets rises by 8%. Demand for Brand Q tablets rises by 12%. Calculate the cross elasticity of demand and state the relationship between the goods. **[3]**
**(b)** The price of a games console rises by 5%. Demand for its games falls by 6%. Calculate the cross elasticity of demand and state the relationship. **[2]**

**7.** A market has the demand schedule below (invented).

| Price | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 | 14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity | 210 | 180 | 150 | 120 | 90 |

**(a)** Work out the total revenue at every price in the table. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand when price rises from 6 to 8. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand when price rises from 12 to 14. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the two values differ and identify the price that maximises revenue. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why the price elasticity of demand for one brand of breakfast cereal is likely to be higher than for breakfast cereal as a whole. **[6]**

**9.** Ferry operator R raises its fares by 4%. The cross elasticity of demand for ferry operator S's tickets with respect to R's fares is +2.1.

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage change in demand for S's tickets. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse how this information could help S decide on its own pricing and capacity. **[4]**

**10.** Country D's real incomes are forecast to grow by 3.5% next year. A firm sells 4,000 home cinema systems (income elasticity +2.2) and 9,000 own-label canned meals (income elasticity −0.8) a year.

**(a)** Calculate the forecast annual sales of each product, other things equal. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the firm should move production capacity from canned meals to home cinema systems. **[4]**

**11.** A small publisher sells a guidebook at 20 and sells 2,000 copies a month. It raises the price to 22 and sales fall to 1,900.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand and the change in total revenue. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the publisher was right to raise the price. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) Movement along the curve (an extension of quantity demanded). [1] (ii) Shift left, because the substitute has become cheaper. [1] (iii) Shift right, because wealth rises and normal goods are bought more. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A direction is needed as well as the word movement or shift. Answers tend to gain credit when each choice is tied to the cause, not just asserted.

**2.** Fashion or peer influence: people want what friends or others are wearing. [1] This raises the quantity demanded at every price, shifting the curve right, until the trend fades. [1] Status or mood: buying to signal success or to lift spirits. [1] Demand shifts right when feelings favour the purchase and left when they do not. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming a factor earns little unless it is linked to a change in demand at every price. Keep each factor to its own paragraph so the two points stay separate.

**3. (a)** % change in price = 3 ÷ 30 × 100 = +10%. [1] % change in quantity = −60 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = −6%. [1] PED = −6 ÷ 10 = **−0.6**. [1]
**(b)** The value is under 1 in size, so demand is **price inelastic**. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Set out each percentage change before dividing. Showing each step makes the method clear and lets a slip be spotted.

**4.** Quantity change = −2.2 × −5% = +11%, so new quantity = 400 × 1.11 = **444 cups**. [1] New price = 3.00 × 0.95 = **2.85**. [1] Revenue was 400 × 3.00 = 1,200 and is now 444 × 2.85 = 1,265.40, a rise of **about 5.5%**. [1] Demand is elastic, so the proportional rise in quantity outweighs the fall in price. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Working in multipliers (0.95 × 1.11) is a quick check. Adding the two percentages gives −5 + 11 = 6, which only approximates 5.5.

**5. (a)** YED = % change in quantity ÷ % change in income = −3 ÷ 6. [1] = **−0.5**. [1]
**(b)** A negative value shows an inferior good. [1] As incomes rise, households switch from it to preferred alternatives, so demand falls. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The minus sign carries the meaning. Dropping it loses the classification as well as the value.

**6. (a)** XED = +12 ÷ +8. [1] = **+1.5**. [1] Positive, so the goods are **substitutes**. [1]
**(b)** XED = −6 ÷ +5 = **−1.2**. [1] Negative, so the goods are **complements**. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Put the quantity change of the other good on top and the price change on the bottom. Reversing them gives a different, wrong value.

**7. (a)** Revenue = price × quantity: 6 × 210 = 1,260; 8 × 180 = 1,440; 10 × 150 = 1,500; 12 × 120 = 1,440; 14 × 90 = 1,260. [1] All five values correct. [1]
**(b)** % change in price = 2 ÷ 6 = +33.3%. % change in quantity = −30 ÷ 210 = −14.3%. [1] PED = **−0.43**. [1]
**(c)** % change in price = 2 ÷ 12 = +16.7%. % change in quantity = −30 ÷ 120 = −25%. [1] PED = **−1.5**. [1]
**(d)** The same absolute price rise is a large percentage of a low price but a smaller percentage of a high price, and the reverse for quantity, so a straight-line curve is inelastic at the bottom and elastic at the top. [1] Revenue is highest at **10**, where demand is unit elastic. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use the original value as the base in each calculation and say so. A comment that separates the percentage changes from the absolute changes shows understanding.

**8.** Closer substitutes: other brands of cereal are very similar, so a price rise on one brand sends buyers to rivals. [1] Quantity falls by a larger proportion than price rises, so PED is large. [1] For cereal as a whole the alternatives (toast, porridge) are fewer or less close. [1] Breadth of definition: the narrower the market, the more substitutes outside it. [1] Buyers of one brand lose little by changing, but buyers of the whole category must change what they eat. [1] So the category's demand is less responsive to its price. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Compare the brand with the whole category in every sentence. Answers that describe only the brand tend to leave the comparison unproven.

**9. (a)** % change in demand = XED × % change in R's fare. [1] = 2.1 × 4 = **+8.4%**. [1]
**(b)** S can expect more demand, so it may keep its fares, because the gain arrives without a cut. [1] A high XED means S's own customers may leave if R cuts again, so S must watch R's price. [1] If S is near capacity it could raise fares, but the effect on revenue depends on S's own PED, which this figure does not give. [1] R's rise may be temporary, so S should avoid building long-run capacity on a short-run change. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Stay with what the figure tells you and flag what it does not. Analysis tends to score when it follows a chain: higher demand, then the decision, then the limit on the decision.

**10. (a)** Cinema systems: 2.2 × 3.5 = +7.7%. [1] 4,000 × 1.077 = **4,308**. [1] Canned meals: −0.8 × 3.5 = −2.8%. [1] 9,000 × 0.972 = **8,748**. [1]
**(b)** For: sales of cinema systems grow while canned meals shrink, so capacity is better used on the growing product. [1] Against: the forecast depends on income growing as predicted and on other influences staying unchanged, which is unlikely. [1] Further, 308 extra cinema systems against 252 fewer canned meals is a small shift in volume, and costs and margins are not given. [1] Judgement: partial or gradual reallocation is wise until the forecast and margins are confirmed. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned judgement tends to gain credit, not a list of points. State what extra information would settle the decision.

**11. (a)** % change in price = +10%; % change in quantity = −5%; PED = **−0.5**. [2] Revenue was 20 × 2,000 = 40,000 and is now 22 × 1,900 = 41,800, a rise of **4.5%**. [2]
**(b)** Demand is inelastic, so revenue rose as the rule predicts. [1] Fewer copies are printed, so costs of production fall, and profit rises by more than revenue. [1] However, the fall in sales is one month's response, and in the long run readers may find alternatives such as other guides, so PED may rise. [1] A 10% rise is fairly large, so the rule is less reliable. [1] Other things may not have stayed equal, for example rival prices or the season. [1] Judgement: the rise looks right on current evidence, but the publisher should monitor sales over several months. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided answer rarely reaches the top. Credit is more likely when each evaluative point is explained and a final judgement is supported.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using absolute changes in a calculation rather than percentage changes.
- Missing the sign on YED and XED, then misreading the good.
- Writing "shift" for a price change.
- Leaving the answer as a number with no statement of what it means.
- Stating what happens to revenue without saying why in terms of elasticity.
- Describing a factor without linking it to a more or less elastic response.
- Giving a list of evaluation points with no judgement at the end.
- Forgetting that a rule based on elasticity is weaker for large changes.
