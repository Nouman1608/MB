## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Spending to buy or improve a non-current asset, including delivery, installation and other costs of getting it ready for use |
| Revenue expenditure | Day-to-day running costs and the upkeep of assets: repairs, servicing, decoration, fuel, wages |
| Capital receipt | Money received that is not trading income: owner's capital, a loan, proceeds of selling a non-current asset |
| Revenue receipt | Income from running the business: sales, rent received, commission received |
| Depreciation | Part of an asset's cost used up in a period by wear and tear, time or becoming out of date. No cash is paid |
| Net book value | Cost less accumulated depreciation (the balance on the provision for depreciation account) |
| Profit or loss on disposal | Proceeds minus net book value at the date of sale |

## 4.1 Capital and revenue

| | Where it is recorded | Where it ends up |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Debit non-current asset account | Statement of financial position, then depreciation to the statement of profit or loss |
| Revenue expenditure | Debit expense account | Statement of profit or loss |
| Capital receipt | Credit capital, loan or disposal account | Statement of financial position (only a profit or loss on disposal is taken to the statement of profit or loss) |
| Revenue receipt | Credit income account | Statement of profit or loss |

**Method in steps for any payment or receipt**

1. Ask whether it adds to or improves what the business owns, or only keeps it running or earns income.
2. Choose the account type: non-current asset, expense, capital or loan, or income.
3. Write the double entry, with the bank or supplier on the other side.

## Effect of incorrect treatment

| Mistake | Profit | Non-current assets |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital | Overstated | Overstated |
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue | Understated | Understated |
| Capital receipt credited as income | Overstated | No change if the bank was debited correctly |
| Revenue receipt credited to capital or a liability | Understated | No change |

Where depreciation was charged on the wrongly capitalised item, the overstatement is the amount **less** that depreciation. Reminder: 800 of repairs debited to machinery, depreciated at 25% on cost for a full year. Profit and assets are both overstated by 800 − 200 = 600.

## 4.2 Depreciation: why and how

**Why:** it matches an asset's cost against the revenue it helps to earn, so profit is not overstated, and it stops the statement of financial position showing the asset at more than a realistic value.

| Method | Calculation | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Straight-line | Yearly cost after deducting residual value, spread over the years of use | Premises, machinery, fixtures and fittings |
| Reducing balance | Chosen rate applied to the opening net book value each year | Motor vehicles, computer equipment |
| Revaluation | Valuation at start, plus additions, minus valuation at end | Loose tools and other small, low-cost items |

Apply the stated method, rate and policy (full year in the year of purchase, or months owned) exactly, and keep the method the same from year to year.

## Disposal in steps, with a worked reminder

1. Find accumulated depreciation on the asset sold only.
2. Net book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.
3. Compare proceeds with net book value, not with cost.
4. Post the cost, the accumulated depreciation and the proceeds to the disposal account. The balance is the profit or loss.

A vehicle costing 40,000 is depreciated at 25% reducing balance. After two years it is sold for 24,000.

```
Year 1 depreciation 25% × 40,000 = 10,000   NBV 30,000
Year 2 depreciation 25% × 30,000 =  7,500   NBV 22,500
Accumulated depreciation         = 17,500
Profit on disposal = 24,000 − 22,500 = 1,500

Disposal of vehicles account
Vehicles (cost)          40,000    Provision for depreciation   17,500
Profit or loss (profit)   1,500    Bank                         24,000
                         41,500                                 41,500
```

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cost versus net book value.** Disposal profit uses net book value; the asset account is removed at cost.
- **Asset account versus provision account.** Depreciation is credited to the provision, so the asset stays at cost.
- **Capital receipt versus revenue receipt.** Only revenue receipts are income. A sale of an asset is a capital receipt.
- **Straight-line versus reducing balance.** Straight-line is the same each year; reducing balance falls each year because the base falls.
- **Depreciation versus cash.** Depreciation is not cash set aside to replace the asset.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify each as capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, capital receipt or revenue receipt: (a) fitting a lift in a hotel; (b) insurance premium on vehicles; (c) a loan received; (d) fees received from customers.
2. A repair costing 540 was debited to buildings, which are depreciated at 10% on cost with a full year's charge. State the effect on profit and on non-current assets.
3. An asset costs 54,000, has a residual value of 4,000 and lasts 10 years. Calculate straight-line depreciation for one year.
4. An asset costing 20,000 is depreciated at 30% reducing balance. Calculate year 3 depreciation, and the net book value when year 3 ends.
5. Tools were valued at 1,420 at the start of the year, 380 of tools were bought, and they were valued at 1,150 at the end. Calculate depreciation.
6. State the journal entry for a machine bought on credit from a supplier for 12,000.
7. A press cost 22,000 and has accumulated depreciation of 13,200. It is sold for 9,500. Calculate net book value and the profit or loss.
8. For question 7, state the entry that transfers the result to the statement of profit or loss.
9. A bank loan of 8,000 was credited to sales. State the effect on profit and on asset valuations.
10. Which method suits a fleet of delivery vans, and which suits a large number of small crates? Does charging depreciation use cash?

### Answers

1. (a) Capital expenditure; (b) revenue expenditure; (c) capital receipt; (d) revenue receipt.
2. Depreciation charged was 54, so profit and non-current assets are both overstated by 540 − 54 = 486.
3. (54,000 − 4,000) ÷ 10 = 5,000.
4. Year 1: 6,000, net book value 14,000. Year 2: 4,200, net book value 9,800. Year 3: 30% × 9,800 = 2,940, net book value 6,860.
5. 1,420 + 380 − 1,150 = 650.
6. Debit machinery 12,000; credit the supplier 12,000.
7. Net book value = 22,000 − 13,200 = 8,800. Profit = 9,500 − 8,800 = 700.
8. Debit disposal account 700; credit statement of profit or loss 700.
9. Profit is overstated by 8,000. Asset valuations are not affected, because the bank was debited correctly.
10. Reducing balance for vans; revaluation for crates. No: depreciation is a book entry and uses no cash.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating delivery and installation of a new asset as expenses, or repairs as part of the asset.
- Calling the proceeds of selling an asset sales, or a loan income.
- Using cost, not net book value, to find the profit or loss on disposal.
- Using original cost, not net book value, as the base for reducing balance each year.
- Crediting depreciation to the asset account when a provision for depreciation account is used.
- Leaving the sold asset's accumulated depreciation in the provision account.
- Missing the part-year or full-year policy the question states.
- Giving the effect of an error on profit without saying whether it is overstated or understated, or without a figure.
- Forgetting to bring the balances down on the asset and provision accounts.
