## Definitions

| Term | Short definition |
|---|---|
| Lean production | Producing with less waste and greater efficiency, so each input adds value |
| Waste | Any use of materials, time or effort that adds no value for the customer |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement through many small changes made by all employees |
| Just-in-time (JIT) | Receiving materials and producing goods only as they are needed, with minimal inventory |
| Just-in-case | Holding buffer inventory to cover delays or demand spikes |
| Cradle to cradle | Design and manufacturing in which materials are reused or safely returned to nature at end of life |
| Quality control (QC) | Inspecting output and removing defective items |
| Quality assurance (QA) | Preventing defects by building quality into the process |
| Quality circle | Small group of workers who meet to solve quality problems |
| Benchmarking | Comparing performance with a best performer and closing the gap |
| Total quality management (TQM) | Business-wide approach in which all staff are responsible for quality, aiming at zero defects |
| Quality standard | Published requirements an organization can be independently checked against |

## Lean production in steps

1. Find the waste: scrap, rework, excess inventory, waiting, extra movement, overproduction.
2. Choose methods: kaizen for steady gains, JIT for inventory.
3. Involve employees, since they see the problems first.
4. Measure the result in costs, defect rate and delivery time.
5. Repeat, because the target moves.

## Kaizen versus JIT

| Point | Kaizen | JIT |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Habit of small, continuous improvement | System of timed delivery and production |
| Main aim | Better processes over time | Lower inventory and holding cost |
| Who drives it | All employees | Operations and purchasing, with suppliers |
| Main risk | Ideas ignored, enthusiasm fades | Late delivery stops production |
| Depends on | Open culture and manager response | Reliable suppliers and accurate forecasts |

## Cradle to cradle in brief

- Aim: no waste, because end-of-life materials become new inputs.
- Features: reusable or safely returnable materials, design for disassembly, renewable energy, avoiding harmful substances, whole-life thinking.
- Contrast: the linear cradle-to-grave model makes, uses and disposes.
- Pros: lower raw material use, reputation, ethical appeal.
- Cons: design and research cost, supplier cooperation, difficulty collecting used products.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Key difference |
|---|---|
| QC and QA | QC detects defects in output. QA prevents defects in the process. |
| QA and TQM | QA is a set of systems. TQM is a whole-organization culture where everyone owns quality. |
| Quality circle and kaizen | A circle is a defined group meeting on a problem. Kaizen is a continuous habit for everyone. |
| Benchmarking and market research | Benchmarking compares processes against a best performer. Market research studies customers. |
| National and international standards | A national body sets one, applying in one country. An international one, such as ISO 9001, applies across countries. |
| JIT and just-in-case | JIT holds minimal inventory. Just-in-case holds buffers. |

## Three quality methods in steps

**Quality circle:** form a small group, meet regularly, identify a problem, analyze causes, propose a solution, present it to management, review results.

**Benchmarking:** choose the process, pick a best performer (internal, competitive or functional), collect data, measure the gap, plan changes, re-measure.

**TQM:** set customer-focused quality goals, train and empower staff, involve suppliers, use continuous improvement, and measure defects against a zero-defect target.

## Impact checklist

For a 5.3 evaluation, address:

- Costs: savings versus training and set-up costs.
- Customers: fewer defects, reputation, repeat sales.
- Employees: motivation and involvement versus pressure and resistance.
- Risk: supplier dependence, little spare capacity.
- Time: costs first, benefits later.
- Context: size, product, supplier base, culture.

End with a judgement tied to the case, for example "likely worthwhile if its suppliers are reliable".

## Quality standards in brief

- Why they matter: customer trust, access to buyers who demand certification, documented processes, market entry abroad.
- Costs: fees, audits, paperwork, staff time.
- Limit: certification shows a system exists, not that every unit is perfect.

## Calculation method

1. Convert the rate to units: units × rate.
2. Multiply by the unit cost.
3. Do this before and after, then subtract.
4. For payback, divide the one-off cost by the annual saving, then say years or months.

Worked reminder: Veltrin Plastics makes 12,000 units with a 5% scrap rate and USD 8 of materials per unit. At 5%, 600 units are lost, costing 4,800. At 3%, 360 units are lost, costing 2,880. The saving is **USD 1,920**.

## Case-study skills for 5.3

Use these moves in any question set around a business you have not met before.

1. **Name the method, then link it to the case.** Do not write "JIT reduces costs". Write what in the case would be delivered later, and which supplier or product is affected.
2. **Pick the stakeholder.** Say who gains or loses: employees, customers, suppliers, owners.
3. **Use numbers if the case gives them.** A defect rate, inventory value or delivery record turns a general point into a case point.
4. **Weigh up.** Compare benefit with cost, short term with long term, and say which matters more for this business.
5. **Finish with a judgement.** One sentence that answers the question and gives the main reason.

### More worked reminders

Kaizen: a team of 25 makes 2 suggestions each a year. Half are adopted, each saving USD 100 a year. Adopted ideas: 25 × 2 × 0.5 = 25. Saving: 25 × 100 = **USD 2,500** a year.

Benchmarking: a call centre answers 70% of calls within 20 seconds, and the best performer answers 90%. The gap is **20 percentage points**. The next step is to study how the best performer rosters staff.

Standards: a firm pays USD 6,000 to be certified and wins extra annual profit of USD 3,000 because of it. Payback is 6,000 ÷ 3,000 = **2 years**. A longer-term judgement also counts customer trust that the figures do not show.

### Answers

1. Continuous improvement through many small changes, made by all employees.
2. Less waste and greater efficiency.
3. QC inspects output to detect defects. QA builds quality into the process to prevent them.
4. A late delivery can stop production, since little stock covers the gap.
5. Before: 12,000 × 0.05 × 8 = 4,800. After: 12,000 × 0.03 × 8 = 2,880. Saving **USD 1,920**.
6. Holding cost is 8,000 a year. Inventory falls by 40%, so the new holding cost is 4,800. Saving: **USD 3,200**.
7. A small group of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems.
8. Internal, competitive and functional.
9. Cradle to grave ends in disposal. Cradle to cradle designs for reuse or safe return of materials.
10. ISO 9001. Benefit: customers trust a certified supplier (other valid benefits also fit).
11. (4 − 1) ÷ 4 = 0.75, so a **75% fall**.
12. 25,000 ÷ 5,000 = 5 years, which is **60 months**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "reduce costs" for lean production without naming waste or efficiency.
- Confusing QC with QA, or calling TQM a type of inspection.
- Describing JIT only as "less stock", with no mention of supplier reliability.
- Listing benefits of a method without any drawback or judgement in an evaluation.
- Generic answers that never use the case-study business.
- Forgetting units, or giving a payback in years when the question asks for months.
- Stating a standard's name without explaining why it matters to customers or the business.
- Treating quality circles and kaizen as identical.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between kaizen and just-in-time?**
Kaizen is a habit of small, continuous improvement made by all employees, aimed at better processes over time. Just-in-time is a system of timed delivery and production aimed at lower inventory and holding costs.

**What is the difference between quality assurance and TQM?**
Quality assurance is a set of systems that builds quality into the process to prevent defects. Total quality management is a whole-organization culture in which every employee owns quality.

**What is a quality circle?**
A quality circle is a small group of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems. It is not identical to kaizen, which is a wider habit of continuous improvement across the business.

**What are the three types of benchmarking?**
The notes name internal, competitive and functional benchmarking. In each case a business compares its processes or performance with a best performer and then works to close the gap.
