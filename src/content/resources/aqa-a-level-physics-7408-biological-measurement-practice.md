These questions cover section **3.10.3 Biological measurement** of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408): simple ECG machines and the normal ECG waveform. It is part of the A-level only Medical physics option. Learn the content first in the [biological measurement study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-biological-measurement/) and the [biological measurement revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-biological-measurement-revision-notes/).

Resistance values for electrode contacts are illustrative values for practice.

## Section A

**1.** State the physical quantity that an ECG records against time. **[1]**

**2.** Name the region of the heart that acts as its natural pacemaker. **[1]**

**3.** State the electrical event that produces the T wave. **[1]**

**4.** State why a conducting gel is placed between each electrode and the skin. **[1]**

**5.** A patient's heart rate is 72 beats per minute. Calculate the distance between successive R peaks on a trace recorded at 25 mm s⁻¹. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the patient is asked to lie still and relaxed while an ECG is recorded. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A normal ECG waveform has three main features: the P wave, the QRS complex and the T wave.

**(a)** State the electrical event in the heart that produces each feature. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the QRS complex has the largest amplitude. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the trace shows no separate wave for the repolarisation of the atria. **[2]**

**8.** An ECG is recorded at a paper speed of 25 mm s⁻¹ with a vertical scale of 10 mm per mV. Successive R peaks are 18 mm apart and each R peak is 12 mm high.

**(a)** Calculate the heart rate in beats per minute. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the amplitude of the R peak in mV. **[1]**
**(c)** The amplifier output for the R peak is 1.5 V. Calculate the gain of the amplifier. **[2]**

**9.** The electrode–skin contacts have a total resistance of 40 kΩ. The heart produces a signal of 1.0 mV between the electrodes.

**(a)** Calculate the p.d. across the input of an amplifier of input resistance 8.0 MΩ. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the input of an amplifier of input resistance 200 kΩ. **[1]**
**(c)** The contact resistance changes slightly as the patient breathes. Explain why this matters more for the 200 kΩ amplifier. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how a good-quality ECG trace is obtained from a patient. Your answer should refer to the electrodes, the skin, the amplifier and the patient. **[5]**

**11.** Mains cables near the patient induce an interfering p.d. of the same size at every electrode. Explain how the type of amplifier used in an ECG machine reduces this interference. **[2]**

**12.** Describe the sequence of electrical events in one heartbeat, from the start of the beat to the end of the T wave, and link each event to the part of the ECG trace it produces. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Potential difference (voltage) between electrodes on the skin [1]

**2.** The sinoatrial (SA) node [1]

**3.** Repolarisation of the ventricles [1]

**4.** To give a low-resistance electrical contact (no air gaps) between electrode and skin [1]

**5.** Period = 60/72 = 0.833 s [1]; distance = 0.833 × 25 = **20.8 mm** [1]

**6.** Skeletal muscles produce electrical signals when they contract [1]; these would add noise to the trace and could hide or distort the heart's signal [1]

**7. (a)** P wave: depolarisation of the atria [1]; QRS complex: depolarisation of the ventricles [1]; T wave: repolarisation of the ventricles [1]
**(b)** The ventricles contain much more muscle than the atria, so more cells depolarise together [1]
**(c)** Atrial repolarisation occurs at the same time as ventricular depolarisation [1]; its small signal is hidden within the much larger QRS complex [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name the chamber in each answer to (a); "depolarisation" alone for both P and QRS scores only once.

**8. (a)** T = 18/25 = 0.72 s [1]; rate = 60/0.72 = **83 beats per minute** [1]
**(b)** 12 mm ÷ 10 mm per mV = **1.2 mV** [1]
**(c)** Gain = 1.5/(1.2 × 10⁻³) [1]; = **1250** [1]

**9. (a)** V = 1.0 × 8.0/(8.0 + 0.040) [1]; = **0.995 mV** [1]
**(b)** V = 1.0 × 200/(200 + 40) = **0.83 mV** [1]
**(c)** With the 200 kΩ input the contact resistance is a significant fraction of the total, so a change in it changes the share of the signal reaching the amplifier noticeably [1]; the trace amplitude would vary with breathing, distorting the waveform; with 8.0 MΩ the fraction stays almost exactly 1 [1]
*Examiner insight:* Treat this as a potential divider; the signal p.d. splits in proportion to the resistances.

**10.** One mark each for any five of the following points, to a maximum of [5]: electrodes placed at standard positions on the limbs and chest so traces can be compared; skin cleaned to remove grease and dead skin (and hair removed) to lower contact resistance; conducting gel between electrode and skin for good contact; amplifier with high gain because the signal is only about 1 mV; amplifier with high input impedance so most of the signal p.d. is across the amplifier; differential amplifier to reject interference common to both electrodes; patient still and relaxed to avoid signals from skeletal muscles; output displayed against time on a screen or chart recorder.

**11.** The amplifier amplifies the difference between the p.d.s at two electrodes (a differential amplifier) [1]; the interference is the same at both electrodes, so it cancels in the difference while the heart's signal, which differs between electrodes, is amplified [1]

**12.** The SA node starts the beat and depolarisation spreads across the atria, which contract: P wave [1]; the AV node delays the signal: flat section between P and QRS [1]; the signal is conducted rapidly through the ventricles, which depolarise and contract: QRS complex [1]; the ventricles repolarise and relax: T wave [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the ECG as a record of heart sounds or pressure.
- Using P to T, rather than R to R, as the period of the heartbeat.
- Forgetting to divide by the paper speed before finding the rate.
- Explaining high input impedance as "letting more current flow".
- Missing the hidden atrial repolarisation in the waveform explanation.
