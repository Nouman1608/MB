## What section 2.4 asks you to do

| Point | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| 2.4 (a) | Explain the terms capital expenditure and revenue expenditure |
| 2.4 (b) | Explain the importance of the correct treatment of capital expenditure and revenue expenditure |

Both points say "explain", so a definition alone is not enough. You need the reason an item belongs in one category and the consequence of putting it in the other.

## The two terms

**Capital expenditure** is spending on acquiring, adding to or improving non-current assets: items kept for use in the business over more than one accounting period and not bought for resale. It includes every cost needed to bring the asset into working condition at its place of use. It is debited to a non-current asset account and appears in the statement of financial position.

**Revenue expenditure** is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, including the cost of maintaining non-current assets in their existing condition. It is debited to an expense account and charged in full against income in the income statement for the period.

The key word is *benefit*. Capital expenditure benefits several future periods, so its cost is spread over them through depreciation. Revenue expenditure is used up within the period, so it is charged in that period.

## Deciding borderline cases

Use three questions:

1. **Does it create, add to or improve a non-current asset?** If yes, capital.
2. **Is it needed to get the asset ready for its first use?** If yes, capital, because it is part of the asset's cost.
3. **Does it only keep the asset running as before, or is it used up in the period?** If yes, revenue.

| Item | Capital or revenue | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Carriage on a new machine | Capital | Needed to bring it to the place of use |
| Installation of a new machine by the business's own electrician (wages for that work) | Capital | Cost of making the asset ready for use |
| Insurance on a new machine for its first year | Revenue | A running cost of the year, not a cost of acquiring it |
| Training staff to use a new machine | Revenue | Develops people, not the asset |
| Fuel and servicing for a delivery van | Revenue | Running and maintenance costs |
| Replacing a van's engine with a more powerful one that adds years to its life | Capital | Improves the asset beyond its original condition |
| Replacing worn tyres on a van | Revenue | Keeps the van in its existing condition |
| Building an extension to the shop | Capital | Adds to the asset |
| Repainting the shop | Revenue | Maintenance |

### Worked example 1: classifying a year's spending

Granton Bakery paid the following during the year.

| Payment | Amount | Category |
|---|---:|---|
| New oven, purchase price | 9,800 | Capital |
| Delivery of the oven | 240 | Capital |
| Bakery electrician's wages for installing the oven | 360 | Capital |
| Insurance on the oven, first year | 420 | Revenue |
| Training two bakers to use the oven | 300 | Revenue |
| Roof repairs | 1,150 | Revenue |
| Extension to the bakery shop | 18,000 | Capital |
| Fuel for the delivery van | 2,650 | Revenue |

The oven is recorded at 9,800 + 240 + 360 = **10,400**. Total capital expenditure is 10,400 + 18,000 = **28,400**. Total revenue expenditure is 420 + 300 + 1,150 + 2,650 = **4,520**.

The ledger entries follow the category. For the oven: debit Ovens 10,400, credit Bank 10,040 (price and delivery) and credit Wages 360, which moves the electrician's installation wages out of the expense account and into the asset. For the roof: debit Repairs 1,150, credit Bank.

## Why correct treatment matters

The specification asks you to explain the importance. Build your explanation on four points.

1. **Profit is measured correctly.** Charging capital spending as an expense understates this year's profit; capitalising an expense overstates it.
2. **Assets are stated correctly.** Non-current assets in the statement of financial position, and therefore capital (the owner's equity), depend on the classification.
3. **Later years are affected.** Depreciation follows the asset, so an error in one year distorts several.
4. **Users are not misled.** The owner may base drawings on profit; a lender judges security from assets; the tax authority assesses profit; and year-on-year comparisons rely on consistent treatment.

The accruals concept is behind all of this: costs should be matched with the periods that benefit from them.

### Worked example 2: an error followed through an asset's life

Sandford Prints buys a printer for 8,000 at the start of Year 1. It should be depreciated by the straight line method over 4 years with no residual value, so 2,000 a year. The bookkeeper debits the 8,000 to repairs in Year 1 and never records the printer as an asset.

| Year | Charged as recorded | Charge that should have been made | Effect on profit | Carrying value omitted from assets |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | 8,000 | 2,000 | Understated by 6,000 | 6,000 |
| 2 | 0 | 2,000 | Overstated by 2,000 | 4,000 |
| 3 | 0 | 2,000 | Overstated by 2,000 | 2,000 |
| 4 | 0 | 2,000 | Overstated by 2,000 | 0 |

Over the four years total profit is the same (−6,000 + 2,000 + 2,000 + 2,000 = 0), but each year's profit is wrong. Year 1 looks poor and Years 2 to 4 look better than they were. That is why the error matters even though it "evens out": every individual year's statements mislead their users.

### Worked example 3: revenue expenditure treated as capital

Harlan Motors repaints its showroom for 2,500 and debits the cost to the premises account. Premises are not depreciated by this business.

- Expenses are understated by 2,500, so profit for the year is overstated by 2,500.
- Premises in the statement of financial position are overstated by 2,500.
- Because profit is added to capital, the owner's capital is also overstated by 2,500.
- With no depreciation on premises, the overstatement of assets stays in every later statement until it is corrected.

If the owner draws extra money believing profit was higher, the business may be left short of cash.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the terms**: define each, then add how it is recorded (asset account and statement of financial position; expense account and income statement).
- **Classify**: one word is not enough when the question says "explain"; give the reason from the three questions above.
- **Explain the importance**: name the effect on profit *and* on assets, say which direction, and link to a user who would be misled.
- **Calculate the effect**: take depreciation into account. A capital item wrongly expensed understates profit by its cost less the depreciation that should have been charged.

## Common errors

- Treating everything paid at the time of purchase as capital, including first-year insurance and training.
- Calling any expensive item capital. A large repair is still revenue expenditure if it only restores the asset.
- Forgetting that a misclassification changes capital (equity) as well as profit and assets.
- Saying an error "does not matter because it cancels out over time".
- Ignoring depreciation when calculating the effect on profit.
- Writing "it affects the accounts" without saying which figure and in which direction.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the cost of installing a machine capital or revenue expenditure?**
Capital. Installation is needed to bring the machine into working condition, so it forms part of the machine's cost, even when the business's own staff do the work.

**Why is insurance on a new asset revenue expenditure?**
Insurance covers a period of time and is used up in that period. It is a running cost, not part of acquiring the asset.

**What happens to profit when capital expenditure is treated as revenue expenditure?**
Profit for that year is understated by the cost less any depreciation that should have been charged, and later years' profits are overstated because no depreciation is charged.
