## 1. Why cash matters

Cash is the money a business can spend now: the balance in the bank account and the notes and coins in the till. A business needs it for three everyday jobs.

- **Paying suppliers.** Suppliers of materials, stock or services expect payment. If a business pays late, suppliers may refuse to deliver, ask for cash up front, or stop dealing with it.
- **Paying overheads.** Rent, electricity, insurance and similar bills fall due on set dates whether or not sales have been good that week.
- **Paying employees.** Wages must be paid on the agreed day.

**Insolvency** means a business cannot pay its debts when they fall due. A business that keeps enough cash coming in, and plans for the gaps, avoids this. Many profitable small businesses still fail because the cash runs out before the money from customers arrives.

## 2. Cash and profit are different

- **Cash** is money actually received or paid out. It moves when money changes hands.
- **Profit** is sales revenue minus costs over a period. It is recorded when the sale is made or the cost is incurred, even if the money moves later.

Because customers often pay after the sale (and a business often pays for materials first), the two figures can point in different directions in the same month.

### Worked example: Pellam Joinery

Pellam Joinery agrees to fit a set of wardrobes for £9,000. In March it pays £5,400 for timber and fittings. The customer receives a bill and pays in May.

- Profit on the job = 9,000 - 5,400 = **£3,600**. The job is profitable.
- Cash in March: £5,400 goes out and nothing comes in, so the cash effect is **-£5,400**.
- Cash in May: £9,000 arrives.

For two months the firm is out of pocket by £5,400 on a job that makes £3,600 profit. If its bank balance is below £5,400 in March, it cannot buy the timber without borrowing. A customer who pays a deposit in advance does the reverse: cash improves before profit is made.

A loan received is also an inflow of cash, but it is not sales revenue and it is not profit.

## 3. Building a cash-flow forecast

A **cash-flow forecast** is a prediction, month by month, of the cash expected to come in and go out, with the bank balance it leaves. Owners use one to spot gaps early. The specification names these parts.

- **Cash inflows**: money coming in, such as sales receipts (cash sales and customers paying their bills), a loan, or the owner's capital.
- **Cash outflows**: money going out, such as materials, wages, rent, utilities and equipment.
- **Net cash flow** = cash inflows - cash outflows in a given period.
- **Opening balance** = the closing balance of the previous period.
- **Closing balance** = opening balance + net cash flow.

The first opening balance is the cash the business starts with. Every later opening balance is copied from the previous closing balance. If a closing balance is negative, the business is forecasting that it will have spent more cash than it has.

### Worked example: Dunmore Bike Repairs

Dunmore Bike Repairs starts January with £2,000 in the bank. Monthly wages are £2,400 and rent and overheads are £1,350. In March it buys a lifting stand for £3,000. It plans to take a £4,000 loan in February.

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales receipts | 6,500 | 5,800 | 7,200 | 8,100 |
| Loan received | 0 | 4,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total inflows** | 6,500 | 9,800 | 7,200 | 8,100 |
| Parts | 2,600 | 2,300 | 2,900 | 3,200 |
| Wages | 2,400 | 2,400 | 2,400 | 2,400 |
| Rent and overheads | 1,350 | 1,350 | 1,350 | 1,350 |
| Equipment | 0 | 0 | 3,000 | 0 |
| **Total outflows** | 6,350 | 6,050 | 9,650 | 6,950 |
| **Net cash flow** | 150 | 3,750 | -2,450 | 1,150 |
| **Opening balance** | 2,000 | 2,150 | 5,900 | 3,450 |
| **Closing balance** | 2,150 | 5,900 | 3,450 | 4,600 |

Check one column. March inflows are 7,200 and outflows are 2,900 + 2,400 + 1,350 + 3,000 = 9,650. Net cash flow = 7,200 - 9,650 = -2,450. Closing balance = 5,900 + (-2,450) = 3,450, and that becomes the April opening balance.

## 4. Interpreting a forecast

Calculation is only half of the topic. A good answer also says what the figures mean for the business.

- **Look at the closing balances.** Dunmore never forecasts a negative balance, so it can pay its bills each month.
- **Ask what the balance depends on.** The £4,000 loan makes the difference. Without it, the closing balances would be £2,150, £1,900, -£550 and £600. March would be a gap of £550. That is useful to know in January, because the owner could arrange an overdraft, delay the stand purchase or collect payments faster.
- **Find the cause of a dip.** March falls because of the one-off equipment purchase, not because trading is poor. Without the £3,000 stand, March net cash flow would be +£550.
- **Remember the limits.** A forecast is built from estimates. Sales may be lower, customers may pay later and costs may rise, so owners compare it with actual figures and update it.
- **Act on what you find.** Responses to a gap include short-term finance, asking customers to pay sooner, negotiating longer supplier credit, or timing large purchases for a stronger month.

## 5. Short-term sources of finance

Short-term sources cover temporary cash gaps and day-to-day running costs. The specification names two.

**Overdraft.** The bank lets the business spend more than it has in its current account, up to an agreed limit. It is flexible: the business only borrows what it needs, when it needs it, and interest is charged on the amount overdrawn. Interest rates can be high and the bank may reduce the limit. It suits a gap such as Dunmore's March dip, not something that takes years to pay back.

**Trade credit.** A supplier delivers goods now and lets the business pay later, for example after 30 days. There is no interest in the usual arrangement, so the supplier is in effect lending the money. It helps cash flow because stock can be sold before it is paid for. The drawbacks: a new business without a track record may not be offered credit, the supplier may offer a discount for early payment that is lost, and late payment can damage the relationship.

## 6. Long-term sources of finance

Long-term sources fund start-up costs, equipment, premises and growth. The specification names six.

- **Personal savings.** The owner puts in their own money. There is no interest and no outsiders to answer to, but the amount is limited and the owner can lose it if the business fails.
- **Venture capital.** An investor provides a sizeable sum in return for a share in the business and often a say in decisions. It can give a growing firm large funds without monthly repayments, but the owner gives up part of the ownership and some control, and a share of future profit.
- **Share capital.** A company raises money by selling shares. Shareholders become part-owners. The money does not have to be repaid, but profits are shared through dividends and ownership is spread out.
- **Loans.** A lender provides a fixed sum, repaid with interest over an agreed time. Loans give certainty and keep ownership intact. The business must make the repayments even in a poor month, and lenders may ask for some form of security.
- **Retained profit.** Profit kept in the business rather than paid out to the owners. It has no interest and no outsiders, but it exists only if the business has been profitable and keeping it means less for the owners.
- **Crowd funding.** Many people each give a small amount, usually through an online platform, in return for a reward, a product or a stake. It can test demand and raise money without a bank, but a business must attract enough backers, and the project may need a strong pitch and public exposure of the idea. Terms vary between platforms.

### Worked example: loan interest

The specification gives interest on loans in % as (total repayment - borrowed amount) / borrowed amount × 100.

Dunmore's owner considers borrowing £15,000 and repaying £17,400 in total. Interest = 17,400 - 15,000 = £2,400. As a percentage: 2,400 / 15,000 × 100 = **16%**.

## 7. Choosing between sources

A start-up has no trading history and no retained profit. It usually relies on personal savings, a loan, share capital, venture capital or crowd funding, and may find trade credit harder to obtain. An established small business can also use retained profit, and its record often makes lenders and suppliers more willing.

Match the source to the need. A short gap in cash suits an overdraft or trade credit. A van or machine suits a loan or retained profit. Large growth, where the owner will share ownership, suits venture capital or share capital. A small sum to test an idea with a ready audience suits crowd funding.

### Worked example: Hollin Garden Design

Hollin Garden Design needs £20,000 for a van. Option A is a loan: repay £23,200 over 48 months, which is £483.33 a month and 16% interest in total ((23,200 - 20,000) / 20,000 × 100). Option B is crowd funding, with pledges of £14,500 so far against a £20,000 target.

A justified recommendation weighs both sides. The loan is certain and keeps ownership, but costs £3,200 extra and the payment must come from cash flow. Crowd funding has no interest, but pledges are £5,500 short of the target. Unless Hollin can close that gap, the loan is the safer route. The conclusion would change if cash flow could not support the monthly payment.

## Common errors

- Treating profit as if it were cash in the bank.
- Counting a loan as sales revenue or profit.
- Forgetting that the closing balance becomes the next opening balance.
- Adding outflows to inflows when finding net cash flow.
- Naming a source without saying why it suits this business.
- Calling an overdraft a long-term source, or retained profit a short-term one.
- Saying a negative closing balance means a loss. It means cash is short.
