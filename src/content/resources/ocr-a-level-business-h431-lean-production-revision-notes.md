## What the specification lists

- Meaning of lean production.
- How and why lean production is used by a business.
- Evaluate the impact and importance of lean production to a business and its stakeholders.
- Recommend and justify methods of lean production for a business.

## Core recall

### Definition

- **Lean production:** using fewer resources to make the same or more output by cutting waste at every stage while keeping quality.
- **Waste:** anything that uses time, money, space or effort without adding value the customer would pay for.
- Focus on **flow** and **value**.

### Kinds of waste (a common classification; not listed in the specification)

| Waste | Example |
|---|---|
| Overproduction | Making more than ordered |
| Waiting | Idle workers waiting for materials |
| Transport | Moving materials further than needed |
| Over-processing | Finishing beyond what the customer wants |
| Excess stock | Cash and space tied up |
| Unnecessary movement | Walking and reaching |
| Defects | Rework and scrap |

### Methods

| Method | What it is | Benefit | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| JIT | Materials arrive as needed; goods leave soon after being made | Less stock, space and waste; cash freed | No buffer; needs reliable suppliers |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement through small suggestions by employees | Many small savings; motivation | Needs training and management action; slow |
| Cell production | Small teams complete a whole part of the process; machines close together | Less movement and waiting; multi-skilled staff | Layout change and training cost |
| Time-based management | Cut the time from order to delivery or from idea to launch | Fast response to customers | Quality can suffer if speed dominates |

### Why businesses use it

- Lower costs and higher profit.
- Better quality and fewer defects.
- Quicker response to demand.
- Cash freed from stock.
- Staff involvement.
- Competitive pressure on price and delivery.

### Stakeholder impact

| Stakeholder | Gain | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Profit, cash | Cost of change; stoppages |
| Employees | Skills, involvement | Fear of redundancy; pressure |
| Customers | Quality, speed | Less choice if stock is low |
| Suppliers | Long-term partners | Tight deliveries and quality rules |
| Community | Less waste and traffic | Job losses |

### Recommending methods: matching problem to method

- Large stocks: JIT or kanban, if suppliers are reliable.
- Many defects: kaizen and quality at each stage.
- Scattered process: cell production.
- Slow response: time-based management.
- Unreliable suppliers: start with kaizen, not JIT.

## Worked reminder

Greaves Joinery (invented): 20,000 units a year, material £12 a unit.

- Scrap falls from 6% to 3%: 600 units × £12 = **£7,200** saved.
- Stock falls from £90,000 to £55,000 at 20% holding cost: £35,000 × 20% = **£7,000**.
- 3 minutes saved per unit: 60,000 minutes = 1,000 hours × £15 = **£15,000**.
- Total saving £29,200 a year; cost £35,000; payback = 35,000 ÷ 29,200 = **1.2 years** (about 14 months).
- If scrap falls only to 4.5%, the scrap saving is £3,600 and the payback is longer.
- The labour saving is real only if hours are cut or the extra output can be sold.

## Evaluation checklist

1. Savings against the cost of change (use payback).
2. Risk from low stock and supplier failure.
3. Staff involvement and attitude of managers.
4. Fit with the product and the size of the business.
5. Time to see the benefit.
6. Judgement on method, order and conditions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Lean production | Cutting waste while keeping quality |
| Waste | Activity that uses resources without adding value |
| JIT | Receiving materials only when they are needed |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement by small changes |
| Cell production | Teams that complete a whole part of a process |
| Time-based management | Using speed as a source of competitive advantage |
| Kanban | Card system that signals replacement of used stock |
| Payback | Time taken to recover the cost of an investment |

## Exam traps

- Lean does not mean redundancy; it means removing waste.
- JIT is one method, not the whole of lean production.
- Explain how each method cuts waste, not just what it is called.
- Check supplier reliability before recommending JIT.
- Count a labour-time saving as cash only if hours are actually reduced.
- Use the figures in the case and state the payback period.

## Self-test

1. Define lean production.
2. Name three kinds of waste.
3. Explain what kaizen means.
4. Why does JIT need reliable suppliers?
5. How does cell production reduce waste?
6. A firm scraps 8% of 10,000 units, costing £5 each in materials. It cuts scrap to 5%. Calculate the annual saving.
7. A lean change costs £6,000 and saves £4,000 a year. Calculate the payback period in months.
8. Give two benefits of lean production to employees.
9. Give one reason an employee might oppose lean production.
10. What is time-based management?

### Answers

1. An approach that uses fewer resources to produce the same or more output by cutting waste at every stage while keeping quality.
2. Any three: overproduction; waiting; transport; over-processing; excess stock; unnecessary movement; defects.
3. Continuous improvement through small changes suggested by employees.
4. There is no buffer stock, so a late or faulty delivery stops production.
5. Machines and workers are grouped in the order of the process, so there is less movement, waiting and stock between stages.
6. Scrap falls by 3% of 10,000 = 300 units × £5 = **£1,500 a year**.
7. £6,000 ÷ £4,000 = 1.5 years = **18 months**.
8. Any two: wider skills; more say in how work is done; job satisfaction; teamwork.
9. Fear that improvements will lead to job losses, or pressure to improve every year.
10. Treating time as a source of competitive advantage by cutting the time between order and delivery or between idea and launch.
