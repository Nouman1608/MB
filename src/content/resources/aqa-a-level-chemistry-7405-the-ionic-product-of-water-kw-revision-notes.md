These notes condense section **3.1.12.3 The ionic product of water, Kw** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Ionic product of water, Kw study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-the-ionic-product-of-water-kw/), and exam-style questions are in the [Ionic product of water, Kw practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-the-ionic-product-of-water-kw-practice/). The pH definition is in the [Definition and determination of pH study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-definition-and-determination-of-ph/).

## What you must be able to do

- Write the **dissociation of water** and derive **Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]**.
- State that **Kw varies with temperature**.
- Use Kw to calculate the **pH of a strong base** from its concentration.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ionic product of water, Kw | [H⁺][OH⁻] for aqueous solutions, at a given temperature |
| Units of Kw | mol² dm⁻⁶ |
| Value at 298 K | 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ |
| Neutral | [H⁺] = [OH⁻] (at any temperature) |
| Strong base | Fully dissociated in water, for example NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂ |
| pOH | –log₁₀[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 only at 298 K |

## Derivation

```
H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻
Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]
[H₂O] is effectively constant (about 55.5 mol dm⁻³)
Kw = Kc × [H₂O] = [H⁺][OH⁻]
```

In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √Kw. At 298 K that is 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, so pH = 7.00.

Adding acid or alkali changes [H⁺] and [OH⁻], but the product stays equal to Kw at that temperature.

## pH of a strong base: four-step method

1. [OH⁻] = concentration of base × number of OH⁻ per formula unit.
2. [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻].
3. pH = –log₁₀[H⁺].
4. Check: a base should give a pH above the neutral value.

| Base | [OH⁻] for 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ | pH at 298 K |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH | 0.0100 | 12.00 |
| Ba(OH)₂ | 0.0200 | 12.30 |
| NaOH (0.100 mol dm⁻³) | 0.100 | 13.00 |

Worked example: 0.0250 mol dm⁻³ Ba(OH)₂.

```
[OH⁻] = 2 × 0.0250 = 0.0500
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.0500 = 2.00 × 10⁻¹³
pH = 12.70
```

From pH to concentration: [H⁺] = 10^(–pH), then [OH⁻] = Kw ÷ [H⁺]. For a monohydroxide base the concentration equals [OH⁻]; for Ba(OH)₂ halve it.

## Temperature and Kw

Ionisation of water is **endothermic**. Raising the temperature moves the equilibrium to the right (Le Chatelier), so Kw increases.

| Temperature / °C | Kw / mol² dm⁻⁶ | pH of pure water |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ | 7.00 |
| 40 | 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴ | 6.77 |
| 50 | 5.48 × 10⁻¹⁴ | 6.63 |

At higher temperature pure water has a lower pH but is still **neutral**, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

A strong base: use the Kw for the right temperature. 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is pH 13.00 at 298 K but pH 12.53 at 40 °C.

## Mixtures of acid and alkali

1. Work out moles of H⁺ and OH⁻.
2. Subtract to find which is in excess.
3. Divide by the **total volume**.
4. If H⁺ is in excess, pH = –log[H⁺]. If OH⁻ is in excess, use Kw to find [H⁺].

## Worked examples to practise

**Concentration of OH⁻ in an acid.** For 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 298 K, [H⁺] = 0.0100, so [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.0100 = 1.00 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³. Even acid solutions contain OH⁻ ions.

**Dilution of a base.** 10.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is made up to 1000 cm³. [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻³, [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹¹, pH = 11.00.

**Excess alkali.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ NaOH mixed with 10.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl: n(OH⁻) = 3.75 × 10⁻³ mol, n(H⁺) = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol, excess = 2.75 × 10⁻³ mol in 0.0350 dm³, [OH⁻] = 0.0786, [H⁺] = 1.27 × 10⁻¹³, pH = 12.90.

**Different temperature.** 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 40 °C (Kw = 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴): [H⁺] = 2.92 × 10⁻¹³, pH = 12.53, not 13.00.

## Checking an answer is sensible

- A strong base at 0.01 to 1 mol dm⁻³ should give a pH of about 12 to 14 at 298 K.
- A tenfold dilution of a strong base lowers the pH by 1.
- Doubling [OH⁻] raises the pH by about 0.30.
- If your pH for a base is below 7 at 298 K, you have probably used [OH⁻] in the pH formula.

## Exam traps

- Using [OH⁻] in the pH formula directly.
- Not doubling [OH⁻] for Ba(OH)₂ or Ca(OH)₂.
- Using pH + pOH = 14 at a temperature other than 298 K.
- Writing the units of Kw as mol dm⁻³.
- Saying hot water is acidic, when it is neutral with a lower pH.
- Including [H₂O] in the Kw expression.
- Saying Kw changes when acid is added.
- Using the volume of one solution rather than the total after mixing.

## Self-test

1. Write the expression for Kw.
2. Give the units of Kw.
3. Why is [H₂O] not in the Kw expression?
4. Calculate the pH of 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 298 K.
5. Calculate the pH of 0.0050 mol dm⁻³ Ba(OH)₂ at 298 K.
6. Why does Kw increase with temperature?
7. State the pH of pure water at 50 °C (Kw = 5.48 × 10⁻¹⁴) to two decimal places.
8. Is water at 50 °C acidic, neutral or alkaline?
9. What is [OH⁻] in a solution of pH 11.00 at 298 K?
10. What happens to Kw when NaOH is added to water at constant temperature?
11. Why can pH + pOH = 14 not be used at 40 °C?
12. A solution has pH 13.00 at 298 K. What is the concentration of KOH?

### Answers

1. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻].
2. mol² dm⁻⁶.
3. Water is in huge excess and its concentration is effectively constant, so it is part of Kw.
4. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹²; pH = 12.00.
5. [OH⁻] = 0.0100, [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹², pH = 12.00.
6. The ionisation of water is endothermic, so the equilibrium moves right on heating.
7. √(5.48 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 2.34 × 10⁻⁷, so pH = 6.63.
8. Neutral, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
9. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹¹, so [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³.
10. Nothing; Kw only changes with temperature.
11. It only holds when Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴, which is true only at 298 K.
12. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹³, so [OH⁻] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³ and the KOH concentration is 0.100 mol dm⁻³.
