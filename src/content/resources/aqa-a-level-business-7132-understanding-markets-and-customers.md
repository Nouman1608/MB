This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.2 Understanding markets and customers** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **the value of primary and secondary marketing research**, using qualitative and quantitative data, including calculating market and sales growth, market share and market size
- **the value of sampling**: random, stratified and quota
- **the interpretation of marketing data**: positive and negative correlation and the strength of a relationship, confidence intervals and extrapolation
- **the interpretation of price and income elasticity of demand data**, and their impact on revenue (you do not need to calculate them)
- **the value of elasticity to marketing decision makers**, and **the use of data in marketing decision making and planning**.

The wider [marketing management study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-marketing-management/) covers the whole topic. This page gives the detail and the working. Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-understanding-markets-and-customers-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-understanding-markets-and-customers-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why businesses research markets

Marketing research reduces the risk of decisions by showing who the customers are, what they want, how much they will pay and what rivals are doing. It cannot remove risk, because customers' answers do not always match what they later do, but it improves the odds.

### Primary and secondary research

| | Primary research | Secondary research |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | New data collected first-hand for this purpose | Data that already exists, collected by others |
| Methods | Surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, trials | Government statistics, trade reports, competitors' accounts, the firm's own sales records, websites |
| Strengths | Specific to the question; up to date; the firm owns it | Quicker and cheaper; shows wide trends |
| Weaknesses | Slow and expensive; sample may be biased | May be out of date; not designed for this question; rivals can see it too |

Most businesses start with secondary research to understand the market, then use primary research to fill gaps.

### Qualitative and quantitative data

**Quantitative data** is numerical: how many, how much, how often. It shows patterns and can be analysed statistically. **Qualitative data** explains opinions, feelings and reasons: why customers choose a brand, what they dislike about the packaging. Good research uses both. A survey may show that 40% of customers would not recommend a product (quantitative), and a focus group may reveal that the reason is a confusing instruction leaflet (qualitative).

### Calculations with market data

The specification expects you to calculate market size, market and sales growth and market share. The formulas are in the [setting marketing objectives guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-marketing-objectives/). Remember that market share must be calculated on a matching basis, volume with volume or value with value.

## Sampling

Researchers rarely ask every customer, so they take a **sample**. The sample must represent the whole group (the **population**) or the findings will mislead.

| Method | How it works | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, for example by computer selection from a customer list | Free from the researcher's bias; simple to explain | Needs a full list; the sample may by chance miss small groups |
| Stratified | The population is divided into groups (strata) such as age bands, and each group is sampled in proportion to its size | Guarantees every group is represented in the right proportion | Needs information about the population to form the groups; more complex |
| Quota | The interviewer is told to find a set number of people of each type, but chooses them personally | Quick and cheap; no sampling list needed | Interviewers may pick whoever is easiest, so bias is possible; not random |

### Worked example: stratified sample

A gym has 5,000 members: 2,000 aged 18 to 34, 1,800 aged 35 to 54 and 1,200 aged 55 or over. It wants a sample of 250.

- Aged 18 to 34: 2,000 ÷ 5,000 × 250 = **100**
- Aged 35 to 54: 1,800 ÷ 5,000 × 250 = **90**
- Aged 55 or over: 1,200 ÷ 5,000 × 250 = **60**

Check: 100 + 90 + 60 = 250. Members in each group are then chosen at random from that group.

**Larger samples** give more reliable results but cost more. A sample that is large but biased (for example, only people who answer an online poll) can still be misleading.

## Interpreting marketing data

### Correlation

**Correlation** is a relationship between two sets of data, often shown on a scatter graph.

- **Positive correlation**: as one rises, the other rises (advertising spend and sales).
- **Negative correlation**: as one rises, the other falls (price of a product and quantity demanded).
- **No correlation**: no pattern.
- **Strength**: the closer the points lie to a straight line, the stronger the relationship. Points widely scattered around a rising line show a weak positive correlation.

Correlation is not proof of cause. Ice cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer, but one does not cause the other: hot weather causes both. A business should ask whether other factors, such as a new competitor or the season, could explain a change.

### Confidence intervals

A survey of a sample gives an estimate for the whole population, and the estimate is uncertain. A **confidence interval** is the range within which the true value is likely to lie, with a stated level of confidence, often 95%. It is usually written as an estimate plus or minus a margin.

*Example.* A survey of 400 of a café chain's customers finds that 60% would buy a new vegan pastry, with a margin of ±5 percentage points at 95% confidence. The true proportion in the population is likely to be between 55% and 65%. If the chain has 20,000 regular customers, demand is likely to come from 11,000 to 13,000 of them (55% × 20,000 and 65% × 20,000).

Points to know:

- A **larger sample** gives a **narrower** interval, meaning a more precise estimate.
- A **wider interval** means more uncertainty, so the business should be more cautious.
- The interval says nothing about whether customers will really behave as they said they would.

### Extrapolation

**Extrapolation** extends a trend beyond the data you have in order to forecast the future.

*Example.* A company's monthly sales (£000) over six months were 40, 43, 45, 49, 52 and 54. Sales rose by 14 in 5 months, an average of £2.8k a month. If the trend continues, month 9 sales are forecast at 54 + 3 × 2.8 = **£62.4k**.

Extrapolation is quick and useful for planning, but it assumes that conditions stay the same. It becomes less reliable the further ahead it goes, and it cannot predict a competitor's launch, a change in fashion or a recession.

## Price and income elasticity of demand

You must interpret elasticity figures and say what they mean for revenue. You do not need to calculate them.

### Price elasticity of demand (PED)

PED measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in price. Ignoring the minus sign, we describe it as follows:

| PED value | Description | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity changes by a bigger percentage than the price |
| Less than 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than the price |
| Exactly 1 | Unit elastic | Quantity and price change by the same percentage |

A PED of −1.5 means that a 10% price rise leads to about a 15% fall in quantity demanded.

**Effect on revenue (price × quantity):**

- If demand is **elastic**, a price rise **reduces** revenue and a price cut **raises** it.
- If demand is **inelastic**, a price rise **raises** revenue and a price cut **reduces** it.

*Worked example.* A business sells 1,000 units at £20 (revenue £20,000).

- With PED −1.5: price up 10% to £22; quantity falls 15% to 850; revenue = £22 × 850 = **£18,700**, a fall.
- With PED −0.5: price up 10% to £22; quantity falls 5% to 950; revenue = £22 × 950 = **£20,900**, a rise.

Demand tends to be more inelastic when there are few substitutes, the product is a necessity, the brand is strongly loved, or the item is a small part of income. It tends to be more elastic when close substitutes exist or the purchase is easy to delay.

### Income elasticity of demand (YED)

YED measures how responsive demand is to a change in consumers' incomes.

| YED value | Type of good | Effect of a rise in income |
|---|---|---|
| Positive and greater than 1 | Luxury (income elastic) | Demand rises by a bigger percentage |
| Positive and less than 1 | Normal necessity | Demand rises by a smaller percentage |
| Negative | Inferior good | Demand falls |

*Example.* A YED of +2 means a 5% rise in incomes increases demand by about 10%. A YED of −0.4 means a 5% rise in incomes reduces demand by about 2%, as customers trade up to better brands.

## The value of elasticity to decision makers

- **Pricing**: knowing demand is inelastic supports a price rise; elastic demand warns that a rise may lose revenue.
- **Promotion**: if demand is elastic, discounts may work. If it is inelastic, spending on branding may be better, since it can make demand less price-sensitive.
- **Planning**: with a high positive YED, a business should expect sales to fall in a recession and can plan stock and staff; an inferior good may do relatively well in a downturn.
- **Product range**: a business may add budget lines if its goods are income elastic.

Limits: elasticity estimates come from past data and may not hold when conditions change, rivals' reactions are hard to predict, and other factors change at the same time.

## Using data in decisions and planning

Data helps a business to spot trends, choose target segments, set prices, forecast sales and judge campaigns. Technology has made this faster: loyalty cards, website analytics and social media listening supply large amounts of customer data. The risks are drawing firm conclusions from small or biased samples, confusing correlation with cause, and paying too little attention to ethical limits on how personal data is collected and used. Data should support managers' judgement, not replace it.

## Technique for exam questions

- **Calculate**: formula, working, units.
- **Analyse**: link the figure to a consequence, for example "demand is inelastic, so a 10% price rise should increase revenue".
- **Evaluate**: comment on reliability (sample size, bias, age of data) and on what else the decision depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a quota sample as random.
- Reading a strong correlation as proof of cause.
- Saying a wide confidence interval is "more accurate".
- Forgetting that elastic demand means a price rise cuts revenue.
- Confusing PED with YED.
- Using extrapolation for a long forecast without comment on its limits.
