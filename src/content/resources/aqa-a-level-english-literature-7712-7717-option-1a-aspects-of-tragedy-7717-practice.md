## Section A: the genre and the option

**1.** State the three types of text studied for Option 1A and the date requirement that applies to
them. **[3]**

**2.** AQA describes the villain as "partly responsible for the hero's demise". Explain why this
wording matters when writing an essay on a tragic villain. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between classical and domestic tragedy as AQA defines it, naming the
example AQA gives for each. **[4]**

**4.** Explain why AQA includes "humour and moments of happiness" among the aspects of tragedy.
**[2]**

**5.** Name the assessment objectives that reward connections between texts and different
interpretations, and explain how one of them could be met in a Section C answer. **[3]**

## Section B: original extracts and essay skills

**Extract 1 (original verse).** An ageing king has just announced that he will give his crown to his
younger son.

> **KING:** There. It is done, and I am lighter for it.
> Take the seal, boy; it has worn a groove in me.
> **ELDER SON:** You promised it to me before the court.
> **KING:** I promised many things. A king may change.
> **ELDER SON:** A king may. Can a father?
> **KING:** *(laughing)* Hear him, lords!
> He argues like a lawyer, not a prince.
> *(The lords laugh. The ELDER SON does not.)*
> Tomorrow I shall hunt, and sleep at noon,
> And let the kingdom carry its own weight.

**6.** Analyse how Extract 1 presents the king's blindness at the start of a tragic fall. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how Extract 1 sets up a future contest of power. **[4]**

**Extract 2 (original prose).** A young woman waits at a railway station for a man who has promised
to marry her.

> The six o'clock train came and went, and then the seven. Ellen sat on the bench with her box at
> her feet, the good shawl folded on top so that it would not crease. The porter had stopped
> asking. A boy selling apples offered her one for nothing, and she refused it, because a woman who
> accepted gifts from strangers on a railway platform might be thought anything at all. By the
> time the last train had gone the lamps were being lit, one by one, down the line, as if the
> station were preparing for a celebration to which she had not been invited.

**8.** Analyse how Extract 2 presents an ordinary person as a tragic victim. **[6]**

**9.** Explain one way in which Extract 1 and Extract 2 could be connected in a Section C-style
argument, and one important difference between them. **[4]**

**10.** A student writes: "Othello has hamartia, which is jealousy, and this causes his downfall."
Explain two ways to turn this into a stronger, debated point. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer on your own two non-Shakespeare texts for the question: "Tragedy
shows that suffering is caused by society rather than by individuals." List five elements your
plan should contain. **[5]**

**12.** Explain how a student could use an alternative interpretation (AO5) without simply naming a
critic. Give an example applied to any one set text. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** One Shakespeare play [1], a second drama text [1] and one further text, with at least one of
the two non-Shakespeare texts written before 1900 [1].

**2.** "Partly" means the villain shares responsibility with the hero [1], so a strong essay must
also weigh the hero's own flaws, choices and blindness rather than blaming the villain alone [1].

**3.** Classical tragedy is about public figures of high rank whose fall affects a state [1]; AQA's
example is Lear [1]. Domestic tragedy represents ordinary people in private life [1]; AQA's example
is Tess [1].

**4.** Moments of happiness and humour show what the characters stand to lose, so the tragedy is
heightened by contrast [1]; they also test how far a text is wholly tragic, which invites debate
about genre [1].

**5.** AO4 for connections across texts [1] and AO5 for different interpretations [1]. Explanation,
either [1]: AO4 is met by organising the answer around shared or contrasting aspects of tragedy in
both texts; AO5 is met by weighing more than one reading of the question's view across both texts.

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- "I am lighter for it" shows the king thinks giving up power removes his burden, not his identity.
- The seal that "has worn a groove in me" makes kingship physical; he does not see that it has
  shaped who he is.
- "Boy" diminishes the son who receives the crown, suggesting the king still expects to command.
- "A king may change" reveals a belief that his word is above obligation, an early sign of pride.
- His laughter and "Hear him, lords!" turn a serious objection into entertainment, refusing
  insight.
- The stage direction separating the laughing lords from the unsmiling son warns the audience of
  danger the king cannot see.
- His plan to "hunt, and sleep at noon" imagines retirement as pleasure, a moment of happiness that
  the tragic structure is likely to destroy.
- "Let the kingdom carry its own weight" dramatically ironises his confidence: kingdoms in tragedy
  do not order themselves.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the elder son's claim that the crown was "promised" before the court
establishes a grievance; his retort "Can a father?" shifts the dispute from politics to family,
deepening the conflict; the shared verse lines show the son matching the king's speech, suggesting
equal will; his silence while others laugh marks him as outside the court's mood and a likely
opponent; the king's public mockery humiliates him, giving him motive.

**8.** Any six, [1] each:

- The repetition of trains that "came and went" builds a slow structure of waiting and
  disappointment.
- The box and folded "good shawl" are small domestic details that make her hopes concrete and
  ordinary.
- "The porter had stopped asking" shows others giving up before she does, isolating her.
- Her refusal of the free apple shows how social rules about respectability control her even when
  alone.
- The phrase "might be thought anything at all" exposes the judgement society would pass on her,
  making society a kind of opponent.
- The lamps lit "one by one" mark time passing and the end of hope.
- The final simile of a celebration "to which she had not been invited" turns the setting into an
  image of exclusion, inviting pity.
- The narrator's restraint lets the reader understand the betrayal without it being stated.

**9.** Connection [2]: both extracts show a promise broken or about to be broken, so trust is the
seed of the tragedy [1]; in both, an audience or reader sees danger that the central character
does not fully grasp [1]. Difference [2]: Extract 1 is a public tragedy in which a king's choice
will affect a kingdom, whereas Extract 2 is domestic and its suffering is private [1]; the king
causes his own danger, while Ellen is a victim of another's betrayal and of social codes [1].

**10.** Two improvements, each [2] for a developed method or [1] for a partial one: test the label
by asking whether jealousy is Othello's own flaw or something Iago creates, weighing the "contest
of power" against his insecurity; consider other causes AQA lists, such as the villain, his
outsider status in Venice, or his trust in "honest" Iago; analyse how Shakespeare presents the
jealousy through language (for example, the collapse of his verse in the temptation scene) rather
than asserting it; consider recognition in the final scene and whether he understands his flaw.

**11.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on "rather than"; the aspects to test (the
villain, fate, social codes, the protagonist's choices); evidence from each text organised by
point of connection; a point where individual choice clearly matters, to complicate the view;
relevant contexts for each text tied to social pressure; an alternative interpretation weighed; a
conclusion that compares how far each text supports the view.

**12.** Present an interpretation as a reading the text supports, then test it with evidence [1];
show why the text allows more than one reading [1]. Example [1]: in *The Great Gatsby*, a reader
could see Gatsby as a romantic idealist destroyed by a careless class, or as a self-deceiving
criminal whose dream was always false; Nick's narration supports both, so the essay can weigh them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling conventions instead of debating them.
- Neglecting the hero's responsibility when a villain is present.
- Writing Section C as two separate essays.
- Interpretations reduced to a critic's name with no argument.

For full practice, choose a passage from your Shakespeare play and write all three 25-mark answers
under timed, closed-book conditions, about 50 minutes each.
