## Measuring development

**Development** is improvement in people's economic, social and political well-being, not only
growth in income.

| Measure | Strength / limit |
|---|---|
| GNI per capita | Simple, but ignores distribution and non-monetary well-being |
| Human Development Index (HDI) | Combines life expectancy, knowledge (years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (income); dimensions combined with a geometric mean; hides inequality, environmental cost and gender gaps unless adjusted |
| Gini coefficient | Shows how evenly income is shared within a country |
| Other approaches | Gender equality, multidimensional poverty, happiness or well-being |

A country can rise on average income while inequality widens.

More measures to know:

| Measure | What it adds |
|---|---|
| GNI per capita at purchasing power parity (PPP) | Adjusts income for what money buys locally |
| Inequality-adjusted HDI | Lowers the HDI according to how unequally each dimension is shared |
| Gender Inequality Index | Reproductive health, empowerment and labour-market gaps between women and men |
| Multidimensional Poverty Index | Counts overlapping deprivations in health, education and living standards at household level |
| Gross National Happiness | Bhutan's approach, weighting well-being, culture and environment alongside income |

**Gini coefficient:** 0 means perfect equality and 1 means one person has all the income. South
Africa has one of the highest recorded values; Nordic countries are among the lowest.

## HDI method in outline

Each dimension is converted to an index between 0 and 1 using fixed minimum and maximum values,
and the three indices are combined with a geometric mean. A single figure says nothing about which
regions or groups sit far below it.

The geometric mean is used so that a very low score in one dimension cannot be fully offset by a
high score in another.

## Development goals

The **Millennium Development Goals** (2000 to 2015) were followed by the **Sustainable Development
Goals** (17 goals, adopted in 2015 for 2030). Progress has been uneven: extreme poverty fell most
sharply in East Asia, particularly China, while progress in much of sub-Saharan Africa has been
slower, and the COVID-19 pandemic set back several goals.

## Routes to development, and their costs

| Route | Cost or risk |
|---|---|
| Trade | Exposure to price changes and competition |
| Foreign direct investment | Jobs, but profit outflows |
| Aid | Can help, but may create dependence |
| Remittances | Support households, but may signal skilled emigration |
| Education and skills | Benefit depends on jobs being available |
| Technology transfer | Depends on local capacity |
| Governance | Quality of institutions shapes every other route |

**Spatial inequality:** opportunity is often concentrated in cities and coastal regions. Gender,
ethnicity and age also affect who can take opportunities.

## Examples of each route

- **Trade-led growth:** Bangladesh built a large export garment industry that employs millions,
  many of them women. The Rana Plaza factory collapse near Dhaka in 2013, which killed more than
  1,100 workers, exposed the safety costs and led to a building-safety accord between brands and
  unions.
- **Foreign direct investment:** China's Special Economic Zones, starting with Shenzhen in 1980,
  attracted foreign firms with tax breaks and infrastructure, transforming a small town into a major
  city. Gains were concentrated on the coast, widening inequality with inland provinces.
- **Aid:** bilateral (government to government), multilateral (through bodies such as the World
  Bank), NGO, and emergency versus long-term development aid. **Tied aid**, which must be spent on
  the donor's goods or services, is criticised as serving donor interests.
- **Debt relief:** the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative (1996) and the Multilateral Debt
  Relief Initiative (2005) cancelled debts for many low-income countries, freeing money for
  services, but conditions attached were criticised.
- **Microfinance:** the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, founded by Muhammad Yunus (Nobel Peace Prize
  2006), lends small sums mainly to women. Critics point to high interest rates and over-indebtedness
  in some markets.
- **Remittances:** in some countries, such as Nepal and Tajikistan, money sent home by migrant
  workers is equivalent to a large share of GDP. It funds food, schooling and housing, but depends
  on economic conditions in host countries.

## Worked example: an income-share ratio (invented figures)

The poorest 20 per cent of households receive 4 per cent of national income; the richest 20 per
cent receive 50 per cent.

- Ratio of top to bottom quintile = 50 / 4 = 12.5.
- Meaning: the average household in the richest fifth has 12.5 times the income of one in the
  poorest fifth.
- If average GNI per capita rose by 20 per cent while the bottom share fell to 3 per cent, the
  ratio would become 50 / 3, about 16.7: growth with widening inequality.

## Model paragraph (evaluate)

*"Shenzhen shows that foreign direct investment can drive extremely rapid development: manufacturing
jobs raised incomes and drew millions of migrants. However, the benefits were concentrated in coastal
China, and many migrant workers lacked urban residence rights and access to services. FDI was
therefore highly effective for national growth but less effective at spreading opportunity evenly."*

## Exam traps

- Equating development with income growth.
- Quoting HDI as if it shows inequality within a country.
- Listing routes without costs.
- Describing aid as a single thing rather than distinguishing its types.
- Assuming that a route which worked in one country will transfer to another without the same
  governance and timing.

## Self-test

1. Name the three HDI dimensions.
2. Why does GNI per capita limit understanding of development?
3. What does the Gini coefficient show?
4. Give one benefit and one cost of foreign direct investment.
5. What is spatial inequality?
6. Why is the geometric mean used in the HDI?
7. What does the Multidimensional Poverty Index measure?
8. What is tied aid, and why is it criticised?
9. Give one strength and one criticism of microfinance.
10. What does a Gini coefficient of 0 mean?
11. Why might remittances be a fragile route to development?

**Answers:** 1. Life expectancy, years of schooling and income. 2. It ignores distribution and
non-monetary well-being. 3. How evenly income is shared within a country. 4. Jobs; profit outflows.
5. Opportunity concentrated in some places, such as cities and coasts, leaving others behind.
6. So a very low score in one dimension cannot be fully offset by a high score in another.
7. Overlapping household deprivations in health, education and living standards. 8. Aid that must be
spent on the donor's goods or services; it can serve donor interests more than recipients' needs.
9. Strength: credit for people excluded from banks, often women; criticism: high interest and
over-indebtedness. 10. Perfect equality. 11. They depend on jobs and policies in host countries,
which can change suddenly.
