This guide covers section 4.4, Globalisation, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0), in topic 4, International trade and the global economy. The qualification is untiered. Countries, firms and figures in the examples are invented.

Pair it with the [globalisation revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-globalisation-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/) and the [globalisation practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-globalisation-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). The [exchange rates, globalisation and development overview](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-exchange-rates-globalisation-and-measuring-development/) gives a shorter summary alongside section 4.3.

## What section 4.4 asks you to do

| Specification statement | Command word |
|---|---|
| Globalisation, including its driving factors | Explain |
| How development is measured, including GDP per capita, life expectancy, access to health care, technology and education | Explain |
| Costs and benefits of globalisation to producers, workers and consumers in developed countries, including the impact on economic, social and environmental sustainability | Evaluate |
| The same costs and benefits in less developed countries | Evaluate |

The two "evaluate" statements name three groups and three kinds of sustainability. Questions often pick one group in one type of country, so learn the grid rather than a single list.

## What globalisation means

Globalisation is the process by which the world's economies become more closely connected. Four kinds of flow grow at once:

- **Goods and services** traded between countries, from cars to online banking.
- **Capital**: firms and investors putting money into other countries, including multinational companies building factories abroad (foreign direct investment).
- **People**: workers, students and tourists moving between countries.
- **Information and technology**: ideas, designs, brands and know-how spreading across borders.

A useful test in an answer: if a product is designed in one country, assembled in a second from parts made in several others, and sold in a fourth, that is a global supply chain, and it is only possible because all four flows have grown.

## The driving factors, with the chain of reasoning

"Explain" questions reward the link from the factor to more integration, not just its name.

| Driving factor | How it drives globalisation |
|---|---|
| Cheaper, faster transport | Container shipping and air freight cut the cost of moving goods, so producing far from the customer becomes profitable |
| Information and communication technology | The internet lets firms manage suppliers worldwide, sell online to any country and offshore services such as call centres |
| Trade liberalisation | Free trade agreements and the World Trade Organization (set up in 1995) reduce tariffs and quotas, so trade becomes cheaper |
| Growth of multinational companies | Multinationals place each stage of production where it is cheapest, spreading jobs, capital and technology |
| Freer movement of capital | Fewer limits on moving money between countries make it easier to invest abroad |
| Rising incomes in emerging economies | Growing middle classes create new markets and new suppliers |

Several factors work together. Cheap transport matters most for bulky goods, while technology has done most to globalise services.

## Measuring development

Development is a wider idea than income: it is about living standards, health, knowledge and choice. The specification names five indicators.

| Indicator | What it shows | A weakness |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | Average output, and so average income, per person | It is an average, so it hides how unequally income is shared |
| Life expectancy | The average number of years a newborn can expect to live; reflects health, diet and safety | Can be pulled down by high infant deaths even where adults live long |
| Access to health care | Doctors or hospital beds per 1,000 people, or vaccination rates | Counts facilities, not their quality or cost to patients |
| Technology | Internet users or mobile phone subscriptions per 100 people | Access does not mean the technology is used productively |
| Education | Literacy rates or average years of schooling | Years in school do not guarantee skills learned |

Composite measures such as the Human Development Index, published by the United Nations, combine health, education and income into one score, so one high figure cannot hide weak results elsewhere.

### Worked example 1: GDP per capita over time

Country R has GDP of $120 billion and 30 million people: GDP per capita is 120,000,000,000 ÷ 30,000,000 = **$4,000**. Ten years later GDP is $168 billion and the population is 35 million: GDP per capita is **$4,800**.

- GDP grew by (168 − 120) ÷ 120 = 40%.
- GDP per capita grew by (4,800 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 = **20%**.

Half of the growth was absorbed by the larger population. A question that says "GDP has risen 40%" is inviting you to point this out.

### Worked example 2: indicators that disagree

| Indicator | Country P | Country Q | Country S |
|---|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita ($) | 12,000 | 9,500 | 4,000 |
| Life expectancy (years) | 68 | 76 | 66 |
| Doctors per 1,000 people | 1.1 | 2.9 | 0.6 |
| Internet users per 100 people | 55 | 72 | 30 |
| Average years of schooling | 7.5 | 10.8 | 5.2 |

P has the highest GDP per capita, but Q leads on the other four indicators. Possible explanations: P's income may come from a single export earned by a small group, or its government may spend little on health and schools. The analysis point is that judging development by GDP per capita alone would rank P first, while the wider indicators suggest Q has the higher living standards. S is behind on every measure.

## Costs and benefits in developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Larger markets abroad; cheaper parts and labour through offshoring; economies of scale | Stronger foreign competition; firms that cannot cut costs may close |
| Workers | New jobs in exporting and high-skill sectors, such as finance, design and technology | Job losses in industries that move abroad; pressure on wages for low-skilled work; need to retrain |
| Consumers | Lower prices and wider choice of goods | Less local production; some lose income if they work in declining industries |

### Worked example 3: moving assembly abroad

A phone maker in Country A (developed) assembles 1 million phones a year at £42 each. In Country B (less developed) the cost, including shipping, is £30.

- Annual saving: (42 − 30) × 1,000,000 = **£12 million**.
- 400 assembly jobs in Country A paying £26,000 a year are lost: 400 × 26,000 = **£10.4 million** of wages.
- If the firm cuts the price by £5 a phone, consumers save £5 million a year.
- In Country B, 600 workers are hired at $8 a day, against $5 a day on local farms, which is 60% more.

Notice who gains and who loses: consumers and shareholders gain small amounts each, spread widely, while the cost falls heavily on 400 workers in one town. That pattern is the basis of many strong evaluations.

## Costs and benefits in less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Access to world markets; new technology and management skills from multinationals; investment in roads, ports and power | Local firms out-competed by multinationals; reliance on a few exports or one foreign buyer |
| Workers | Jobs that often pay more than farming or informal work; training and skills | Low wages by world standards, long hours, unsafe conditions, weak protection; child labour in some supply chains |
| Consumers | Cheaper imported goods and more choice, such as mobile phones | Local products and traditions may be displaced; prices shaped by world markets |

Two further points for less developed countries: multinationals may send most of their profit back to their home country, so less income stays where it was produced; and skilled people may emigrate to richer countries.

## The three kinds of sustainability

Sustainability means meeting today's needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Apply each one to the case in the question.

- **Economic sustainability**: does globalisation create lasting growth and skills, or a dependence that collapses if a multinational leaves? In Country A, can displaced workers move to new jobs?
- **Social sustainability**: does it reduce poverty and inequality, or widen gaps between regions, skill groups or rich and poor? Are working conditions acceptable?
- **Environmental sustainability**: more transport means more emissions; rapid industrial growth can pollute rivers and air in less developed countries. Against that, multinationals can spread cleaner technology.

## Writing an "evaluate" answer

1. Name the group and the type of country in the question.
2. Give a benefit as a chain: factor, effect, consequence for that group.
3. Give a cost as a chain.
4. Add a sustainability point if the question names it.
5. Judge, saying what the outcome depends on: the pace of change, government policy (retraining, safety and environmental standards), the time period, and whether gains are shared.

A strong judgement often reads: "Benefits are widespread but small for each consumer, while costs are concentrated on particular workers and places, so the overall effect depends on how well those groups are helped to adjust."

## Common mistakes

- Defining globalisation as "trade". It also covers capital, people and ideas.
- Naming a driving factor without explaining how it increases integration.
- Using GDP rather than GDP per capita to compare development.
- Treating all of a country's workers as one group.
- Leaving out environmental effects when the question names sustainability.
