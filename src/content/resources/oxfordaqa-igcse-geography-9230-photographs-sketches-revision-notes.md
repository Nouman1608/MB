These notes cover the "Maps in association with photographs" part of section 3.3.1.1, Cartographic skills, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3, which includes "photographs: use and interpret ground, aerial and satellite photographs", "draw sketches from photographs" and "label and annotate diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs". For all the skills in the unit, see the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/); for full explanations, read the [photographs and sketches study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-photographs-sketches/).

## Specification checklist

- Compare maps.
- Sketch maps: draw, label, understand, interpret.
- Use and interpret ground, aerial and satellite photographs.
- Describe landforms, natural vegetation, land use, settlement and geographical phenomena from photographs.
- Draw sketches from photographs.
- Label and annotate diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs.
- Skills can be tested on **all three papers**.

## Photograph types

| Type | View | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | Across, from ground level | Detail; how a place looks | Narrow view; foreground hides background |
| Oblique aerial | Angled, from the air | Shape and height together | Scale varies front to back |
| Vertical aerial | Straight down | Layout like a map | Height hard to judge |
| Satellite | From space | Large areas; change over time | Less detail |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Foreground / middle ground / background | Near, middle and far parts of a ground or oblique photograph |
| Direction of view | Compass direction the camera faced |
| Field sketch | Simple drawing of a view, picking out relevant features |
| Sketch map | Simplified map of an area, as seen from above |
| Label | Names a feature |
| Annotation | Explains or describes a feature |
| Land use | What land is used for: farming, housing, industry, transport, recreation |
| Settlement pattern | How buildings are arranged: clustered, linear, dispersed |

## Reading system

1. Read caption and question.
2. Divide: foreground, middle, background; left, centre, right.
3. Check four categories: **landforms, vegetation, land use, settlement**.
4. Use scale clues: people, vehicles, trees.
5. Quote what you see, with its position.

## Matching photograph and map

- Find two features in both → work out the **direction of view**.
- Give **grid references** for features.
- Map: height, distance, names. Photograph: appearance, land use, vegetation.
- Differences may show the map is older.

## Comparing maps

- **Same place, two dates:** list what has appeared, disappeared or grown; give grid references and rough distances ("housing has spread about 1 km east along the main road, from 6240 to 6440").
- **Different information, same area:** look for links between maps, such as settlement on low, flat land shown on a relief map, or dense population along a river.
- **Order:** similarity, then difference, then a reason only if the question asks you to explain or suggest.
- **Check the dates and keys** before comparing; a change in symbols is not a change on the ground.

## Field sketch steps

Frame → horizon and main lines → relevant features only → labels → annotations → title, place, direction of view.

## Sketch map essentials

Title, north arrow, approximate scale, key; only relevant features; measurements are approximate.

## Label versus annotation

- Label: "Cliff".
- Annotation: "Steep cliff of resistant rock, undercut at its base by wave erosion".

## Condensed example

Photograph: an oblique aerial view of a coastal town. Housing is densely packed on flat land around a harbour; newer, larger houses spread up a hillside to the right; a beach lies in the left foreground; farmland covers the background.

**Describe the land use (4 marks):** "Dense housing surrounds the harbour in the centre. Newer, larger detached houses spread up the hillside on the right. A sandy beach used for recreation lies in the left foreground. The background is farmland with fields and hedges." Four uses, each placed.

## Exam traps

- Writing about everything instead of what the question asks.
- No positions: "there is a river" instead of "a river in the middle ground".
- Explaining on a describe question.
- Field sketches with no title, direction or labels.
- Labels when annotations are required.
- Ignoring the map when one is provided.
- Forgetting that a photograph is one moment, one season and one chosen framing.

## Self-test

1. Name the four types of photograph in these notes.
2. Which type of photograph is most like a map?
3. Give one weakness of an oblique aerial photograph.
4. What four categories should you check when describing a landscape?
5. How do you find the direction of view of a photograph using a map?
6. What three things should a field sketch have besides the drawing?
7. What is the difference between a field sketch and a sketch map?
8. What four items should a sketch map include?
9. Turn the label "farmland" into an annotation.
10. Why might a map and a photograph of the same place disagree?
11. Give one limitation of using a single photograph as evidence.

**Answers**

1. Ground, oblique aerial, vertical aerial, satellite.
2. Vertical aerial.
3. Scale changes from front to back, so distances are hard to compare.
4. Landforms, natural vegetation, land use, settlement.
5. Identify two features in the photograph, find them on the map, and work out which way the camera faced.
6. Labels or annotations, a title (with place), and the direction of view.
7. A field sketch shows a view from one point; a sketch map shows a layout from above.
8. Title, north arrow, approximate scale, key.
9. For example: "Flat farmland with large fields, probably arable on fertile valley soils."
10. One may be older than the other, so new buildings or roads appear in only one.
11. It shows one moment, one season and a framing chosen by the photographer, so it may not be typical.
