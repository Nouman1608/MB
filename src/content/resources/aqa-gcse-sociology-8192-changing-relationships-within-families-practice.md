## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** In Willmott and Young's theory, which stage is the work-centred family in which the wife is responsible for home and children?

A. Stage 1
B. Stage 2
C. Stage 3
D. Stage 4

**[1]**

**2.** Which statement best describes an arranged marriage?

A. A marriage in which one partner does not consent
B. A marriage in which families choose or introduce partners, who both consent
C. A marriage between two people of the same sex
D. A marriage to more than one partner at the same time

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** features of family life in pre-industrial Britain. **[2]**

**4.** Describe Willmott and Young's Stage 2, early industrial family. **[3]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** reason for the growth of the privatised, home-centred nuclear family in the twentieth century. **[4]**

**6.** Read Item A.

> **Item A (fictional).** Farah is 52. She works part time and looks after her mother, who has dementia and lives with Farah's family. Farah also has two teenage children. Her brother lives 200 miles away and sends money each month but rarely visits.

- **(a)** Identify the type of family Farah lives in. **[1]**
- **(b)** Explain how a feminist sociologist might interpret Farah's situation. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** way in which relationships between teenagers and parents have changed. **[3]**

**8.** Describe what is meant by an arranged marriage, and explain **one** way in which arranged marriage in the UK has changed over time. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Read Item B.

> **Item B (fictional).** A researcher wants to study the quality of parenting in different families. She plans to use overt participant observation, spending a week living with each of five families who answered an advert in a parenting magazine.

- **(a)** From Item B, examine **one** weakness of the research. **[2]**
- **(b)** Identify **one** other research method that could be used to study relationships between teenagers and parents, and explain **one** advantage of using it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** criticisms of Willmott and Young's theory of the symmetrical family. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that families have become more child-centred. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the principle of stratified diffusion explains changes in family life. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** D [1]. Stage 3 is the symmetrical, home-centred family. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. A describes a forced marriage, which is a crime in England and Wales. **[1]**

**3.** The family was a unit of production, working together on the land or in cottage industry [1]; children were expected to work from a young age [1]. *Also credit:* most households were nuclear (Laslett); marriage was often an economic arrangement. **[2]**

**4.** In the early industrial family, men worked long hours outside the home in factories or docks [1] and often spent leisure time away from the family [1]. Women, especially in poor working-class areas, relied on their mothers and other female kin for support, so extended kin networks were important [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Identify: rising living standards and better housing [1]. Explain: as homes became more comfortable, with more space, heating and home entertainment such as television [1], couples spent more leisure time at home together rather than with friends or kin [1]. This made the nuclear family more home-centred and privatised, and less dependent on wider kin and neighbours [1]. *Also credit:* geographical mobility; smaller families; more married women in paid work. **[4]**

**6. (a)** A vertically extended family [1]. *Also credit:* beanpole family.

**(b)** Feminists would argue that care of the elderly falls mainly on women [1]. Farah combines paid work, caring for her mother and raising teenagers, which adds to the dual burden or triple shift [1]. Her brother's contribution is money rather than time, which shows how caring work is gendered and unequal, even when it looks like a family decision [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Relationships have become more democratic [1]: teenagers are more likely to negotiate rules about curfews, money and phones rather than simply obeying [1], reflecting a more child-centred family and greater respect for young people's views [1]. *Also credit:* longer dependency, as young people stay in education or training to 18 and live at home longer because of housing costs. **[3]**

**8.** Describe: families play a central role in choosing or introducing partners [1], but both partners consent to the marriage [1]. Change: many younger people now experience a more "assisted" process [1], in which they meet possible partners and have a strong say in the final choice, so the balance between family and individual choice has shifted [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** One weakness, examined. The five families volunteered through a parenting magazine, so they may be unusually interested in parenting [1], which makes the sample unrepresentative and the findings hard to generalise [1]. *Also credit:* families may behave differently while the researcher lives with them (the observer effect), so the data may lack validity. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify: unstructured interviews [1]. Advantage: the interviewer can build rapport [1] and follow up answers to explore sensitive topics such as arguments over rules [1], giving detailed qualitative data that show how teenagers and parents themselves see their relationship, which is high in validity [1]. *Also credit:* questionnaires, which reach a large sample and allow comparison. **[6]**

**10.** First, feminists such as Oakley found little evidence of symmetry [1]: women still did most housework and childcare, and men's contribution was often limited to "helping" [1]. Second, the specification notes that feminists saw little evidence of a move towards Stage 4 among working-class families [1], so the principle of stratified diffusion did not seem to work as predicted, and Stage 4 is in any case less equal than Stage 3 [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- A child-centred family is organised around children's needs and interests [1].
- In pre-industrial families, children worked from a young age as part of the unit of production [1].
- Smaller families mean parents can spend more time and money on each child [1].
- Childhood is now seen as a protected stage, with the state setting rules on parenting [1].
- Scotland and Wales have removed the defence of reasonable punishment, showing more concern for children's rights [1].
- Relationships with teenagers are more democratic and negotiated [1].
- Functionalists see this as progress, as families adapt to a society that values education [1].
- Against: parents still hold economic power, and dependency now lasts longer [1].
- Feminists note that child-centred parenting increases pressure mainly on mothers [1].
- Marxists argue child-centredness makes families into bigger consumers of toys, technology and activities, which benefits capitalism [1].
- Child-centredness varies by class and culture, as the Rapoports' work on diversity suggests [1].
- Judgement: families have become more child-centred in attention and spending, but power over children and the burden of care have changed less [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the three periods to show change over time, rather than describing only modern families.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Stratified diffusion is the theory that what happens at the top of the stratification system spreads downwards over time [1].
- Willmott and Young, writing from a functionalist perspective, used it to predict that the Stage 4 managing director family would spread from managers to other classes [1].
- Their survey of nearly 2,000 people in Greater London gave a large data set for their claims [1].
- Some changes do seem to start among the middle class, such as dual career families [1].
- Against: they found the symmetrical family more common in working-class families, so not all change starts at the top [1].
- Feminists saw little evidence of a move towards Stage 4 among working-class families [1].
- Feminists also saw little evidence of symmetry itself (Oakley) [1].
- Changes in family life also come from law, such as divorce reform and same-sex marriage, which affect all classes at once [1].
- Changes in women's employment and attitudes, rather than class position, may drive change [1].
- Cultural diversity means changes such as arranged marriage becoming more "assisted" follow their own paths [1].
- Evaluation: the theory is a functionalist "march of progress" view that assumes change is generally for the better [1].
- Judgement: stratified diffusion explains some changes, but family change has many causes and does not simply flow down the class structure [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Point out the tension: if Stage 4 spreads, families become less symmetrical, which undercuts Willmott and Young's own optimism.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that pre-industrial families were mostly extended.
- In 6(b), describing Farah's day without naming the dual burden or linking care to gender.
- In 8, describing an arranged marriage as one without consent.
- Getting stratified diffusion backwards in Question 12.
