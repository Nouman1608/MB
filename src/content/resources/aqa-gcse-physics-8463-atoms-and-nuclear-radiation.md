## What the specification asks for

- Explain that some nuclei are unstable and give out radiation, randomly, as they become more stable.
- Define **activity** (in becquerel, Bq) and **count-rate**.
- Describe alpha, beta, gamma and neutron emission, and compare alpha, beta and gamma by **penetration through
  materials, range in air and ionising power** only.
- Apply this to uses of radiation and **evaluate the best source** for a situation.
- Write balanced nuclear equations for single alpha and beta decays, by balancing mass and atomic numbers.
- Explain half-life and its link to randomness; determine half-life from information.
- Higher tier only: calculate the net decline, as a ratio, after a given number of half-lives.
- Compare the hazards of **contamination** and **irradiation**; know why precautions and peer review matter.

## Radioactive decay

Some atomic nuclei are **unstable**. Such a nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable. This
is **radioactive decay**, and it is **random**: there is no way to predict when a particular nucleus will decay.

- **Activity** is the rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays, measured in **becquerel (Bq)**. 1 Bq is
  one decay per second.
- **Count-rate** is the number of decays recorded each second by a detector, such as a Geiger-Muller tube.

The count-rate is usually lower than the activity, because the detector only catches radiation travelling towards
it.

## The four emissions

| Emission | Nature | Effect on the nucleus |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha particle (α) | Two protons and two neutrons; the same as a helium nucleus | Mass number −4; atomic number −2 |
| Beta particle (β) | High-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton | Mass number unchanged; atomic number +1 |
| Gamma ray (γ) | Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus | No change in mass or charge |
| Neutron (n) | A neutron emitted from the nucleus | Mass number −1 |

### Comparing alpha, beta and gamma

| | Alpha | Beta | Gamma |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penetration | Stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air | Passes through paper; stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium | Passes through aluminium; only reduced by thick lead or concrete |
| Range in air | A few centimetres | Further than alpha | Very large |
| Ionising power | Strongly ionising | Moderately ionising | Weakly ionising |

Notice the pattern: the more strongly ionising an emission is, the more quickly it transfers its energy, so the
shorter its range and the less it penetrates.

## Choosing the best source

Specification questions ask you to evaluate which source suits a job. Ask three questions: what must the
radiation pass through, what must stop it, and what harm must be avoided?

**Worked example 1 (thickness monitoring).** A factory makes paper sheets. A source above the sheet and a
detector below it monitor thickness. Which emission should be used?

- **Alpha** would be stopped completely by paper, so the count-rate would not change with small changes in
  thickness.
- **Gamma** would pass through almost unaffected, so small changes would not show either.
- **Beta** is partly absorbed by paper; a thicker sheet absorbs more, so the count-rate falls. **Use beta.**

**Worked example 2 (detecting a source inside the body).** A doctor needs radiation to leave the patient's body and
reach a detector outside. Gamma is chosen, because it passes out of the body easily and is weakly ionising, so it
causes the least damage on the way. Alpha would not leave the body and would be highly damaging inside it.

## Nuclear equations

In nuclear equations, an alpha particle is written ⁴₂He and a beta particle ⁰₋₁e. In every equation the mass
numbers on both sides must add up to the same total, and so must the atomic numbers. You only balance the numbers;
identifying the daughter element is not required.

**Worked example 3 (alpha).** Radium-226 (atomic number 88) decays by alpha emission to radon.

```
²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
mass:   226 = 222 + 4
atomic:  88 =  86 + 2
```

**Worked example 4 (beta).** Carbon-14 (atomic number 6) decays by beta emission to nitrogen.

```
¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e
mass:   14 = 14 + 0
atomic:  6 =  7 + (−1)
```

**Worked example 5 (finding the missing numbers).** Cobalt-60 (atomic number 27) emits a beta particle. Find the
mass and atomic numbers of the new nucleus.

```
mass:   60 = A + 0     → A = 60
atomic: 27 = Z + (−1)  → Z = 28
```

If a gamma ray is also emitted, nothing else changes: gamma carries no mass or charge.

## Half-life and randomness

The **half-life** of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for the **number of nuclei of the isotope in a
sample to halve**, or for the **count-rate (or activity) to fall to half** its initial level.

**Why a random process has a fixed half-life.** Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in any given time,
but which nuclei decay is random. With a very large number of nuclei, the same fraction decays in each equal time
interval, so the time to halve is always the same, whatever the starting amount. With only a few nuclei, the
results would be unpredictable.

**Worked example 6 (from data).** A source's activity is 1200 Bq. After 18 hours it is 150 Bq. Find the half-life.

```
1200 → 600 → 300 → 150     (3 halvings)
half-life = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 hours
```

**From a graph.** Read the starting count-rate, find half that value on the y-axis, read across to the curve and
down to the time axis. Repeat from a later point and take a mean.

**Higher tier only: net decline as a ratio.** After n half-lives the activity falls to (1/2)ⁿ of its starting
value. After 5 half-lives: (1/2)⁵ = 1/32, so the net decline is from 32 to 1, a ratio of **1 : 32** of the initial
activity remaining. For worked example 6, 1200 Bq after 5 half-lives (30 hours) becomes 1200 ÷ 32 = 37.5 Bq.

## Contamination and irradiation

| | Radioactive contamination | Irradiation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials | Exposing an object to nuclear radiation |
| Does the object become radioactive? | It carries the radioactive atoms, so it emits radiation | No |
| Hazard | From the decay of the contaminating atoms, continuing until they have decayed or been removed | Only while exposed to the source |

The type of radiation emitted affects the level of hazard from contamination. Contamination is often the greater long-term hazard: if radioactive atoms are swallowed or breathed in, alpha
emitters are especially dangerous inside the body because they are so strongly ionising.

**Precautions.** Suitable precautions protect against the source used in irradiation: keep a distance, use
shielding, limit the time of exposure, handle sources with tongs, and store them in lead-lined containers.
Wearing gloves also prevents contamination of the skin.

**Peer review.** Findings of studies into the effects of radiation on humans must be **published and shared** with
other scientists so they can be checked by **peer review**. This guards against errors and helps set safe
practice.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a beta particle comes from the electron shells; it is ejected from the nucleus.
- Giving gamma a mass number of 4 or a charge.
- Balancing only the mass numbers.
- Saying half-life is "half the time for the source to decay completely".
- Saying an irradiated object becomes radioactive.
- Using "more dangerous" without saying whether the source is inside or outside the body.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a source: rule out the unsuitable emissions with reasons, then justify the choice.
- **Explain** half-life and randomness: individual nuclei are unpredictable, but large numbers behave predictably.
- **Determine** half-life from a graph: show the halving lines and the reading.
- **Compare** contamination and irradiation: give a matching difference, not two separate definitions.
