## Section A: short questions

**1.** Name the three fossil fuels. **[3]**

**2.** Natural gas is a fossil fuel.

**(a)** Name the main constituent of natural gas. **[1]**

**(b)** State why this substance is described as a hydrocarbon. **[1]**

**3.** Which of the following compounds is **not** a hydrocarbon?

A: C₃H₈

B: C₂H₄

C: CH₃OH

D: C₈H₁₈

**[1]**

**4.** State whether petroleum is an element, a compound or a mixture, and give a reason. **[2]**

**5.** Name the fraction obtained from petroleum that is used:

**(a)** as jet fuel **[1]**

**(b)** for making roads **[1]**

**(c)** as a chemical feedstock. **[1]**

**6.** Which statement about the fractions collected from the **top** of a fractionating column, compared with those from the bottom, is correct?

A: They have longer chains and are more viscous.

B: They have shorter chains and are less volatile.

C: They have lower boiling points and are more volatile.

D: They have higher boiling points and are less viscous.

**[1]**

**7.** Name the fraction used for each of the following:

**(a)** fuel for ships and home heating systems **[1]**

**(b)** lubricants, waxes and polishes **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**8.** Petroleum is separated into fractions in a fractionating column.

**(a)** Describe how the temperature changes from the bottom to the top of the column. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe what happens to the petroleum before it enters the column. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why different fractions are collected at different heights in the column. **[2]**

**(d)** State **one** way in which a fraction differs from a pure substance. **[1]**

**9.** A student is given three liquid fractions, labelled R, S and T. Fraction R was collected nearest the top of the column and fraction T nearest the bottom.

**(a)** Which fraction has the longest molecules? **[1]**

**(b)** Which fraction would pour most easily? Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(c)** The student puts a few drops of each fraction on separate watch glasses at room temperature. Predict which fraction evaporates fastest. **[1]**

**10.** A student writes: "Fractional distillation breaks the long hydrocarbon molecules in petroleum into shorter, more useful ones."

**(a)** Explain why this statement is incorrect. **[2]**

**(b)** Name the property of the hydrocarbons that fractional distillation depends on. **[1]**

**11.** Gasoline (petrol) is used as fuel in cars, and bitumen is used for making roads.

**(a)** Compare the viscosity of these two fractions. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why a fraction with high volatility is suitable as a fuel for a car engine, in which the fuel must be mixed with air as a vapour. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why bitumen, not gasoline, is suitable for road surfaces. **[1]**

**12.** Kerosene and diesel oil are both fuels obtained from petroleum. Diesel oil is collected lower in the column than kerosene.

**(a)** State which of the two fractions has the higher boiling range. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain, in terms of the forces between molecules, why this fraction has the higher boiling range. **[2]**

**(c)** State the use of diesel oil named in the syllabus. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Coal [1]; natural gas [1]; petroleum (crude oil) [1].

**2.** **(a)** Methane [1].

**(b)** It is a compound containing hydrogen and carbon **only** [1].

**3.** **C** [1]. CH₃OH contains oxygen as well as carbon and hydrogen; each of the other formulae contains only carbon and hydrogen.

**4.** A mixture [1]; it contains many different hydrocarbons that are not chemically bonded to one another / it has no fixed boiling point / it can be separated by a physical method [1].

**5.** **(a)** Kerosene / paraffin [1].

**(b)** Bitumen [1].

**(c)** Naphtha [1].

**6.** **C** [1]. Moving up the column, chain length decreases, so boiling point falls and volatility rises. Option B has the volatility trend reversed; A and D describe the bottom of the column.

**7.** **(a)** Fuel oil [1].

**(b)** Lubricating oil [1].

**(c)** Refinery gas [1].

**8.** **(a)** The temperature decreases going up / the column is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top [1].

**(b)** It is heated so that it vaporises (turns into vapour) [1].

**(c)** The vapours rise and cool [1]; each fraction condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point, so fractions with lower boiling points condense higher up [1].

**(d)** A fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons, so it boils over a range of temperatures rather than at one fixed temperature [1]. Accept: it contains more than one compound.

*Do not credit "the fractions evaporate at different levels" in (c): condensing is the key process inside the column.*

**9.** **(a)** T [1].

**(b)** R [1]; it has the shortest molecules, so it has the lowest viscosity [1].

**(c)** R [1]; it is the most volatile fraction.

**10.** **(a)** Fractional distillation is a physical separation; it separates the hydrocarbons already present according to their boiling points [1] and does not break any covalent bonds within the molecules, so no new, shorter molecules are made [1]. *Credit a reference to cracking as the process that does break long molecules.*

**(b)** Boiling point [1].

**11.** **(a)** Gasoline has a much lower viscosity than bitumen / bitumen is far more viscous [1].

**(b)** A volatile fraction vaporises easily, so it mixes with air readily before burning [1].

**(c)** Bitumen is very viscous (almost solid) at normal temperatures, so it stays in place and holds the road surface together; gasoline is a runny, volatile liquid that would flow away and evaporate [1].

**12.** **(a)** Diesel oil [1].

**(b)** Diesel oil has longer molecules [1], so the intermolecular forces between its molecules are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them [1]. *Do not credit "stronger bonds" or "breaking covalent bonds": boiling separates molecules, it does not break bonds inside them.*

**(c)** Fuel used in diesel engines [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out **only** in the definition of a hydrocarbon.
- Writing that the column is hottest at the top.
- Describing fractions as pure substances or petroleum as a compound.
- Confusing fractional distillation (physical, separates by boiling point) with cracking (chemical, breaks molecules).
- Explaining boiling points in terms of breaking covalent bonds instead of overcoming intermolecular forces.
- Mixing up volatility and viscosity: short chains are more volatile and less viscous.
