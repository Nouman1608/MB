These questions cover section 3.5 Electricity of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, version 1.3, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards): 3.5.1.1 to 3.5.1.6, including Required practicals 5 and 6. Electricity is AS and A-level content, assessed at A-level in Paper 1 and assumed in Paper 2; it is not one of the options (sections 3.9 to 3.13), one of which you study for Paper 3 Section B.

Learn the content first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-electricity/). Take the charge on an electron as 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C in magnitude. Treat meters as ideal and supplies as having negligible internal resistance unless told otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by:

**(a)** electric current **[1]**
**(b)** potential difference. **[1]**

**2.** A phone charger supplies a steady 0.75 A for 40 minutes at 5.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate the charge that flows. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of electrons that flow. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy transferred. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the I–V characteristic of a filament lamp, with I on the vertical axis, and explain its shape. **[4]**

**4.**

**(a)** Describe the I–V characteristic of a semiconductor diode in forward and reverse bias. **[3]**
**(b)** In forward bias a diode has a pd of 0.70 V across it and a current of 14 mA. Calculate its resistance at this point. **[1]**

**5.** A wire is 1.20 m long with diameter 0.25 mm. The material has resistivity 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of the wire. **[3]**
**(b)** The wire is connected across a 6.0 V supply. Calculate the power dissipated. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how to determine the resistivity of the metal in a wire using a micrometer, an ammeter, a voltmeter and a metre rule. Include how you would use a graph. **[6]**

**7.** A 12 V supply is connected to two parallel branches. Branch X is a 2.0 Ω resistor in series with a 4.0 Ω resistor. Branch Y is a 9.0 Ω resistor in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor. The 2.0 Ω and 9.0 Ω resistors are connected to the positive terminal. Point P lies between the 2.0 Ω and 4.0 Ω resistors; point Q lies between the 9.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω resistors.

**(a)** Calculate the total resistance of the circuit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current from the supply. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the current in each branch. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the pd between P and Q. **[3]**

**8.** State the conservation law that explains each of the following:

**(a)** the total current into a junction equals the total current out **[1]**
**(b)** round a closed loop, the sum of the emfs equals the sum of the pds. **[1]**

**9.** A 6.0 V supply is connected across a 10 kΩ fixed resistor in series with an LDR. V_out is measured across the fixed resistor. The LDR's resistance is 90 kΩ in the dark and 2.0 kΩ in bright light.

**(a)** Calculate V_out in the dark and in bright light. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest how to change the circuit so that V_out rises when it gets dark. **[1]**
**(c)** In bright light, a 10 kΩ load is connected across the output. Calculate the new V_out. **[3]**

**10.** A student investigates the emf and internal resistance of a battery (Required practical 6). The results are:

| I / A | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 4.20 | 3.90 | 3.60 | 3.30 | 3.00 |

**(a)** Describe the circuit used and how the readings are varied. **[3]**
**(b)** Use the data to determine the emf ε and internal resistance r. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the student should switch off between readings. **[1]**
**(d)** The battery is connected to a 6.0 Ω resistor. Calculate the terminal pd. **[2]**

**11.**

**(a)** Compare how the resistance of a metal conductor and of an ntc thermistor change as temperature rises, and explain each. **[3]**
**(b)** State what is meant by a superconductor. **[2]**
**(c)** Give two applications of superconductors. **[2]**

**12.** Four identical cells each have emf 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.40 Ω.

**(a)** The cells are connected in series to a 4.4 Ω resistor. Calculate the current and the terminal pd. **[3]**
**(b)** The cells are instead connected in parallel to the same resistor. Calculate the current. **[2]**
**(c)** For the series arrangement, calculate the energy transferred in the 4.4 Ω resistor in 10 minutes. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The rate of flow of charge (I = ΔQ/Δt) [1].
**(b)** The work done (energy transferred) per unit charge (V = W/Q) [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Energy per charge" without saying work done or energy transferred may not be accepted; give the full wording.

**2. (a)** Δt = 40 × 60 = 2400 s [1]; Q = IΔt = 0.75 × 2400 = **1800 C** [1].
**(b)** N = 1800 / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.1 × 10²² electrons** [1].
**(c)** W = QV [1] = 1800 × 5.0 = **9000 J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Using 40 instead of 2400 s loses the conversion mark; write Q on its own line so that later steps can still be followed and credited.

**3.** Straight line through the origin at low current [1]; gradient decreases as V increases, and the graph is symmetrical for reversed pd [1]. Larger current raises the filament temperature [1], so the metal ions vibrate more, the charge carriers collide more and the resistance increases [1].
*Examiner insight:* The explanation needs the whole chain from current to temperature to ion vibration to resistance; "it gets hot" alone is not enough.

**4. (a)** Forward bias: almost no current below a threshold pd [1], then current rises steeply [1]. Reverse bias: very high resistance, current about zero [1].
**(b)** R = V/I = 0.70 / 0.014 = **50 Ω** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert 14 mA to 0.014 A; a bare "0.05" with no unit or power of ten earns nothing.

**5. (a)** A = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)²/4 = 4.91 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]. R = ρL/A [1] = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.20 / 4.91 × 10⁻⁸ = **27 Ω** [1].
**(b)** P = V²/R [1] = 6.0² / 26.9 = **1.3 W** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Using the diameter as the radius in πr² gives four times the area; show A on its own line so the method mark is visible.

**6.** Measure the diameter with the micrometer at several points along the wire and in different orientations, then take the mean [1]. Calculate A = πd²/4 [1]. Connect the wire in series with the ammeter and a supply, with the voltmeter across a measured length L, using the metre rule [1]. Record V and I and find R = V/I for a range of lengths [1]. Plot R against L; the gradient is ρ/A [1], so ρ = gradient × A [1].
*Examiner insight:* A six-mark method needs measurements, the calculation and the graph analysis; a list of equipment with no gradient step leaves marks behind.

**7. (a)** Branch X = 6.0 Ω, branch Y = 12 Ω [1]. 1/R = 1/6.0 + 1/12, so **R = 4.0 Ω** [1].
**(b)** I = 12 / 4.0 = **3.0 A** [1].
**(c)** I_X = 12 / 6.0 = **2.0 A** [1]; I_Y = 12 / 12 = **1.0 A** [1].
**(d)** Pd across 4.0 Ω = 2.0 × 4.0 = 8.0 V, so P is 8.0 V above the negative terminal [1]. Pd across 3.0 Ω = 1.0 × 3.0 = 3.0 V, so Q is 3.0 V above it [1]. Pd between P and Q = 8.0 − 3.0 = **5.0 V** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Measure both potentials from the same reference point; subtracting a pd taken from the positive end from one taken from the negative end gives a wrong answer.

**8. (a)** Conservation of **charge** [1].
**(b)** Conservation of **energy** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Conservation of current" or "of voltage" is not a physical conservation law, so it does not answer the question.

**9. (a)** Dark: V_out = 6.0 × 10/(10 + 90) [1] = **0.60 V** [1]. Light: V_out = 6.0 × 10/12 = **5.0 V** [1].
**(b)** Take V_out across the LDR instead (or swap the two components) [1].
**(c)** 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ = 5.0 kΩ [1]. V_out = 6.0 × 5.0/(5.0 + 2.0) [1] = **4.3 V** [1].
*Examiner insight:* For a loaded divider, the parallel combination must replace the fixed resistor in both the numerator and the total.

**10. (a)** Battery in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor [1]; voltmeter connected across the battery terminals [1]; change the variable resistor to vary I and record V each time [1].
**(b)** Gradient = (3.00 − 4.20)/(1.00 − 0.20) = −1.5 V A⁻¹ [1], so **r = 1.5 Ω** [1]. Intercept: ε = V + Ir = 4.20 + 0.20 × 1.5 [1] = **4.5 V** [1].
**(c)** To stop the battery warming up, which would change its internal resistance [1].
**(d)** I = 4.5/(6.0 + 1.5) = 0.60 A [1]; V = IR = 0.60 × 6.0 = **3.6 V** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Read the gradient over a wide interval of the line, not from two adjacent points, and give r as a positive value.

**11. (a)** Metal: resistance increases, because the ions vibrate more and impede charge carriers [1]. ntc thermistor: resistance decreases [1], because more charge carriers are released as temperature rises [1].
**(b)** A material with zero resistivity [1] at and below a critical temperature, which depends on the material [1].
**(c)** Producing strong magnetic fields [1]; reducing energy loss in the transmission of electric power [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Very low resistance" is wrong for a superconductor; state zero resistivity and include the critical temperature condition.

**12. (a)** ε = 4 × 1.5 = 6.0 V and r = 4 × 0.40 = 1.6 Ω [1]. I = 6.0/(4.4 + 1.6) = **1.0 A** [1]. V = IR = **4.4 V** [1].
**(b)** ε = 1.5 V, r = 0.40/4 = 0.10 Ω [1]. I = 1.5/4.5 = **0.33 A** [1].
**(c)** E = I²Rt = 1.0² × 4.4 × 600 [1] = **2.6 × 10³ J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Identical cells in parallel keep the emf of one cell; multiplying the emf by four is a common slip that loses both marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving minutes or mA unconverted before using I = ΔQ/Δt or R = V/I.
- Using the diameter as the radius when finding a wire's cross-sectional area.
- Taking resistance from the gradient of a curved I–V graph.
- Forgetting that the parallel branches share the supply pd only when nothing is in series with them.
- Missing the temperature step when explaining the filament lamp.
- Ignoring the loading effect of a resistor placed across a potential divider output.
- Quoting r as negative because the gradient of V against I is negative.
- Writing "very low resistance" for a superconductor.
