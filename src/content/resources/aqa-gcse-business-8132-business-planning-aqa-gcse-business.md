This guide goes deeper into section 3.1.6 Business planning of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. The section sits in Topic 1, Business in the real world, which can be assessed on either written paper. The wider page, [Business location and Business planning](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-business-location-and-business-planning/), sets out the basics. This page looks at who uses a plan and why, how to read the financial forecasts inside one, and how to judge whether a plan is worth the effort.

Use it with the [Business planning revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-planning-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) and the [Business planning practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-planning-aqa-gcse-business-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Content | Students should be able to |
|---|---|
| The purpose of business planning | "understand the reasons why businesses create plans, including importance in setting up a new business, raising finance, setting objectives and detailing how functions of a business will be organised" |
| The main sections within a business plan | "understand the main sections of a business plan" |
| Benefits and drawbacks | "analyse the benefits and drawbacks of business planning" |
| Basic financial terms | "understand the difference between variable costs, fixed costs and total costs" |
| Basic financial calculations | "understand the concept of revenue, costs, profit and loss" |

The specification adds: "Students will not be expected to write a business plan." You will be expected to read one, use its figures and comment on it.

## Who reads a business plan, and what each wants

A plan has several readers. Thinking about the reader is the quickest way to explain why a plan matters.

| Reader | What they look for | Link to the specification's reasons |
|---|---|---|
| The owner or entrepreneur | Whether the idea works on paper; a route to follow | Setting up a new business; setting objectives |
| A bank | Whether the business can repay a loan and interest | Raising finance |
| An investor | Whether the business can grow and give a return | Raising finance |
| Managers and staff | Who does what, and what targets they work towards | Detailing how functions will be organised; setting objectives |

A plan written only to impress a bank is less useful to the owner, because it may hide weaknesses. A plan that the owner uses as a working document is updated as results come in.

## The four reasons in more depth

### Setting up a new business

Writing the plan forces the entrepreneur to answer practical questions before spending money: who will buy, at what price, from which premises, with what equipment and suppliers. Gaps appear early. An owner who cannot say how customers will find the business has found a problem cheaply, on paper, rather than expensively, after opening.

### Raising finance

A new business has no trading record, so lenders and investors judge it on the plan. Its financial forecasts show expected revenue, costs and profit. Its market research gives evidence that customers exist. Its description of the owner's skills shows whether the team can deliver. A clear plan reduces the risk a lender sees, which makes finance more likely.

### Setting objectives

A plan states targets, such as a level of sales, a number of customers or a profit figure by the end of the first year. Objectives give direction and a way to measure progress. If actual sales fall short of the plan by the third month, the owner knows to act, for example by changing promotion.

### Detailing how functions will be organised

A business has four functional areas: operations, human resources, marketing and finance. The plan sets out how each will work and who is responsible. In a one-person business the owner does all four, and the plan shows where outside help, such as an accountant, is needed.

## The main sections of a plan

The specification does not give a fixed list. Most plans include:

- an overview of the business idea and its objectives
- the target market and the market research behind it
- a marketing plan: the product, price, promotion and place
- operations: premises, suppliers, equipment and how the product is made or delivered
- people: the owners' experience and the staff needed
- financial forecasts: start-up costs, revenue, costs, profit and cash flow

In an exam, match a section to a purpose. The market research section supports raising finance because it is evidence of demand. The people section supports organising functions because it shows who does each job.

## The financial terms inside the plan

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with the level of output, such as rent and insurance |
| Variable costs | Costs that change directly with output, such as raw materials and packaging |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + total variable costs |
| Revenue | Income from sales: price × quantity sold |
| Profit | Revenue − total costs, when revenue is greater |
| Loss | When total costs are greater than revenue |

Total variable costs = variable cost per unit × quantity. Note that "costs" in the plan means total costs unless the question says otherwise.

## Worked example 1: reading a forecast

Figures in this example are invented. Juniper Bikes plans to hire out electric bikes to tourists. Its plan forecasts the first summer month.

| Item | Forecast |
|---|---|
| Hire charge per day | £35 |
| Expected hire days in the month | 360 |
| Variable cost per hire day (charging, cleaning, insurance per ride) | £6 |
| Fixed costs per month (unit rent, loan repayment, website) | £6,800 |

1. Revenue = 360 × £35 = £12,600.
2. Total variable costs = 360 × £6 = £2,160.
3. Total costs = £2,160 + £6,800 = £8,960.
4. Profit = £12,600 − £8,960 = £3,640.

Now test the forecast, as a bank would. If wet weather cuts hire days to 220:

- Revenue = 220 × £35 = £7,700.
- Total costs = (220 × £6) + £6,800 = £1,320 + £6,800 = £8,120.
- Result = £7,700 − £8,120 = −£420, a loss of £420.

The fixed costs did not move, so a fall of 140 hire days turned a £3,640 profit into a £420 loss: a swing of £4,060, which is 140 × (£35 − £6). This shows why a lender reads the sales forecast first and asks what evidence supports it.

## Worked example 2: finding a missing figure

Questions sometimes give you the answer and ask for an input.

Figures invented. A plan shows revenue of £24,000, a profit of £5,500 and fixed costs of £8,000 for 1,500 units. What is the variable cost per unit?

1. Total costs = revenue − profit = £24,000 − £5,500 = £18,500.
2. Total variable costs = total costs − fixed costs = £18,500 − £8,000 = £10,500.
3. Variable cost per unit = £10,500 ÷ 1,500 = £7.

Work backwards one step at a time, and write each step down so that method marks are available if you slip.

## Benefits and drawbacks: making them specific

Generic points such as "it takes time" earn little unless applied. Turn each into a chain:

- **Benefit:** the plan makes the owner research demand → a weak idea is spotted before money is spent → the owner avoids losing savings or borrowed money.
- **Benefit:** the plan includes forecasts → a bank can judge the risk → a loan is more likely, possibly on better terms.
- **Drawback:** forecasts are estimates → if sales are lower than planned, as with Juniper Bikes in wet weather → the owner may borrow too much or spend on the wrong things.
- **Drawback:** writing a plan takes weeks → an owner keen to launch before a busy season may miss it → lost sales.

A balanced judgement usually says a plan is most valuable for a start-up that needs finance, and least valuable if it is written once and never updated.

## Exam technique

- **State or identify:** name a reason or section.
- **Explain:** point, then why, then a consequence for the business in the case.
- **Calculate:** show every step and give units. Label a negative answer as a loss.
- **Analyse:** chain benefits or drawbacks, using the case study's figures.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the plan's usefulness to a named reader, then decide. Comment on how reliable the forecasts are, since every figure in a new business's plan is an estimate.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a plan "guarantees success" or "removes risk". It reduces risk; it cannot remove it.
- Giving raising finance as the only purpose of planning.
- Multiplying fixed costs by quantity. Fixed costs are a single total.
- Forgetting that a fall in sales cuts revenue but leaves fixed costs unchanged.
- Treating forecasts as facts when evaluating.
- Writing a plan when asked about one. The specification says you will not be asked to write one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to write a business plan in the AQA GCSE Business exam?**
No. The specification says students will not be expected to write one, but you must understand its purpose, sections, benefits and drawbacks, and the financial terms it uses.

**Why do banks want to see a business plan?**
A new business has no trading history, so the plan's forecasts and market research are the main evidence the bank has that the loan can be repaid.

**How do I find total costs if I know revenue and profit?**
Subtract the profit from the revenue. Total costs = revenue − profit.
