These questions cover section 3.5.3 Making financial decisions: sources of finance of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [sources of finance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-sources-of-finance/) and the [sources of finance revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-sources-of-finance-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an internal source of finance? **[1]**

A: A bank overdraft

B: Retained profit

C: Venture capital

D: A share issue to the public

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes debt factoring? **[1]**

A: Borrowing a fixed sum from a bank over several years

B: Selling shares to the public

C: Selling invoices to a specialist firm in return for immediate cash

D: Delaying payments to suppliers

**3.** Define the term "venture capital". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage of using retained profit to fund expansion. **[3]**

**5.** Tindal Textiles has invoices outstanding of £120,000. A factor advances 75% immediately and charges a fee of 2% of the invoice value.

**(a)** Calculate the immediate advance. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the factor's fee. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the balance Tindal receives when the customers pay. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain **one** reason why debt factoring might suit Tindal. **[2]**

**6.** A bakery takes out a £250,000 loan at 6% interest to buy new ovens. The ovens are expected to add £22,000 a year to profit before interest.

**(a)** Calculate the annual interest. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the extra profit after interest. **[1]**

**(c)** The loan has a variable rate, and the rate rises to 9%. Calculate the effect on the extra profit. **[2]**

**7.** A company issues 80,000 shares at £1.50 each.

**(a)** Calculate the money raised. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why paying dividends on these shares is not a fixed commitment. **[1]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** reasons why a start-up might choose to raise money through crowd funding. **[6]**

**9.** Brightwater Brewery needs £2 million to open a second site. It is a private limited company. Assess whether it should use a bank loan or an issue of shares to raise the money. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse why an overdraft would be an unsuitable way to finance the purchase of a new warehouse. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** disadvantages for the founders of using venture capital. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the most suitable source of finance for a rapidly growing online retailer that needs to double its stock before the winter season. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Retained profit comes from within the business; the others come from outside. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. The business sells its trade receivables to a factor for most of their value at once. **[1]**

**3.** Money invested in a young, higher-risk business with growth potential [1] by specialist firms or wealthy individuals, in return for a share of the ownership (equity) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Retained profit is available only if the business has made enough profit [1], so a young or loss-making business may not have enough for a large project [1]. Using profit for expansion also means lower dividends for owners, and the money could have earned a return elsewhere (opportunity cost) [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 75% × £120,000 = **£90,000** [1].

**(b)** 2% × £120,000 = **£2,400** [1].

**(c)** £120,000 − £90,000 − £2,400 = **£27,600** [1].

**(d)** It gives cash quickly from sales already made, so Tindal can pay wages and suppliers without waiting for customers [1], without taking a loan or giving up ownership; the factor may also collect the debts, saving administration costs [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** £250,000 × 6% = **£15,000** [1].

**(b)** £22,000 − £15,000 = **£7,000** [1].

**(c)** New interest = £250,000 × 9% = £22,500 [1]; extra profit = £22,000 − £22,500 = **−£500**, so the ovens would cause a small loss [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** 80,000 × £1.50 = **£120,000** [1].

**(b)** Directors decide whether to pay a dividend, and may skip it in a poor year, unlike loan interest which must always be paid [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Reason 1: access to finance without banks [1]. A start-up with no trading record may be refused a loan [1], but a campaign can raise money from many small backers, so the idea can go ahead [1]. Reason 2: it tests demand and builds publicity [1]. If many people pledge money, this is evidence that customers want the product [1], and backers become early customers who tell others, helping marketing [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Loan: the owners keep full control [1], interest is a fixed cost that can be planned for [1]. But interest must be paid even if the new site is slow to make a profit [1], and the bank may demand security, putting assets at risk [1]. Shares: no repayment or interest, so there is less cash pressure while the site establishes itself [1], but existing owners would lose some control and share future profit [1]. Factors: how much existing borrowing the brewery has, how quickly profit from the new site is expected, and whether the owners want to share control [1]. Judgement: a loan is better if the brewery is profitable and the owners want control [1]; shares are better if borrowing is already high or the new site is risky [1]. **[9]**

**10.** An overdraft is short-term finance that the bank can withdraw at short notice [1], while a warehouse is a long-term asset that will earn returns over many years [1]. If the bank asked for repayment, the business might have to sell the warehouse or close [1]. Overdraft interest rates are usually higher than loan rates [1], so financing such a large purchase would be costly [1]. An overdraft limit is normally too small for such a large purchase [1]. A long-term loan or retained profit would be better. **[6]**

**11.** Disadvantage 1: loss of ownership and control [1]. The investor takes a share, such as 30%, of the company and may demand a seat on the board, so founders cannot make all decisions [1]. Disadvantage 2: pressure for returns and an exit [1]. Venture capitalists usually want to sell their stake within a few years, which may force the founders to sell the business or float it on the stock market earlier than they wish [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Overdraft: quick and flexible for stock bought before winter and repaid when sales come in [1]; but interest is high and the bank can withdraw it [1]. Debt factoring: releases cash tied up in customers' invoices [1]; but online retailers often receive payment at the time of sale, so there may be few invoices to factor [1]. Retained profit: no interest or loss of control [1]; but a fast-growing retailer may have little profit left to retain [1]. Share issue or venture capital: large sums without repayment pressure [1]; but ownership and control are diluted [1]. Bank loan: good for long-term growth but a seasonal stock purchase is short-term [1]. Factors: how quickly the stock turns into cash, the retailer's existing debt, and how much control the owners want to keep [1]. Judgement: a short-term source such as an overdraft or a short-term loan is the best fit for seasonal stock because it can be repaid from winter sales [1]; if growth is lasting, a mix with retained profit and share capital would support the longer term [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Begin with how long the money is needed for, then select the source whose repayment pattern matches it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating share capital as a loan that must be repaid.
- Recommending an overdraft for a long-term asset.
- Describing venture capital as free because there is no interest.
- Forgetting that retained profit has an opportunity cost.
- Listing sources without linking them to the case.
- Ignoring the effect of interest on profit.
