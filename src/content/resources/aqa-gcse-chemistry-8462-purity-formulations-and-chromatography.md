## 4.8.1.1 Pure substances

In chemistry, a **pure substance** is a **single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance**.

This is different from everyday language, where "pure" can mean that nothing has been added, such as pure milk
or pure orange juice. Those are mixtures in chemistry. The "pure" in chemistry is much stricter.

### Using melting and boiling points

**Pure elements and compounds melt and boil at specific temperatures.** For example pure water melts at 0 °C
and boils at 100 °C at normal pressure. A mixture or impure substance:

- **melts over a range of temperatures**, not at one sharp temperature
- usually has a **lower melting point** than the pure substance
- usually has a **higher boiling point** than the pure substance (and boils over a range).

You can compare measured values with **data book values** to decide whether a sample is pure.

### Worked example 1: melting point data

*A solid is known to melt at 122 °C when pure. A student heats three samples.*

| Sample | Melting range / °C |
|---|---|
| A | 122 to 122 |
| B | 115 to 120 |
| C | 118 to 124 |

- **A** melts at exactly 122 °C, so it is likely to be **pure**.
- **B** and **C** melt over a **range**, and B melts below 122 °C, so they are **impure**.

Even a small proportion of an impurity changes the melting point, which is why chemists use melting point
checks on medicines and other products.

## 4.8.1.2 Formulations

A **formulation** is a **mixture that has been designed as a useful product**. Many products are complex mixtures
in which each chemical has a **particular purpose**. Formulations are made by **mixing the components in
carefully measured quantities** so that the product has the **required properties**.

Examples named in the specification:

| Formulation | Example of a component and its purpose |
|---|---|
| Fuels | Additives that stop an engine knocking |
| Cleaning agents | Surfactants to lift grease, perfume for smell |
| Paints | Pigment for colour, solvent to make it flow, binder to make it stick |
| Medicines | Active ingredient to treat illness, filler to make a tablet |
| Alloys | A mixture of metals, for example brass, with chosen properties |
| Fertilisers | Compounds containing nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in set proportions |
| Foods | Preservatives, flavourings, emulsifiers |

You do not need to know the components of proprietary products. You must be able to **identify formulations from
information given**. Clues in a question: a list of ingredients with their purposes, a precise recipe, or a
description of a product with several substances in set amounts.

Formulations are not pure substances. A pure substance has a single melting point; a formulation melts over a
range.

## 4.8.1.3 Chromatography

**Chromatography** separates mixtures and can give information to help identify substances. It involves two
phases:

- the **stationary phase**, which does not move (for paper chromatography, the paper)
- the **mobile phase**, which moves through the stationary phase (a **solvent**).

Separation depends on the **distribution of substances between the two phases**. A substance that is more
soluble in the solvent and sticks less to the paper moves further up the paper. A substance that sticks more
strongly to the paper moves less.

### Required practical 6: paper chromatography

1. Draw a **pencil line** (the **origin**) about 1.5 to 2 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper. Use
   pencil because it does not dissolve in the solvent, whereas ink would run and add colours.
2. Place small **spots** of each sample on the line, with spacing between them. Dry and re-spot to build up
   concentration if needed.
3. Stand the paper in a beaker with a little **solvent** (water or ethanol) so that the solvent is **below the
   line**. If the spots are under the solvent, they would dissolve into it.
4. Cover the beaker with a lid so the solvent does not evaporate.
5. When the solvent is **near the top**, remove the paper and immediately mark the **solvent front** with a pencil.
6. Allow the chromatogram to dry. Measure the distances.

### Rf values

The **Rf value** is the ratio of the distance moved by the compound to the distance moved by the solvent:

**Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent**

- Distance of the substance is measured from the **origin** to the **centre of the spot**.
- Distance of the solvent is measured from the origin to the **solvent front**.
- The Rf value has **no units** and is always **less than 1**.
- The same compound has the same Rf in the same solvent. **Different compounds have different Rf values in
  different solvents**, so Rf can help to identify compounds.

### Worked example 2: calculating Rf

*A dye spot has moved 3.6 cm and the solvent front has moved 7.5 cm.*

```
Rf = 3.6 / 7.5 = 0.48
```

Answers are given to an appropriate number of significant figures. With data given to two significant figures,
give two: **0.48**.

### Worked example 3: identifying components

*A chromatogram has a solvent front at 8.0 cm. An ink X gives spots at 2.4 cm and 5.6 cm. Pure dyes P and Q give
single spots with Rf values 0.30 and 0.70.*

```
Rf of first spot  = 2.4 / 8.0 = 0.30  (matches P)
Rf of second spot = 5.6 / 8.0 = 0.70  (matches Q)
```

Ink X is a mixture containing dyes P and Q.

### Pure or impure?

- A **pure compound** produces a **single spot** in **all** solvents.
- A **mixture** may separate into **several spots** (depending on the solvent).
- If a sample gives one spot in one solvent, it might still be a mixture whose components happened to travel
  together. Test with a second solvent: a single spot in both is good evidence of purity.

### Worked example 4: finding a spot height

*A compound has an Rf of 0.35 in a solvent that has moved 7.2 cm. How far should the spot be from the origin?*

```
distance = Rf x solvent distance = 0.35 x 7.2 = 2.52 cm  (2.5 cm to 2 s.f.)
```

## Exam technique

- For "explain how chromatography separates", use the words **stationary phase**, **mobile phase** and
  **distribution between the phases**, and link to different solubility.
- Always state **pencil**, and that the **line is above the solvent level**.
- Show the Rf working line: distance of substance, distance of solvent, ratio.
- To say a substance is pure, state **one spot** and add that this is in **different solvents**.
- For melting data, compare with the **expected value** and mention the **range**.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring to the bottom or top of the spot rather than the centre.
- Using ink for the origin line.
- Letting the solvent level be above the origin line.
- Giving Rf with units, or a value above 1.
- Saying a formulation is a "pure" product just because it is a manufactured product.
- Saying a pure substance has a melting point in a range.

## Putting it together: a model extended answer

*A forensic scientist compares the ink on a ransom note with three pens using paper chromatography. Describe the
method and how the results are used.*

> A pencil line is drawn near the bottom of the chromatography paper. A small spot of ink from the note and a
> spot from each pen are placed on the line. The paper is stood in a beaker with a little solvent, with the
> solvent below the line, and a lid is put on. The solvent moves up the paper, carrying the dyes with it at
> different rates, because they are distributed differently between the stationary phase (paper) and the mobile
> phase (solvent). When the solvent is near the top, the paper is removed, the solvent front is marked and the
> paper is dried. The Rf value of each spot is calculated. If the ink from the note gives spots with the same
> Rf values as one pen in the same solvent, that pen is a likely match.
