## Balance of payments in one table (1a)

| Account | Contains | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Current | Goods, services, primary income, secondary income | "Balance of payments deficit" usually means this account |
| Capital | Small capital transfers | Often tiny |
| Financial | FDI, portfolio investment, other investment, reserves | Finances the current account |

Current account balance = (goods X - M) + (services X - M) + net primary income + net secondary income.
Current + capital + financial + net errors and omissions = 0 (capital inflows counted positive).

**Reminder.** Goods -30, services +18, primary income -6, secondary +2: current account = -30 + 18 - 6 + 2 = -16. With capital account +1 and no errors, the financial account must be +15.

## Causes (1b)

| Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|
| Fast growth, high income elasticity of imports | Slow domestic growth, weak import demand |
| Rising unit labour costs, poor quality | Strong competitiveness |
| High exchange rate | Low exchange rate |
| Narrow export base, commodity price fall | Resource or specialist export earnings |
| Interest and profit paid abroad | Net income earned from overseas assets |
| Low saving | High saving |

Distinguish a cyclical deficit (boom, likely to reverse) from a structural one (weak competitiveness, persists).

## Measures to reduce an imbalance (1c)

Method in steps:
1. Name the type: expenditure-reducing, expenditure-switching or supply-side.
2. Give the chain: policy → change in price or income → imports or exports → balance.
3. Give one cost and one condition for success.
4. Make a judgement: short-run versus long-run.

**Worked reminder: a tariff.** Country D imports 100 units at a world price of 8 cu (spending 800). A 20% tariff raises the home price to 9.6. Price elasticity of demand for imports is -1.5.

```
Quantity change = -1.5 × 20% = -30% → 70 units
Spending on imports at world price = 70 × 8 = 560 (saving of 240)
Tariff revenue = 70 × (9.6 - 8) = 112
Consumers pay 70 × 9.6 = 672
```

The trade deficit narrows by 240, but consumers pay more, and trading partners may retaliate. Tariffs may also breach trade-agreement commitments.

**Worked reminder: cutting incomes.** Country E needs imports to fall by 9. The marginal propensity to import is 0.20, so income must fall by 9 ÷ 0.20 = 45. With a multiplier of 3, spending must be cut by 45 ÷ 3 = 15. The price of fixing the deficit this way is lost output and jobs, which is why supply-side policy is the lasting fix.

**Chains to learn.**
- Deficit by demand: higher incomes → more imports (via income elasticity of demand for imports) → larger trade deficit.
- Deficit by cost: wages grow faster than productivity → ULC rises → export prices rise → exports fall, imports rise.
- Fix by switching: lower exchange rate → cheaper exports abroad, dearer imports → balance improves if demand is responsive enough.
- Fix by supply side: training and investment → productivity up → ULC down → competitiveness and balance improve over time.

## Global imbalances (1d)

- Persistent surpluses abroad are the other side of deficits at home.
- Deficit countries: growing external liabilities, vulnerability to capital flight, exchange-rate pressure, and cuts in spending that slow growth.
- Surplus countries: weak spending may depress world demand; risk that debtors default.
- World-level risks: protectionism, competitive depreciation, financial instability.
- Evaluate: size relative to GDP, what the borrowing finances, how fast liabilities grow compared with national income.

**Reminder.** If the deficit is 5% of GDP and nominal GDP grows 4%, liabilities tend towards 5 ÷ 4 = 125% of GDP.

## Measuring competitiveness (3a)

| Measure | Formula | Better when |
|---|---|---|
| Output per worker | Output ÷ workers | Higher |
| Relative productivity | Own ÷ rival output per worker × 100 | Above 100 |
| Unit labour cost | Total labour cost ÷ output | Lower |
| Relative ULC | Own ULC ÷ rival ULC, common currency | Falling |
| Relative export prices | Own index ÷ rival index, common currency | Falling |

**Worked reminders.**
- Firm F: labour cost 2,400,000 for 80,000 units = 30 per unit. Next year labour cost 2,520,000 (+5%) for 82,000 units (+2.5%) = 30.73 per unit, +2.4%.
- Export price indices: Country A 108, Country B 102 (same currency terms). Relative price = 108 ÷ 102 × 100 = 105.9, so A is 5.9% dearer. If A's currency rises 3%, A's index in B's currency becomes 108 × 1.03 = 111.2, and the relative price is 111.2 ÷ 102 × 100 = 109.1.

**Worked reminder: productivity growth.** Output per worker rises from 40 to 42 in Country G: growth = 2 ÷ 40 = 5%. If hourly pay rises 5%, ULC is unchanged: 1.05 ÷ 1.05 = 1. Pay can rise as fast as productivity without harming competitiveness. If rivals' ULC fall, though, relative ULC still rise.

## Factors and measures (3b, 3c)

| Factor | Policy response | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity, human capital | Education and training | Time lag, cost |
| Capital and technology | Investment incentives | Cost; deadweight |
| Regulations, costs | Privatisation, deregulation | Monopoly, job losses |
| Exchange rate | Measures to lower it | Inflation, retaliation |
| Cheap inputs, competition | Trade liberalisation | Adjustment costs |
| Infrastructure, non-price factors | Public investment; support for design and innovation | Cost; slow |

## Significance (3d)

- Competitive economy: export-led growth, more jobs, smaller deficit, FDI inflows, rising incomes, stable currency.
- Uncompetitive economy: persistent deficit, job losses in traded sectors, weak growth, lower FDI, falling currency, rising foreign debt.
- Evaluate: competition based on low pay may keep living standards low; a high-value, high-price strategy can be competitive.

## Evaluation toolkit

Use these questions to turn a chain into a judgement:
- **Time:** does the policy act within months (exchange rate, tariffs) or years (education, infrastructure)?
- **Size:** is the deficit small relative to GDP, or large and growing?
- **Cause:** is the problem cyclical, structural, or a one-off such as a commodity price fall?
- **Cost:** what is given up (growth, jobs, living standards, government spending)?
- **Side effects:** inflation, retaliation, inequality, regional job losses.
- **Context:** how open is the economy and how responsive are exports and imports to price and income?

A strong conclusion picks the policy mix that suits the cause, and says what would change the judgement.

## Must-know distinctions

- Relative productivity (level) versus productivity growth (rate of change).
- Wage growth versus unit labour cost growth: ULC rises only if wages grow faster than productivity.
- Expenditure-switching versus expenditure-reducing.
- Price versus non-price competitiveness.
- Current account versus the whole balance of payments.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the two accounts other than the current account.
2. Goods -30, services +18, primary income -6, secondary +2: find the current account.
3. With capital account +1 and no errors, what is the financial account?
4. Define unit labour cost.
5. Output per worker is 50 in A and 40 in B. Give A's relative productivity.
6. Wages +4%, productivity +1%: find the percentage change in unit labour cost to 2 decimal places.
7. State two expenditure-switching measures.
8. A surplus of 36 in an economy with GDP of 600: express it as a percentage of GDP.
9. Give two non-price factors of competitiveness.
10. Why might a lower exchange rate fail to improve competitiveness for long?
11. Give one problem of international uncompetitiveness.

### Answers

1. Capital account and financial account.
2. -16.
3. +15 (the three accounts sum to zero).
4. Total labour cost divided by output.
5. 50 ÷ 40 × 100 = 125%.
6. 1.04 ÷ 1.01 = 1.0297, so +2.97%.
7. Any two: devaluation or depreciation policy, tariffs, quotas, subsidies to exporters.
8. 6.0%.
9. Any two: quality, design, reliability, delivery, after-sales service, branding, innovation.
10. Dearer imports raise costs and inflation, so wages may rise and cancel the gain.
11. Any one: persistent deficit, unemployment in traded industries, slow growth, lower FDI.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the capital account" when the question asks about capital and financial accounts.
- Dividing the wrong way round when calculating relative measures.
- Comparing ULC or export prices without allowing for the exchange rate.
- Saying a deficit is always harmful without asking what the borrowing pays for.
- Listing policies without a chain of reasoning to the balance.
- Offering no evaluation (time lag, cost, side effects, retaliation).
- Ignoring the context given in the question, such as whether the data show a cyclical or a structural problem.
- Mixing up the direction of a relative index: above 100 means the country is dearer or more productive, depending on the measure, so say which.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three accounts of the balance of payments?**
The current account records trade in goods and services, income and transfers. The capital account records small transfers of capital, and the financial account records cross-border investment and lending.

**Is a current account deficit always harmful?**
Not always. It depends on what the borrowing pays for and whether the deficit is cyclical, which may reverse when growth slows, or structural, which is harder to fix.

**What does a relative index above 100 mean?**
It depends on the measure. For relative export prices or unit labour costs, above 100 means the country is dearer than its rivals; for relative productivity, it means the country is more productive.

**What is the difference between expenditure-reducing and expenditure-switching policies?**
Expenditure-reducing policies, such as higher taxes or interest rates, cut incomes and so spending on imports. Expenditure-switching policies, such as a lower exchange rate or tariffs, shift spending from imports to domestic goods.
