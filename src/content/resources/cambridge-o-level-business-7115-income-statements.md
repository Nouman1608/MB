This guide goes deeper into section **5.3 Income statements** of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. The section has two parts:

- **5.3.1 What profit is and why it is important**: how a profit is made, the importance of profit to private sector businesses (for example as a reward for risk-taking and enterprise, and as a source of finance), and the difference between profit and cash.
- **5.3.2 Income statements**: the main features (for example revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, profit and retained profit), and using simple income statements in decision-making based on profit calculations.

## How a profit is made

Profit is what is left when all the costs of a period are taken away from the revenue of that period. A business can increase profit in three basic ways:

1. **Sell more**, as long as each extra sale brings in more than it costs.
2. **Raise the price**, as long as customers do not buy so much less that revenue falls.
3. **Cut costs**, either the direct cost of each item (cheaper materials, less waste) or overheads (cheaper premises, less spending on advertising), as long as quality and sales do not suffer.

Each route has a catch, and exam questions are often built around that catch.

## Why profit matters to a private sector business

- **Reward for risk-taking and enterprise.** Owners invest money that they could lose. Profit is their return, and the chance of profit is the reason enterprise happens at all.
- **Source of finance.** Profit kept in the business (retained profit) can pay for equipment, stock or expansion without interest or giving away ownership.
- **A measure of success.** Owners and managers use profit to judge whether decisions worked.
- **Attracting finance from others.** Lenders and new investors are more willing to provide money to a business with a record of profit.

Note that the syllabus says "private sector". A public sector organisation may aim to provide a service rather than to make a profit.

## Profit and cash are not the same

Profit is a calculation that matches revenue and costs to a period. Cash is the money the business actually holds. They differ because:

- **sales on credit** count as revenue when the sale is made, but the cash arrives later
- **buying inventory** uses cash now, but it only becomes a cost of sales when the goods are sold
- **buying non-current assets** such as machinery uses a lot of cash at once, but it is not an expense in the income statement in the same way
- **loans** bring in cash but are not revenue, and repaying them uses cash but is not a cost.

So a business can be profitable and still run out of cash, and a business with plenty of cash in the bank can be making a loss.

## The main features, line by line

| Line | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Revenue | The value of sales in the period (price × quantity sold) |
| Cost of sales | The direct cost of the goods actually sold |
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales: what trading earns before overheads |
| Expenses | Overheads such as rent, salaries, advertising, insurance |
| Profit | Gross profit − expenses |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business after tax and payments to owners |

Retained profit links the income statement to the statement of financial position, because it is added to the owners' funds and can be used to finance the business.

## Worked example 1: why did profit fall?

Tarnwell Tiles, income statements for two years ($):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 250,000 | 300,000 |
| Cost of sales | (150,000) | (195,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **100,000** | **105,000** |
| Expenses | (70,000) | (81,000) |
| **Profit** | **30,000** | **24,000** |
| Tax | (6,000) | (4,800) |
| Dividends to owners | (10,000) | (10,000) |
| **Retained profit** | **14,000** | **9,200** |

**Step 1, the headline.** Revenue rose by 50,000, which is 50,000 ÷ 250,000 = 20%. Profit fell by 6,000, which is 6,000 ÷ 30,000 = 20%. Higher sales did not bring higher profit.

**Step 2, trace the cause.** Cost of sales rose by 45,000, which is 30%, faster than revenue. Gross profit rose by only 5,000. Possible reasons: suppliers raised prices, or Tarnwell cut its prices to win the extra sales. Expenses also rose by 11,000, perhaps for extra staff or advertising.

**Step 3, the consequence.** The owners kept dividends the same, so retained profit fell from 14,000 to 9,200. There is less internal finance for next year.

**Step 4, the decision.** Tarnwell should find out why cost of sales rose so fast. If the cause is supplier prices, it could look for a cheaper supplier or raise its own prices. If it cut prices to grow, it must judge whether the extra sales are worth the lower gross profit on each tile.

The skill being tested is following the figures down the statement to the line where the problem starts.

## Worked example 2: should a branch close?

Pellow Pharmacy has two branches ($):

| | East | West |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 180,000 | 320,000 |
| Cost of sales | (117,000) | (192,000) |
| Gross profit | 63,000 | 128,000 |
| Expenses | (70,000) | (88,000) |
| Profit | (7,000) loss | 40,000 |

Total profit is 40,000 − 7,000 = 33,000. The owner wants to close East. But East's expenses include 12,000 of costs (the accountant and a share of the delivery van) that will still be paid if it closes.

If East closes: profit = 40,000 − 12,000 = 28,000. Closing East **reduces** total profit by 5,000, because East's gross profit of 63,000 more than covers the 58,000 of costs that would actually be saved.

A good answer then looks beyond the numbers: could East's rent be renegotiated, would East's customers move to West, and how would staff and the local community be affected?

## Limits of income statements in decisions

- They show the **past**; decisions are about the future.
- They do not show **cash**, so a profitable plan may still be unaffordable.
- They do not show **non-financial** factors such as staff morale, reputation or quality.
- A forecast built from them depends on assumptions, such as customers' reaction to a price change.

## Exam technique for the 7115 command words

- **Define**: give the precise meaning, for example "gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales".
- **Calculate**: show the working, label the line you are calculating and give the unit.
- **Explain**: link the figure to a cause and to an effect on the business.
- **Consider / Justify**: use profit figures to support a decision, then add what the figures do not show.

## Common mistakes

- Saying revenue is profit, or that higher revenue must mean higher profit.
- Mixing up gross profit and profit.
- Treating retained profit as cash in the bank. It may already have been spent on assets.
- Claiming a profitable business cannot have cash problems.
- Deciding to close a loss-making part of a business without asking which costs would really disappear.
- Trying to construct a full statement when the question only asks for one line.
