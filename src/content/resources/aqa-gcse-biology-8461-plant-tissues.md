## What the specification asks for

Students should be able to "explain how the structures of plant tissues are related to their functions." The tissues listed are:

- epidermal tissues;
- palisade mesophyll;
- spongy mesophyll;
- xylem and phloem;
- meristem tissue found at the growing tips of shoots and roots.

"The leaf is a plant organ. Knowledge limited to epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll, xylem and phloem, and guard cells surrounding stomata." The skills column names AT 7: "Observation and drawing of a transverse section of leaf."

## The leaf as an organ

An organ is an aggregation of tissues performing a specific function. The leaf's main function is **photosynthesis**, and it also carries out **gas exchange**. Several tissues each contribute one part of the job, which is why the leaf is an organ rather than a tissue.

From top to bottom, a transverse section of a leaf shows:

```
upper epidermis
palisade mesophyll
spongy mesophyll (with a vein: xylem and phloem)
lower epidermis (with stomata, each between two guard cells)
```

## Structure and function, tissue by tissue

Use the pattern **structure → so → function** for every tissue.

### Epidermal tissues

- The **upper epidermis** is a single layer of cells that are **transparent**, so light passes through to the photosynthesising cells below. It is covered by a waxy layer that reduces water loss.
- The **lower epidermis** contains most of the **stomata**. Each stoma is surrounded by two **guard cells**, which open and close it, controlling the entry of carbon dioxide and the loss of water vapour and oxygen.

### Palisade mesophyll

- Cells are **tall and tightly packed** in a layer just under the upper epidermis, so a large number of cells receive the most light.
- Each cell contains **many chloroplasts**, so it can absorb a lot of light and carry out photosynthesis quickly.

### Spongy mesophyll

- Cells are **irregular and loosely packed**, with **large air spaces** between them.
- The air spaces let carbon dioxide diffuse from the stomata to the palisade cells and oxygen diffuse out, and the cells' surfaces give a **large surface area** for gas exchange.

### Xylem

- Made of **hollow tubes strengthened by lignin**.
- Hollow tubes with no cell contents let water and mineral ions flow up from the roots to the leaves; lignin makes the walls strong, so the tubes do not collapse and they help support the plant.

### Phloem

- Made of **tubes of elongated cells**, with **pores in the end walls**.
- Cell sap carrying dissolved sugars can move from one phloem cell to the next through the pores, transporting sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant. The detailed structure of phloem is not required.

### Meristem

- Found at the **growing tips of shoots and roots**.
- Its cells **divide by mitosis** and can **differentiate into many types of plant cell**, so the plant can grow longer shoots and roots and produce new tissues throughout its life.

## How the tissues work together in photosynthesis

Photosynthesis needs light, carbon dioxide and water, and produces glucose and oxygen. Follow each substance:

1. **Light** passes through the transparent upper epidermis and is absorbed by chloroplasts in the palisade mesophyll.
2. **Carbon dioxide** enters through open stomata in the lower epidermis and diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll to the palisade cells.
3. **Water** arrives in the xylem of the leaf veins and moves into the mesophyll cells.
4. **Oxygen** diffuses out through the air spaces and stomata.
5. **Sugars** made in the leaf are carried away in the phloem.

A six-mark question on how the leaf is adapted for photosynthesis is answered well by this sequence, with a structural feature named at every step.

## Measuring tissue layers from a micrograph

Use actual size = image size ÷ magnification (from Topic 1).

### Worked example 1

A micrograph of a leaf section at × 150 shows the palisade layer as 18 mm deep and the whole leaf as 45 mm deep.

```
Palisade layer: 18 / 150 = 0.12 mm = 120 um
Whole leaf:     45 / 150 = 0.30 mm = 300 um
Palisade as a percentage of thickness = 0.12 / 0.30 x 100 = 40%
```

## Applying the ideas to unfamiliar leaves

### Worked example 2: sun and shade leaves

A student compares leaves from the top of a tree, in full sun, with leaves from low, shaded branches (invented data).

| Measurement | Sun leaf | Shade leaf |
|---|---|---|
| Palisade layer thickness (µm) | 120 | 60 |
| Leaf surface area (cm²) | 30 | 55 |

How to explain the differences:

- The sun leaf has a thicker palisade layer: there is plenty of light, so more layers of chloroplast-filled cells can use it.
- The shade leaf has a larger surface area: it receives less light per cm², so a larger area absorbs more light in total.

Notice the method: state the difference using the data, then link it to the function of the tissue involved. Questions like this test the same structure-and-function skill in a context you have not met.

## AT 7: drawing a transverse section of a leaf

- Observe a prepared slide at low power first so the whole section is visible.
- Draw the **layers in the correct order and proportions**; you are drawing tissues, so you need not draw every cell.
- Use **pencil**, **clear continuous lines** and **no shading**.
- Label upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, xylem, phloem, lower epidermis, a stoma and guard cells with **ruled label lines** that touch the structure.
- Add a title and the magnification.

## Exam technique

- **Name** a tissue from a description or diagram: use the specification's names.
- **Explain** how a tissue is adapted: one structure plus its effect on its function; two or three such pairs for higher marks.
- **Suggest** differences in an unfamiliar leaf: use data, then link to light, gas exchange or water.
- **Calculate**: magnification, actual size and percentages, with units.

## Common errors

- Saying the leaf is a tissue.
- Saying palisade cells have chloroplasts "to make food" without linking to absorbing light.
- Saying stomata are in the palisade layer; most are in the lower epidermis.
- Calling xylem "living tubes" or giving phloem lignin.
- Putting meristem in the leaf; it is at the growing tips of shoots and roots.
- Shading or sketching individual cells in a tissue-level drawing.
