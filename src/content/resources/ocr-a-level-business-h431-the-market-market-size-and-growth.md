## Defining the market

A **market** is any arrangement in which buyers and sellers exchange a good or service, whether in a physical place or online. How a market is **defined** matters, because it changes both the size of the market and a firm's share of it.

**Worked example 1: one business, three markets.** Pennick Coffee runs six independent coffee shops in a city, with sales of £2.4 million a year.

| Market definition | Market size | Pennick's share |
|---|---|---|
| All hot drinks bought outside the home in the city | £60 million | 2.4 ÷ 60 × 100 = **4%** |
| Coffee shops in the city | £40 million | 2.4 ÷ 40 × 100 = **6%** |
| Independent coffee shops in the city | £9.6 million | 2.4 ÷ 9.6 × 100 = **25%** |

Pennick is a small player in the broad market and a leading one in the narrow one. A business may quote the narrow definition to sound strong, while a lender or investor may look at the broader one to judge the competition. When you analyse share, state the definition used and ask whether it is the one that matters for the decision. If customers will happily switch between an independent shop and a national chain, the broader definition gives the truer picture of the competition Pennick faces.

## Competition

**Competition** is rivalry between businesses to win the same customers. It includes:

- **direct competitors**, selling similar products to the same customers (another coffee shop);
- **indirect competitors**, selling different products that meet the same need (a bakery café or a vending machine).

The intensity of competition depends on the number and size of rivals, how similar their products are, how easily customers can compare and switch, and how easily new firms can enter.

## Value and volume

**Market size** is the total sales in a market over a period, measured by **value** (sales revenue) or **volume** (units sold). **Market growth** is the percentage change in market size over a period: change in size ÷ original size × 100.

Value and volume can move in opposite directions.

**Worked example 2.** The UK market for a type of garden tool:

| | Last year | This year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume (units) | 12.0 million | 11.4 million | −5% |
| Average price | £2.50 | £2.75 | +10% |
| Value | £30.0 million | £31.35 million | +4.5% |

Value = volume × average price: 11.4 million × £2.75 = £31.35 million. Value growth = (31.35 − 30.0) ÷ 30.0 × 100 = 4.5%.

By value the market is growing; by volume it is shrinking. A business planning production capacity should look at volume, because it will make fewer units in total. A business judging revenue potential will look at value. Rising prices can also come from general inflation, so value growth may overstate how much the market is really expanding.

## Why size and growth matter

- **Revenue potential.** The market's size limits how much a business can sell without taking customers from rivals.
- **Viability of fixed costs.** A large market can support large fixed costs, such as a factory or national advertising, spread over many sales. A small market may suit only small firms or niche products.
- **Attractiveness to entrants.** Large, growing markets draw new competitors, which can reduce prices and margins later.
- **Strategy.** In a **growing** market a business can grow without anyone losing sales, so rivals react less aggressively. In a **static or shrinking** market, growth must come from rivals, which invites retaliation.

### Worked example 3: is the business really doing well?

Greystone Pet Foods' sales rose from £18 million to £20.16 million. The market grew from £120 million to £138 million.

- Greystone's sales growth = (20.16 − 18) ÷ 18 × 100 = **12%**.
- Market growth = (138 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **15%**.
- Share last year = 18 ÷ 120 × 100 = **15.0%**; this year = 20.16 ÷ 138 × 100 = **14.6%**.

Greystone's sales grew strongly, but slower than the market, so it **lost** market share. To keep a 15% share, sales would have needed to be 0.15 × 138 = £20.7 million. Rivals have captured more of the growth. The figures do not say why: perhaps Greystone lacked capacity, or a rival launched a better product. A manager who looked only at the 12% rise would miss the warning.

## Raising market share

The approach that works best depends on the market.

| Situation | Most suitable approaches | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Growing market | Wider distribution, extra capacity, promotion | Share can rise by capturing new customers without provoking rivals |
| Static market, similar products | Differentiation, service, loyalty schemes | Price cuts are easily matched and start price wars |
| Fragmented market of many small firms | Acquiring rivals | Quick gains; integration is easier with small firms |
| Oligopoly with a few large rivals | Branding, innovation | Price moves are watched and matched |

Evaluate by weighing cost, speed, how easily rivals can copy, and the effect on profit. A rise in share is only worthwhile if it is profitable: buying share with deep price cuts can leave a business bigger but poorer.

## Market structure

| Feature | Monopoly | Oligopoly | Monopolistic competition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | One firm dominates | A few large firms dominate | Many firms |
| Barriers to entry | Very high | High | Low |
| Products | No close substitutes | Similar, often branded | Differentiated |
| Interdependence | Little | High | Low |

A common way to measure how concentrated a market is, which the specification does not name, is a **concentration ratio**: the combined share of the largest few firms. If the four largest firms in a £500 million market sell £180 million, £110 million, £70 million and £40 million, their combined share is 400 ÷ 500 × 100 = **80%**, a sign of oligopoly.

### Structure and decision making power

| Decision | Monopoly | Oligopoly | Monopolistic competition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | High power: few substitutes, limited by what customers will pay and any regulation | Constrained: rivals may match cuts but not rises | Low: close substitutes keep prices near rivals' |
| Output | Can restrict output to support price | Set with an eye on rivals' capacity | Set by own demand |
| Product and promotion | Less pressure to innovate | Heavy branding and advertising; non-price competition | Differentiation through service, location, style |

The link to analyse is that **decision making power depends on how easily customers can switch and how easily rivals can enter**. A business in monopolistic competition may still gain some power locally, for example a café that is the only one in a village, so the structure of the relevant market, not the industry label, decides.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a market share without saying how the market is defined.
- Confusing value growth with volume growth.
- Treating a rise in sales as a rise in market share.
- Recommending a price cut to raise share in an oligopoly without considering rivals' reactions.
- Describing market structures without linking them to the firm's power over price and output.

## Exam technique

"Explain the importance of market size" needs the point and its effect on a decision. "Evaluate how a business could increase its market share" needs at least two methods, judged against the market's growth and structure, with a recommendation. "Analyse the relationship" needs a chain of reasoning from a feature of the structure to the firm's power.
