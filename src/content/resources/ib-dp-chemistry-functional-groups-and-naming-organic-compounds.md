## Writing organic formulas (3.2.1)

| Formula type | What it shows | 1,4-dibromobutane |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical | Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms | C₂H₄Br |
| Molecular | Atoms of each element actually present in one molecule | C₄H₈Br₂ |
| Full structural | Every atom and every bond | see below |
| Condensed structural | Atoms grouped carbon by carbon, no bond lines | BrCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂Br |
| Skeletal | Carbon chain as a zigzag; H atoms on carbon left out | Br at each end of a four-vertex zigzag |
| Stereochemical | 3D shape, with wedges for bonds towards you and dashes for bonds away | used when shape matters |

You should recognise stereochemical formulas, but the guide does not expect you to draw them unless a question asks.

**Skeletal rules.** Each corner of the zigzag, and each free end of a line, stands for a carbon atom. Each carbon has enough hydrogen atoms to make four bonds, but they are not drawn. Atoms other than carbon and hydrogen are always written in, and so is any hydrogen bonded to them (O–H, N–H).

**3D models.** A kit or modelling software shows the tetrahedral arrangement around each saturated carbon, which a flat drawing hides.

### Worked example 1: from a skeletal formula to the others

A skeletal formula is an unbranched zigzag with four corners and a Br atom at each end. Give the other formulas.

Step 1: the ends are Br atoms, so only the four corners are carbons.

Step 2: each carbon already has two bonds (to C or Br), so each needs two H atoms.

```
    H   H   H   H
    |   |   |   |
Br––C – C – C – C––Br
    |   |   |   |
    H   H   H   H
```

Step 3: condensed formula **BrCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂Br**; molecular formula **C₄H₈Br₂**.

Step 4: the ratio 4 : 8 : 2 has a common factor of 2, so the empirical formula is **C₂H₄Br**.

## Functional groups (3.2.2)

Think of a **functional group** as the reactive region of a molecule: it decides how the compound typically reacts and shapes many of its physical properties. You need these nine.

| Functional group | Structure | Found in |
|---|---|---|
| halogeno | –X (X = F, Cl, Br, I) | halogenoalkanes |
| hydroxyl | –OH | alcohols |
| carbonyl | C=O | aldehydes (–CHO at a chain end) and ketones (C=O inside the chain) |
| carboxyl | –COOH | carboxylic acids |
| alkoxy | –O–R | ethers |
| amino | –NH₂ | amines |
| amido | –CONH₂ (or –CONH– when N carries a carbon group) | amides |
| ester | –COO–R | esters |
| phenyl | –C₆H₅, a benzene ring as a substituent | aromatic compounds |

**Saturated** compounds have only single carbon–carbon bonds. **Unsaturated** compounds have at least one C=C or C≡C bond.

The O–H in alcohols and carboxylic acids allows hydrogen bonding, which raises boiling points and helps small members mix with water. A linking question uses functional group reactivity to plan routes: ethene can become ethanol, which can be oxidised to ethanoic acid, one group change per step.

### Worked example 2: spotting groups in a real molecule

The sweetener aspartame has the condensed formula HOOC–CH₂–CH(NH₂)–CO–NH–CH(CH₂C₆H₅)–COO–CH₃. Name every functional group.

1. HOOC– is a **carboxyl** group.
2. –NH₂ on the CH is an **amino** group.
3. –CO–NH– is an **amido** group.
4. –C₆H₅ is a **phenyl** group.
5. –COO–CH₃ is an **ester** group.

The C=O joined to the nitrogen is what makes –CO–NH– amido, not amino.

## Homologous series (3.2.3)

Compounds in a **homologous series** share one functional group, and neighbouring members differ by a CH₂ unit. Members share a general formula, react in similar ways and show a gradual change in physical properties.

| Series | Functional group | General formula | Name ending or prefix | First member |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| alkanes | none (C–C only) | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | -ane | methane |
| alkenes | C=C | CₙH₂ₙ | -ene | ethene |
| alkynes | C≡C | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | -yne | ethyne |
| halogenoalkanes | halogeno | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X | chloro-, bromo- etc. | chloromethane |
| alcohols | hydroxyl | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | -ol | methanol |
| aldehydes | carbonyl, chain end | CₙH₂ₙO | -al | methanal |
| ketones | carbonyl, inside chain | CₙH₂ₙO | -one | propanone |
| carboxylic acids | carboxyl | CₙH₂ₙO₂ | -oic acid | methanoic acid |
| ethers | alkoxy | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O | alkoxyalkane | methoxymethane |
| amines | amino | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁NH₂ | -amine | methanamine |
| amides | amido | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁CONH₂ | -amide | methanamide |
| esters | ester | CₙH₂ₙO₂ | -oate | methyl methanoate |

Alcohols and ethers, aldehydes and ketones, and acids and esters share general formulas, so a molecular formula alone cannot fix the series.

### Worked example 3: finding a member from its molar mass

An ester has a molar mass of 116.18 g mol⁻¹. Find its molecular formula. Use Ar values C = 12.01, H = 1.01, O = 16.00.

Esters fit CₙH₂ₙO₂. One CH₂ unit has mass 12.01 + 2(1.01) = 14.03.

14.03n + 2(16.00) = 116.18, so 14.03n = 84.18 and n = 6.

The ester is **C₆H₁₂O₂**.

## Trends in melting and boiling points (3.2.4)

Each member of a series has one more CH₂ than the last, so its molecules have more electrons and a larger surface. London (dispersion) forces get stronger, more energy is needed to separate the molecules, and boiling points rise steadily.

| Alcohol | Boiling point / K |
|---|---|
| methanol | 338 |
| ethanol | 352 |
| propan-1-ol | 370 |
| butan-1-ol | 391 |
| pentan-1-ol | 411 |

All five alcohols hydrogen bond through –OH. That contribution stays about the same along the series, so the rise comes from the growing London forces.

Melting points also rise, but less smoothly, because they also depend on how well molecules pack in the solid. Two other factors matter:

- **Branching.** A branched isomer is more compact, so molecules touch over a smaller area, London forces are weaker and the boiling point is lower.
- **Functional group.** Hydrogen bonding (alcohols, carboxylic acids) gives higher boiling points than dipole–dipole forces (aldehydes, ketones), which in turn beat London forces alone (alkanes) for molecules with similar numbers of electrons.

### Worked example 4: predicting the next member

Estimate the boiling point of hexan-1-ol from the table.

The last three gaps are 18 K, 21 K and 20 K, about 20 K per CH₂. 411 + 20 = 431 K, so estimate **about 430 K**.

## IUPAC naming (3.2.5)

IUPAC nomenclature is the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry's rule set for systematic names. You name branched and straight-chain compounds with a parent chain of up to six carbons, at most one C=C or C≡C, and one type of functional group: halogeno, hydroxyl, carbonyl or carboxyl.

**Method**

1. Pick the longest chain of carbons that includes the functional group (and any multiple bond). Its length gives the stem: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex.
2. Add the ending: -ane, -ene or -yne for the chain; then -ol, -al, -one or -oic acid for the group. Halogens go in front as fluoro, chloro, bromo or iodo.
3. Number the chain so the suffix group gets the lowest number. Next give the multiple bond the lowest number, then the prefixes.
4. Name branches as methyl or ethyl. Use di- and tri- for repeats. List prefixes in alphabetical order, ignoring di- and tri-.
5. Separate numbers from each other with commas, and numbers from words with hyphens.

The –CHO and –COOH carbons are always carbon 1, so they need no number.

### Worked example 5: naming six compounds

| Condensed formula | Reasoning | Name |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃CH(CH₃)C(CH₃)₂CH₂CH₃ | Left gives 2,3,3; right gives 3,3,4; first difference decides | **2,3,3-trimethylpentane** |
| CH₃C(Cl)(CH₃)CH(Cl)CH₃ | {2,2,3} beats {2,3,3}; chloro before methyl | **2,3-dichloro-2-methylbutane** |
| (CH₃)₂CHCOCH₂CH₃ | C=O is carbon 3 either way; methyl gets 2, not 4 | **2-methylpentan-3-one** |
| CH₃CH₂CH(C₂H₅)CHO | Longest chain through CHO has four carbons | **2-ethylbutanal** |
| CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH(OH)CH₃ | Number from the OH end | **hex-4-en-2-ol** |
| CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂COOH | COOH carbon is 1 | **3-methylpentanoic acid** |

### Worked example 6: from a name to a structure

Draw 2-bromo-3-methylbut-2-ene as a condensed formula.

Four carbons with C=C between carbons 2 and 3; Br on carbon 2 and CH₃ on carbon 3: **CH₃C(Br)=C(CH₃)CH₃**. Either numbering gives {2,3}, so the prefix first in the alphabet (bromo) takes the lower number.

## Structural isomers (3.2.6)

**Structural isomers** have the same molecular formula but different connectivity:

- **Branched and straight-chain (chain) isomers**: the carbon skeleton differs.
- **Position isomers**: the same group sits on a different carbon.
- **Functional group isomers**: the isomers belong to different series.

### Worked example 7: five isomers of C₄H₈O

Butanal (CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO), 2-methylpropanal ((CH₃)₂CHCHO), butan-2-one (CH₃COCH₂CH₃), but-3-en-1-ol (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂OH) and but-3-en-2-ol (CH₂=CHCH(OH)CH₃) all have the formula C₄H₈O.

- Butanal and 2-methylpropanal: **chain** isomers.
- But-3-en-1-ol and but-3-en-2-ol: **position** isomers (the OH moves).
- Butanal and butan-2-one: **functional group** isomers (aldehyde and ketone).

Propan-1-ol and methoxyethane (both C₃H₈O) are another functional group pair: alcohol and ether.

### Primary, secondary and tertiary

For **alcohols** and **halogenoalkanes**, look at the carbon carrying the –OH or halogen and count the carbon atoms bonded to it: one is primary, two is secondary, three is tertiary.

For **amines**, count the carbon atoms bonded to the **nitrogen** instead.

### Worked example 8: classifying

| Compound | What to count | Class |
|---|---|---|
| pentan-1-ol | C–OH carbon has 1 carbon neighbour | primary |
| pentan-3-ol | 2 carbon neighbours | secondary |
| 2-methylbutan-2-ol | 3 carbon neighbours | tertiary |
| 1-iodo-2,2-dimethylpropane | C–I carbon has 1 carbon neighbour | primary |
| butan-2-amine, CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₂CH₃ | N bonded to 1 carbon | primary amine |
| N,N-dimethylethanamine, (CH₃)₂NCH₂CH₃ | N bonded to 3 carbons | tertiary amine |

Butan-2-amine is the trap: its NH₂ carbon has two carbon neighbours, but only the nitrogen counts.

**HL link (AHL Structure 2.2).** A linking question asks why only three dibromobenzene isomers supports the modern benzene model. Alternating single and double bonds would allow two different 1,2-isomers; only one exists, so all six ring bonds are equal.

## Common errors

- Choosing the longest chain without checking it contains the functional group.
- Giving the halogen or methyl the low number when an -ol or -one is present.
- Naming 1-methylpentan-2-ol: a methyl on carbon 1 extends the chain, giving hexan-3-ol.
- Classifying amines by the carbon, like alcohols.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a functional group?**
A functional group is the reactive region of a molecule that decides how a compound typically reacts and shapes its physical properties. Examples include the hydroxyl group in alcohols, the carbonyl group in aldehydes and ketones, and the carboxyl group in carboxylic acids.

**What is the difference between an amino group and an amido group?**
An amino group is –NH₂ on a carbon atom, while an amido group, –CO–NH–, has the nitrogen bonded to a C=O carbon. The C=O joined to the nitrogen is what makes the group amido rather than amino.

**How do you find the longest chain when naming an organic compound?**
Pick the longest carbon chain that includes the functional group and any multiple bond, then number it so the suffix group gets the lowest number. A methyl on carbon 1 extends the chain, so "1-methylpentan-2-ol" is really hexan-3-ol.

**Do you need to draw stereochemical formulas in IB DP Chemistry Structure 3.2?**
You should recognise stereochemical formulas, but the IB guide does not expect you to draw them unless a question asks. The other formula types are empirical, molecular, full structural, condensed structural and skeletal.
