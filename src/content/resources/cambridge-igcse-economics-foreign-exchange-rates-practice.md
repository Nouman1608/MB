Setting for most questions: Country T uses the tarn and Country V uses the vesk. Unless told otherwise, 1 tarn = 0.80 vesk.

## Questions

**1.** Convert 560 vesk into tarns, and 1,200 tarns into vesk. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a floating and a fixed foreign exchange rate system. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows the market for tarns, in millions of tarns per week.

| Rate (vesk per tarn) | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 0.60 | 100 | 50 |
| 0.70 | 90 | 65 |
| 0.80 | 80 | 80 |
| 0.90 | 70 | 95 |
| 1.00 | 60 | 110 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium rate and the quantity of tarns traded. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the position at a rate of 1.00 and what is likely to happen next. **[2]**

**4.** Foreign demand for Country T's exports rises by 25 million tarns at every rate in question 3.
**(a)** State the new equilibrium rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the rate and state whether it is an appreciation or a depreciation. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why a country's currency might depreciate. **[4]**

**6.** Country T raises its rate of interest while other countries leave theirs unchanged. Explain the likely effect on the value of the tarn. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how speculation could cause the tarn to fall in value. **[3]**

**8.** A multinational company (MNC) is considering Country T.
**(a)** Explain the effect on the tarn if it opens a large factory there. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the effect on the tarn if it later closes the factory and sends the proceeds home. **[2]**

**9.** The rate rises from 1 tarn = 0.80 vesk to 1 tarn = 1.00 vesk.
**(a)** A bag made in Country T sells for 50 tarns. Calculate its price in vesk before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** A machine made in Country V costs 60 vesk. Calculate its price in tarns before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the tarn price of the machine. **[1]**

**10.** Following the change in question 9, the bag's price in Country V rises from 40 to 50 vesk. Before the change, Country V bought 8,000 bags.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in price. **[1]**
**(b)** PED of the bags is −0.6. Calculate the new quantity bought. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate spending on the bags before and after. **[2]**
**(d)** If PED were −1.8, state whether spending would rise or fall and why. **[1]**

**11.** Country W is a small economy that sends most of its trade to one large partner. Its government is deciding between a fixed and a floating exchange rate. Evaluate which is better for Country W. **[8]**

**12.** Country T fixes 1 tarn = 0.80 vesk. At this rate supply of tarns exceeds demand by 15 million tarns each month. The central bank holds 96 million vesk in reserves.
**(a)** State two ways in which the central bank could defend the rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many months the reserves would last if used to buy the surplus tarns. **[3]**
**(c)** Instead the government lowers the fixed rate to 1 tarn = 0.64 vesk. A good costs 40 vesk abroad. Calculate its price in tarns before and after. **[2]**
**(d)** Country T buys 600 of the good before the change and PED is −0.4. Calculate the new quantity and the change in spending in tarns, and explain the result. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Tarns: 560 ÷ 0.80 = **700 tarns** [1]. Vesk: 1,200 × 0.80 = **960 vesk** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the direction before calculating: converting from the quoted currency multiplies, and a sensible answer for vesk into tarns is a larger number, which gives you a quick test.

**2.** In a floating system the rate is determined by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market [1]. In a fixed system the government or central bank sets the rate [1] and must act, for example by buying or selling currency or changing interest rates, to keep it there [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs both systems in the same answer with a contrast, not two separate definitions with no link between them.

**3. (a)** Demand equals supply at 80 million, so the equilibrium rate is **0.80 vesk per tarn** [1] and **80 million tarns** are traded [1].
**(b)** At 1.00 supply is 110 and demand is 60, a surplus of 50 million tarns [1]. Sellers compete to get rid of tarns, so the rate falls towards 0.80 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table, such as the size of the surplus, and not only the word "surplus"; a read-off shows you used the data.

**4. (a)** New demand is 125, 115, 105, 95, 85 [1]. It equals supply (95) at **0.90 vesk per tarn**, where **95 million** tarns are traded [1].
**(b)** (0.90 − 0.80) ÷ 0.80 × 100 = **12.5%** [1]. The tarn has **appreciated** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Work the new demand figures out for every row before looking for the crossing point; spotting the new equilibrium by eye often lands on the wrong row.

**5.** First reason: Country T's households and firms buy more imports [1], so more tarns are supplied to buy foreign currency and the rate falls [1]. Second reason: foreign demand for its exports falls [1], so demand for tarns shifts left and the rate falls [1]. Other valid pairs include lower interest rates or speculators selling. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a link to the demand or supply curve of the currency; "people buy more imports" alone stops one step short of the exchange rate.

**6.** Higher interest rates give a better return on savings held in tarns [1]. Foreign savers buy tarns to deposit them, so demand for tarns shifts right [1]. Residents of Country T also keep more savings at home, so supply of tarns shifts left [1]. The tarn appreciates [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention that the comparison is with other countries' rates; a rise in rates that is matched abroad gives investors no reason to switch.

**7.** If speculators expect the tarn to fall, they sell tarns now to avoid a loss [1]. This increases the supply of tarns [1], which pushes the rate down, so the expected fall happens [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers show the expectation causing the movement; saying that speculators "cause instability" without the sell-now step gives little to credit.

**8. (a)** The MNC must buy tarns to pay wages, land and local suppliers [1]. Demand for tarns rises, so the tarn appreciates [1].
**(b)** On closing, the MNC sells its assets and exchanges the proceeds for foreign currency [1]. Supply of tarns rises, so the tarn depreciates [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say what the MNC does with the currency, such as buying tarns or selling them; entering and leaving are mirror images, and showing that matters more than the label.

**9. (a)** Before: 50 × 0.80 = **40 vesk** [1]. After: 50 × 1.00 = **50 vesk** [1].
**(b)** Before: 60 ÷ 0.80 = **75 tarns** [1]. After: 60 ÷ 1.00 = **60 tarns** [1].
**(c)** (60 − 75) ÷ 75 × 100 = **−20%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Multiply for the home-made export and divide for the foreign-made import; stating which operation you are using makes your working easy to follow and easier to check.

**10. (a)** (50 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **25%** [1].
**(b)** Quantity falls by 0.6 × 25 = 15% [1]. New quantity = 8,000 × 0.85 = **6,800 bags** [1].
**(c)** Before: 8,000 × 40 = **320,000 vesk** [1]. After: 6,800 × 50 = **340,000 vesk** [1].
**(d)** Spending would **fall**, because demand would be elastic and a price rise cuts quantity by a larger percentage than the price rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the calculation to a spending figure and then say what it shows; a quantity alone does not answer a question about spending.

**11.** A fixed rate gives traders certainty about prices when most trade is with one partner, which can encourage trade and investment [2]. Tying to a stable currency can also steady import prices and restrain inflation [1]. Against this, a small economy may have limited reserves to defend the rate [1], and its interest rates may have to be set to protect the rate, not to suit its own economy [1]. A floating rate adjusts automatically and leaves interest rates free, but brings uncertainty and swings that harm a small trading country [1]. Judgement: a fixed rate is likely to suit Country W if it holds large reserves and its partner's economy is stable and moves with its own; with small reserves, a floating system is safer [2]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that depends on a stated condition, such as the size of reserves, shows evaluation; an answer that lists points and then says "it depends" without saying on what does not.

**12. (a)** Buy surplus tarns using foreign exchange reserves [1]. Raise interest rates to attract demand for tarns [1].
**(b)** Cost per month = 15 × 0.80 = 12 million vesk [1]. Months = 96 ÷ 12 [1] = **8 months** [1].
**(c)** Before: 40 ÷ 0.80 = **50 tarns** [1]. After: 40 ÷ 0.64 = **62.5 tarns** [1].
**(d)** Price rises 25%, so quantity falls 0.4 × 25 = 10%, to **540** [1]. Spending rises from 600 × 50 = 30,000 tarns to 540 × 62.5 = **33,750 tarns**, a rise of 3,750 [1]. Demand is inelastic, so the fall in quantity is smaller than the rise in price [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In a multi-part question each part usually builds on the last, so label your figures clearly; the price you find in one part is the starting point for the next.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing when you should multiply, and the reverse, in currency conversions.
- Stating the percentage change for one currency and applying it to the other.
- Describing an effect on the exchange rate without naming the curve and its direction.
- Treating higher interest rates as a reason for a fall in the currency's value.
- Stopping at the quantity change in a PED question instead of reaching spending.
- Giving only advantages of one system when the question asks you to evaluate.
- Giving a conclusion that never says what the answer depends on.
- Leaving figures unlabelled so that the currency of each number is unclear.
