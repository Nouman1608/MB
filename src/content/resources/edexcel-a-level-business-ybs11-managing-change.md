This guide covers section **3.3.6 Managing change** in Unit 3 (Business decisions and strategy) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. The specification prints the heading as "Manging change" in the body but "Managing change" in the unit overview, and this guide uses the overview spelling. The section has two parts: the factors that decide how well a business handles change, and contingency planning for events that disrupt it. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Business decisions and strategy study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-business-decisions-and-strategy/). Condensed notes are in the [Managing change revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-managing-change-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Managing change practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-managing-change-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Key factors in change:** organisational culture; size of organisation; time or speed of change; managing resistance to change; transformative leadership.
2. **Contingency planning:** identifying key risks through risk assessment (natural disasters, IT systems failure, loss of key staff); planning for risk mitigation (business continuity and succession planning).

## Why businesses must change

Change may be **internal** (a new strategy, a merger, new technology, a new leader) or **external** (new competitors, changing customer tastes, legislation, economic shifts, as covered in earlier sections). Some changes are planned; others are forced on a business by events. Either way, success depends on how well the change is managed. Poorly managed change can lead to lost staff, falling productivity and failure to achieve the aim.

## Key factors in change

### Organisational culture

A culture that values learning, teamwork and flexibility (often a task or person culture) adapts more readily. A role culture, with fixed procedures, or a strong culture that has worked for many years may see change as a threat. Managers should therefore ask what the current culture is, how far the planned change conflicts with it, and whether the culture must change too. Changing the culture takes longer than changing a system.

### Size of organisation

A small business can change quickly: the owner can decide, tell everyone in a morning and adjust at once. A large business has more layers, more sites and more staff to inform and train, so change takes longer and costs more, and communication problems are more likely. On the other hand, a large business has more financial resources to fund change, specialist managers and the ability to run pilot schemes in one region before rolling out.

### Time and speed of change

| | Rapid change ("big bang") | Gradual (phased) change |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Quick; benefits arrive early | Slow; benefits arrive later |
| Disruption | High for a short time | Spread out; lower at any one time |
| Risk | High; errors affect everyone | Lower; lessons can be learned and applied |
| Staff reaction | Shock; little time to adjust | More time to adapt, but uncertainty may drag on |
| Suitable when | There is a crisis or a hard deadline | Change is large and complex |

**Worked example 1.** A retail chain is installing a new IT system. A one-weekend "big bang" is expected to cause three days of disruption at lost sales of $40,000 a day: 3 x 40,000 = **$120,000**. A phased rollout over 18 months needs extra project management of $5,000 a month: 18 x 5,000 = **$90,000**, with no lost sales.

On cost alone, phasing saves $30,000. But the big bang delivers the benefits of the new system up to 18 months sooner, and it removes the cost of running two systems side by side. The best choice depends on how much the business values early benefits and on how well it can cope with the risk of failure. A business facing urgent competition may choose speed, while one with a fragile workforce may choose phasing.

### Managing resistance to change

Staff resist change because of fear of job loss or lower pay, loss of status or familiar routines, lack of skills, mistrust of management, poor communication or a belief that the change is unnecessary. Resistance may be shown through absence, lower productivity, complaints or leaving.

**Ways to manage it:**

- **Communicate** early and honestly, explaining why the change is needed.
- **Involve** staff in planning so they feel ownership.
- **Train** staff so they have the skills.
- **Offer incentives or security**, such as guarantees on jobs or pay.
- **Use pilot schemes** and quick, visible successes.
- **Negotiate** with unions or staff representatives.
- **Use authority** as a last resort; it works fast but harms trust.

Each method has costs: involvement takes time, incentives cost money, and force can reduce morale and increase resignations.

### Transformative leadership

A **transformative leader** inspires staff to commit to a clear vision, sets high standards and motivates people to go beyond their own interests for the sake of the organisation. They communicate the vision, lead by example, encourage new ideas and develop their staff. This style suits major change, because it builds commitment rather than compliance. Its weaknesses are the heavy reliance on a single leader, the risk that the vision is wrong, the time it takes to build commitment and the possibility of burnout. An authoritarian style may be better in a real emergency where there is no time to consult.

## Contingency planning

**Contingency planning** prepares the business for events that could seriously disrupt it. The two steps are to identify the risks and then to plan how to reduce their impact.

### Risk assessment

A **risk assessment** identifies possible events, judges how likely each is and how much damage it would do, and ranks them. The specification names three key risks: **natural disasters** (floods, fires, storms or earthquakes), **IT systems failure** (hardware faults, cyberattacks, power cuts) and **loss of key staff** (resignation, illness, poaching by rivals).

**Worked example 2** (an illustration, as the specification does not require a particular formula). Calder Logistics estimates the yearly chance and cost of each risk.

| Risk | Chance per year | Cost if it happens ($) | Expected yearly loss ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| IT systems failure | 10% | 250,000 | 25,000 |
| Loss of a key manager | 20% | 120,000 | 24,000 |
| Flood at the depot | 2% | 900,000 | 18,000 |
| **Total** | | | **67,000** |

The flood is the most expensive event but the least likely. The IT failure has the highest expected loss. A backup system costing $12,000 a year would cut the cost of an IT failure from $250,000 to $50,000 (the chance stays at 10%), leaving an expected loss of 10% x 50,000 = **$5,000**. That saves 25,000 - 5,000 = 20,000 a year, so after the $12,000 cost the net benefit is **$8,000** a year. The figures show why firms usually spend money on reducing risks that are likely and costly, and consider insurance for rare but severe ones.

### Planning for risk mitigation

**Business continuity planning** sets out how the business will keep operating, or restart quickly, after a disruption. It covers: backup data and systems held off-site or in the cloud; a second site or agreements with partners to use their premises; alternative suppliers; insurance; emergency communication with staff and customers; and a tested recovery plan with named responsibilities. Plans must be **tested and updated** regularly, or they fail when needed. The cost of preparation is justified by the cost of downtime avoided.

**Succession planning** prepares for the loss of key staff by identifying and developing future leaders, training deputies, documenting key knowledge and offering career paths that encourage people to stay. **Worked example 3.** A key manager earns $90,000. Recruiting a replacement costs 20% of salary (**$18,000**) and the vacancy for three months costs $60,000 in lost output, so the total cost of an unplanned departure is 18,000 + 60,000 = **$78,000**. A trained deputy can take over at once, saving most of the lost output. **Limitations:** succession planning costs time and money, trained deputies may leave for better jobs, and the plan can create resentment among staff who are not chosen.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that all change is bad for staff, or that resistance is always irrational.
- Describing the speed of change without saying why it suits the business in the question.
- Treating transformative leadership as the same as autocratic leadership.
- Listing risks without ranking them by likelihood and impact.
- Describing business continuity or succession planning without a cost or limit.
- Forgetting that a plan which is never tested may not work.

## Exam technique

For **explain** and **analyse** questions, name the factor (for example culture or size) and trace its effect on the change: "the business has a strong role culture, so staff are used to rules and may resist a new, flexible way of working, which would slow the change". For **discuss** and **assess** questions, weigh different approaches (rapid or gradual change, communication or incentives) and make a judgement that depends on the business's situation, such as urgency, size and relationships with staff.
