These notes condense section 13.4 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), for examination in 2025 and 2026: the case study of "one tourist area or resort, its growth and development, showing the issues of sustainability it faces and evaluating the impacts of tourism on the destination's environment(s), society and economy." The full explanation and an essay plan are in the [13.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination/); exam-style questions are in the [13.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-tourist-destination-practice/). The wider option, including the Venice case study, is in the [Topic 13 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-global-interdependence-revision-notes/).

## The case study in one frame

Learn your destination under four headings, in this order:

1. **Growth and development**: first visitors, the trigger for mass growth, today's tourism types.
2. **Sustainability issues**: environmental, social and economic pressures, each with a named example.
3. **Management**: what was done, by whom, and when.
4. **Evaluation of impacts**: positive and negative, by dimension and by stakeholder, with a final judgement.

Keep a single sheet per destination with at least three dates, three named places within it, and one piece of evidence for each judgement.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for 13.4 |
|---|---|
| Tourist area or resort | A bounded destination (bay, island, resort town, park, historic centre), not a whole country |
| Sustainable tourism | Tourism that meets visitors' and hosts' needs now without damaging the environment, community or economy for the future |
| Ecological carrying capacity | Visitor use an ecosystem can absorb before unacceptable damage |
| Perceptual carrying capacity | Crowding that visitors or residents will tolerate before the experience or support for tourism declines |
| Stakeholder | Any group affected by, or affecting, tourism in the destination |
| Leakage | Tourist spending that leaves the destination, for example to tour firms or hotel owners based elsewhere |
| Displacement | Pressure moved from a managed site to nearby unmanaged ones |
| Hardening | Building surfaces or structures (boardwalks, moorings, paths) that absorb wear |
| Zoning | Dividing a destination into areas with different permitted uses or access |
| Rejuvenation | A Butler stage in which a stagnating destination is renewed by new attractions or management |

## Management tools and what each tackles

| Tool | Main pressure it addresses | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Daily cap or timed slots | Crowding, trampling | Needs booking and enforcement; can raise prices |
| Entry fee or tourist tax | Raises money, may deter some visitors | Small fees rarely cut numbers |
| Closure (full or seasonal) | Ecological recovery | Lost income; displacement |
| Boat or vessel bans | Anchor damage, pollution, wave damage | Operators lose access; pressure moves elsewhere |
| Boardwalks and fixed moorings | Trampling, anchoring | Changes the look of the site; cost |
| Limits on short-term lets | Resident displacement in lived-in towns | Hard to enforce online |
| Education and guides | Visitor behaviour | Slow, hard to measure |

## Worked case study summary: Maya Bay, Thailand

- **Place:** a cliff-enclosed bay on uninhabited Ko Phi Phi Leh, Krabi Province, inside a national park.
- **Growth:** divers and backpackers first; global fame after the film *The Beach* (2000); mass day trips by speedboat from Phuket, Krabi and Ko Phi Phi Don.
- **Issues:** coral broken by anchors and boats, polluted shallows, a crowded beach; dependence of tour firms on one site; crowding transferred to inhabited islands nearby.
- **Management:** closed in June 2018; coral replanted; reopened January 2022 with boats kept out of the bay, a pier and boardwalk on the far side, no swimming, timed visits with a cap on numbers.
- **Evaluation:** environmental recovery is clear (coral replanting, return of blacktip reef sharks); economic impact was negative for operators in the short term but protects the attraction; social pressure was redistributed rather than reduced. Works because one authority controls access to an uninhabited site.

## Comparing two destinations

| Question | Small, uninhabited site (Maya Bay) | Lived-in historic city (Venice, see the Topic 13 notes) |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls access? | One park authority | Many owners, councils and national bodies |
| Can it close? | Yes, completely | No |
| Main sustainability issue | Ecological | Social (depopulation) and built heritage |
| Typical tool | Closure, caps, boat ban | Fees, group limits, vessel bans, rental controls |

A comparison like this lets you show that the success of management depends on the type of destination, which is an evaluative point in itself.

## Exam traps

- A whole-country answer for an "area or resort" question limits the mark.
- Description of growth with no judgement answers the knowledge objective only.
- Saying a measure "worked" without evidence; name the evidence (coral cover, numbers, resident population, income).
- Treating all stakeholders as gaining or losing together.
- Forgetting the future: sustainability is about whether the destination can keep functioning.
- Ignoring displacement to nearby sites.
- Using Butler's model as a list of stages rather than a tool to judge change.

## Self-test

1. Quote the four parts of the 13.4 case study requirement.
2. Why is a whole country an unsuitable 13.4 case study?
3. Distinguish ecological from perceptual carrying capacity.
4. Give two stakeholders who might oppose a daily cap on visitors, and why.
5. What is displacement, and why does it matter when evaluating management?
6. Name three management tools that limit demand or access.
7. What triggered the rapid growth of visits to Maya Bay?
8. Give two environmental impacts at Maya Bay before 2018.
9. State three rules introduced when Maya Bay reopened in 2022.
10. Give one piece of evidence that the Maya Bay closure helped ecological recovery.
11. Why might a small entry fee fail to cut visitor numbers?
12. Which Butler stage does the Maya Bay closure aim to produce?

### Answers

1. Its growth and development; the issues of sustainability it faces; evaluation of the impacts of tourism on its environment(s), society and economy (one area or resort).
2. The syllabus asks for an area or resort, and a national scale hides the local detail needed to evaluate impacts.
3. Ecological: what ecosystems can absorb before damage. Perceptual: the crowding visitors or residents will accept.
4. Tour operators (fewer customers and trips) and local workers or businesses (less spending); also accept tourists who cannot get a slot.
5. Pressure moving from a managed site to nearby unmanaged ones; the regional impact may be unchanged even if the site improves.
6. Any three of: daily caps, timed slots, booking systems, closures, entry fees, vessel bans.
7. The film *The Beach* (2000), which made it a famous day-trip destination.
8. Any two of: coral broken by anchors and propellers, water pollution from fuel and sunscreen, trampling of the beach.
9. Boats not allowed into the bay; landing at a pier on the far side with a boardwalk; no swimming; timed visits with a cap on numbers.
10. Return of blacktip reef sharks to the shallows, or the replanting and recovery of coral.
11. If the fee is low relative to the cost of the trip, demand hardly changes; it raises money rather than reducing numbers.
12. Rejuvenation (or avoiding decline after stagnation).
