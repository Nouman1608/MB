## Questions

**1.** (a) State the difference between a need and a want. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why something that was once a want may come to be regarded as a need. **[2]**

**2.** State the factor of production, and the reward it earns, in each case. **[4]**
- A grower pays a monthly charge to use an orchard owned by someone else.
- A bakery borrows money and buys a mixing machine.
- A bus driver is paid for a week's work.
- Someone opens a bicycle repair stall using their own savings and may lose them if it fails.

**3.** Country D (illustrative) is making plans. Identify which key economic decision each statement is mainly about: what to produce, how to produce or who is to benefit. **[3]**
- Should farmland be used for more wheat or for new houses?
- Should the railway tunnel be dug by hand or with a tunnelling machine?
- Should free dental care go only to children or to everyone?

**4.** Explain one way in which consumers interact with producers and one way in which government interacts with producers. **[4]**

**5.** A shop owner says, "Our capital is the £50,000 in our bank account." Explain why an economist would describe capital differently, and give two examples of capital the shop might own. **[3]**

**6.** A print workshop (illustrative) has one machine for 6 hours a day. It can print 25 posters an hour or 70 leaflets an hour. **(a)** The workshop prints posters for 2 hours. State how many posters it makes and how many leaflets it gives up. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one poster in leaflets. **[2]**

**7.** In one month a small workshop in Town H (illustrative) has revenue of £12,000. It pays rent of £1,500, wages of £4,200, interest of £300 on a loan for equipment, and £3,000 for materials. **(a)** Calculate the profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate profit as a percentage of revenue. **[1]**
**(c)** State which payment is the reward to land and which is the reward to labour. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why scarcity creates the basic economic problem. **[4]**

**9.** Country E (illustrative) has £90 million. It can build three clinics at £30 million each, two colleges at £45 million each, or one bypass at £90 million. It chooses the bypass. **(a)** Calculate how many clinics the £90 million could have funded. **[2]**
**(b)** Advisers say the colleges were the next best option. State the opportunity cost of the bypass. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why opportunity cost helps the government more than the money cost here. **[2]**

**10.** Amir has a free five-hour Saturday. A shift pays £8 an hour. He values a football match with friends at £35 and a revision session at £28. **(a)** Calculate his earnings from the shift. **[1]**
**(b)** Assuming football is his next best option, state the opportunity cost of working. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain whether Amir should take the shift. **[4]**

**11.** A farmer in Region F (illustrative) has 12 hectares. Profit per hectare is £640 for barley, £820 for maize and £1,040 for an orchard. **(a)** Calculate profit if all 12 hectares become an orchard. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in profit if 5 hectares move from barley to orchard. **[2]**
**(c)** If all 12 hectares become an orchard, state the opportunity cost in £, taking maize as the next best use. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why the farmer might not plant an orchard on all the land. **[2]**

**12.** Country C (illustrative) can afford only one project. Estimates in £ million: a school has benefits of 95 and costs of 62; a flood barrier has benefits of 88 and costs of 52. Evaluate which project the government should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A need is essential for survival and health [1]; a want is desirable but a person can do without it [1].
**(b)** Technology or social change can make a product normal and widely used [1]; going without it then puts a person at a real disadvantage, for example a broadband connection for job applications [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" answers gain credit when the reason is linked to the consequence; naming broadband alone is only an example.

**2.** Orchard hired by a grower: land, rent [1]. Mixing machine: capital, interest paid to the lender [1]. Bus driver: labour, wages [1]. Stall owner: enterprise, profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each case needs the factor and its matching reward; giving rent for labour or interest for land loses the pair.

**3.** Wheat or houses: what to produce [1]. Dug by hand or machine: how to produce [1]. Dental care for children or everyone: who is to benefit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Look at the verb in each statement: choosing between outputs is "what", choosing between methods is "how", and choosing between recipients is "who".

**4.** Consumers and producers: consumers supply labour and receive wages [1], then spend that income on goods that producers sell, which gives the producers revenue [1]. Government and producers: government can tax producers' profits or sell them a licence [1], or it can set rules or pay a subsidy, which changes what firms produce and charge [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers state the flow in both directions and say what it changes, rather than only naming the groups.

**5.** In economics, capital means man-made physical goods used to produce other goods and services, not money [1]. The shop might own a freezer [1] and a till or delivery van [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two examples must be physical goods used in production; stock for resale does not fit.

**6. (a)** Posters: 2 × 25 = 50 [1]. Leaflets given up: 2 × 70 = 140 [1].
**(b)** 70 / 25 [1] = 2.8 leaflets per poster [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the unit with each answer; a bare 2.8 leaves it unclear what is being compared.

**7. (a)** Payments: 1,500 + 4,200 + 300 + 3,000 = 9,000 [1]. Profit: 12,000 - 9,000 = £3,000 [1].
**(b)** 3,000 / 12,000 × 100 = **25%** [1].
**(c)** Rent is the reward to land [1]; wages are the reward to labour [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not treat interest or materials as profit; profit is what is left after every payment is subtracted.

**8.** Resources are limited in supply [1]. Human wants are unlimited [1]. Therefore not every want can be satisfied, so choices must be made [1]. Each choice means giving up something else, which is the opportunity cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the four ideas in a chain is stronger than listing "limited resources, unlimited wants" and stopping.

**9. (a)** 90 / 30 [1] = 3 clinics [1].
**(b)** Two colleges [1].
**(c)** All three options cost the same £90 million, so money cost cannot separate them [1]. Opportunity cost shows what the community loses in services, which is what the decision actually turns on [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State the opportunity cost as the forgone item itself, with its quantity, not as a sum of money.

**10. (a)** 5 × 8 = **£40** [1].
**(b)** The football match, valued at **£35** [1].
**(c)** The shift earns £40, which exceeds the £35 value of football by £5 [1], so on the figures alone, Amir should work [1]. However, the margin is small and the values are his own estimates [1]. Non-financial factors such as tiredness, friendship or time for revision could outweigh £5, so the decision depends on how he ranks them [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Reaching a decision after weighing both sides is stronger than computing the numbers alone.

**11. (a)** 12 × 1,040 = **£12,480** [1].
**(b)** Gain: 5 × 1,040 = 5,200 [1]. Loss: 5 × 640 = 3,200. Change = +£2,000 [1].
**(c)** 12 × 820 = **£9,840** [1].
**(d)** An orchard may need money to buy trees and several years before it gives any profit [1], so the farmer could face cash problems or risk if the profit estimate is wrong [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason on its own is only a statement; linking it to its effect on the farmer's decision is what makes it an explanation.

**12.** School: net benefit 95 - 62 = £33m [1]. Barrier: 88 - 52 = £36m [1]. The barrier is higher by £3m [1], so on money alone it is better, and its opportunity cost is the school's net benefit of £33m [1]. The difference is small compared with the estimates being used, which may be wrong [1]. A school's benefits, such as skills and long-term earnings, are hard to price, and flood protection may rely on unpredictable events [1]. Different groups gain: children and families from the school, residents and businesses from the barrier [1]. The government cannot afford both, so scarcity forces a choice [1]. Conclusion: it could choose the barrier if it trusts the figures and the flood risk is high; otherwise the school is defensible [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that states the choice and the condition it depends on tends to be more convincing than the calculations alone.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating opportunity cost as the money spent rather than the best option given up.
- Naming all alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Treating capital as cash.
- Mixing up the matching rewards for land, labour, capital and enterprise.
- Answering "explain" with an example and no reason.
- Leaving out units, for example loaves, leaflets or £.
- Giving a calculation answer with no decision or evaluation when one is asked for.
- Writing about needs and wants as fixed, with no reference to time or circumstances.
