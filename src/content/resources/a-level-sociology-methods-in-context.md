This guide covers **3.1.2 Methods in Context**, the second of three compulsory content areas
within Paper 1 for AQA A-Level Sociology (7192), first teaching September 2015. Where
[3.1.1 Education](/resources/a-level-aqa-sociology-role-of-education-and-differential-achievement/)
supplies substantive knowledge and [3.1.3 Theory and Methods](/resources/a-level-sociology-course-structure/)
supplies method-and-theory knowledge in the abstract, Methods in Context is the skill of combining
the two: applying what you know about research methods specifically to the study of education.

## Where this fits in 7192

Paper 1 (Education with Theory and Methods) is a 2-hour written exam worth 80 marks, 33.3% of the
A-level. It is split into three question areas: Education (short answer and extended writing,
50 marks), Methods in Context (extended writing, 20 marks), and Theory and Methods (extended
writing, 10 marks). The specification states the requirement for this content area in a single
sentence: "Students must be able to apply sociological research methods to the study of education."
Unlike 3.1.1 and 3.1.3, which each list several bullet points of named content, 3.1.2 names no
separate content of its own — it is a skill applied to content already covered elsewhere in the
paper, which is exactly why it cannot be revised as a standalone topic in the way Education or
Theory and Methods can.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA A-LEVEL SOCIOLOGY (7192) — 3.1.2 METHODS IN CONTEXT**

The specification's full requirement for this content area: "Students must be able to apply
sociological research methods to the study of education." In practice, this draws on two other
content areas of the specification simultaneously: the research methods knowledge set out in
3.1.3 Theory and Methods (quantitative and qualitative methods, sources of data such as
questionnaires, interviews, observation, experiments, documents and official statistics, and the
practical, ethical and theoretical considerations influencing choice of method), and the
substantive knowledge of education from 3.1.1 (the role and functions of education, differential
achievement, in-school relationships and processes, and educational policy). The methods-in-context
question tests whether a student can apply the first body of knowledge specifically to the second.

## How to approach it

The exam question is built around a short item describing a research situation — for example,
noting that a method is being used to research a particular group of pupils or a particular
in-school process. The item is not background colour; it is there to be used directly, since every
top-band point should reference a specific detail from the item rather than writing generically
about a method's strengths and weaknesses. The governing principle is that a method-only answer
(stating a method's strengths and weaknesses in the abstract) and a context-only answer (stating
facts about education without linking them to a method) both score low: the marks are earned by
explaining how a specific feature of the chosen method interacts with a specific feature of
researching that particular topic in an educational setting. Because education research routinely
involves children, always have a bank of education-specific practical and ethical issues ready to
draw on — gatekeeper access via head teachers and parents, school timetabling constraints, the power
imbalance between adult researchers and pupils, and the informed-consent complications of working
with minors — since these recur across many different methods-in-context items.

## Worked example: applying a method to an education research topic

A methods-in-context item asks about using structured interviews to investigate teachers'
expectations of different groups of pupils.

```
Method-only (low marks):    Structured interviews are quick to administer and produce
                             data that is easy to compare across respondents.
Context-only (low marks):   Teachers might not want to admit to treating pupils
                             differently.
Combined (high marks):      Structured interviews are relatively quick to administer,
                             which suits teachers' limited free time during the school
                             day -- but teachers, as professionals used to being
                             observed and evaluated, may give socially desirable
                             answers rather than admit to differential treatment of
                             pupils, a validity issue that is especially acute for
                             this specific, sensitive topic and this specific,
                             standardised method.
```

The combined version earns marks precisely because it names a feature of the method (quick,
standardised administration) and a feature of the education context (teachers' limited time, and
the sensitivity of admitting to differential treatment) and explains how the two interact, rather
than stating either fact in isolation.

## Common mistakes

Writing generic method strengths and weaknesses without connecting them to education specifically —
a fluent account of interviews or observation in the abstract earns little credit here, however
accurate, if it never touches the item's actual research situation. Discussing a topic's
sensitivity without tying it to a specific feature of the named method. Reading the item once at
the start and then writing generically, rather than returning to its specific details throughout
the answer. Covering many combined points shallowly instead of developing three or four in genuine
depth and reaching an overall judgement about whether the method suits the topic on balance.

## Quick revision checklist

- Learn the practical, ethical and theoretical issue categories from 3.1.3 well enough to apply them
  to a topic you have not seen before.
- Build a bank of education-specific practical and ethical issues (access, timetabling, the
  researcher-pupil power imbalance, informed consent with minors).
- Practise identifying a specific detail from a short item and building a combined method-and-context
  point directly from it.
- Aim for three or four genuinely combined points explored in depth, closing with an explicit
  judgement, rather than many shallow points.
