Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Hydrogen levels: n = 1: −13.6 eV; n = 2: −3.40 eV; n = 3: −1.51 eV; n = 4: −0.85 eV; n = 5: −0.54 eV.

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a discrete energy level. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why the energies of electrons in an atom are given negative values. **[2]**

**3.** Sodium vapour emits a bright yellow line of wavelength 589 nm. Calculate the energy difference, in eV, between the two levels involved. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate the minimum wavelength of a photon that can ionise a hydrogen atom whose electron is in the n = 2 level. **[2]**

**5.** A hydrogen atom emits a photon of frequency 3.08 × 10¹⁵ Hz. Calculate the photon energy in eV and identify the transition. **[3]**

**6.** State **two** differences between the appearance of an emission line spectrum and an absorption line spectrum of the same element. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A low-pressure gas of hydrogen in a discharge tube emits light that is viewed through a diffraction grating.

**(a)** Explain how the gas produces a spectrum of separate lines. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the wavelength of the line produced by the transition from n = 5 to n = 2. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the line from n = 2 to n = 1 is not seen through the grating by eye. **[2]**

**8.** An imaginary atom has four energy levels: −8.0 eV, −5.2 eV, −3.0 eV and −1.2 eV.

**(a)** State the ionisation energy of the atom in its ground state. **[1]**

**(b)** Determine how many different emission lines are possible when electrons are excited to the −1.2 eV level. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the longest wavelength emitted. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the shortest wavelength emitted. **[2]**

**9.** White light passes through a sample of cool hydrogen gas in which all the atoms are in the ground state.

**(a)** Describe the spectrum of the light after it has passed through the gas. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why some wavelengths are much weaker in the transmitted light, even though the excited atoms re-emit photons. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the spectrum contains fewer dark lines than the number of bright lines in the emission spectrum of hot hydrogen. **[2]**

**10.** Photons of energies 10.2 eV, 11.0 eV and 12.09 eV are directed at hydrogen atoms in the ground state. For each photon, state whether it can be absorbed and give a reason. **[3]**

**11.** A student says: "A filament lamp, a hot gas of sodium and the Sun all produce line spectra because they all contain atoms with energy levels."

Explain which part of this statement is incorrect. **[2]**

**12.** An astronomer observes a dark line at 656 nm in the spectrum of a star.

**(a)** Show that the photon energy at this wavelength is about 1.9 eV. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest which transition in hydrogen is responsible, and state where in the star the absorbing gas is found. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** One of a set of particular allowed energies an electron in an atom can have, with no allowed values in between [1].

**2.** Zero energy is defined as an electron that has just been freed from the atom (at infinity) [1]; a bound electron has less energy than this, so energy must be supplied to free it and its energy is negative [1].

**3.** E = hc/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 589 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.38 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.11 eV [1].

**4.** Energy needed = 3.40 eV = 5.44 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; λ = hc/E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.44 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.66 × 10⁻⁷ m (366 nm) [1].

**5.** E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.08 × 10¹⁵ = 2.04 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1] = 12.8 eV (12.75 eV) [1]; this equals −0.85 − (−13.6), so the transition is from n = 4 to n = 1 [1].

**6.** Any two [1 each]: emission shows bright lines, absorption shows dark lines; emission lines are on a dark background, absorption lines are on a continuous (coloured) background; the emission spectrum usually has more lines.

**7. (a)** Electrons in the gas atoms are excited to higher levels by the discharge [1]. They fall back to lower levels, each transition emitting one photon [1] with energy equal to the difference between the two levels, hf = E₁ − E₂ [1]. The levels are discrete, so only certain energy differences, and therefore only certain wavelengths, are produced [1].

**(b)** ΔE = −0.54 − (−3.40) = 2.86 eV = 4.58 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; λ = hc/ΔE = 4.35 × 10⁻⁷ m (435 nm) [1].

**(c)** ΔE = 10.2 eV, giving λ = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m [1]; this is in the ultraviolet, outside the visible range of about 400–700 nm [1].

**8. (a)** 8.0 eV [1].

**(b)** 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 lines [1].

**(c)** Longest wavelength comes from the smallest gap, −1.2 to −3.0 eV, ΔE = 1.8 eV = 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; λ = hc/ΔE = 6.9 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**(d)** Shortest wavelength comes from the largest gap, −1.2 to −8.0 eV, ΔE = 6.8 eV = 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1]; λ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**9. (a)** A continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines at particular wavelengths [1].

**(b)** Photons whose energies exactly equal the gap between the ground state and a higher level are absorbed [1], raising electrons to excited states [1]. When the electrons fall back, the photons are re-emitted in all directions, so few travel on along the original beam [1].

**(c)** In the cool gas nearly all electrons are in the ground state, so absorption only occurs for jumps from the ground state [1]. In hot hydrogen, electrons fall between any pair of levels, giving many more possible transitions [1].

**10.** 10.2 eV: absorbed, as it exactly equals the gap from n = 1 to n = 2 [1]. 11.0 eV: not absorbed, as it matches no gap from the ground state and is less than the ionisation energy [1]. 12.09 eV: absorbed, as it exactly equals the gap from n = 1 to n = 3 [1].

**11.** The filament lamp produces a continuous spectrum, not a line spectrum [1], because its atoms are in a solid, close together, so their energy levels interact and spread out instead of being sharp, as in isolated atoms [1].

**12. (a)** E = hc/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 656 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.9 eV [1].

**(b)** n = 2 to n = 3 (gap 1.89 eV) [1]; the absorbing gas is in the cooler outer layers (atmosphere) of the star, through which light from the hotter interior passes [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Errors with signs when subtracting negative energy levels.
- Leaving energies in eV when using λ = hc/E with h in J s.
- Explaining absorption lines without mentioning re-emission in all directions.
- Assuming a photon with more than a gap energy will be absorbed for that gap.
- Forgetting that a level diagram with n levels gives n(n − 1)/2 possible emission lines.
- Calling the spectrum from a hot solid a line spectrum.
