Condensed notes for **section 6.5 Policies to correct imbalances in the current account of the balance of payments** (AS Level, Cambridge 9708, 2026–2028). For worked examples, use the [current account policies study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-policies-to-correct-current-account-imbalances/). Figures are invented.

## The two syllabus statements

| Statement | What to recall |
|---|---|
| 6.5.1 | Stability of the current account as a government objective |
| 6.5.2 | Effect of fiscal, monetary, supply-side and protectionist policies on the current account |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Current account stability | Avoiding imbalances that are large relative to GDP and persistent |
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Higher taxes or lower government spending to reduce AD |
| Contractionary monetary policy | Higher interest rates, less money supply or tighter credit to reduce AD |
| Supply-side policy | Measures that raise productivity and capacity, shifting LRAS right |
| Protectionism | Restricting imports or helping domestic producers against foreign competition |
| Price competitiveness | Selling at lower prices than rivals, helped by lower unit costs |
| Non-price competitiveness | Winning sales through quality, design, reliability or service |
| Retaliation | A trading partner imposing its own barriers in response |

## Why stability matters

- Large deficits need finance; if lenders lose confidence, the currency may fall sharply.
- Large surpluses mean consumption below production and create pressure from partners.
- Swings in the account bring swings in AD, the exchange rate and import prices.

## Policy summary

| Policy | Deficit correction | Route | Speed | Main side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Tax up / spending down | Lower incomes → fewer imports | Fairly quick | Lower output and jobs |
| Monetary | Rates up / credit tighter | Less spending → fewer imports; but appreciation hurts exports | Quick, with lags | Lower output; appreciation offsets gain |
| Supply-side | Training, infrastructure, technology | Lower costs, better quality → more exports | Slow | Costly; uncertain |
| Protectionist | Tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, embargoes, red tape | Imports dearer or limited | Quick | Higher prices, retaliation, dearer inputs |

To reduce a **surplus**: expansionary fiscal or monetary policy, or removing protection.

## The key numbers chain (fiscal)

```
Fall in disposable income  x  share consumed         = fall in consumption
Fall in consumption        x  share spent on imports = fall in imports
Fall in consumption        -  fall in imports        = fall in spending on home output
```

Example: tax rise 20, share consumed 0.75, import share 0.4. Consumption falls 15; imports fall 6; spending on home output falls 9.

## Monetary policy: two routes

```
Rates up -> C and I down -> imports down        -> account improves
Rates up -> money flows in -> currency up -> X down, M up -> account worsens
Net effect depends on the size of the appreciation
```

## Protection: what can go wrong

- Consumers pay higher prices and have less choice.
- Firms using imported inputs face higher costs, harming their exports.
- Partners may retaliate, cutting exports.
- Trade agreements may forbid the measure.
- Protected firms may have less pressure to become efficient.

## Matching policy to cause

| Cause | Best fit |
|---|---|
| Domestic boom | Contractionary fiscal or monetary |
| Low productivity | Supply-side |
| Poor quality | Supply-side (training, technology) |
| Import surge in one industry | Temporary protection |
| Fall in world price of main export | Supply-side to diversify; no quick fix |

## Exam traps

- Ignoring the fall in domestic output when fiscal policy cuts imports.
- Missing the exchange-rate route of monetary policy.
- Saying supply-side policy works fast.
- Forgetting retaliation.
- Assuming the aim is zero.
- Building an AS answer around exchange rate policy or expenditure-switching terms; these are A Level section 11.1 content.

## Self-test

1. What does stability of the current account mean?
2. Give one reason why a government may want to reduce a large surplus.
3. How does a rise in income tax reduce a deficit?
4. Income tax rises by 12; households consume 0.9 of income; 25% of the spending cut is on imports. Calculate the fall in imports.
5. In question 4, by how much does spending on home-produced goods fall?
6. Give the two routes by which higher interest rates affect the current account.
7. Why may supply-side policy be the best long-term answer to a deficit caused by low productivity?
8. Name the five tools of protection listed in the syllabus.
9. A country spends 40 on imports of a good at a world price of 8. A quota halves the quantity. With the world price unchanged, what is the new import spending?
10. In question 9, a partner retaliates by restricting 25 of the country's exports. What is the net effect on the account?
11. Which policy would reduce a surplus caused by weak domestic demand?
12. Why is protection unlikely to fix a deficit caused by poor product quality?

Tariffs, import quotas, export subsidies, embargoes and excessive administrative burdens. 9. Quantity falls from 5 to 2.5, so spending is 2.5 × 8 = 20. 10.
