## Section A: Crime and Deviance

**1.** Which sociologist is associated with "status frustration" as an explanation for working-class delinquency?

A. Becker
B. Cohen
C. Davis and Moore
D. Weber

**[1]**

**2.** Which of these is an informal method of social control?

A. The police
B. The courts
C. Family disapproval
D. Prison

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** reasons why official crime statistics may not give an accurate picture of the true level of crime. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **three** formal or informal methods of social control. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how subcultural theory accounts for delinquent behaviour among young people. **[4]**

**6. Item-based question.** *Item: A local newspaper reports that recorded burglaries in a town have fallen by 20% over the past year, and concludes that burglary itself must be 20% less common than it was.*

Using the item and your knowledge, explain why the newspaper's conclusion may not be justified. **[4]**

**7.** Evaluate the view that official crime statistics give an accurate picture of the true social distribution of crime. **[12]**

## Section B: Social Stratification

**8.** Which sociologists argued that stratification is necessary because society must motivate its most able members to fill its most important roles?

A. Marx
B. Weber
C. Davis and Moore
D. Cohen

**[1]**

**9.** Which of these is a Marxist term for the class that owns the means of production?

A. Proletariat
B. Bourgeoisie
C. Precariat
D. Peerage

**[1]**

**10.** Identify **two** factors, other than social class, that can affect a person's life chances. **[2]**

**11.** Explain the difference between the functionalist and Marxist views of social stratification. **[3]**

**12.** Explain **two** ways gender can affect a person's life chances. **[4]**

**13. Item-based question.** *Item: Two people with identical qualifications apply for the same senior role. One is offered a significantly higher starting salary than the other during negotiation, and later research finds this pattern holds consistently across many similar pairs of candidates in the same industry.*

Using the item and your knowledge, explain how sociologists would account for this pattern. **[4]**

**14.** Evaluate the view that social class remains the single most important factor affecting a person's life chances in society today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B — Cohen.

**2.** C — Family disapproval.

**3.** Any two: **unreported crime** — the "dark figure" of crime includes offences victims never report, for example through embarrassment or a belief the police cannot act [1]; **unrecorded crime** — even reported incidents may not be formally recorded by police, for instance if judged too minor [1]. (Also accept: changes in the law or policing priorities altering the count without the real underlying rate changing.)

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: **formal** — the **police** [1]; the **courts** [1]. **Informal** — **peer group disapproval** [1]; **family sanctions** [1] (also accept: religion, the media).

**5.** **Status frustration** — Cohen argued working-class boys, denied status by mainstream success criteria, form delinquent subcultures that invert conventional values, gaining status by breaking rules instead [1] [1]. **Group loyalty and identity** — subcultural theory holds that once formed, the subculture's own norms reward and reinforce delinquent behaviour as a source of belonging, making it self-sustaining [1] [1].

**6.** The newspaper conflates **recorded** crime with actual crime, ignoring the "dark figure" of unreported and unrecorded offending [1] [1]. A fall in recorded burglaries could instead reflect a change in police recording practices, reduced public reporting (for example if victims believe nothing will be done), or a shift in police priorities toward or away from burglary, none of which mean the true underlying rate has fallen by the same amount [1] [1].

**7.** Indicative content — award up to 12 marks using the levels descriptors in the specification; look for a developed, evaluative argument rather than a checklist of points. **For "accurate"**: official statistics are systematically compiled, cover the whole population reported to the police, and allow comparison over time and between areas [1] [1]. **Against**: the "dark figure" of unreported and unrecorded crime, and the influence of policing priorities and recording practices, mean official statistics reflect the criminal justice system's activity as much as underlying behaviour — self-report and victim studies often reveal offending that never appears in official data [1] [1]. **Evaluation**: strong answers weigh the practical value of official statistics (scale, consistency, availability) against their known distortions, and consider whether patterns by class, gender or ethnicity in the statistics reflect real differences in offending or differences in how groups are policed and recorded [1] [1] [1] [1].

**8.** C — Davis and Moore.

**9.** B — Bourgeoisie.

**10.** Any two: **gender** [1]; **ethnicity** [1]. (Also accept: disability, sexuality, age, religion and belief.)

**11. Functionalist view (Davis and Moore):** stratification is **necessary and beneficial** — society allocates its most able members to its most functionally important roles by attaching greater rewards to them, motivating competition for demanding positions [1]. **Marxist view:** stratification exists because the **ruling class (bourgeoisie) owns the means of production** and uses that ownership to exploit the working class (proletariat); inequality serves the interests of the powerful rather than society as a whole [1]. The functionalist view treats inequality as the fair outcome of open competition, while the Marxist view treats the competition itself as never genuinely fair, given unequal ownership from the start [1].

**12.** Any two, 2 marks each: women have historically faced narrower occupational and educational opportunities [1] [1]; a persistent **gender pay gap** affects income and career life chances even where qualifications are equal [1] [1].

**13.** The pattern in the item is consistent with a **gender pay gap** — even with identical qualifications, women are on average offered lower starting salaries than men in comparable roles, a difference that recurs consistently across candidates rather than reflecting any one individual case [1] [1]. Sociologists would explain this through structural and cultural factors rather than individual merit — for example, unconscious bias in salary negotiation, occupational segregation, and the lasting effect of career breaks associated with childcare on women's pay trajectories [1] [1].

**14.** Indicative content — award up to 12 marks using the levels descriptors in the specification. **For class as most important**: class strongly predicts income, wealth, education and health outcomes, and Marxist theory argues ownership of the means of production is the fundamental basis of inequality that other factors operate within [1] [1] [1]. **Against**: gender (the gender pay gap), ethnicity (discrimination in employment and the criminal justice system) and disability each independently affect life chances, sometimes compounding class disadvantage rather than simply following from it — Weber's view that class, status and party are separate dimensions of stratification is relevant evaluation here [1] [1] [1]. **Evaluation**: strong answers avoid treating the factors as competing in isolation, noting instead that class often intersects with gender and ethnicity to produce compounded disadvantage, so ranking a single "most important" factor may oversimplify how life chances are actually shaped [1] [1] [1].

## Building a "perspective on an issue" answer quickly

Many of Paper 2's higher-mark questions, like Questions 11 and 14 above,
reward the same underlying skill regardless of the specific topic: naming
the relevant sociological perspective or factor, stating its key claim
accurately, and — where the question asks for comparison — explicitly
setting two views side by side rather than describing them one after
another with no direct link. A quick planning habit worth building: for
any "explain the difference between X and Y views" question, jot each
view's one-sentence core claim first, then write a single linking
sentence stating what they specifically disagree about, before writing
the fuller explanation of each.

## Keeping crime and stratification content distinct

Because both sections of Paper 2 draw on the same four perspectives
(functionalism, interactionism, feminism and Marxism), it is easy to
blur which specific argument belongs to which topic during revision.
Keep a clear mental separation: Cohen's status frustration and Becker's
labelling belong specifically to the causes of crime, while Davis and
Moore's functionalist justification and Marx's and Weber's accounts
belong specifically to stratification — even though several are,
broadly speaking, "functionalist" or "conflict" arguments. Practising
short-answer questions that name a theorist and ask which topic, not
just which perspective, they belong to helps fix this distinction
before the exam.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing a factor (e.g. "gender") without explaining *how* it affects
  the outcome asked about, which only accesses the lower mark band.
- Describing the functionalist and Marxist views of stratification
  separately with no explicit comparison, when the question specifically
  asks for a difference.
- Confusing the "dark figure" of crime with the overall crime rate.
- Treating subcultural theory as simply "peer pressure" rather than
  explaining the status-frustration mechanism the specification
  actually describes.
- Naming a sociologist without being able to state which perspective
  they represent or their specific argument — the specification's named
  theorists (Davis and Moore, Cohen, Marx, Weber and others) are
  routinely the basis of short-answer questions.
- On the item-based questions, describing the item's scenario without
  linking it explicitly to a named sociological concept or study.
