These questions cover section 3.9.2 (Exponential changes in radioactivity) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, version 4.4, for exams from May/June 2020 onwards. Section 3.9 is International A-level only. Where needed, take 1 year = 3.156 × 10⁷ s and N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Links: [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-exponential-radioactive-decay/), [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-exponential-radioactive-decay-revision-notes/), the [OxfordAQA Physics hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/physics/), the [printable checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/physics/) and the [free 10-minute diagnostics](/diagnostics/).

## Questions

**1.** (a) Say what cannot be predicted about one nucleus in a radioactive sample. **[1]**
**(b)** Define the decay constant of a nuclide. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the half-life of a nuclide is the same for a large sample as for a small one. **[1]**

**2.** A nuclide has a half-life of 3.8 days. A sample contains 2.4 × 10¹² undecayed nuclei.
**(a)** Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the activity of the sample. **[2]**

**3.** A sample initially contains 6.0 × 10¹⁰ undecayed nuclei of a nuclide with decay constant 0.018 h⁻¹.
**(a)** Calculate the number of undecayed nuclei after 48 h. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gradient of a graph of N against t at t = 0, in nuclei per second. **[2]**

**4.** A source has an initial activity of 7.5 × 10⁵ Bq and a half-life of 2.4 h.
Calculate how long it takes for the activity to fall to 1.2 × 10⁴ Bq. **[4]**

**5.** A small power source contains 0.50 mg of plutonium-238, which has a molar mass of 238 g mol⁻¹ and a half-life of 87.7 years.
**(a)** Find how many plutonium-238 nuclei the source contains. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the initial activity. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the activity 25 years later. **[2]**

**6.** A sample of mass 2.0 µg of a pure nuclide, with molar mass 210 g mol⁻¹, has a measured activity of 3.3 × 10⁸ Bq.
**(a)** Find how many nuclei the sample contains. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the decay constant, and from it the half-life in days. **[3]**

**7.** A detector placed near a decaying source recorded these measured count rates. The background rate, measured with the source removed, was 30 counts per hour.

| t / h | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured rate / counts per h | 930 | 738 | 587 | 468 | 375 | 301 | 243 |

**(a)** Explain why the background rate must be subtracted before taking logarithms. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the corrected rate at t = 0, 6 and 12 h, and the natural logarithm of each. **[3]**
**(c)** Use these three points to find the decay constant. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the half-life. **[1]**

**8.** A numerical model of decay starts with N = 2000 nuclei and uses λ = 0.30 s⁻¹ with a time step Δt = 0.5 s.
**(a)** Calculate the change in N, and the new value of N, in the first step. **[2]**
**(b)** Find N after the second and third steps. **[2]**
**(c)** The exact value of N at t = 1.5 s is given by N = N₀e^(−λt). Calculate it, and the percentage by which the model value is too low. **[2]**
**(d)** State one way to make the model more accurate. **[1]**

**9.** A program simulates 800 nuclei. In each 1.0 s tick, every nucleus still present has a probability of 0.040 of decaying.
**(a)** Calculate the expected number of nuclei left after 10 ticks. **[2]**
**(b)** Treating each tick as 1.0 s, find the decay constant and the half-life of the simulated nuclide. **[2]**
**(c)** One run leaves 519 nuclei after 10 ticks. Explain why this differs from your answer to (a), and why a real sample of 10¹⁵ nuclei shows far smaller relative differences. **[2]**

**10.** A luminous sign contains 0.30 µg of tritium. Tritium has a molar mass of 3.02 g mol⁻¹ and a half-life of 12.3 years.
**(a)** Calculate the number of tritium nuclei. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the decay constant in s⁻¹ and the initial activity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the activity after 5.0 years. **[2]**
**(d)** Find how many years pass before the activity is 60% of its initial value. **[2]**

**11.** An industrial radiography source contains iridium-192, with a half-life of 73.8 days and a molar mass of 192 g mol⁻¹. When new, its activity is 3.7 × 10¹² Bq.
**(a)** Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of iridium-192 nuclei in the new source. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of iridium-192 in the source, in mg. **[2]**
**(d)** The source must be replaced when its activity falls to 2.0 × 10¹¹ Bq. Calculate the time, in days, after which it must be replaced. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Whether it will decay in the next moment, so which nucleus goes next and when [1].
**(b)** The chance, per unit time, that one given nucleus decays [1].
**(c)** T½ = ln2/λ and λ is a property of the nuclide, so N₀ does not appear in it [1].
*Examiner insight:* A good answer on randomness says what cannot be predicted for one nucleus.

**2. (a)** T½ = 3.8 × 24 × 3600 = 3.28 × 10⁵ s [1]. λ = 0.693 ÷ 3.28 × 10⁵ = **2.1 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** A = λN [1] = 2.11 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.4 × 10¹² = **5.1 × 10⁶ Bq** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the days-to-seconds conversion line.

**3. (a)** λt = 0.018 × 48 = 0.864 [1]. N = 6.0 × 10¹⁰ × e^(−0.864) = **2.5 × 10¹⁰** [1].
**(b)** Gradient = −λN₀ [1] = −(0.018 ÷ 3600) × 6.0 × 10¹⁰ = **−3.0 × 10⁵ nuclei per second** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the sign and the unit both matter.

**4.** λ = 0.693 ÷ 2.4 = 0.289 h⁻¹ [1]. A₀/A = 7.5 × 10⁵ ÷ 1.2 × 10⁴ = 62.5 [1]. ln(62.5) = 4.135 [1]. t = 4.135 ÷ 0.289 = **14 h** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Inverting the ratio gives a negative time. Check the size against whole half-lives: 2⁶ = 64 is close to 62.5, so about 6 × 2.4 = 14.4 h.

**5. (a)** Moles = 0.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 238 [1]. N = 2.10 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.02 × 10²³ = **1.3 × 10¹⁸** [1].
**(b)** λ = 0.693 ÷ (87.7 × 3.156 × 10⁷) = 2.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ s⁻¹ [1]. A = λN = 2.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 1.26 × 10¹⁸ = **3.2 × 10⁸ Bq** [1].
**(c)** 25 ÷ 87.7 = 0.285 half-lives, so A/A₀ = 2^(−0.285) = 0.821 [1]. A = 3.17 × 10⁸ × 0.821 = **2.6 × 10⁸ Bq** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert milligrams to grams before dividing by molar mass, and keep extra digits in λ until the final answer.

**6. (a)** Moles = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 210 [1]. N = 9.52 × 10⁻⁹ × 6.02 × 10²³ = **5.7 × 10¹⁵** [1].
**(b)** λ = A/N = 3.3 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.73 × 10¹⁵ = 5.8 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹ [1]. T½ = 0.693 ÷ 5.76 × 10⁻⁸ = 1.2 × 10⁷ s [1]. Dividing by 86 400 gives **1.4 × 10² days** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange A = λN for λ first, then use the half-life equation.

**7. (a)** Measured rate = source rate + background. ln of a sum is not the sum of the ln values, so the graph would curve [1].
**(b)** Corrected rates are 900, 438 and 213 counts per hour [1]. The ln values are 6.802, 6.082 and 5.361 [2].
**(c)** Gradient = (5.361 − 6.802) ÷ 12 = −0.120 h⁻¹ [1], so **λ = 0.120 h⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** T½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.120 = **5.8 h** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use points far apart on the line; the gradient is negative and λ is its magnitude.

**8. (a)** ΔN = −λNΔt = −0.30 × 2000 × 0.5 = −300 [1]. New N = **1700** [1].
**(b)** Step 2: ΔN = −0.30 × 1700 × 0.5 = −255, so N = **1445** [1]. Step 3: ΔN = −0.30 × 1445 × 0.5 = −216.75, so N = **1228** [1].
**(c)** Exact N = 2000e^(−0.45) = **1275** [1]. Model is too low by (1275 − 1228) ÷ 1275 = **3.7%** [1].
**(d)** Use a smaller time step [1].
*Examiner insight:* The model uses the rate at the start of each step, which overestimates the loss.

**9. (a)** Each tick leaves 0.96 of the nuclei [1]. N = 800 × 0.96¹⁰ = **532** [1].
**(b)** e^(−λ) = 0.96, so λ = −ln0.96 = **0.041 s⁻¹** [1]. T½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.0408 = **17 s** [1].
**(c)** Each nucleus decays by chance, so the count scatters about the expected value, here by about 13 either way [1]. With 10¹⁵ nuclei the scatter is about √N, a tiny fraction of N, so the curve is smooth [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link the scatter to the small number of nuclei, not to a faulty program.

**10. (a)** Moles = 0.30 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 3.02 [1]. N = 9.93 × 10⁻⁸ × 6.02 × 10²³ = **6.0 × 10¹⁶** [1].
**(b)** T½ = 12.3 × 3.156 × 10⁷ = 3.88 × 10⁸ s [1]. λ = 0.693 ÷ 3.88 × 10⁸ = **1.8 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹** [1]. A = λN = 1.79 × 10⁻⁹ × 5.98 × 10¹⁶ = **1.1 × 10⁸ Bq** [1].
**(c)** 5.0 ÷ 12.3 = 0.407 half-lives, so A/A₀ = 2^(−0.407) = 0.754 [1]. A = 1.07 × 10⁸ × 0.754 = **8.1 × 10⁷ Bq** [1].
**(d)** λ in years = 0.693 ÷ 12.3 = 0.0564 y⁻¹, and ln(1/0.6) = 0.511 [1]. t = 0.511 ÷ 0.0564 = **9.1 years** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (d), using λ in y⁻¹ avoids converting to seconds and back.

**11. (a)** T½ = 73.8 × 86 400 = 6.38 × 10⁶ s [1]. λ = 0.693 ÷ 6.38 × 10⁶ = **1.09 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** N = A/λ = 3.7 × 10¹² ÷ 1.09 × 10⁻⁷ = **3.4 × 10¹⁹** [1].
**(c)** Moles = 3.40 × 10¹⁹ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 5.65 × 10⁻⁵ [1]. Mass = 5.65 × 10⁻⁵ × 192 = 1.09 × 10⁻² g = **11 mg** [1].
**(d)** A₀/A = 3.7 × 10¹² ÷ 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 18.5 [1]. λ = 0.693 ÷ 73.8 = 9.39 × 10⁻³ day⁻¹ and ln(18.5) = 2.918 [1]. t = 2.918 ÷ 9.39 × 10⁻³ = **3.1 × 10² days** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Set the chain out line by line so each result is visible.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Substituting a half-life in days, hours or years into an equation that needs seconds.
- Using log to base 10 where the exponential needs natural logs.
- Inverting the activity ratio, which makes the time negative.
- Not converting micrograms and milligrams to grams before using molar mass.
- Taking ln of measured rather than corrected count rates.
- Confusing the decay constant, a probability per unit time, with the activity of a sample.
- Rounding λ early.
