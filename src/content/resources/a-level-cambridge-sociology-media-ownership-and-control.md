Who owns and controls the media, and does it matter? Syllabus heading 9 of Cambridge International
A Level Sociology (9699), *Ownership and control of media*, is the first half of Paper 4's Media
section. It covers trends in media organisation and the difference between traditional and new
media (9.1), competing theories of what shapes media content (9.2), and the impact of the new
media on power, globalisation and relationships (9.3). Essays are worth 35 marks and must weigh
pluralist against Marxist and postmodern views.

This guide sits under the [Paper 4 overview guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-media-religion/).
Representation and effects (heading 10) are covered in the
[media representation and effects guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-representation-and-effects/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section B, heading 9:

- **9.1 Traditional media and the new media** — trends including cross-media ownership,
  digitalisation, media conglomerates and social media; debates about who controls the media;
  differences between traditional and new media; whether traditional media has been undermined by
  new media.
- **9.2 Theories of the media and influences on media content** — pluralist theories; Marxist and
  neo-Marxist theories; factors influencing selection and presentation of news; mass manipulation
  and hegemony; the postmodernist contribution; censorship; how the media may influence the
  political process (agenda setting, opinion polls, news reporting).
- **9.3 The impact of the new media** — contribution to globalisation; challenge to existing power
  structures; digital optimism versus digital pessimism; impact on social identities and
  interpersonal relationships.

## What trends are changing media organisation?

| Trend | Meaning | Example of significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Cross-media ownership** | One company owning different types of media (press, TV, film, online) | Concentrates influence across formats |
| **Media conglomerates** | Very large corporations with many media and non-media interests | Ben Bagdikian's *The Media Monopoly* (first edition 1983) tracked shrinking numbers of controlling corporations in the USA |
| **Digitalisation** | Conversion of all content to digital form | Allows *convergence* — one device carries news, film, music and communication (Henry Jenkins, *Convergence Culture*, 2006) |
| **Social media** | Platforms where users create and share content | Shifts some gatekeeping power from editors to algorithms and users |

## How do traditional and new media differ?

| Traditional media | New media |
|---|---|
| One-to-many broadcasting | Many-to-many, interactive |
| Professional producers, editorial gatekeepers | User-generated content, algorithmic curation |
| Scheduled, linear | On demand, mobile, always on |
| National audiences and regulation | Global reach; harder to regulate |
| High entry cost | Low entry cost for producers |

**Has traditional media been undermined?** Newspaper circulation and scheduled TV audiences have
fallen and advertising has moved online. Yet traditional organisations remain trusted sources,
many of the most-shared online stories originate from them, and the largest new-media firms are
themselves conglomerates — so concentration of ownership may have moved rather than disappeared.

## Who controls the media? The main theories

**Pluralism.** Society contains many competing groups, and the media market reflects them.
Consumers choose, so owners must give audiences what they want; professional journalists value
independence; regulation and public-service broadcasting protect balance. *Criticism:* ownership is
highly concentrated and audiences can only choose from what is offered.

**Traditional (instrumental) Marxism.** Owners use the media to spread ruling-class ideology. Ralph
Miliband (*The State in Capitalist Society*, 1969) saw the media as a tool for legitimising
capitalism. Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's *propaganda model* (*Manufacturing Consent*, 1988)
identified five "filters" — ownership, advertising, reliance on official sources, "flak" and
anti-communism — that shape US news. This is the **mass manipulation** model.

**Neo-Marxism (hegemonic model).** Drawing on Gramsci's idea of **hegemony**, the Glasgow
University Media Group (*Bad News*, 1976) argued that ruling-class ideas dominate because
journalists, mostly from middle-class backgrounds, share a common-sense view of the world —
not because owners give orders. Stuart Hall's work at the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary
Cultural Studies developed this approach.

**Postmodernism.** Jean Baudrillard (*Simulacra and Simulation*, 1981) argued that media images
have become **hyperreal** — more real to us than reality. Postmodernists see media-saturated
societies with fragmented audiences who create their own meanings, making single-source control
less meaningful. *Criticism:* this underplays the economic power of media corporations.

## What influences the selection and presentation of news?

- **News values** — Johan Galtung and Mari Ruge (1965) identified criteria such as negativity,
  elite persons, unexpectedness and proximity that make an event "newsworthy".
- **Gatekeeping and agenda-setting** by editors.
- **Commercial pressures** — advertising and the need for audiences; competition for clicks.
- **Owners' influence** — direct or indirect.
- **Sources** — reliance on official spokespeople and public-relations material.
- **Practical constraints** — deadlines, budgets, location of journalists.
- **Censorship** — formal state censorship, legal limits (defamation, national security),
  regulation, and self-censorship by journalists who anticipate owners' or advertisers' views.

## How might the media influence the political process?

- **Agenda setting** — Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw's study of the 1968 US presidential
  election (published 1972) found that issues the media emphasised were the issues voters rated
  most important: the media tell people what to think *about*.
- **Opinion polls** — published polls can create bandwagon or underdog effects and shape coverage.
- **News reporting** — framing, bias and personalisation of politics.
- **Social media** — targeted political advertising; the Cambridge Analytica scandal (2018) raised
  concern about the use of personal data to influence voters.

## What is the impact of the new media?

**On globalisation:** new media speed up global communication, allowing instant global audiences,
global brands and diaspora connections (Manuel Castells, *The Rise of the Network Society*, 1996).

**As a challenge to power:** citizen journalism and social media can bypass gatekeepers, expose
abuses and help organise protest (for example during the Arab Spring, 2010–2011).

**Digital optimism versus digital pessimism:**

| Digital optimists | Digital pessimists |
|---|---|
| More choice, participation and access to information (Jenkins, 2006) | Andrew Keen, *The Cult of the Amateur* (2007): quality and expertise undermined |
| Empowerment of marginal groups | Evgeny Morozov, *The Net Delusion* (2011): authoritarian states use the internet for surveillance and propaganda |
| Networked social movements | Eli Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011): algorithms show us what we already agree with |
| Global knowledge sharing | Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019): personal data turned into profit |

**On identities and relationships:** new media allow people to experiment with and display
identities and to maintain long-distance ties. Sherry Turkle's *Alone Together* (2011) argued
constant connection can weaken face-to-face intimacy, whereas danah boyd's *It's Complicated*
(2014) found that teenagers used social media to extend, not replace, their social lives.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is pluralism or Marxism more convincing?** Many sociologists take a middle view: ownership is
concentrated, but journalists, regulation and audiences limit owners' control. Use evidence to
justify your position.

**What is the difference between manipulation and hegemony?** Manipulation is deliberate control by
owners; hegemony is dominance of ruling-class ideas through shared assumptions, without direct
orders.

**Does the new media make Marxist theory outdated?** Not necessarily — the largest platforms are
global corporations, so ownership concentration may simply have moved online.
