Section 12.1 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three learning outcomes. You must be able to name apparatus for measuring time, temperature, mass and volume (stop-watches, thermometers, balances, burettes, volumetric pipettes, measuring cylinders and gas syringes); "suggest advantages and disadvantages of experimental methods and apparatus"; and describe six terms: solvent, solute, solution, saturated solution, residue and filtrate. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so all of it is examined for every candidate, in the theory papers and in the practical paper or the Alternative to Practical.

The broader [Practical Techniques guide](/resources/practical-techniques-titrations-chromatography-separation/) lists the apparatus and the definitions. This guide concentrates on the second outcome, which is where most marks are lost: judging apparatus and methods, and explaining the judgement.

## Measuring the four quantities

| Quantity | Apparatus | Typical resolution in a school laboratory | Read to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time | stop-watch | 1 s | the nearest second |
| Temperature | thermometer, often −10 °C to +110 °C | 1 °C divisions | the nearest 0.5 °C |
| Mass | balance | at least 0.1 g | the full display |
| Volume of liquid, fixed | volumetric pipette | one marked volume, often 25.0 cm³ | the line, bottom of the meniscus |
| Volume of liquid, variable and precise | burette, 50 cm³ | 0.1 cm³ divisions | the nearest 0.05 cm³ |
| Volume of liquid, approximate | measuring cylinder | depends on size: 10 cm³ to 100 cm³ | the nearest half division |
| Volume of gas | gas syringe | a marked scale on the barrel | the nearest half division |

The syllabus asks you to take readings "to the nearest half-scale division where required". On a thermometer marked every 1 °C, a reading such as 23.5 °C is acceptable; 23.4 °C is not, because it claims more precision than the scale gives. On a burette marked every 0.1 cm³, readings end in .00 or .05, so 24.35 cm³ is correct and 24.3 cm³ should be written 24.30 cm³.

## Judging apparatus: the four questions examiners reward

When a question says "suggest an advantage" or "suggest a disadvantage", the mark goes to an answer that names a specific property and links it to the result. Ask:

1. **Is it precise enough?** A measuring cylinder is quick but its readings are less precise than a burette or a pipette. A 100 cm³ measuring cylinder is poor for measuring 8 cm³, because the reading is a small fraction of the scale; a 10 cm³ cylinder is better.
2. **Does it measure the right thing?** A pipette delivers one fixed volume only. A burette can deliver any volume up to 50 cm³, a drop at a time, which is why it is used to add a solution gradually.
3. **Can the measurement be lost or distorted?** Gas collected over water in an upturned measuring cylinder is lost if the gas dissolves in water, so ammonia cannot be collected this way and the volume of carbon dioxide may read slightly low. A gas syringe avoids contact with water.
4. **Is it practical?** A gas syringe has a limited capacity and the plunger can stick. A stop-watch depends on the reaction time of the person using it, so very short times are unreliable.

### Methods, not just apparatus

The same reasoning applies to whole methods.

- **Following a reaction by mass loss.** Placing the flask on a balance and recording the mass as gas escapes works well for carbon dioxide. It works badly for hydrogen: hydrogen has such a low mass that the change in mass is tiny and may not register on a balance reading to 0.1 g. A cotton wool plug in the neck lets gas out but stops liquid spray escaping, which would add a false loss of mass.
- **Measuring a temperature change.** A polystyrene cup loses less energy to the surroundings than a glass beaker, so the measured temperature change is closer to the true value. A lid reduces heat loss further. Stirring makes the temperature even throughout the mixture.
- **Repeating.** Repeating a measurement and finding a mean reduces the effect of random errors and shows whether a result is anomalous. It does not remove a systematic error, such as a balance that is not zeroed.

## Worked example 1: choosing volume apparatus

*A student needs 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution in a flask, then adds hydrochloric acid until the mixture is neutral. Name the apparatus for each solution and give one reason for each choice.*

**Model answer.** Volumetric pipette for the sodium hydroxide [1]: it measures one fixed volume, 25.0 cm³, precisely [1]. Burette for the hydrochloric acid [1]: the acid can be added drop by drop and the volume added read precisely [1].

*Why this scores:* each reason states what the apparatus does that the alternative does not. "Because it is accurate" alone usually scores nothing; "measures a fixed volume precisely" or "adds small, measured amounts" does.

## Worked example 2: comparing two gas methods

*Two students measure the volume of hydrogen made when magnesium reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid. Student A collects the gas over water in an upturned 100 cm³ measuring cylinder. Student B uses a gas syringe. Suggest one advantage of each method.*

**Model answer.**

- Student A: the measuring cylinder is cheap and does not stick; a larger cylinder can be used if more gas is produced [1].
- Student B: the syringe measures the gas directly, without bubbling it through water, so none of the gas can dissolve in water before it is measured [1].

*Note:* hydrogen is only very slightly soluble in water, so for this gas both methods give similar volumes. If the gas had been ammonia or carbon dioxide, the solubility argument would be much stronger, and the best answers say so.

## The six terms, used precisely

| Term | Syllabus meaning | A precise example |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | a substance that dissolves a solute | water in sea water |
| Solute | a substance that is dissolved in a solvent | sodium chloride in sea water |
| Solution | a mixture of one or more solutes dissolved in a solvent | sea water: several solutes, one solvent |
| Saturated solution | contains the maximum concentration of a solute dissolved in the solvent at a specified temperature | a solution with undissolved crystals sitting in it at 20 °C |
| Residue | a substance that remains after evaporation, distillation, filtration or a similar process | sand left on the filter paper; salt left in the dish after evaporation |
| Filtrate | a liquid or solution that has passed through a filter | the salt solution collected below the funnel |

Three points examiners look for:

- **"At a specified temperature" is part of the definition of a saturated solution.** Most solids are more soluble in hot water, so a solution saturated at 60 °C will deposit crystals when it cools to 20 °C. This is the idea behind crystallisation, covered in the [separation and purification guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-separation-and-purification/).
- **A residue is not only something left on filter paper.** The solid left in an evaporating basin, and the liquid left in the flask after distillation, are also residues.
- **A filtrate can be a solution.** It has passed through the filter, but it may still contain dissolved solutes. Filtering salty water through paper does not remove the salt.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "pipette" when the question asks for a variable volume; a volumetric pipette delivers one volume only.
- Recording a thermometer reading to 0.1 °C from a scale marked in whole degrees.
- Using "accurate" as the whole answer. Name the property: fixed volume, finer scale divisions, no contact with water, less heat loss.
- Describing a saturated solution as "a solution that cannot dissolve any more" without stating the solute or the temperature.
- Calling the solution that passes through filter paper "pure water" or "the solvent"; it is the filtrate and may contain solutes.
- Claiming that repeating removes all errors. Repeats reduce random error and identify anomalies; they do not correct faulty apparatus.

## Exam technique: command words in this section

- **Name**: give the apparatus only, spelled correctly: "volumetric pipette", "burette", "gas syringe". No explanation is needed and a wrong extra item can cost the mark.
- **Suggest**: you are expected to apply ideas to an unfamiliar set-up. Name the feature and say what it does to the measurement.
- **Describe**: for a definition, state the meaning in full; for a method, give the steps in order.
- **Explain**: give the reason, usually with "because" or "so that".

In the Alternative to Practical paper, apparatus is often shown as a diagram to identify, or a scale to read. Practise reading thermometer, burette and syringe scales to half a division, and always write the unit.
