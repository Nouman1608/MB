These questions cover section 1.3 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "Bias: how it arises and is avoided". Learn the method first from the [study guide on bias](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-bias-how-it-arises-and-is-avoided/) or the [bias revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-bias-how-it-arises-and-is-avoided-revision-notes/). Every scenario below is invented for practice.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain the difference between bias and sampling variation. **[2]**

**2.** A radio station invites listeners to text YES or NO to the question "Should the town centre be closed to cars?" 74% of the texts say YES.

**(a)** Name the type of bias in this method. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why 74% may not be a good estimate for the town's adults. **[2]**

**3.** State why each of the following questions may produce biased answers.

**(a)** "Do you agree that our excellent bus service should be expanded?" **[1]**

**(b)** "How many hours of television did you watch last year?" **[1]**

**4.** A teacher wants to estimate the mean time that the 600 pupils of a school spend on homework each night. She asks the 30 pupils in her own top-set mathematics class.

**(a)** Give one reason why the sample may be biased. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest the direction of the bias and give a reason. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe how she could choose an unbiased sample of 30 pupils. **[2]**

**5.** A pupil says, "My survey of 20 people was biased, so I will ask 200 people using the same method instead." Comment on this plan. **[2]**

**6.** A set of digital scales reads 0.2 kg when nothing is on it. It is used to weigh 40 bags of rice.

**(a)** State the effect on the mean mass recorded. **[1]**

**(b)** State the effect on the range of the masses recorded. **[1]**

**7.** Give two ways in which an interviewer could cause bias in a face-to-face survey. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**8.** A sports club with 400 members wants to know members' views on raising the annual fee. A questionnaire is left on the reception desk for a month, with a box to post completed forms. 52 forms are returned.

**(a)** Identify two features of this method that may cause bias. **[2]**

**(b)** For one of the features, explain how it could affect the results. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe an improved method that would reduce bias, including how members are chosen and how non-response is handled. **[3]**

**9.** A market researcher must interview 50 men and 50 women in a shopping centre about mobile phone use. She chooses whom to stop.

**(a)** Name this sampling method. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the results may be biased even though the numbers of men and women are fixed. **[2]**

**(c)** The shopping centre is visited only on weekday mornings. Explain how this could bias the results. **[2]**

**10.** An inspector checks the quality of loaves from a bakery with 5 ovens. Loaves come from the ovens in strict rotation: oven 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 1, 2, ... The inspector checks every 10th loaf, starting with the 3rd.

**(a)** Explain why this sample is biased. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how the inspector could obtain a representative sample of the loaves. **[2]**

**11.** A health officer asks a random sample of 150 adults, face to face, "How many cigarettes do you smoke on a typical day?"

**(a)** Explain why the mean number reported may be too low. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two changes to the method that would reduce this bias. **[2]**

**(c)** The officer says that because the adults were chosen at random, the survey cannot be biased. Explain why this is not correct. **[1]**

**12.** A town council wants to estimate the proportion of households that own a car. It uses the list of households that have paid for a residents' parking permit as its sampling frame and chooses 100 households at random from the list.

**(a)** Explain why this sampling frame will give a biased estimate, and state the direction of the bias. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest a more suitable sampling frame. **[1]**

**(c)** Of the 100 households chosen from the better frame, 30 do not reply. Explain why this could cause bias, and give one way to reduce it. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Bias is a systematic error that pushes results consistently in one direction because of the method [1]. Sampling variation is the chance difference between random samples, which scatters on both sides of the true value and decreases as the sample size increases [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Self-selection (voluntary response) bias [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Only listeners to that station who chose to text are included [1]; people with strong views, or one particular view, are more likely to text, so they may not represent the town's adults [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause to the population named in the question: listeners who text are not the same as all adults.

**3. (a)** It is a leading question: "excellent" and "agree" push the respondent towards yes [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** It relies on memory over a long period, so answers are guesses that may be systematically rounded or understated [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Only one class is asked, and a top set is not typical of all 600 pupils (for example, only one year group and one ability set) [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** The mean may be too high, because pupils in a top set may be set more homework, or spend longer on it, than average (any justified direction) [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Number all 600 pupils on the school roll [1] and choose 30 using random numbers, ignoring repeats, or take a stratified sample by year group in proportion [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The plan does not remove the bias: the same method will push the results in the same way [1]. A larger sample only reduces sampling variation, so the pupil must change the method, for example by sampling at random from a full list [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Every mass is recorded 0.2 kg too high, so the mean is 0.2 kg too high [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** The range is unchanged, because every value moves by the same amount [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Measurement bias of a constant amount shifts the average but not the spread.

**7.** Any two, for [1] each: the interviewer's tone or facial reactions suggest an answer; respondents give answers they think the interviewer wants; the interviewer rephrases questions differently for different people; the interviewer records unclear answers the way they expect; the interviewer chooses who to ask. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Any two, for [1] each: members choose themselves whether to reply (self-selection); only members who visit reception during that month can take part; the response rate is low (52 out of 400); there is no control over one person returning more than one form. **[2]**

**(b)** For example: members with strong feelings about the fee, especially those opposed to a rise, are most likely to complete the form [1], so the proportion opposed may be overstated compared with all 400 members [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Use the membership list as the sampling frame [1]; select a simple random sample (for example 80 members) using random numbers [1]; contact each chosen member directly and send reminders or follow up those who do not reply [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Quota sampling [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Within each quota the researcher chooses who to stop [1], so she may approach people who look friendly, young or not in a hurry, who may use phones differently from shoppers in general [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Weekday-morning shoppers exclude most people in full-time work or school [1], so groups with different phone use, such as working adults or teenagers, are under-represented [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Loaves numbered 3, 13, 23, ... are taken, and every 10th loaf comes from the same oven in a 5-oven cycle [1]. All loaves in the sample come from oven 3, so faults in the other ovens cannot be detected [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Take a stratified sample with the same number of loaves chosen at random from each oven [1], or use a sampling interval that is not a multiple of 5, such as every 7th loaf from a random start, so all ovens are included [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* With a cycle of 5, an interval of 10 always returns to the same oven. Check the interval against any pattern in the list.

**11. (a)** Smoking can be a sensitive topic, and in a face-to-face interview people may give a lower, more acceptable number [1]; this response bias makes the reported mean too low [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Any two, for [1] each: use an anonymous self-completed form; collect forms in a sealed box; use a trained interviewer who reads questions neutrally; ask for the number on a specific recent day rather than a "typical" day. **[2]**

**(c)** Random selection removes selection bias only; bias can still come from the question or the interview situation [1]. **[1]**

**12. (a)** Households with a parking permit almost all own a car, while households without a car are not on the list [1], so the proportion owning a car will be too high [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** A complete list of all households in the town, such as the council's register of addresses [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** The 30 households that do not reply may differ from those that do, for example in how often they are at home or their attitude to the council, so the estimate may not represent all households [1]. Reduce it by sending reminders or calling at different times of day [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a cause but not saying how it changes the result.
- Proposing "ask more people" as the remedy for bias.
- Describing a better method without saying how individuals are chosen.
- Forgetting that randomness within a poor frame still leaves bias.
- Giving a direction of bias with no reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail does a remedy need?**
Enough to be carried out: the sampling frame, how individuals are selected (for example random numbers) and, where relevant, how non-response is followed up.

**Do I always have to state the direction of bias?**
Only when the question asks for it, and then with a reason based on the context.
