## Section A

**1.** Which pair of forces forms a couple?

A: two equal forces acting in the same direction along parallel lines

B: two equal and opposite forces acting along the same line

C: two equal and opposite forces acting along parallel lines that do not coincide

D: two unequal forces acting in opposite directions along parallel lines **[1]**

**2.** A force of 50 N is applied to the end of a wrench 0.30 m long, at an angle of 40° to the wrench. What is the moment of the force about the nut?

A: 9.6 N m

B: 11.5 N m

C: 15 N m

D: 19.5 N m **[1]**

**3.** Define the torque of a couple. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a couple produces rotation but no change in linear motion. **[2]**

**5.** State where the centre of gravity of a uniform rectangular sheet of metal lies, and explain why it may be taken as the point where the weight acts. **[2]**

**6.** A compass needle experiences two equal and opposite forces of 4.5 × 10⁻³ N whose lines of action are 0.080 m apart. Calculate the torque on the needle. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A crane jib is 6.0 m long and pivoted at its lower end. A cable attached to the far end pulls with a force of 2000 N at 30° to the jib.

**(a)** Calculate the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the cable force. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the moment of the cable force about the pivot. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why pulling at 90° to the jib would give a larger moment for the same force. **[1]**

**8.** A non-uniform plank of length 3.6 m and weight 150 N rests horizontally on two supports, one at each end, A and B. The support at A pushes up with a force of 90 N.

**(a)** Calculate the force from the support at B. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the distance of the centre of gravity from A. **[2]**

**(c)** State, with a reason, which end of the plank is heavier. **[1]**

**9.** A torque wrench must apply a couple of torque 12 N m to a valve, using two handles whose lines of action are 0.30 m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the force that must be applied at each handle. **[2]**

**(b)** Show that the torque calculated about one handle is the same as the torque calculated about the midpoint between the handles. **[2]**

**10.** A uniform rod of length 2.0 m and weight 30 N is hinged at one end. It is held horizontal by a string attached to the other end.

**(a)** The string is vertical. Calculate the tension in the string. **[2]**

**(b)** The string is now attached at the same point but at 50° to the rod, with the rod still horizontal. Calculate the new tension. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the tension increases in (b). **[1]**

**11.** A cabinet stands on a level floor. Its base is 0.60 m deep and its centre of gravity is 0.90 m above the floor, halfway between the front and back.

**(a)** Calculate the angle through which it can be tilted forwards about its front edge before it topples. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain, using moments, why it returns to its original position if released before this angle. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest how loading the bottom drawer with heavy books affects its stability. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 50 × 0.30 × sin 40° = 9.6 N m, so **A** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The product of one of the forces [1] and the perpendicular distance between the lines of action of the two forces [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The two forces are equal and opposite, so the resultant force is zero and there is no linear acceleration [1]. Their lines of action are separated, so there is a resultant moment that turns the object [1]. **[2]**

**5.** At its geometric centre (where the diagonals cross) [1]. The weights of all parts of the sheet produce the same total moment about any point as a single force mg acting there, so the whole weight can be treated as acting at that point [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Torque = 4.5 × 10⁻³ × 0.080 = **3.6 × 10⁻⁴ N m** [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** d = 6.0 sin 30° = **3.0 m** [1].

**(b)** Moment = 2000 × 3.0 = **6000 N m** [1].

**(c)** At 90° the perpendicular distance equals the full 6.0 m, its largest possible value [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** F_B = 150 − 90 = **60 N** [1].

**(b)** Moments about A: 60 × 3.6 = 150 × x [1]; x = **1.44 m** from A [1].

**(c)** End A, because the centre of gravity is nearer A (and A's support carries more of the weight) [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Torque = F × d, so F = 12 ÷ 0.30 [1] = **40 N** [1].

**(b)** About one handle: that force has zero moment; the other gives 40 × 0.30 = 12 N m [1]. About the midpoint: each force gives 40 × 0.15 = 6.0 N m in the same sense, total 12 N m [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Moments about the hinge: T × 2.0 = 30 × 1.0 [1]; T = **15 N** [1].

**(b)** T sin 50° × 2.0 = 30 × 1.0 [1]; T = 30 ÷ (2.0 × 0.766) = **19.6 N** [1].

**(c)** Only the component of the tension perpendicular to the rod (T sin 50°) has a moment about the hinge, so T must be larger for that component to provide the same moment [1]. **[5]**

**11. (a)** tan θ = 0.30 ÷ 0.90 [1]; θ = **18°** [1].

**(b)** Before this angle, the vertical line through the centre of gravity still falls inside the base, behind the front edge [1], so the weight has a moment about the front edge that turns the cabinet back [1].

**(c)** It lowers the centre of gravity, so the toppling angle increases and the cabinet is more stable [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the length of the lever rather than the perpendicular distance to the line of action.
- Calculating the torque of a couple as twice F × d.
- Leaving out a beam's own weight, or placing it at the middle of a non-uniform beam.
- Writing definitions without "perpendicular" and "line of action".
- In toppling problems, using the full base width instead of the distance from the centre of gravity's vertical to the edge.
