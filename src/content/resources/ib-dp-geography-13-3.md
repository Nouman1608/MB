This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 13.3 of Unit 6, Global risks and resilience, part of the Higher Level extension of IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019), examined in Paper 3. The [13.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-local-and-global-resilience-revision-notes/) already define resilience, redundancy, diversification, risk transfer and maladaptation, give responses by scale, and summarise cyclone preparedness in Bangladesh, Japan, Singapore's water supply, the Netherlands, the Sendai Framework and the Paris Agreement. This page adds a three-part way of analysing resilience, a method for comparing measures by cost and benefit, the problem of uninsured losses, the role of risk perception and technology, and a further national example. Practise with the [13.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-13-3-practice/); the [Global risks and resilience study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-global-risks-resilience-study-guide/) gives the unit overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how people, places and systems build resilience to global risks.
- Examine how resilience is built at local, national and global scales, and how the scales interact.
- Evaluate the effectiveness of resilience strategies, including the role of technology and governance.
- Analyse why resilience is unevenly available.

## Three kinds of capacity

Resilience is easier to analyse when split into three capacities, a framework used widely in development and disaster research.

| Capacity | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Absorptive | Coping with a shock using existing resources, so that basic functions continue | Household savings, food stores, emergency shelters |
| Adaptive | Making gradual adjustments that reduce future harm | Switching to drought-tolerant crops, raising buildings, diversifying income |
| Transformative | Changing the underlying system so the risk is fundamentally reduced | Relocating a settlement, reforming land rights, building social protection |

Most measures strengthen one capacity more than the others. Emergency food aid supports absorptive capacity but does not change why a household is at risk. Transformative change is slower, more costly and often politically contested, but it can address root causes. Using the three terms in an evaluation shows whether a strategy deals with symptoms or causes.

## Comparing measures: benefit-cost ratios

Governments must choose between resilience measures with limited budgets. A **benefit-cost ratio** divides the expected benefits, usually damage avoided, by the cost. A ratio above 1 means benefits exceed costs.

### Worked example: two flood measures

Figures are invented and, for simplicity, ignore discounting of future benefits.

```
Measure A: concrete flood wall
  Cost                           40 million dollars
  Damage avoided per year         3 million dollars
  Benefits over 25 years         3 x 25 = 75 million dollars
  Benefit-cost ratio             75 / 40 = 1.875

Measure B: early-warning system and evacuation plan
  Cost                            5 million dollars
  Damage avoided per year         1 million dollars
  Benefits over 25 years         1 x 25 = 25 million dollars
  Benefit-cost ratio             25 / 5 = 5.0
```

Measure B gives far more benefit per dollar, though Measure A avoids more damage in total (75 against 25 million dollars). The evaluation: warnings save lives and movable property but cannot protect buildings, so the measures do different jobs; a benefit-cost ratio also ignores who benefits, values lives and livelihoods imperfectly, and can favour protecting expensive property over poorer neighbourhoods. Real appraisals discount future benefits, which lowers the value of long-lasting structures.

## The insurance protection gap

Insurance spreads the cost of a disaster, helping households and businesses recover quickly. The **protection gap** is the share of economic losses that is not insured. In many lower-income countries, most losses are uninsured, so households rely on savings, family networks, aid or debt. This slows recovery and can push families into poverty after each event.

Ways of closing the gap include:

- **Microinsurance**, small low-cost policies for low-income households.
- **Index insurance**, which pays out when a measurable trigger is reached, such as rainfall below a set level, rather than after inspecting each loss. It is quicker and cheaper to run, but a farmer may suffer losses without receiving a payout if the index does not match conditions on their land.
- **Regional risk pools**, through which governments share risk and receive rapid payouts.

The protection gap is a clear example of resilience being unevenly available between and within countries.

## Risk perception and behaviour

Resilience depends on what people do, and what people do depends on how they perceive risk.

- People who have not experienced a hazard, or who survived previous events unharmed, may underestimate the danger.
- Trust in authorities affects whether warnings are followed.
- Competing needs, such as protecting livestock or property from theft, can make evacuation seem riskier than staying.

Effective strategies therefore include communication, education and involving communities in planning, not only physical infrastructure.

## A national example: hurricane preparedness in Cuba

Cuba is often cited for its hurricane preparedness. A national civil defence system organises evacuations through local structures, with annual drills before the hurricane season and plans that identify vulnerable residents in each neighbourhood. Large numbers of people are evacuated ahead of major storms, and death tolls have often been lower than in some neighbouring countries hit by storms of similar strength. Evaluation: the system is strong on absorptive capacity and saving lives, but economic losses to housing and agriculture remain severe, and limited resources slow reconstruction.

## Technology and resilience

Technology strengthens resilience at all scales: satellite monitoring tracks storms, droughts and crop conditions; mobile phones deliver warnings directly to people; and data on exposure guides planning. Its limits are access, maintenance and trust. Warnings sent to phones do not reach people without devices, signal or electricity, and a technical system without local understanding of what to do may achieve little.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the effectiveness of one national strategy for building resilience.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Cuba's civil defence system builds absorptive capacity effectively: annual drills and neighbourhood plans that identify vulnerable residents allow large evacuations before major hurricanes, and death tolls have often been low compared with some neighbouring countries **[strength, using a capacity term]**. Its weakness is economic: homes, crops and infrastructure are still badly damaged, and limited resources slow reconstruction **[limitation on a second criterion]**. The strategy also does little to transform underlying exposure, such as the condition of housing **[transformative capacity assessed]**. It is therefore highly effective at protecting lives but much less effective at protecting livelihoods, so its success depends on which outcome is used to judge it **[criterion-based judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating every resilience measure as equal, without asking which capacity it builds.
- Judging measures only by total damage avoided, or only by cost.
- Ignoring the insurance gap when explaining slow recovery in poorer places.
- Assuming technology alone creates resilience.
- Describing one scale only, without showing how local, national and global action interact.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** resilience strategies with criteria: lives saved, livelihoods protected, cost, fairness and whether root causes are addressed.
- **Examine** questions on scale reward showing how global finance and national policy enable or limit local action.
- **To what extent** answers on technology need a position on how far it substitutes for, or depends on, governance and community action.
- **Discuss** should include the views of different groups, such as governments, insurers and residents.
- Interpret benefit-cost data by commenting on what the numbers leave out.
