## Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by the **adult literacy rate**? **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** ways in which educational provision differs between countries. **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by the **digital divide**? **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** inequalities in access to education other than gender. **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — Percentage of young people completing secondary school, by area, in three countries

**(a)** Using Item A, identify the country with the largest gap between urban and rural completion,

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Describe Freire's view of the purpose of literacy. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** ways in which poverty can affect the quality of schools. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why girls may have less access to secondary education than boys in some
societies. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how inequalities in access to digital education became more visible during the COVID-19
school closures. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **one** limitation of using literacy rates to compare education between countries.
**[3]**

**11.** Discuss how far poverty is the main barrier to education around the world. Use examples from
your own society or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks]

The **percentage of people aged 15 and over** [1] who can read and write, with understanding, a short
simple statement about their everyday life (UNESCO definition) [1].

**A2** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: number of compulsory years; whether schooling is free or fee-paying; numbers of trained
teachers and class sizes; buildings and resources; language of teaching; types of school; access to
pre-school, secondary or tertiary education.

**A3** [2 marks]

The gap between people who have **access to digital technology** such as devices and the internet [1]
and the **skills** to use it, and those who do not [1].

**A4** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: disability; rural location; ethnicity, caste or language; refugee status or conflict; social
class.

**A5 (a)** [2 marks]

**Country T** [1]; the gap is **42 percentage points** (64 − 22) [1].

**A5 (b)** [2 marks]

22 ÷ 64 × 100 [1] = **34%** (to the nearest whole number) [1].

**A5 (c)** [2 marks]

Any one, developed: **distance** [1] — secondary schools are often far from rural villages, so travel
is costly, time-consuming or unsafe [1]; **child labour** [1] — rural families may need young people
for farm work [1]; **teacher shortages** [1] — rural schools may struggle to attract trained teachers
[1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

Freire worked on adult literacy programmes in Brazil [1]. In *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* he argued
that literacy should develop **critical consciousness** [1], helping people understand and question
the conditions of their lives [1], rather than teaching them to repeat words passively; literacy is
therefore political [1].

**A7** [6 marks — up to 3 per way]

Two from: **large classes** [1] — too few teachers for the number of pupils [1] means less individual
attention and slower learning [1]; **untrained teachers** [1] — low pay or funding means many teachers
lack training [1], reducing the quality of teaching [1]; **resources** [1] — shortages of textbooks,
desks or electricity [1] make it hard to follow the curriculum [1]; **buildings** [1] — poor or unsafe
buildings, and no toilets or clean water [1], discourage attendance, especially by girls [1].

**A8** [6 marks — up to 3 per reason]

Two from: **domestic expectations** [1] — girls may be expected to do housework or care for siblings
[1], leaving less time for school [1]; **early marriage** [1] — girls who marry young often leave
school [1], ending their education [1]; **family priorities** [1] — families with limited money may
pay for sons' schooling first [1], seeing it as a better investment [1]; **safety** [1] — long or
unsafe journeys [1] lead parents to keep daughters at home [1]; **sanitation** [1] — lack of separate
toilets [1] keeps adolescent girls away [1]; **legal restriction** [1] — for example, the bar on
girls' secondary schooling in Afghanistan since 2021 [1] [1].

**A9** [4 marks]

When schools closed in 2020, many moved teaching online [1]. Children with their own devices and
reliable internet could keep learning [1], while those sharing a phone, lacking a connection or living
without reliable electricity could not [1], so existing gaps between richer and poorer families, and
between countries, widened [1].

**A10** [3 marks]

Literacy may be **measured differently** in different countries [1], for example by self-report in a
census or by a test [1], so the rates may not be directly comparable [1]. Or: literacy measures only a
**basic** skill [1], so it does not show differences in the quality or level of education [1], such as
secondary completion [1].

**A11** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Poverty as the main barrier:** child labour; fees and costs; hunger; underfunded schools; low
  national spending; the digital divide reflects poverty.
- **Other barriers:** gender expectations and early marriage; legal restrictions; disability; rural
  location; ethnicity, caste and language; conflict and displacement.
- **Intersection:** poverty often turns other inequalities into exclusion, but some barriers, such as
  legal restrictions on girls, exist regardless of income.
- **Perspectives:** functionalist human capital arguments; Marxist and feminist views; Freire.
- **Application:** a named society, for example how fees or distance affect school completion there.
- **Judgement:** for example, poverty is the most widespread barrier, but gender and other
  inequalities can be decisive in particular societies.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting the wrong pair of figures in a gap question.
- Confusing a percentage-point gap with a percentage.
- Describing Freire's banking model when the question asks about literacy.
- Listing effects of poverty without explaining them.
- An extended answer with no named society.
