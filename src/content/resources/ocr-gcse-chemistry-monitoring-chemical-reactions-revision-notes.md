## Key definitions

- **Amount of substance:** measured in moles (mol). A mole is **not** a mass; amount = mass ÷ Mr links the two.
- **Concentration:** how much solute is in 1 dm³ of solution, in g/dm³ or mol/dm³.
- **Standard solution:** a solution whose concentration is known accurately.
- **Titre:** the volume added from the burette to reach the end point.
- **Concordant titres:** accurate titres that agree closely, usually within 0.10 cm³.
- **Molar gas volume:** the volume of one mole of any gas; assume 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure (RTP).
- **Theoretical yield (theoretical mass):** the most product the reactants could form.
- **Actual yield:** the mass of product really collected.
- **Atom economy:** the percentage of the total mass of reactants that ends up in the desired product, from the balanced equation.

## C5.1a and C5.1f: concentration (Higher Tier only)

**Method in steps**

1. Convert the volume to dm³.
2. For g/dm³, divide the mass by the volume.
3. For mol/dm³, first find the amount (mass ÷ Mr), then divide by the volume.
4. To find a mass needed, rearrange: amount = concentration × volume, then mass = amount × Mr.

**Small worked reminder.** 2.92 g of hydrogen chloride (Mr 36.5) in 400 cm³ of solution: amount = 0.0800 mol; concentration = 0.0800 ÷ 0.400 = **0.200 mol/dm³**, which is 2.92 ÷ 0.400 = **7.30 g/dm³**.

**Standard solution checklist:** weigh accurately; dissolve in a little distilled water; transfer with all rinsings to a volumetric flask; make up to the line, bottom of meniscus at eye level; stopper and invert.

## C5.1b: titration technique (both tiers, PAG C6)

**Method in steps**

1. Pipette a measured volume of one solution (for example the alkali) into a conical flask.
2. Add two or three drops of one indicator, either phenolphthalein (pink in alkali, colourless in acid) or methyl orange (yellow in alkali, red in acid).
3. Fill a rinsed burette with the other solution; no air bubble below the tap; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
4. Run in the solution, swirling, over a white tile; slow to single drops as the colour starts to linger.
5. Stop when the colour change no longer fades. Titre = final reading − initial reading.
6. Do one rough run, then accurate runs until titres are concordant. Average the concordant ones only.

Record burette readings to the nearest 0.05 cm³ (two decimal places).

## C5.1c: titration calculations (Higher Tier only)

**Method in steps**

1. Balance the equation first, then read off the mole ratio.
2. Amount of the known solution = concentration × titre (dm³).
3. Scale by the ratio to get the amount of the unknown.
4. Divide by the unknown's volume in dm³.

**Small worked reminder.** 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide needs 16.00 cm³ of 0.125 mol/dm³ nitric acid (1 : 1). Amount of HNO₃ = 0.125 × 0.01600 = 0.00200 mol, so amount of KOH = 0.00200 mol; concentration = 0.00200 ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0800 mol/dm³**.

## C5.1d and C5.1e: gas volumes (Higher Tier only)

- At RTP, each mole of gas fills 24 dm³, or 24 000 cm³.
- Gas volumes react in the same ratio as the moles in the equation. Count only gases.
- In PAG C8, gas goes into a gas syringe or an inverted measuring cylinder full of water; wait for it to cool to room temperature before reading.

**Small worked reminder.** 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). 80 cm³ of hydrogen needs **40 cm³** of oxygen. 0.0250 mol of oxygen at RTP occupies 0.0250 × 24 = **0.600 dm³**.

## C5.1g: theoretical mass (Higher Tier only)

**Method in steps:** mass of reactant → moles (÷ Mr) → moles of product (ratio) → mass of product (× Mr).

**Small worked reminder.** CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. From 3.18 g of copper(II) oxide (Mr 79.5): 0.0400 mol CuO gives 0.0400 mol Cu, so theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 63.5 = **2.54 g**.

## C5.1h: percentage yield (both tiers)

Reasons the actual yield is lower than theoretical:

- the reaction is incomplete or reversible;
- product is lost during filtering, transferring or crystallising;
- side reactions form unwanted products.

A value above 100% means the product was weighed wet or impure.

## C5.1i and C5.1j: atom economy (both tiers)

- Use the **balanced** equation and multiply each Mr by its coefficient.
- One product only means 100% atom economy (addition reactions, for example).
- High atom economy means less waste, so it is better for sustainability and cost.

**Small worked reminder.** Making ammonia in the laboratory: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃. Total Mr of reactants = (2 × 53.5) + 74.1 = 181.1; desired product 2NH₃ = 2 × 17.0 = 34.0. Atom economy = 34.0 ÷ 181.1 × 100 = **18.8%**.

## C5.1k: choosing a pathway (Higher Tier only)

Weigh these factors for each route, using the data given:

- atom economy (waste built into the equation);
- percentage yield (what is really obtained);
- rate (how fast product forms);
- equilibrium position (for reversible reactions);
- usefulness of by-products (a saleable by-product reduces waste).

Compare the routes factor by factor, then state and justify one choice.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Atom economy vs percentage yield:** atom economy is fixed by the equation; yield is measured in the experiment.
- **g/dm³ vs mol/dm³:** convert using Mr.
- **cm³ vs dm³:** divide by 1000 going from cm³ to dm³.
- **Rough vs concordant titres:** only concordant titres are averaged.
- **Gas volume ratio vs mass ratio:** only gases follow the volume ratio, and only at the same temperature and pressure.

## Quick self-test

1. 5.85 g of sodium chloride (Mr 58.5) is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Find its concentration in mol/dm³.
2. A sodium hydroxide solution contains 4.0 g/dm³ (Mr 40.0). Give its concentration in mol/dm³.
3. How many moles of solute does 18.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol/dm³ solution contain?
4. 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide is neutralised by 12.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. Find the concentration of the alkali.
5. What volume does 0.250 mol of oxygen occupy at RTP?
6. How many moles of gas are in 96 cm³ at RTP?
7. Oxygen is made by 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Find the atom economy for oxygen (Mr: H₂O₂ = 34.0, O₂ = 32.0).
8. The theoretical yield is 5.6 g and the actual yield is 4.2 g. Find the percentage yield.
9. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. Find the theoretical mass of sodium chloride from 2.30 g of sodium (Ar Na = 23.0).
10. Accurate titres are 24.15, 24.40 and 24.25 cm³. Which are concordant, and what is the mean?

### Answers

1. 0.100 mol ÷ 0.500 dm³ = **0.200 mol/dm³**
2. 4.0 ÷ 40.0 = **0.10 mol/dm³**
3. 0.150 × 0.0180 = **0.00270 mol**
4. 0.00125 mol HCl, so 0.00125 mol NaOH; 0.00125 ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0500 mol/dm³**
5. 0.250 × 24 = **6.00 dm³**
6. 96 ÷ 24 000 = **0.00400 mol**
7. 32.0 ÷ (2 × 34.0) × 100 = **47.1%**
8. 4.2 ÷ 5.6 × 100 = **75%**
9. 0.100 mol Na gives 0.100 mol NaCl; 0.100 × 58.5 = **5.85 g**
10. **24.15 and 24.25 cm³** (within 0.10 cm³); mean = **24.20 cm³**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by a volume still in cm³, giving a concentration 1000 times too small.
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio when the equation shows 2 : 1, as with sulfuric acid and a hydroxide.
- Averaging all titres, including the rough one or an outlier.
- Recording burette readings as 24 or 24.2 instead of 24.20 cm³.
- Using 24 when the volume is in cm³ (it should be 24 000), or the reverse.
- Counting liquid water in a gas-volume ratio.
- Putting the theoretical yield on top in a percentage yield calculation.
- Leaving out a coefficient, such as the 2 in 2NH₃, when totalling Mr values for atom economy.
- In pathway questions, naming the route with the best atom economy without using the other data given.
