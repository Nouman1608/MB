## Costs and terms

The running example is **Hessary Lamps**, which makes one desk lamp and sells it for 75. A cost can be described in two ways, and answers often need both.

**Can it be traced to one lamp?**

- A **direct cost** is spent on a particular unit and can be identified with it. Hessary's direct materials (21 per lamp) and the assemblers' piece-rate pay (14 per lamp) are direct.
- An **indirect cost** supports production as a whole, such as workshop rent.

**What happens to it as output changes?**

- A **variable cost** changes in total in line with output, so the cost per lamp stays level. Materials and piece-rate pay behave this way.
- A **fixed cost** keeps the same total for the period across the normal range of output, so it falls per lamp as output grows.
- A **semi-variable cost** combines both. Hessary leases a delivery van for 6,000 a year and pays fuel of 0.80 for each lamp delivered. At 2,200 lamps the van costs 6,000 + (2,200 × 0.80) = **7,760**.
- A **stepped cost** holds steady until a capacity limit, then jumps. Hessary hires a packing machine for 9,000 a year; one machine handles up to 3,000 lamps, so lamp 3,001 doubles the cost to 18,000.

Hessary's variable overheads (power plus van fuel) come to 5 per lamp. Its fixed costs for the year are rent 22,000, salaries 19,500, packing machine 9,000, van lease 6,000 and insurance 3,000: a total of **59,500**.

**Marginal cost** is what one additional lamp adds to total cost. That is the variable cost per unit:

```
Marginal cost = 21 + 14 + 5 = 40 per lamp
```

**Contribution** is the selling price less the marginal cost. Each lamp's margin goes first towards the fixed costs; only once they are paid does it become profit.

```
Contribution per unit = 75 − 40 = 35 per lamp
Total contribution    = contribution per unit × units sold
                      = sales revenue − total variable costs
Profit                = total contribution − fixed costs
```

**Break-even** is the output at which total contribution just pays the fixed costs. Profit there is zero.

## Break-even calculations

```
Break-even point (units)  = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Break-even sales revenue  = break-even units × selling price
```

### Worked example 1: Hessary's break-even

1. Break-even = 59,500 ÷ 35 = **1,700 lamps**.
2. Break-even revenue = 1,700 × 75 = **127,500**.
3. Hessary expects to sell 2,200 lamps. Profit = (2,200 × 35) − 59,500 = **17,500**.
4. Expected sales exceed break-even by 500 lamps (about 22.7%), so sales could drop that far before a loss. This gap is usually called the margin of safety.

A lower price, a higher variable cost or extra fixed costs all push break-even up.

## Break-even charts

A break-even chart puts units of output on the horizontal axis and dollars on the vertical axis. It carries:

- a **fixed cost line**, flat at 59,500
- a **total cost line**, starting from 59,500 where output is zero and climbing by 40 for every lamp
- a **sales revenue line**, starting from zero and climbing by 75 for every lamp.

Points for Hessary's chart:

| Lamps | Total costs | Sales revenue | Profit or (loss) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 59,500 | 0 | (59,500) |
| 1,000 | 99,500 | 75,000 | (24,500) |
| 1,700 | 127,500 | 127,500 | 0 |
| 3,000 | 179,500 | 225,000 | 45,500 |

**Reading the chart:**

- Break-even is the point where the sales revenue line cuts the total cost line: 1,700 lamps and 127,500. Read both axes.
- Below 1,700 lamps total cost is above revenue (loss); above 1,700 revenue is higher (profit).
- The vertical gap between the two lines at any output equals the profit or loss at that output. At 1,000 lamps the gap is 24,500 of loss.
- Beyond 3,000 lamps both cost lines would jump by 9,000 (the stepped packing cost).

### Uses and limitations of break-even analysis

| Uses | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Shows the lowest sales that avoid a loss | Every cost must be sorted into fixed or variable; semi-variable costs need splitting |
| Tests "what if" changes in price or costs before acting | Straight lines assume one price and one variable cost per unit at all outputs |
| Shows how far sales could fall before a loss | Fixed costs assumed constant, which stepped costs break |
| A chart is easy for non-accountants, such as a lender, to follow | Assumes all output is sold and a single product (or fixed sales mix) |
| | Only as good as the forecasts; a hand-drawn chart is less precise than a calculation |

## Calculating profit using marginal costing

Under marginal costing, only variable production costs are product costs. Fixed costs are charged in full to the period, and closing inventory is valued at marginal cost.

### Worked example 2: Hessary's marginal costing income statement

Hessary makes 2,400 lamps and sells 2,200. There is no opening inventory.

```
                                              $
Sales revenue (2,200 × 75)                 165,000
Variable cost of production (2,400 × 40)    96,000
Less closing inventory (200 × 40)           (8,000)
Variable cost of sales                      88,000
Contribution                                77,000
Fixed costs                                 59,500
Profit for the year                         17,500
```

The 200 unsold lamps carry no share of the fixed costs.

### Benefits and limitations of marginal costing

**Benefits**

- It shows contribution clearly, so managers see what each product adds towards fixed costs and profit.
- It suits short-term decisions, because unchanged fixed costs stay out of the comparison.
- No arbitrary sharing out of fixed overheads is needed.

**Limitations**

- Some costs are hard to split into fixed and variable elements.
- Prices based on marginal cost alone may not cover fixed costs in the long run.
- Inventory valued at variable cost ignores the fixed production costs used to make it.

## Marginal costing in decision making

Every decision uses one test: compare the contribution gained or lost with any fixed costs that actually change. Fixed costs paid whatever the choice are not relevant. Then weigh non-financial factors.

### Make or buy

Hessary makes its own lamp shades: materials 6.50, labour 4.20 and variable overheads 1.30, plus 3.00 of absorbed fixed overheads. An outside supplier quotes 13.10 a shade. Hessary needs 2,400 shades a year.

- Marginal cost of making = 6.50 + 4.20 + 1.30 = **12.00**. The full cost of 15.00 is the wrong comparison, because the absorbed fixed overheads continue either way.
- Buying costs 13.10 − 12.00 = 1.10 more per shade: 2,400 × 1.10 = **2,640** extra cost.
- Buying would let Hessary end a 3,500 lease on a shade press. Net effect = 3,500 − 2,640 = **860** better off by buying.

Non-financial factors: the supplier's quality and reliability, and loss of control and of skilled staff.

### Acceptance of additional work

A hotel group offers to buy 300 lamps at 52 each, with its logo plate fitted at an extra 4 per lamp. Hessary has spare capacity.

- Contribution per lamp on the order = 52 − 40 − 4 = **8**. Total = 300 × 8 = **2,400**.

If only 200 lamps of capacity were spare, 100 normal sales would be lost at 35 each: 2,400 − 3,500 = **1,100 worse off**. Always check capacity first.

Non-financial factors: regular customers may ask for the lower price; the hotel group may reorder.

### Price setting

Short term, any price above marginal cost adds contribution. Long term, the price must also cover fixed costs and leave a profit.

Next year Hessary expects to sell 2,000 lamps, fixed costs rise to 63,000, and the owner wants profit of 15,000.

```
Contribution needed   = 63,000 + 15,000 = 78,000
Contribution per lamp = 78,000 ÷ 2,000  = 39
Price                 = 40 + 39         = 79
```

Check competitors' prices and whether 2,000 lamps will still sell at 79.

### Optimum use of scarce resources

When one input is short, the **limiting factor**, rank products by contribution per unit of that input.

Corrallo Rugs has 1,880 weaving hours next quarter and fixed costs of 19,000.

| Rug | Price | Variable cost | Contribution | Hours each | Contribution per hour | Demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kilim | 180 | 108 | 72 | 6 | 12 | 150 |
| Runner | 95 | 59 | 36 | 2 | 18 | 300 |
| Medallion | 320 | 200 | 120 | 8 | 15 | 100 |

All demand needs 2,300 hours, so hours are limiting. Rank: Runner, Medallion, Kilim.

| Rug | Rugs made | Hours | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runner | 300 | 600 | 10,800 |
| Medallion | 100 | 800 | 12,000 |
| Kilim | 80 | 480 | 5,760 |
| Total | | 1,880 | 28,560 |

Maximum profit = 28,560 − 19,000 = **9,560**. Ranking by contribution per rug instead (Medallion, Kilim, Runner) would give only 26,040 of contribution. Non-financial point: Kilim customers turned away may not return.

### Closing a potentially loss-making line or production department

Carrowmore Textiles runs three production lines. Fixed costs of 136,500 are apportioned by floor area.

| | Line X | Line Y | Line Z | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 210,000 | 140,000 | 90,000 | 440,000 |
| Variable costs | 126,000 | 77,000 | 64,000 | 267,000 |
| Contribution | 84,000 | 63,000 | 26,000 | 173,000 |
| Fixed costs | 63,000 | 42,000 | 31,500 | 136,500 |
| Profit or (loss) | 21,000 | 21,000 | (5,500) | 36,500 |

If line Z closed, only 9,000 of its fixed costs would stop. Closing loses 26,000 of contribution and saves 9,000, so profit falls by **17,000** to 19,500. Even if the space were let for 12,000 a year, profit would be 31,500, still lower than now. Line Z should stay open on financial grounds.

Non-financial factors: job losses, morale, and customers who buy across lines.

### Target profit

```
Units for a target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit
```

For a profit of 28,000, Hessary needs (59,500 + 28,000) ÷ 35 = **2,500 lamps**.

For a profit of 50,000 the first answer is (59,500 + 50,000) ÷ 35 = 3,128.6 lamps. That passes the 3,000-lamp limit of one packing machine, so fixed costs rise by 9,000:

```
(68,500 + 50,000) ÷ 35 = 3,385.7, so 3,386 lamps
```

Round up: 3,385 lamps would fall just short of the target.

## Common errors

- Setting a supplier's quote against full cost (with absorbed overheads) when marginal cost is the right comparison.
- Choosing the product order by contribution per unit although an input is in short supply.
- Treating every apportioned fixed cost as saved when a line or department closes.
- Valuing closing inventory at full cost in a marginal costing statement.
