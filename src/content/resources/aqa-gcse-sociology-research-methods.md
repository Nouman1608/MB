This guide covers **Section 3.7: Sociological Research Methods** in AQA GCSE Sociology (8192). Unlike
the site's existing content-topic guides (Families, Education, and the Paper 2 topics of Crime and Social
Stratification), Research Methods is a cross-cutting skills section, examined across both Paper 1 and
Paper 2 rather than confined to one paper.

## Where this fits in 8192

Because Research Methods is examined on both papers, questions on this section can appear alongside
questions on Families, Education, Crime, or Social Stratification, often asking candidates to evaluate how
a particular method would work for investigating a specific sociological topic rather than testing methods
in isolation.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA GCSE SOCIOLOGY (8192) -- SECTION 3.7: SOCIOLOGICAL RESEARCH METHODS**

- Research design: aims, hypotheses, pilot studies, sampling methods, and how researchers analyse the
  data they collect
- Qualitative and quantitative methods of research, including questionnaires, interviews, observations
  (participant and non-participant, overt and covert), and mixed-methods approaches
- Types of data: qualitative versus quantitative data, and official versus non-official statistics
- Primary and secondary sources of data
- Interpreting and presenting data: graphs, diagrams, charts and tables
- Practical issues affecting method choice: time, cost, and access to research subjects
- Ethical issues in sociological research: informed consent, confidentiality, and protection from harm

## How to approach it

Because this section is assessed within the context of the other topics rather than as a standalone paper
section, the strongest preparation links each research method to the kinds of sociological questions it
suits -- for example, why a sociologist studying family life behind closed doors might prefer covert
participant observation over a questionnaire, despite its practical and ethical complications.

## Qualitative vs quantitative data

Quantitative data is numerical and allows statistical analysis and easy comparison across a large sample,
typically gathered through structured questionnaires, official statistics, or structured observation
using tally counts. Qualitative data is descriptive and captures depth, meaning and context that numbers
alone cannot, typically gathered through unstructured interviews, open-ended questionnaire responses, or
participant observation. Many sociological studies combine both (a mixed-methods approach) to gain the
statistical generalisability of quantitative data alongside the depth and validity of qualitative data.

## Practical and ethical issues, together

A method that is practically convenient is not automatically ethical, and a method that is ethically
sound is not automatically practical -- exam answers that evaluate a research method well typically address
both dimensions rather than only one. Covert observation, for instance, can solve the practical problem of
participants changing their behaviour when they know they are being watched (the observer effect), but
raises serious ethical concerns around informed consent that a researcher must weigh against the
practical benefit.

## Sampling and pilot studies

Before collecting data, researchers must decide how to select participants and often test their approach
first. A pilot study is a small-scale trial run of the research method, carried out before the main study,
to identify problems with question wording, timing, or procedure while they are still cheap and easy to
fix. Sampling methods used in sociological research include random sampling (every member of the target
population has an equal chance of selection), stratified sampling (the population is divided into
relevant subgroups, such as age or gender, and sampled proportionally from each), and snowball sampling
(existing participants recruit further participants from their own networks, useful for hard-to-reach
groups). The choice of sampling method affects how representative the final sample is of the wider
population being studied, which in turn affects how confidently findings can be generalised.

## Worked example: choosing a method for a given research question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official past
paper or mark scheme.

```
Research question: "Do informal rules within a family
differ from its formally stated rules?"

Step 1 - consider what kind of data is needed:
detailed, contextual understanding of everyday family life
points toward qualitative data.

Step 2 - consider access:
family life is largely private, so a method that can observe or
elicit detail about private life is needed.

Step 3 - weigh a specific method's practical and ethical
trade-offs:
participant observation could reveal real informal rules, but
raises consent and privacy concerns if family members are
unaware they are being studied.

Step 4 - propose a method and justify it against an alternative:
e.g. recommend semi-structured interviews with informed consent
over covert observation, trading some depth of insight for a
stronger ethical basis.
```

## Common mistakes

Describing a research method without linking it to the specific sociological topic or research question
being investigated. Treating practical issues and ethical issues as the same category, when they are
distinct and both need addressing in a full evaluation. Assuming quantitative data is always "more
scientific" or superior to qualitative data, when each has its own strengths depending on the research
aim. Confusing primary data (collected first-hand by the researcher) with secondary data (already
existing, collected by someone else, such as official statistics).

## Quick revision checklist

- Be able to compare qualitative and quantitative methods, including a strength and weakness of each.
- Practise justifying a method choice against a specific sociological research question, not just in the
  abstract.
- Distinguish primary from secondary data, and official from non-official statistics.
- Address both practical issues (time, cost, access) and ethical issues (consent, confidentiality, harm)
  when evaluating a method.
- Practise interpreting data presented in graphs, charts and tables, since these commonly accompany
  exam questions on this section.
