This guide covers **3.2.2.2 Socialisation**, part of the Socialisation
and Social Control topic in Paper 2 of OxfordAQA International GCSE
Sociology (9292), Version 1.0. This is a new qualification, first
examined May/June 2028.

## Scope of this guide

3.2.2 Socialisation and Social Control has three parts: 3.2.2.1
Individuals and Society, 3.2.2.2 Socialisation, and 3.2.2.3 Social
Identities and Social Change. This resource focuses on 3.2.2.2, the
socialisation content itself — types of socialisation, its agencies, and
the sociological perspectives applied to it — since this alone covers
enough distinct content for a full resource. The concepts of culture and
the sociological imagination (3.2.2.1) and social control/social
identity (3.2.2.3) are left for separate resources.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE SOCIOLOGY (9292) — 3.2.2.2 SOCIALISATION**

- Primary and secondary socialisation; re-socialisation; inadequate
socialisation
- The social construction of identities through socialisation
- Agencies of socialisation: families; schools; peers; media; religions;
workplace
- Processes/methods used by these agencies
- Sociological perspectives on socialisation: functionalist; Marxist;
feminist; social action/interactionist

## How to approach it

Start by fixing the vocabulary of socialisation types precisely, since
these terms are easy to blur together: **primary socialisation** is the
early learning of norms and values, mainly within the family;
**secondary socialisation** happens later, through wider agencies such
as schools, peers, media and the workplace; **re-socialisation** is the
process of learning new norms and values that replace previously
learned ones, often following a major life change; and **inadequate
socialisation** refers to socialisation that has failed to properly
instil a society's shared norms and values, which some sociological
perspectives link to deviant or antisocial behaviour later in life.

For the agencies of socialisation, prepare each one (family, school,
peers, media, religion, workplace) with a specific process or method it
uses — for example, families through direct instruction and role
modelling, schools through the formal curriculum and the "hidden
curriculum" of implicit rules, media through the modelling of
behaviours and norms in the content it presents — since simply listing
agency names without explaining how each one socialises individuals is
a common source of lost marks.

The four perspectives (functionalist, Marxist, feminist, social
action/interactionist) each explain socialisation differently: the
functionalist view sees socialisation as necessary for social order and
value consensus; the Marxist view sees it as a means by which the
ruling class reproduces its dominance and controls the workforce; the
feminist view focuses on how socialisation reproduces gender roles and
inequality; and the social action/interactionist view focuses on how
individuals actively interpret and negotiate the norms they are
socialised into, rather than simply absorbing them passively.

The feminist and social action/interactionist perspectives deserve the
same specific treatment. A feminist answer should point to a concrete
mechanism, such as how family and media agencies model different
behavioural expectations for boys and girls, reinforcing gendered
divisions of labour and identity. A social action/interactionist answer
should emphasise that individuals are not simply passive recipients of
socialisation — they actively interpret, negotiate and sometimes resist
the norms and values presented to them by different agencies, which is
the key point of difference from the more structural functionalist and
Marxist views.

## Worked example: comparing two perspectives on socialisation

A question asks candidates to compare the functionalist and Marxist
views of socialisation.

```
Functionalist:  Socialisation instils shared norms and values that
                create value consensus, allowing society to function
                smoothly and cohesively; agencies like the family and
                school are seen as performing a positive, necessary
                function.

Marxist:        Socialisation is used by the ruling class (the
                bourgeoisie) to instil values that benefit capitalism,
                such as obedience and acceptance of inequality,
                preparing individuals to accept their position in the
                workforce; agencies like schools are seen as
                reproducing class inequality rather than serving
                everyone equally.

Contrast:       Functionalism presents socialisation as broadly
                beneficial and consensual, while Marxism presents it as
                serving the interests of one social class over another.
```

This side-by-side structure — what each perspective claims, followed by
an explicit point of contrast — is what "compare" questions on
perspectives are looking for.

## Key terms to define precisely

**Socialisation** — the lifelong process by which individuals learn the
norms, values and behaviours expected within their culture or society.
**Agency of socialisation** — any group or institution, such as the
family, school or media, through which socialisation takes place.
**Norms** — the specific, unwritten rules of behaviour expected in a
given situation. **Values** — the broader beliefs a society or group
holds about what is important, right or desirable. **Social
construction of identity** — the idea that a person's sense of who they
are is not fixed or natural, but built up over time through their
interactions with socialising agencies. Sociology mark schemes
frequently distinguish between candidates who use these terms
accurately and candidates who use them as loose synonyms for each
other, so keeping norms and values clearly distinct — and being precise
about what counts as an "agency" versus a "process" of socialisation —
is worth the extra revision time.

## Common mistakes

Confusing primary and secondary socialisation, or omitting
re-socialisation and inadequate socialisation as distinct concepts in
their own right. Listing agencies of socialisation without explaining
the specific process or method by which each one socialises
individuals. Describing a perspective's view of socialisation in vague
terms ("Marxists think it's about class") rather than explaining the
specific mechanism the perspective identifies. Treating the four
perspectives as interchangeable rather than being able to state how
each one differs from the others.

## Quick revision checklist

- Define primary, secondary, re- and inadequate socialisation
precisely and be able to distinguish all four.
- Prepare each agency of socialisation with a specific process it uses.
- Learn one clear claim from each of the four sociological perspectives
on socialisation.
- Practise structuring a "compare two perspectives" answer with an
explicit point of contrast.
