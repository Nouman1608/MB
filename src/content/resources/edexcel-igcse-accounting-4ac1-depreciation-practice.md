## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the main cause of depreciation for each asset.

**(a)** Laptops used by office staff. **[1]**
**(b)** A five-year lease on a shop. **[1]**
**(c)** A tractor used every day on a farm. **[1]**

**2.** Explain two causes of depreciation. **[4]**

**3.** A machine costs 14,500 and installation costs 500. It has a residual value of 1,500 and a useful life of 6 years. Calculate the annual straight line depreciation. **[2]**

**4.** Equipment costing 18,000 is depreciated at 25% reducing balance. Calculate the depreciation for each of the first three years and the carrying value at the end of Year 3. **[3]**

**5.** Distinguish between the straight line and reducing balance methods of depreciation. **[4]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** Oakridge Printing has a year end of 31 March. It bought a printer for 12,600 on 1 July 2026. Printers are depreciated at 20% a year on cost, time apportioned by month.

**(a)** Calculate depreciation for the year ended 31 March 2027. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate depreciation for the year ended 31 March 2028 and the carrying value at that date. **[2]**

**7.** Hartley Haulage has a 31 December year end. Lorries are depreciated at 20% a year on cost, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal. At 1 January 2026:

| Lorry | Bought | Cost |
|---|---|---:|
| X | 2024 | 36,000 |
| Y | 2025 | 24,000 |

The provision for depreciation at 1 January 2026 was 19,200. On 1 March 2026 lorry Z was bought for 30,000 by bank transfer.

**(a)** Show that the opening provision of 19,200 is correct. **[2]**
**(b)** Prepare the lorries at cost account and the provision for depreciation account for 2026. **[5]**
**(c)** Prepare the statement of financial position extract for lorries at 31 December 2026. **[2]**

**8.** Continuing question 7, on 30 June 2027 Hartley Haulage sold lorry X for 12,500, received by bank transfer.

**(a)** Prepare the disposal account. **[5]**
**(b)** Prepare the provision for depreciation account for 2027. **[4]**

**9.** Mossley Kitchens bought equipment for 8,000 in Year 1. It is depreciated at 40% reducing balance, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal. It was sold in Year 4 for 2,100.

**(a)** Calculate the profit or loss on disposal. **[4]**
**(b)** Prepare the journal entries to record the disposal. Narratives are not required. **[3]**

**10.** Brennan Crafts buys a machine for 40,000. It expects a residual value of 4,000 after 5 years. Profit before depreciation is 50,000 in each of Years 1 and 2.

**(a)** Calculate profit for Years 1 and 2 using the straight line method. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate profit for Years 1 and 2 using 36% reducing balance. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the choice of method changes the trend in profit. **[2]**

**11.** The owner of a shop says: "Depreciation is pointless because no money leaves the business, and if we do charge it, it should be put aside to buy a replacement."

**(a)** Explain why depreciation is charged even though it involves no payment. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the owner is wrong that depreciation provides money for a replacement. **[2]**

**12.** Recommend, with reasons, a depreciation method for each asset.

**(a)** Shop fittings expected to be used evenly over 10 years. **[2]**
**(b)** Delivery vans that lose much of their value in the first two years and need more repairs as they age. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Obsolescence [1]. (b) Passage of time [1]. (c) Wear and tear [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Any two, each explained. Wear and tear [1]: using an asset makes it deteriorate, for example a van's engine wears with mileage, shortening its useful life [1]. Obsolescence [1]: newer, more efficient versions make the asset outdated even if it still works, for example old computers [1]. Also acceptable: passage of time (lease); inadequacy (asset too small for the business). **[4]**

**3.** (14,500 + 500 − 1,500) ÷ 6 [1] = **2,250** a year [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Year 1: 18,000 × 25% = 4,500 [1]. Year 2: 13,500 × 25% = 3,375 [1]. Year 3: 10,125 × 25% = 2,531.25; carrying value **7,593.75** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Straight line charges the same amount every year [1], calculated as (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life [1]. Reducing balance applies a fixed percentage to the opening carrying value [1], so the charge is highest in Year 1 and falls each year, and residual value is not deducted first [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 12,600 × 20% × 9/12 [1] = **1,890** [1].
**(b)** 12,600 × 20% = **2,520** [1]. Carrying value = 12,600 − 1,890 − 2,520 = **8,190** [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Lorry X: 36,000 × 20% × 2 years (2024, 2025) = 14,400 [1]. Lorry Y: 24,000 × 20% × 1 year = 4,800. Total 14,400 + 4,800 = 19,200 [1].
**(b)**

Lorries at cost

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 Jan Balance b/d | 60,000 | 31 Dec Balance c/d | 90,000 |
| 1 Mar Bank | 30,000 | | |
| | **90,000** | | **90,000** |

Provision for depreciation of lorries

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 31 Dec Balance c/d | 37,200 | 1 Jan Balance b/d | 19,200 |
| | | 31 Dec Depreciation | 18,000 |
| | **37,200** | | **37,200** |

Lorries account: opening 60,000 and purchase 30,000 [1], balance 90,000 [1]. Depreciation for 2026: X 7,200 + Y 4,800 + Z 6,000 = 18,000 [1]. Provision opening balance on the credit side [1]. Balance c/d 37,200 [1].
**(c)** Lorries: cost 90,000 [1]; accumulated depreciation 37,200; carrying value **52,800** [1]. **[9]**

**8. (a)** Accumulated depreciation on X: 2024, 2025 and 2026 at 7,200 = 21,600. Carrying value 36,000 − 21,600 = 14,400, so loss = 14,400 − 12,500 = 1,900.

Disposal of lorry

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 30 Jun Lorries at cost | 36,000 | 30 Jun Provision for depreciation | 21,600 |
| | | 30 Jun Bank | 12,500 |
| | | 31 Dec Income statement (loss) | 1,900 |
| | **36,000** | | **36,000** |

Cost 36,000 debited [1]. Accumulated depreciation 21,600 credited [1]. Proceeds 12,500 credited [1]. Loss of 1,900 [1] on the credit side, transferred to the income statement [1].
**(b)** 2027 depreciation: Y 4,800 + Z 6,000 = 10,800 (none on X in the year of disposal).

Provision for depreciation of lorries

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 30 Jun Disposal | 21,600 | 1 Jan Balance b/d | 37,200 |
| 31 Dec Balance c/d | 26,400 | 31 Dec Depreciation | 10,800 |
| | **48,000** | | **48,000** |

Transfer to disposal 21,600 [1]. Opening 37,200 [1]. Charge 10,800 [1]. Balance c/d 26,400 [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Only lorry X's share of the provision moves to the disposal account; the rest stays for Y and Z.

**9. (a)** Year 1: 8,000 × 40% = 3,200; Year 2: 4,800 × 40% = 1,920; Year 3: 2,880 × 40% = 1,152 [1]. Accumulated depreciation 6,272 [1]. Carrying value 8,000 − 6,272 = 1,728 [1]. Profit on disposal 2,100 − 1,728 = **372** [1].
**(b)** Dr Disposal 8,000, Cr Equipment at cost 8,000 [1]. Dr Provision for depreciation 6,272, Cr Disposal 6,272; Dr Bank 2,100, Cr Disposal 2,100 [1]. Dr Disposal 372, Cr Income statement (profit on disposal) 372 [1]. **[7]**

**10. (a)** Straight line: (40,000 − 4,000) ÷ 5 = 7,200 [1]. Profit 42,800 in both years [1].
**(b)** Year 1: 40,000 × 36% = 14,400, profit 35,600 [1]. Year 2: 25,600 × 36% = 9,216, profit 40,784 [1].
**(c)** Under straight line profit is flat because the charge is constant [1]; under reducing balance the charge falls, so profit appears to rise even though trading is unchanged [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* This is why the consistency concept matters: switching method would distort comparisons.

**11. (a)** The machine or asset helps earn revenue over several years [1]; under the accruals concept part of its cost must be matched against each year's revenue [1], otherwise profit would be understated in the year of purchase and overstated afterwards [1].
**(b)** Depreciation is a book entry: debit expense, credit provision; no cash is moved or set aside [1]. The cash was spent when the asset was bought, so a replacement needs cash from other sources [1]. **[5]**

**12. (a)** Straight line [1], because the fittings give equal benefit each year, so an equal charge matches cost to the periods that benefit [1].
**(b)** Reducing balance [1], because the high early charge reflects the fast early fall in value, and falling depreciation plus rising repairs gives a more even yearly cost [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the reducing balance rate to cost in later years.
- Deducting residual value before applying a reducing balance rate.
- Ignoring the time-apportionment policy, or applying it in the wrong year.
- Moving the full provision balance into the disposal account.
- Placing the profit or loss on disposal on the wrong side.
- Describing depreciation as a cash reserve.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know whether to charge depreciation in the year an asset is sold?**
Follow the policy stated in the question. A common policy is a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal; another is month-by-month apportionment.

**Why does only part of the provision go to the disposal account?**
The provision covers every asset in that class. Only the accumulated depreciation on the asset being sold is removed; the rest belongs to the assets still owned.
