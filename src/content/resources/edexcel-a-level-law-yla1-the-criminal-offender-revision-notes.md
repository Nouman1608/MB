## Actus reus (2.2.1)

- **Factual causation**: but for (*R v White* [1910]).
- **Legal causation**: operating and substantial cause (*R v Smith* [1959]); thin skull (*R v Blaue* [1975]); medical treatment breaks the chain only if palpably wrong (*R v Jordan* [1956]) or the wound becomes insignificant (*R v Cheshire* [1991]); foreseeable escape does not break it (*R v Roberts* [1971]).
- **Omissions**: contract (*Pittwood* [1902]), relationship (*Gibbins and Proctor* [1918]), assumed care (*Stone and Dobinson* [1977]), public office (*Dytham* [1979]), dangerous situation created (*Miller* [1983]), statute.
- **More on intervening acts**: a reasonable act of self-defence by a third party does not break the chain (*R v Pagett* [1983], where police shot a girl used as a human shield); the **free, deliberate and informed** act of an adult victim can break it (*R v Kennedy (No 2)* [2007], where the victim injected himself with drugs D had supplied).
- **Answer order**: state the general rule (no liability for omissions), then the duty situation that applies, then whether D breached it and whether the breach caused the result.

## Mens rea (2.2.2) and strict liability (2.2.3)

- **Direct intention**: aim or purpose (*Mohan* [1975]). **Oblique**: virtual certainty, appreciated by D (*Woollin* [1998]).
- **Subjective recklessness**: D foresaw the risk and took it unreasonably (*Cunningham* [1957]; *R v G* [2003]).
- **Transferred malice**: same offence only (*Latimer* [1886] v *Pembliton* [1874]).
- **Coincidence**: continuing act (*Fagan* [1969]).
- **OAPA 1861**: s 47 (ABH), s 20 (wound or inflict GBH, foresight of some harm), s 18 (with intent).
  - **s 47**: an assault or battery occasioning actual bodily harm; the *mens rea* is only that of the assault or battery, with no need to foresee ABH (*R v Savage; DPP v Parmenter* [1991]).
  - **s 20**: a **wound** means breaking both layers of the skin (*JCC v Eisenhower* [1984]); **GBH** means really serious harm (*DPP v Smith* [1961]); D must foresee **some** harm, not serious harm (*R v Mowatt* [1968]).
  - **s 18**: wounding or causing GBH **with intent** to cause GBH (or to resist arrest), so it is a specific intent offence with a maximum of life imprisonment.
- **Strict liability**: presumption of *mens rea* (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970]); *Gammon* [1985] test; *Callow v Tillstone* [1900]; *Harrow LBC v Shah* [1999].

## Property offences (2.2.4 to 2.2.7)

| Offence | Source | Elements | Key cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theft | TA 1968 ss 1 to 6 | Dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with intention permanently to deprive | *Ivey* [2017]; *Morris* [1983]; *Gomez* [1993]; *Lloyd* [1985] |
| Robbery | TA 1968 s 8 | Theft plus force or fear of force, immediately before or at the time, in order to steal | *Dawson and James* [1976]; *Hale* [1978]; *Robinson* [1977] |
| Burglary | TA 1968 s 9(1)(a), (b) | Entry as trespasser to building or part, with intent (a) or then offending (b) | *Collins* [1972]; *Ryan* [1996]; *Walkington* [1979] |
| Blackmail | TA 1968 ss 21, 34 | Unwarranted demand with menaces, with a view to gain or intent to cause loss | |
| Fraud | FA 2006 ss 2, 5 | Dishonest false representation intending gain or loss | |
| Obtaining services | FA 2006 s 11 | Dishonestly obtaining services, intending not to pay | |
| Making off | TA 1978 s 3 | Knowing payment on the spot required, dishonestly makes off, intending to avoid payment permanently | *Allen* [1985] |
| Criminal damage | CDA 1971 s 1 | Destroying or damaging another's property, intending or reckless | Lawful excuse s 5; *Jaggard v Dickinson* [1981] |

**Dishonesty (s 2)**: not dishonest if belief in a legal right, belief in owner's consent, or owner not findable by reasonable steps.

**Theft section by section:**

- **s 3 appropriation**: any assumption of the rights of an owner; touching or swapping a price label can be enough (*Morris*), and consent of the owner does not prevent appropriation (*Gomez*).
- **s 4 property**: money, real and personal property, things in action; confidential information is not property (*Oxford v Moss* [1979]); limits on land, wild plants and wild creatures in s 4(2) to (4).
- **s 5 belonging to another**: anyone with possession, control or a proprietary interest; s 5(3) property received for a particular purpose; s 5(4) property got by another's mistake that must be returned.
- **s 6 intention to permanently deprive**: borrowing is not enough unless the thing is returned with its "goodness" or value gone (*Lloyd*, where films were copied and returned, was not theft).
- **Dishonesty test** (*Ivey*, applied to criminal cases in *R v Barton and Booth* [2020]): (1) what did D actually believe about the facts? (2) was the conduct dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people? D need not realise it was dishonest.

**Other offences, key points:**

- **Robbery**: if there is no theft there is no robbery (*Robinson*, a claim of right); "immediately before or at the time" is read flexibly because appropriation can be continuing (*Hale*).
- **Burglary**: entry need only be effective, not complete (*Ryan*, head and arm through a window); s 9(1)(a) intent offences are theft, GBH or criminal damage; s 9(1)(b) offences committed after entry are theft or GBH.
- **Blackmail**: a demand is **unwarranted** unless D believes there are reasonable grounds for making it **and** that using the menaces is a proper way to reinforce it (s 21(1)).
- **Fraud by false representation (s 2)**: the representation may be express or implied, about fact, law or a state of mind; the offence is complete when the representation is made, even if no one is deceived and nothing is gained.
- **Criminal damage**: damage need not be permanent if it costs time or money to put right (*Hardman v Chief Constable of Avon* [1986], water-soluble paint on a pavement).

## Defences (2.2.8, 2.2.9)

- **Duress**: threat of death or serious injury; *Graham* [1982] test; not for murder (*Howe* [1987]) or attempted murder (*Gotts* [1992]); lost by voluntary association (*Hasan* [2005]). **Of circumstances**: *Willer* [1986]; *Martin* [1989].
- **Intoxication**: voluntary, no defence to basic intent (*Majewski* [1977]); Dutch courage (*Gallagher* [1963]); involuntary only if no *mens rea* formed (*Kingston* [1994]; *Hardie* [1985]).

## Sentencing (2.2.10)

Custody (so serious nothing else will do; may be suspended); community orders (requirements such as unpaid work, curfew); fines (by seriousness and means); discharges (conditional, absolute). Guilty plea: up to one third off.

**Purposes of sentencing** for adults (Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 142, the Act the specification names; now restated in s 57 of the Sentencing Act 2020): punishment; reduction of crime, including by deterrence; reform and rehabilitation; protection of the public; and reparation by offenders to those affected. Courts follow **Sentencing Council guidelines**, assessing **culpability** and **harm**, then adjusting for **aggravating** factors (such as previous convictions or offences against vulnerable victims) and **mitigating** factors (such as genuine remorse or good character).

**Evaluating sentences**: custody protects the public but is expensive and may not rehabilitate; community orders allow reparation and keep employment and family ties, but may be seen as soft; fines are cheap to administer but depend on the offender's means.

## Worked problem example

*Kai pushes Lena so that she falls, then grabs her bag and runs. Later he finds she had only a few coins in it and throws the bag into a canal.*

1. **Theft**: grabbing the bag is an appropriation (s 3) of property (s 4) belonging to Lena (s 5); throwing it away shows intention to permanently deprive (s 6); applying *Ivey*, ordinary decent people would see this as dishonest.
2. **Robbery (s 8)**: the push is force, used immediately before the theft and in order to steal; even slight force can be enough (*Dawson and James*). Kai is likely guilty of robbery.
3. **Non-fatal offence**: if Lena is bruised, consider s 47 ABH: a battery causing harm, with no need for Kai to foresee the harm (*Savage*).
4. **Sentence**: robbery is a serious offence, and the use of force on a victim is relevant to harm and culpability under the guidelines.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Skipping factual causation and going straight to legal causation.
- Using direct intention when the facts point to oblique intention (D's aim was something else).
- Treating s 20 as needing foresight of serious harm: some harm is enough.
- Stating robbery without first proving every element of theft.
- Saying borrowing is never theft: *Lloyd* allows for borrowing that removes the goodness of the thing.
- Running intoxication as a defence to a basic intent offence such as s 47 or s 20.
- Forgetting that duress is not available for murder or attempted murder.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actus reus | The guilty act, omission or state of affairs |
| Mens rea | The guilty mind |
| Oblique intention | Intention inferred from foresight of a virtually certain result |
| Basic intent | An offence whose *mens rea* includes recklessness |
| Specific intent | An offence that requires intention, such as s 18 or theft |
| Appropriation | Any assumption of the rights of an owner |
| Novus actus interveniens | An intervening act that breaks the chain of causation |
| Thin skull rule | D must take the victim as they find them |
| Wound | A break in both layers of the skin |
| Menaces | Threats, including of unpleasant consequences, used in blackmail |
| Aggravating factor | A factor that makes an offence more serious and increases the sentence |
