## Actus reus (2.2.1)

- **Factual causation**: but for (*R v White* [1910]).
- **Legal causation**: operating and substantial cause (*R v Smith* [1959]); thin skull (*R v Blaue* [1975]); medical treatment breaks the chain only if palpably wrong (*R v Jordan* [1956]) or the wound becomes insignificant (*R v Cheshire* [1991]); foreseeable escape does not break it (*R v Roberts* [1971]).
- **Omissions**: contract (*Pittwood* [1902]), relationship (*Gibbins and Proctor* [1918]), assumed care (*Stone and Dobinson* [1977]), public office (*Dytham* [1979]), dangerous situation created (*Miller* [1983]), statute.

## Mens rea (2.2.2) and strict liability (2.2.3)

- **Direct intention**: aim or purpose (*Mohan* [1975]). **Oblique**: virtual certainty, appreciated by D (*Woollin* [1998]).
- **Subjective recklessness**: D foresaw the risk and took it unreasonably (*Cunningham* [1957]; *R v G* [2003]).
- **Transferred malice**: same offence only (*Latimer* [1886] v *Pembliton* [1874]).
- **Coincidence**: continuing act (*Fagan* [1969]).
- **OAPA 1861**: s 47 (ABH), s 20 (wound or inflict GBH, foresight of some harm), s 18 (with intent).
- **Strict liability**: presumption of *mens rea* (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970]); *Gammon* [1985] test; *Callow v Tillstone* [1900]; *Harrow LBC v Shah* [1999].

## Property offences (2.2.4 to 2.2.7)

| Offence | Source | Elements | Key cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theft | TA 1968 ss 1 to 6 | Dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with intention permanently to deprive | *Ivey* [2017]; *Morris* [1983]; *Gomez* [1993]; *Lloyd* [1985] |
| Robbery | TA 1968 s 8 | Theft plus force or fear of force, immediately before or at the time, in order to steal | *Dawson and James* [1976]; *Hale* [1978]; *Robinson* [1977] |
| Burglary | TA 1968 s 9(1)(a), (b) | Entry as trespasser to building or part, with intent (a) or then offending (b) | *Collins* [1972]; *Ryan* [1996]; *Walkington* [1979] |
| Blackmail | TA 1968 ss 21, 34 | Unwarranted demand with menaces, with a view to gain or intent to cause loss | |
| Fraud | FA 2006 ss 2, 5 | Dishonest false representation intending gain or loss | |
| Obtaining services | FA 2006 s 11 | Dishonestly obtaining services, intending not to pay | |
| Making off | TA 1978 s 3 | Knowing payment on the spot required, dishonestly makes off, intending to avoid payment permanently | *Allen* [1985] |
| Criminal damage | CDA 1971 s 1 | Destroying or damaging another's property, intending or reckless | Lawful excuse s 5; *Jaggard v Dickinson* [1981] |

**Dishonesty (s 2)**: not dishonest if belief in a legal right, belief in owner's consent, or owner not findable by reasonable steps.

## Defences (2.2.8, 2.2.9)

- **Duress**: threat of death or serious injury; *Graham* [1982] test; not for murder (*Howe* [1987]) or attempted murder (*Gotts* [1992]); lost by voluntary association (*Hasan* [2005]). **Of circumstances**: *Willer* [1986]; *Martin* [1989].
- **Intoxication**: voluntary, no defence to basic intent (*Majewski* [1977]); Dutch courage (*Gallagher* [1963]); involuntary only if no *mens rea* formed (*Kingston* [1994]; *Hardie* [1985]).

## Sentencing (2.2.10)

Custody (so serious nothing else will do; may be suspended); community orders (requirements such as unpaid work, curfew); fines (by seriousness and means); discharges (conditional, absolute). Guilty plea: up to one third off.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actus reus | The guilty act, omission or state of affairs |
| Mens rea | The guilty mind |
| Oblique intention | Intention inferred from foresight of a virtually certain result |
| Basic intent | An offence whose *mens rea* includes recklessness |
| Specific intent | An offence that requires intention, such as s 18 or theft |
| Appropriation | Any assumption of the rights of an owner |

## Self-test

1. What did *R v White* show?
2. Give three sources of a duty to act.
3. State the *Woollin* direction in one sentence.
4. Why is *Pembliton* different from *Latimer*?
5. What must a robber do in addition to stealing?
6. Can duress be a defence to murder?
7. Name the four types of sentence in the specification.

**Answers:** 1. That "but for" causation is needed: the poison did not cause death. 2. Any three of contract, relationship, voluntary assumption of care, public office, creating a dangerous situation, statute. 3. The jury may find intention if death or serious injury was a virtual certainty and D appreciated that. 4. *Mens rea* to hit a person cannot transfer to damage to property, a different offence. 5. Use force, or put someone in fear of force, immediately before or at the time of stealing, in order to steal. 6. No (*Howe*). 7. Custodial sentences, community orders, fines and discharges.
