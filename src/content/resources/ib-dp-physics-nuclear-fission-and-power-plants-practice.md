These questions cover sub-topic E.4, Fission, from the IB *Physics guide* for first assessment 2025. The content is the same for SL and HL. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-physics-nuclear-fission-and-power-plants/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-physics-nuclear-fission-and-power-plants-revision-notes/) first, then use the [IB DP Physics hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/physics/) and [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/physics/) to track progress.

Data used where needed: 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV/c²; 1 MeV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ J; mass of a nucleus taken as A × 1 u. Other values are given in each question.

## Questions

**1.** A uranium-235 nucleus (proton number 92) absorbs a neutron and splits into strontium-93 (proton number 38), xenon-140 (proton number 54) and x free neutrons.
**(a)** Find x and show that proton number is conserved. **[2]**
**(b)** State one difference between spontaneous fission and neutron-induced fission. **[1]**

**2.** Explain, with reference to the curve of binding energy per nucleon, why energy is released when uranium-235 undergoes fission. **[3]**

**3.** In a fission, U-235 + n becomes two fragments plus 2 neutrons. Masses: U-235 235.043930 u; neutron 1.008665 u; the two fragments together 233.856000 u.
**(a)** Calculate the mass difference between reactants and products. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy released in MeV. **[1]**
**(c)** Convert your answer to joules. **[1]**

**4.** U-235 splits into two nuclei and two free neutrons. Per nucleon, the binding energies are 7.59 MeV (U-235), 8.41 MeV (the nucleus with nucleon number 134) and 8.63 MeV (the nucleus with nucleon number 99). Estimate the energy released. **[4]**

**5.** The multiplication factor k of a reactor is the average number of neutrons from each fission that trigger a further fission.
**(a)** State what k = 1 means for the rate of fission. **[1]**
**(b)** In a reactor with k = 1.03, how many generations are needed for the number of fissions per generation to increase by a factor of 1.0 × 10⁵? **[2]**
**(c)** With k = 0.92, calculate the fraction of the original number of fissions per generation left after 20 generations. **[2]**

**6.** Each fission of uranium-235 releases 190 MeV. Calculate the energy released if all the nuclei in 1.00 kg of uranium-235 undergo fission, and compare it with the 4.2 × 10⁷ J obtained from 1.00 kg of oil. **[4]**

**7.** A power plant delivers 480 MW of electrical power at an overall efficiency of 31%. Each fission supplies 185 MeV.
**(a)** Calculate the thermal power produced. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the power wasted as heat. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the number of fissions per second. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the mass of uranium-235 that undergoes fission in 365 days. **[2]**

**8.** A visitor to a power plant makes four statements. Each is wrong. Correct each one and give the true role of the part named.
**(a)** "The moderator absorbs neutrons so the reaction cannot run away." **[2]**
**(b)** "Control rods slow neutrons down so that they cause fission." **[2]**
**(c)** "The heat exchanger sends steam from the core straight to the turbine." **[2]**
**(d)** "Shielding is what stops the chain reaction." **[2]**

**9.** A fast neutron of mass m and speed v collides head-on and elastically with a stationary carbon-12 nucleus of mass 12m.
**(a)** Use conservation of momentum and of kinetic energy to show that the neutron's speed after the collision is 11v/13. **[3]**
**(b)** The neutron starts with 2.0 MeV. Find its kinetic energy after five such collisions. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why a moderator made of much lighter nuclei slows neutrons in fewer collisions. **[1]**

**10.** The primary coolant of a reactor carries 1.4 GW of thermal power out of the core and warms by 35 K. Take c = 4.2 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
**(a)** Calculate the mass of coolant that passes through the core each second. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the energy is passed to a second circuit in a heat exchanger. **[1]**

**11.** Spent fuel contains caesium-137 (half-life 30.1 years) and plutonium-239 (half-life 2.41 × 10⁴ years).
**(a)** Calculate the fraction of caesium-137 left after 120 years. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the time for the caesium-137 to fall to 0.1% of its original amount. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the fraction of plutonium-239 left after the time in (b). **[1]**
**(d)** Evaluate deep geological storage as a way of managing this waste. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Nucleon number: 235 + 1 = 236 = 93 + 140 + x, so **x = 3** [1]. Proton number: 38 + 54 = 92, equal to uranium's 92 [1].
**(b)** Spontaneous fission needs no trigger, whereas neutron-induced fission begins when the nucleus absorbs a neutron [1].
*Examiner insight:* A conservation check works best when you show both sums, nucleon number and proton number, not just the answer.

**2.** Uranium-235 has a lower binding energy per nucleon (about 7.6 MeV) than the mid-mass fragments (about 8.5 MeV) [1]. The fragments are therefore more tightly bound, so total binding energy increases [1]. The products have less mass than the reactants, and the mass difference is released as energy through ΔE = Δm c² [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say which side of the curve each nucleus sits on and what happens to total binding energy; "fragments are more stable" alone is too vague.

**3. (a)** Reactants: 235.043930 + 1.008665 = 236.052595 u [1]. Products: 233.856000 + 2 × 1.008665 = 235.873330 u, so Δm = **0.179265 u** [1].
**(b)** E = 0.179265 × 931.5 = **167 MeV** [1].
**(c)** E = 166.99 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ = **2.68 × 10⁻¹¹ J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Include every neutron on both sides of the mass sum. Keep the unrounded energy for the joule conversion.

**4.** Binding energy of U-235 = 235 × 7.59 = 1783.7 MeV [1]. Fragments: 134 × 8.41 + 99 × 8.63 = 1126.9 + 854.4 = 1981.3 MeV [1]. Free neutrons add nothing [1]. Energy released = 1981.3 − 1783.7 = **198 MeV** (about 2 × 10² MeV) [1].
*Examiner insight:* This is an estimate from rounded values, so say so; a response that gives products minus parent, in that order, avoids a negative answer.

**5. (a)** The fission rate stays constant (a steady chain reaction) [1].
**(b)** 1.03ⁿ = 1.0 × 10⁵, so n = ln(1.0 × 10⁵) ÷ ln(1.03) [1] = 389.5, so **390 generations** [1].
**(c)** Fraction = 0.92²⁰ [1] = **0.19** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Round a count of generations up to a whole number, and state that k < 1 means the chain dies away.

**6.** Energy per fission = 190 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ J [1]. Nuclei in 1.00 kg = 1.00 ÷ (235 × 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷) = 2.56 × 10²⁴ [1]. Energy = 2.56 × 10²⁴ × 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ = **7.80 × 10¹³ J** [1]. Ratio to oil = 7.80 × 10¹³ ÷ 4.2 × 10⁷ = **1.9 × 10⁶** times as much [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert MeV to joules before multiplying by the number of nuclei, and make the comparison explicit as a ratio.

**7. (a)** Thermal power = 480 ÷ 0.31 = **1.55 × 10³ MW**, 1.55 × 10⁹ W [1].
**(b)** Wasted = 1548 − 480 = **1.07 × 10³ MW** [1].
**(c)** Energy per fission = 185 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ = 2.96 × 10⁻¹¹ J [1]. Fissions per second = 1.548 × 10⁹ ÷ 2.96 × 10⁻¹¹ = **5.2 × 10¹⁹** [1].
**(d)** Mass per second = 5.22 × 10¹⁹ × 235 × 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ = 2.04 × 10⁻⁵ kg [1]. Over 365 days (3.1536 × 10⁷ s): **643 kg** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The number of fissions depends on thermal power, not on electrical output; using 480 MW here understates the fuel used.

**8. (a)** A moderator slows fast neutrons by elastic collisions [1] so they are more likely to cause fission; it should absorb few neutrons [1].
**(b)** Control rods absorb neutrons [1]; moving them in or out changes k to hold the reaction steady or shut it down [1].
**(c)** Primary coolant carrying energy from the core passes it to a separate circuit [1] that makes the steam, so the steam does not carry radioactive material from the core [1].
**(d)** Shielding (thick concrete or steel) absorbs neutrons and gamma rays from the core to protect people [1]; the chain reaction is controlled by the control rods, not the shielding [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the correct role and the mechanism for each part. "Controls the reaction" earns less than saying the rods absorb neutrons and so change k.

**9. (a)** Momentum: m v = m v₁ + 12m v₂, so v = v₁ + 12v₂ [1]. Kinetic energy: v² = v₁² + 12v₂² [1]. Substituting v₂ = (v − v₁)/12 gives v₁ = −11v/13, so the speed is **11v/13** with direction reversed [1].
**(b)** Energy fraction per collision = (11/13)² = 0.716 [1]. After five: 2.0 × 0.716⁵ = **0.38 MeV** [1].
**(c)** A lighter nucleus takes a larger share of the neutron's energy in each collision, so fewer collisions are needed [1].
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" answer needs both conservation equations written out and the substitution shown, ending on the stated result.

**10. (a)** P = (m/t) c ΔT [1], so m/t = 1.4 × 10⁹ ÷ (4.2 × 10³ × 35) [1] = **9.5 × 10³ kg s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** The primary coolant becomes radioactive in the core, so the steam circuit is kept separate [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep GW converted to W before dividing; a missing factor of 10⁹ is the usual slip here.

**11. (a)** 120 ÷ 30.1 = 3.99 half-lives [1]. Fraction = 0.5³·⁹⁹ = **0.063** [1].
**(b)** 2ⁿ = 1000, so n = 9.97 half-lives [1]. Time = 9.97 × 30.1 = **300 years** [1].
**(c)** 300 ÷ 2.41 × 10⁴ = 0.0124 half-lives, so fraction = 0.5⁰·⁰¹²⁴ = **0.991** [1].
**(d)** For: sealed containers in stable rock keep radioactive material away from people for a very long time [1]. Plutonium-239 would stay almost unchanged for centuries (0.991 left), so storage must last far longer than institutions [1]. Against: leaks, ground movement and security risks remain, and future generations carry the burden; a judgement should weigh these against the alternatives [1].
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation reaches a conclusion; listing advantages and disadvantages without a judgment usually leaves marks unclaimed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out a free neutron when adding product masses.
- Giving the energy in MeV when joules are asked for.
- Using electrical power rather than thermal power for the fission rate.
- Stating that control rods "slow" neutrons or that the moderator "absorbs" them.
- Describing waste storage without a half-life, time scale or risk.
- Forgetting to round generations up to a whole number.
- Taking a binding-energy answer as exact when it comes from curve values.
- Mixing MW and GW.
