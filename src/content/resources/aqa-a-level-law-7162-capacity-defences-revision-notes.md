Condensed notes for the section 3.2 row **Capacity defences**: insanity, automatism and intoxication. For explanation and a worked problem, use the [capacity defences study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-capacity-defences/).

## Choose the defence by its cause

```
What caused the condition?
  Internal disease affecting reasoning ............... INSANITY
  External factor, total loss of control ............. AUTOMATISM
       ... but self-induced and reckless ............. no defence to basic intent
  Alcohol / illegal drugs, voluntarily taken ......... VOLUNTARY INTOXICATION
  Spiked drink / unexpected effect of a sedative ..... INVOLUNTARY INTOXICATION
```

## Insanity: M'Naghten Rules (1843)

D proves on the balance of probabilities:

| Element | Meaning | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| Defect of reason | Reasoning impaired, not just not used | *Clarke* (1972) |
| Disease of the mind | Internal cause; legal not medical; mind not brain | *Kemp* (1956); *Sullivan* (1984); *Hennessy* (1989); *Burgess* (1991); *Bratty* (1963) |
| Did not know nature and quality | Did not know what he was physically doing | — |
| Or did not know it was wrong | Legally wrong | *Windle* (1952) |

**Outcome:** not guilty by reason of insanity. Disposal under the Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act 1964, as amended: hospital order (with or without restrictions), supervision order or absolute discharge; for murder, a restricted hospital order where the hospital order conditions are met.

## Automatism

- **Bratty** (1963): act done by the muscles without any control by the mind.
- **External cause:** *Hill v Baxter* (1958) bees example; *Quick* (1973) hypoglycaemia; *T* (1990) PTSD after rape.
- **Total loss of control:** *A-G's Ref (No 2 of 1992)*; *Broome v Perkins* (1987).
- **Outcome:** complete acquittal.

**Self-induced automatism**

| Offence type | D reckless about the risk | D unaware of the risk |
|---|---|---|
| Specific intent | Defence available | Defence available |
| Basic intent | No defence (*Bailey*, 1983) | Defence available (*Bailey*; *Hardie*, 1984) |

Automatism from voluntary drink or drugs follows the intoxication rules (*Lipman*, 1970).

## Intoxication

| | Specific intent offence | Basic intent offence |
|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | Defence if no intent formed (*Beard*, 1920), usually convicted of basic intent alternative | No defence (*Majewski*, 1977) |
| **Involuntary** | Defence if no mens rea formed | Defence if no mens rea formed |
| **Mens rea formed anyway** | No defence (*Kingston*, 1994) | No defence (*Kingston*) |

- **Dutch courage:** *Gallagher* (1963): no defence.
- **Stronger drink than expected:** still voluntary (*Allen*, 1988).
- **Sedatives taken normally:** treated as involuntary unless D knew of risk (*Hardie*).
- **Drunken mistake in self-defence:** not available (*O'Grady*, 1987; *Hatton*, 2005; s76(5) CJIA 2008).

## The diabetes pair

| Condition | Cause | Defence | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypoglycaemia (low sugar) | Insulin taken (external) | Automatism | *Quick* |
| Hyperglycaemia (high sugar) | Diabetes itself, insulin not taken (internal) | Insanity | *Hennessy* |

## Finish with the outcome

Every capacity answer should end by naming the result, because the defences lead to very different places:
- **Insanity:** special verdict and a disposal, not a simple acquittal.
- **Automatism:** full acquittal, unless self-induced and reckless for a basic intent offence.
- **Voluntary intoxication:** acquittal of the specific intent offence, but usually conviction of the basic intent alternative (murder to manslaughter; s18 to s20).
- **Involuntary intoxication:** acquittal if no mens rea; conviction as charged if mens rea was formed.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Defect of reason | Impaired power of reasoning |
| Disease of the mind | Legal term for an internal condition affecting mental faculties |
| Special verdict | Not guilty by reason of insanity |
| Non-insane automatism | Total loss of control from an external cause; complete defence |
| Self-induced automatism | Automatism the defendant caused by his own conduct |
| Voluntary intoxication | Knowingly taking alcohol or drugs |
| Involuntary intoxication | Spiked drinks, or unexpected effects of medication |
| Dutch courage | Drinking to give oneself courage after forming the intent |

## Evaluation points

- Insanity is a legal label that stigmatises epileptics and diabetics.
- The internal and external line produces the hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia anomaly.
- *Windle* excludes ill defendants who know the act is illegal.
- The reverse burden for insanity conflicts with *Woolmington*.
- Law Commission discussion paper (2013): proposed a "not criminally responsible by reason of a recognised medical condition" defence.
- *Majewski* ignores genuine lack of mens rea, but protects the public.

## Exam traps

- Diabetes cases the wrong way round.
- Partial loss of control accepted as automatism.
- Voluntary intoxication offered for s20, s47 or manslaughter.
- Forgetting that involuntary intoxication fails if mens rea was formed.
- Calling insanity a medical test.

5. Name the three disposals after a special verdict.
6. Define automatism, with an authority.
7. What did *A-G's Reference (No 2 of 1992)* decide?
8. When is self-induced automatism a defence to a basic intent offence?
9. What did *DPP v Majewski* decide?
10. What did *R v Kingston* decide?
11. What is the Dutch courage rule?
12. Which diabetic condition leads to insanity and which to automatism?
