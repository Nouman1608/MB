These notes cover section 8.2 *Globalisation and migration* of Cambridge International A Level
Sociology (9699) Paper 4: the causes of global migration, its positive and negative consequences,
and debates about who benefits. For full explanations and a model paragraph, see the
[globalisation and migration study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-migration/);
to apply them, use the
[globalisation and migration practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-migration-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

Migration is shaped by both agency and structure. Individuals weigh push and pull factors (Lee),
but the routes they take, the jobs they fill and the rights they hold are set by global labour
markets, state policy and historical ties (Piore, Sassen, Castles and Miller). Consequences are
mixed and fall unevenly: receiving economies, sending families and migrants gain different things,
and perceptions of migration are often shaped more by media framing than by evidence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Immigration** | Movement into a country to live |
| **Emigration** | Movement out of a country to live elsewhere |
| **Net migration** | Immigration minus emigration over a period; can be positive or negative |
| **Push factor** | Condition that drives people away (poverty, conflict, persecution, disaster) |
| **Pull factor** | Attraction of a destination (jobs, wages, safety, family, study) |
| **Intervening obstacles** | Lee's term for barriers between origin and destination: cost, distance, law |
| **Refugee** | Person outside their country owing to a well-founded fear of persecution (1951 UN Refugee Convention) |
| **Asylum seeker** | Person who has applied for refugee protection and awaits a decision |
| **Irregular migration** | Entering or staying without legal permission |
| **Chain migration** | Earlier migrants help relatives and neighbours follow |
| **Remittances** | Money migrants send to families in the sending country |
| **Brain drain / brain circulation** | Loss of skilled workers / later return flow of skills and money |
| **Global care chain** | Series of links in which care work passes from richer to poorer women across borders (Hochschild) |
| **Diaspora** | Dispersed population keeping ties to an original homeland |
| **Lifestyle migration** | Moving abroad for quality of life, often after tourism |

## Causes in brief

**Individual level**
- Lee (1966): factors at origin, factors at destination, intervening obstacles, personal factors.
- Strength: explains why people facing similar conditions decide differently.
- Weakness: ignores structure; the poorest often cannot afford to move.

**Structural level**
- Piore, *Birds of Passage* (1979): dual labour market; rich economies need workers for the
  low-paid secondary sector, a permanent pull.
- Sassen: core investment disrupts peripheral economies and creates migration routes; global
  cities need both professionals and low-paid service workers.
- Networks: chain migration lowers costs and channels flows along existing links.

**Global labour patterns**
- Care and domestic work: feminised; Ehrenreich and Hochschild, *Global Woman* (2002).
- Gulf construction and services: temporary contracts; kafala sponsorship ties workers to employers.
- Skilled recruitment: doctors, nurses, engineers, technology workers.
- Castles and Miller (1993): migration is globalised, accelerated, differentiated and feminised.

**Tourism**
- Creates seasonal labour demand in resort areas.
- Tourists may become retirees or second-home owners (lifestyle migration).
- Urry, *The Tourist Gaze* (1990): places are packaged for visitors, shaping images of destinations.

## Consequences

| | Receiving society | Sending society | Migrants |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Labour shortages filled; taxes; business start-ups; cultural diversity | Remittances; returning skills and investment | Higher income; safety; education for children |
| **Negative** | Concern over scarce resources; tension; negative perceptions | Brain drain; family separation; dependence on remittances | Exploitation; insecure status; loss of occupational status; racism |

**Perception versus evidence:** concern over scarce resources may reflect real local pressure
where services have not expanded, or media framing of migrants as a threat. Good answers examine
both.

**Cultural diversity:** Hall (1990) sees diasporic identities as hybrid and always being remade,
linking to 7.2.

## Who benefits?

- **Functionalist / neo-liberal:** mutual gains as labour moves to where it is needed.
- **Marxist:** Castles and Kosack (1973) — migrants form the lowest layer of the working class;
  hostility to migrants divides workers and serves employers.
- **Feminist:** richer households gain care; migrant women and their children bear emotional and
  practical costs.
- **Transnational view:** migrants are active agents who build lives across borders.
- **Judgement:** depends on type of migrant, gender, legal status and time scale.

## Exam traps

- Net migration hides gross flows; a fall in net migration may mean more people left, not that
  fewer arrived.
- "Refugee", "asylum seeker" and "economic migrant" are not interchangeable.
- Tourism is easy to forget; it is named in the syllabus.
- Always say who gains or loses, not just that migration has "positive and negative effects".
- Do not present public concern as proven or as mere prejudice; weigh evidence and framing.
- Link agency (choice) and structure (labour markets, law): the heading 8 introduction names
  this key concept.

## Self-test

1. Define net migration.
2. A country has 250,000 immigrants and 290,000 emigrants in a year. What is net migration?
3. Name Lee's two additional elements beyond push and pull factors.
4. What is dual labour market theory, and who proposed it?
5. Give one way tourism can lead to longer-term migration.
6. What is the kafala system?
7. Distinguish a refugee from an asylum seeker.
8. What is a global care chain?
9. Give one benefit of migration for a sending country.
10. What did Castles and Kosack (1973) argue about migrant workers?
11. Why might the poorest people in a country be less likely to migrate internationally?
12. Name one concept from another part of Paper 4 that strengthens a migration essay.

### Answers

1. Immigration minus emigration over a given period.
2. 250,000 − 290,000 = −40,000 (net emigration of 40,000).
3. Intervening obstacles and personal factors.
4. Michael Piore (1979): rich economies have a low-paid, insecure secondary labour market that
   native workers avoid, so employers recruit migrants to fill it.
5. Any one: resort areas draw in seasonal workers; tourists later retire abroad or buy second homes;
   tourism creates images of places that act as pull factors.
6. A sponsorship system used in Gulf states that ties a migrant worker's legal status to an
   employer, limiting freedom to change jobs.
7. A refugee has been recognised as fleeing persecution; an asylum seeker has applied for that
   status and is awaiting a decision.
8. A series of links in which women from poorer countries care for richer families abroad while
   their own children are cared for by others (Hochschild).
9. Any one: remittances; skills and investment brought back by returning migrants; reduced
   unemployment pressure.
10. That migrant workers in Western Europe formed the lowest stratum of the working class, doing
    the lowest-paid and least secure work.
11. Migration has costs (travel, recruiters' fees, visas), so the very poorest often lack the
    resources to move.
12. Any one: hybrid identity (7.2), global inequality and dependency (8.1), moral panic (10.1).
