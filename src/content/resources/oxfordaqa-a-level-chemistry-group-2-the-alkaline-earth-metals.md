## Why the group behaves as a family

Mg, Ca, Sr and Ba each have two outer electrons in an s sub-shell (3s² to 6s²), so they are s-block
elements. They react by losing both and forming M²⁺ ions (oxidation state +2). Down the group, the outer
electrons sit in a higher shell and are held less tightly; most of this topic follows from that.

## Atomic radius

Atomic radius **increases** from Mg to Ba. Each step down adds a whole new occupied shell, so the outer
electrons are further out. Nuclear charge rises too, but the new inner shell shields the outer electrons
from most of that extra charge.

## First ionisation energy

First ionisation energy measures how much energy it takes to strip one electron from every atom in one
mole of gaseous atoms, leaving one mole of 1+ ions. For strontium:

```
Sr(g) → Sr⁺(g) + e⁻
```

First ionisation energy **decreases** from Mg to Ba. Three points build the explanation:

- the outer electron is in a higher shell, so it is further from the nucleus;
- more inner shells sit between it and the nucleus, so shielding is greater;
- together these weaken the attraction to the nucleus by more than the larger nuclear charge
  strengthens it, so less energy is needed.

### Worked example 1: calcium against magnesium

*Explain why calcium has a lower first ionisation energy than magnesium.*

1. Calcium's outer electron is in the 4s sub-shell; magnesium's is in 3s. Calcium's is further from its
   nucleus.
2. Calcium has three full inner shells; magnesium has two. Calcium's outer electron is more shielded.
3. Calcium has more protons, but distance and shielding outweigh this. Its outer electron is attracted
   less strongly, so less energy removes it.

## Melting point

Each element is a giant lattice of M²⁺ ions held together by metallic bonding: the electrostatic
attraction between the positive ions and the sea of delocalised electrons.

From calcium to barium the melting point **falls**. Every ion still carries a 2+ charge, but the ions grow
larger, so the delocalised electrons are further from the centre of each ion. The attraction is weaker and
less energy breaks up the lattice.

Magnesium breaks the pattern: it melts at a lower temperature than calcium. The usual explanation is that
magnesium packs its atoms in a different crystal arrangement. Treat magnesium as the exception and apply
the model to Ca, Sr and Ba.

### Worked example 2: strontium against barium

*Explain why strontium has a higher melting point than barium.*

- Both have giant metallic lattices.
- Both form 2+ ions and contribute two delocalised electrons per atom.
- The Sr²⁺ ion is smaller than Ba²⁺, so the delocalised electrons are closer to the ion's nucleus.
- The metallic bonding in strontium is stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome it.

## Reactions with water

Each metal is oxidised from 0 to +2, and hydrogen in water is reduced from +1 to 0. The reactions get
**more vigorous down the group**, because the two outer electrons are lost more easily as ionisation
energies fall.

| Metal | What you see | Product with water |
|---|---|---|
| Mg | almost no reaction with cold water; burns with a bright white flame in steam | Mg(OH)₂ (cold, very slow); MgO (steam) |
| Ca | steady fizzing; the metal dissolves; the liquid turns cloudy white | Ca(OH)₂ |
| Sr | faster fizzing than calcium | Sr(OH)₂ |
| Ba | the most vigorous of the four | Ba(OH)₂ |

With liquid water the general equation is:

```
M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → M(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
```

The cloudiness with calcium is undissolved Ca(OH)₂. With steam, magnesium forms the oxide instead:

```
Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)
```

The reaction of magnesium with water appears again at International A2 in
[Period 3 elements and their oxides](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-properties-of-period-3-elements-and-their-oxides/).

### Worked example 3: the solution left after strontium reacts

*1.314 g of strontium reacts completely with water and the solution is made up to 500 cm³. Calculate the
concentration of hydroxide ions. (Aᵣ Sr = 87.6)*

```
n(Sr)       = 1.314 ÷ 87.6          = 0.0150 mol
n(Sr(OH)₂)  = n(Sr)                 = 0.0150 mol
[Sr(OH)₂]   = 0.0150 ÷ 0.500        = 0.0300 mol dm⁻³
[OH⁻]       = 2 × 0.0300            = 0.0600 mol dm⁻³
```

Each Sr(OH)₂ releases two OH⁻ ions, hence the doubling.

## Extracting titanium with magnesium

Titanium is obtained from titanium(IV) chloride by heating it with magnesium:

```
TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂
```

Titanium goes from +4 to 0, so it is reduced. Magnesium goes from 0 to +2, so it is oxidised and acts as
the **reducing agent**.

### Worked example 4: percentage yield

*A batch of 250 kg of TiCl₄ gives 58.5 kg of titanium. Calculate the percentage yield.
(Aᵣ Ti = 47.9, Cl = 35.5)*

```
Mr of TiCl₄       = 47.9 + 142.0          = 189.9
amount of TiCl₄   = 250 × 10³ ÷ 189.9     = 1316 mol
theoretical Ti    = 1316 × 47.9           = 63.1 × 10³ g = 63.1 kg
percentage yield  = 58.5 ÷ 63.06 × 100    = 92.8%
```

The ratio of TiCl₄ to Ti is 1 : 1, so the moles carry straight across.

## Hydroxides: solubility and uses

Going from Mg to Ba, each hydroxide is **more soluble** than the one above it:

```
Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Sr(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂
sparingly soluble  →  most soluble
```

A saturated solution of barium hydroxide therefore holds far more OH⁻ ions than a saturated solution of
magnesium hydroxide, and is more strongly alkaline.

**Mg(OH)₂ in medicine.** A suspension of magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) is taken as an antacid. It
neutralises excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach:

```
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
```

Being sparingly soluble, it releases little free hydroxide at once, so the stomach never becomes
strongly alkaline.

**Ca(OH)₂ in agriculture.** Farmers spread calcium hydroxide on acidic soil to raise its pH, which helps
crops grow:

```
Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O
```

### Worked example 5: one dose of antacid

*A 10.0 cm³ dose of milk of magnesia contains 0.800 g of Mg(OH)₂. What volume of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ HCl
can it neutralise? (Mr Mg(OH)₂ = 58.3)*

```
n(Mg(OH)₂) = 0.800 ÷ 58.3          = 0.01372 mol
n(HCl)     = 2 × 0.01372           = 0.02744 mol
volume     = 0.02744 ÷ 0.150       = 0.183 dm³ = 183 cm³
```

## Removing SO₂ from flue gases

Power stations that burn sulfur-containing fuel release sulfur dioxide, an acidic gas. Passing the flue
gases through calcium oxide or calcium carbonate removes it, because both are bases:

```
CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂      (limestone)
CaO + SO₂ → CaSO₃              (lime)
```

CaCO₃ releases one mole of CO₂ per mole of SO₂ captured; CaO does not.

### Worked example 6: impure limestone

*A plant removes 0.800 tonnes of SO₂ a day with powdered limestone that is 90.0% CaCO₃ by mass. What
mass of limestone is used each day? (Mr SO₂ = 64.1, CaCO₃ = 100.1)*

```
n(SO₂)          = 0.800 × 10⁶ ÷ 64.1     = 1.248 × 10⁴ mol
mass CaCO₃      = 1.248 × 10⁴ × 100.1    = 1.249 × 10⁶ g
mass limestone  = 1.249 × 10⁶ ÷ 0.900    = 1.39 × 10⁶ g = 1.39 tonnes
```

Divide by the purity fraction, because the impure rock must contain the required CaCO₃.

## Sulfates: solubility, the sulfate test and BaSO₄ in medicine

Solubility of the sulfates **decreases** down the group: MgSO₄ dissolves readily, CaSO₄ and SrSO₄ much
less, and the specification describes **BaSO₄ as insoluble**. This runs the opposite way to the hydroxides.

**The test for sulfate ions.** Add dilute hydrochloric acid to the sample, then a few drops of barium
chloride solution. A white precipitate shows sulfate ions:

```
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

- **Why barium chloride?** BaSO₄ does not dissolve, so added barium ions pull sulfate out of solution
  as a visible solid even when little sulfate is present.
- **Why acidified?** Carbonate ions would also give a white precipitate (BaCO₃) with barium ions. Added acid
  turns carbonate into carbon dioxide gas, `2H⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)`, so it cannot interfere. Sulfuric acid must never be used, since it
  supplies sulfate ions itself.

### Worked example 7: a misleading result

*A student adds barium chloride, without acid, to a washing soda (sodium carbonate) solution and sees a
white precipitate. Does this prove sulfate is present?*

No. The solution contains carbonate ions, so the solid could be BaCO₃, BaSO₄ or both. After adding dilute
HCl the carbonate is gone; any precipitate that still forms must be BaSO₄.

**BaSO₄ in medicine.** Patients swallow a "barium meal", a suspension of barium sulfate. It absorbs
X-rays, so the gut lining shows clearly on an X-ray image. Dissolved barium ions are toxic, but BaSO₄ is
insoluble, so they do not pass into the blood. Magnesium sulfate, by contrast, is soluble and is also used
in medicine (as Epsom salts).

## Required practical 3: telling Group 2 ions apart

Add sodium hydroxide solution to one portion of the unknown and dilute sulfuric acid to a fresh portion.
The two solubility trends predict the results.

| Ion | With NaOH(aq) | With dilute H₂SO₄ |
|---|---|---|
| Mg²⁺ | white precipitate | none |
| Ca²⁺ | slight white precipitate | slight white precipitate |
| Sr²⁺ | very little | white precipitate |
| Ba²⁺ | none | dense white precipitate |

Use solutions of similar concentration, or the amounts of precipitate cannot be compared. Limewater, the
test reagent for carbonate in the same practical, is itself saturated Ca(OH)₂ solution:
`Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O`.

## Common errors

- Writing "nuclear charge decreases down the group". It increases; distance and shielding win.
- Describing metallic bonding as attraction "between atoms" rather than between ions and delocalised
  electrons.
- Giving Mg(OH)₂ as the product with steam (it is MgO).
- Forgetting to double [Sr(OH)₂] or [Ba(OH)₂] to get [OH⁻].
- Saying the sulfate-test acid "helps the precipitate form". It removes carbonate.
