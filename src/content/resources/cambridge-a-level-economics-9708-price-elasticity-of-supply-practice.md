## Section A

**1.** The price of a good rises by 10% and quantity supplied rises by 4%. What is the price elasticity of supply?

A: -0.4

B: 0.4

C: 2.5

D: 14

**[1]**

**2.** Which product is likely to have the most price-elastic supply in the short run?

A: Mature mango trees

B: Hospital beds

C: Mass-produced plastic toys from a factory with idle machines

D: Tickets for a football final in a full stadium

**[1]**

**3.** Define price elasticity of supply. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what a PES coefficient of 0 means for a supply curve and give one example. **[2]**

**5.** PES for an invented brand of bricks is 0.25. Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied if the price rises by 12%. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why PES is normally positive. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An invented solar panel firm's output responds to price as follows.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Price per panel (cu) | 200 | 240 |
| Panels supplied per month | 4,000 | 5,200 |

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply between Year 1 and Year 2. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe supply over this range and explain what the coefficient shows about the relative percentage changes. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons why this firm's supply might be price elastic. **[2]**

**8.** The supply of a good is given by Qs = 5P - 50, where P is the price in cu and Q is units per week.

**(a)** Calculate quantity supplied at prices of 20 cu and 24 cu. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate PES for this price rise. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why this supply curve is price elastic even though it is a straight line. **[2]**

**9.** A city has 600 hotel rooms. A major sports event raises demand for rooms.

**(a)** Explain why the supply of hotel rooms is perfectly inelastic in the week of the event. **[2]**

**(b)** Over the next five years, room prices rise by 50% and the number of rooms rises to 900. Calculate the long-run PES. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain two reasons why supply became more elastic over the five years. **[4]**

**10.** A government wants farmers to raise output of lentils by 10% to reduce imports. Estimates of PES for lentils are 0.2 in the short run and 1.25 in the long run.

**(a)** Calculate the rise in price needed to achieve the target in the short run and in the long run. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the implication of these figures for how quickly farmers can react to the government's aim. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how holding stocks of finished goods affects a firm's price elasticity of supply, and state one disadvantage of holding them. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how the price elasticity of supply affects the size of the price change when demand for a product rises. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 4 / 10 = 0.4, positive because price and quantity supplied rise together.

**2.** **C** [1]. Idle machines are spare capacity, so output can rise quickly.

**3.** The **responsiveness of quantity supplied** [1] **to a change in the price of the good itself** [1].

**4.** Quantity supplied does **not change at all** when price changes, so the supply curve is **vertical** [1]. Example: seats in a stadium for one match, or the stock of a rare painting [1].

**5.** 0.25 x 12 = **+3%** [1].

**6.** A higher price makes supplying the good **more profitable** and covers the higher cost of extra output [1], so firms supply more: price and quantity supplied **move in the same direction**, giving a positive coefficient [1].

**7. (a)** % change in quantity = (5,200 - 4,000) / 4,000 x 100 = 30%; % change in price = (240 - 200) / 200 x 100 = 20% [1]. PES = 30 / 20 = **1.5** [1].

**(b)** Supply is **price elastic** over this range [1]. Quantity supplied rose by a **larger percentage** than price, one and a half times as much [1].

**(c)** Any two, for example: the firm had **spare capacity** in Year 1 [1]; panels can be **stored**, so stocks could be released [1]. Also creditworthy: inputs are easy to obtain; labour can be added quickly; a year is long enough to expand.

**8. (a)** At 20 cu: 5(20) - 50 = 50; at 24 cu: 5(24) - 50 = 70 [1].

**(b)** % change in quantity = 20 / 50 x 100 = 40%; % change in price = 4 / 20 x 100 = 20% [1]. PES = 40 / 20 = **2** [1].

**(c)** Elasticity compares **percentage changes**, not the slope [1]. This line meets the **price axis** at a positive price (10 cu), so the quantity base is small relative to the price base and each percentage change in quantity is larger than the percentage change in price [1].

**9. (a)** The number of rooms is **fixed in the short term** [1]; new hotels cannot be built in a week, so quantity supplied cannot respond to the higher price [1].

**(b)** % change in quantity = (900 - 600) / 600 x 100 = 50% [1]. PES = 50 / 50 = **1** (unitary) [1].

**(c)** **Time**: over five years new hotels can be planned and built [1], so fixed factors become variable [1]. **Entry**: higher prices and profits attract new hotel firms [1], which add rooms to market supply [1]. (Also creditworthy: existing buildings converted into hotels as resources switch use.)

**10. (a)** Short run: 10 / 0.2 = **50%** [1]. Long run: 10 / 1.25 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** In the short run farmers **cannot react quickly**, because land and seasons limit output, so a very large price rise would be needed [1]. Over the long run they can expand more easily, so the target is more realistic over a longer period [1].

**11.** Stocks let the firm **increase sales immediately** when price rises, without waiting for production [1], so **supply is more price elastic** [1]. Disadvantage: **storage costs**, or the risk that stock spoils or goes out of fashion [1].

**12.** When demand rises, a shortage appears at the original price [1]. If **supply is inelastic**, firms cannot raise output much, so price must rise a lot to remove the shortage [1]. If **supply is elastic**, firms expand output readily [1], so the shortage is removed mainly by more quantity and the **price rises only a little** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Working out percentages from the new values in Question 7 and Question 9.
- Writing the reciprocal of the PES formula.
- Saying "time" in Question 9(c) without explaining what can change over time.
- Judging elasticity from the slope of the line in Question 8.
