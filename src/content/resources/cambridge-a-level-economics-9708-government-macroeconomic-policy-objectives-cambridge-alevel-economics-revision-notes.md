These notes condense section **5.1 Government macroeconomic policy objectives** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus (examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028), AS content in Topic 5. For explanations and a worked data example, use the [policy objectives study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-government-macroeconomic-policy-objectives-cambridge-alevel-economics/); for exam-style questions, use the [policy objectives practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-government-macroeconomic-policy-objectives-cambridge-alevel-economics-practice/).

**Scope:** the syllabus asks for the use of government policy to achieve **price stability, low unemployment and economic growth**. Policy conflicts and trade-offs are **not required** at AS.

## The objectives at a glance

| Objective | Meaning | Measured by | Not the same as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price stability | Low and steady inflation | Annual % change in the CPI | Zero inflation or falling prices |
| Low unemployment | Few people able, willing and seeking work are without a job | Unemployment rate (survey or claimant count) | Zero unemployment |
| Economic growth | Sustained rise in real GDP | Annual % change in real GDP | A rise in nominal GDP |

## Why each matters

**Price stability**

- Confidence to plan and invest.
- Protects savings, pensions and fixed incomes.
- Keeps exports price competitive.
- Avoids the costs of frequent price changes.

**Low unemployment**

- Output closer to potential; fewer wasted resources.
- Higher household incomes.
- Lower benefit spending and higher tax revenue.
- Fewer social costs of long spells out of work.

**Economic growth**

- Higher average living standards.
- More tax revenue to fund public services.
- Rising sales encourage investment and jobs.

## Policy types (detail in 5.2 to 5.4)

| Policy | Instruments | Main effect |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Government spending, taxation | AD (and capacity through capital spending and incentives) |
| Monetary | Interest rates, money supply, credit regulations | AD |
| Supply-side | Training, incentives, infrastructure and other measures to raise productivity | LRAS |

- **Expansionary:** raises AD (more spending, lower taxes, lower interest rates).
- **Contractionary:** reduces AD (less spending, higher taxes, higher interest rates).

## Matching problem to policy

- Demand-pull inflation above target -> contractionary fiscal or monetary policy.
- Cyclical unemployment in a downturn -> expansionary fiscal or monetary policy.
- Slow growth with spare capacity -> expansionary demand-side policy.
- Slow growth of capacity -> supply-side policy.
- Structural unemployment (skills mismatch) -> supply-side measures such as training.

**Diagnose the cause first**, then choose the policy.

**Two routes to growth.** When there is spare capacity, raising AD lifts actual output towards what the economy can already produce. Once capacity is fully used, sustained growth needs the capacity itself to rise, which is why supply-side policy and capital spending matter for the growth objective over the long run.

## Reading indicator data

- Compare each figure with the **target** if one is given (for example, a 2% inflation target with a band of one point either side means 1% to 3% is on target).
- A falling inflation rate means prices rise **more slowly**, not that they fall.
- A falling growth rate means output rises **more slowly**, not that it falls.
- Changes in rates are measured in **percentage points**.
- Growth compounds: 2% then 3% gives 1.02 x 1.03 = 1.0506, a 5.1% rise.

**Worked recall (invented).** Target inflation 2% (band 1% to 3%).

| | Growth | Inflation | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year A | 3.0% | 2.4% | 4.5% |
| Year B | -1.2% | 0.8% | 7.0% |

Year A: all three objectives broadly met. Year B: output is falling, unemployment has risen by 2.5 percentage points and inflation is below the band, which points to weak AD; expansionary fiscal or monetary policy is the likely response.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Macroeconomic objective | A target for the performance of the whole economy |
| Price stability | Low and stable inflation |
| Inflation target | A numerical rate of inflation that policy aims to achieve |
| Low unemployment | A low unemployment rate, allowing for some frictional and seasonal unemployment |
| Economic growth | A sustained increase in real GDP |
| Expansionary policy | Policy that raises aggregate demand |
| Contractionary policy | Policy that reduces aggregate demand |
| Supply-side policy | Policy that raises productive capacity, shifting LRAS right |

## Exam traps

- Defining price stability as zero inflation.
- Expecting zero unemployment.
- Using nominal instead of real GDP for growth.
- Writing about conflicts and trade-offs when the AS question does not ask for them.
- Recommending a policy without naming the cause of the problem.
- Treating a falling rate as a falling level.

## Self-test

1. Name the three macroeconomic objectives in AS section 5.1.
2. Why is price stability not the same as zero inflation?
3. Why is zero unemployment not a realistic objective?
4. Which measure of output is used for the growth objective?
5. Give two reasons why governments aim for price stability.
6. Give two benefits of low unemployment to the government.
7. Name the three types of macroeconomic policy.
8. Which policy type works mainly on LRAS?
9. Inflation is 5% against a target of 2%, caused by fast-rising AD. What direction of policy is aimed at this?
10. Unemployment is caused by a mismatch of skills. Which policy type is most relevant?
11. Growth falls from 3% to 1%. What has happened to real GDP?
12. Real GDP grows by 4% one year and 2% the next. What is the total rise?

**Answers**

1. Price stability, low unemployment and economic growth.
2. Price stability means low and steady inflation; prices may still rise, but predictably.
3. Some frictional and seasonal unemployment always exists as people move between jobs and work varies by season.
4. Real GDP.
5. Any two: confidence to plan and invest; protects savings and fixed incomes; keeps exports competitive; avoids costs of frequent price changes.
6. Lower benefit spending; higher tax revenue.
7. Fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy.
8. Supply-side policy.
9. Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy.
10. Supply-side policy, such as training.
11. It is still rising, but more slowly.
12. 1.04 x 1.02 = 1.0608, a rise of about 6.1%.
