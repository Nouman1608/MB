## Key definitions

- **Purchasing power parity (PPP) theory:** exchange rates move so that a given sum of money buys the same basket of goods in every country.
- **Absolute PPP:** the exchange rate equals the ratio of the two price levels.
- **Relative PPP:** the exchange rate changes by the gap between the two inflation rates.
- **Managed exchange rate:** the central bank or government acts to influence the value of its currency instead of leaving it wholly to the market.
- **Marshall-Lerner condition:** a depreciation improves the trade balance only if PEDx + PEDm > 1, using positive values.
- **J-curve:** the trade balance worsens after a depreciation, then improves.
- **International competitiveness:** the ability of a country's firms to sell against foreign rivals, through price and non-price factors.

## Formulas and conversions

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| PPP rate | Basket price in country A divided by basket price in country B (A-currency per B-currency) |
| Over- or undervaluation | (market rate − PPP rate) / PPP rate × 100 |
| Relative PPP | New rate = old rate × (1 + inflation A) / (1 + inflation B) |
| Overseas price of an export | Its home price times the rate quoted as foreign units per home unit |
| Home-currency cost of an import | Its price abroad divided by the rate quoted as foreign units per home unit |
| PED | % change in quantity demanded / % change in price |
| Marshall-Lerner | PEDx + PEDm > 1 |
| Multiplier | 1 / marginal propensity to withdraw (save + tax + import) |

## PPP in steps

1. Find the PPP rate: basket cost at home divided by basket cost abroad.
2. Compare it with the market rate. If the market rate shows the foreign currency buying more home currency than PPP implies, the foreign currency is overvalued.
3. Express the gap as a percentage of the PPP rate.
4. For a forecast, apply relative PPP using the inflation gap.

**Worked reminder.** The rate is 1.60 arcs per bel. A basket costs 192 arcs in Country A and 150 bels in Country B. The PPP rate is 192 / 150 = 1.28, so the bel is overvalued by (1.60 − 1.28) / 1.28 = 25%.

**Evaluating PPP.** Say what limits it, then judge.

- Non-tradable goods cannot be arbitraged.
- Transport costs and trade barriers keep prices apart.
- Products are differentiated, and baskets differ.
- Capital flows and speculation dominate the short run.
- Judgement: weak short-run predictor, better long-run guide and a useful benchmark.

## Why countries manage their exchange rate

- Keep the currency low to hold export prices down abroad and protect home producers from import competition.
- Give firms a stable rate to plan against.
- Help correct a persistent current account deficit.

**Costs:** inflation from the extra money created when buying foreign currency, reserves that are finite and have an opportunity cost, interest rate conflict, retaliation by trading partners, and dearer imports.

## Marshall-Lerner in steps

1. Work out the percentage change in prices from the depreciation.
2. Use PEDx to find the change in export volume, and PEDm to find the change in import volume.
3. Revalue exports at the new foreign or home price, and imports at their new home price.
4. Compare the balance before and after, or simply add PEDx and PEDm and compare with 1.

**Reminder example.** Trade is balanced at 5,000 on both sides: 500 exports at 10, and 250 imports at 20. A 5% depreciation, with PEDx = 0.8 and PEDm = 0.5 (sum 1.3):

- Export volume up 4% to 520; revenue 520 × 10 = 5,200.
- Import price up 5% to 21; volume down 2.5% to 243.75; cost 243.75 × 21 = 5,118.75.
- Balance = 5,200 − 5,118.75 = about +81. The sum exceeds 1, so the balance improves.

**Competitiveness reminder.** In a separate illustration, a firm makes a unit in 3 hours at 20 arcs an hour, so unit labour cost is 60 arcs. If 1 arc buys 0.50 bels, that is 30 bels. After the arc falls to 0.40 bels, the same cost is 24 bels, 20% lower in Country B's currency. Imported components, pass-through and rivals' responses can reduce this gain.

**J-curve points.** Short-run elasticities are low because of existing contracts, slow buyer responses and supply lags. The balance falls to a trough, passes its starting level, then rises. A J-curve needs the condition to hold in the long run.

## Competitiveness and macro objectives: chain of reasoning

| Link | Effect |
|---|---|
| Depreciation | Foreign price of exports falls, home price of imports rises |
| If elasticities are high | Volumes respond, net exports rise |
| Aggregate demand | Rises, then the multiplier amplifies it |
| Growth and jobs | Improve, if there is spare capacity |
| Inflation | Rises through imported costs, and from demand-pull if capacity is tight |
| Balance of payments | Improves after the J-curve, if the condition holds |

**Evaluate with:** the size of the elasticities, time lags, spare capacity, pass-through, imported inputs, competitor responses and the weight of imports in spending. Supply-side improvements in productivity and quality give lasting non-price competitiveness.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Absolute and relative PPP | Level of the rate against change in the rate |
| Marshall-Lerner and J-curve | Whether the balance improves at all, against the timing of the improvement |
| Price and non-price competitiveness | Cost and price factors against quality, design and reliability |
| Fall in a currency and rise in import prices | They differ: a 20% fall in what one arc buys (0.50 to 0.40 bels) raises import prices in arcs by 25% |

## Quick self-test

1. State what absolute PPP predicts about the same basket in two countries.
2. Basket cost: 160 arcs in Country A, 80 bels in Country B. The market rate is 2.30 arcs per bel. Find the PPP rate and say how far the bel is overvalued.
3. The rate is 2.00 arcs per bel. Country A inflation is 4% and Country B is 1%. Use relative PPP to forecast the new rate.
4. An export is priced at 60 arcs. The rate falls from 0.50 to 0.40 bels per arc. Find the old and new bel price and the percentage change.
5. An import costs 30 bels. Using the same rates as question 4, find the arc price before and after and the percentage change.
6. A 5% fall in the foreign price of exports raises volume by 9%. A 5% rise in the home price of imports cuts volume by 2%. Find PEDx and PEDm and say if the condition is met.
7. Trade is balanced at 5,000 on each side (500 exports at 10, 250 imports at 20). A 5% depreciation occurs with PEDx = 0.8 and PEDm = 0.5. Find the new balance.
8. Give two reasons for the J-curve.
9. Give two reasons why PPP predicts poorly in the short run.
10. Net exports rise by 3 billion arcs. Saving takes 0.1 of extra income, tax 0.2 and imports 0.2. Find the multiplier and the rise in income.
11. Imports are 20% of the price basket and import prices rise 10%. By how much does the price level rise?
12. Give two costs to a country of keeping its currency low.

### Answers

1. The rate settles where one basket costs the same once converted, so it equals the price ratio of the two countries.
2. PPP rate = 160 / 80 = 2.00. Overvalued by (2.30 − 2.00) / 2.00 = 15%.
3. 2.00 × 1.04 / 1.01 = 2.059 arcs per bel, a rise of about 3.0%. The arc depreciates.
4. 60 × 0.50 = 30 bels; 60 × 0.40 = 24 bels; a fall of 20%.
5. 30 / 0.50 = 60 arcs; 30 / 0.40 = 75 arcs; a rise of 25%.
6. PEDx = 9 / 5 = 1.8; PEDm = 2 / 5 = 0.4; sum 2.2, above 1. Met.
7. About +81 (5,200 − 5,118.75).
8. Contracts fixed in advance, and slow buyer and supplier adjustment. Import costs rise before volumes change.
9. Non-tradable goods, trade costs and differentiated products; also capital flows and speculation.
10. Multiplier = 1 / 0.5 = 2; income rises by 6 billion arcs.
11. 0.20 × 10 = 2%.
12. Any two of: inflation from money creation, reserve costs, interest rate conflict, retaliation, dearer imports and lower living standards.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Computing PEDx + PEDm but not comparing the sum with 1.
- Reversing the quoting convention, so the depreciation seems to raise foreign prices.
- Calling the Marshall-Lerner condition a rule for the short run. The J-curve is the short-run story.
- Describing what PPP says but offering no evaluation.
- Evaluation that lists "it depends" without naming on what: the elasticities, spare capacity or time.
- Leaving out imported inflation when judging depreciation.
- Missing the final step in a chain, from exports to aggregate demand to output and jobs.
- Writing every currency fact as general when the question gives a context.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between absolute and relative PPP?**
Absolute PPP says the exchange rate equals the ratio of the two countries' price levels. Relative PPP says the exchange rate changes by the gap between the two countries' inflation rates.

**What are the costs of keeping a currency low?**
Buying foreign currency to hold the value down creates extra money that can cause inflation, uses reserves that have an opportunity cost, may conflict with interest rate policy, risks retaliation by trading partners and makes imports dearer.

**What is a managed exchange rate?**
A managed exchange rate is one where the central bank or government acts to influence the value of its currency rather than leaving it wholly to the market.

**What gives lasting competitiveness instead of depreciation?**
The OCR A Level Economics (H460) notes point to supply-side improvements in productivity and quality, which give lasting non-price competitiveness that does not fade as a depreciation feeds into domestic costs.
