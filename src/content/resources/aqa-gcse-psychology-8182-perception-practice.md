## Section A

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a binocular depth cue? **[1]**

A. Occlusion
B. Relative size
C. Convergence
D. Linear perspective

**2.** For each illusion, identify the explanation that best accounts for it: ambiguity, fiction or misinterpreted depth cues. **[3]**

**(a)** Rubin's vase
**(b)** The Kanizsa triangle
**(c)** The Ames Room

**3.** Explain what is meant by **size constancy**. **[2]**

**4.** Leo is looking out of the window of a moving bus. The bus stop beside the road seems to rush past, but a tower block in the distance seems to move very slowly.

Identify the depth cue Leo is using and explain how it tells him which object is nearer. **[2]**

**5.** An artist wants the mountains in her painting to look much further away than the farmhouse in the foreground. Identify **two** monocular depth cues she could use and explain how each would create an impression of distance. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** **(a)** Describe Gilchrist and Nesberg's study of motivation and perception. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** weakness of this study. **[2]**

**7.** After watching a documentary about snakes, Mia goes for a walk in a wood and jumps back from a curved stick on the path, which she sees as a snake. Using your knowledge of **perceptual set**, explain Mia's reaction. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how Gregory's constructivist theory accounts for the Müller-Lyer illusion. **[4]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to test whether hunger affects how quickly people recognise food-related words. One group of participants skips breakfast; another group eats breakfast. Both groups then see words flashed briefly on a screen.

**(a)** Write a suitable alternative hypothesis for this study. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the experimental design used. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify **one** ethical issue in this study and explain how the psychologist could deal with it. **[2]**

**10.** Describe and evaluate Gibson's direct theory of perception. Refer to Gregory's theory in your answer. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** (convergence) [1].

**2. (a)** Ambiguity [1]. **(b)** Fiction [1]. **(c)** Misinterpreted depth cues [1].

**3.** Size constancy is the tendency to perceive an object as **staying the same size** [1] even when the **size of its image on the retina changes**, for example as it moves further away [1].

**4.** **Motion parallax** [1]. When moving, **nearer objects appear to move past faster** than distant ones, so the fast-moving bus stop is perceived as near and the slow-moving tower block as far away [1].

**5.** Any two cues, 2 marks each (1 for the cue, 1 for how it creates distance):
- **Height in plane** [1]: paint the mountains **higher up the canvas**, nearer the horizon, as objects higher in the visual field are seen as further away [1].
- **Relative size** [1]: paint the mountains **smaller than expected** relative to the farmhouse, as smaller images of large objects are seen as distant [1].
- **Occlusion** [1]: have the farmhouse **partly cover** the base of the mountains, so the mountains are seen as behind it [1].
- **Linear perspective** [1]: paint a road or field edges **converging** towards the mountains, so they seem to be at the far end [1].

**6. (a)** Participants were **deprived of food** for different lengths of time [1] and asked to judge the **brightness of pictures**, including pictures of **food** [1]. Those who had gone **without food for longer judged the food pictures as brighter**, showing that motivation (hunger) affects perception [1].
**(b)** Any one, developed: the task was **artificial** -- judging the brightness of pictures in a laboratory is not like everyday perception, so the study has **low ecological validity** [1] [1]. Or: depriving participants of food may cause **discomfort**, raising an ethical issue of protection from harm [1] [1].

**7.** Perceptual set is a **readiness to perceive things in a particular way** [1]. The documentary created an **expectation** of snakes (and possibly **fear**, an emotional factor) [1], so Mia **interpreted the ambiguous shape of the stick as a snake**, perceiving what she was primed to expect rather than what was there [1].

**8.** Gregory argued perception is **top-down**, using **past experience** to make inferences [1]. He suggested the line with **outward fins** resembles the **inside corner of a room**, which would be further away, and the line with **inward fins** resembles the **outside corner of a building**, which would be nearer [1]. Because both lines cast the same-sized retinal image, the brain applies **size constancy** and scales up the line it reads as further away [1], so it is **perceived as longer** even though the lines are equal [1].

**9. (a)** Two marks for a directional or non-directional hypothesis with both variables operationalised, for example: "Participants who skip breakfast will **recognise food-related words faster** than participants who eat breakfast." [2] One mark if a variable is vague (for example, "hunger affects perception").
**(b)** **Independent groups** design [1].
**(c)** Issue [1] and way of dealing with it [1], for example: **protection from harm** -- skipping a meal may cause discomfort, so only healthy adults should take part and food should be provided straight after; or **informed consent** -- participants should be told they will be asked to skip breakfast before agreeing; or **right to withdraw** -- remind participants they can stop and eat at any time.

**10.** Marked by levels.

*Indicative content (description):* perception is **direct** and **bottom-up**; the **optic array** contains enough information without inference; **motion parallax** gives distance information as we move (near objects pass quickly, far objects slowly); the ability is **innate**, reflecting **nature** and evolution.

*Indicative content (evaluation):* **strength** -- explains why perception is usually fast and accurate in real, moving environments, such as driving or catching; **weakness** -- cannot easily explain **visual illusions**, which Gregory's top-down theory explains as misapplied inferences; **counterpoint** -- illusions are often flat, still drawings with less information than real scenes, so they may be a weak test of Gibson; **weakness** -- does not explain **perceptual set**: research such as Bruner and Minturn's shows expectation changes perception, which supports Gregory; **judgement** -- each theory may explain different situations: Gibson for rich, real-world viewing, Gregory for brief or ambiguous input.

- **Level 3 (7-9):** accurate, detailed description; well-developed evaluation that uses Gregory effectively; clear judgement.
- **Level 2 (4-6):** mostly accurate description; some evaluation, partly developed; limited use of Gregory.
- **Level 1 (1-3):** basic description; evaluation absent or asserted.
- **0:** no relevant content.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a cue without explaining **how** it shows distance.
- Saying the Kanizsa triangle is an ambiguous figure (it is fiction).
- Writing a hypothesis such as "hunger affects perception" without saying what is measured.
- Naming an ethical issue without a practical way to deal with it.
- In question 10, describing Gregory instead of using him to evaluate Gibson.

## Frequently asked questions

**What perception topics do these practice questions cover?**
They cover AQA GCSE Psychology 8182 section 3.1.2: depth cues, visual illusions, Gibson's and Gregory's theories, motion parallax, perceptual set and the Gilchrist and Nesberg study.

Name the type of explanation, link it to features of the figure, and say how the brain's interpretation differs from the retinal image.
