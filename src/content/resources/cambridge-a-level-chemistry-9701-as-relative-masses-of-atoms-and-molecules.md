## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. define the unified atomic mass unit as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom;
2. define relative atomic mass, Ar, relative isotopic mass, relative molecular mass, Mr, and relative formula mass in terms of the unified atomic mass unit.

Two short outcomes, but every later calculation in the course rests on them, and the definitions are a regular source of lost marks.

## The unified atomic mass unit

Atoms are far too light to weigh individually in grams, so chemists use a unit based on one standard atom.

**The unified atomic mass unit (symbol u) is one twelfth of the mass of one atom of carbon-12.**

Its value is about 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g). A proton and a neutron each have a mass very close to 1 u, which is why mass numbers are close to the relative masses of isotopes.

Carbon-12 is the reference, so by definition one carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u. Every other mass is measured against it.

### Worked example: converting an atom's mass to u

One atom of iron-56 has a mass of 9.288 × 10⁻²³ g. Find its relative isotopic mass.

1. Mass in u = mass of the atom ÷ mass of 1 u.
2. 9.288 × 10⁻²³ g ÷ 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 55.93.
3. Relative isotopic mass of ⁵⁶Fe = **55.93** (no units, because it is a ratio of two masses).

Note that the answer is close to, but not exactly, the mass number 56. Only carbon-12 has a relative isotopic mass that is exactly a whole number.

## The four definitions

All four have the same structure: *a mass* compared with *one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom* (that is, divided by 1 u).

| Quantity | Definition | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| Relative isotopic mass | Mass of one atom of a particular isotope, relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom | One isotope |
| Relative atomic mass, Ar | Weighted mean mass of an atom of an element, relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom | An element as it occurs (a mixture of isotopes) |
| Relative molecular mass, Mr | Weighted mean mass of a molecule, relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom | Simple molecular substances |
| Relative formula mass | Weighted mean mass of one formula unit, relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom | Ionic compounds and giant structures |

Three points examiners check:

- **Weighted mean** appears in Ar, Mr and relative formula mass, because the element or compound contains a mixture of isotopes. It does not appear in relative isotopic mass, which is for one kind of atom.
- The comparison is with **one twelfth** of the carbon-12 atom, not with the whole atom and not with "carbon".
- None of these quantities has units.

**Relative molecular mass or relative formula mass?** Use Mr for substances made of molecules, such as H₂O, CO₂ and C₆H₁₂O₆. Use relative formula mass for ionic compounds such as NaCl and MgO and for giant covalent substances such as SiO₂, which contain no molecules. Both are calculated in exactly the same way, by adding the Ar values in the formula.

## Calculating relative atomic mass

Ar = Σ (relative isotopic mass × percentage abundance) ÷ 100

### Worked example: three isotopes

Magnesium contains ²⁴Mg (79.0 %), ²⁵Mg (10.0 %) and ²⁶Mg (11.0 %). Use mass numbers as relative isotopic masses.

1. 24 × 79.0 = 1896.0
2. 25 × 10.0 = 250.0
3. 26 × 11.0 = 286.0
4. Sum = 2432.0; divide by 100: Ar = **24.3**

Check the answer: it must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotopic masses, closest to the most abundant isotope.

### Worked example: working backwards to an abundance

Boron has two isotopes, ¹⁰B and ¹¹B, and Ar = 10.8. Find the percentage of ¹⁰B.

1. Let the fraction of ¹⁰B be x; the fraction of ¹¹B is 1 − x.
2. 10x + 11(1 − x) = 10.8
3. 11 − x = 10.8, so x = 0.20
4. Boron is **20 % ¹⁰B** and 80 % ¹¹B.

With two isotopes, one unknown is enough. Always define x as a fraction of the whole so the two fractions add to 1.

### Worked example: using precise isotopic masses

Chlorine is 75.8 % ³⁵Cl (relative isotopic mass 34.97) and 24.2 % ³⁷Cl (36.97).

Ar = (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100 = (2650.7 + 894.7) ÷ 100 = **35.45**, which rounds to the data-book value 35.5.

## Calculating relative molecular and formula masses

Add the Ar values of every atom in the formula. Use the Ar values from the Periodic Table you are given (for example H 1.0, C 12.0, N 14.0, O 16.0, Mg 24.3, S 32.1, Ca 40.1, Cu 63.5).

### Brackets

Multiply everything inside a bracket by the subscript outside it.

- Mg(NO₃)₂: 24.3 + 2 × (14.0 + 3 × 16.0) = 24.3 + 2 × 62.0 = **148.3**
- (NH₄)₂SO₄: 2 × (14.0 + 4 × 1.0) + 32.1 + 4 × 16.0 = 36.0 + 32.1 + 64.0 = **132.1**

### Hydrates

The dot in a hydrated formula means "combined with". Add the water molecules as a separate term.

- CuSO₄·5H₂O: 63.5 + 32.1 + 4 × 16.0 + 5 × (2 × 1.0 + 16.0) = 159.6 + 90.0 = **249.6**

The anhydrous salt CuSO₄ alone is 159.6, so water makes up 90.0 ÷ 249.6 × 100 = 36.1 % of the hydrated crystal by mass.

## Linking relative mass to real mass

Because 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g, the actual mass of one particle in grams is its relative mass × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴. One water molecule: 18.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ = 2.99 × 10⁻²³ g. Scaling this up to a usable amount of substance is the job of the mole, in [subtopic 2.2](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-mole-and-the-avogadro-constant/).

## Common mistakes

- Defining Ar as "the mass of an atom compared with carbon-12", missing *weighted mean* and *one twelfth*.
- Including "weighted mean" in the definition of relative isotopic mass.
- Giving units such as g or g mol⁻¹ to a relative mass. Molar mass has units; relative mass does not.
- Calling the relative mass of NaCl a relative molecular mass. NaCl has no molecules.
- Forgetting to multiply the whole bracket, giving Mg(NO₃)₂ as 24.3 + 14.0 + 6 × 16.0.
- Dropping the water of crystallisation from a hydrate's formula mass.
- An Ar answer outside the range of the isotopic masses: a sign of an arithmetic error.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: learn each definition word for word; the key phrases carry the marks.
- **Calculate** Ar: show the multiplication for each isotope, then the division.
- **Deduce** an abundance: set up the equation with x and 1 − x.
- Give answers to the number of significant figures the data justify, normally three or four.
