## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Normal profit | Minimum reward to keep the owner in the industry; TR = TC; included in AC as an opportunity cost |
| Supernormal profit | TR above TC |
| Loss | TR below TC |
| Price taker | A firm with no power over price; takes the market price |
| Allocative efficiency | P = MC |
| Productive efficiency | Output at the lowest point of AC |
| Excess capacity | Output below the output at minimum AC |
| Barrier to entry | Any obstacle that raises the cost or difficulty of starting up as a newcomer |
| Barrier to exit | Any obstacle that makes leaving the market expensive |

## Perfect competition at a glance

**Characteristics:** many buyers and sellers; identical product; perfect knowledge; free entry and exit; profit-maximising firms.

**Firm diagram in words.** Horizontal line at the market price, labelled P = AR = MR = demand. U-shaped MC cuts the line from below at the profit-maximising output. AC and AVC are U-shaped, with MC cutting both at their minimum points.

**Market diagram in words.** Downward-sloping demand and upward-sloping supply cross at the equilibrium price. A shift in supply moves the price that every firm takes.

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Price vs AC | Above (supernormal), equal, or below (loss) | Equal, at minimum AC |
| Profit | Supernormal, normal or loss | Normal only |
| Allocative efficiency (P = MC) | Yes | Yes |
| Productive efficiency | No, unless price is at minimum AC | Yes |

### Method: the firm's decision

1. Find output where MC = MR = P (MC cutting from below).
2. Compare P with AC at that output to find profit or loss.
3. If there is a loss, compare P with AVC. If P ≥ AVC, stay open. If P < AVC, shut down.
4. In the long run, supernormal profit brings entry and a lower price. Loss brings exit and a higher price. Both end at normal profit.

### Worked reminder

A price taker faces price 20 and MC = 20 at 12 units, with AC of 17 at that output. TR = 20 × 12 = 240, TC = 17 × 12 = 204, profit = **36**. If AC were 23, TC = 276 and the result is a loss of **36**. Whether to stay open then depends on AVC: if it is below 20, the firm continues.

## Why the loss-making firm may stay open

Fixed costs are paid whether or not the firm produces. If P ≥ AVC, each unit covers its variable cost and gives something towards fixed cost, so the loss from producing is smaller than the loss from closing. In the long run all costs are variable, so a firm that cannot reach AC will leave.

## Usefulness of perfect competition: evaluation chain

- **For:** benchmark for efficiency; shows how entry erodes supernormal profit; helps explain price-taking in standardised-goods markets.
- **Against:** unrealistic assumptions; large MES may rule out many small firms; normal-only profit may limit funds for innovation; allocative efficiency is private only if externalities exist.
- **Judgement:** better as a yardstick than as a description. Say what the answer depends on, such as the market and the question asked.

## Monopolistic competition at a glance

**Characteristics:** many firms; differentiated products; downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand; low barriers to entry and exit; non-price competition; independent decisions.

**Firm diagram in words.** Downward-sloping AR (demand), with MR below it. MC cuts MR from below at the profit-maximising output. Read price up to the AR curve at that output.

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Position and elasticity set by rivals | Shifts left and becomes more elastic after entry (right for survivors after exit) |
| Profit | Supernormal or loss | Normal; AR just touches AC (tangency) |
| P vs MC | P > MC | P > MC |
| Efficiency | Neither allocative nor productive | Neither: excess capacity |

### Worked reminder

A firm has MC = MR at 6 units, reads a price of 15 from its demand curve, and has AC of 12 at 6 units. Profit = (15 − 12) × 6 = **18**. In the long run, entry shifts demand left until price equals AC at the new output, so profit is zero.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Choice and variety | P > MC: allocative inefficiency |
| Competition keeps profit near normal | Excess capacity; AC above the minimum |
| Low barriers let new firms in | Advertising and differentiation costs |
| Incentive to improve quality | Differences may be superficial |

**Evaluation points:** the inefficiency is smaller when demand is very elastic; consumers may value variety more than the lost efficiency; it depends on whether differentiation is real.

## Barriers to entry and exit

| Entry | Exit |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale | Sunk costs |
| High start-up capital | Specialised, hard-to-sell assets |
| Patents and licences | Redundancy and contract costs |
| Brand loyalty and advertising | Long-term supplier contracts |
| Control of inputs or distribution | Reputation damage |

Perfect competition: none. Monopolistic competition: low. A barrier to exit can keep a loss-making firm open longer, which slows adjustment.

## Reasoning chains to learn

**Entry in perfect competition.** Supernormal profit signals that the industry is attractive. New firms enter because there are no barriers. Market supply shifts right. The market price falls, so every firm's horizontal demand line moves down. Entry stops when price equals minimum AC and profit is normal.

**Entry in monopolistic competition.** Supernormal profit attracts firms whose products are close substitutes. Each existing firm loses customers, so its demand curve shifts left. More substitutes also make demand more elastic, so the curve becomes flatter. Entry stops when demand just touches AC, where profit is normal. The touching point is to the left of the lowest point of AC, which gives excess capacity.

**Exit after a fall in demand.** Price falls below AC and firms make losses. Firms leave once they can, and barriers to exit slow this. Supply shifts left in perfect competition, or the demand curve for each survivor shifts right in monopolistic competition, until normal profit returns.

## Where the two structures differ

| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Identical | Differentiated |
| Demand facing the firm | Horizontal | Downward-sloping |
| Price power | None | Some |
| MR vs price | Equal | MR below price |
| Barriers | None | Low |
| Long-run P vs MC | Equal | P above MC |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Normal vs supernormal profit:** normal is TR = TC; supernormal is TR above TC.
- **Shut-down vs break-even:** shut down when P < AVC; break even when P = AC.
- **Allocative vs productive efficiency:** P = MC versus lowest AC.
- **Price taker vs price maker:** perfectly elastic demand versus downward-sloping demand.
- **Short run vs long run:** fixed factors exist in the short run only, and entry and exit happen in the long run.

## Quick self-test

1. A price taker sells 50 units at 8 each, with TC of 420. State its profit or loss.
2. What are the AR and MR of a perfectly competitive firm?
3. At the long-run price, market demand is 500 units and each firm produces 10. How many firms are there?
4. Which efficiency does P = MC show?
5. Why does a perfectly competitive firm achieve productive efficiency only in the long run?
6. State the PED of the demand curve facing a perfectly competitive firm.
7. State the short-run shut-down rule.
8. A monopolistically competitive firm sells 10 units at 12 each, with AC of 9. Calculate its profit.
9. A firm has demand P = 20 − q. Find MR at 6 units, and TR.
10. Why does entry shift a monopolistically competitive firm's demand curve left?
11. Define excess capacity.
12. Give two barriers to exit.

### Answers

1. TR = 8 × 50 = 400; profit = 400 − 420 = **loss of 20**.
2. Both equal the market price.
3. 500 ÷ 10 = **50 firms**.
4. **Allocative efficiency**.
5. Only in the long run does entry drive price down to the minimum of AC, so output is at the lowest point of AC. In the short run, price can be above AC.
6. **Infinite** (perfectly elastic).
7. Stay open if price is at least AVC; shut down if price is below AVC.
8. (12 − 9) × 10 = **30**.
9. P = 20 − q gives MR = 20 − 2q, so MR = 20 − 12 = **8**; TR = (20 − 6) × 6 = **84**.
10. Entrants take customers, so each existing firm sells less at every price.
11. Producing below the output at which AC is lowest.
12. Any two of: sunk costs, specialised assets, redundancy costs, long-term contracts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- A downward-sloping demand curve drawn for a perfectly competitive firm.
- Profit shown without comparing price with AC at the output chosen.
- Writing "supernormal profit is made because price is high" with no mention of AC.
- Shut-down judged by loss rather than by price against AVC.
- Treating efficiency as a yes-or-no label with no link to P = MC or minimum AC.
- Claiming the monopolistically competitive firm makes supernormal profit in the long run.
- Describing entry without saying what happens to price or demand next.
- An evaluation that lists strengths and limits but gives no judgement.
- Listing barriers without saying how each one blocks entry or exit.
