## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a human resource management (HRM) objective. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs clear HR objectives. **[2]**

**3.** Brindle Logistics employs an average of 150 staff. During the year 33 left. Calculate its labour turnover. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which an HR objective can support a corporate objective of growth. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is the best example of a SMART HR objective?

A: Make staff happier.

B: Reduce absence from 6% to 4% by the end of next year.

C: Recruit more drivers soon.

D: Improve training.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Brindle Logistics has a labour turnover of 22%. It sets an objective to reduce this to 16% within two years. Each leaver costs £2,500 in recruitment and training. A retention programme would cost £15,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the number of leavers at the target rate, for 150 staff, and the annual saving if the target is met. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the net annual benefit of the programme and the number of months the saving takes to cover the cost of the programme. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Brindle Logistics should adopt the programme. **[8]**

**7.** Brindle Logistics pays wages of £1,350,000 on revenue of £4,500,000. A director proposes an objective of cutting the labour cost ratio to 27%.

**(a)** Calculate the current labour cost ratio and the wage bill at 27% of the current revenue. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of this objective to the business and its stakeholders. **[8]**

**8.** Recommend and justify two HR objectives for a fast-growing restaurant chain that plans to open eight new sites in two years. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An HRM objective is a target set by the HR function [1] to make sure the business has the right people, with the right skills, working effectively at an acceptable cost [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Clear objectives show managers what to aim for [1], so they can decide between competing uses of money and measure whether HR activity has worked [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 33 ÷ 150 [1] × 100 = **22%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** If the business plans to grow by opening new sites, an HR objective to recruit and train the staff for those sites on time [1] ensures that the growth is not held back by a lack of skilled people [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Leavers at 16% = 0.16 × 150 = **24** [1]. Current leavers = 33, so 9 fewer [1]. Saving = 9 × £2,500 = **£22,500** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Net benefit = £22,500 − £15,000 = **£7,500** a year [1]. Months to cover the cost = £15,000 ÷ £22,500 [1] × 12 = **8 months** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** On the turnover savings alone, the programme gains £7,500 a year [1]. It needs only 6 fewer leavers to cover its cost (£15,000 ÷ £2,500), against the target of 9 fewer, so there is a margin of safety of 3 leavers [1].
Other benefits are not in the figures: experienced drivers make fewer mistakes, and customers prefer a regular driver [1]; lower turnover may reduce accidents and training time [1]. Higher morale may also reduce absence [1].
Limits: the target may not be met, and drivers may leave for pay that the programme does not match [1]. The programme's content matters; if people leave because of poor management, a retention programme may not help [1].
Judgement: Brindle should adopt the programme, because the target is realistic and the benefit is positive even if only part of it is achieved; it should first find out why drivers leave, and review the results after a year [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Current ratio = £1,350,000 ÷ £4,500,000 [1] × 100 = **30%** [1]. At 27%: 0.27 × £4,500,000 = **£1,215,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Benefits: it is measurable and clear, and ties people costs to revenue, so wages cannot grow faster than the business [1]. A saving of £135,000 would raise profit if revenue stays the same [1]. Owners gain from higher profit [1].
Limits: it can be reached by cutting staff or pay rather than by raising revenue or productivity [1], which could harm service, raise turnover and reduce morale [1]. Employees may see the objective as a threat to jobs [1]. A single ratio hides quality of service, and a fixed ratio may be wrong in a quiet year when revenue falls [1].
Judgement: the objective is useful as a control on costs, but it should be paired with objectives on productivity and retention so that it is not met by cuts that harm service [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Objective 1: recruit and train 120 new staff by the opening date of each site, with trained staff in place two weeks before opening [1]. This links to the corporate growth objective, because sites cannot open without staff [1]. It is measurable and time-bound [1]. It needs a recruitment budget and a training team, and a plan for each site [1].
Objective 2: keep labour turnover below 25% a year [1]. High turnover is common in restaurants and is costly for a growing chain, because every leaver must be replaced and trained [1]. A lower rate protects service quality, a source of reputation for a new chain [1]. It might be achieved by better induction, career steps to supervisor and fair rotas, at a cost of pay and training [1].
Judgement: these two objectives fit together, but there is a risk of conflict with a labour cost objective if the business also needs to control wages; the chain should prioritise recruitment first in the expansion years and review cost targets after the sites are open [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing HR activities rather than objectives.
- Using total staff rather than the average in labour turnover.
- Giving objectives that are not measurable.
- Evaluating with benefits only.
- Forgetting the link to the corporate objectives in the case.
