These notes condense section 12.01, Sampling, of OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560, Version 2.1 (August 2026). The single statement, 12.01a Populations and samples, asks you to define the population in a study, understand the difference between population and sample, infer properties of populations or distributions from a sample, and understand simple random sampling and bias. It is content for **both tiers**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Every person, item or event a study wants to draw conclusions about | All Year 10 students at one school |
| Sample | Part of the population, chosen to represent it | 40 of those students |
| Census | Data collected from every member of the population | Asking all 210 Year 10 students |
| Infer | Use a sample to estimate a property of the population | 30% of the sample, so about 30% of all |
| Representative | A sample whose make-up reflects the population | Similar mix of ages and groups |
| Sampling frame | A complete list of the population, used to choose the sample | The school's Year 10 register |
| Simple random sample | Every member has an equal chance of selection; every sample of that size is equally likely | Names chosen by a random number generator |
| Bias | A tendency of the method to favour some members, pushing results one way | Surveying only people at a gym |
| Reliability | How consistent estimates would be if the sampling were repeated | Larger fair samples are more reliable |
| Non-response | Chosen members who do not reply | Only 1 in 5 posted forms returned |

## Population or sample?

- The **population** is defined by the question being asked, including any place and time it mentions.
- The **sample** is the group actually measured or asked.
- Reasons to sample: the population is large, hard to list or reach; a census costs too much or takes too long; or testing destroys the item.
- The cost of sampling: results are estimates, and different samples give different estimates.

## Inferring from a sample

```
estimate of a count  = (sample count ÷ sample size) × population size
estimate of a total  = sample mean × population size
scale factor         = population size ÷ sample size
```

- Estimate a whole distribution by multiplying every category frequency by the same scale factor. The estimates must add to the population size.
- To combine two fair samples, add the counts and add the sample sizes. Do not average the two proportions unless the samples are the same size.
- A larger fair sample usually gives a more reliable estimate.
- Write "about" or "estimate" in your conclusion.

## Simple random sampling: the method

1. Get a full list of the population (the sampling frame).
2. Number every member, using the same number of digits (001, 002, …).
3. Generate random numbers.
4. Ignore any number with no matching member, and any repeat.
5. Stop when you reach the sample size.

The method gives every member an equal chance of selection. The sample that comes out might still look unusual by chance; that is not bias.

## Bias: where it comes from

| Cause | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Incomplete sampling frame | Members missing from the list can never be chosen |
| Convenience sampling | Only people easy to reach are asked |
| One time or one place | People who are elsewhere at that time are left out |
| Self-selection (volunteers) | People with strong views are over-represented |
| Non-response | Those who reply may differ from those who do not |
| Leading questions | Wording pushes people towards one answer |

To fix bias, change **how** the sample is chosen or the question asked. Collecting more data in the same biased way does not help.

## Exam traps

- **Population written as the sample:** "the 50 people asked" is the sample.
- **Population too wide:** "all people" when the question is about one town or one school.
- **"Bigger means unbiased":** size improves reliability, not fairness.
- **Averaged proportions:** combine counts instead.
- **Random numbers out of range:** ignore them and draw again; do not round them into range.
- **Generic criticism:** "not random" earns little without saying who is missed and why it matters.
- **Exact language:** an estimate from a sample is never certain; do not say "exactly 420".
- **Distribution check missed:** category estimates must total the population.

## Quick self-test

1. A head teacher wants to know how much homework Year 9 students at her school do each week. Define the population.
2. Give two reasons why a study might use a sample instead of the whole population.
3. A factory tests 20 bolts by bending each one until it snaps. Why must the factory use a sample?
4. In a sample of 80 voters, 52 support a plan. The town has 9000 voters. Estimate how many support the plan.
5. A sample of 25 eggs has a total mass of 1.55 kg. Estimate the total mass of 600 eggs.
6. A library has 2000 members. A sample of 40 gives their main use: fiction 18, non-fiction 14, audiobooks 8. Estimate the number of members in each group.
7. One sample finds 9 of 30 households own a dog; another finds 16 of 50. Combine them to estimate the number of dog-owning households in a village of 1600 households.
8. Members are numbered 01 to 75. The random numbers are 83, 07, 41, 07, 00, 66, 52. Choose a sample of four.
9. To study a town's bus service, a researcher asks the first 50 people off a morning train. Give one reason the sample may be biased.
10. A survey asks: "Wouldn't you agree our café is excellent?" What is wrong with this question?
11. True or false: a sample of 2000 people who phoned a radio show is unbiased because it is large. Give a reason.
12. State what makes a sample a simple random sample.

### Answers

1. **All Year 9 students at her school.**
2. Any two: the population is too large to survey; a census costs too much; it takes too long; testing destroys the items.
3. The test **destroys** the bolts, so testing them all would leave none to sell.
4. 52/80 = 0.65; 0.65 × 9000 = **about 5850**.
5. Mean 1.55 ÷ 25 = 0.062 kg; 0.062 × 600 = **about 37.2 kg**.
6. Scale factor 2000 ÷ 40 = 50: **fiction 900, non-fiction 700, audiobooks 400** (total 2000).
7. Combined 25/80 = 0.3125; 0.3125 × 1600 = **about 500 households**.
8. Ignore 83 (out of range), the repeat 07 and 00: **07, 41, 66, 52**.
9. For example: train passengers may not use buses, so their views do not represent bus users; or only people travelling at that time are asked.
10. It is a **leading question**: it encourages people to agree, so the results are biased.
11. **False.** The people are self-selected, so those with strong views are over-represented. Size does not remove bias.
12. Every member of the population has an **equal chance** of being chosen, and every possible sample of that size is equally likely.
