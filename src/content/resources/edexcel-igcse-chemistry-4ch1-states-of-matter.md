## Using the particle model to explain properties (1.1)

Every answer in this sub-topic uses the same three ideas: how the particles are **arranged**, how they **move**, and how much **energy** they have. Use them to explain the everyday properties of each state rather than just listing them.

- **Solids keep a fixed shape** because the particles are held in fixed positions by strong forces of attraction; they only vibrate.
- **Liquids flow and take the shape of the container** because the particles are still close together but can move past one another.
- **Gases can be compressed** because the particles are far apart with large spaces between them. Solids and liquids are hard to compress because their particles are already touching.
- **Gases fill any container** because the particles move quickly and randomly in all directions.

Particle energy rises from solid to liquid to gas; forces of attraction between particles are strongest in the solid and weakest in the gas.

## Interconversions and the energy changes behind them (1.2)

The names are melting (solid to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid), evaporating or boiling (liquid to gas), condensing (gas to liquid) and sublimation (solid straight to gas). Melting, boiling, evaporating and sublimation are achieved by **heating**: energy is taken in so the particles can overcome forces of attraction. Freezing and condensing are achieved by **cooling**: energy is given out as forces of attraction pull the particles closer.

### Reading a cooling curve

A cooling curve plots temperature against time as a liquid cools and freezes. For a pure substance it has a flat section at the freezing point.

**Worked example 1.** A boiling tube of molten substance X (a pure solid at room temperature) is left to cool. The temperature is recorded every minute.

| Time / min | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 85 | 77 | 70 | 70 | 70 | 70 | 63 | 56 | 50 |

(a) State the freezing point of X. (b) Explain why the temperature stays constant from 2 to 5 minutes. (c) Describe the particles at 8 minutes.

```
(a) 70 °C: the flat section is where liquid and solid are both present.
(b) As X freezes, particles move closer and forces of attraction form,
    which gives out energy. This energy replaces the heat lost to the
    surroundings, so the temperature does not fall until freezing is complete.
(c) Solid: particles close together in a regular arrangement,
    vibrating about fixed positions, with less energy than at 0 minutes.
```

The same logic explains a heating curve the other way round: during melting the energy taken in is used to overcome forces between particles, not to make them move faster, so the temperature stays constant.

### Predicting the state at a given temperature

If a substance's melting point and boiling point are known, compare them with the temperature in the question.

- Below the melting point: solid.
- Between the melting point and the boiling point: liquid.
- Above the boiling point: gas.

**Worked example 2.** State the physical state of each substance at 20 °C.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| P | −95 | 56 |
| Q | 801 | 1413 |
| R | −210 | −196 |
| S | 18 | 290 |

```
P: 20 is between -95 and 56   -> liquid
Q: 20 is below 801            -> solid
R: 20 is above -196           -> gas
S: 20 is between 18 and 290   -> liquid (only just above its melting point)
```

The trap is negative numbers: −196 °C is lower than 20 °C, so R has already boiled.

## Explaining the dilution and diffusion experiments (1.3)

The specification asks you to **explain results**, so learn what each observation proves.

**Dilution.** If a solution is still coloured after many dilutions, there must have been a very large number of coloured particles in the original crystals, so each particle must be very small. Each dilution spreads the same number of coloured particles through more water, so the colour becomes paler.

**Diffusion of gases.** Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, caused by their random motion. The key observations are:

- A coloured gas such as bromine spreads into a jar of air without any stirring, so the particles must be moving on their own.
- Mixing takes time because gas particles collide with air particles and change direction many times.
- Diffusion is faster at a higher temperature because the particles have more energy and move faster.

**Model answer: the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube.** Cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is placed at one end of a long glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid at the other. After a few minutes a white ring forms, nearer the hydrochloric acid end.

> The two gases diffuse along the tube because their particles move randomly [1]. Where they meet, they react to form solid ammonium chloride, NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl, which is the white ring [1]. Ammonia particles (Mr 17) are lighter than hydrogen chloride particles (Mr 36.5), so they move faster and travel further in the same time [1]. So the ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end [1].

Saying the ring forms "in the middle" loses the last two marks.

## Solutions and saturated solutions (1.4)

The **solute** dissolves in the **solvent** to make a **solution**. A **saturated solution** is one in which no more solute will dissolve at that temperature, so some undissolved solid stays at the bottom.

## Solubility and solubility curves (1.5C, 1.6C) — Paper 2 only

**Solubility** is the mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a stated temperature, in **g per 100 g of solvent**.

To **plot** a solubility curve, put temperature (°C) on the x-axis and solubility (g per 100 g of water) on the y-axis, choose scales that use more than half the grid, plot each point accurately and draw a smooth curve of best fit, not dot-to-dot lines.

To **interpret** a curve:

- A point **on** the curve is a saturated solution.
- A point **below** the curve is an unsaturated solution: more solid could dissolve.
- When a saturated solution is cooled, the mass of crystals that forms equals the solubility at the higher temperature minus the solubility at the lower temperature, scaled to the actual mass of water.

**Worked example 3.** Salt Z has these solubilities.

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solubility / g per 100 g water | 24 | 32 | 42 | 54 | 68 |

A student dissolves 21 g of Z in 50 g of water at 60 °C and lets the solution cool. At what temperature do crystals first appear?

```
21 g in 50 g of water = 21 x 100 / 50 = 42 g per 100 g of water
the curve reaches 42 g per 100 g at 40 °C
crystals first appear at about 40 °C
```

Scaling to 100 g of water first is the step most often missed.

## Practical: measuring solubility (1.7C) — Paper 2 only

Questions often ask you to justify a step, so learn the reason for each.

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Stir excess solid in water held at the chosen temperature (water bath) until some stays undissolved | Undissolved solid shows the solution is saturated; solubility depends on temperature |
| Pour off or filter only clear solution into a weighed evaporating basin | Undissolved solid in the basin would make the mass of solute too high |
| Evaporate gently | Strong heating can spit out solid, making the mass too low |
| Heat, cool and reweigh until the mass is constant | Shows all the water has gone |

Mass of water = mass of solution − mass of dry solid. Solubility = mass of solid ÷ mass of water × 100.

## Common mistakes

- Saying particles in a solid "do not move". They vibrate about fixed positions.
- Saying temperature stays constant during melting because "no energy is going in". Energy is going in; it is used to overcome forces of attraction.
- Giving a solubility without the temperature, or in g/dm³.
- Forgetting to scale the mass of water to 100 g.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (for example, the particles in a gas): give arrangement, movement and energy as separate statements.
- **Explain** (for example, a plateau on a curve or a diffusion result): link the observation to a particle reason with "because" or "so".
- **Calculate**: show the scaling to 100 g of water and give units.
