These notes condense two terms from the cognitive approach (SL and HL, first assessment 2027): **cognitive load theory** and **dual processing theory**. The guide's learning objectives are the role of cognitive load theory in understanding human cognition, and the role of dual processing theory in understanding decision-making. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-cognitive-load-and-dual-processing/), then try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-cognitive-load-and-dual-processing-practice/).

## At a glance

| Theory | Core claim | Key terms | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cognitive load theory | Learning is limited by working memory capacity | Intrinsic, extraneous and germane load; schemas | Sweller and Cooper (1985); Chandler and Sweller (1991) |
| Dual processing theory | Thinking uses a fast automatic mode and a slow effortful mode | System 1, System 2, heuristics, cognitive biases | Frederick (2005); Tversky and Kahneman (1974); Shiv and Fedorikhin (1999) |

## Key definitions

- **Working memory:** the limited-capacity system that holds and manipulates information briefly.
- **Cognitive load:** the demand placed on working memory by a task.
- **Intrinsic load:** demand set by the complexity of the material and the learner's prior knowledge.
- **Extraneous load:** demand caused by poor presentation.
- **Germane load:** effort that builds schemas.
- **Schema:** an organised framework of knowledge; it lets many items be handled as one chunk.
- **System 1:** fast, automatic, low-effort thinking.
- **System 2:** slow, controlled, effortful thinking.
- **Heuristic:** a mental shortcut.
- **Cognitive bias:** a systematic error in thinking, such as anchoring or confirmation bias.
- **Secondary task:** an extra task used to measure how much capacity the main task uses.

## Cognitive load theory

- **Three loads:** intrinsic (task and learner), extraneous (presentation), germane (schema building). Aim: cut extraneous load, manage intrinsic load, protect germane load.
- **Worked-example effect:** novices learn better from studying solved examples before practising (Sweller and Cooper, 1985).
- **Split-attention effect:** separated text and diagram force the learner to integrate them; placing text within the diagram helps (Chandler and Sweller, 1991).
- **Redundancy effect:** repeating the same information in several forms adds load.
- **Expertise reversal effect:** support that helps novices can hinder experts.
- **Measurement:** self-rated effort, secondary-task performance, pupil dilation. All indirect.
- **Evaluate:** strong experimental support and practical use; the three loads are hard to separate; subjective measures; short lab tasks and student samples.
- **Concepts:** measurement, causality, change.

## Dual processing theory

| Feature | System 1 | System 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Effort | Low | High |
| Awareness | Mostly unconscious | Conscious |
| Risk | Bias from heuristics | Slow and limited capacity |

- **Bat and ball problem:** the intuitive answer, 10 cents, is wrong; the correct answer is 5 cents. Many students gave the intuitive answer (Frederick, 2005).
- **Anchoring and availability (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974):** examples of System 1 heuristics.
- **Load and food choice (Shiv and Fedorikhin, 1999):** a harder memory task made participants more likely to choose cake over fruit salad.
- **Evaluate:** simple and explains many biases; descriptive labels, not two brain systems; hard to falsify; lab tasks; Western student samples.
- **Concepts:** bias, perspective, causality, change (nudges and debiasing).

## Links between the theories

- High load uses up working memory, so System 2 has less capacity and System 1 errors increase.
- Strong schemas make System 1 accurate for experts.
- Reducing extraneous load leaves room for System 2 analysis.

## Concept links to prepare

- **Measurement:** how to operationalise load and which system is used.
- **Bias:** cognitive biases in decisions; sampling bias in research.
- **Causality:** experiments isolate design effects; real classrooms are more complex.
- **Change:** redesigning lessons, defaults and nudges.
- **Perspective:** cognitive explanation compared with biological or sociocultural explanations.

## A model Section A answer

**Question:** Explain cognitive load theory, with one example. (4 marks, written for practice.)

**Strong answer:** Cognitive load theory proposes that learning is limited because working memory can handle only a small amount of information at once. Material that is complex (intrinsic load) or badly presented (extraneous load) can overload working memory, leaving little capacity to build schemas (germane load). For example, if a student has to look back and forth between a diagram and a separate paragraph, the effort of linking them is extraneous load. Placing the labels on the diagram would reduce the load and improve learning.

## A model Section B approach

Scenario: a shopper in a noisy market, while trying to remember a list, buys a larger bottle because it is "50 per cent off".
1. Name the term: dual processing; System 1 relies on the discount as a heuristic.
2. Apply: distraction and memory load reduce System 2 capacity, so the shopper does not check the unit price.
3. Add a qualification: System 1 is often efficient, and the error shows when the situation is unusual.

## Common mistakes

- Treating cognitive load as stress or tiredness.
- Saying System 1 is always wrong.
- Describing a study with no link to the theory.
- Leaving out measurement problems.
- Calling the two systems "two parts of the brain".

## Self-test

1. What are the three types of cognitive load?
2. What is the split-attention effect?
3. Why can worked examples become less useful for experts?
4. What is the correct answer to the bat and ball problem, and why is the intuitive answer wrong?
5. Give one limitation of dual processing theory.
6. How can high cognitive load increase biased decisions?
7. Name one way to measure cognitive load.

**Answers:** 1. Intrinsic, extraneous and germane. 2. Learning is harder when related information is separated, because the learner must hold and integrate the parts. 3. Experts already have the schema, so the examples add redundant load (expertise reversal effect). 4. The ball costs 5 cents. The intuitive answer of 10 cents would make the bat 1.10 and the total 1.20, but the intuition is fast and unchecked. 5. The two systems are descriptive labels, hard to test or falsify, and not separate brain systems. 6. High load uses up working memory, so System 2 checking is less available and fast heuristics dominate. 7. Self-rated mental effort, secondary-task performance or pupil dilation.
