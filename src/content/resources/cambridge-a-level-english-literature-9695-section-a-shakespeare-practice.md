## Section A — Knowledge and terms

**1.** State the assessment objectives tested in Paper 3 Section A and the weighting of each. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between what **AO3** and **AO5** reward. **[2]**

**3.** Define **blank verse** and explain why a departure from it can be significant. **[2]**

**4.** What is a **shared line**, and what can it suggest about the speakers? **[2]**

**5.** Explain how the Induction of *The Taming of the Shrew* can affect an audience's interpretation of
the main play. **[3]**

## Section B — Extracts and evaluation

**6.** Read this **original** verse extract.

> KING: You have been absent from the court too long.
>
> LORD: My lord, my mother —
>
> KING: Died. I know. We grieved.
>
> And grief, like any guest, must leave in time.

**(a)** Identify the **shared line** and explain its effect. **(b)** Explain how the simile in the KING's
final line presents his attitude. **[5]**

**7.** Read this **original** passage, in which a nobleman suddenly speaks in prose.

> DUKE: Why, then, let it be so. Let the fool keep his crown of paper, and the priest his candle, and I
> will keep my wits, if I can find where I left them.

Explain **two** possible reasons for the shift from verse into prose here, and the effect on an audience.
**[4]**

**8.** Read this **original** soliloquy.

> They think me idle. Let them think it so.
> The hawk that seems asleep upon the glove
> sees farther than the falconer, and waits.
> When I unhood, they'll see what I have seen.

Explain how this soliloquy positions the audience in relation to the speaker. **[4]**

**9.** A student writes: "Critic X says Hamlet delays because he thinks too much." Explain why this
sentence alone earns little AO5 credit, and rewrite it as a stronger AO5 point. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** contrasting ways a director might stage Katherina's final speech in *The Taming of
the Shrew*, and how each changes the meaning of the ending. **[4]**

**11.** For *Henry IV, Part 1*, explain how the **alternation of tavern and court scenes** contributes to
the play's presentation of Hal. **[4]**

**12.** Explain **three** features of a strong plan for the (a) question: "How far does the play you have
studied present order restored at its ending?" **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **AO1, AO2, AO3, AO4 and AO5** [1], each weighted **20%** of the paper [1].

**2.** **AO3** rewards the candidate's **own informed, independent interpretation** [1]; **AO5** rewards
**discussing and evaluating other interpretations**, such as critical readings or staging choices,
against the candidate's own [1].

**3.** Blank verse is **unrhymed iambic pentameter** [1]. Because it is the expected pattern for serious
speech, a **break** in metre, or a move into prose or rhyme, signals a change in mood, status or
self-control [1].

**4.** A shared line is a single pentameter **split between two or more speakers** [1]. It can suggest
**intimacy, urgency or conflict**, as one speaker completes or seizes another's line [1].

**5.** The Induction shows a drunken tinker, **Christopher Sly, tricked into believing he is a lord**, for
whom the main story is performed as a play [1]. This frame presents the action as **a performance within
a performance** [1], so the audience may read the "taming" and Katherina's final submission as staged or
ironic rather than as straightforward endorsement [1].

**6. (a)** The shared line is "My lord, my mother — / Died. I know. We grieved." [1] The KING **seizes
the LORD's line** and finishes it, cutting off his explanation [1], so power is shown through control of
the verse itself [1]. **(b)** The simile makes grief a **guest who outstays a welcome** [1], presenting the
KING as impatient and dismissive of mourning, placing duty to the court above private loss [1].

**7.** Possible reasons: the DUKE's **self-control has slipped**, so the loss of metre reflects inner
disorder [1]; or he adopts a **mocking, satirical register**, dismissing the "fool" and "priest" with
contempt [1]. Effect: the audience hears a sudden **change of tone** [1], which marks this as a turning
point in his attitude or state of mind [1].

**8.** The soliloquy makes the audience **confidants**, sharing knowledge the other characters lack [1].
This creates **dramatic irony**: we watch later scenes knowing the speaker's idleness is a pose [1]. The
hawk imagery presents him as **patient and predatory** [1], so the audience may admire his cunning or be
uneasy at being drawn into his calculation [1].

**9.** It **reports** a view without **evaluating** it, and "Critic X" is not doing any work [1] [1]. A
stronger version: "One reading presents Hamlet's delay as excessive reflection; this is persuasive in the
soliloquies, where he argues with himself, but it underplays his practical reason for testing the Ghost's
truth, which makes the delay seem caution as much as paralysis." [1] [1] (One mark for testing against
the text, one for a judgement.)

**10.** **Played sincerely**, the speech presents Katherina's conversion as complete, so the ending
**celebrates social and marital order** [1] [1]. **Played ironically**, with knowing delivery or shared
glances, it suggests a **performance of obedience** that hides her independence, so the ending becomes
subversive or troubling [1] [1].

**11.** The alternation sets **two worlds side by side**, inviting the audience to compare their values
[1]. It shows Hal **moving between them** [1], and because his early soliloquy reveals his plan, the
tavern scenes appear as a **testing ground** for the role he will later play at court [1]. The structure
builds towards his reconciliation with his father and his public **change of role** in battle [1].

**12.** A thesis that **makes a judgement** on "How far" (for example: order is restored formally, but
the ending leaves tensions unresolved) [1] [1]. Selection of moments from across the play, **including the
final scene**, to show development [1] [1]. **AO5** woven in, for example by contrasting a reading of the
ending as reassuring with one that finds it uneasy, then evaluating them [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming critics or productions without evaluating their readings.
- Leaving all AO5 material to the final paragraph.
- Never commenting on verse, prose shifts or shared lines.
- In (b) answers, never connecting the passage to the rest of the play.
