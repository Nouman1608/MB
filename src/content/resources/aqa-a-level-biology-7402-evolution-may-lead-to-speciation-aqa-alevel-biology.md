This guide covers **3.7.3 Evolution may lead to speciation** from AQA A-level Biology (7402), in Topic 7, *Genetics, populations, evolution and ecosystems*. It is A-level-only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3, and it is a common source of extended-response questions. The broader guide to [populations, Hardy-Weinberg, selection and speciation](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-populations-hardy-weinberg-selection-and-speciation/) sets out each idea with a worked selection calculation; this page concentrates on building full explanations, reading selection graphs, the reasoning behind drift, and planning speciation answers. Use the [evolution and speciation revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-evolution-may-lead-to-speciation-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) for recall and the [evolution and speciation practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-evolution-may-lead-to-speciation-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/) to test yourself.

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Individuals in a population may show a wide range of variation in phenotype, due to genetic and environmental factors | Explain the sources of variation |
| The primary source of genetic variation is mutation; meiosis and random fertilisation produce further variation | Name each source and say what it does |
| Predation, disease and competition result in differential survival and reproduction (natural selection) | Explain the process |
| Organisms with advantageous phenotypes produce more offspring and pass on favourable alleles; the effect on allele frequencies | Link phenotype to allele frequency |
| The effects of stabilising, directional and disruptive selection | Describe and recognise each from data |
| Evolution as a change in the allele frequencies in a population | Use this definition |
| Reproductive separation of two populations leads to differences in gene pools; new species arise when they can no longer interbreed to give fertile offspring | Explain speciation |
| Allopatric and sympatric speciation | Distinguish and explain both |
| The importance of genetic drift in causing changes in allele frequency in small populations | Explain why drift matters only in small populations |
| Explain how evolutionary change over a long time has resulted in a great diversity of species | Link repeated speciation to diversity |

## Variation: three genetic sources and the environment

| Source | What it does |
|---|---|
| Mutation | Creates new alleles; the primary source of genetic variation |
| Meiosis | Produces new combinations of alleles in gametes, by crossing over and independent segregation |
| Random fertilisation | Any gamete can fuse with any other, giving new combinations in offspring |
| Environment | Modifies how a genotype is expressed in the phenotype |

Only mutation produces new alleles. Meiosis and fertilisation reshuffle existing ones. Mark schemes often split these into separate marks, so do not merge them into "genetic differences". Environmental variation is not inherited, so selection acting on it does not change allele frequencies.

## Natural selection as a chain

Most marks in selection questions come from a fixed sequence. Write it in order and fit it to the context:

1. **Variation:** individuals differ in phenotype, partly because of different alleles (originally from mutation).
2. **Selection pressure:** predation, disease or competition for the means of survival.
3. **Advantage:** individuals with a particular phenotype are more likely to survive.
4. **Reproduction:** they produce more offspring.
5. **Inheritance:** they pass on the alleles for that phenotype.
6. **Change:** the frequency of those alleles increases in the gene pool over many generations.

Step 6 is the definition of evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population. An answer that stops at "the organisms survive" has missed the last three steps.

## Reading selection from graphs

Questions show a frequency distribution of a characteristic before and after selection. Compare the mean and the spread.

| Pattern after selection | Type | When it happens |
|---|---|---|
| Same mean, narrower range | Stabilising | Environment stable; intermediate phenotypes have the advantage; extremes selected against |
| Mean shifted to one side | Directional | Environment changes; one extreme now has the advantage |
| Two peaks, fewer intermediates | Disruptive | Environment has two or more different niches; both extremes favoured |

Human birth mass is a standard example of stabilising selection: babies of intermediate mass have higher survival than very small or very large babies.

**Worked example 1 (invented data).** A population of lizards was measured before and after a drought that left only very hard seeds.

| | Mean jaw width / mm | Range / mm |
|---|---|---|
| Before | 8.2 | 6.0–10.4 |
| Five generations after | 9.1 | 7.1–11.2 |

> The mean has shifted from 8.2 to 9.1 mm, an increase of 0.9 mm (11%), and the whole range has moved upwards: directional selection. Lizards with wider jaws could crack the hard seeds, so they were more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on alleles for wider jaws. The frequency of these alleles rose.

## Genetic drift: why population size matters

Genetic drift is a change in allele frequency due to chance: which individuals happen to survive, mate, or pass on which alleles. It has no direction and is not related to advantage.

Think of it as sampling. Every generation, the gene pool is a sample of the alleles from the previous one.

**Worked example 2 (invented numbers).** An allele has a frequency of 0.5 in two populations. In each, three individuals that are homozygous for the allele die by chance before breeding.

```
Population of 10 (20 alleles):    10 copies of the allele → lose 6
                                  new frequency = 4 ÷ 14 = 0.29
Population of 1000 (2000 alleles): 1000 copies → lose 6
                                  new frequency = 994 ÷ 1994 = 0.498
```

The same chance event changes the frequency greatly in the small population and hardly at all in the large one. This is why the specification asks you to explain that drift is important only in small populations. In a small population an allele can even be lost completely, or become the only allele (fixed), by chance alone.

## Speciation

The specification's sequence:

1. Two populations become **reproductively separated**, so there is no gene flow between them.
2. Each population has different mutations, and may face different selection pressures (and, if small, drift).
3. **Differences accumulate in their gene pools**: allele frequencies change in different ways.
4. Eventually the genetic differences lead to an **inability of members of the populations to interbreed and produce fertile offspring**.
5. They are now separate species. In this way, new species arise from existing species.

### Allopatric and sympatric

| | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Cause of reproductive separation | Geographical isolation: a physical barrier, such as a mountain range, sea or river | Reproductive isolation within the same area, without a geographical barrier |
| Example of mechanism | Populations on separate islands | Different breeding seasons, different courtship behaviour, or changes in genitalia or flowers that prevent successful mating |
| Gene flow | Prevented by the barrier | Prevented by the isolating mechanism |

In a sympatric answer, the key is to say what stops the two groups breeding even though they live in the same place, and then follow the same steps 2 to 5.

## Planning a six-mark speciation answer

A question might read: "Explain how a new species of fish could arise after a lake was divided into two by a landslide." A strong plan:

- Landslide = geographical barrier → allopatric; no interbreeding, no gene flow.
- Variation within each population from mutation.
- Different conditions in each part of the lake → different selection pressures; name one (for example different food).
- Different alleles favoured, so allele frequencies change differently in each gene pool.
- Over many generations, genetic differences accumulate.
- If the populations meet again, they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring: separate species.

Each bullet is a likely marking point; context words ("lake", "food") show you have applied the idea.

## Great diversity of species over long periods

Speciation can happen repeatedly. Each new species can itself be split into populations that later become further species. Over very long periods, this branching produces a large number of species descended from common ancestors, which links back to phylogenetic classification in 3.4.5.

## Common mistakes

- Saying organisms "adapt" or "mutate in response to" the environment: mutations are random; selection acts on existing variation.
- Stopping at survival without reproduction, inheritance and allele frequency.
- Confusing directional and disruptive selection.
- Defining evolution as "change in organisms over time" rather than a change in allele frequencies.
- Saying drift happens because alleles are advantageous.
- In speciation, writing "they become different species" without "cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring".
- Describing sympatric speciation without naming an isolating mechanism.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

| Command word | What earns the marks |
|---|---|
| Explain | The six-step selection chain, or the five-step speciation chain, in context |
| Describe (a graph) | Compare means and ranges before and after, with values |
| Suggest | Name a plausible selection pressure or isolating mechanism from the context |
| Compare | Allopatric against sympatric; selection against drift |
