## Questions

**1.** Define internal economies of scale. **[2]**

**2.** Identify each situation as an internal economy of scale, an external economy of scale or a diseconomy of scale.

**(i)** A component maker opens beside a group of furniture firms, cutting every firm's delivery costs.
**(ii)** Brindle Biscuits buys a larger oven and its cost per biscuit falls.
**(iii)** Brindle's head office grows so large that decisions take weeks and cost per biscuit rises. **[3]**

**3.** Brindle Biscuits has these monthly total costs.

| Output | 1,000 | 3,000 | 6,000 | 8,000 | 10,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost | 30,000 | 42,000 | 66,000 | 100,000 | 160,000 |

**(a)** Calculate average total cost at 3,000 and at 8,000 units. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the output with the lowest average total cost and state what the figures show beyond it. **[2]**

**4.** Explain one reason why the average total cost of a fast-growing business may begin to rise. **[3]**

**5.** Identify the production process used by each business.

**(a)** Corrigan Joinery builds one fitted wardrobe to each customer's measurements.
**(b)** Sellars Bakery bakes 300 rolls of one recipe, cleans down, then bakes 300 of another.
**(c)** Penhallow Water fills and caps bottles on a line that runs all day. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain one advantage to Penhallow of its process compared with Corrigan's. **[2]**

**6.** Tamsin Tailors has 36 workers. It made 5,400 garments in a week. After a training programme, the same workers made 6,300 garments. Each worker is paid 500 a week.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker before and after the programme. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the labour cost per garment before and after, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**

**7.** Explain one benefit and one risk of just-in-time (JIT) for a bakery chain. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse how Kaizen could help a packing department use its resources effectively. **[4]**

**9.** Pellam Foods has labour costs of 48,000 and total costs of 90,000. Quimby Fabrics has labour costs of 20,000 and total costs of 95,000.

**(a)** Calculate labour cost as a percentage of total cost for each firm, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify which firm is labour intensive and justify your choice. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one impact on Pellam if it switched to capital intensive production. **[2]**

**10.** Nolan Components has 12 workers making 600 units a day. Each worker is paid 70 a day. It is considering robots. With robots, 4 workers would make 900 units a day, and the robots would cost 260 a day to run.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker now and with robots. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate labour and robot cost per unit now and with robots. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether Nolan should introduce the robots. **[5]**

**11.** Dalvi Pottery has 8 employees and makes bowls one at a time to order, at a cost of 52 each. A hotel chain wants 400 identical bowls a month at 38 each. Batch production would cost 31 per bowl. Justify whether Dalvi should use batch production for this order. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Falling average total costs [1] as the business's output increases [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that only says "costs fall when a firm gets bigger" is less precise; naming average total cost and output is what turns a loose idea into a definition.

**2.** (i) External economy [1]. (ii) Internal economy [1]. (iii) Diseconomy of scale [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Ask who caused the change: a firm's own decision points to internal, an outside change points to external.

**3. (a)** 42,000 ÷ 3,000 = **14.00** [1]. 100,000 ÷ 8,000 = **12.50** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The lowest is at 6,000 units (66,000 ÷ 6,000 = 11.00) [1]. Beyond it average cost rises (12.50, then 16.00), which shows diseconomies of scale [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing total cost by output at every level is needed before you can name the lowest point; quoting total cost alone does not show the pattern.

**4.** Communication becomes slower as more layers of managers are added [1], so decisions are late and mistakes are repeated [1], which adds cost to each unit produced, so average total cost rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Any valid reason can score, but it must end with the effect on cost per unit; stopping at "it is hard to manage" leaves out the link.

**5. (a)** Job [1]. **(b)** Batch [1]. **(c)** Flow [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Flow gives a much lower cost per bottle at high output [1], so Penhallow can sell at a lower price than a business making each item to order [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two processes on the same point, here unit cost; a statement about flow alone does not answer a comparison.

**6. (a)** 5,400 ÷ 36 = **150** [1]; 6,300 ÷ 36 = **175** garments per worker [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (175 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 [1] = **16.7%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Wages 36 × 500 = 18,000. Before: 18,000 ÷ 5,400 = **3.33** [1]. After: 18,000 ÷ 6,300 = **2.86** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and divide by the original value for a percentage change; include the unit, such as garments per worker.

**7.** Benefit: stock is delivered only when needed [1], so less money is tied up in stock and less is wasted if it goes out of date [1]. Risk: a late delivery from a supplier [1] could stop baking and leave customers without products [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each of the two named points needs its own development; two bare statements earn less than one benefit and one risk explained.

**8.** Kaizen means continuous improvement through many small suggestions from staff [1]. Packers might suggest firmer boxes or a better layout [1]. This cuts damaged goods and wasted materials and time [1], so each unit costs less, which protects profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis is a chain of linked steps; give a specific change in the packing department rather than a general claim that Kaizen reduces waste.

**9. (a)** Pellam: 48,000 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = **53.3%** [1]. Quenby: 20,000 ÷ 95,000 × 100 = **21.1%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Pellam is labour intensive, because over half its costs are wages while Quenby's labour share is much smaller [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Machines could replace workers, so output per worker rises [1], although it would need a large spending on equipment up front [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Support a labour-intensive label with both firms' figures; the specification sets no cut-off, so a comparison is safer than a fixed rule.

**10. (a)** 600 ÷ 12 = **50** units [1]; 900 ÷ 4 = **225** units per worker [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Now: 12 × 70 = 840; 840 ÷ 600 = **1.40** [1]. With robots: 4 × 70 + 260 = 540 [1]; 540 ÷ 900 = **0.60** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Cost per unit falls from 1.40 to 0.60 and productivity rises from 50 to 225 [1]. The robots' purchase and installation cost is not included, so savings may take time to repay it [1]. Robots give consistent quality, though a fault could be repeated across many units [1]. They may be less flexible if customers want different products [1]. Judgement: if Nolan makes large volumes of similar units, introducing robots is probably right, since cost and productivity gains are large; if its products vary, the loss of flexibility matters more [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation scores on the decision and the reason given for it; weighing cost against flexibility for this firm matters more than listing every factor.

**11.** At 38 selling price and 31 batch cost, profit is 7 per bowl [1], which is 7 × 400 = 2,800 a month [1]. Producing the order by job production at 52 would lose 14 per bowl [1]. Against this, Dalvi would rely on one customer, and new equipment may be needed [1]. Dalvi could keep job production for individual customers and use batch only for the hotel order [1]. Judgement: batch production should be used for this order because it turns a loss into a profit, as long as the hotel order is regular [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data in the stem, then weigh a risk against it before deciding; a justification that never mentions the figures stays general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "average total cost" when defining economies of scale.
- Treating any cost increase as a diseconomy of scale.
- Mixing up internal and external economies.
- Not dividing total cost by output before comparing cost levels.
- Giving a production type without explaining its impact on cost, flexibility or quality.
- Using the new value instead of the old one for percentage change.
- Describing JIT or robotics with advantages only.
- Giving a verdict with no reason tied to the business in the stem.
