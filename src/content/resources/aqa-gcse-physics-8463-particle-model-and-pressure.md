## Gas molecules, temperature and pressure

The molecules of a gas are in **constant random motion**: they move in straight lines at a range of speeds, in
every direction, until they collide with each other or with the container walls.

**Temperature.** The temperature of a gas is related to the **average kinetic energy** of its molecules. A hotter
gas has molecules that are, on average, moving faster.

**Pressure.** When a molecule hits a wall it changes direction, so the wall exerts a force on the molecule and the
molecule exerts an equal force on the wall. Millions of collisions every second add up to a steady total force.
Pressure is that force on each unit of area of the wall.

## Temperature and pressure at constant volume

If a gas in a sealed, rigid container is heated, its volume cannot change. The chain of reasoning:

1. The temperature rises, so the molecules' average kinetic energy increases.
2. The molecules move faster on average.
3. They hit the walls **more often** (more collisions each second).
4. Each collision is **harder**, exerting a larger force.
5. The total force on each unit area increases, so the **pressure increases**.

Cooling the gas reverses every step, so the pressure falls. This explains why a sealed container that is heated
can burst, and why a ball pumped up indoors feels softer on a cold day outside.

## Pressure as a force at right angles

A gas can be compressed or expanded by pressure changes. The pressure of a gas produces a **net force at right
angles** to the wall of its container, or to any surface in contact with it. Because molecules arrive from all
directions, the sideways parts of their pushes cancel along the surface; what remains is a push perpendicular to
the surface. That is why a balloon swells evenly outward in all directions and why gas pressure can push a piston
straight along a cylinder.

## Volume and pressure at constant temperature

Now keep the temperature the same and let the volume increase, for example by pulling out the plunger of a sealed
syringe.

- The temperature is unchanged, so the molecules' average speed is unchanged, and each collision exerts the same
  average force.
- The molecules now have more space, so each unit area of wall is hit **less often**.
- Fewer collisions each second on each unit area means a smaller force per unit area: the **pressure decreases**.

Compressing the gas into a smaller volume does the opposite: collisions with each unit area become more frequent,
so the pressure rises.

## pV = constant

For a **fixed mass of gas** held at a **constant temperature**:

```
pressure × volume = constant        p V = constant
p₁ V₁ = p₂ V₂
p in pascals (Pa), V in m³
```

This equation is on the equation sheet. Pressure and volume are **inversely proportional**: halve the volume and
the pressure doubles; treble the volume and the pressure falls to a third. Because the equation compares two
states, any pressure unit and any volume unit may be used, provided each is the same on both sides.

**Worked example 1 (compression).** A syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The end is sealed and the plunger is
pushed in slowly until the volume is 15 cm³. Find the new pressure, assuming constant temperature.

```
p₁ V₁ = p₂ V₂
100 × 60 = p₂ × 15
p₂ = 6000 ÷ 15 = 400 kPa
```

The volume is a quarter of the original, so the pressure is four times as large.

**Worked example 2 (expansion, SI units).** A gas cylinder of volume 0.012 m³ contains gas at 1.5 × 10⁶ Pa.
The gas is released slowly into a large bag at constant temperature, where its pressure becomes 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa.
Find the volume of the gas in the bag.

```
V₂ = p₁ V₁ ÷ p₂ = (1.5 × 10⁶ × 0.012) ÷ (1.0 × 10⁵)
V₂ = 18 000 ÷ 100 000
V₂ = 0.18 m³
```

**Worked example 3 (checking data).** A student records the readings below for a trapped gas.

| p (kPa) | 100 | 125 | 200 | 250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V (cm³) | 50 | 40 | 25 | 20 |
| p × V | 5000 | 5000 | 5000 | 5000 |

The product is the same each time, so the results agree with pV = constant. If one product were clearly
different, the most likely causes are a misread volume or a temperature change while that reading was taken.

**Why "slowly" matters.** Compressing a gas quickly warms it (see the next section). Questions say "slowly" or "at
constant temperature" to tell you pV = constant applies.

## Increasing the pressure of a gas (Higher tier only)

**Work** is the transfer of energy by a force. When you push in the piston of a bicycle pump with the outlet
closed or restricted, you apply a force through a distance on the gas: you **do work on the gas**.

- The work done transfers energy to the gas, so the **internal energy of the gas increases**.
- The molecules bounce off the moving piston faster than they arrived, so their average kinetic energy rises.
- A higher average kinetic energy of the molecules means a **higher temperature**.

That is why the barrel of a bicycle pump becomes warm after several quick strokes. The same idea explains why air
in a tyre warms as it is pumped up.

## Common mistakes

- Saying molecules "move more" without saying they hit the walls more often and harder.
- Explaining the volume–pressure change with "the molecules move slower" when the temperature is constant. The
  speed does not change; only the frequency of collisions with each unit area does.
- Using pV = constant when the temperature changes.
- Mixing units: kPa on one side and Pa on the other, or cm³ with m³.
- Saying the molecules expand when the gas is heated.
- In the Higher-tier explanation, writing "friction heats the pump" instead of work done on the gas.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a pressure change: state what happens to the speed (or not), then to the frequency of collisions,
  then to the force, then to the pressure.
- **Calculate** with pV = constant: write p₁V₁ = p₂V₂, check units match, and say whether the answer should be
  larger or smaller before you calculate.
- **Explain** bicycle-pump heating (Higher tier): work done on the gas → internal energy increases → temperature
  rises.
