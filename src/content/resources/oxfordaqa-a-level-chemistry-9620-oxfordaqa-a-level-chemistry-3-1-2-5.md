It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1, drawn on by Unit 2, and central to the practical and synoptic Unit 5. ## What the specification asks for

- Equations (full and ionic).
- Percentage atom economy = (molecular mass of desired product ÷ sum of molecular masses of all reactants) × 100.
- Economic, ethical and environmental advantages for society and for industry of developing chemical processes with a high atom economy.
- Write balanced equations for reactions studied, and balance equations for unfamiliar reactions when reactants and products are specified.
- Use balanced equations to calculate masses, volumes of gases, percentage yields, percentage atom economies, and concentrations and volumes for reactions in solutions.
- Required practical 1: make up a volumetric solution and carry out a simple acid–base titration.

## Balancing equations

Balance by changing the numbers in front of formulae, never the subscripts inside them. A reliable order for combustion and similar reactions: balance the element that appears in fewest formulae first, then hydrogen, and oxygen last. Fractions are allowed while you work, then multiply through.

**Example.** Butane burning: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Carbon: 4CO₂. Hydrogen: 5H₂O. Oxygen on the right = 8 + 5 = 13 atoms, so 6½O₂. Multiply by 2: **2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O**.

**Unfamiliar example.** Ammonia is oxidised to nitrogen monoxide and water: NH₃ + O₂ → NO + H₂O. Nitrogen balances with equal numbers of NH₃ and NO. Hydrogen: 2NH₃ gives 3H₂O. Oxygen on the right = 2 + 3 = 5, so 2½O₂. Multiply by 2: **4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O**.

State symbols, (s), (l), (g) and (aq), are expected whenever a question asks for them and are good practice in ionic equations.

## Ionic equations

An ionic equation shows only the species that change; **spectator ions**, present unchanged on both sides, are left out.

1. Write the balanced full equation with state symbols.
2. Split aqueous ionic compounds (strong acids, soluble salts, soluble hydroxides) into ions.
3. Cancel ions that appear identically on both sides.
4. Check that both atoms and charges balance.

**Example.** Silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators, leaving **Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)**.

Common ionic equations to know: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) for any strong acid with any soluble hydroxide; CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) for carbonates in acid.

## Calculations from equations

Every calculation follows the same three moves: **find moles of what you know; use the mole ratio; convert moles into what you want**.

### Worked example 1: limiting reagent and gas volume

**Question.** 5.00 g of magnesium is added to 100 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Calculate the volume of hydrogen produced at 298 K and 100 kPa. (Ar Mg 24.3; R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)

**Method.**

1. n(Mg) = 5.00 ÷ 24.3 = 0.206 mol; n(HCl) = 1.00 × 0.100 = 0.100 mol.
2. 0.100 mol of HCl needs only 0.0500 mol of Mg, so magnesium is in excess and **HCl is the limiting reagent**.
3. n(H₂) = 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500 mol.
4. V = nRT ÷ p = (0.0500 × 8.31 × 298) ÷ 100 000 = 1.24 × 10⁻³ m³ = **1240 cm³**.

Always base the product amount on the limiting reagent. Using the magnesium would predict four times too much gas.

### Worked example 2: percentage yield

Percentage yield = (actual mass of product ÷ theoretical mass of product) × 100.

**Question.** Heating 10.0 g of CaCO₃ (Mr 100.1) gives 5.04 g of CaO (Mr 56.1). CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Calculate the percentage yield.

**Method.** Theoretical n(CaO) = n(CaCO₃) = 0.0999 mol; theoretical mass = 0.0999 × 56.1 = 5.60 g. Yield = 5.04 ÷ 5.60 × 100 = **89.9%**.

Yields are less than 100% because of incomplete reaction, side reactions, and product lost during separation and purification.

### Worked example 3: atom economy

**Question.** Ethanol can be made by fermentation, C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, or by hydration of ethene, C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH. Compare the percentage atom economies. (Mr: C₆H₁₂O₆ 180.0, C₂H₅OH 46.0)

**Method.** Fermentation: (2 × 46.0) ÷ 180.0 × 100 = **51.1%**. Hydration: the only product is ethanol, so the atom economy is **100%**.

Notice the "2" in front of ethanol: the desired product's mass must use the equation's coefficient. Atom economy depends only on the equation; percentage yield depends on what actually happens in the experiment. A reaction can have a high yield and a low atom economy at the same time.

### Why high atom economy matters

| Type of advantage | What it means |
|---|---|
| Economic | Less of the raw materials ends up in unwanted products, so less is bought per tonne of product and less waste must be treated or disposed of |
| Environmental | Less waste and fewer by-products to dispose of; finite raw materials are used more efficiently |
| Ethical | Resources are conserved for future generations, and less waste reduces harm to people living near plants and landfill |

If a by-product can be sold or used, the effective waste is lower than the atom economy suggests; this is a useful evaluative point in extended answers.

## Reactions in solution and required practical 1

In required practical 1 you make up a volumetric (standard) solution and use it in an acid–base titration.

**Making the solution.** Weigh the solid accurately, dissolve it in distilled water in a beaker, transfer the solution and all washings to a volumetric flask, make up to the mark so the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line, then stopper and invert to mix.

**Titrating.** Pipette a fixed volume into a conical flask, add a few drops of indicator, and add the other solution from a burette, swirling, until the indicator just changes colour. Repeat until you have **concordant titres** (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and average only those.

### Worked example 4: concentration from a titration

**Question.** 25.0 cm³ of sodium carbonate solution needs a mean titre of 23.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Find the concentration of the sodium carbonate.

**Method.** n(HCl) = 0.100 × 23.40 ÷ 1000 = 2.34 × 10⁻³ mol; n(Na₂CO₃) = 2.34 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 1.17 × 10⁻³ mol; c = 1.17 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0468 mol dm⁻³**.

## Common mistakes

- Changing subscripts to balance an equation.
- Leaving spectator ions in an ionic equation, or forgetting to balance charge.
- Ignoring the mole ratio, especially 1 : 2 in carbonate and sulfuric acid reactions.
- Working out the product from the reagent in excess.
- Using the coefficient of the product in percentage yield but forgetting it in atom economy, or the reverse.
- Including the rough titre in the mean.
- Leaving volumes in cm³ in n = c × V.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Write** or **Balance**: check every element and, for ionic equations, the total charge on each side.
- **Calculate**: label each line (n of A, ratio, n of B, answer) so method marks are clear even if an arithmetic slip occurs.
- **Explain** why yield is below 100%: give specific causes, not "human error".
- **Evaluate** or **Discuss** a process: use atom economy and yield together, and mention whether by-products are useful.
- **Describe** a practical: give the apparatus and the reason for key steps, such as rinsing washings into the volumetric flask.
