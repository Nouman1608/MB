This set practises **3.3.4.1 Structure, bonding and reactivity** from section 3.3.4 Alkenes of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and in the synoptic Unit 5. Revise first with the [alkene structure and reactivity study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-4-1/) or the [alkene structure and reactivity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-4-1-revision-notes/). Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0 and Br = 79.9.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which species can act as an electrophile in a reaction with but-1-ene?

A: CN⁻

B: NH₃

C: H⁺

D: OH⁻ **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by the term *unsaturated hydrocarbon*. **[1]**

**3.** Which formula could belong to a chain alkene with one C=C bond?

A: C₇H₁₆

B: C₇H₁₄

C: C₇H₁₂

D: C₇H₈ **[1]**

**4.** State the shape around each carbon atom of the C=C bond in ethene and give the H–C–H bond angle. **[2]**

**5.** Define the term *electrophile*. **[1]**

**6.** Give the structural formulae of **two** compounds with the formula C₄H₈ that are **not** alkenes. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Ethane and ethene both contain two carbon atoms.

**(a)** Explain why ethene reacts readily with electrophiles but ethane does not. **[3]**

**(b)** Name the type of reaction ethene undergoes with electrophiles. **[1]**

**8.** Bromine, Br₂, is a non-polar molecule but it reacts with cyclohexene at room temperature. Explain how Br₂ is able to act as an electrophile. **[3]**

**9.** A student says: "Alkenes are reactive because the C=C double bond is weaker than a C–C single bond." Use these mean bond enthalpies to evaluate this statement: C=C 612 kJ mol⁻¹; C–C 348 kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**10.** Hydrocarbon Y has the formula C₈H₁₂. 0.540 g of Y reacts with exactly 1.60 g of bromine, Br₂.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of Y and of Br₂. **[2]**

**(b)** Deduce the number of C=C bonds and the number of rings in Y. Explain your reasoning. **[3]**

**11.** Calculate the mass of bromine, Br₂, that reacts exactly with 1.26 g of hex-1-ene, C₆H₁₂. **[2]**

**12.** Hydroxide ions react with bromoethane but not with ethene.

**(a)** Explain why hydroxide ions react with bromoethane. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why hydroxide ions do not react with ethene. **[2]**

**13.** Pent-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃, exists as two stereoisomers. Explain, in terms of its bonding, why these isomers exist and do not interconvert at room temperature. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]

**2.** A compound of carbon and hydrogen only that contains at least one carbon–carbon double (multiple) bond [1]

**3.** **B** [1]

**4.** Trigonal planar [1]; about 120° [1]

**5.** An electron pair acceptor [1]

**6.** Any two of: cyclobutane (a four-carbon ring of CH₂ groups) [1]; methylcyclopropane (a three-carbon ring with a CH₃ group) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Rings are drawn or written clearly, for example as a square of four CH₂ groups; an isomer of butene does not score.

**7. (a)** Ethene has a C=C bond containing four electrons, a centre of high electron density [1]; electrophiles are electron pair acceptors and are attracted to this region [1]; ethane has only single C–C and C–H bonds with no region of high electron density (and almost no polarity), so electrophiles are not attracted [1]

**(b)** Electrophilic addition [1]

**8.** As Br₂ approaches the C=C, the high electron density of the double bond repels the bonding electrons in Br–Br [1]; this induces a dipole, so the nearer bromine atom becomes δ+ [1]; the δ+ bromine accepts an electron pair from the C=C bond (acts as an electrophile) [1] **[3]**

**9.** The statement is wrong: 612 kJ mol⁻¹ is larger than 348 kJ mol⁻¹, so the double bond is stronger [1]; but 612 is less than twice 348 (696), so the second bond of C=C is weaker than a C–C single bond and needs only about 264 kJ mol⁻¹ to break [1]; alkenes are reactive mainly because the C=C is a centre of high electron density that attracts electrophiles [1] **[3]**

**10. (a)** Mr(C₈H₁₂) = 108.0, so n(Y) = 0.540 ÷ 108.0 = 0.00500 mol [1]; n(Br₂) = 1.60 ÷ 159.8 = 0.0100 mol [1]

**(b)** Br₂ : Y = 2 : 1, and each Br₂ adds across one C=C, so Y has two C=C bonds [1]; C₈H₁₈ − C₈H₁₂ = 6 H missing, so three rings or double bonds in total [1]; therefore Y has one ring [1]

**11.** n(C₆H₁₂) = 1.26 ÷ 84.0 = 0.0150 mol [1]; mass of Br₂ = 0.0150 × 159.8 = **2.40 g** [1]

**12. (a)** Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–Br carbon is δ+ [1]; OH⁻ is a nucleophile and donates a lone pair to the electron-deficient carbon [1]

**(b)** OH⁻ is a nucleophile (an electron pair donor) and is negatively charged [1]; it is repelled by the high electron density of the C=C bond, so it cannot attack [1]

**13.** Rotation about the C=C double bond is restricted [1]; each carbon of C=C carries two different groups (H and CH₃; H and C₂H₅), so the groups can be on the same side (Z) or opposite sides (E), and converting one into the other would need the double bond to break [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- "Has a double bond" without *carbon–carbon*.
- Calling alkenes reactive because the double bond is weak.
- Mixing up electron pair acceptor (electrophile) and donor (nucleophile).
- Saying Br₂ is polar; the dipole is induced.
- Forgetting rings when counting hydrogen deficiency.
