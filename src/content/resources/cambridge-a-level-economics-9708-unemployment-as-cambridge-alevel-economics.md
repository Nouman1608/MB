## The meaning of unemployment

A person is **unemployed** if they are of working age, without a job, available for work and actively seeking it. Three groups are easily confused:

- **Employed:** in paid work, including part-time work.
- **Unemployed:** no job, available and looking.
- **Economically inactive:** neither working nor looking, such as full-time students, carers and people who have retired early.

The **labour force** (working population) is the employed plus the unemployed. The **unemployment rate** uses the labour force, not the whole population, as its base.

```
Unemployment rate = number unemployed / labour force x 100
```

### Worked example 1: employment and unemployment both rise

An invented country has a labour force of 40 million, of whom 2.4 million are unemployed.

```
Rate = 2.4 / 40 x 100 = 6.0%
```

Next year 0.5 million school leavers join the labour force. 0.3 million of them find jobs and nothing else changes.

```
Unemployed   = 2.4 + 0.2 = 2.6 million
Labour force = 40 + 0.5 = 40.5 million
Rate         = 2.6 / 40.5 x 100 = 6.4%
```

Employment rose by 0.3 million, yet the unemployment rate rose too, because the labour force grew faster than jobs. A rising unemployment rate does not always mean jobs are being lost; check what happened to the labour force.

## Measuring unemployment

Two measures are widely used.

| | Labour force survey | Claimant count |
|---|---|---|
| Method | A sample of households is asked about work and job search, using the International Labour Organization (ILO) definition | The number of people receiving unemployment-related benefits is counted |
| Who is counted | People without work who are available to start and have recently looked for work | Only those eligible for and claiming benefit |
| Strengths | Internationally comparable; includes jobless people who cannot or do not claim | Cheap; available quickly; no sampling error |
| Weaknesses | Costly; subject to sampling error; relies on honest answers | Changes whenever benefit rules change; misses people not entitled to benefit |

The claimant count is often the lower figure, because many jobless people (young people living with parents, people whose partner works, those who have used up entitlement) are not eligible to claim. A government that tightens benefit rules can reduce the claimant count without a single extra person finding work.

### Difficulties in measurement

- **Discouraged workers** stop looking and are counted as inactive, so unemployment is understated.
- **Underemployment:** people in part-time work who want full-time hours count as employed.
- **The informal sector:** in many economies a large share of people work in unregistered jobs, such as street trading or casual farm labour. Official figures may show such workers as unemployed or miss them entirely, so measured unemployment can be a poor guide to living standards.
- **Hidden unemployment**, such as people on training schemes or long-term sickness benefits who would work if jobs were available.
- **Averages hide differences** between regions, age groups and skill levels.

## Causes and types

The syllabus names five types. In data-response questions, the skill is to **diagnose** the type from the evidence.

| Type | Cause | Evidence that points to it |
|---|---|---|
| **Frictional** | Time taken to move between jobs, because of imperfect information about vacancies | Short spells; vacancies and job-seekers both present in the same area and occupation |
| **Structural** | Long-term decline of an industry, leaving a mismatch of skills or location | Unemployment concentrated in one region or industry; long spells; vacancies exist elsewhere needing different skills |
| **Cyclical** (demand-deficient) | A fall in aggregate demand in a downturn | Rising across most industries and regions together; falling real GDP; few vacancies anywhere |
| **Seasonal** | Demand for labour varies with the time of year | A regular pattern repeating each year (tourism, farming, construction in harsh winters) |
| **Technological** | New technology replaces workers | Job losses linked to automation or software in a particular occupation |

Technological unemployment often becomes **structural**: if displaced workers lack the skills for the new jobs created, the mismatch lasts. Distinguish them by cause (technology) versus the lasting result (mismatch).

### Worked example 2: diagnosing from an extract

*Original extract:* "In the northern province, the closure of three textile mills has left 12,000 people without work. Most have been unemployed for over a year. Meanwhile, software firms in the capital report 9,000 unfilled vacancies."

The evidence shows long spells, concentration in one region and one declining industry, and vacancies elsewhere needing different skills. This is **structural** unemployment. Higher aggregate demand would do little for these workers; retraining and help with moving are more relevant. If the extract had said "unemployment rose in every province as national output fell", the diagnosis would be **cyclical**.

## Consequences of unemployment

| Who | Consequences |
|---|---|
| The unemployed | Lower income and living standards; loss of skills the longer the spell; stress and poorer health; harder to find work as employers judge long gaps |
| Firms | Lower sales as household incomes fall; but a larger pool of applicants and less pressure to raise wages |
| Government | More spending on benefits, less income tax and indirect tax revenue, so a wider budget deficit; opportunity cost of that spending |
| The economy | Output below potential (a point inside the production possibility curve); wasted resources; greater inequality; possible rise in crime and social problems |

### Evaluating the consequences

- **Type and duration matter most.** Short frictional spells can improve the match between workers and jobs. Long structural spells do lasting damage to skills and confidence.
- **Benefit systems** soften the fall in income but raise government spending.
- **Who is affected:** youth unemployment can damage a whole career; unemployment concentrated in one region can lead to decline in local services.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between structural unemployment and cyclical unemployment. **[4]**

> **Structural unemployment** arises from a **long-term change in the structure of the economy**, such as the decline of an industry, which leaves workers whose skills or location do not match the available jobs *(1: cause)*. It can last a long time even when the economy as a whole is growing *(1: feature)*. **Cyclical unemployment** is caused by a **fall in aggregate demand** during a downturn, so firms cut output and need fewer workers *(1: cause)*. It affects many industries at once and falls when demand recovers *(1: feature, contrasted)*.

## Common mistakes

- Using the total population as the base for the unemployment rate.
- Counting students or retired people as unemployed.
- Treating a fall in the claimant count as proof that jobs were created.
- Confusing technological with seasonal unemployment, or naming a type without giving its cause.
- Listing consequences without saying who bears them.
- Calling all unemployment harmful without considering type and duration.

## Exam technique

- **Define** unemployment with "without a job", "available for work" and "actively seeking work".
- **Calculate** rates with the labour force as the base, and give the answer to one decimal place unless told otherwise.
- **Explain** a type of unemployment with its cause and one feature that identifies it.
- In data responses, quote the evidence that supports your diagnosis.
- For **Assess** or **Discuss** questions on consequences, judge by type, duration and who is affected.
