This study guide teaches Criterion B: Inquiring and designing in IB MYP Sciences. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Sciences guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion B is about planning a fair, safe, workable investigation before you touch any equipment. The guide says the MYP emphasises experimental work and scientific inquiry, so this criterion matters in almost every unit you do.

Use it with the [Criterion B revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-b-inquiring-and-designing-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion B practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-b-inquiring-and-designing-practice/). See also [investigation skills exam preparation](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-investigation-skills-exam-preparation/) and the [IB MYP Sciences course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/).

## What the guide says about Criterion B

The objective is "Inquiring and designing". Intellectual and practical skills are developed through designing, analysing and performing scientific investigations. The guide stresses that teachers must give an open-ended problem to investigate. An open-ended problem has several independent variables that are suitable for investigation, and enough scope for you to identify both independent and controlled variables. To reach the highest level for the method strand, your method should be logical, complete and safe, and should include only relevant information, correctly sequenced.

At the end of Year 5 the four strands are:

1. **Explain** a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
2. **Formulate** a testable hypothesis and **explain** it using scientific reasoning.
3. **Explain** how to manipulate the variables, and explain how data will be collected.
4. **Design** scientific investigations.

Criterion B has a maximum of 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Strand | 1-2 | 3-4 | 5-6 | 7-8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| i Problem or question | state | outline | describe | explain |
| ii Hypothesis | outline a testable hypothesis | formulate a hypothesis using scientific reasoning | formulate and explain a hypothesis using scientific reasoning | formulate and explain using correct scientific reasoning |
| iii Variables and data | outline the variables | outline how to manipulate variables and how relevant data will be collected | describe how to manipulate variables and how sufficient, relevant data will be collected | explain how to manipulate variables and how sufficient, relevant data will be collected |
| iv Method | design a method, with limited success | design a safe method, selecting materials and equipment | design a complete and safe method with appropriate materials and equipment | design a logical, complete and safe method with appropriate materials and equipment |

Two words matter most. **Sufficient** means enough data to answer the question, and **relevant** means data that bears on the question. At the top band you also have to say why your approach works, which is what "explain" asks for.

## Key words from the guide's glossary

- **Hypothesis**: a tentative explanation for an observation or phenomenon that needs experimental confirmation. It can take the form of a question or a statement.
- **Independent variable**: the variable that you choose and change.
- **Dependent variable**: the variable whose values are measured in the experiment.
- **Controlled variable**: a factor you keep the same so that the test is fair. The guide's open-ended problem wording expects you to identify both independent and controlled variables.
- **Data**: measurement of a parameter, either quantitative (volume, temperature, pH) or qualitative (colour, shape, texture).
- **Prediction**: an expected result of an upcoming action or event. Earlier years use predictions. By Year 5 you write a hypothesis that includes a reason.

## Strand i: explaining the problem or question

A good question names the two variables and links them. It can be tested, and the answer will be data.

- Weak: "Does temperature matter?" (matters for what?)
- Better: "How does the temperature of water affect the time an effervescent tablet takes to dissolve?"

To **explain** the question, add two or three sentences: why this question is worth testing, and the science background that makes it a sensible one. For example, dissolving is a familiar change that depends on how particles of water meet particles of the tablet, so changing the temperature should change how often they collide.

## Strand ii: a testable hypothesis with scientific reasoning

A testable hypothesis states a relationship between the independent and dependent variable. It has two parts: **what you expect** and **why**.

**Template.** "If [independent variable changes in a stated way], then [dependent variable changes in a stated way], because [scientific reason]."

**Worked example.** If the water temperature is raised from 10 °C to 50 °C, then the time taken for the tablet to dissolve will decrease, because the water particles have more kinetic energy at higher temperatures. They collide with the surface of the tablet more often and with more energy, so the reaction and dissolving happen faster.

What makes it good: it is testable (you can measure time), it names the direction of change and it is explained through particles. "Hot water is better" is not testable and has no reason.

## Strand iii: variables and data collection

State all three kinds of variable and **how** you will handle each.

| Variable | In the example | How it is managed |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | water temperature | five values: 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, set with ice, a kettle and a thermometer |
| Dependent | time for the tablet to dissolve completely | measured with a stopwatch in seconds, from drop to the last visible solid |
| Controlled | volume of water, type and size of tablet, beaker size, stirring, observer | 100 cm³ measured in a measuring cylinder, tablets from one pack, the same beaker, no stirring, the same person times every run |

Data collection needs numbers and a plan.

- **Range and interval**: five temperatures at equal steps give enough points to see a pattern.
- **Repeats**: three trials at each temperature allow you to calculate a mean and spot an anomaly.
- **Table**: draw the results table before the experiment with headings and units.
- **Units and precision**: record the temperature to the nearest 1 °C and the time to the nearest 1 s.

"Sufficient" data means a range of values wide enough to show the trend and repeated measures that let you trust it. "Relevant" means you do not record things you will not use, such as the colour of the beaker.

## Strand iv: designing the method

A top-band method is **logical** (steps in order), **complete** (someone else could repeat it) and **safe**. It also **selects appropriate materials and equipment**, with sizes and units.

**Equipment list for the example.** Effervescent tablets (same brand), five 250 cm³ beakers, a 100 cm³ measuring cylinder, a thermometer (range at least 0-100 °C), a kettle, ice, a stopwatch, a tray, paper towels and eye protection.

**Method.**

1. Put on eye protection. Check that the work area is clear.
2. Prepare water at 10 °C using cold tap water and ice. Prepare water at 50 °C by mixing hot and cold water until the thermometer reads the target.
3. Measure 100 cm³ of the water in the measuring cylinder and pour it into a clean beaker. Check the temperature again and adjust it if it differs by more than 1 °C.
4. Drop one tablet into the water and start the stopwatch at once.
5. Stop the stopwatch when no solid remains. Record the time in seconds in the table.
6. Repeat steps 3 to 5 two more times at the same temperature, using a clean beaker each time.
7. Repeat steps 2 to 6 for 20, 30 and 40 °C.
8. Clear up. Wipe spills at once, because the floor may be slippery.

**Safety.** Water at 50 °C or lower is unlikely to scald, but pour carefully. Fizzing can splash, so wear eye protection. Do not eat or drink anything in the laboratory. Wash hands afterwards.

**Why the order matters.** Reaching a temperature before adding the tablet means the tablet always starts in water at the right temperature. If you timed from pouring and not from dropping the tablet, you would add random delays.

## Open-ended tasks

When a task is open-ended, you choose the independent variable. A practical way to start is to write a list of factors that could change the outcome (here: temperature, amount of water, tablet size, stirring). Pick **one** to change and keep the others fixed. Explain in a sentence why you picked it and why the others are controlled. Choosing a variable you can measure with the equipment in your school is part of the design.

## How Criterion B is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for sciences, the Investigation skills task carries 50 marks, shared between Criterion B (25) and Criterion C (25). The guide says you should expect to formulate hypotheses, plan investigations, collect data from simulations, present data appropriately and interpret and evaluate data and hypotheses. The task may be a single investigation or several short scenarios. Response tools include a drawing canvas, a table drawing tool and a graph plotter.

## Common errors

- A question that does not name the variables.
- A hypothesis with no reason, or a reason that is not scientific ("because it is hotter").
- Changing more than one variable at once.
- No units or no measuring equipment in the method.
- A method that says "measure the temperature" without saying how, how often or with what.
- Only one trial and no range of values.
- Leaving out a safety step, or listing generic safety rules that have nothing to do with the investigation.
- A method in the wrong order or with missing steps.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion B in IB MYP Sciences?**
Criterion B is Inquiring and designing. It assesses how well you explain a testable question, formulate and explain a hypothesis, explain how to manipulate variables and collect data, and design a safe, complete method.

**What is the difference between a prediction and a hypothesis?**
A prediction is an expected result. A hypothesis is a tentative explanation that can be tested, and in Year 5 you formulate it and explain it with scientific reasoning.

**What does "sufficient, relevant data" mean?**
Sufficient means enough measurements and repeats to answer the question. Relevant means the data bears on the question you are testing.

**Do I need to carry out the investigation for Criterion B?**
Criterion B is about the design. Your teacher will say whether you also carry out the method, which then leads to Criterion C.
