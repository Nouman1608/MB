This guide covers subtopic **29.3, Shapes of aromatic organic molecules; σ and π bonds**, from Topic 29, An
introduction to A Level organic chemistry, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027
series. It is A Level content. The overview in
[A Level Organic Chemistry: Naming, Mechanisms and Aromatic Shape](/resources/a-organic-chemistry-naming-mechanisms-and-aromatic-shape/)

to count σ and π bonds and assign bond angles atom by atom, and gives a model answer for the standard
"describe and explain" question. Afterwards, use the
[aromatic shapes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-shapes-of-aromatic-organic-molecules-revision-notes/)

## What the syllabus asks for

One learning outcome: **describe and explain the shape of benzene and other aromatic molecules, including
sp² hybridisation, in terms of σ bonds and a delocalised π system.**

It builds on AS Level bonding: σ bonds from head-on overlap, π bonds from sideways overlap of p orbitals, and
sp, sp² and sp³ hybridisation.

## Building benzene from its orbitals

**Step 1: hybridise each carbon.** Each carbon atom mixes its 2s orbital with two of its 2p orbitals to make
**three sp² hybrid orbitals**. These lie in **one plane**, pointing to the corners of an equilateral triangle,
**120°** apart. The third 2p orbital is left **unhybridised**, at right angles to that plane.

**Step 2: form the σ framework.** Each carbon uses its three sp² orbitals to form three **σ bonds** by head-on
overlap: two to neighbouring carbons and one to a hydrogen atom. Six carbons and six hydrogens give
**12 σ bonds**: 6 C–C and 6 C–H. Because every carbon's σ bonds are in the same plane at 120°, the molecule is a
**flat, regular hexagon**, and all twelve atoms lie in one plane.

**Step 3: form the π system.** Each carbon has one electron in its unhybridised p orbital. Each p orbital
overlaps **sideways** with the p orbitals on **both** neighbouring carbons, not just one. The overlap continues
all the way round the ring, so the six p electrons are **delocalised** in a ring-shaped region of electron
density **above and below** the plane of the molecule.

**Why the ring must be planar.** Sideways overlap needs the p orbitals to be **parallel**. That is only possible
if all six carbons lie in one plane. The planar σ framework and the delocalised π system support each other.

## Consequences of delocalisation

- **Equal bond lengths.** All six carbon–carbon bonds are the same length, 0.139 nm, between C–C (0.154 nm) and
  C=C (0.134 nm). There are no separate single and double bonds.
- **Extra stability.** Hydrogenation of cyclohexene releases about 120 kJ mol⁻¹; a structure with three
  localised C=C bonds would be expected to release about 360 kJ mol⁻¹. Benzene releases only about
  208 kJ mol⁻¹, so it is about 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than the localised model predicts.
- **Substitution rather than addition**, because addition would destroy the delocalised system.

## Worked example 1: a model answer

*Describe and explain the shape of a benzene molecule, in terms of hybridisation and bonding.*

> Each carbon atom is **sp² hybridised** **[1]**. Each carbon forms three σ bonds, two to carbon atoms and one
> to hydrogen, using its sp² orbitals, which are arranged trigonally in a plane, so the bond angles are **120°**
> and the molecule is a **planar hexagon** **[1]**. Each carbon has one remaining p orbital, perpendicular to the
> plane of the ring **[1]**. These p orbitals overlap sideways with those on both adjacent carbons, so the six
> p electrons form a **delocalised π system** above and below the plane of the ring **[1]**.

Four points, four marks: hybridisation, σ framework and angle, p orbitals, delocalised π system.

## Other aromatic molecules

The rule to apply: **every ring carbon is sp², with 120° angles, and the ring is planar.** Then decide each
substituent atom separately.

**Methylbenzene, C₆H₅CH₃.** The ring is as in benzene. The CH₃ carbon forms **four σ bonds**, so it is **sp³**
and tetrahedral, with H–C–H angles of about 109.5°. The carbon of the CH₃ group lies in the plane of the ring
(it is bonded by a σ bond along a ring sp² direction), but its three hydrogens do not all lie in that plane.

**Phenol, C₆H₅OH; chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl; phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂.** The O, Cl or N atom has a **lone pair in a
p orbital** that can overlap with the ring's π system. The lone pair becomes partly delocalised into the
ring. This explains:

- the **activated ring** of phenol and phenylamine (more electron density for electrophiles);
- the **partial double-bond character** of the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene (shorter, stronger, less reactive);
- the **stabilised phenoxide ion** when phenol loses H⁺.

**Benzoic acid, C₆H₅COOH, and phenylethanone, C₆H₅COCH₃.** The carbonyl carbon forms three σ bonds and one
π bond (C=O), so it is **sp²**, trigonal planar, with 120° angles. In phenylethanone the CH₃ carbon is sp³.

**Naphthalene, C₁₀H₈.** Two benzene rings share one edge. All ten carbons are sp² and the molecule is planar,
with ten p electrons delocalised over both rings. Naphthols, the phenols of naphthalene, are the "other
phenolic compounds" named in 32.2.

## Counting σ and π bonds

**Rule for σ bonds:** every bond between two atoms contains exactly **one σ bond**. A double bond adds one π
bond; a triple bond adds two. A quick check: for a molecule with one ring, number of σ bonds = number of atoms
− 1 + 1 = number of atoms.

**π electrons in a benzene ring** are counted as **one delocalised π system of six electrons**, not as three
π bonds. If a question asks for "the number of π bonds" in a molecule containing benzene, state the
localised ones and describe the ring separately, unless the question tells you how to treat it.

## Worked example 2: phenylethanone

*For phenylethanone, C₆H₅COCH₃: (a) count the σ bonds; (b) describe the π bonding; (c) give the hybridisation
of each type of carbon and the bond angles around it.*

**(a)** Atom count: C₈H₈O = 17 atoms. One ring, so σ bonds = 17. Check by listing: ring C–C 6, ring C–H 5,
ring C–C(O) 1, C=O 1, C–CH₃ 1, C–H in CH₃ 3. Total **17**.

**(b)** One **localised π bond** in C=O, from sideways overlap of a p orbital on C and a p orbital on O, and the
**delocalised π system** of six electrons in the ring.

**(c)**

| Carbon | σ bonds | π bonding | Hybridisation | Angles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ring carbons | 3 | delocalised | sp² | 120° |
| C=O carbon | 3 | one π bond | sp² | 120° |
| CH₃ carbon | 4 | none | sp³ | 109.5° |

The ring, the carbonyl carbon and its oxygen can all lie in one plane; the CH₃ hydrogens point out of it.

## Common mistakes

- **Drawing or describing three fixed double bonds** in an explanation of shape. Use the delocalised model.
- **Saying the p orbital overlaps with "the next carbon"**. It overlaps with **both** neighbours; that is what
  makes the system delocalised.
- **Putting the π electrons "in the plane of the ring"**. They are above and below it.
- **Calling every carbon in a substituted arene sp²**. Side-chain carbons with four single bonds are sp³.
- **Forgetting that each bond has one σ component**, including the σ part of C=O.
- **Mixing bond angles**: 120° for sp², 109.5° for sp³.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** the shape: planar, hexagonal, bond angles 120°, all C–C bonds equal in length.
- **Explain** the shape: sp², three σ bonds per carbon in a plane, unhybridised p orbitals, sideways overlap,
  delocalised π system above and below the ring.
- **State** or **deduce** the number of σ bonds: show the count as a list or use the atoms-and-rings check.
- **Sketch** the π system: draw the ring flat with two continuous ring-shaped regions, one above and one below.
