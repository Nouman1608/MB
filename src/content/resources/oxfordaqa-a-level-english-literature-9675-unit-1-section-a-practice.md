## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** State what is printed on the paper for the Section A question and explain how the rest of the
play must be used in the answer. **[2]**

**2.** The specification says "the absence of an 'aspect' can be as significant as its presence".
Explain what this means and give one example of an aspect of tragedy that could be significant by
its absence. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a soliloquy and an aside, and give one tragic effect of each.
**[4]**

**4.** Explain two ways in which a shared verse line can create tension in a tragic scene. **[2]**

**5.** The specification distinguishes tragedy that is "classical and about public figures" from
tragedy that is "domestic and about representations of ordinary people". Explain the difference and
why a single play might contain elements of both. **[3]**

**6.** Give two features of the morality-play tradition and explain how one of them could shape the
reading of a passage from a tragedy. **[3]**

## Section B: original extracts and planning

**Extract 1 (original).** Earlier in this invented play, Marcello has told the audience in a
soliloquy that he means to ruin Count Lorenzo, the Duke's closest friend.

> **MARCELLO:** You trust him, then?
> **DUKE:** As I trust my own right hand.
> **MARCELLO:** Then keep your hand about you, sir. No more.
> I spoke too freely. Honest men are fools
> To meddle in another's happiness.
> **DUKE:** What do you mean? Speak plainly.
> **MARCELLO:** Nothing, my lord.
> A cough, a glance across the supper table,
> A letter folded twice. These things are nothing.
> *(He turns to go.)*
> **DUKE:** Stay. Tell me what you saw.

**7.** Analyse two methods Marcello uses to gain power over the Duke in Extract 1. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how the dramatist creates dramatic irony in Extract 1 and its effect on the audience.
**[4]**

**Extract 2 (original).** Isabella, a noblewoman imprisoned by her brother, speaks at the point of
death.

> **ISABELLA:** Open the window. Let me see the sea.
> I was a girl there once, and thought the waves
> Came only to the shore to play with me.
> They told me I was born to be obeyed;
> I find that I was born to be undone,
> And it is quieter than I had feared.
> Tell my brother — no. Tell him nothing. Let him guess.
> *(She dies.)*

**9.** Explain how the language of Extract 2 presents insight at the point of death. **[6]**

**10.** Suggest two staging choices for Extract 2 and explain how each could heighten the audience's
pity. **[4]**

**11.** A student begins an answer on Extract 2: "This extract is about death and is very sad. It
uses lots of imagery." Explain two weaknesses of this opening and write an improved first sentence.
**[4]**

**12.** A student writes: "In Jacobean times people believed in the Great Chain of Being. Isabella
dies." Explain why this use of context earns little credit and rewrite it so that the context
explains part of Extract 2. **[3]**

**13.** You are planning an answer on a passage from your own set play in which the villain or
opponent dominates the scene. List five elements a strong plan should contain, in a sensible order.
**[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A passage from the candidate's set play is printed [1]. The answer must connect the passage to
the play as a whole, using knowledge and short quotations recalled from memory, because Unit 1 is
closed book [1].

**2.** A tragic convention that a play leaves out or withholds can create meaning [1], because the
audience expects it and notices the gap [1]. Example, any one [1]: a hero who never reaches insight
or recognition; a villain with no clear motive; no restoration of order at the end; a death without
a final speech.

**3.** A soliloquy is spoken by a character alone on stage [1]; it reveals private thought, so the
audience may understand the hero's suffering or a villain's plan better than anyone on stage [1].
An aside is a brief remark the other characters present do not hear [1]; it creates dramatic irony
and can make the audience uneasy accomplices in the deception [1].

**4.** Any two, [1] each: one speaker completes another's line, so the exchange feels fast and
pressured; one character overrides or interrupts another, showing a shift in power; a pause left in
a short line creates suspense; quick exchanges suggest one character controlling the other's
responses.

**5.** Classical or public tragedy concerns figures of high rank whose fall affects a state or
kingdom [1]; domestic tragedy concerns private lives, family and household [1]. A single play can
combine both because a ruler's private relationships (marriage, children, friendship) can become
the cause of public disorder, so the household and the state fall together [1].

**6.** Any two features, [1] each: personified virtues and vices; the Good and Evil Angels or a
contest for the soul; a choice between salvation and damnation; an emblematic show such as the
Seven Deadly Sins; a moral lesson for the audience. Explanation [1]: for example, a passage in which
a character is pulled between two voices can be read as a battle for the soul, so the hero's choice
seems freely made, which increases his responsibility for his fall.

**7.** For each of two methods: method identified [1], supported with reference to the extract [1],
effect on the contest of power explained [1].

- **Retraction**: "No more. / I spoke too freely" withdraws the hint at once [1]; refusing to explain
  makes the Duke demand more [1], so the victim appears to drive the conversation that ruins him [1].
- **False modesty about honesty**: "Honest men are fools / To meddle" [1] presents Marcello as an
  honest man reluctant to interfere [1], which wins trust and disguises the attack [1].
- **Trivial, vague evidence**: "A cough, a glance ... A letter folded twice" [1] lists small details
  and calls them "nothing" [1], leaving the Duke's imagination to supply the guilt [1].
- **Exit as a lure**: the stage direction "He turns to go" [1] forces the Duke to call him back with
  "Stay" [1], so the Duke begs for the lie [1].

**8.** The audience already knows from the soliloquy that Marcello intends to ruin Lorenzo [1], so
his claim to be an honest man is heard as a lie [1]. The Duke's confident image "As I trust my own
right hand" becomes ironic, since his trust is about to be turned against his friend [1]. The effect
is dread and pity: the audience watches the hero being misled without being able to warn him [1].

**9.** Up to six points, [1] each, for analysis linked to insight:

- The command "Let me see the sea" turns her final wish to sight, so seeing becomes a metaphor for
  understanding.
- The memory of girlhood sets past innocence against present suffering, showing a fall from
  prosperity and happiness.
- The child's belief that the waves came "to play with me" exposes an earlier self-centred view of
  the world that she has now outgrown.
- The antithesis "born to be obeyed" / "born to be undone" is the moment of recognition: the status
  she was raised to expect has become the cause of her destruction.
- "They told me" places the false belief in other voices, suggesting a society that shaped her
  pride.
- "Quieter than I had feared" presents death with a calm that dignifies her and moves the audience.
- The broken last line, "Tell my brother — no", shows a final choice: refusing him the satisfaction
  of a message is a last act of will.

**10.** For each of two choices: choice described [1], effect on pity explained [1]. For example: the
window opened to bring light into a dark prison [1], contrasting her lost freedom with the open sea
[1]; the actor speaking from the floor or a low chair while the jailer stands [1], making her
physical weakness and her captor's power visible [1]; a long silence after "Let him guess" before
the stage direction "She dies" [1], letting the audience absorb the refusal [1].

**11.** Weakness one [1]: it summarises the content ("about death", "very sad") rather than stating an
argument. Weakness two [1]: "lots of imagery" is vague and names no specific method or effect, and
no aspect of tragedy is identified. Improved sentence [2] for an argued, specific opening, [1] for a
partial improvement. Example: "Calm rather than anguished, the speech presents Isabella's death as a
moment of insight in which the pride she was raised to feel is recognised as the cause of her fall."

**12.** The context is not linked to the passage, so it explains nothing about how the extract works
[1]. Improved version [2] for context used to explain a line, for example: "Isabella's recognition
that she was 'born to be obeyed' reflects a hierarchy in which rank was thought to be fixed by
birth; her discovery that rank cannot protect her exposes that order as fragile."

**13.** Any five, [1] each, credited in a logical order:

- a one-sentence argument answering the question;
- where the passage sits in the play's structure and what the audience knows at that point;
- the tragic aspect at stake (the villain's contest of power and responsibility for the fall);
- two or three methods in the passage to analyse, each with a short quotation;
- one earlier and one later moment from memory that the passage connects to;
- one relevant context that changes the reading;
- an alternative interpretation to weigh (for example, how far the hero is responsible, not only
  the villain);
- a conclusion on the audience's response.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the extract as a springboard and writing about something else.
- Spotting techniques without saying what they do for the tragedy.
- Leaving out the audience and the stage.
- Offering context as a separate paragraph rather than inside the analysis.
