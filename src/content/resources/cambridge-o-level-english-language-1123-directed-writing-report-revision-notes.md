These notes cover the **report**, one of the five forms the syllabus names for Paper 2 Section A: candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article". The general method is in the [directed writing revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/); the full explanation and a model report are in the [writing a report study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-report/).

## Task facts

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Marks | 25: writing 15 (W1–W5), reading 10 (R3, R5) |
| Length | about 250–350 words |
| Form objectives | W2 organisation (headings, sections); W4 register (impersonal, objective) |
| Natural purpose | discursive: both sides, then a recommendation |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Terms of reference | a statement of what the report was asked to cover |
| Purpose statement | the opening line saying why the report was written |
| Findings | the evidence, grouped by theme |
| Attribution | saying who made a claim: "according to...", "teachers reported..." |
| Evaluation | judging how reliable, complete or biased the evidence is |
| Conclusion | the overall judgement drawn from the findings |
| Recommendation | a specific proposed action that follows from the findings |
| Impersonal register | objective style: third person, cautious verbs, some passives |

## Report skeleton (about 300 words)

| Part | Words | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Title | — | subject and reader |
| Purpose | 20–30 | why, what it covers |
| Findings 1 | 70–80 | one theme: points from both sources, developed and evaluated |
| Findings 2 | 70–80 | second theme |
| Findings 3 (optional) | 40–60 | practical issues or costs |
| Conclusion and recommendations | 60–80 | judgement + two or three specific actions |

## Grouping by theme, not by source

| By source (weak) | By theme (strong) |
|---|---|
| "Text 1 says... Text 2 says..." | "Benefits", "Concerns", "Costs", "Practical issues" |
| repeats the order of the texts | combines points from both texts under each heading |
| little evaluation | comparison and judgement built in |

## Impersonal toolkit

- Third person: "students", "the school", "teachers".
- Passives: "it was found that", "concerns were raised".
- Cautious verbs: "suggests", "indicates", "appears", "may".
- Attribution: "according to", "the supplier claims", "staff reported".
- Precise nouns and numbers from the sources.
- "It is recommended that..." or "The school should...".

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Tablets are awesome." | "The evidence suggests tablets would offer several benefits." |
| "The company says they're cheaper." | "The supplier claims lower costs over five years, though no independent figures are given." |
| "We should probably think about it more." | "It is recommended that a one-term trial be run with one year group." |
| a heading for every sentence | two or three headings with developed sections |

## Conclusion versus recommendation

| Conclusion | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| what the evidence shows overall | what should be done about it |
| "On balance, the benefits are real but unproven." | "Run a one-term trial and measure results." |
| a judgement | an action, with who, what and when |

A report needs both: the conclusion shows the reader your judgement, and the recommendation turns it into a decision they can take.

## Recommendations checklist

- Specific: who, what, when.
- Follow directly from a finding.
- Realistic for the reader to act on.
- Two or three, not a long list.
- Address the main concern raised in the findings.

## Mini example (original)

**Finding:** "Several parents said the new parking rule had made the school entrance safer, but the
caretaker reported that cars now queue on the main road. The parents' view is based on impressions,
while the caretaker's observation identifies a new risk."

**Recommendation that follows:** "It is recommended that the school ask the council to mark a short
drop-off zone on the side road."

## Exam traps

- Treating the report as a speech: rhetorical questions and exclamations have no place in it.

- No headings or recommendation.
- Retelling sources in order.
- Emotional or persuasive language.
- Vague recommendations ("more research").
- Recommendations unrelated to the findings.
- Forgetting the named reader.
- One sentence per heading.

## Self-test

1. Who is a report written for?
2. Why is a report naturally discursive?
3. Name four standard parts of a report.
4. Why group findings by theme rather than by source?
5. What is attribution, and how does it help evaluation?
6. Give two features of impersonal register.
7. Rewrite objectively: "Everyone hates the new timetable."
8. What makes a recommendation strong?
9. Is "I" ever acceptable in a report?
10. How long should the report be?
11. Why is a heading like "Part 2" weak?
12. Which writing objective do headings mainly support?

**Answers**

1. A named decision-maker, such as a head teacher, committee or manager.
2. It gives the reader a fair account of the evidence on different sides before reaching a judgement.
3. Any four: title; purpose or introduction; findings with headings; evaluation; conclusion; recommendations.
4. It combines evidence from both sources, makes comparison easier and avoids retelling the texts.
5. Saying who made a claim; it shows whether a source might be biased or reliable without loaded language.
6. Any two: third person; passive constructions; cautious verbs; precise nouns; attribution.
7. For example: "Many students and staff expressed dissatisfaction with the new timetable."
8. It is specific, realistic and follows from a finding.
9. Yes, occasionally, if the writer gathered information ("I spoke to four teachers"), but judgements should rest on evidence.
10. About 250–350 words.
11. It tells the reader nothing about the content of the section.
12. W2, organising and structuring ideas for deliberate effect.
