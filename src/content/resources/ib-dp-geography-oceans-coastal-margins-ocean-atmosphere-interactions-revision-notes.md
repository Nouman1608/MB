Condensed recall for **2.1 Ocean-atmosphere interactions** (IB DP Geography, Option B: Oceans and coastal margins). Fuller explanation is in the [Oceans and coastal margins study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-study-guide/).

## Heat redistribution

- Oceans store and redistribute heat.
- **Surface currents** are driven by prevailing winds and deflected by the **Coriolis effect**, forming large circular **gyres**.
- Warm currents carry heat from low latitudes towards the poles; cold currents move the other way. This is why places at similar latitudes can have different climates.

### The pattern of gyres

- Gyres turn **clockwise** in the northern hemisphere and **anticlockwise** in the southern hemisphere, because the Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the north and to the left in the south.
- Currents on the **western** side of ocean basins carry warm water poleward (the Gulf Stream in the Atlantic, the Kuroshio in the Pacific).
- Currents on the **eastern** side carry cool water towards the equator (the Canary, California, Humboldt or Peru, and Benguela currents).

**Located example:** the Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carry warm water towards north-west Europe, so winters in Ireland and Norway are much milder than in Labrador in Canada at similar latitudes. Cold eastern-boundary currents cool and stabilise the air above them, which helps explain coastal deserts such as the Atacama and the Namib.

## Thermohaline circulation

Also called the global ocean conveyor. It is driven by **density** differences, which depend on **temperature and salinity**.

1. Cold, salty water sinks in polar regions.
2. It flows at depth.
3. Warmer surface water moves to replace it.

Why polar water becomes so dense: it is very cold, and when sea ice forms, salt is left behind in the surrounding water, raising its salinity. Major sinking areas lie in the North Atlantic near Greenland and around Antarctica. A complete circuit of the conveyor is thought to take on the order of a thousand years.

Its Atlantic branch, the **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)**, carries heat northward. Climate assessments consider it very likely to weaken this century as warming and fresh meltwater from Greenland make North Atlantic surface water less dense, which could alter European and tropical rainfall patterns.

**Upwelling** brings cold, nutrient-rich water to the surface and supports productive fisheries.

Upwelling is strongest along eastern ocean margins, where winds blowing along the coast push surface water offshore and deeper water rises to replace it. The anchoveta fishery off Peru depends on it.

## ENSO (El Nino-Southern Oscillation)

| | Normal | El Nino | La Nina |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade winds | Push warm water west | Weaken | Stronger |
| Warm water | Pooled in the west | Spreads east | Pushed further west |
| Upwelling off South America | Present | Reduced | Enhanced |
| Effects | | Rainfall patterns shift: drought in some regions, floods in others | Opposite phase |

Be able to describe the pattern, explain the cause (ocean and atmosphere coupled) and discuss consequences for people and ecosystems, such as fisheries and water supply.

### The atmosphere's part: Walker circulation

In normal conditions, warm water in the western Pacific heats the air, which rises over Indonesia and northern Australia, giving heavy rain. The air returns eastward aloft and sinks over the cooler eastern Pacific, producing high pressure and dry conditions off South America. This loop is the **Walker circulation**. The **Southern Oscillation** is the see-saw in air pressure between the western and eastern Pacific that accompanies the change in sea temperatures.

### Typical El Nino impacts

- **Peru and Ecuador:** heavy rain and flooding on the normally dry coast; warmer water reduces upwelling, so anchoveta catches fall, hitting fishing communities and the fishmeal industry.
- **Australia and Indonesia:** drought and heightened fire risk; the strong 1997-98 El Nino was associated with severe forest and peat fires in Indonesia.
- **Wider effects:** El Nino years tend to raise global average temperature temporarily.

During **La Nina**, the reverse tends to happen: wetter conditions and flood risk in eastern Australia and drier conditions on the Pacific coast of South America. Effects are tendencies, not certainties, and they vary between events.

## Oceans and climate change

- Oceans absorb carbon dioxide, causing **ocean acidification**.
- They absorb most of the extra heat from global warming, causing **thermal expansion**, which adds to sea level rise alongside melting land ice.
- Warm sea surface temperatures fuel tropical cyclones.

### The processes in more detail

- **Carbon sink:** the oceans have taken up roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide released by human activity, partly by dissolving it at the surface and partly through the **biological pump**, in which plankton take in carbon and some of it sinks to the deep ocean when they die.
- **Acidification:** dissolved carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, lowering pH and reducing the carbonate that corals, shellfish and some plankton need to build shells and skeletons. Surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1 since pre-industrial times, which is a large change on a logarithmic scale.
- **Coral bleaching:** when water stays unusually warm, corals expel the algae that live in their tissues and give them food and colour. Repeated bleaching, such as the mass events on the Great Barrier Reef in 2016 and 2017, leaves too little time for recovery.
- **Tropical cyclones** generally need sea surface temperatures of around 26 to 27 degrees Celsius or more; warmer oceans supply more water vapour and energy.

## Model paragraph

*Explain how an El Nino event affects people in coastal Peru.* "During El Nino the trade winds weaken and warm surface water spreads east across the Pacific. Off Peru, this warm layer suppresses the upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water, so plankton and the anchoveta that feed on them decline, cutting catches and the income of fishing communities. At the same time, warmer water heats and destabilises the air, bringing heavy rain and flooding to a normally arid coast, damaging homes, roads and farmland."

## Exam traps

- Confusing El Nino with La Nina, or giving effects without the cause.
- Saying thermohaline circulation is wind-driven: it is density-driven (surface currents are wind-driven).
- Forgetting that melting land ice, not only thermal expansion, raises sea level.
- Treating one El Nino impact as global: effects vary by region.
- Saying melting sea ice raises sea level: floating ice displaces its own mass of water, so it is melting land ice that matters.
- Describing acidification as the ocean becoming acidic: it is becoming less alkaline.
