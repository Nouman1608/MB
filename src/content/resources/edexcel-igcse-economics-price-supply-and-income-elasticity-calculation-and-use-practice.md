## Questions

**1.** What is meant by price elasticity of demand? **[2]**

**2.** A bookshop raises the price of a diary from 12 to 15. Weekly sales fall from 480 to 432. Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**

**3.** State the name for demand or supply with each of these values (ignore sign): **(a)** 0, **(b)** 0.7, **(c)** 1, **(d)** 2.4, **(e)** infinity. **[5]**

**4.** A firm cuts the price of a gadget from 30 to 24. Sales rise from 200 to 220.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in total revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** State what your answers show about the demand. **[1]**

**5.** Explain two factors that make the demand for table salt price inelastic. **[6]**

**6.** A potter raises the price of a bowl from 50 to 55. The number supplied each week rises from 300 to 315.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether supply is elastic or inelastic. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the potter's supply may be this elastic. **[2]**

**7.** Describe how you would draw a diagram to show that supply of one good is more price elastic than supply of another. **[4]**

**8.** In Country D the price of tea leaves and the price of bicycles both rise by 10%. Explain why the quantity of bicycles supplied may rise by more than the quantity of tea leaves in the first year. **[4]**

**9.** In Country E household income rises by 8%. Demand for good X rises by 12%, good Y rises by 2.4% and good Z falls by 4%.

**(a)** Work out the income elasticity of demand of each good. **[3]**
**(b)** Classify each good as luxury, normal or inferior. **[3]**

**10.** Country A plans a tax of 0.50 on a snack. The price is 4.00 and weekly sales are 20,000. The price rises by the full tax.

**(a)** Calculate the new weekly quantity and the tax revenue if PED is −0.8, and again if PED is −2.0. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain which PED gives the government more revenue. **[2]**

**11.** A café chain sells luxury coffee (income elasticity +1.8) and plain bread rolls (income elasticity −0.4). Incomes are forecast to fall by 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage change in sales of each product. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the chain should change its menu in response. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** It shows how strongly buyers respond [1] by changing the quantity they demand of a good when its price changes [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both parts of the definition, the idea of responsiveness and what it responds to. "How much people want a good" has neither.

**2.** Price change = (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = +25% [1]. Quantity change = (432 − 480) ÷ 480 × 100 = −10% [1]. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = **−0.4** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write each percentage on its own line before dividing. The command word Calculate asks for working to be shown, so a bare answer is risky.

**3.** (a) Perfectly price inelastic [1]. (b) Price inelastic [1]. (c) Unitary [1]. (d) Price elastic [1]. (e) Perfectly price elastic [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the full names. "Elastic" alone does not separate perfectly elastic from elastic.

**4. (a)** Price change = −6 ÷ 30 × 100 = −20% [1]. Quantity change = +20 ÷ 200 × 100 = +10% [1]. PED = 10 ÷ −20 = **−0.5** [1].
**(b)** Old revenue = 30 × 200 = 6,000 [1]. New revenue = 24 × 220 = 5,280, a fall of **720** [1].
**(c)** Revenue fell after a price cut, so demand is price inelastic [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) link the revenue result to the type of demand. The two answers should agree with each other.

**5.** Any two, each with a statement and two linked expansions:
Necessity [1]: salt is needed for cooking and food storage [1], so buyers keep buying when the price rises and quantity falls by a small percentage [1].
Small share of income [1]: a packet costs very little compared with income [1], so buyers barely react to a rise in price [1].
(Also accept few close substitutes, with a linked explanation.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a chain: factor, why it matters here, what happens to quantity. A list of factor names with no context is a weak answer.

**6. (a)** Price change = 5 ÷ 50 × 100 = +10% [1]. Quantity change = 15 ÷ 300 × 100 = +5% [1]. PES = 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5** [1].
**(b)** Inelastic, because PES is below 1 [1].
**(c)** A potter has limited spare capacity or a single kiln [1], so output cannot rise quickly even when the price is higher [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep PES positive. A negative PES in the working suggests demand and supply have been confused.

**7.** Name the two axes, price upwards and quantity across [1]. Draw two upward-sloping supply curves through the same starting point [1]. Label the flatter curve as the more elastic supply and the steeper as the more inelastic [1]. Mark the same price rise on both to show quantity supplied rising by more along the flatter curve [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say what each curve represents. A diagram with unlabelled curves is hard to credit.

**8.** Bicycles are manufactured goods [1]. A factory can use spare capacity, hold stocks or add shifts [1], so output can rise within the year [1]. Tea bushes take time to grow, so tea supply is inelastic in the short run [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two products explicitly. Describing only bicycles does not answer why one rises by more.

**9. (a)** X: 12 ÷ 8 = **+1.5** [1]. Y: 2.4 ÷ 8 = **+0.3** [1]. Z: −4 ÷ 8 = **−0.5** [1].
**(b)** X is a luxury good, since YED is above 1 [1]. Y is a normal good, since YED is between 0 and 1 [1]. Z is an inferior good, since YED is negative [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Support each label with the value. The sign is part of the answer for income elasticity.

**10. (a)** New price = 4.50, a rise of 12.5% [1]. PED −0.8: quantity falls 10% to 18,000 and revenue is 18,000 × 0.50 = **9,000** [1]. PED −2.0: quantity falls 25% to 15,000 [1] and revenue is **7,500** [1].
**(b)** PED −0.8, because demand is inelastic [1], so buyers cut their purchases proportionally less than the price rise, leaving more units to be taxed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compute the percentage fall in quantity before the new quantity. Revenue is tax per unit multiplied by units still sold.

**11. (a)** Coffee: 1.8 × −5 = **−9%** [1]. Rolls: −0.4 × −5 = **+2%** [1].
**(b)** Coffee is a luxury good, so sales fall by more than incomes [1]. Rolls are inferior, so sales rise [1]. The fall in coffee is larger than the rise in rolls [1], so total revenue is likely to fall unless rolls make up most of sales [1]. Estimates may be inaccurate or the fall in incomes may not happen [1]. Rolls may earn less profit per item than coffee, and a price cut on coffee could be tested [1]. Conclusion: keep coffee, add cheaper options, and track sales [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs a supported decision. Use the percentages and reach a conclusion rather than listing the elasticities.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the later figure, not the starting figure, as the base of a percentage change.
- Giving a PED value with no statement of whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
- Defining elasticity as "how much" without the idea of responsiveness.
- Writing a factor name with no link to quantity or to the product in the question.
- Describing a diagram without naming the axes and curves.
- Mixing up income elasticity labels, or dropping the minus sign for an inferior good.
- Treating a revenue rise after a price rise as a sign of elastic demand.
- Giving an evaluation with no conclusion.
