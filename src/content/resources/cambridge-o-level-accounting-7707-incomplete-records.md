This guide goes deeper into section **5.6 Incomplete records** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus covers "why businesses keep different types of accounting records rather than using the full double entry system", the advantages and disadvantages of not keeping full records, "opening and closing statements of affairs", profit "from changes in capital over time", calculating "sales, purchases, gross profit, trade receivables and trade payables and other figures from incomplete information", preparing the statements from incomplete records with the 5.1 adjustments, and using "mark-up, margin and inventory turnover to arrive at missing figures". Questions are set only on sole trader businesses, and not on service or manufacturing businesses.

The shared [incomplete records study guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-incomplete-records/) explains each technique. This page solves one trader's year in two ways and shows that both give the same profit, which is the best check you have in the exam. Use the [incomplete records revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-incomplete-records-revision-notes/) for recall and the [incomplete records practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-incomplete-records-practice/) to test yourself. All traders and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Why records are incomplete

Many small traders keep only a bank statement, a list of customers who owe money and a file of unpaid invoices. Full double entry takes time, skill and often the cost of a book-keeper, and a one-person business may feel it does not need it.

| Advantages of not keeping full records | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Less time spent on book-keeping | No trial balance, so errors are hard to find |
| Lower cost; no specialist needed | Profit can only be estimated or rebuilt later |
| Simple enough for the owner to manage | Theft or loss of inventory may go unnoticed |
| | Harder to control receivables and payables |
| | Lenders and tax authorities may not accept the figures |

## Method 1: profit from the change in capital

A **statement of affairs** lists a trader's assets and liabilities at a date. Capital is the difference. Comparing two dates shows how much capital grew, and profit explains most of that growth.

**Profit for the year = closing capital - opening capital + drawings - capital introduced.**

Drawings are added back because they reduced capital without being a loss. Capital introduced is deducted because it raised capital without being a profit.

**Worked example: Imran's hardware shop**, year ended 31 December 2028.

| | 1 January 2028 | 31 December 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Shop fittings | 12,000 | 10,800 |
| Inventory | 8,400 | 9,700 |
| Trade receivables | 3,600 | 4,250 |
| Bank | 2,150 | 3,900 |
| **Total assets** | **26,150** | **28,650** |
| Trade payables | 4,900 | 5,300 |
| Electricity owing | - | 350 |
| **Capital** | **21,250** | **23,000** |

During the year Imran withdrew $18,000 from the bank and took goods costing $600 for his family. He paid in $2,000 he inherited.

Profit = 23,000 - 21,250 + (18,000 + 600) - 2,000 = **$18,350**.

This method gives only one figure. It cannot show revenue, gross profit or which expenses rose, so a lender learns little from it.

## Method 2: rebuilding the statement of profit or loss

Imran's bank statements show the following for 2028.

| Receipts | $ | Payments | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| From credit customers | 64,950 | To suppliers | 70,100 |
| Cash sales banked | 40,200 | Rent | 6,000 |
| Capital introduced | 2,000 | Wages | 9,500 |
| | | Electricity | 1,800 |
| | | Drawings | 18,000 |

Check: 2,150 + 107,150 - 105,400 = $3,900, which agrees with the closing bank balance.

**Credit sales.** Receipts from customers include money for last year's sales and exclude this year's sales not yet paid for. Credit sales = 64,950 - 3,600 + 4,250 = $65,600. Total sales = 65,600 + 40,200 = **$105,800**.

**Purchases.** By the same logic, purchases = 70,100 - 4,900 + 5,300 = **$70,500**. The goods Imran took are deducted from purchases, because they were not for resale.

**Depreciation.** Fittings fell from $12,000 to $10,800 with no additions, so depreciation is $1,200.

```
Imran
Statement of profit or loss for the year ended 31 December 2028
                                                USD        USD
Revenue                                                105,800
Cost of sales
  Opening inventory                           8,400
  Purchases (70,500 - 600 drawings)          69,900
                                             78,300
  Less Closing inventory                      9,700
                                                        68,600

Less Expenses
  Rent                                        6,000
  Wages                                       9,500
  Electricity (1,800 + 350)                   2,150
```

Both methods give $18,350. If they did not agree, a figure has been missed: often goods taken by the owner, an accrual or capital introduced.

The statement of financial position is the closing statement of affairs, with the capital section set out in full: opening capital 21,250 + capital introduced 2,000 + profit 18,350 - drawings 18,600 = $23,000.

## Other missing figures: a cash summary

Some traders spend cash takings before banking them, so the bank statement does not show every sale or payment. A cash summary finds the gap. Joy's opening cash was $300 and closing cash $450. Cash takings were $52,000; she banked $41,500, paid wages of $6,200 in cash and paid $900 of cash expenses. The rest was taken for private use:

Drawings = 300 + 52,000 - 41,500 - 6,200 - 900 - 450 = **$3,250**.

The full $52,000 is cash sales in the statement of profit or loss, not the $41,500 banked.

## Mark-up, margin and inventory turnover

When records are lost, these relationships fill the gaps.

- **Mark-up** = gross profit / cost of sales x 100. **Margin** (gross profit margin) = gross profit / revenue x 100.
- A mark-up of 25% means gross profit is 25 for every 100 of cost, so revenue is 125 and the margin is 25/125 = 20%. A margin of 1/3 means a mark-up of 1/2, that is 50%.
- **Rate of inventory turnover** = cost of sales / average inventory.

**Worked example: missing inventory.** Ruth's revenue is $84,000 and she always adds a mark-up of 40%. Cost of sales = 84,000 x 100/140 = $60,000, so gross profit is $24,000 and the margin is 28.57%. Opening inventory was $7,500 and purchases were $61,800, so closing inventory should be 7,500 + 61,800 - 60,000 = $9,300. The count shows only $8,100, so **$1,200** of inventory is missing, possibly through theft.

**Worked example: inventory turnover.** A trader's cost of sales is $60,000 and inventory turns over 8 times a year. Average inventory = 60,000 / 8 = $7,500. If closing inventory is $8,200, opening inventory = (7,500 x 2) - 8,200 = **$6,800**.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing drawings and capital introduced in the profit formula.
- Taking cash received from customers as sales without adjusting for opening and closing receivables.
- Applying the mark-up percentage to revenue instead of cost of sales.
- Forgetting goods taken by the owner, which are drawings and reduce purchases.
- Missing an accrual in the closing statement of affairs.

## Exam technique

The syllabus says Paper 2 may ask you to "perform calculations" or "prepare accounts based on information given in the question". In incomplete records, label every working with its name, such as "Credit sales", because a later part often needs it. **Calculate** answers need the formula line before the arithmetic. **Explain** answers on why a trader should keep full records should name a specific problem in the case, such as a lender asking for a full statement of profit or loss. When both methods are possible, check one against the other.
