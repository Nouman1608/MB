## Questions

**1.** Define the term *adding value*. **[2]**

**2.** Ravensgill Textiles makes wool scarves. Each scarf sells for £19.50. Bought-in wool and dye cost £6.40 a scarf and packaging costs £0.60 a scarf. The business makes 1,800 scarves a month. The owner is considering recycled wool. Total bought-in cost would rise to £7.90 a scarf and the new selling price would be £22.00.

**(a)** Calculate the value added per scarf at the current price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total monthly value added at the current price. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in monthly value added if the new price and cost apply and sales stay at 1,800. **[3]**

**3.** State which factor of production is best shown by each of the following in a pottery business.

**(a)** A kiln. **[1]**
**(b)** The founder's decision to start the business and take the risk. **[1]**
**(c)** The clay dug from a local pit. **[1]**

**4.** The owner of Thessaly Pies has £15,000 to invest. A new oven would raise profit by £2,400 a year. An online ordering system would raise profit by £2,100 a year. Hiring a part-time worker would raise profit by £1,900 a year. She chooses the oven.

**(a)** Identify the opportunity cost of her choice. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why opportunity cost matters when a business makes decisions. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between business risk and uncertainty, using an example of each. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **two** barriers to entrepreneurship. **[6]**

**7.** Oriel Fenn is an employee of Sennen Paper, a large stationery producer. She designs a recycled-paper range in her own time, persuades managers to fund a trial and leads the team that launches it. Sennen Paper carries the cost of the launch.

**(a)** Explain why Oriel is an intrapreneur rather than an entrepreneur. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way in which intrapreneurship may help Sennen Paper's long-term success. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **two** differences between a local business and a multinational business. **[4]**

**9.** Amberley Loom has traded for three months. The owner's business plan forecast average monthly sales revenue of £9,000. Actual average monthly sales revenue is £5,100. Monthly fixed costs are £6,200 and monthly variable costs are £1,900. The owner started with £40,000 of loan funding. Equipment cost £22,000, and £18,000 of cash remains today to cover monthly losses from now on. No other income is available.

**(a)** Calculate the average monthly profit or loss. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many months the remaining £18,000 will last at this rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse **two** reasons, using the data, why Amberley Loom is likely to fail. **[4]**

**10.** Cormac Tull plans Dunmore Pots, a mail-order pottery business. His business plan forecasts 80 orders a month at an average price of £25, and he will use it to ask a bank for a £12,000 loan.

**(a)** Calculate forecast monthly sales revenue. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate monthly sales revenue if orders turn out to be 30% lower than forecast. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** benefit and **one** limitation of Cormac's business plan. **[4]**
**(d)** Justify whether the bank should place heavy reliance on the plan when deciding the loan. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether enterprise is the most important way a country's businesses contribute to its development. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Adding value is the process of turning inputs into outputs that sell for more than the cost of the bought-in materials and services [1], so that the difference between selling price and bought-in cost is the value added [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns more when it names the inputs and the selling price; "making something better" gives no clear measure.

**2. (a)** Bought-in cost = £6.40 + £0.60 = £7.00 [1]. Value added = £19.50 − £7.00 = **£12.50** per scarf [1].
**(b)** 1,800 × £12.50 = **£22,500** [1].
**(c)** New value added per scarf = £22.00 − £7.90 = £14.10 [1]. New monthly value added = 1,800 × £14.10 = £25,380 [1]. Change = £25,380 − £22,500 = **an increase of £2,880** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In a calculation, show the bought-in total before subtracting and state the unit; a clear route lets a slip in one line cost less than a bare answer would.

**3. (a)** **Capital**, a man-made resource used in production [1].
**(b)** **Enterprise**, the ability to seek out and develop opportunities and accept the risk [1].
**(c)** **Land**, a natural resource [1].
*Examiner insight:* The context decides the answer; a kiln is capital even if you think of it as "equipment" in everyday language, so use the board's four terms.

**4. (a)** The **£2,100 a year** extra profit from the online system, the next best alternative given up [1].
**(b)** Resources are limited, so using money one way means it cannot be used another way [1]. Looking at the opportunity cost helps a business compare options and judge whether the chosen one is worth more than the best alternative [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the oven, or adding up all three options, is a common slip; only the single next best alternative counts.

**5.** **Risk** is where the possible outcomes and their likelihood can be estimated [1], for example the chance a restaurant loses money, estimated from sales of similar restaurants [1]. **Uncertainty** is where outcomes cannot be reliably predicted, so no probability can be set [1], for example the effect of a new technology that makes a product obsolete [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each half needs its own example to gain the second mark; two definitions that sound alike show the difference has not been understood.

**6.** Any two barriers, three steps each. For example, **difficulty raising finance** [1]: a new entrepreneur has no trading record or security, so banks may refuse a loan or ask a high interest rate [1], which can stop a viable idea from starting [1]. **Lack of skills or experience** [1]: an entrepreneur who cannot manage cash or marketing may make costly errors in the early months [1], so many ideas fail or never begin [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a barrier gains little alone; the explanation of why it stops a person starting is where credit is usually earned.

**7. (a)** Oriel acts entrepreneurially, creating and leading a new product [1], but she does it inside an existing business [1], and Sennen Paper, not Oriel, carries the financial risk of the launch [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Intrapreneurship brings new products and ideas from within [1], which stops a large business becoming slow and fixed [1], so Sennen Paper can keep its range fresh and adapt as customer tastes change, supporting long-term success [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the facts in the stem, such as who carries the cost; a general definition of an intrapreneur without that link is less convincing.

**8.** Any two differences, two steps each. For example, **location of operations**: a local business operates in one area, whereas a multinational owns and runs operations in more than one country [1], so the multinational can use lower-cost sites and reach larger markets [1]. **Complexity and risk**: a local business has simple, familiar rules and one currency [1], whereas a multinational faces different laws, cultures and exchange-rate changes [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A difference needs both sides stated; describing only the multinational leaves the comparison unfinished.

**9. (a)** Monthly costs = £6,200 + £1,900 = £8,100 [1]. Result = £5,100 − £8,100 = **a loss of £3,000 a month** [1].
**(b)** £18,000 ÷ £3,000 = **6 months** [1], so the cash runs out about six months from now [1].
**(c)** Any two, each developed with data. **Sales far below plan**: actual sales revenue of £5,100 is £3,900 (43.3%) below the £9,000 forecast [1], so the forecast was unrealistic and the business cannot cover its costs [1]. **Costs above revenue / weak cash control**: fixed costs of £6,200 alone exceed sales revenue of £5,100 [1], so each month loses cash and only six months of funds remain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quoting the numbers and linking them to failure is what lifts analysis above a list of causes such as "poor planning".

**10. (a)** 80 × £25 = **£2,000** [1].
**(b)** Orders = 80 × 0.70 = 56 [1]. Revenue = 56 × £25 = **£1,400** (£600 lower) [1].
**(c)** Benefit: the plan makes Cormac test his idea, costs and market before spending, so weaknesses can be found early [1], and it gives the bank evidence of his thinking [1]. Limitation: the forecast depends on assumptions such as 80 orders, and if they prove wrong, as in part (b), revenue falls by £600 a month [1], so the plan may mislead the owner and the bank [1].
**(d)** The bank should not rely heavily on the plan alone [1], because the forecasts are only estimates, and a 30% fall in orders would cut revenue by 30% [1]. It should also look at his experience, security for the loan and evidence such as early orders, then judge [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A justify question needs a reasoned decision backed by a reason from the case; "it depends" with no conclusion leaves the judgement unfinished.

**11.** For: enterprise creates new businesses, jobs and incomes [1], adds output and tax revenue [1], and brings innovation and more competition and choice [1]. Against or limits: other contributions matter too, such as large established firms, which employ many people and bring investment and exports [1]; a high failure rate among new ventures wastes resources and losses can harm lenders and workers [1]; and some enterprise may damage the environment or local communities [1]. Judgement: enterprise is important because it starts the process of new jobs and ideas, but it is not the only or always the most important contribution [1], as its success depends on finance, skills and stable conditions, and existing businesses are also vital [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation is shown when you weigh the claim against rival contributions and reach a supported conclusion, not by repeating the benefits of enterprise.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing profit when a question asks for value added.
- Giving the chosen option as the opportunity cost.
- Leaving out units and working in calculation answers.
- Giving definitions of risk and uncertainty that sound the same.
- Describing barriers to entrepreneurship without saying why each stops people starting.
- Calling an employee who innovates an entrepreneur, ignoring who bears the financial risk.
- Using "international" and "multinational" as if they were the same.
- Analysing failure with generic causes and no figures from the case.
- Ending an evaluate or justify answer without a clear, reasoned judgement.
