These questions cover section 3.6.5 Making human resource decisions: improving employer-employee relations of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [employer-employee relations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations/) and the [employer-employee relations revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following best describes collective bargaining? **[1]**

A: A manager deciding pay for each employee separately

B: A union negotiating pay and conditions with an employer for its members

C: Employees buying shares in the company

D: A suggestion scheme for new ideas

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a main purpose of a works council? **[1]**

A: To organise strikes

B: To set the company's prices

C: To inform and consult employee representatives and managers about workplace matters

D: To recruit new staff

**3.** Define the term "works council". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit to a business of involving employees in decision making. **[3]**

**5.** Pell Pottery has a payroll of £1.5 million. The union asks for a 6% pay rise and the employer offers 4%.

**(a)** Calculate the annual cost of each pay rise. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual gap between the claim and the offer. **[1]**

**(c)** A five-day strike would lose sales of £120,000. The gross profit margin is 40%. Calculate the lost gross profit. **[1]**

**(d)** Wages of £9,000 would not be paid during the strike. Calculate the net cost of the strike. **[1]**

**(e)** Explain **one** reason why the net cost may understate the true cost of a strike. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** way in which two-way communication can improve employer-employee relations. **[2]**

**7.** State **two** barriers to effective communication in a business. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a supermarket chain of having a works council. **[6]**

**9.** A manufacturer has no trade union. Assess whether it should recognise a trade union. **[9]**

**10.** A business has announced a restructuring. Analyse how it could improve communication with employees to protect good relations. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** influences on the extent to which a business involves its employees in decisions. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of good employer-employee relations to a business facing a shortage of skilled workers. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Collective bargaining is negotiation by a union on behalf of its members. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A works council consults and shares information; it is not mainly a bargaining body. **[1]**

**3.** A group of elected employee representatives [1] who meet management regularly to be informed and consulted about workplace matters such as safety, working hours and changes [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Employees often know the practical problems and possible solutions, so decisions are better informed [1]. Staff who have been consulted feel valued and understand the reasons for changes [1], so they are more motivated and accept changes more readily, which reduces conflict and turnover [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 6% × £1,500,000 = **£90,000** [1]. 4% × £1,500,000 = **£60,000** [1].

**(b)** £90,000 − £60,000 = **£30,000** [1].

**(c)** 40% × £120,000 = **£48,000** [1].

**(d)** £48,000 − £9,000 = **£39,000** [1].

**(e)** It ignores lasting damage, such as customers who switch to rivals and do not return [1], and the fall in staff morale and trust, which may reduce productivity and raise turnover after the strike [1]. **[7]**

**6.** Employees can raise concerns and ideas upwards as well as receive information [1], so problems are spotted early and staff feel listened to, which builds trust [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Any two: many layers of management [1]; shift or remote working [1]; lack of trust [1]; information overload [1]; managers who do not listen [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: early warning of problems [1]. Representatives from stores bring staff concerns about rotas, safety or pay to management [1], so problems can be solved before they become disputes [1]. Benefit 2: smoother change [1]. Consulting representatives about new self-checkouts or opening hours helps staff understand the reasons [1], leading to less resistance and fewer disruptions [1]. **[6]**

**9.** For: a union provides one voice for many workers, which can make negotiations on pay more efficient [1]. It can help to settle disputes through agreed procedures and reduce the risk of unofficial action [1]. Recognition may improve trust and morale because staff feel protected [1]. A union can improve safety and conditions, reducing absence [1]. Against: unions may press for higher pay and resist changes, raising costs [1]. Industrial action could disrupt output if talks fail [1]. Decision making may be slower because of negotiation [1]. Judgement: recognition may be sensible if the workforce wants representation and the firm values stability [1], provided both sides agree clear procedures for bargaining and for dispute resolution so that cooperation is more likely than conflict [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Explain the reasons for the change early and honestly, through briefings and written updates, so that rumours do not take over [1], since uncertainty reduces trust [1]. Set up two-way channels, such as question sessions, surveys and representatives, so that staff concerns are heard [1] and visible action follows ("you said, we did") [1]. Train managers to listen and to deal with anxiety [1]. Consult representatives on how redundancies or changes of role will be handled, and offer support such as retraining [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Influence 1: management style [1]. An autocratic manager prefers to decide alone and involves staff little, while a democratic one consults widely [1]. Influence 2: skills of the workforce [1]. Skilled staff understand the work better and can offer valuable advice, so involving them is more worthwhile [1]. Accept: size of the business; urgency of the decision; legal requirements; union presence. **[4]**

**12.** For: when skilled workers are scarce, they can easily find jobs elsewhere, so good relations are a way to keep them [1]. Recruiting and training replacements is costly and slow [1]. Employees who feel listened to are more committed and productive [1]. A reputation as a good employer helps attract new recruits [1]. Involving skilled staff in decisions also uses their expertise to improve quality [1]. Good relations reduce disputes, which protect output when there are few staff [1]. Against: pay, working conditions and career prospects may matter more to skilled workers than the quality of communication [1]. Good relations take time to build and cost money [1]. Competitors may be able to offer higher pay whatever the relations are like [1]. Factors: how mobile the skilled staff are, the pay competitors offer and the culture of the business [1]. Judgement: good relations are very important in a skills shortage, because they cost less than the cost of replacing staff [1], but they work best when combined with competitive pay and development opportunities [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Distinguish unions from works councils precisely, and keep the focus on the workforce and management style in the case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating trade unions and works councils as the same.
- Assuming unions always cause conflict.
- Saying that more involvement is always better.
- Comparing a one-off strike cost with a permanent pay rise without comment.
- Describing communication as one-way only.
- Not applying general points to the business in the case.
