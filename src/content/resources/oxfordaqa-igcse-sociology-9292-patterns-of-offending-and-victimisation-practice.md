These questions cover "Patterns of offending and victimisation by: gender; social class; age;
ethnicity; explanations of these patterns" in 3.2.2.4, part of Section B of Paper 2.

---

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify the age group with the highest rate of recorded offending in most societies. **[1]**

**2.** What is meant by the **chivalry thesis**? **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** explanations of why working-class people are over-represented in official crime
statistics. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by **institutional racism**? **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — People found guilty of offences in one year in an invented country, by sex and age group
>
> *Illustrative data, invented for this practice question.*
>
> | Age group | Males | Females |
> |---|---|---|
> | 10–17 | 6,000 | 1,500 |
> | 18–24 | 14,000 | 2,800 |
> | 25–39 | 11,000 | 2,200 |
> | 40 and over | 4,000 | 1,000 |

**(a)** Using Item A, identify the age group with the most males found guilty, and state how many
times larger the male figure is than the female figure in that group. **[2]**

**(b)** Using Item A, calculate the total number of females found guilty, and the percentage of all
people found guilty who were female. Give the percentage to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why Item A may not accurately show differences in offending between
males and females. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Explain what Merton meant by **innovation** as a response to strain. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why recorded offending declines after people reach their twenties.
**[6]**

**8.** Explain how labelling may help to explain the over-representation of some groups in crime
statistics. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** patterns of victimisation, one by gender and one by age. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss how far gender socialisation explains why men commit more crime than women. Use
examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[12]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [1 mark]

Late teens and early twenties (accept 18–24 or 15–24) [1].

**A2** [2 marks]

The claim, made by Pollak, that a **male-dominated justice system treats women more leniently** [1],
so women's offending is under-recorded or punished less severely [1].

**A3** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: strain or blocked opportunities (Merton); status frustration and subcultures (Albert Cohen);
material deprivation; selective policing of working-class areas (Marxist); labelling by police
(Cicourel); under-recording of white-collar and corporate crime.

**A4** [2 marks]

Racism that is **built into the policies, practices or culture of an organisation** [1], so it produces
unequal treatment even when individuals do not intend it, as the Macpherson Report concluded of the
Metropolitan Police [1].

**A5 (a)** [2 marks]

The **18–24** age group [1]; the male figure is **5 times** the female figure (14,000 ÷ 2,800) [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks]

Total females = 1,500 + 2,800 + 2,200 + 1,000 = **7,500** [1]. Total males = 35,000, so the total
found guilty is 42,500 [1]. Percentage female = 7,500 ÷ 42,500 × 100 = **17.6%** [1].

**A5 (c)** [2 marks]

Any one, developed: the data shows only people **found guilty**, not all offending [1], so differences
in reporting, policing or prosecution, such as the chivalry thesis, may affect the figures [1]; or
crimes more often committed by one sex, such as domestic abuse, may be under-reported [1] so they are
missing from convictions [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

Merton argued that society sets **approved goals**, such as wealth and success, for everyone [1], but
not everyone has **legitimate means** to achieve them, such as education or good jobs [1]. This gap
creates strain [1]. **Innovation** is the response of accepting the goals but using **illegal means**
to reach them, such as theft or fraud [1].

**A7** [6 marks — up to 3 per reason]

Two from: **more commitments** [1] — older people are more likely to have jobs, partners, children
and homes [1], so they have more to lose from offending (Hirschi's social bonds) [1]; **less peer
influence** [1] — time spent with peer groups declines [1], reducing pressure to take risks for status
[1]; **less visibility** [1] — older people spend less time in public spaces [1], so they attract less
police attention [1]; **maturity** [1] — people develop greater self-control and different priorities
with age [1], making impulsive offending less likely [1].

**A8** [4 marks]

Interactionists argue that police and courts act on **stereotypes** of the "typical offender" [1].
Cicourel found that officers saw working-class youths as typical delinquents [1], so these young
people were more likely to be stopped, arrested and charged [1], which increases their numbers in the
statistics even if their offending is no higher [1]. Accept equivalent points on ethnicity and
stereotyping (Gilroy; Hall and colleagues).

**A9** [6 marks — up to 3 per pattern]

**Gender** [1]: women are far more likely to be victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence [1],
much of which happens in private and is under-reported [1]; or men are more likely to be victims of
violence in public and by strangers [1] [1]. **Age** [1]: young people are more likely to be victims
of violent crime [1], because they spend more time in public at night and among peers [1]; or older
people are targeted by fraud and scams [1] and may report high fear of crime [1].

**A10** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Gender socialisation as an explanation:** boys socialised into toughness and risk-taking;
  Connell's hegemonic masculinity; girls socialised into caring and conformity.
- **Other explanations:** greater social control of women (Heidensohn); different opportunities;
  chivalry thesis (Pollak) and under-recording of women's crime; double deviance.
- **Change:** Adler's liberation thesis; changing gender roles in many societies.
- **Methods:** official statistics may exaggerate the gap; victim surveys and self-report studies
  help to check it.
- **Application:** a named society, for example how expectations of boys and girls differ there.
- **Judgement:** for example, socialisation is a major explanation, but control, opportunity and the
  workings of the justice system also shape the pattern.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong totals when calculating a percentage from a table.
- Describing a pattern with no explanation.
- Confusing Albert Cohen with Stanley Cohen.
- Treating statistics as a complete picture of offending.
- An extended answer with no named society.
