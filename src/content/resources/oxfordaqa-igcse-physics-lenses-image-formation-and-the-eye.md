## What this unit covers

Points a to d cover refraction by lenses, convex and concave lenses and focal length. Points e to g cover describing images, ray diagrams and magnification. Points h to j cover the eye, range of vision and sight defects. Points k and l cover lasers and the camera.

## How a lens forms an image (3.3.6 a)

A lens is a piece of transparent material with curved surfaces. Rays strike the curved surfaces at different angles, so they are bent by different amounts. This refraction lets a lens send rays from one point of an object towards one point of an image.

## Convex lenses (3.3.6 b, d)

A convex lens is thicker at the middle than at the edges. It is also called a converging lens.

- Rays parallel to the principal axis meet at one point on the far side, the **principal focus**.
- The **focal length** is the gap between the middle of the lens and the principal focus.
- A lens has a principal focus on each side, equally far from it.

### Three rays you can always draw

1. From the tip of the object, a ray running parallel to the axis is refracted and goes through the principal focus on the far side.
2. A ray from the top of the object aimed at the middle of the lens passes through without changing direction.
3. A ray through the principal focus on the object side leaves the lens parallel to the axis.

Any two are enough. Where they cross (or appear to cross) is the top of the image. If the foot of the object is on the axis, so is the foot of the image.

## Describing an image (3.3.6 e)

Describe every image using three pairs of words.

| Feature | Choices |
|---|---|
| Size compared with the object | larger (magnified), same size, smaller (diminished) |
| Orientation compared with the object | upright, inverted |
| Type | real, virtual |

A **real** image forms where rays actually cross and can be caught on a screen. A **virtual** image is where rays only appear to have come from. Your eye can see it, but a screen cannot show it.

## Images from a convex lens at different distances (3.3.6 b)

Let f be the focal length. The object distance is measured from the lens.

| Object position | Image | Size | Orientation | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Further than 2f | between f and 2f on the far side | smaller | inverted | real |
| At 2f | at 2f on the far side | same size | inverted | real |
| Between f and 2f | beyond 2f on the far side | larger | inverted | real |
| At f | no image: emerging rays are parallel | not applicable | not applicable | not applicable |
| Closer than f | on the object side, behind the object | larger | upright | virtual |

As the object moves towards the lens, the real image moves away from it and grows. Inside the principal focus the rays leaving the lens still spread out, so they never meet. Your eye traces them back to a larger, upright, virtual image.

### The magnifying glass

A magnifying glass is a convex lens used with the object **closer to the lens than the principal focus**. The image is virtual, upright and larger, and it is on the same side as the object. You cannot project its image onto a wall.

## Concave lenses (3.3.6 c)

A concave lens is thinner at the middle than at the edges. It is also called a diverging lens.

- Parallel rays spread out after the lens. They appear to have come from a point on the object side of the lens, the principal focus.
- The focal length is the distance from the lens to that point.
- For any object distance, the image is **virtual, upright and smaller**, on the same side of the lens as the object, between the object and the lens.

Draw ray 1 (parallel to the axis) spreading away from the axis, with a dotted line back to the principal focus on the object side. Ray 2 passes straight through the centre. The refracted rays never cross, so extend them backwards as dotted lines. Their intersection is the image.

## Ray diagrams on graph paper (3.3.6 f)

You may be asked to draw or read a diagram on graph paper. Use the same scale on both axes unless told otherwise. Draw the lens as a vertical line, the axis as a horizontal line and mark each principal focus on the axis. A ray bends at the lens line itself. Read the image distance and height from the grid.

### Worked example 1: a real image

A convex lens has a focal length of 4 cm. An object 3 cm tall stands 12 cm from the lens. Find the position and nature of the image. Take the lens at x = 0, the axis along y = 0, and the object tip at (−12, 3).

| Step | Working |
|---|---|
| Ray 1 hits the lens at height 3, then passes through the principal focus at (4, 0) | Line from (0, 3) to (4, 0): y = 3 − 0.75x |
| Ray 2 goes through the centre from (−12, 3) | Line through (0, 0): y = −0.25x |
| They cross where 3 − 0.75x = −0.25x | 3 = 0.5x, so x = 6, y = −1.5 |

The image is 6 cm from the lens on the far side and 1.5 cm below the axis. It is **real, inverted and smaller** (1.5 cm against 3 cm).

### Worked example 2: a magnified real image

With the same lens, an object 1.5 cm tall is 6 cm away. Ray 1 runs from (0, 1.5) through (4, 0) and ray 2 from (−6, 1.5) through the centre. They cross at x = 12, y = −3: the image is 12 cm from the lens, 3.0 cm tall, **real, inverted and larger**.

### Worked example 3: a magnifying glass

A convex lens has focal length 5 cm. An object 2 cm tall is 3 cm from the lens, inside the principal focus. Put the tip at (−3, 2).

- Ray 1 leaves the lens along the line from (0, 2) through (5, 0), which has equation y = 2 − 0.4x. The rays spread, so extend it backwards to the left of the lens.
- Ray 2 goes through the centre: y = −(2/3)x.
- They cross when 2 − 0.4x = −(2/3)x. This gives x = −7.5, y = 5.

The image is 7.5 cm from the lens, on the same side as the object, and 5 cm tall. It is **virtual, upright and larger**.

### Worked example 4: a concave lens

A concave lens has a focal length of 6 cm. An object 3 cm tall is 12 cm away, tip at (−12, 3). Ray 1 spreads as if from (−6, 0), so its line is y = 3 + 0.5x. Ray 2 is y = −0.25x. They meet at x = −4, y = 1: the image is 4 cm from the lens on the object side, 1 cm tall, **virtual, upright and smaller**.

## Magnification (3.3.6 g)

magnification = image height ÷ object height

Magnification has no unit. Both heights must be in the same unit before you divide. Above 1 means a larger image, below 1 a smaller one.

For the examples above: example 1 gives 1.5 ÷ 3 = 0.5, example 2 gives 3.0 ÷ 1.5 = 2.0, example 3 gives 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 and example 4 gives 1 ÷ 3 = 0.33.

Rearranged, image height = magnification × object height, and object height = image height ÷ magnification.

### Worked example 5

A slide shows a cell 0.040 mm across. A lens system produces an image 6.0 mm across. magnification = 6.0 ÷ 0.040 = **150**.

## The eye (3.3.6 h)

The eye detects only visible light, a limited range of the electromagnetic waves. Light is refracted by the **cornea** (mostly) and then by the **lens**.

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Cornea | Transparent front surface. It refracts light entering the eye |
| Pupil | The opening that lets light in |
| Iris | Ring of muscle that changes the size of the pupil, so controls how much light enters |
| Lens | Variable focus lens. It fine-tunes the focusing so the image is sharp on the retina |
| Ciliary muscle | Ring of muscle that changes the shape of the lens |
| Suspensory ligaments | Fibres connecting the ciliary muscle to the lens. They pull the lens thinner when the muscle relaxes |
| Retina | Layer at the back with light-sensitive cells. The image must be focused here; signals pass to the brain |

### Accommodation

Changing the power of the lens so that objects at different distances are in focus is accommodation.

| | Looking at a near object | Looking at a distant object |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliary muscle | contracts | relaxes |
| Suspensory ligaments | slacken | pulled tight |
| Lens shape | thicker, more curved | thinner, less curved |
| Effect on focal length | shorter | longer |

A near object sends in strongly diverging rays, which need more bending. A distant object sends in almost parallel rays, which need less.

## Range of vision (3.3.6 i)

The **near point** is the closest distance at which the eye can form a sharp image. For a typical eye it is about 25 cm. The **far point** is the furthest distance, which for a typical eye is infinity. The distance between the near point and far point is the **range of vision**: from about 25 cm to infinity.

## Correcting long and short sight (3.3.6 j)

### Long sight

The eyeball is too short, or the eye lens cannot focus a sharp image. Light from near objects would focus behind the retina, so near objects are blurred and the near point is further away than normal. A **convex** lens in front of the eye converges the rays slightly before they enter, so the eye can focus them on the retina.

### Short sight

The eyeball is too long, or the eye lens cannot focus a sharp image. Light from distant objects focuses in front of the retina, so distant objects are blurred and the far point is closer than infinity. A **concave** lens spreads the rays slightly before they enter, so the eye can focus them on the retina.

| | Long sight | Short sight |
|---|---|---|
| Blurred objects | near | distant |
| Image forms | behind the retina | in front of the retina |
| Corrective lens | convex | concave |

## Lasers (3.3.6 k)

A laser is a concentrated source of light. Because its energy is concentrated, it can be used for **cutting**, **cauterising** (sealing tissue by heating it) and **burning**. In eye surgery, lasers can be used to correct visual defects, for example by reshaping the cornea so that it refracts light differently. You do not need to know how a laser works.

## The eye and the camera (3.3.6 l)

| Eye | Camera |
|---|---|
| Lens focuses light | Lens focuses light |
| Image falls on the retina | Image falls on film or a CCD sensor |
| Focus by changing the shape of the lens | Focus by changing the distance between lens and film or sensor |

The film or CCD sensor in a camera is the equivalent of the retina.

## Common errors

- Writing that a concave lens forms a real image of a real object. It does not.
- Saying you cannot see a virtual image. You can. A screen cannot catch it.
- Saying the lens moves in the eye. The lens changes shape.
- Using a concave lens for long sight, or the reverse.
- Dividing heights measured in different units when finding magnification.
