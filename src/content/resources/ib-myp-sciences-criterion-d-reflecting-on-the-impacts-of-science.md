This study guide teaches Criterion D: Reflecting on the impacts of science in IB MYP Sciences. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Sciences guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion D asks you to step back from the laboratory. You explain how science is used to address a real problem, then judge what follows from using it. Because it is the only criterion that is not about practical work, it is also the one where clear writing and sources matter most.

Use it with the [Criterion D revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-d-reflecting-on-the-impacts-of-science-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion D practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-d-reflecting-on-the-impacts-of-science-practice/). The wider course is on the [IB MYP Sciences course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/); the [global contexts page](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-global-contexts/) shows where real issues come from.

## What the guide says about Criterion D

The objective is "Reflecting on the impacts of science". Students gain global understanding of science by evaluating the implications of scientific developments and their applications to a specific problem or issue. Varied scientific language is applied to show understanding, and students are expected to become aware of the importance of documenting the work of others. The guide requires you to reflect on the implications of using science, interacting with **one** of these factors, as appropriate to the task: moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural or environmental. Your chosen factor may be related to other factors.

At the end of Year 5 the four strands are:

1. **Explain** the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue.
2. **Discuss and evaluate** the various implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue.
3. **Apply** scientific language effectively.
4. **Document** the work of others and sources of information used.

Criterion D has a maximum of 8.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Strand | 1-2 | 3-4 | 5-6 | 7-8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| i Application | outline the ways science is used | summarize | describe | explain the ways science is applied and used |
| ii Implications | outline the implications, interacting with a factor | describe the implications | discuss the implications | discuss and evaluate the implications |
| iii Language | applies it with limited success | sometimes applies it | usually applies it clearly and precisely | consistently applies it clearly and precisely |
| iv Sources | documents with limited success | sometimes correctly | usually correctly | completely |

At the top band you do not just say what happened or what might happen. You weigh the implications and reach a judgment.

## Factors: what each one means

The guide's glossary defines each factor.

| Factor | Glossary meaning | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Moral | principles of right or wrong behaviour derived from a particular society | does this fit what the society thinks is right? |
| Ethical | a process of rational inquiry to decide on issues as right or wrong, as applied to people and their actions | can the actions be justified by reasoning? |
| Social | interactions between groups of people involving welfare, safety, rights, justice or class | who gains, who loses, who is treated fairly? |
| Economic | production, distribution and use of income, wealth and commodities | what does it cost, and who pays or earns? |
| Political | relates to government or public affairs | what laws, policies or decisions are involved? |
| Cultural | patterns of knowledge, behaviour, beliefs, shared attitudes, values and practices of groups | how does it affect traditions and values? |
| Environmental | circumstances, objects or conditions by which one is surrounded | what effect does it have on air, water, land and living things? |

Pick one main factor that really fits the issue and, if it helps, link it to another. Do not list all seven with one line each. Depth is better than breadth.

## Strand i: explaining how science is applied

Name the problem, identify the science, and **explain how** the science addresses the problem. Use the science from your course.

**Worked example.** The case is a fictional coastal city, Port Merrow, whose reservoirs are running low. The council considers a reverse osmosis desalination plant.

- **Problem**: the city does not have enough fresh water for its people.
- **Science**: osmosis is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution to a more concentrated one. Seawater is a concentrated solution of salts.
- **How it is applied**: in reverse osmosis, a pump raises the pressure on the seawater above its osmotic pressure. This forces water molecules through a partially permeable membrane in the opposite direction to natural osmosis. Water passes through, while most salt ions are held back. The fresh water is collected, and the remaining water, now more concentrated, is a waste stream called brine.

Explain, don't just name. "Reverse osmosis cleans the water" is an outline. The three bullets above explain.

## Strand ii: discussing and evaluating implications

Choose the factor (or factors) and **develop** each implication: a cause, an effect and who or what is affected.

**Worked example (environmental, linked to economic).**

- *Environmental implication.* The brine is saltier than seawater. If it is released into the sea close to the shore, the extra salt can raise the salinity near the outlet. Organisms that cannot tolerate a change in salinity may move away or die, and this could reduce biodiversity near the outlet. The plant also needs a lot of energy to run the high-pressure pumps. If that energy comes from burning fossil fuels, carbon dioxide emissions will rise and add to climate change.
- *Economic implication.* Building and running the plant costs money, which could raise water bills. On the other hand, a secure supply of water protects businesses and jobs that need it, such as hotels and food processing, and may reduce the cost of rationing. The cost per unit of water depends on energy prices.
- *Weighing up.* The benefits are large and local: a reliable supply for the whole city. The harms are real but can be reduced. Brine can be diluted and released through diffusers far from the shore, and the plant can be powered by renewable energy. Other options such as repairing leaking pipes, recycling water or reducing demand might cost less. The best choice depends on how much the city values security of supply compared with the risk to marine life.
- *Judgment.* A desalination plant is justified if it is combined with renewable energy and a well-designed brine outlet, and if leak repair and conservation are done at the same time.

What makes it top band: each implication has a cause and an effect; there are both benefits and harms; there is a comparison with alternatives; the final judgment follows from the weighing and states a condition.

**A useful structure.** For each implication use *point, evidence, so what*. Then compare, and conclude.

## Strand iii: scientific language

You are expected to use scientific terms correctly and consistently, and to be clear and precise.

| Imprecise | Precise |
|---|---|
| the water goes through the filter | water molecules pass through the partially permeable membrane |
| the salty water is thrown away | the concentrated brine is discharged |
| it uses lots of power | it requires a large input of electrical energy |
| it kills fish | it raises salinity, which affects organisms that cannot tolerate the change |

Define a key term once, then use it. Do not use a term you cannot explain.

## Strand iv: documenting the work of others

The command term "document" means to credit sources of information by referencing (or citing), following one recognised referencing system. References should appear in the text and at the end of the piece of work in a reference list or bibliography.

**Practical rules.**

- Choose one recognised system and use it for the whole piece. Ask your teacher which one your school uses.
- Cite in the text whenever you use a fact, figure, quotation or idea that is not your own. For example (Author, year).
- Put every source in the reference list, giving the information the system requires, such as the author or organisation, year, title, publisher or website and date you accessed it.
- Quote sparingly. Paraphrase in your own words and still cite.
- Judge the source: Who wrote it? Why? Is there evidence? A government agency or a scientific journal usually deserves more trust than an unnamed blog or an advert.

**Illustration (invented source).** In the text: "Brine released near the shore can raise local salinity (Alvarez, 2021)." In the reference list: Alvarez, M. (2021) *Coastal effects of desalination outfalls*. Port Merrow: Harbour Science Press. Accessed 4 March 2025. This source is made up for the example. Use real sources in your own work.

## Putting it together: a plan for a Criterion D response

1. **Introduction**: the issue and why it matters, in two sentences.
2. **Science**: how the science works and is applied (strand i).
3. **Implications**: two or three developed points, linked to your chosen factor (strand ii).
4. **Evaluation**: benefits against harms, alternatives, and a judgment.
5. **Language and sources**: checked throughout, with a reference list at the end.

## How Criterion D is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for sciences, the Applying science task carries 25 marks, assessed against Criterion D. It asks you to explain how science is used to address a real-life issue and expects extended responses that consider one or more of the factors in the guide. Marks may also be awarded against other criteria when appropriate. The whole examination is out of 100.

## Common errors

- Describing the issue but not explaining the science used.
- Listing the factors with no development.
- Giving only benefits or only harms.
- Opinions with no scientific or factual support.
- Everyday words where scientific terms are needed.
- No in-text citations, or a reference list with missing details.
- A conclusion that does not follow from the argument.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion D in IB MYP Sciences?**
Criterion D is Reflecting on the impacts of science. It assesses how well you explain the ways science is applied to a problem or issue, discuss and evaluate the implications of using it, use scientific language, and document sources.

**Which factors can I use?**
Moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural or environmental, as appropriate to the task. The guide says your chosen factor may be interrelated with others.

**What does "document" mean?**
It means crediting sources by referencing or citing them with one recognised referencing system, in the text and in a reference list or bibliography at the end.

**How many marks is Criterion D worth in the on-screen examination?**
The Applying science task is worth 25 marks, out of 100 for the whole examination.
