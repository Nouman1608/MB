## What 2.2.2 contains

The specification has two bullet points under Price:

- pricing strategies
- influences on pricing strategies: technology, competition, market segments, product life cycle.

It does not list the strategies, so this guide uses the standard ones: penetration pricing, price skimming, competitive pricing, cost-plus pricing, psychological pricing and promotional pricing. The four influences, however, are named, and examiners can ask about any of them directly.

## Pricing strategies: choosing, not just defining

A pricing strategy is the overall approach a business uses to set its price. Knowing definitions earns little; the marks come from matching a strategy to a situation.

| Strategy | Best suited when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration (low launch price) | Entering a market with established rivals; customers switch easily | Low profit per unit; customers may leave when the price rises |
| Skimming (high launch price, lowered later) | Product is new, unusual or technologically advanced; some buyers want to be first | Rivals enter with cheaper copies; early buyers feel cheated by later cuts |
| Competitive (close to rivals) | Many similar products; customers compare prices | Little control; profit depends on keeping costs as low as rivals' |
| Cost-plus (unit cost plus a mark-up) | Costs are known and the business wants a set profit per unit | Ignores what customers will pay and what rivals charge |
| Psychological (£9.99 not £10) | Everyday purchases where the first digit catches the eye | Can look cheap for luxury products |
| Promotional (temporary cut) | Clearing stock, boosting a slow period, encouraging trial | Customers may wait for the next offer |

### Worked example 1: penetration pricing at Northgate Gym

Northgate Gym opens near two established gyms that charge about £25 a month. The owner compares two launch prices. Each member adds £4 a month in variable costs (towels, energy, cleaning).

| | Penetration: £15 | Competitive: £25 |
|---|---|---|
| Members forecast | 600 | 300 |
| Monthly revenue | 600 × £15 = £9,000 | 300 × £25 = £7,500 |
| Monthly contribution | 600 × (£15 − £4) = £6,600 | 300 × (£25 − £4) = £6,300 |

Penetration gives slightly more contribution and twice as many members, which builds word of mouth. The plan is to raise the price to £20 after six months. If 500 members stay, monthly contribution becomes 500 × £16 = **£8,000**. The risk is that more than 100 members leave once the low price ends, especially if the rival gyms respond with their own offers.

## Influence 1: technology

- **Price comparison:** websites and apps let customers compare prices in seconds, which pushes prices for similar products close together and favours competitive pricing.
- **Lower costs:** automated production, online selling and self-service systems can reduce unit costs, giving a business room to cut prices or widen margins.
- **Changing prices quickly:** online sellers can change prices many times a day according to demand or time, for example raising prices for busy periods. This is often called dynamic pricing (a standard term).
- **New products:** technology creates innovative products that can be skimmed at launch, before rivals catch up.

## Influence 2: competition

The number and strength of competitors limits pricing freedom.

- **Many close rivals:** the business is a price taker, close to competitive pricing, unless it can differentiate.
- **Few rivals or a strongly differentiated product:** more freedom to charge a higher price or to skim.
- **A rival's price change:** a cut may force a response; ignoring it risks losing sales, matching it cuts profit.
- **New entrants:** an established business may lower prices to make it harder for a newcomer to win customers.

## Influence 3: market segments

Different groups of customers value a product differently and have different incomes, so a business may set different prices for different segments of the same product.

- Students, children and older people often pay less for travel, cinemas and gyms.
- Customers who book early, or travel off-peak, pay less than those who want peak times.
- A premium version with extra features is priced for a high-income segment, while a basic version targets price-sensitive buyers.

### Worked example 2: off-peak prices at Riverside Cinema

Riverside Cinema charges £11 for evening tickets. Weekday afternoons are mostly empty. The manager introduces £6 afternoon tickets for students and retired customers and expects to sell 1,200 extra tickets a month. Each extra customer adds £1.50 in variable costs (cleaning and card fees); the film licence and staff costs are already paid.

Extra contribution = 1,200 × (£6 − £1.50) = **£5,400** a month.

The lower price makes sense because these seats would otherwise earn nothing. The risk is that some evening customers switch to cheaper afternoons, so the cinema limits the offer to particular groups and times.

## Influence 4: the product life cycle

The appropriate price changes as a product moves through its phases.

| Phase | Typical pricing approach |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Skimming for an innovative product, or penetration to win share quickly |
| Growth | Skimmed prices start to fall as rivals arrive; penetration prices may rise once loyalty is built |
| Maturity | Competitive pricing; promotional offers to defend share |
| Decline | Price cuts to clear stock, or a higher price for a few loyal buyers if the business no longer promotes it |

**Example.** Halcyon Audio launches noise-cancelling earbuds at £180 when no rival has a similar product (skimming). When competitors arrive in the growth phase it lowers the price to £140, and in maturity it competes at £99. Each cut brings in a more price-sensitive segment.

## Combining influences in an answer

Real decisions involve more than one influence. A strong analysis answer names the main influence for the business in the case, then shows how another changes the picture: "Halcyon could skim because the product was new, but technology allowed rivals to copy it within a year, so competition forced the price down."

## Common mistakes

- Defining a strategy but not explaining why it suits the business in the question.
- Confusing penetration (low price to gain market share) with promotional pricing (a temporary cut).
- Assuming a lower price always increases revenue. It increases revenue only if sales rise by enough to make up for the lower price.
- Using cost-plus as the answer to every question; it ignores customers and competitors.
- Treating the four influences as a list to recite instead of applying the one that matters most in the case.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** revenue at different prices, contribution, a mark-up, or the percentage change in price.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as how a rival's entry affects Northgate's price.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why technology pushes prices together.
- **Analyse:** follow the chain from an influence to the price decision to sales and profit.
- **Justify:** recommend one of two pricing options using the figures, noting the risk of the one chosen.
- **Evaluate:** weigh how far an influence such as competition limits the business's choice, and conclude on what the decision depends.
