O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series). The broader [kinematics and motion graphs guide](/resources/kinematics/)

## Speed, velocity and units

Speed is a scalar; velocity is a vector, so a velocity needs a direction. A car going round a
roundabout at a steady 10 m/s has constant speed but changing velocity, because its direction
changes.

**Converting km/h to m/s:** 1 km/h = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s, so divide by 3.6.

```
90 km/h = 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25 m/s          15 m/s = 15 × 3.6 = 54 km/h
```

Always convert before using a = Δv ÷ Δt if times are in seconds.

## Average speed: the classic trap

Average speed is **total distance ÷ total time**, not the average of the speeds.

**Worked example.** A driver travels 30 km at 60 km/h and then another 30 km at 30 km/h.

```
time for first part  = 30 ÷ 60 = 0.50 h
time for second part = 30 ÷ 30 = 1.0 h
average speed = 60 km ÷ 1.5 h = 40 km/h   (not 45 km/h)
```

The slower part takes longer, so it counts for more of the journey time. Stops count too: time
spent stationary adds to the total time but not to the distance.

## Acceleration, uniform and non-uniform

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time, measured in m/s².

- **Uniform acceleration:** velocity changes by the same amount every second, for example an
  object in free fall with negligible air resistance, or a trolley running down a constant slope.
- **Non-uniform acceleration:** the rate of change of velocity itself changes, for example a car
  pulling away and changing gear, or a skydiver whose acceleration falls as air resistance grows.
- **Deceleration** is negative acceleration. A car slowing from 20 m/s to 8 m/s in 4.0 s has

```
a = (8 − 20) ÷ 4.0 = −3.0 m/s²   (a deceleration of 3.0 m/s²)
```

Keep the minus sign in the calculation; say "deceleration of 3.0 m/s²" in words if asked.

## Reading graph shapes

**Distance–time graphs**: the gradient is the speed.

| Shape | Motion |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | At rest |
| Straight sloping line | Constant speed |
| Curve getting steeper | Accelerating |
| Curve getting less steep | Decelerating |

**Speed–time graphs**: the gradient is the acceleration; the area underneath is the distance.

| Shape | Motion |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line on the time axis (speed zero) | At rest |
| Horizontal line above the axis | Constant speed |
| Straight sloping line | Constant acceleration (upwards) or constant deceleration (downwards) |
| Curved line | Changing acceleration |

A common error is reading a horizontal line on a speed–time graph as "at rest". It means
constant speed unless the line lies on the time axis.

**Gradient of a curve.** For a curved distance–time graph, draw a tangent at the point of
interest and find its gradient using a large triangle. This gives the speed at that instant.

## Worked example: a three-stage train journey

A train starts from rest and accelerates uniformly to 24 m/s in 40 s. It travels at 24 m/s for
120 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 30 s.

**Accelerations (gradients):**

```
stage 1: a = (24 − 0) ÷ 40 = 0.60 m/s²
stage 3: a = (0 − 24) ÷ 30 = −0.80 m/s²
```

**Distances (areas under the speed–time graph):**

```
stage 1 (triangle):  ½ × 40 × 24  = 480 m
stage 2 (rectangle): 120 × 24     = 2880 m
stage 3 (triangle):  ½ × 30 × 24  = 360 m
total distance = 3720 m
```

**Average speed:**

```
total time = 40 + 120 + 30 = 190 s
average speed = 3720 ÷ 190 = 19.6 m/s
```

The average is below 24 m/s because the train spends 70 s moving more slowly than its top speed.
The syllabus limits area calculations to constant speed or constant acceleration, so every
section here is a rectangle or triangle; a trapezium (for example, accelerating from 10 m/s to
20 m/s) can be split into a rectangle plus a triangle.

## Free fall

Near the Earth's surface, an object falling with negligible air resistance accelerates at
g ≈ 9.8 m/s², and g is approximately constant over everyday heights. Its speed–time graph is a
straight line through the origin with gradient 9.8 m/s².

**Worked example.** A stone is dropped from rest. Find its speed and the distance fallen after
1.5 s, ignoring air resistance.

```
speed = g × t = 9.8 × 1.5 = 14.7 m/s
distance = area under speed–time graph = ½ × 1.5 × 14.7 = 11.0 m (3 s.f.)
```

If air resistance is significant, the graph curves and becomes less steep: the acceleration
decreases, which is changing acceleration.

## Sketching and plotting graphs

- Label both axes with quantity and unit.
- Choose scales that use more than half the grid in each direction.
- Plot points with small crosses and draw a smooth curve or a ruled straight line of best fit.
- For a **sketch**, show the correct shape and label key values; exact scales are not needed.

## Common mistakes

- Averaging speeds instead of dividing total distance by total time.
- Forgetting to convert minutes or km/h into seconds or m/s.
- Treating a horizontal speed–time line as "stopped".
- Using the area under a **distance**–time graph; only the speed–time area gives distance.
- Dropping the minus sign for a deceleration in a calculation.
- Calculating a gradient from two points that are close together, which makes it imprecise.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Define**: give the formal statement, such as "change in velocity per unit time".
- **Sketch**: correct shape and labelled axes; key values marked.
- **Determine**: take values from the graph and show the working.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution, answer with unit.
- **Describe** (motion from a graph): name each stage, such as "constant acceleration for 40 s,
  then constant speed".

## Frequently asked questions

**Can speed be negative?** Speed is a scalar, so it is never negative. Velocity can be negative,
meaning motion in the opposite direction to the one chosen as positive.

**Is a deceleration the same as a negative velocity?** No. Deceleration means the speed is
decreasing; the object can still be moving forwards.

**What does a curve on a speed–time graph mean?** The acceleration is changing: steeper means a
larger acceleration.
