## Section A

**1.** Define the term fetch. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** characteristics of a destructive wave. **[2]**

**3.** Name **one** type of mechanical weathering and **one** type of chemical weathering. **[2]**

**4.** Name the **three** types of mass movement listed in the specification. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between abrasion and attrition. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how longshore drift moves sediment along a coastline. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why deposition happens in coastal areas. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how freeze-thaw weathering can lead to a rock fall from a cliff. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the formation of a wave-cut platform. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a headland can be eroded to form a stack. **[6]**

**11.** Explain the formation of a spit. **[4]**

**12.** A stretch of invented coastline has alternating bands of granite and clay running at right angles to the sea. Explain how this geology would affect the shape of the coastline. **[4]**

**13.** Explain how sand dunes develop. **[4]**

**14.** "Rock type is the most important factor in the formation of coastal landforms." Do you agree? Justify your answer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The distance of open water over which the wind blows to create waves [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two: high and steep [1]; frequent [1]; backwash stronger than swash [1]; erode and remove beach material [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Mechanical: freeze-thaw or salt weathering [1]. Chemical: carbonation (acidic rainwater dissolving limestone or chalk) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Sliding [1]; slumping [1]; rock falls [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Abrasion is when waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away [1]. Attrition is when sediment carried by waves knocks together, becoming smaller and more rounded [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The prevailing wind makes waves approach the beach at an angle [1]. The swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle [1]. The backwash returns it straight down the beach under gravity [1]. This is repeated, so sediment moves in a zigzag along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Any two, 2 marks each: in sheltered bays wave energy is low [1], so waves cannot carry their sediment [1]; where constructive waves dominate, the swash is stronger than the backwash [1], so material is left on the beach [1]; where the coastline changes direction, longshore drift carries sediment into deeper, calmer water [1], where it is dropped [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Water enters cracks in the cliff face [1]. It freezes and expands, widening the cracks; repeated freezing and thawing weakens the rock [1]. Eventually fragments break off and fall under gravity, forming a rock fall [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Destructive waves erode the cliff base between high and low tide by hydraulic power and abrasion [1], forming a wave-cut notch [1]. The overhanging rock above becomes unsupported and collapses, so the cliff retreats [1]. Repetition leaves a gently sloping rocky platform at the foot of the cliff, exposed at low tide [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Waves attack a weakness such as a joint or fault in the headland [1]. Hydraulic power and abrasion widen it into a cave [1]. Erosion continues until the cave breaks through the headland, forming an arch [1]. The base of the arch is eroded and weathering weakens the roof [1]. The roof collapses [1], leaving an isolated pillar of rock, a stack [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Longshore drift transports sediment along the coast [1]. Where the coastline changes direction, for example at a river mouth, sediment is carried into calmer water and deposited [1]. Over time a long, narrow ridge builds out to sea [1]. Changes in wind direction may curve the end into a recurved tip, and salt marsh may form in the sheltered water behind [1]. **[4]**

**12.** This is a discordant coast [1]. The clay is less resistant, so it is eroded faster and forms bays [1]. The granite is more resistant and is left projecting as headlands [1]. Waves then concentrate energy on the headlands, while the bays become sheltered and may develop beaches [1]. **[4]**

**13.** A wide beach dries at low tide, and onshore winds blow sand inland [1]. Sand collects against obstacles such as driftwood, forming embryo dunes [1]. Marram grass colonises the dunes; its long roots stabilise the sand and its leaves trap more [1]. Over time dunes further inland become older, higher and more vegetated as soils develop [1]. **[4]**

**14.** Indicative content:
- Agree: rock type controls the rate of erosion, so resistant rock forms headlands and cliffs and weak rock forms bays [1].
- Agree: weak clay cliffs are prone to slumping, giving lower, gentler profiles [1].
- Disagree: structure matters too: joints and faults allow caves and arches to form, and the arrangement of bands decides whether headlands and bays form [1].
- Disagree: processes and wave energy matter; without destructive waves erosion would be slow [1].
- Disagree: depositional landforms such as spits depend mainly on longshore drift and the shape of the coast, not on rock type [1].
- Judgement: rock type is important for erosional landforms, but structure and processes are just as important, and processes matter most for depositional landforms [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing longshore drift without the angle of the swash and the straight-down backwash.
- Calling weathering "erosion".
- Skipping the arch stage in the formation of a stack.
- Forgetting that a spit forms where the coast changes direction.
- Explaining geology without naming discordant or concordant structure.
- Extended answers with no final judgement.
