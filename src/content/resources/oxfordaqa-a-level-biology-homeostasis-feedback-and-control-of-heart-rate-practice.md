## Questions

**1.** A marathon runner's core temperature rises from 37.0 °C to 41.5 °C in hot weather. Explain why this rise threatens the enzymes in her cells. **[3]**

**2.** A patient's blood pH is 7.1. The normal value is about 7.4. Explain how this change could reduce the activity of enzymes. **[4]**

**3.**

**(a)** State what negative feedback is. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why separate mechanisms for correcting a rise and a fall give better control than one mechanism. **[2]**

**4.** A volunteer sat in cold water. The table shows the results.

| Time (min) | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core temperature (°C) | 37.0 | 36.6 | 36.0 | 35.2 | 34.2 |
| Metabolic heat production (W) | 90 | 120 | 170 | 150 | 110 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in heat production between 0 and 30 minutes. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate of fall in core temperature between 45 and 60 minutes in °C per hour. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what the data show about feedback in the first 30 minutes and after 45 minutes. **[2]**

**5.** During labour, stretching of the cervix sends impulses to the brain, which makes the pituitary gland release more oxytocin. Oxytocin increases contractions of the uterus, which stretch the cervix further.

**(a)** Identify the type of feedback and give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how this loop is brought to an end. **[2]**

**6.**

**(a)** Cardiac muscle is described as myogenic. State what this means. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the role of the sinoatrial node (SAN). **[2]**

**7.** Describe how the wave of electrical activity from the SAN is passed on so that the ventricles contract after the atria and from the apex upwards. **[6]**

**8.** A runner's heart rate rises from 72 to 156 beats per minute.

**(a)** Calculate the duration of one cardiac cycle at each rate, in seconds to 2 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage reduction in the duration of the cycle. Round your answer to a whole number. **[2]**

**9.** The table shows data from a volunteer.

| Condition | Lying down | 10 s after standing | 2 min after standing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean arterial pressure (mmHg) | 93 | 78 | 90 |
| Impulses per second from pressure receptors | 30 | 19 | 27 |
| Heart rate (beats per minute) | 66 | 82 | 70 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in heart rate between lying down and 10 s after standing. Round your answer to a whole number. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the data to explain the change in heart rate. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how heart rate is increased at the start of exercise and returned to its resting level afterwards. **[7]**

**11.** In an anaesthetised mammal, the nerves to the heart were cut or stimulated and the heart rate recorded.

| Treatment | Heart rate (beats per minute) |
|---|---|
| Nerves intact | 72 |
| Vagus nerves cut | 94 |
| Sympathetic nerves to heart cut | 71 |
| Both cut | 93 |
| Vagus nerve stimulated | 48 |
| Sympathetic nerve stimulated | 130 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in heart rate when the vagus nerves were cut. Express the answer to three significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the results show about the nerves at rest. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how the results support the idea that separate mechanisms give greater control of heart rate. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The high temperature breaks hydrogen bonds and other weak bonds in the enzyme [1]; its tertiary structure alters, so the active site changes shape [1]; substrate no longer fits, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, the enzyme is denatured and reaction rate falls [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that say only "enzymes stop working" are too vague; name the bonds and the active site.

**2.** The change in pH alters the hydrogen ion concentration [1]; this changes the charges on R groups, so ionic and hydrogen bonds break or form in the wrong places [1]; the tertiary structure alters and the active site changes shape [1]; fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, so activity falls (or the enzyme is denatured) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* pH answers tend to gain credit when they name hydrogen ions or R group charge, not just "the enzyme changes shape".

**3. (a)** A receptor detects a change from the original level [1]; the response reverses the change and restores the original level [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each direction is corrected actively and quickly, and an overshoot is reversed at once [1]; the level stays within narrower limits [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "greater control" needs a comparison with a single mechanism; saying "two mechanisms are better" without the reason gains nothing.

**4. (a)** (170 − 90) ÷ 90 × 100 [1] = **88.9 %** [1]. **(b)** Fall = 35.2 − 34.2 = 1.0 °C in 15 min [1]; 1.0 ÷ 15 × 60 = **4.0 °C per hour** [1]. **(c)** In the first 30 minutes heat production rises as temperature falls, which opposes the change: negative feedback [1]; after 45 minutes heat production falls as temperature falls, so the response adds to the change (positive feedback), because slower enzyme-controlled reactions release less heat [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* in a calculation answer, show the substitution and the units; in interpretation, say whether the response makes the change smaller or bigger.

**5. (a)** Positive feedback [1]; the response (contractions) increases the stimulus (stretching), so the departure grows [1]. **(b)** Birth of the baby removes the stretching of the cervix [1]; less oxytocin is released, so contractions stop [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "positive" must be justified by what the response does to the stimulus; a bare label does not show understanding.

**6. (a)** It contracts and relaxes without needing a nerve impulse; the stimulus starts in the muscle itself [1]. **(b)** The SAN acts as the pacemaker and starts a wave of depolarisation [1] that spreads across the atria and makes them contract [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "the SAN controls the heart" is too general; say what it initiates and where the wave goes.

**7.** The wave spreads over the atria, which contract [1]; fibrous tissue between atria and ventricles blocks direct crossing [1]; the wave reaches the AVN [1]; the AVN holds it briefly, giving the atria time to empty before the ventricles contract [1]; the wave passes down the bundle of His in the septum [1]; Purkinje tissue carries it to the apex and up the walls, so the ventricles contract from the apex upwards [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* the order matters in a "describe the route" answer; the delay belongs to the AVN, not Purkinje tissue.

**8. (a)** 60 ÷ 72 = **0.83 s** [1]; 60 ÷ 156 = **0.38 s** [1]. **(b)** (0.833 − 0.385) ÷ 0.833 × 100 [1] = **54 %** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* rounding early can shift the final figure; carrying full values until the end is safer.

**9. (a)** (82 − 66) ÷ 66 × 100 [1] = **24 %** [1]. **(b)** Standing lowers the arterial pressure (93 to 78 mmHg) [1]; pressure receptors send fewer impulses (30 to 19 per second) to the medulla [1]; the medulla sends more sympathetic (and fewer parasympathetic) impulses to the SAN, so heart rate rises to 82 [1]; this raises pressure again, and after 2 min the pressure (90), impulses (27) and heart rate (70) are near their lying values, which is negative feedback [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* use numbers from the table at each link in the chain; an answer that uses only general statements loses the data-use credit.

**10.** Respiring muscle makes more carbon dioxide [1]; blood pH falls [1]; the fall is detected by chemoreceptors located in the carotid arteries and aorta [1]; they send more impulses to the cardiac control centre in the medulla [1]; impulses pass along sympathetic neurones to the SAN [1]; the SAN generates waves faster, so heart rate rises [1]; afterwards carbon dioxide falls and pH returns to normal, so impulses along the sympathetic neurones decrease and the vagus (parasympathetic) nerve slows the SAN back to the resting rate [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* give both phases, the rise and the return; many answers stop after the rise.

**11. (a)** (94 − 72) ÷ 72 × 100 [1] = **30.6 %** [1]. **(b)** Cutting the vagus raises the rate, so at rest the vagus is slowing the heart [1]; cutting the sympathetic nerves has almost no effect (71 against 72), so there is little sympathetic stimulation at rest [1]; the heart still beats at 93 with both cut, because the SAN generates the beat itself (myogenic) [1]. **(c)** Stimulating the vagus lowers the rate to 48 [1]; stimulating the sympathetic nerve raises it to 130 [1]; the two nerves act in opposite directions, so the rate can be corrected quickly whether it needs to go up or down [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* in an evaluation of data, tie each conclusion to a quoted value and state what the nerve does, not just whether the number changed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that cold denatures enzymes, instead of saying they slow.
- Describing enzyme changes without bonds, tertiary structure and the active site.
- Not saying that a negative feedback response reverses the change.
- Labelling positive feedback without explaining that the response increases the change.
- Percentage change calculated from the wrong starting value.
- Mixing up the SAN, AVN and Purkinje tissue.
- Forgetting the cardiac control centre in the medulla and writing "the brain" only.
- Forgetting the way back (recovery) in heart-rate explanations.
- Giving data answers without quoting figures.
