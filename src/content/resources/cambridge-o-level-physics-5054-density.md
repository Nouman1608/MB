This guide covers subtopic **1.4 Density** from Topic 1, Motion, forces and energy, in Cambridge
O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series). The broader
[measurement, mass, weight and density guide](/resources/measurement-mass-weight-and-density/)
introduces the equation and the three methods. This page goes further: converting units,
rearranging the equation, volumes of cylinders and other regular shapes, sources of error in each
method, and the calculations examiners expect alongside each experiment.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL PHYSICS 5054, subtopic 1.4**

1. Define density as mass per unit volume; recall and use the equation ρ = m ÷ V
2. Describe how to determine the density of a liquid, of a regularly shaped solid and of an
   irregularly shaped solid which sinks in a liquid (volume by displacement), including appropriate
   calculations

5054 is **not tiered**.

## The equation and its rearrangements

```
ρ = m / V          m = ρ V          V = m / ρ
```

Density depends on the **material**, not on the size of the sample. Cutting a block of aluminium
in half halves both its mass and its volume, so the density stays the same.

## Units and conversions

The SI unit is **kg/m³**. Laboratory measurements often use grams and cm³, giving g/cm³.

```
1 g/cm³ = 0.001 kg ÷ 0.000 001 m³ = 1000 kg/m³
```

| g/cm³ | kg/m³ | Example material |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 1000 | Water (approximately) |
| 2.7 | 2700 | Aluminium |
| 0.91 | 910 | A cooking oil |
| 0.0012 | 1.2 | Air (approximately) |

To convert g/cm³ to kg/m³, **multiply by 1000**. To convert kg/m³ to g/cm³, divide by 1000. Never
mix units inside one calculation: use grams with cm³, or kilograms with m³.

## Method 1: density of a liquid

1. Place an empty measuring cylinder on an electronic balance and record its mass (or tare the
   balance).
2. Pour in a measured volume of liquid, reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
3. Record the new mass.
4. Mass of liquid = full reading − empty reading; then ρ = m ÷ V.

**Worked example.** The empty cylinder has mass 84.0 g. With 50.0 cm³ of oil it has mass 129.5 g.

```
mass of oil = 129.5 − 84.0 = 45.5 g
ρ = 45.5 / 50.0 = 0.910 g/cm³ = 910 kg/m³
```

**Improving accuracy:** use a larger volume so the percentage uncertainty in each reading is
smaller; repeat with different volumes and plot mass against volume; the gradient of the
straight line is the density.

## Method 2: density of a regularly shaped solid

1. Measure the dimensions with a ruler (or a micrometer for small dimensions), taking several
   readings and averaging.
2. Calculate the volume.
3. Measure the mass on a balance; then ρ = m ÷ V.

| Shape | Volume |
|---|---|
| Cuboid | length × width × height |
| Cylinder | π r² × length (r = diameter ÷ 2) |
| Cube | side³ |

**Worked example (cylinder).** A metal rod has diameter 2.0 cm, length 5.0 cm and mass 123 g.

```
r = 1.0 cm
V = π × 1.0² × 5.0 = 15.7 cm³
ρ = 123 / 15.7 = 7.83 g/cm³ = 7830 kg/m³
```

The common mistake is using the diameter instead of the radius, which gives a volume four times
too large and a density four times too small.

## Method 3: density of an irregular solid that sinks

1. Measure the solid's mass on a balance **first**, while it is dry.
2. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume.
3. Tie the solid to a thread and lower it gently until it is fully submerged.
4. Record the new volume. The solid's volume is the **rise** in level.
5. Calculate ρ = m ÷ V.

**Worked example.** A stone has mass 31.2 g. The water level rises from 40.0 cm³ to 52.0 cm³.

```
V = 52.0 − 40.0 = 12.0 cm³
ρ = 31.2 / 12.0 = 2.60 g/cm³ = 2600 kg/m³
```

**Sources of error and how to reduce them**

| Problem | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Air bubbles cling to the stone | Measured volume too large, density too small | Tap the cylinder or stir gently to release bubbles |
| Water splashes out as the stone drops in | Rise too small, density too large | Lower the stone slowly on a thread |
| Stone weighed after being in water | Water clinging to it adds mass, density too large | Weigh first, while dry |
| Coarse cylinder scale | Large uncertainty in a small rise | Use the narrowest cylinder the stone fits in |
| Stone absorbs water (porous) | Water level rises less than the stone's volume | Choose a non-porous sample or note the limitation |

The syllabus specifies a solid **which sinks**. A floating solid would not be fully submerged, so
the rise in level would not equal its whole volume.

## Calculations that go beyond ρ = m ÷ V

**Mass of air in a room.** A room measures 5.0 m × 4.0 m × 3.0 m; air has density 1.2 kg/m³.

```
V = 5.0 × 4.0 × 3.0 = 60 m³
m = ρ V = 1.2 × 60 = 72 kg
```

**Volume from density.** A gold ring has mass 0.0050 kg; gold has density 19 300 kg/m³.

```
V = m / ρ = 0.0050 / 19 300 = 2.6 × 10⁻⁷ m³  (0.26 cm³)
```

**Identifying a material.** If a measured density is close to a known value, such as 2.7 g/cm³
for aluminium, the sample may be that material. Closeness depends on the measurement's
uncertainty, so say "consistent with" rather than "proves".

## Common mistakes

- Mixing grams with m³ or kilograms with cm³.
- Using the final cylinder reading as the solid's volume.
- Using diameter in place of radius for a cylinder.
- Forgetting to subtract the container's mass for a liquid.
- Weighing the stone after the displacement measurement.
- Converting g/cm³ to kg/m³ by dividing instead of multiplying.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Define**: "mass per unit volume".
- **Describe** (an experiment): list apparatus, the measurements in order and the calculation; a
  numbered list is clear.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution with units, answer with unit and sensible significant
  figures.
- **Explain** (an error): say what happens to the measured value and why.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does density change with temperature?** Most materials expand slightly when heated, so density
falls a little. O Level questions treat density as constant unless they say otherwise.

**Why plot mass against volume?** The gradient gives the density from several readings, which
reduces the effect of any one poor measurement.

**What if a solid floats?** The syllabus method is for solids that sink; questions will not ask you
to use simple displacement for a floating solid.
