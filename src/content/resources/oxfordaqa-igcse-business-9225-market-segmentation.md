This guide covers section **3.5.2 Market segmentation** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 5, Marketing. The specification asks you to understand **how and why different businesses use segmentation to target customers**, including segmentation **by gender, age, location and income**, and to **recommend the most appropriate marketing methods for given situations**. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Marketing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-marketing/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-market-segmentation-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-market-segmentation-practice/).

## What is a market, and what is a segment?

A **market** is all the actual and potential customers for a product, together with the businesses that sell it. Customers within a market are not all the same: a teenager, a retired person and a business executive want different things from a phone, a holiday or a restaurant meal.

**Market segmentation** means dividing a market into smaller groups of customers (segments) who share similar characteristics, needs or buying habits. A business can then target the segment or segments that are most likely to buy its product, and design the product, price, promotion and distribution to suit them.

A **mass market** approach sells the same product to all customers. A **niche market** approach targets one small, specialised segment, such as vegan trainers or luxury watches. Segmentation lets a business choose between the two.

## Why do businesses segment markets?

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| To target spending | Advertising money is spent on the customers most likely to buy, not wasted on others |
| To meet needs more precisely | Products can be designed for the needs of a particular group |
| To increase sales and profit | Customers who feel a product is made for them are more likely to buy and to pay more |
| To compete | A business may find a segment ignored by rivals |
| To choose suitable marketing methods | The right media, prices and places can be chosen for each group |
| To spot opportunities | Growing segments, such as an ageing population, show where to expand |

There are also drawbacks. Segmentation needs research, which costs money. A narrow segment may be too small to be profitable, and targeting one group can put off others. Segments also change over time.

## Types of segmentation named in the specification

### Segmentation by age

Customers of different ages have different needs, tastes and incomes.

| Age group | Typical needs | Example product |
|---|---|---|
| Children | Fun, safe, colourful products; parents decide to buy | Toys, cartoon cereals |
| Teenagers and young adults | Fashion, technology, social life | Trainers, phones, music streaming |
| Middle-aged | Family needs, careers, homes | Family cars, insurance |
| Older people | Health, comfort, simplicity | Mobility aids, travel for retirees |

### Segmentation by gender

Some products are aimed mainly at one gender, for example razors, cosmetics or particular clothing. Businesses must be careful: stereotypes can offend and exclude customers, and many products sell better when they are not restricted to one gender.

### Segmentation by location

Customers in different places have different needs, because of climate, culture and local habits.

- **Region and climate:** raincoats in a wet region, air conditioners in a hot one.
- **Urban and rural:** city dwellers may want small, convenient packs; rural customers may buy in bulk.
- **Country:** language, religion and taste vary between countries.

### Segmentation by income

Income affects what customers can afford and how they spend.

- **Higher incomes:** can pay for luxury goods and premium brands.
- **Middle incomes:** want value for money with good quality.
- **Lower incomes:** price-sensitive; value basic, low-priced products and discounts.

Other forms of segmentation exist, such as lifestyle and interests, but the four above are the ones named in the specification.

## Worked example: sizing segments

**Worked example 1: age segments for a clothing shop.** A town has 40,000 people in four age segments. The shop estimates the proportion that buy from it each year.

| Age group | People | Percentage who buy | Number of buyers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 to 24 | 8,000 | 20% | 1,600 |
| 25 to 44 | 15,000 | 12% | 1,800 |
| 45 to 64 | 12,000 | 8% | 960 |
| 65 and over | 5,000 | 3% | 150 |
| **Total** | **40,000** | | **4,510** |

```
Total buyers = 1,600 + 1,800 + 960 + 150 = 4,510
Share of buyers aged 16 to 44 = (1,600 + 1,800) / 4,510 x 100 = 75.4%
If each buyer spends $60 a year, revenue = 4,510 x $60 = $270,600
```

Three-quarters of the shop's buyers are aged 16 to 44, so a marketing plan should aim mainly at them. The 25 to 44 group has the most buyers (1,800), though the 16 to 24 group is the most likely to buy (20%). Shop owners must decide whether to target the largest group or the one most likely to buy.

## Choosing marketing methods for a segment

The specification asks you to recommend the most suitable marketing methods for given situations. Match the method to the segment.

| Segment | Suitable methods | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young adults, 16 to 24 | Social media, influencers, mobile promotions, student discounts | They spend time online and are price-conscious |
| Families with children | Family offers, TV and online adverts, packs for sharing | Parents decide; value for money matters |
| Older customers | Local newspapers, direct mail, in-store advice, telephone ordering | Less likely to use social media, value personal service |
| High-income customers | Premium packaging, magazines, exclusive events, high-quality service | Image and quality matter more than price |
| Low-income customers | Low prices, discounts, loyalty points, special offers | Price is the main concern |
| Customers in a certain region | Local radio, local posters, regional offers | Reach only the people in the area |

Marketing includes all four elements of the marketing mix (price, product, promotion and place), so segmentation affects each one. A product aimed at students might be low priced (price), small and portable (product), advertised online (promotion) and sold through campus shops and websites (place).

**Worked example 2: choosing between segments.** A gym has $12,000 to spend on advertising. A social media campaign aimed at 18 to 30-year-olds would reach 15,000 people, of whom 10% join. A local newspaper advert for over-51s would reach 25,000 people, of whom 2% join.

```
18 to 30: 10% x 15,000 = 1,500 members; cost per member = $12,000 / 1,500 = $8.00
Over 51:   2% x 25,000 =   500 members; cost per member = $12,000 / 500   = $24.00
```

The social media campaign wins three times as many members for the same budget. The over-51s are a larger group, but fewer are likely to join. The gym also needs to consider that older members might stay longer, so the lifetime value may differ.

## Limits of segmentation

- Segments overlap. A 20-year-old may be a student on a low income or a young professional on a high one.
- Customers do not always behave as the average of their group.
- Research data can be out of date.
- Over-targeting can lose customers outside the target segment.
- Segmentation can encourage stereotypes.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Explain how a clothing shop could use segmentation by age.

**Answer:** The shop could divide its customers into age groups such as 16 to 24, 25 to 44 and over 45. Each group wants different things: younger customers want fashion and low prices, while older customers may want comfort and quality. If 20% of 8,000 people aged 16 to 24 buy (1,600) and 12% of 15,000 people aged 25 to 44 buy (1,800), the shop can see where most of its buyers are. It can then use social media for the younger group and local newspapers for older customers, and stock the styles each group prefers. This targets spending on the people most likely to buy, which raises sales for each dollar of advertising.

## Common mistakes

- Defining segmentation as "choosing a product". It is dividing the market into groups of customers.
- Mixing up market segmentation (dividing customers) with market research (collecting information).
- Naming a marketing method without explaining why it suits the segment.
- Treating all people of an age or gender as the same.
- Forgetting that segmentation affects price, product, promotion and place.
- Ignoring the size of the segment and the cost of reaching it.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** questions, say that segmentation divides a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics. For **explain** questions, give the type of segmentation and its effect: "segmenting by income lets a business price products to match what each group can afford". For **recommend** questions, name a method, link it to the segment's characteristics and say what result you expect. For **evaluate** questions, compare segments by size, spending power and cost of reaching them, then make a reasoned choice.
