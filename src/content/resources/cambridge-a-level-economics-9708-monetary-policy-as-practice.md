## Section A

**1.** Define monetary policy. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is an example of contractionary monetary policy?

A: a cut in the rate of income tax

B: the central bank buying government bonds from banks

C: a rise in the share of deposits that banks must hold as reserves

D: a cut in the central bank's policy interest rate **[1]**

**3.** State two ways in which a cut in interest rates could increase consumption. **[2]**

**4.** A bank holds deposits of 800 million. The reserve requirement rises from 8% to 10%. Calculate the change in the maximum amount the bank can lend. **[2]**

**5.** A household earns 36,000 a year. The maximum loan-to-income multiple for mortgages is cut from 5.0 to 4.5. Calculate the fall in the maximum mortgage, in money and as a percentage. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a central bank that increases the money supply is likely to see interest rates fall. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** After a rise in its policy interest rate, Country R's currency rises from 1 R-unit = 1.50 foreign units to 1 R-unit = 1.62 foreign units.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage appreciation of the R-unit. **[1]**

**(b)** A Country R export is priced at 30 R-units. Calculate its price in foreign units before and after the change. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how this change in the exchange rate affects aggregate demand in Country R. **[2]**

**8.** A group of firms in Country S is considering four investment projects. Each would be financed by borrowing.

| Project | Cost (million) | Expected annual return |
|---|---|---|
| A | 10 | 7.0% |
| B | 6 | 5.5% |
| C | 8 | 4.0% |
| D | 5 | 3.0% |

**(a)** The interest rate is 3.5%. State which projects are worth undertaking and calculate the total investment. **[2]**

**(b)** The interest rate rises to 5.0%. Calculate the new total investment and the percentage fall. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how this change could affect employment in Country S. **[2]**

**9.** Country T has high unemployment and inflation of 1%, below its target of 2%.

**(a)** Using an AD/AS diagram, explain the likely effect of a cut in interest rates on real output, the price level and employment in Country T. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the effect on real output would be different if Country T were at full capacity. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why contractionary monetary policy may increase unemployment in the short run. **[3]**

**11.** In Country V, households already carry large debts and business confidence is low. The central bank cuts its policy rate from 2.0% to 0.5%. Assess whether this cut is likely to increase aggregate demand significantly. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Changes to interest rates, the money supply or credit regulations by the central bank or government [1], to influence aggregate demand and meet macroeconomic objectives such as price stability [1].

**2.** **C** [1]. A higher reserve requirement reduces the funds banks can lend. A is fiscal policy; B and D are expansionary.

**3.** Any two of: borrowing for goods such as cars becomes cheaper, so credit-financed spending rises [1]; variable-rate mortgage payments fall, raising disposable income [1]; saving becomes less rewarding, so households save less and spend more.

**4.** Before: 800 × (1 − 0.08) = 736 million; after: 800 × (1 − 0.10) = 720 million [1]. The maximum lending falls by **16 million** [1].

**5.** Before: 36,000 × 5.0 = 180,000; after: 36,000 × 4.5 = 162,000 [1]. The maximum falls by **18,000, or 10%** [1].

**6.** With a larger money supply and unchanged demand for money, banks and households hold more money than they want at the old interest rate [1], so banks compete to lend it and the price of borrowing, the interest rate, falls until the money is willingly held [1].

**7.** **(a)** (1.62 − 1.50) ÷ 1.50 × 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** Before: 30 × 1.50 = **45 foreign units** [1]; after: 30 × 1.62 = **48.60 foreign units** [1].

**(c)** Exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, so export volumes fall and import volumes rise [1]. Net exports (X − M) fall, so AD shifts left [1].

**8.** **(a)** Projects A, B and C earn more than 3.5% [1], so investment = 10 + 6 + 8 = **24 million** [1].

**(b)** Only A and B earn more than 5.0%, so investment = **16 million** [1]. The fall is 8 ÷ 24 × 100 = **33.3%** [1].

**(c)** Investment is a component of AD, so AD shifts left and firms producing capital goods receive fewer orders [1]. Labour is a derived demand, so these firms, and others affected by lower spending, need fewer workers and employment falls [1].

**9.** **(a)** Lower rates raise consumption and investment, so AD shifts right [1]. Country T has spare capacity, so it is on the highly elastic or lower part of the upward-sloping section of AS [1]. Real output rises and the price level rises little, if at all, which brings inflation back towards target [1]. Firms need more workers to produce more, so employment rises and unemployment falls [1]. (A diagram with labelled axes, AD1, AD2 and both equilibria supports the marks.)

**(b)** At full capacity the economy is on the vertical section of AS [1], so a rightward shift of AD raises the price level but cannot raise real output in the long run, because there are no spare resources [1].

**10.** Higher interest rates reduce consumption, investment and net exports, so AD shifts left [1]. Firms sell less and cut output [1]. Because labour is a derived demand, they reduce hiring or lay off workers, and wages and prices may adjust only slowly, so unemployment rises before inflation falls fully [1].

**11.** For a significant rise: lower rates cut the cost of variable-rate debt, so indebted households gain disposable income and consumption may rise [1]; a cut of 1.5 percentage points is large relative to the starting rate [1]; the currency may depreciate, raising net exports [1].

Against: households with large debts may use the extra income to repay debt rather than spend [1]; with low confidence, firms expect weak sales, so investment depends more on expected returns than on the interest rate [1]; at 0.5% there is little room for further cuts if the first does not work [1]; many loans may be on fixed rates, so the effect is delayed [1].

Judgement: the cut is likely to raise AD only modestly in the short run, because confidence and debt weaken the main routes; its effect depends on how quickly confidence recovers and on whether it is supported by other policies [1].
