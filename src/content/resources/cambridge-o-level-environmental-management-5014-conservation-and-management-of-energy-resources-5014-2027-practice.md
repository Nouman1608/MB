## Section A

**1.** State the difference between blue hydrogen and green hydrogen. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** ways of insulating a house. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the product formed when hydrogen is used as a fuel. **[1]**

**4.** State what is meant by an electrically propelled vehicle. **[1]**

**5.** Name **two** strategies from the syllabus that reduce the energy used for travel. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how a ground source heat pump provides heating for a building. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how battery storage helps a country rely more on wind and solar power. **[3]**

**8.** *Invented data.* A family's yearly heating bill was 900 currency units. After fitting roof insulation
costing 1350 units, the bill fell to 630 units.

(a) Calculate the yearly saving and the percentage reduction in the bill.

(b) Calculate the payback time for the insulation.

(c) Suggest **one** reason why some families do not fit insulation even though it saves money. **[6]**

**9.** A government wants people to buy energy-efficient appliances and electrically propelled vehicles.
Describe **three** ways education could help. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why replacing petrol cars with electric cars may not reduce carbon dioxide emissions in a
country that generates most of its electricity from coal. **[3]**

**11.** A country with large natural gas reserves and few renewable resources is deciding whether to
produce blue or green hydrogen. Discuss the benefits and limitations of each choice. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of air source heat pumps for heating homes. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Blue hydrogen is produced from natural gas [1]; green hydrogen is produced using renewable
resources [1].

**2.** Any two: roof (loft) insulation [1]; cavity wall insulation [1]; double or triple glazing [1];
draught-proofing doors and windows [1].

**3.** Water [1].

**4.** A vehicle driven by electric motors, such as an electric car, bus or train, or a hybrid [1].

**5.** Any two: transport policies [1]; energy-efficient vehicles including electrically propelled
vehicles [1]; reducing consumption, for example making fewer journeys [1]; education on energy
conservation [1].

**6.** Any three:

- Pipes are buried in the ground near the building [1].
- Fluid circulating in the pipes absorbs heat from the ground, which stays at a fairly steady temperature
  [1].
- The heat pump, powered by electricity, concentrates the heat [1].
- The heat is transferred into the building's heating system and hot water [1].

**7.** Any three:

- Wind and solar output does not always match demand: solar stops at night, wind drops when calm [1].
- Batteries store surplus electricity when generation is greater than demand [1].
- The stored electricity is released when demand is high or generation is low [1].
- So fewer fossil fuel power stations are needed as back-up [1].

**8.** (a) Saving = 900 - 630 = **270 units** [1]. Percentage = 270 / 900 x 100 = **30%** [1].
(b) 1350 / 270 = **5 years** [1 for the working, 1 for the answer].
(c) One reason [1] with an explanation of how it stops insulation being fitted [1], for example:

- The upfront cost is high [1], so a family with little savings cannot pay before the savings arrive [1].
- The family rents its home [1], so the landlord decides and may not want to pay [1].
- They plan to move before the payback time [1], so they would not get the full saving [1].

**9.** Any three:

- Energy labels on appliances show how much energy each one uses, so buyers can compare [1].
- Campaigns explain the money saved over the life of an efficient appliance or vehicle [1].
- School lessons teach young people why energy conservation matters [1].
- Information about charging points and running costs makes people more willing to buy electric vehicles
  [1].

**10.** Any three:

- Electric cars produce no exhaust emissions where they are driven [1].
- But they must be charged with electricity [1].
- If that electricity comes from burning coal, carbon dioxide is still released at the power station [1].
- Coal releases more carbon dioxide than other fuels for the same electricity, so the overall saving may be
  small [1].

**11.** Blue hydrogen, any three:

- Benefit: uses the country's own natural gas, so production can start quickly and cheaply [1].
- Benefit: hydrogen produces only water when used [1].
- Limitation: natural gas is finite [1].
- Limitation: carbon dioxide is produced and must be captured and stored, which is costly, or it adds to
  climate change [1].

Green hydrogen, any two:

- Benefit: very low emissions in production [1].
- Limitation: needs large amounts of renewable electricity, which this country lacks [1].
- Limitation: currently more expensive to produce [1].

Judgement linked to the country, for example blue hydrogen in the short term with carbon capture, moving
to green as renewables are developed [1].

Maximum 6 marks.

**12.** Benefits, any three:

- Delivers more heat than the electrical energy it uses, so energy use falls [1].
- No fuel is burned in the home, so there are no local emissions [1].
- Easier and cheaper to fit than a ground source heat pump, because no pipes need burying [1].
- Low carbon if the electricity comes from renewable resources [1].

Limitations, any three:

- High cost to buy and install [1].
- Less effective in very cold weather [1].
- Needs electricity, so emissions depend on how it is generated [1].
- The outdoor unit can be noisy and needs space [1].

Maximum 6 marks; at least one benefit and one limitation for full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping blue and green hydrogen.
- In 8(a), dividing the saving by 630 instead of the original 900.
- Saying batteries generate electricity.
- Treating electric cars as zero-carbon whatever the electricity source.
- Giving benefits only in questions 11 and 12.
