## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define diseconomies of scale. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is an external economy of scale?

A: A discount for buying raw materials in bulk

B: A local college training technicians for the industry

C: A lower interest rate on a large firm's loan

D: Employing specialist managers **[1]**

**3.** The table shows long-run costs for an invented furniture maker.

| Output (units a year) | 500 | 1,000 | 2,000 | 4,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost (£) | 10,000 | 16,000 | 28,000 | 64,000 |

**(a)** Calculate long-run average cost at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** State the range of output over which the firm experiences diseconomies of scale. **[1]**

**4.** A firm increases its scale. Output rises by 40% and total cost rises by 25%. State whether the firm is experiencing economies or diseconomies of scale and explain your answer. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between internal and external economies of scale. **[2]**

**6.** A small firm pays 8% interest on a £5 million loan. A large firm pays 5.5% on the same loan. Calculate the large firm's annual saving. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Explain two internal economies of scale a large car manufacturer might gain. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two causes of diseconomies of scale in a firm that has grown rapidly by opening many new branches. **[4]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the difference between the effect of an internal economy of scale and the effect of an external economy of scale on a firm's costs. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how specialisation and the division of labour can be a source of economies of scale. **[3]**

**11.** Annual sales in a market are 100,000 units. The minimum efficient scale is 40,000 units. Explain what this suggests about the number of firms that can produce at lowest cost. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate whether economies of scale always benefit consumers. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Diseconomies of scale are rises in long-run average cost [1] that occur when a firm increases its scale beyond a certain size [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The saving comes from the growth of the industry in the area, not of one firm; A, C and D are internal economies. **[1]**

**3. (a)** LRAC = £20 and £16 at 500 and 1,000 units [1]; £14 and £16 at 2,000 and 4,000 units [1].

**(b)** From **2,000 to 4,000** units, where LRAC rises from £14 to £16 [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Economies of scale [1], because total cost rises by a smaller percentage than output, so cost per unit falls [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Internal economies result from the growth of the firm itself, such as bulk buying [1]. External economies result from the growth of the industry or area, such as a local pool of skilled labour, and benefit all firms there [1]. **[2]**

**6.** 5,000,000 × (0.08 − 0.055) = **£125,000** a year [1]. **[1]**

**7.** Technical economies: a large manufacturer can afford automated production lines that produce many cars per hour [1], so the cost of capital per car falls as output rises [1]. Purchasing economies: buying steel and components in very large quantities gives bargaining power with suppliers [1], lowering the input cost per car [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each economy needs a mechanism and an effect on average cost; naming two types alone earns little.

**8.** Communication problems: more branches and management layers mean messages pass through many people [1], so decisions are slow and mistakes raise costs per unit [1]. Control problems: head office finds it harder to monitor many branches [1], so waste and poor practices go unnoticed and average cost rises [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Diagram with LRAC against output [1]. An internal economy is a movement down along the firm's LRAC curve as it increases its own output [1]. An external economy is a downward shift of the whole LRAC curve, labelled LRAC1 to LRAC2 [1], because costs fall at every output when the industry grows, whatever the firm's own size [1]. **[4]**

**10.** In a large firm, work can be split into narrow tasks [1], so each worker becomes faster and more skilled at one task and less time is lost switching between tasks [1]. More output from the same labour lowers average cost, which is a technical economy of scale available only at high output [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Each firm must produce 40,000 units to reach lowest cost [1]. The market of 100,000 units can support at most two firms at that scale [1], so the market is likely to be dominated by a few large firms, and smaller firms face higher costs [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Economies of scale reduce long-run average cost [1], so firms can charge lower prices [1].
- Lower costs may fund investment in better products or new technology [1].
- Firms with low costs can compete with foreign rivals, protecting choice and jobs [1].
- But firms may keep the savings as profit rather than cut prices [1], especially where a large MES means few competitors [1].
- Large firms may use market power to raise prices or reduce choice [1].
- Growth beyond MES brings diseconomies, so costs and prices may rise [1].
- Standardised products from very large firms may reduce variety [1].
- Judgement: consumers benefit when competition is strong enough to pass on lower costs, but not where scale leads to market dominance [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest judgements identify the condition that decides the outcome, here the strength of competition.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining an economy of scale without saying how it lowers cost per unit.
- Confusing total and average cost.
- Mixing up internal and external economies.
- Using diminishing returns and diseconomies of scale as if they were the same thing.
- Claiming large firms always charge lower prices.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the easiest way to spot economies of scale in data?**
Compare the percentage change in total cost with the percentage change in output, or calculate average cost for each output. If cost per unit falls as scale rises, the firm is gaining economies of scale.

**Why do diseconomies of scale happen?**
They are mainly managerial: as firms grow, communication, co-ordination and motivation become harder, so waste rises and average cost increases.
