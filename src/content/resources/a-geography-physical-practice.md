## Section A

**1.** Define the drainage basin hydrological cycle and state whether it is an open or closed system, explaining why. **[3]**

**2.** Name the inputs, stores, flows and outputs of a drainage basin, giving one example of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain the factors affecting the shape of a storm hydrograph, referring to **four** factors. **[8]**

**4.** Explain how the energy budget of a ground surface differs between day and night, referring to the components of the diurnal energy budget. **[8]**

**5.** Explain the difference between constructive, destructive and conservative plate margins, giving a landform or hazard associated with each. **[9]**

**6.** With reference to an urban area you have studied, evaluate the extent to which human activity has changed its climate. **[12]**

**7.** Explain what the Hjulström curve shows, and describe the counter-intuitive feature of its treatment of fine clay. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The **movement of water through a river basin, from precipitation to its output as river discharge or evaporation** [1]. It is an **open system** [1], because **energy and matter cross its boundaries** — precipitation enters from outside and water leaves as evaporation and channel flow [1].

**2. Input** — **precipitation** [1]. **Stores** — soil moisture, groundwater, interception, surface storage [1]. **Flows** — throughflow, infiltration, percolation, overland flow, baseflow [1]. **Outputs** — **evapotranspiration and river discharge into the sea** [1].

**3.** Any four, 2 marks each: **Rainfall intensity and duration** — intense rain exceeds the infiltration capacity, so more water reaches the channel quickly as overland flow, giving a **shorter lag time and higher peak** [1] [1]. **Soil and rock type** — impermeable clay or granite prevents infiltration, producing a **flashy hydrograph**, whereas permeable chalk gives a **flatter one with a longer lag** [1] [1]. **Basin relief** — steep slopes accelerate overland flow, shortening lag time [1] [1]. **Vegetation** — dense woodland **intercepts rainfall and increases evapotranspiration**, reducing and delaying peak discharge; deforestation does the opposite [1] [1]. **Urbanisation** — impermeable surfaces and storm drains deliver water to the channel almost immediately, giving a very short lag and high peak [1] [1]. **Antecedent conditions** — saturated soil from earlier rain leaves no storage capacity [1] [1].

**4.** Up to 4 marks for the daytime budget and up to 4 marks for the night-time budget, 1 mark per valid point.
**Day (max 4):** **incoming shortwave solar radiation** is the main energy input, greatest around midday [1]; part is **reflected**, the proportion depending on the surface **albedo** (high for snow or pale surfaces, low for dark soil or tarmac) [1]; energy is **absorbed into the surface and conducted into the subsurface**, warming the ground [1]; **sensible heat transfer** moves heat from the warm surface into the air by conduction and convection [1]; **latent heat transfer** uses energy to evaporate water, cooling a moist surface [1]; the warmed surface also emits **longwave radiation** [1].
**Night (max 4):** with no solar input, the surface loses energy mainly by **net emission of longwave radiation**, so it cools [1]; **cloud cover** absorbs and re-radiates longwave radiation back towards the surface, so cloudy nights cool less than clear nights [1]; heat stored during the day is conducted back upwards as **absorbed energy returned to the surface** [1]; **sensible heat transfer** now runs from the warmer air to the cooler surface [1]; as the surface cools to dew point, **dew forms and releases latent heat**, slowing further cooling [1].

**5. Constructive (divergent)** — plates **move apart** as magma rises from the mantle, forming new crust [1]; landforms include **mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys** [1]; hazards are **shallow-focus, low-magnitude earthquakes and effusive basaltic volcanoes** — e.g. Iceland [1].
**Destructive (convergent)** — the **denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the less dense continental plate** [1]; landforms include **ocean trenches, fold mountains and island arcs** [1]; hazards are **deep-focus, high-magnitude earthquakes and explosive andesitic volcanoes** — e.g. the Andes [1].
**Conservative (transform)** — plates **slide past one another with no crust created or destroyed** [1]; **friction causes stress to build until it is released suddenly** [1]; the hazard is **large shallow-focus earthquakes with no volcanic activity** — e.g. the San Andreas Fault [1].

**6.** The syllabus case study covers the effects of human activity on an urban area's temperature (heat island), humidity, precipitation and winds. Marks are capped per element as shown; maximum 12.
**Temperature — urban heat island (max 3):** the urban area is **warmer than the surrounding rural area**, with the difference usually greatest on **calm, clear nights** [1]; dark, low-albedo surfaces and **concrete, brick and tarmac absorb and store heat by day and release it at night** [1]; **heat from vehicles, industry, heating and air conditioning** adds energy, impermeable surfaces leave little water for evaporation so less energy is used as latent heat, and polluted air absorbs outgoing longwave radiation [1].
**Humidity (max 2):** **relative humidity is usually lower** in the urban area because rapid drainage and little vegetation reduce evapotranspiration [1]; the higher urban temperatures also lower relative humidity [1].
**Precipitation (max 2):** extra heat and the uplift of air over tall buildings can **strengthen convection, giving more convectional rainfall and thunderstorms**, often downwind of the city [1]; **pollution particles act as condensation nuclei**, encouraging cloud and rain formation [1].
**Winds (max 2):** buildings increase **surface roughness, lowering mean wind speeds** [1]; but **tall buildings channel and accelerate air between them (the canyon effect)**, producing gusts and eddies [1].
**Evaluation and judgement (max 3):** supports the answer with **named, located detail from the case study**, such as a measured urban–rural temperature difference [1]; recognises that the **extent varies** — the heat island is strongest at night and in calm anticyclonic conditions and weaker in windy, cloudy weather, and varies with the size and density of the built-up area [1]; reaches a **reasoned overall judgement**, for example that temperature is the most clearly changed element, while changes in precipitation are smaller and harder to separate from the regional climate [1].

**7.** The **Hjulström curve** shows the relationship between a river's **velocity** and whether a given particle size is **eroded, transported or deposited** [1]. It has one counter-intuitive feature: **fine clay needs a surprisingly high velocity to be entrained** (picked up), higher than for sand, because clay particles are small and **cohesive**, sticking together and resisting erosion [1] [1]; once suspended, though, clay stays in transport at very low velocities and so is deposited only when flow is almost still [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing infiltration with percolation.
- Describing hydrograph factors without stating the effect on lag time and peak.
- Describing the night-time energy budget as if solar radiation still applied, or forgetting that dew formation releases latent heat.
- Describing the urban heat island without explaining its causes or the conditions in which it is strongest.
