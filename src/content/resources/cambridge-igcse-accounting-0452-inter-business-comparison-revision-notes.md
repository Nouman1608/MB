Condensed recall for **6.3 Inter-business comparison**. The syllabus asks for two things: *the factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses* and *the problems of inter-business comparison*. Figures below are invented; amounts in dollars.

## The method in four steps

1. **Calculate** the same ratio for both businesses, using only the syllabus formulas.
2. **Compare**: which is higher, and by how much.
3. **Explain** the gap with a factor from the case (type of goods, finance, premises and so on).
4. **Qualify**: state a problem that limits the comparison, then give a judgement.

## Factors that move the ratios

| Factor | What to say | Ratio it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Type of goods or service | Perishable or everyday goods sell quickly at low margins | Gross profit margin; inventory turnover |
| Pricing policy | Discount pricing lifts volume but cuts margin | Gross profit margin; profit margin |
| Size and buying power | Bulk discounts lower cost of sales; suppliers may allow longer credit | Gross profit margin; trade payables days |
| Location | City-centre rent and wages raise expenses | Profit margin |
| Cash or credit sales | Credit customers create trade receivables | Trade receivables days; acid test |
| Method of finance | A loan adds interest and adds to capital employed | ROCE; profit for the year |
| Owned or rented premises | Rent is an expense; owned premises add to capital employed | Profit margin; ROCE |
| Age of non-current assets | Old assets have low book values and little depreciation | ROCE; profit margin |
| Accounting policies | Depreciation method, inventory valuation, allowance for irrecoverable debts | Profit margin; ROCE; current ratio |

**Quick illustration.** Two cafés each have revenue of $200,000 and identical trading. Café A owns its building; Café B pays rent of $18,000. Café B's profit margin is 18,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **9 percentage points** lower, with no difference in how well either is run.

## Problems of inter-business comparison

- **Information not available**: sole traders and partnerships do not publish accounts; summaries may omit credit sales, credit purchases or opening inventory.
- **Different accounting policies**: same transactions, different profit and asset figures.
- **Different year-end dates**: seasonal businesses show very different inventory and receivables at different dates.
- **Not truly alike**: size, product range, location, premises, finance.
- **Historic cost**: assets bought in different years at different prices distort ROCE.
- **Untypical year**: a one-off event in one business.
- **Non-financial aspects ignored**: staff skill, reputation, local economy.
- **Ratios show results, not causes**: further information is needed to explain them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inter-business comparison | Comparing the ratios of one business with those of another business, usually in the same trade |
| Yardstick | A standard against which a result is judged |
| Capital employed | Issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities (syllabus definition) |
| Accounting policy | The method a business chooses to apply, such as a depreciation method |
| Year-end date | The last day of the financial year to which the statements are drawn up |
| Untypical year | A year affected by an unusual event, so its results are not representative |
| Non-financial aspect | Something that affects performance but is not measured in money |

## Exam traps

- **One-ratio verdicts.** "Business X is better because its ROCE is higher" earns little. Look for an explanation in finance or premises first.
- **Same ratio, different make-up.** Two current ratios of 2.00 : 1 can hide very different acid test ratios. Check the inventory.
- **ROCE numerator.** Profit for the year **before** interest. Add interest back for the business with a loan.
- **Trade receivables days.** A business that sells on credit to trade customers will always have more days than a cash retailer. Compare with the credit period it allows.
- **Generic problems.** "Different policies" scores more when you name one that fits the case, such as straight line against reducing balance depreciation.
- **No judgement.** Evaluate and Advise questions need a conclusion.
- **Wrong formula.** Only the syllabus formulas are accepted, so use credit sales for receivables days and average inventory for inventory turnover.

## Linking factors to a likely examiner prompt

| Prompt in the question | Factor to use |
|---|---|
| "One business rents its premises" | Owned or rented premises; ROCE and profit margin |
| "Financed by a bank loan" | Method of finance; interest and capital employed |
| "Year ends 31 March and 30 September" | Different year-end dates |
| "Sells to trade customers on 60 days' credit" | Cash or credit sales |
| "Belongs to a national chain" | Size and buying power |
| "Bought its machinery ten years ago" | Age of assets; historic cost |

## Self-test

1. State the two points the syllabus lists for 6.3.
2. Give two factors that could explain why one business's gross profit margin is higher than another's.
3. Business P rents its premises and Business Q owns them. Which ratios are affected, and how?
4. Why might a supermarket have a much faster rate of inventory turnover than a furniture shop?
5. Business R has a loan of $50,000 at 8% interest; Business S has no loan. How does this affect the comparison of profit for the year and ROCE?
6. Two businesses have the same current ratio. Why might their acid test ratios still differ?
7. Explain why different year-end dates are a problem for inter-business comparison.
8. Why are sole traders' ratios often impossible to compare with a rival's?
9. One business uses straight line and the other reducing balance depreciation on identical new equipment. What is the effect on profit in the first year?
10. How does historic cost cause a problem when comparing ROCE?
11. Give two non-financial aspects that ratios ignore.
12. A café's trade receivables turnover is 2 days and a catering supplier's is 45 days. Does this show the café has better credit control?

**Answers:** 1. The factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses; the problems of inter-business comparison. 2. Any two: lower purchase prices from bulk buying; a higher-price policy; selling goods with a naturally higher margin. 3. P pays rent, which lowers its profit margin; Q's premises add to capital employed, which lowers its ROCE. 4. Groceries sell quickly and are often perishable, while furniture is bought rarely and held longer. 5. R pays $4,000 interest (50,000 × 8%), so its profit for the year is lower; for ROCE the interest is added back, but the loan increases R's capital employed, so R's ROCE is lower for the same profit before interest. 6. One may hold much more inventory, which the acid test excludes. 7. Inventory, receivables and payables vary through the year, so figures at different dates are not like for like. 8. They do not publish their accounts, so the information is not available. 9. Reducing balance usually gives the larger charge in year 1, so that business shows lower profit. 10. Assets bought at different times are recorded at the prices of those years, so capital employed is not measured on the same basis. 11. Any two: workforce skill; reputation; location; customer loyalty; economic climate. 12. No. The café sells mainly for cash, while the supplier sells on credit; the difference comes from the type of business. Compare each with its own credit terms.
