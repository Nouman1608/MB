## 3.4.1 Definition

A **trade union** is an organisation of workers formed to protect and promote the interests of its members, especially their pay, working hours, working conditions and job security. Members pay a subscription, which funds the union's officials, legal advice and support during disputes.

## 3.4.2 The role of trade unions

### Collective bargaining

**Collective bargaining** is negotiation between a union, on behalf of all its members, and an employer or group of employers. A single worker who asks for a pay rise can easily be refused or replaced; a union speaking for most of the workforce cannot.

Bargaining covers the three areas the syllabus names:

- **Wages:** pay rises, overtime rates, bonuses.
- **Working hours:** length of the working week, shift patterns, holidays.
- **Working conditions:** health and safety, equipment, breaks, training.

A typical sequence:

1. The union presents a **claim**, often justified by rising prices, higher profits or higher productivity.
2. The employer makes an **offer**, usually lower.
3. Both sides negotiate, trading items such as pay against hours or new working methods.
4. If talks fail, the union may take **industrial action**: a strike (refusing to work), an overtime ban, or a work-to-rule (doing only the minimum the contract requires).
5. A **settlement** is reached, sometimes with help from an independent third party.

**Worked example 1 (invented).** A bus company employs 250 drivers on 600 a week. The union claims 7%; the company offers 2%. The union threatens a 5-day strike. During a strike the company would lose fares of 40,000 a day but would not pay wages.

```
Weekly wage bill now           = 250 x 600 = 150,000
Yearly cost of the 7% claim    = 150,000 x 0.07 x 52 = 546,000
Yearly cost of the 2% offer    = 150,000 x 0.02 x 52 = 156,000
Gap between claim and offer    = 390,000 a year
Strike: fares lost             = 5 x 40,000 = 200,000
        wages saved            = 150,000 (5 working days = one week's pay)
        net loss to company    = 50,000, a one-off
Drivers' lost pay from strike  = 600 each
```

The company might accept a short strike, a one-off loss of 50,000, rather than pay an extra 390,000 every year. But if passengers switch to other transport after the strike, its losses continue. This is why both sides usually settle between the claim and the offer, for example at 4%, which costs 312,000 a year.

### Productivity agreements

A union may accept new machines or flexible shifts in return for higher pay. If output per worker rises by as much as the wage, labour cost per unit does not rise.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** Workers each produce 200 units a week for 500. A deal raises pay by 10% to 550 and output to 230 units.

```
Labour cost per unit before = 500 / 200 = 2.50
Labour cost per unit after  = 550 / 230 = 2.39
```

Pay rises, yet labour cost per unit falls by 4.3%: both sides gain.

### Protecting employment

Unions try to protect jobs by:

- resisting or negotiating over redundancies and closures;
- arguing for retraining when new technology arrives;
- representing members who face unfair dismissal or discipline;
- pressing for safe workplaces, which protects workers' health and ability to work.

### Influencing government policy

Unions lobby governments, campaign publicly and give evidence to official inquiries. They may press for a higher minimum wage, stronger health and safety laws, limits on working hours, better public services or measures to reduce unemployment.

## 3.4.3 Union strength

A union is strong when the employer cannot easily carry on without its members.

| Factor | Strong union | Weak union |
|---|---|---|
| Membership | A high share of the workforce belongs | Few workers belong |
| Skills | Members have scarce skills that are hard to replace | Members are easily replaced |
| Unemployment | Low, so strikers are hard to replace | High, so many people would take the jobs |
| Employer's finances | Profitable firm that can afford pay rises | Firm making losses |
| Demand for the product | High and rising | Falling |
| Effect of action | Strike stops production quickly and is costly | Firm can use stock, machines or other sites |
| Law | Allows unions to organise and take action | Restricts action |
| Funds and unity | Large strike funds; members united | Small funds; members divided |

**Worked example 3 (invented).** Compare two unions.

| | Union P (airport air-traffic controllers) | Union Q (fruit pickers) |
|---|---|---|
| Members / workforce | 540 / 600 | 900 / 6,000 |
| Membership share | 90% | 15% |
| Training needed | Several years | A few days |
| Effect of a one-day strike | Flights cannot operate | Some fruit picked later by non-members |

Union P is far stronger: almost every controller belongs, they cannot quickly be replaced and a strike stops the service. Union Q's members are a small share of an easily replaced workforce.

## Advantages and disadvantages: three viewpoints

The syllabus asks you to judge union activity from each viewpoint. Each point should be developed into a consequence.

### Workers

- **For:** higher pay and better conditions than individuals could win alone; protection against unfair treatment; a voice in changes at work.
- **Against:** subscriptions cost money; strikes mean lost pay; if unions push wages well above the market level, employers may hire fewer workers, so some people who want jobs cannot find them.

### Firms

- **For:** one set of negotiations instead of hundreds; a channel to explain changes and hear problems early; better-treated staff may be more productive and less likely to leave.
- **Against:** higher labour costs reduce profit or force price rises; strikes lose output and customers; resistance to new technology may slow productivity growth.

### Government

- **For:** unions can help reduce poverty among low-paid workers and push for safer workplaces; they provide information about conditions in industries.
- **Against:** strikes in essential services such as hospitals or transport disrupt the economy; large pay rises can push up costs and prices, conflicting with the aim of stable prices; pressure for more spending.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Discuss whether trade unions benefit firms. [6]

> Unions allow a firm to agree pay and conditions with one body, saving management time [benefit]. They can communicate workers' concerns early, so problems are solved before staff leave, reducing recruitment costs [benefit, developed]. However, unions may win pay rises above what the firm would otherwise pay, raising costs and reducing profit [cost]. Strikes stop production and may lose customers permanently [cost, developed]. Overall, unions benefit firms when they agree productivity deals that keep labour cost per unit stable, but harm them when they resist change in a firm facing strong competition [judgement with conditions].

## Common mistakes

- Defining a trade union as "a group of workers" without stating its purpose.
- Treating collective bargaining as the same as striking. Most bargaining ends without industrial action.
- Giving only the workers' viewpoint when a question names firms or government.
- Assuming unions are always strong. Use the factors to judge the case in the question.
- Saying unions always cause unemployment. That depends on how far wages rise above the market level and on the firm's finances.
