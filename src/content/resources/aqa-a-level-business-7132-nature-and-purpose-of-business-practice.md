These questions cover section 3.1.1 Understanding the nature and purpose of business of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [nature and purpose study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business/) and the [nature and purpose revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be a variable cost for a bakery? **[1]**

A: Rent of the shop

B: Flour

C: Insurance of the premises

D: The manager's annual salary

**2.** A business sells 3,000 units at £20 each. Variable cost is £8 per unit and fixed costs are £30,000. What is its profit? **[1]**

A: £6,000

B: £30,000

C: £36,000

D: £60,000

**3.** Explain how a business's mission can influence the objectives it sets. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why the owners of a business might aim for a satisfactory profit rather than the maximum possible profit. **[3]**

**5.** A business has total costs of £84,000, of which £36,000 are fixed. It produces and sells 8,000 units at £12 each.

**(a)** Calculate its variable cost per unit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its profit. **[1]**

**6.** Explain **one** limitation of setting business objectives. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Fenwick Candles sells 3,000 candles a month at £12 each. Variable cost is £4.50 per candle and fixed costs are £18,000 a month.

**(a)** Calculate Fenwick's monthly profit. **[2]**

**(b)** The owner plans to raise the price by 10% and expects sales to fall by 8%. Calculate the new monthly profit. **[3]**

**(c)** Analyse why Fenwick's profit is so sensitive to changes in price. **[6]**

**8.** Thistle Robotics is two years old. Its sales are growing quickly, but it has only enough cash to cover four months of costs. Its investors want it to double its sales within three years. Assess whether survival or growth should be Thistle Robotics' main objective. **[9]**

**9.** Kindred Care Ltd runs three care homes. Its mission is "to give every resident the dignity of a home". The owners have set an objective to increase profit by 20% next year, and managers plan to reduce the number of care staff on each shift. Analyse how this objective could conflict with Kindred Care's mission. **[6]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons, other than profit, why someone might set up a business. **[4]**

**11.** A supermarket chain sets the objective of reducing food waste. Explain how it could make this objective measurable and why doing so is useful. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether profit is the most important objective for a business. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Flour use rises and falls with the number of loaves baked; the others stay the same whatever the output. **[1]**

**2.** **A** [1]. Revenue £60,000 − total costs (30,000 + 24,000 = £54,000) = £6,000. Option D is revenue, and option C ignores fixed costs. **[1]**

**3.** The mission sets out the broad purpose of the business [1], so the objectives are chosen to move it towards that purpose [1]. For example, a mission based on affordable products leads to objectives about lowering unit costs and increasing volume, while a mission based on luxury leads to objectives about quality and price premiums [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Maximising profit may require more risk, longer hours or decisions the owners find unethical, such as cutting staff pay [1]. A satisfactory profit gives them an acceptable return [1] while leaving room for other aims they value, such as a good work-life balance, staff loyalty or social goals [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Variable costs = 84,000 − 36,000 = £48,000; 48,000 ÷ 8,000 = **£6 per unit** [1].

**(b)** Revenue = 8,000 × 12 = £96,000; profit = 96,000 − 84,000 = **£12,000** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** An objective can quickly become out of date if conditions change [1], for example a growth target set before a recession [1]. If managers keep chasing it, they may take risks or spend cash that the business needs to survive (also credit: unrealistic targets demotivate; narrow targets distort behaviour) [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Revenue = 3,000 × 12 = £36,000; variable costs = 3,000 × 4.50 = £13,500 [1]; profit = 36,000 − 13,500 − 18,000 = **£4,500** [1].

**(b)** New price = £13.20; new sales = 3,000 × 0.92 = 2,760 [1]. Revenue = 2,760 × 13.20 = £36,432; variable costs = 2,760 × 4.50 = £12,420 [1]. Profit = 36,432 − 12,420 − 18,000 = **£6,012** [1].

**(c)** Fixed costs of £18,000 are large compared with profit of £4,500 [1], so profit is only a small amount left after costs are covered [1]. A price rise adds to revenue without adding to costs, so most of the extra revenue becomes profit [1]; here a 10% rise lifts profit by about a third even after an 8% fall in sales [1]. However, the gain depends on the forecast fall in sales; if customers are more price-sensitive than expected, lost sales could wipe out the gain [1], because each candle lost removes £7.50 (or £8.70 at the new price) that was covering fixed costs [1]. **[11]**

*Examiner insight:* Analysis marks come from explaining why the numbers behave as they do, not from repeating them.

**8.** For survival: with four months of cash, a delay in customer payments or a fall in orders could force Thistle to close [1], so protecting cash flow and controlling costs should come first [1]. Fast growth usually needs cash for stock, staff and equipment before revenue arrives [1], which could make the cash shortage worse [1]. For growth: sales are already rising quickly, so the market seems to want its products [1], and meeting the investors' target may persuade them to provide more finance, which would itself secure survival [1]. Missing the growth opportunity could let rivals take the market [1]. Judgement: survival should be the main objective in the short term, but it depends on whether investors will fund growth [1]; if new finance is agreed, Thistle can move to growth while keeping a minimum cash balance as a target [1]. **[9]**

**9.** The profit objective may be met partly by cutting costs, and staff wages are a major cost in care homes [1]. Fewer staff on each shift means each resident receives less time and attention [1], which goes against giving residents "the dignity of a home" [1]. If quality falls, families may move residents elsewhere and Kindred's reputation could suffer [1], reducing occupancy and revenue [1], so the cost cut could fail to deliver the extra profit and damage both the mission and the objective [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Reason 1: independence [1]; the owner can make their own decisions and set their own hours rather than working for an employer [1]. Reason 2: a social aim [1], such as creating jobs in their community or tackling a problem they care about, where trading is the means of funding it (also credit: turning a hobby or passion into work, spotting a gap in the market, family tradition) [1]. **[4]**

**11.** It could set a specific target with a time limit [1], for example to cut the tonnes of food thrown away by 30% within two years, measured from store records [1]. This is useful because managers can check progress against the target [1], and staff know exactly what they are aiming for, which helps motivation and makes managers accountable (also credit: it allows the result to be reported to customers and investors) [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For profit: it rewards owners for their risk and pays dividends [1]; retained profit finances investment [1]; and without profit over time a private sector business cannot survive [1]. Against: for a new or struggling business, survival and cash flow come first, since a profitable firm can still fail through lack of cash [1]. Growth may matter more when a market is expanding, even if it reduces profit for a while [1]. A social enterprise exists for its social aim, using profit only as a means [1], and many businesses face pressure from customers and staff to meet social and ethical objectives [1]. Which objective matters most depends on the business's age, ownership and the market it is in [1]. Judgement: profit is the most important long-run objective for most private sector businesses [1], because the other objectives depend on it [1]; but in the short run survival or cash flow often take priority [1], and for social enterprises profit is a means rather than the goal [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* An evaluation scores highly when the judgement is conditional, explaining when the answer would change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing revenue rather than profit in calculation questions.
- Ignoring fixed costs when working out profit.
- Treating mission and objectives as the same thing.
- Listing objectives without saying which should come first for the business in the case.
- Repeating calculated figures instead of explaining what drives them.
- Evaluations with no judgement, or a judgement with no condition.
