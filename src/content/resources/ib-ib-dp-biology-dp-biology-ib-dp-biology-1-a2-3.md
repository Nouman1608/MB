## A2.3.1 What every virus has, and lacks

Five features are shared by all viruses:

- **small, fixed size**: viruses do not grow, and most are measured in tens to hundreds of nanometres;
- **nucleic acid** as genetic material, either DNA or RNA;
- a **capsid** made of protein units;
- **no cytoplasm**;
- **few or no enzymes**.

The lack of cytoplasm and enzymes is the key to everything else in A2.3. A virus cannot make ATP, synthesise proteins or copy its genome on its own. It must use the metabolism of a host cell, so every virus is an obligate intracellular parasite.

## A2.3.2 Diversity: comparing the named viruses

Beyond the shared features, viruses vary in three ways: the type of nucleic acid, whether it is single- or double-stranded, and whether the capsid is surrounded by an envelope derived from host membrane.

| Feature | Bacteriophage lambda | Coronaviruses | HIV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Host | Bacteria (*Escherichia coli*) | Animal cells, including human | Human cells, mainly helper T cells |
| Genetic material | Double-stranded DNA | Single-stranded RNA | Single-stranded RNA |
| Envelope | None | Yes, from host membrane | Yes, from host membrane |
| Shape | Capsid head with a tail | Roughly spherical with spike proteins | Roughly spherical |
| Notable enzyme carried | — | — | Reverse transcriptase |

**Why the envelope matters.** An enveloped virus leaves the host by budding, wrapping itself in host membrane that carries viral glycoproteins. These glycoproteins, such as the spikes of coronaviruses, bind to receptors on the next host cell. A non-enveloped phage like lambda instead attaches with its tail and injects only its DNA.

**Why reverse transcriptase matters.** HIV's genome is RNA, but it is integrated into the host's DNA. Reverse transcriptase copies the RNA into DNA, which is why HIV is called a retrovirus.

## A2.3.3 and A2.3.4 Lambda's two cycles

Lambda is the guide's example for both cycles. The first stages are shared.

**Shared stages**

1. **Attachment.** The tail binds to a specific receptor protein on the outer surface of the bacterium.
2. **Injection.** The DNA passes through the tail into the cell; the empty capsid stays outside.

The phage DNA then follows one of two paths.

**Lytic cycle**

3. Host enzymes and ribosomes transcribe and translate viral genes, making capsid proteins and viral enzymes.
4. The viral DNA is replicated many times.
5. New capsids assemble and DNA is packaged into them.
6. Viral enzymes break down the cell wall; the cell bursts (lyses), releasing many new phages that infect neighbouring cells.

**Lysogenic cycle**

3. The viral DNA is inserted into the bacterial chromosome at a specific site. The integrated viral DNA is called a **prophage**.
4. A viral repressor protein keeps the genes for the lytic cycle switched off.
5. Each time the bacterium divides, the prophage is copied with the chromosome, so all daughter cells carry it.
6. Conditions that damage the host, such as ultraviolet light, can lead to **induction**: the prophage is cut out of the chromosome and the lytic cycle begins.

| | Lytic | Lysogenic |
|---|---|---|
| Viral DNA | Stays separate and is copied many times | Integrated into host chromosome |
| Host cell | Destroyed | Survives and divides |
| New virions | Released soon after infection | None until induction |
| Number of infected cells | Rises through new infections | Rises through host division |

### Worked example 1: a one-step growth experiment

Phages were added to bacteria, and free phage numbers were counted at intervals. Invented data, phages per mL:

| Time / min | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free phages | 2.0 × 10⁶ | 2.0 × 10⁶ | 2.0 × 10⁶ | 1.6 × 10⁸ | 3.0 × 10⁸ | 3.0 × 10⁸ |

Assume 2.0 × 10⁶ cells per mL were each infected by one phage.

1. Between 0 and 20 min the count does not rise: phages are being made inside the cells but none have been released yet.
2. The rise between 20 and 40 min shows lysis.
3. Phages released = 3.0 × 10⁸ − 2.0 × 10⁶ = 2.98 × 10⁸ per mL; per cell = 2.98 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁶ ≈ **149 phages per cell**.
4. In a lysogenic infection, the count would stay close to the starting value, because the prophage produces no new virions until induction.

## A2.3.5 More than one origin

Viruses are extremely diverse in genome type, structure and replication method. Two observations suggest they did not all arise once:

- Their shared features (capsid, small genome, dependence on a host) are exactly what any obligate parasite would need, so they may have evolved independently by **convergent evolution**.
- Some viral genes closely resemble genes of their hosts, while different groups of viruses share few genes with each other, suggesting separate origins from different cellular lineages.

Hypotheses include viruses arising from fragments of host genetic material that escaped cells, and from cellular parasites that became progressively reduced. All viruses use the same genetic code as cells, which is consistent with their arising from or alongside cellular life rather than before it.

## A2.3.6 Rapid evolution

Viruses evolve quickly because they produce enormous numbers of offspring in a short time, and many RNA viruses copy their genomes with enzymes that lack proofreading, so mutation rates are high. Natural selection then acts strongly, for example through host immune responses and antiviral drugs.

**Influenza.** Mutations in the genes for surface proteins gradually change their shape, so antibodies from previous infection or vaccination bind less well. The influenza genome is in separate segments, and when two strains infect the same cell, segments can be mixed, producing a strain with a new combination of surface proteins in one step.

**HIV.** Reverse transcriptase makes frequent errors and HIV replicates rapidly, so many variants form within one infected person. Variants that resist a drug or escape antibodies are selected.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

*Question (4 marks): Explain why a new influenza vaccine is produced each year, whereas a vaccine against some other viruses lasts for many years.*

> Influenza has a high mutation rate in the genes coding for its surface proteins **[1]**. Changes in these antigens mean antibodies and memory cells produced in response to last year's vaccine no longer bind effectively **[1]**. Natural selection favours variants that escape existing immunity, so the circulating strains change **[1]**. A virus whose surface proteins change little remains recognised by the same memory cells, so one vaccine protects for many years **[1]**.

### Consequences for treatment

| Problem | Response |
|---|---|
| Influenza surface proteins change | Vaccines are reformulated regularly |
| HIV develops resistance to one drug | Several antiretroviral drugs are given together |

Using several drugs at once works because a variant resistant to all of them at the same time is far less likely than a variant resistant to one.

## Common mistakes

- Listing a cell membrane or cytoplasm as a feature of viruses.
- Saying lambda has an envelope or an RNA genome.
- Describing the prophage as free in the cytoplasm.
- Writing that the virus "grows" inside the cell; it is assembled from parts.
- Saying viruses mutate "in order to" escape immunity; mutation is random and selection follows.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Compare and contrast**: use a feature-by-feature comparison of two named viruses or the two cycles.
- **Outline**: give the lytic stages in order.
- **Explain**: link mutation, selection and the outcome, as in worked example 2.
- **Deduce**: read latent period and burst size from growth data.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the evidence for single versus multiple origins.
