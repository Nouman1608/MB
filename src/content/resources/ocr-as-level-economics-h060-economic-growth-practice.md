## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define real GDP. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following shows long run economic growth?

A: A rightward shift of aggregate demand

B: A movement from inside a PPC to a point on it

C: A rightward shift of long run aggregate supply

D: A rise in nominal GDP **[1]**

**3.** Nominal GDP in Country R is 2,100 bn. The price index is 105 (base year = 100). Calculate real GDP. **[1]**

**4.** Real GDP in Country R rises from 2,000 bn to 2,050 bn. Calculate the growth rate. **[1]**

**5.** Over the same year, Country R's population rises from 50.0 million to 50.5 million. Calculate real GDP per capita in each year and its growth rate. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish between a downturn and a recession. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows real GDP (bn) in invented Country P over six quarters.

| Quarter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | 250.0 | 251.5 | 252.0 | 251.0 | 249.8 | 250.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the growth rate in each quarter from quarter 2 to quarter 6. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify when Country P is in recession and state the trough. **[2]**

**(c)** State whether output had recovered by quarter 6, giving a reason. **[1]**

**8.** Nominal GDP in Country Q grows by 6.0% in a year when inflation is 4.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the growth of real GDP. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why nominal GDP growth is a misleading measure of economic growth. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a PPC diagram, how investment in education can lead to long run economic growth. **[4]**

**10.** Explain two consequences of rapid short run growth when an economy is close to full capacity. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether economic growth always raises living standards. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Real GDP is the value of output produced in a country [1] measured at constant prices, so the effect of inflation is removed [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A and B are short run growth, and D may reflect only higher prices. **[1]**

**3.** 2,100 ÷ 105 × 100 = **2,000 bn** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** 50 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **2.5%** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Year 1: 2,000 bn ÷ 50.0 m = **40,000** [1]. Year 2: 2,050 bn ÷ 50.5 m = about **40,594** [1]. Growth = 594 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = about **1.49%** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A downturn is a period when growth slows but real GDP is still rising [1]. A recession is a period when real GDP falls, commonly two consecutive quarters of negative growth [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Quarter 2: +0.60%; quarter 3: +0.20%; quarter 4: −0.40% [1]. Quarter 5: −0.48%; quarter 6: +0.32% [1].

**(b)** Recession in **quarters 4 and 5**, two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP [1]. The trough is **quarter 5** (249.8 bn) [1].

**(c)** No: 250.6 bn in quarter 6 is still below the previous peak of 252.0 bn in quarter 3 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Positive growth after a recession is the start of recovery, not proof that output is back to its peak.

**8. (a)** Real growth = (1.060 ÷ 1.045) − 1 [1] = **1.44%** (approximately 6.0 − 4.5 = 1.5%) [1].

**(b)** Nominal GDP rises when prices rise even if output does not [1], so here most of the 6% rise is inflation and real output grew by under 1.5% [1]. **[4]**

**9.** PPC diagram with an outward shift from PPC1 to PPC2, axes labelled with two goods [1]. Education raises the skills and productivity of the workforce [1], so the same number of workers can produce more output [1]. The productive capacity of the economy rises, so the whole PPC shifts outwards, which is long run growth; the effect takes years to appear [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Inflation: AD rises faster than productive capacity, so firms compete for scarce labour and materials and raise prices [1]; this reduces real incomes and competitiveness [1]. Current account: rising incomes increase spending on imports [1], so the current account balance worsens, especially if domestic firms cannot meet demand [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Growth raises real GDP, so on average more goods and services are available per person [1].
- It creates jobs and raises incomes, reducing unemployment [1], and raises tax revenue for health care and education [1].
- But living standards depend on GDP per capita: if population grows as fast as output, average incomes do not rise [1].
- Gains may be unevenly distributed, so many households see little improvement [1].
- Growth may bring pollution, congestion and longer working hours, which lower quality of life [1].
- Growth driven by credit or a boom may be followed by recession and job losses [1].
- GDP excludes unpaid work, leisure and quality improvements, so it is an imperfect guide [1].
- Judgement: growth is usually necessary for higher living standards [1], but it raises them only when it is sustained, outpaces population growth and is shared widely, without heavy environmental cost [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate total output from per capita and distribution effects; that distinction is where most evaluation marks come from.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal growth as a measure of output.
- Calling a slowdown a recession.
- Saying output has recovered as soon as growth turns positive.
- Showing long run growth with AD.
- Forgetting population when judging living standards.
- Leaving units off answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I calculate real GDP growth from nominal growth and inflation?**
Divide (1 + nominal growth) by (1 + inflation) and subtract 1. Subtracting inflation from nominal growth gives a close approximation.

**Is a fall in the growth rate a recession?**
No. A recession needs real GDP itself to fall. If growth falls from 3% to 1%, output is still rising.
