This guide teaches section 1.5 (Growth and evolution) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. The guide lists internal and external economies and diseconomies of scale and the difference between internal and external growth (both AO2); reasons for businesses to grow and reasons to stay small (AO3); and the external growth methods: mergers and acquisitions (M&As), takeovers, joint ventures, strategic alliances and franchising (AO3).

The definitions and the standard lists of economies of scale are in the [1.5 and 1.6 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-growth-and-evolution-and-multinational-companies-mncs/). This page focuses on the judgements the AO3 points demand: separating one economy of scale from another in figures, deciding whether a business is the right size, choosing between external growth methods for a specific aim, and weighing up a franchise from the franchisee's side. Recall the content with the [1.5 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-5-revision-notes/) and practise with the [1.5 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-5-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented; money is in dollars. Definitions are standard textbook forms.

## Economies of scale: identify the cause in the numbers

An answer gains far more when it shows **which** economy caused a fall in unit cost and by how much.

**Worked example 1: Velda Juice.** Velda bottles fruit juice. It plans to grow from 20,000 to 50,000 bottles a month.

| Item | 20,000 bottles | 50,000 bottles |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit cost per bottle | 0.50 | 0.42 (bulk discount) |
| Other variable cost per bottle | 0.30 | 0.30 |
| Bottling machine lease per month | 8,000 | 14,000 (larger machine) |

Unit cost at 20,000: 0.50 + 0.30 + 8,000/20,000 = 0.80 + 0.40 = **1.20**.

Unit cost at 50,000: 0.42 + 0.30 + 14,000/50,000 = 0.72 + 0.28 = **1.00**.

The total saving is 0.20 per bottle, a fall of 16.7%. Split it by cause:

- **Purchasing economy**: fruit falls from 0.50 to 0.42, saving **0.08** per bottle.
- **Technical economy**: the larger machine costs more in total but less per bottle, 0.40 to 0.28, saving **0.12**.

So the technical economy contributes more. A strong answer would add that the saving depends on actually selling 50,000 bottles; if sales reach only 30,000, the machine cost per bottle would be 14,000/30,000 = 0.47, higher than now.

## Diseconomies: why bigger can cost more

Diseconomies of scale usually come from managing size, not from machines. Three mechanisms are worth explaining in a case.

| Mechanism | How it raises unit cost |
|---|---|
| Communication | More layers of management mean messages are slower and distorted; mistakes are found later |
| Coordination | Several sites or departments pull in different directions; work is duplicated |
| Motivation | Staff feel remote from decisions and less valued; absence and staff turnover rise, adding recruitment and training costs |

External diseconomies come from the area or industry, such as rising rents and wages when too many firms crowd into one district. Always link the diseconomy to evidence in the case, such as "a new regional office" or "falling staff satisfaction".

## Is the business the right size?

There is no single best size. The right size depends on factors you can find in the case.

| Factor | Points toward growth | Points toward staying small |
|---|---|---|
| The market | Large market with standard products | Small niche or personalised products |
| Economies of scale available | High fixed costs that can be spread | Few savings from extra output |
| Owner objectives | Wealth, market power, security | Control, lifestyle, close customer contact |
| Finance | Profits or investors available | Limited funds; growth would mean heavy debt |
| Competition | Rivals are large and compete on price | Rivals are small; customers value service |

Use the table to justify a judgement. A specialist violin maker whose customers value personal attention may lose its advantage if it grows, while a firm selling a standard product against large rivals may not survive unless it grows enough to match their costs.

## Internal or external growth?

Internal (organic) growth uses the firm's own resources; external growth involves another business. When a case asks which route to take, compare them on the aim the business has.

- **Need speed**, for example to beat a rival into a new market: external growth is usually faster.
- **Need to keep the culture and control**: internal growth avoids merging two organizations.
- **Short of money**: internal growth can be paced to retained profit; some external methods (alliances, franchising) also need little capital.
- **Need knowledge the firm lacks**, such as local market knowledge abroad: an external partner supplies it.

## Choosing an external growth method

| Method | Control | Speed | Capital needed | Risk sharing | Ease of exit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Merger / acquisition | High (combined or bought firm) | Fast | High | Low: the buyer carries the risk | Hard |
| Takeover | High | Fast | High, often a premium | Low | Hard |
| Joint venture | Shared | Moderate | Moderate | Shared with partner | Moderate |
| Strategic alliance | Low | Fast | Low | Shared, loosely | Easy |
| Franchising (as franchisor) | Moderate, through the contract | Fast | Low: franchisees invest | Franchisees carry outlet risk | Moderate |

**Worked example 2: Harlow Tea enters a new country.** Harlow Tea wants to sell in Country Z, where it has no presence. It has three proposals, with expected annual profit to Harlow once running.

| Proposal | Harlow's outlay | Expected annual profit to Harlow | Years to recover the outlay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquire Zenda Teas, a local brand | 12.0m | 1.5m | 12.0 / 1.5 = 8.0 |
| 50:50 joint venture with a local distributor | 4.0m | 0.8m (half of 1.6m) | 4.0 / 0.8 = 5.0 |
| Strategic alliance: a supermarket chain stocks Harlow's tea | 0.5m (marketing) | 0.3m | 0.5 / 0.3 = 1.7 |

Analysis: the alliance recovers its cost fastest but is small and depends on one partner, who could end it easily. The acquisition gives full control and an established brand, but it is the slowest to pay back and carries the risk of a culture clash and of paying too much. The joint venture shares cost and risk and brings local knowledge, at the cost of shared control and possible disputes.

Judgement: if Harlow is unsure about demand in Country Z, the alliance is a low-risk test, and success could lead to a joint venture later. If it is confident and wants control, the acquisition becomes more attractive. The answer depends on Harlow's appetite for risk and how much control it needs, and saying so is part of the evaluation.

## Franchising from the franchisee's side

The existing guide shows the franchisor's income. A case may instead ask whether someone should **buy** a franchise.

**Worked example 3: Priya's coffee shop.** Priya can buy a franchise of a known coffee brand or open an independent shop.

| | Franchise | Independent |
|---|---|---|
| Up-front fee | 25,000 | none |
| Royalty | 6% of sales | none |
| Expected annual sales | 300,000 (known brand) | 220,000 |
| Gross margin | 60% | 60% |
| Other annual costs | 120,000 | 120,000 |

Franchise, first year: gross profit 0.6 × 300,000 = 180,000; royalty 18,000; profit before the fee 180,000 − 18,000 − 120,000 = 42,000; after the fee, 17,000.

Independent: gross profit 0.6 × 220,000 = 132,000; profit 132,000 − 120,000 = 12,000.

The franchise earns more even in year one, and 30,000 more each year after the fee is paid. Against that, Priya loses freedom over menu, prices and design, and her result depends on the brand's reputation. The decision rests on the sales forecasts: if the brand added only 40,000 of sales, the royalty and fee would wipe out most of the gain.

## Common mistakes

- Naming an economy of scale without showing how it lowers unit cost in the case.
- Assuming growth always lowers costs; diseconomies and unused capacity can raise them.
- Recommending growth without considering the owners' objectives.
- Treating a joint venture and a strategic alliance as the same; only a joint venture creates a new entity.
- Comparing methods only on cost, ignoring control, speed and risk.
- Ending an evaluation without saying what the judgement depends on.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** and **analyse** (AO2): give the cause, the effect on unit cost or profit, and the link to the case.
- **Discuss**, **evaluate**, **recommend**, **to what extent** (AO3): compare at least two options on criteria from the case, decide, and state the condition that would change the decision.
- **Calculate** (AO4): set out each step, as in the unit cost and payback figures above.
