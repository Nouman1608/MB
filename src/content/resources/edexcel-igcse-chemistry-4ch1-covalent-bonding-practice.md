These questions cover sub-topic 1(g) Covalent bonding of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024): points 1.44 to 1.51, none of which has a C reference. Revise with the [covalent bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-covalent-bonding/) and the [covalent bonding revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-covalent-bonding-revision-notes/). Draw dot-and-cross diagrams on paper, showing outer electrons only.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by a double covalent bond. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why hydrogen atoms form only one covalent bond each. **[1]**

**3.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of hydrogen fluoride, HF. **[2]**

**4.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of methane, CH₄. **[2]**

**5.** State the number of lone pairs in a molecule of water. **[1]**

**6.** Hexane, C₆H₁₄, is a liquid that does not conduct electricity. Explain why it does not conduct. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** This question is about ethane, C₂H₆, and ethene, C₂H₄.

**(a)** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for ethene. **[2]**
**(b)** State one difference between the bonding in ethane and in ethene. **[1]**
**(c)** State the total number of electrons involved in covalent bonds in one molecule of ethane. **[1]**

**8.** Dichloromethane, CH₂Cl₂, is a simple molecular liquid.

**(a)** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for dichloromethane. **[2]**
**(b)** Dichloromethane boils at 40 °C. Explain why its boiling point is low. **[2]**
**(c)** A student writes: "When dichloromethane boils, the C–Cl bonds break." Explain why the student is wrong. **[1]**

**9.** The table shows data for three hydrogen halides.

| Compound | Mr | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| HCl | 36.5 | −85 |
| HBr | 81 | −67 |
| HI | 128 | −35 |

**(a)** Describe the trend in boiling point. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the trend. **[2]**
**(c)** State the physical state of hydrogen bromide at −75 °C. **[1]**

**10.** Explain, in terms of electrostatic attractions, why the atoms in a chlorine molecule stay bonded together. **[2]**

**11.** Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon with giant covalent structures.

**(a)** Explain why both have very high melting points. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the structure of graphite and use it to explain why graphite is soft. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why diamond is very hard. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why graphite, not diamond, is used to make electrodes. **[2]**

**12.** C60 fullerene and diamond are both made only of carbon atoms.

**(a)** Describe the structure of C60 fullerene. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why C60 fullerene is much softer than diamond. **[2]**
**(c)** C60 fullerene is a poor conductor of electricity. Suggest why, compared with graphite. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Two shared pairs of electrons between the same two atoms [1].

**2.** A hydrogen atom has one electron and needs only one more to fill its outer shell (which holds 2) [1].

**3.** One shared pair between H and F, shown as a dot and a cross [1]. Three lone pairs on F, so F has 8 outer electrons [1].

**4.** Four shared pairs, each one dot and one cross, between C and each H [1]. No lone pairs; C has 8 outer electrons and each H has 2 [1].

**5.** Two [1].

**6.** It is made of molecules / it is covalent, so there are no ions [1]. Its electrons are held in covalent bonds, so there are no charged particles free to move [1].

**7. (a)** A C=C double bond (two shared pairs) between the carbon atoms [1]. Two C–H shared pairs on each carbon; no lone pairs [1].
**(b)** Ethane has a C–C single bond; ethene has a C=C double bond [1].
**(c)** 7 shared pairs × 2 = 14 electrons [1].

**8. (a)** Carbon with four shared pairs: two to H and two to Cl [1]. Each Cl has three lone pairs [1].
**(b)** It has a simple molecular structure with weak intermolecular forces between molecules [1]. Little energy is needed to overcome these forces [1].
**(c)** Boiling overcomes the intermolecular forces between molecules; the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break [1].

**9. (a)** Boiling point increases from HCl to HI [1].
**(b)** As Mr increases, the intermolecular forces become stronger [1], so more energy is needed to overcome them [1].
**(c)** Liquid (−75 °C is below its boiling point of −67 °C) [1].
*Marking note:* In (b), "the molecules get heavier" is not enough on its own; the link to stronger intermolecular forces earns the mark.

**10.** The shared pair of electrons is attracted to both nuclei [1]; this electrostatic attraction between the negative electrons and the positive nuclei holds the atoms together [1].

**11. (a)** Many strong covalent bonds must be broken [1], which needs a lot of energy [1].
**(b)** Layers of carbon atoms in hexagons, each carbon bonded to three others [1]. Only weak forces between the layers [1], so the layers can slide over each other [1].
**(c)** Each carbon is covalently bonded to four others [1] in a rigid three-dimensional network of strong bonds [1].
**(d)** Graphite conducts electricity [1] because it has delocalised electrons that can move; diamond has none [1].

**12. (a)** Separate (simple) molecules of 60 carbon atoms in a hollow, ball shape [1].
**(b)** Only weak intermolecular forces hold C60 molecules together [1], whereas diamond has strong covalent bonds throughout its structure [1].
**(c)** Electrons cannot move from one C60 molecule to the next (no delocalised electrons through the solid) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting the lone pairs on the halogen atoms.
- Writing that covalent bonds break on boiling.
- Calling the forces between graphite layers "weak covalent bonds".
- Comparing only one structure in a two-substance question.
- Giving a state without comparing the temperature with the data.
