This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.2 Analysing the existing internal position of a business to assess strengths and weaknesses: financial ratio analysis** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **how to assess the financial performance of a business using balance sheets, income statements and financial ratios**, with ratio analysis covering profitability (return on capital employed), liquidity (current ratio), gearing, and the efficiency ratios payables days, receivables days and inventory turnover
- **the value of financial ratios when assessing performance**, with data analysed over time or in comparison with other businesses.

The Annex to the specification also expects you to calculate and interpret percentages, ratios and index numbers. This page teaches each ratio, how to interpret it, and how to judge how useful ratios are. All businesses and figures are invented.

## The two statements

An **income statement** shows revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit and profit for a period, usually a year. A **balance sheet** shows what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the owners' stake at one date.

| Term used below | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operating profit | Profit before interest and tax |
| Capital employed | Total assets minus current liabilities (equivalently, equity plus non-current liabilities) |
| Current assets | Cash, inventory and receivables, expected to turn into cash within a year |
| Current liabilities | Debts due within a year, including payables and overdrafts |
| Non-current liabilities | Long-term borrowing |
| Receivables | Money owed to the business by customers |
| Payables | Money the business owes to suppliers |

Different textbooks write slightly different formulas. The versions below are standard ones. In an exam, show the formula you use and use the data given.

## The ratios

### Profitability: return on capital employed (ROCE)

**ROCE = operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100**

It shows the profit earned per £100 of long-term funds used. Compare it with the returns available elsewhere, for example the interest rate on borrowing, or the ROCE of competitors. A rising ROCE suggests the business is using its funds more effectively.

### Liquidity: current ratio

**Current ratio = current assets : current liabilities** (current assets ÷ current liabilities, written as a ratio such as 1.8 : 1)

It shows whether the business can pay its short-term debts. A ratio below 1 means current liabilities exceed current assets, which is a warning of cash trouble. A ratio far above 2 may mean cash or inventory is sitting idle. There is no perfect figure, because retailers selling for cash can work with low ratios.

### Gearing

**Gearing = non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100**

It shows how much of the long-term funding comes from borrowing. A business with gearing above about 50% is often described as highly geared. High gearing raises risk because interest must be paid whether or not profit is made, and rising interest rates raise costs. It also gives shareholders a bigger return when profits are good. Low gearing is safer but may mean the business is missing out on growth that borrowing could finance.

### Efficiency ratios

**Receivables days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365**

How long, on average, customers take to pay. Lower is usually better for cash flow. But very strict credit terms may lose customers.

**Payables days = payables ÷ cost of sales × 365**

How long the business takes to pay suppliers. A higher figure helps cash flow, but if it is stretched too far suppliers may withdraw credit or raise prices.

**Inventory turnover = cost of sales ÷ inventory** (number of times a year)

How many times inventory is sold and replaced in a year. A higher figure means stock is moving quickly, with lower storage costs and less risk of unsold stock. Too high may mean stock-outs. Convert to days with 365 ÷ turnover if asked.

## Worked example: Ardent Joinery Ltd

Figures are in £000.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 8,400 | 9,100 |
| Cost of sales | 5,040 | 5,642 |
| Operating profit | 1,050 | 980 |
| Inventory | 900 | 1,260 |
| Receivables | 1,050 | 1,400 |
| Payables | 700 | 900 |
| Current assets | 2,100 | 2,700 |
| Current liabilities | 900 | 1,400 |
| Non-current assets | 5,000 | 6,200 |
| Non-current liabilities | 1,500 | 2,500 |

**Capital employed** = non-current assets + current assets − current liabilities.

- Year 1: 5,000 + 2,100 − 900 = **6,200**
- Year 2: 6,200 + 2,700 − 1,400 = **7,500**

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 | Calculation (Year 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ROCE | 16.9% | 13.1% | 980 ÷ 7,500 × 100 |
| Current ratio | 2.33 : 1 | 1.93 : 1 | 2,700 ÷ 1,400 |
| Gearing | 24.2% | 33.3% | 2,500 ÷ 7,500 × 100 |
| Receivables days | 45.6 days | 56.2 days | 1,400 ÷ 9,100 × 365 |
| Payables days | 50.7 days | 58.2 days | 900 ÷ 5,642 × 365 |
| Inventory turnover | 5.6 times | 4.5 times | 5,642 ÷ 1,260 |

### Interpreting the results

1. **Profitability has weakened.** Revenue rose 8.3% but operating profit fell 6.7%. ROCE fell from 16.9% to 13.1% because capital employed grew by 21% while profit fell. The investment in non-current assets has not yet paid off.
2. **Liquidity has worsened but remains acceptable.** A current ratio of 1.93 : 1 still covers short-term debts, but the fall is a warning.
3. **Gearing has risen** from 24% to 33%. Borrowing of £1m financed part of the expansion. Gearing is still moderate, but lower profit makes interest harder to cover.
4. **Efficiency has weakened in three ways.** Customers take 10.5 days longer to pay, inventory is turning less often, and the business is paying its own suppliers 7.5 days later. Cash is being tied up in inventory and receivables and the company is relying on supplier credit.

**Analysis.** The data suggest Ardent has expanded faster than it can manage. The likely causes are extra inventory built up ahead of sales, generous credit used to win customers, and costs rising faster than revenue. **Judgement.** The position is not yet critical, but if the trend continues the business may face a cash shortage. Management should tighten credit control, reduce inventory, and review whether the new capacity is being used. It would help to know whether Year 2 was a one-off year of investment.

## The value of financial ratios

### Uses

- They turn large numbers into comparable measures, for example allowing a small and a large business to be compared.
- **Over time**, they show trends, such as improving or worsening liquidity.
- **Against other businesses** or industry averages, they show relative strengths and weaknesses.
- They help lenders, investors and managers decide where to act.

### Limits

- **Historic.** They show what has happened, not what will happen.
- **Accounting policies differ.** Different ways of valuing stock or depreciating assets make comparisons less reliable.
- **Year-end distortions.** A balance sheet shows one date. Seasonal businesses may look very different at other times.
- **Context missing.** A ratio does not explain why. Ardent's fall in ROCE may reflect a planned investment.
- **Quantitative only.** Ratios ignore factors such as staff morale, brand strength or product quality.
- **Different industries.** A supermarket and a shipbuilder have very different normal ratios.
- **Single ratios mislead.** Several ratios together tell a better story.

So ratios are a starting point. The strongest assessments combine them with non-financial data (see the guide to overall performance) and with knowledge of the business's plans.

## Linking ratios to strategic decisions

Ratio analysis is not an end in itself. It shows what the business can afford to do.

- **Low gearing and a healthy current ratio** suggest capacity to borrow for expansion or a takeover.
- **Weak ROCE** suggests the business should improve how it uses existing assets before investing more.
- **Poor liquidity with slow receivables** may point to a need for tighter credit control before growth, to avoid overtrading.
- **Rising gearing with falling profit** warns that further borrowing is risky.

When a case asks whether the business should pursue a strategy, use the ratios to judge its financial strength to carry it out.

## Improving the ratios

| To improve | Possible actions | Possible side effect |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | Raise operating profit (prices, cost cuts); use less capital | Lost customers; under-investment |
| Current ratio | Raise cash or cut payables; reduce short-term debt | Idle cash; strained supplier relations |
| Gearing | Repay debt; issue shares | Slower growth; diluted ownership |
| Receivables days | Stricter credit control | Lost sales |
| Payables days | Negotiate longer terms | Supplier anger |
| Inventory turnover | Just-in-time ordering; sell slow lines | Stock-outs |

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute, give the answer with units (%, days, times, or a ratio) and the rounding asked for.
- **Analyse:** say what a change means and why it might have happened, not just that the number went up.
- **Evaluate:** weigh several ratios, note what you cannot tell from them, and reach a judgement with a recommendation if appropriate.

## Common mistakes

- Using profit for the year rather than operating profit in ROCE when the question gives operating profit.
- Dividing payables by revenue instead of cost of sales (or receivables by cost of sales).
- Saying a high current ratio is always good.
- Forgetting that gearing uses non-current liabilities, not all liabilities.
- Stating that a lower receivables figure is always better, ignoring lost sales.
- Describing a ratio without saying what it shows about the business.
