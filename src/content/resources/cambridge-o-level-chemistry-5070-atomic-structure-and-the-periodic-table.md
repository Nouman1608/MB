Section 2.2 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) asks you to describe the atom "as a central nucleus containing neutrons and protons surrounded by electrons in shells"; state the relative charges and masses of the three particles; define proton number and mass number; "determine the electronic configuration of elements and their ions with proton number 1 to 20"; and state three links between configuration and the Periodic Table. Every outcome is required for every 5070 candidate.

The [Atomic Structure overview for O Level 5070](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-atomic-structure/) introduces these ideas. This guide adds a systematic method for counting particles in atoms and ions, a full configuration table for proton numbers 1 to 20, and practice at reading the Periodic Table from a configuration and back again.

## The structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its centre, containing **protons** and **neutrons**. **Electrons** occupy **shells** (energy levels) around the nucleus. Almost all of the atom's mass is in the nucleus, but almost all of its volume is the space occupied by the electrons.

| Particle | Where it is found | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | shells around the nucleus | −1 | 1/1840 (negligible) |

Two consequences follow from the table:

- An **atom is neutral**, because it has equal numbers of protons (+) and electrons (−).
- The **mass of an atom** depends on its protons and neutrons; electrons add almost nothing.

## Proton number and mass number

- **Proton number** (atomic number), Z: the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
- **Mass number** (nucleon number), A: the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

So: **number of neutrons = mass number − proton number.**

The proton number identifies the element. Every atom with 12 protons is magnesium; change the number of protons and you have a different element. The Periodic Table is arranged in order of increasing proton number.

## Counting particles in atoms and ions

Use this three-step method for any particle.

1. **Protons** = proton number (from the Periodic Table or the symbol).
2. **Neutrons** = mass number − proton number.
3. **Electrons** = proton number for an atom; for an ion, **subtract** the charge for a positive ion and **add** it for a negative ion.

**Worked example 1.** Complete the table.

| Particle | Proton number | Mass number | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Argon atom | 18 | 40 | 18 | 40 − 18 = 22 | 18 |
| Sodium ion, Na⁺ | 11 | 23 | 11 | 12 | 11 − 1 = 10 |
| Aluminium ion, Al³⁺ | 13 | 27 | 13 | 14 | 13 − 3 = 10 |
| Fluoride ion, F⁻ | 9 | 19 | 9 | 10 | 9 + 1 = 10 |

Ions form by gaining or losing **electrons only**. The numbers of protons and neutrons never change when an ion forms, so the mass number is unchanged.

## Electronic configuration, proton numbers 1 to 20

Electrons fill shells from the innermost outwards. For the first 20 elements the shells hold:

- first shell: up to **2** electrons
- second shell: up to **8**
- third shell: up to **8** (before the fourth shell starts filling at potassium)

Write the configuration as numbers separated by commas, innermost shell first.

| Z | Element | Configuration | | Z | Element | Configuration |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | H | 1 | | 11 | Na | 2,8,1 |
| 2 | He | 2 | | 12 | Mg | 2,8,2 |
| 3 | Li | 2,1 | | 13 | Al | 2,8,3 |
| 4 | Be | 2,2 | | 14 | Si | 2,8,4 |
| 5 | B | 2,3 | | 15 | P | 2,8,5 |
| 6 | C | 2,4 | | 16 | S | 2,8,6 |
| 7 | N | 2,5 | | 17 | Cl | 2,8,7 |
| 8 | O | 2,6 | | 18 | Ar | 2,8,8 |
| 9 | F | 2,7 | | 19 | K | 2,8,8,1 |
| 10 | Ne | 2,8 | | 20 | Ca | 2,8,8,2 |

Do not try to memorise the table; build each configuration from the proton number. The check is that the numbers add up to the number of electrons.

### Configurations of ions

Work out the number of electrons first, then fill the shells.

**Worked example 2.** Write the configurations of Mg²⁺, N³⁻ and Li⁺.

- Mg²⁺: 12 − 2 = 10 electrons, so **2,8**.
- N³⁻: 7 + 3 = 10 electrons, so **2,8**.
- Li⁺: 3 − 1 = 2 electrons, so **2**.

Notice that Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ and N³⁻ all have the configuration 2,8, the same as neon. Ions of metals in Groups I to III and non-metals in Groups V to VII at this level have noble-gas configurations. That idea is the basis of ionic bonding in section 2.4.

## Linking configuration to the Periodic Table

The syllabus asks you to state three facts.

1. **Group VIII noble gases have a full outer electron shell** (helium 2, neon 2,8, argon 2,8,8).
2. **In Groups I to VII, the number of outer-shell electrons equals the group number.**
3. **The number of occupied electron shells equals the period number.**

**Worked example 3.** An element has the configuration 2,8,6. State its group, its period and its name.

*Answer.* Six outer electrons: Group VI. Three occupied shells: Period 3. Total electrons 2 + 8 + 6 = 16, so the proton number is 16: **sulfur**.

**Worked example 4.** Write the configuration of the element in Group III, Period 2.

*Answer.* Period 2: two shells. Group III: three outer electrons. Configuration **2,3**, which is boron (Z = 5).

### The helium exception

Helium is in Group VIII but has only **two** outer electrons. That is why the group-number rule is stated for Groups I to VII only. Helium's outer shell is full because the first shell holds only two electrons.

### Ions and the period rule

The period rule applies to **atoms**. A sodium ion, Na⁺, has configuration 2,8: two occupied shells, but sodium is in Period 3. Use the atom's configuration, not the ion's, to find the position of an element.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the electron's relative mass as 0. Write 1/1840 or "negligible".
- Using mass number for the number of neutrons, or adding the charge the wrong way for ions.
- Putting a third electron into the first shell, or nine into the second.
- Writing 2,8,9 for potassium: the fourth shell starts after 2,8,8.
- Saying ions form by gaining or losing protons.
- Applying the group-number rule to helium, or the period rule to an ion.
- Defining mass number as "the mass of the atom". It is a **number** of particles, protons plus neutrons.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: use the syllabus wording, including "in the nucleus of an atom".
- **State**: give the value (for example "+1", "2,8,3") with no explanation.
- **Determine** or **deduce**: show the electron count first, then the configuration.
- In particle-counting tables, check each row: for an atom, protons and electrons must match.
