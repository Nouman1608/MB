This guide goes deeper into subtopic **9.3 Alloys and their properties** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Alloys and Extraction of Metals](/resources/alloys-and-extraction-of-metals/) guide introduces alloys alongside uses and extraction. This page spends its time on the skills 9.3 actually tests: explaining why an alloy is a mixture, reading structure diagrams, writing the Supplement explanation of hardness for full marks, and linking alloy properties to uses. For quick recall use the [alloys revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alloys-and-their-properties-revision-notes/), and practise with the [alloys practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-alloys-and-their-properties-practice/).

## What 9.3 asks you to do

| Outcome | Tier | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Core | Describe an alloy as a mixture of a metal with other elements, including brass (copper and zinc) and stainless steel (iron with other elements such as chromium, nickel and carbon) |
| 2 | Core | State that alloys can be harder and stronger than the pure metals and are more useful |
| 3 | Core | Describe the uses of alloys in terms of their physical properties, including stainless steel in cutlery because of its hardness and resistance to rusting |
| 4 | Core | Identify representations of alloys from diagrams of structure |
| 5 | Supplement | Explain in terms of structure how alloys can be harder and stronger than the pure metals: the different sized atoms mean the layers can no longer slide over each other |

Outcomes 1 to 4 are for every candidate. Outcome 5 is for Extended candidates only.

## An alloy is a mixture

The syllabus definition has three parts, and each one matters.

- **A mixture.** The elements in an alloy are not chemically combined in a fixed ratio, so an alloy is not a compound. Brass can contain different proportions of copper and zinc and still be brass. There is no formula such as "CuZn" to learn.
- **Of a metal.** The main element is a metal: copper in brass, iron in stainless steel.
- **With other elements.** These are usually other metals, but not always. Stainless steel contains **carbon**, a non-metal. "An alloy is a mixture of metals" is therefore not the full definition; "a mixture of a metal with other elements" is.

| Alloy | Main metal | Other elements named in the syllabus |
|---|---|---|
| Brass | copper | zinc |
| Stainless steel | iron | chromium, nickel and carbon |

Because an alloy is a mixture, its properties are different from those of the pure metal it is made from. That is the whole reason alloys are made: the mixture is more useful than the pure metal.

## Alloys are harder, stronger and more useful

At Core level you only need to **state** this. Pure copper and pure iron are relatively soft. Brass is harder than copper, and stainless steel is harder and stronger than iron. Stainless steel also resists rusting, which pure iron does not.

### Uses in terms of properties

The syllabus names one use you must know: **stainless steel for cutlery**, because it is **hard** and **resists rusting**. The reasoning follows the same pattern as uses of pure metals in 9.2:

- Knives, forks and spoons must keep their shape and edge, so they need a **hard** material.
- They are washed and in contact with food and water every day, so they must **not rust**.

Pure iron fails on both counts: it is softer and it rusts. Questions may give data about another alloy and ask you to suggest a use; apply the same "property, therefore use" reasoning.

## Reading structure diagrams (Core outcome 4)

Exam diagrams show atoms as circles. You must decide whether a diagram shows a pure metal, an alloy or something else. The text grids below stand in for those diagrams: **O** is an atom of the main metal and **@** is an atom of a different element with a different size (in a real diagram it would be drawn larger or smaller).

```
Pure metal               Alloy
O O O O O O              O O O O O O
O O O O O O              O @ O O O O
O O O O O O              O O O O @ O
O O O O O O              O O @ O O O
```

**How to recognise each:**

- **Pure metal:** every circle is the **same size**, packed in **regular rows** (layers).
- **Alloy:** circles of **two or more different sizes**; the different atoms are **scattered among** the main metal atoms, and the rows are **distorted** near them.

**What an alloy diagram is not:**

- A diagram with two kinds of particle in a strict, repeating alternate pattern (one, then the other, throughout) usually represents a **compound** such as an ionic lattice, not an alloy.
- A diagram with two kinds of particle in **separate regions** or as separate small molecules shows a **mixture of separate substances**, not an alloy.

In an alloy the different atoms sit *within* the metal's own structure, in no fixed ratio.

## Why alloys are harder (Supplement outcome 5)

**In a pure metal**, all the atoms are the same size and are arranged in regular layers. When a force is applied, the layers can **slide over each other**. That is why pure metals are malleable and relatively soft.

**In an alloy**, atoms of a different size are mixed in among the main metal atoms. They **disrupt the regular layers**. The layers can **no longer slide over each other** easily, so more force is needed to change the shape of the metal. The alloy is harder and stronger.

### Model answer, annotated

**Question.** Explain, in terms of structure, why brass is harder than pure copper. [3]

> In pure copper, the atoms are all the same size and are arranged in layers [1]. In brass, zinc atoms of a different size are mixed in among the copper atoms [1]. This disrupts the layers so they can no longer slide over each other [1].

*The marking points are "same size / regular layers in the pure metal", "different sized atoms in the alloy" and "layers cannot slide". Writing "the atoms are bonded more strongly" does not earn credit, because the explanation is about layers sliding, not bond strength.*

## Worked example: a percentage composition

*The figures below are invented for practice.*

**Question.** A 250 g brass key contains 65 % copper by mass. Calculate the mass of zinc in the key. [2]

```
mass of copper = 65/100 × 250 = 162.5 g        [1]
mass of zinc   = 250 − 162.5 = 87.5 g          [1]
```

*This question also shows why brass is a mixture: another brass key could contain a different percentage of copper and still be brass.*

## Common mistakes

- **Calling an alloy a compound.** It is a mixture; the elements are not combined in a fixed ratio.
- **Defining an alloy as "a mixture of metals".** Stainless steel contains carbon, a non-metal. Use "a metal with other elements".
- **Swapping the alloys.** Brass is copper with zinc; stainless steel is iron with chromium, nickel and carbon. Brass contains no iron.
- **Writing "the atoms cannot move".** The point is that **layers** cannot slide over each other.
- **Forgetting the size difference.** The Supplement explanation needs "different sized atoms"; "different atoms" alone is weaker.
- **Giving only one property for cutlery.** Stainless steel cutlery needs both **hardness** and **resistance to rusting**.
- **Choosing an alternating pattern as the alloy diagram.** A strict alternate pattern usually shows a compound.

## Exam technique for 9.3 command words

- **Describe** (outcomes 1 and 3): give the definition with all three parts, or give the use **with** its property.
- **State** (outcome 2): one sentence, such as "alloys are harder and stronger than the pure metal".
- **Identify** (outcome 4): name the diagram letter, then give the feature you used, such as "atoms of different sizes".
- **Explain** (outcome 5, Extended): compare the pure metal and the alloy, mention the size difference and finish with "layers can no longer slide over each other".
- **Suggest:** use data you are given to propose a use for an unfamiliar alloy, justifying it with a property.
