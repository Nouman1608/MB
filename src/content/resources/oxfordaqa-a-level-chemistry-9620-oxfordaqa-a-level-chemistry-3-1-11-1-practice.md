These questions cover **3.1.11.1 Rate equations** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification: orders and the rate constant, rate calculations, the effect of temperature on k and the Arrhenius equation. This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 4. Use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, k = Ae^(−Ea/RT) and ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A. All rate constants and activation energies are illustrative values written for practice.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *overall order of reaction*. **[1]**

**2.** Deduce the units of the rate constant for each rate equation.

**(a)** Rate = k[A] **[1]**

**(b)** Rate = k[C]²[D]² **[1]**

**3.** For 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ the rate equation is Rate = k[NO]²[O₂]. [NO] is halved and [O₂] is multiplied by four. State the effect on the rate. **[1]**

**4.** For the reaction in Question 3, the rate is 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ when [NO] = 0.0050 mol dm⁻³ and [O₂] = 0.0040 mol dm⁻³. Calculate k and give its units. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the rate constant of a reaction increases when the temperature is increased. **[2]**

**6.** State one change that increases the rate of a reaction without changing k, and one change that increases k. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The reaction 2A + B → C has the rate equation Rate = k[A][B], with k = 4.5 × 10⁻³ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ at 298 K.

**(a)** Calculate the rate when [A] = 0.20 mol dm⁻³ and [B] = 0.15 mol dm⁻³. **[1]**

**(b)** In a second experiment at 298 K, [A] is 1.5 times larger and [B] is half as large as in (a). Calculate the rate. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the rate equation is not Rate = k[A]²[B], even though the equation shows 2A. **[1]**

**8.** A first-order reaction has Ea = 88.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and A = 4.6 × 10¹² s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate k at 320 K. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the temperature at which k is ten times the value in (a). **[3]**

**9.** A student measures k for a first-order reaction at several temperatures and plots ln k (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis). The line of best fit passes through the points (3.10 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −4.20) and (3.40 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −7.95).

**(a)** Calculate the gradient of the line, with its units. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the activation energy in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the Arrhenius constant, A. **[2]**

**10.** A reaction has Ea = 65.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the factor by which k increases when the temperature rises from 25 °C to 35 °C. Assume A is constant. **[3]**

**11.** A second-order reaction has k = 0.0120 mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ at 400 K and k = 0.0950 mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ at 430 K. Calculate the activation energy in kJ mol⁻¹. **[3]**

**12.** A catalyst lowers the activation energy of a reaction by 25.0 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300 K.

**(a)** Assuming A is unchanged, calculate the factor by which k increases. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the Arrhenius equation to explain why a reaction with a large activation energy is more sensitive to temperature than one with a small activation energy. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The sum of the orders with respect to each reactant in the rate equation [1]

**2. (a)** **s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Overall order 4: k = mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ / (mol dm⁻³)⁴ = **mol⁻³ dm⁹ s⁻¹** [1]

**3.** Factor = (0.5)² × 4 = 1, so the rate is **unchanged** [1]

**4.** k = 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ / ((0.0050)² × 0.0040) = **360** [1]; **mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹** [1]

**5.** At a higher temperature a larger proportion of molecules have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]; so a larger proportion of collisions lead to reaction, and k is larger [1]

*Examiner insight:* "Molecules move faster and collide more" alone does not explain the large change in k; the energy distribution point is the one credited.

**6.** Increasing the concentration (or pressure) of a reactant that appears in the rate equation [1]; increasing the temperature (or adding a catalyst) [1]

**7. (a)** Rate = 4.5 × 10⁻³ × 0.20 × 0.15 = **1.35 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Factor = 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 [1]; rate = 0.75 × 1.35 × 10⁻⁴ = **1.01 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Orders are found by experiment; they depend on the mechanism (the rate-determining step), not on the balancing numbers in the overall equation [1]

**8. (a)** Ea/RT = 88 000 / (8.31 × 320) = 33.09 [1]; k = 4.6 × 10¹² × e^(−33.09) = **1.95 × 10⁻² s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Target k = 0.195 s⁻¹; rearrange: T = Ea / (R × ln(A/k)) [1]; ln(4.6 × 10¹² / 0.195) = 30.79 [1]; T = 88 000 / (8.31 × 30.79) = **344 K** [1]

**9. (a)** Gradient = (−7.95 − (−4.20)) / (3.40 × 10⁻³ − 3.10 × 10⁻³) = **−1.25 × 10⁴ K** [1]

**(b)** −Ea/R = −1.25 × 10⁴, so Ea = 1.25 × 10⁴ × 8.31 = 1.04 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹ [1]; Ea = **104 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** ln A = ln k + Ea/RT = −4.20 + 1.25 × 10⁴ × 3.10 × 10⁻³ = 34.55 [1]; A = e^34.55 = **1.0 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹** [1]

*Examiner insight:* The intercept at 1/T = 0 is far off the axes, so substitute a point on the line; reading an intercept from the plotted region gives a wrong ln A.

**10.** T₁ = 298 K, T₂ = 308 K [1]; ln(k₂/k₁) = (65 000 / 8.31) × (1/298 − 1/308) = 0.852 [1]; k₂/k₁ = e^0.852 = **2.34** [1]

**11.** ln(0.0950 / 0.0120) = 2.069 [1]; 1/400 − 1/430 = 1.744 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ [1]; Ea = 8.31 × 2.069 / 1.744 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.86 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **98.6 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**12. (a)** k(cat) / k(uncat) = e^(25 000 / (8.31 × 300)) [1]; = e^10.03 = **2.3 × 10⁴** [1]

**(b)** Ea appears in the exponent −Ea/RT, so a change in T changes the exponent by an amount proportional to Ea [1]; with a large Ea the same temperature rise changes e^(−Ea/RT), and therefore k, by a larger factor [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving Ea in kJ mol⁻¹ inside the exponential.
- Forgetting to convert 25 °C and 35 °C to kelvin.
- Writing units of k with the wrong signs on the powers.
- Taking the gradient as positive and getting a negative activation energy.
