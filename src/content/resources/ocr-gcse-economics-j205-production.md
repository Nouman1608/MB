This guide covers section 2.6, Production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019, in component J205/01. The specification is not tiered. All firms and figures are invented.

The [competition, monopoly, production, costs and scale guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-competition-monopoly-and-oligopoly-production-costs/) covers this section with competition. This page goes further on production alone: the three kinds of producer, measuring productivity in different ways, a full cost and revenue workout with three separate changes, and the difference between spreading fixed costs and true economies of scale. Condense it with the [2.6 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.6 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain the role of producers, including individuals, firms and the government;
- evaluate the importance of production and productivity for the economy;
- calculate and explain total cost, average cost, total revenue, average revenue, profit and loss;
- evaluate the importance of cost, revenue, profit and loss for producers, including how costs and revenues affect profit and supply;
- explain what is meant by economies of scale.

Among the specification's quantitative skills are calculating averages (including cost) and totals (including revenue, costs and profit).

## The role of producers

**Production** is the process of combining factors of production to make goods and provide services that satisfy wants.

| Producer | Who | Typical aim | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individuals | Self-employed people, sole traders, freelancers | Earn an income from their own work | A self-employed electrician |
| Firms | Private businesses of every size | Usually profit | A supermarket chain or a local bakery |
| Government | The public sector | Provide services that people need, often free at the point of use | State schools, public hospitals, roads |

Each producer answers the "what" and "how" questions from section 1.2 in its own way. Firms respond to what consumers will pay for; the government provides some services that markets might not provide well, or might provide only to those who can pay.

## Production and productivity

- **Production** is the total quantity of output.
- **Productivity** is output per unit of input over a period. The most common measure is **labour productivity**: output per worker or output per hour worked.

**Worked example: a printing firm (invented).** Four printers each work 40 hours a week: 160 labour hours.

```
Before new equipment: 80 jobs a week
Productivity = 80 ÷ 160 = 0.5 jobs per labour hour

After new equipment: 100 jobs a week, same hours
Productivity = 100 ÷ 160 = 0.625 jobs per labour hour
Rise = (0.625 − 0.5) ÷ 0.5 × 100 = +25%

Wages are £14 an hour, so the weekly wage bill is 160 × 14 = £2,240
Labour cost per job: 2,240 ÷ 80 = £28.00 → 2,240 ÷ 100 = £22.40
```

Output per hour is often a better measure than output per worker, because it is not distorted by changes in the number of hours each person works.

### Why production and productivity matter for the economy

- **Living standards:** more output per hour means more goods and services for each person, and allows higher wages without higher prices.
- **Competitiveness:** lower labour cost per unit helps firms compete with rivals at home and abroad.
- **Government revenue:** more output and income raise tax revenue for public services.

**Limits to weigh:** productivity gains from machines may reduce the number of jobs in that firm; higher production may use up non-renewable resources or cause pollution; gains may go mainly to owners as profit rather than to workers. An evaluation says which of these matters more in the context.

## Cost, revenue, profit and loss

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost (TC) | fixed cost + variable cost |
| Average cost (AC) | TC ÷ output |
| Total revenue (TR) | price × quantity sold |
| Average revenue (AR) | TR ÷ quantity sold (equal to the price when every unit sells at one price) |
| Profit | TR − TC, when TR is greater |
| Loss | TC − TR, when TC is greater |

**Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance, loan repayments). **Variable costs** rise with output (materials, energy used by machines, packaging).

**Worked example: Spoke & Chain bike workshop (invented).** Fixed costs are £1,200 a week; variable cost is £15 per repair; the price is £40.

| Repairs | TC (£) | AC (£) | TR (£) | Profit or loss (£) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 1,200 + 450 = 1,650 | 55.00 | 1,200 | loss of 450 |
| 50 | 1,200 + 750 = 1,950 | 39.00 | 2,000 | profit of 50 |
| 80 | 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400 | 30.00 | 3,200 | profit of 800 |

A useful check: **profit = (AR − AC) × output**. At 80 repairs, (40 − 30) × 80 = £800.

Now change one thing at a time from the 80-repair position:

```
A. Rent rises by £300 (fixed cost £1,500)
   TC = 1,500 + 1,200 = £2,700; profit = 3,200 − 2,700 = £500 (−37.5%)

B. Parts cost rises to £18 per repair (variable cost)
   TC = 1,200 + 1,440 = £2,640; profit = 3,200 − 2,640 = £560 (−30%)

C. Price rises to £44 with costs unchanged
   TR = 44 × 80 = £3,520; profit = 3,520 − 2,400 = £1,120 (+40%)
```

### How costs and revenue affect profit and supply

- **Higher costs (A and B)** reduce profit at every price. Across a whole market, firms are willing to supply less at each price, so the **supply curve shifts left**.
- **Lower costs** (new technology, cheaper materials) raise profit at every price, so **supply shifts right**.
- **A higher price (C)** raises profit per unit, so firms produce more: a **movement along** the supply curve. High profits also attract new firms into the market, increasing supply over time.
- **Losses** are a signal to cut costs, raise revenue or leave the market. A firm can survive losses for a while using savings or loans, but not indefinitely. When firms leave, supply falls.

### Why these figures matter to producers

Profit rewards the entrepreneur's risk, pays for investment in new equipment, and shows whether the business is worth continuing. Revenue alone can mislead: a firm can increase sales and still make a loss if costs rise faster. Average cost shows whether the firm can compete on price; a firm whose AC is above the market price cannot survive for long.

## Economies of scale

**Economies of scale** are the reductions in average cost that a firm gains by increasing its scale of production: becoming a bigger operation with larger premises, more machinery or more branches.

Main types: technical (larger, more efficient machinery and specialised workers), purchasing (bulk-buying discounts), financial (borrowing at lower interest), managerial (specialist managers), marketing (advertising spread over more sales), risk-bearing (a wider product range).

**A distinction worth making.** In the Spoke & Chain table, AC falls from £55 to £30 as repairs rise from 30 to 80. That fall comes from **spreading the same fixed cost** over more repairs in the same workshop. Economies of scale are different: they come from **growing the business**, for example opening three more workshops and buying parts in bulk at a lower price per part. Making this distinction shows precise understanding.

Economies of scale matter because lower average cost lets a firm cut prices, widen its profit margin, or both; this links production back to competition in section 2.5.

## Common mistakes

- Writing profit = TR − AC.
- Confusing productivity (output per input) with production (total output).
- Shifting the supply curve for a price change, or moving along it for a cost change.
- Calling any fall in AC an economy of scale.
- Leaving out the word "loss" or the units in a calculation answer.
- Treating revenue as profit.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute, then give the answer with £ and the word profit or loss.
- **Explain economies of scale:** name a type and say why cost **per unit** falls.
- **Evaluate productivity:** benefits for firms, workers and the economy, then a limit such as job losses or environmental cost, then a judgement.
- **Cost and supply questions:** link the cost change to profit, then to the supply curve.
