## Section A — Multiple choice

**1.** Which of the following causes a movement along the short-run aggregate supply curve?

A) A rise in world energy prices

B) A rise in the general price level

C) A depreciation of the exchange rate

D) A cut in taxes on firms' employment costs **[1]**

**2.** In the classical model, the long-run aggregate supply curve is:

A) Horizontal at the current price level

B) Upward sloping at all levels of output

C) Vertical at the full-employment level of real output

D) Downward sloping **[1]**

**3.** Which of the following is most likely to shift the LRAS curve to the right?

A) A rise in consumer confidence

B) A fall in interest rates

C) Net inward migration of working-age people

D) A rise in the general price level **[1]**

**4.** On the horizontal section of a Keynesian aggregate supply curve, an increase in aggregate demand will:

A) Raise the price level only

B) Raise real output with no change in the price level

C) Reduce real output

D) Leave both real output and the price level unchanged **[1]**

## Section B — Short-answer questions

**5.** Define "long-run aggregate supply". **[2]**

**6.** Country G has 25 million workers, each producing an average of 48 000 a year. Over five years the number of workers rises by 4% and output per worker rises by 10%. Calculate the percentage change in Country G's productive capacity. You are advised to show your working. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how an appreciation of the exchange rate is likely to affect short-run aggregate supply. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse how stronger competition policy might affect long-run aggregate supply. **[6]**

## Section C — Data question

**9.** Read the extract, then answer all parts.

*Extract (invented).* In Country F, firms import all their oil and gas. Energy makes up 15% of firms' average costs, and imported components priced in euros make up a further 10%. Over one year, world energy prices rose by 40%, and Country F's currency depreciated from 1 F-dollar = €0.80 to 1 F-dollar = €0.64. The government has launched a large apprenticeship scheme and plans to relax planning rules so that new factories and homes can be built more quickly.

**(a)** Define "short-run aggregate supply". **[2]**

**(b)** Using the extract, calculate the approximate percentage rise in firms' average costs caused by the energy price rise and the depreciation together. **[4]**

**(c)** With the help of an AD/AS diagram, analyse the likely effect of these cost increases on the price level and real output in Country F. **[6]**

**(d)** Examine whether the apprenticeship scheme is likely to increase long-run aggregate supply. **[8]**

**(e)** Discuss the likely effectiveness of relaxing planning rules as a way to increase long-run aggregate supply in Country F. **[14]**

## Section D — Essay (answer ONE)

**EITHER**

**10.** Evaluate the view that an increase in aggregate demand will always lead to a rise in real output. **[20]**

**OR**

**11.** Evaluate the extent to which net inward migration increases an economy's long-run aggregate supply. **[20]**

## Answers

**1. B)** A change in the general price level moves the economy along the SRAS curve; the other options change costs and shift the curve [1].

Total: 1

**2. C)** Classical LRAS is vertical at full-employment output, because flexible wages and prices return output to capacity in the long run [1].

Total: 1

**3. C)** Net inward migration of working-age people increases the labour force, one of the named LRAS factors (demography and net migration). Options A and B affect AD; D is a movement along a curve [1].

Total: 1

**4. B)** The horizontal section has spare capacity, so output can rise without upward pressure on costs or prices [1].

Total: 1

**5.** The productive capacity of the economy: the maximum real output it can produce [1] when all factors of production are fully employed, in a period long enough for all factors and their prices to change [1].

Total: 2

**6.** Initial capacity = 25 m × 48 000 = 1200 bn [1]. New capacity = (25 m × 1.04) × (48 000 × 1.10) = 26 m × 52 800 [1] = 1372.8 bn [1]. Percentage change = (1372.8 − 1200) ÷ 1200 × 100 = **14.4%** (equivalently 1.04 × 1.10 = 1.144) [1].

Total: 4

*Examiner insight:* Adding 4% and 10% gives 14%, which is wrong; growth factors multiply. Showing the factor 1.144 makes the method clear.

**7.** An appreciation means one unit of home currency buys more foreign currency [1], so imported raw materials, energy and components cost less in home currency [1]. Firms' unit costs fall at every level of output [1], so they are willing to supply more at each price level and SRAS shifts to the right [1].

Total: 4

**8.** Competition policy, such as preventing mergers that reduce rivalry or opening markets to new entrants, increases competition between firms [1] [1]. Firms facing rivals must cut costs and use resources more efficiently to keep customers, raising productivity [1] [1]. Competition also gives firms an incentive to innovate and adopt new technology, increasing output from the same inputs [1]. Higher productivity and better technology raise the economy's productive capacity, so LRAS shifts to the right, increasing potential real output and putting downward pressure on the price level [1].

Total: 6

**9. (a)** The total planned real output that firms will supply at each general price level [1] in a period when some costs, such as wage rates, are fixed [1].

**(b)** Energy: 15% of costs × 40% rise = a 6% rise in average costs [1]. Components: a €8 component cost 8 ÷ 0.80 = 10 F-dollars before and 8 ÷ 0.64 = 12.5 F-dollars after, a 25% rise [1]; 10% of costs × 25% = a 2.5% rise in average costs [1]. Total ≈ **8.5%** rise in average costs [1].

**(c)** Higher energy and import costs raise unit costs at every level of output [1], so SRAS shifts left, from SRAS₁ to SRAS₂ [1]. The diagram shows the price level on the vertical axis and real national output on the horizontal axis, with AD unchanged [1]. At the original price level there is now excess demand, so the price level rises from P₁ to P₂ [1], and the equilibrium moves along AD so real output falls from Y₁ to Y₂ [1]. The result is cost-push inflation together with lower output (and probably higher unemployment) [1].

**(d)** Analysis: apprenticeships raise education and skills, a named LRAS factor [1]; more skilled workers produce more per hour, raising productivity [1] and so productive capacity, shifting LRAS right [1]; filling skill shortages also removes bottlenecks, so firms can expand without costs rising as fast [1]; workers moving from low-skill to higher-value jobs raise potential output further [1]; the diagram shows LRAS₁ shifting to LRAS₂ with a higher full-employment output [1]. Evaluation (any two): the effect is slow, since apprentices take years to train [1]; it depends on whether the training matches the skills firms need [1]; the scheme's scale relative to the labour force matters; trained workers might emigrate.

**(e)** Analysis (up to 8, 2 marks for each developed point): faster approval lets firms build new factories, increasing the capital stock and capacity; more homes allow workers to move to where jobs are, reducing structural mismatch and raising effective labour supply; lower regulatory costs and delays raise expected returns on investment, encouraging more of it; construction itself may bring new technology into use. Evaluation (up to 6, 2 marks for each developed point): new buildings take years to complete, so the gain is long term; relaxed rules may have environmental and social costs, such as loss of green space, which may reduce wellbeing even if output rises; investment also depends on business confidence, interest rates and the availability of credit, so faster approval alone may not raise it; the effect depends on whether planning delays were a real constraint; a supported judgement that the policy can raise LRAS, but slowly, and only if other conditions for investment are in place.

Total: 34

*Examiner insight:* In part (e), "discuss" needs a recognition of different viewpoints and the significance of each argument. A list of points with no weighing would stay in the lower levels.

**10.** *Indicative content.*

**For** (up to 8, 2 marks each): on the horizontal section of the Keynesian curve there is spare capacity, so higher AD raises output without inflation; on the upward-sloping section output still rises, along with the price level; in the short run SRAS slopes upward, so any rise in AD raises real output; the multiplier can make the rise in output larger than the initial injection.

**Against** (up to 8, 2 marks each): on the vertical section of the Keynesian curve, or under the classical view in the long run, extra AD raises only the price level; near full capacity bottlenecks mean most of the effect is inflation; in the classical view, higher wages and costs follow higher prices, shifting SRAS left until output returns to its full-employment level; a rise in AD driven by imports may leak abroad rather than raise domestic output.

**Judgement** (4): an increase in AD raises real output when there is spare capacity and in the short run, but not "always": the effect depends on where the economy is relative to full capacity, the time period, and which model of LRAS is accepted.

Total: 20

**11.** *Indicative content.*

**For** (up to 8, 2 marks each): net inward migration of working-age people enlarges the labour force, a named LRAS factor; migrants may fill skill shortages, removing bottlenecks; younger migrants offset an ageing population; migrants may bring new skills or start businesses, raising productivity and competition.

**Against** (up to 8, 2 marks each): the effect depends on whether migrants find work that uses their skills, so underemployment reduces the gain; if migrants are mostly dependants or not of working age, the labour force grows less; extra workers without extra capital or infrastructure may raise total output but not output per worker; the size of the effect depends on the scale of migration relative to the labour force.

**Judgement** (4): net inward migration usually raises LRAS by increasing the quantity of labour, but the extent depends on the age and skills of migrants, how well they are employed, and whether investment in capital and infrastructure keeps pace.

Total: 20

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a change in the price level as a shift of AS.
- Using a demand-side factor to explain an AS shift.
- Adding percentage changes instead of multiplying growth factors.
- Drawing a diagram without labelled axes, curves and equilibria.
- Giving analysis without evaluation in an "examine", "discuss" or "evaluate" answer.
- Ignoring the shape of the AS curve when judging the effect of an AD change.

**What is the difference between a shift of SRAS and a shift of LRAS?**
SRAS shifts when firms' costs change at every level of output, such as energy prices, exchange rates or taxes. LRAS shifts when the quantity or quality of resources changes, such as technology, skills or the size of the labour force.
