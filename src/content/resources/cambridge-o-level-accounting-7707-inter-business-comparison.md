This guide goes deeper into section **6.3 Inter-business comparison** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The section is short in the syllabus. Candidates should understand "the factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses" and "the problems of inter-business comparison". It matters most when a question asks you to judge two businesses from their ratios.

The shared [inter-business comparison, interested parties and limitations guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-inter-business-comparison-interested-parties-and-limita/) covers 6.3 to 6.5 together. This page concentrates on 6.3 alone, with an example showing how one difference between two shops can reverse a conclusion. Recall the points with the [inter-business comparison revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-inter-business-comparison-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [inter-business comparison practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-inter-business-comparison-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Why compare with another business

Comparing one year with the last shows whether a business is improving, but not whether its results are good. A gross profit margin of 25% may be excellent for a supermarket and poor for a jeweller. Comparing with a similar business, or with the average for the trade, gives a benchmark. An owner deciding whether to buy a second shop, a lender choosing between two applicants, or an investor choosing between two companies all make this kind of comparison.

## Factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses

| Factor | How it changes the ratios |
|---|---|
| Type of business | Different trades have different normal margins and inventory turnover; a bakery's inventory turns over far faster than a furniture shop's |
| Size | A larger business may buy in bulk at lower prices, raising its gross profit margin |
| Pricing policy | One business may sell cheaply in large volumes (low margin, fast turnover), another sells fewer goods at higher prices |
| Location | Rent and wages vary by area, and so do the prices customers accept |
| Owning or renting premises | Owning adds assets to capital employed and depreciation to expenses; renting adds rent to expenses only |
| Method of finance | Loans add interest; a business financed by the owner's capital pays none, so profit margins differ |
| Accounting policies | Depreciation method and rate, inventory valuation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts change profit and asset values |
| Age of non-current assets | Old assets have a low book value, which raises ROCE |
| Credit policy | Generous credit terms raise trade receivables days |
| Year-end date | Inventory, receivables and cash differ at different points in a seasonal year |

## Worked example: two grocery shops

Ali and Bina each own a grocery shop in the same town.

| | Ali | Bina |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 400,000 | 300,000 |
| Gross profit | 100,000 | 90,000 |
| Expenses | 60,000 | 66,000 |
| Profit for the year | 40,000 | 24,000 |
| Capital employed | 250,000 | 60,000 |
| Gross profit margin | 25.00% | 30.00% |
| Profit margin | 10.00% | 8.00% |
| ROCE | 16.00% | 40.00% |

Neither has any loans. At first sight Ali earns the higher profit margin but Bina earns a far higher return on her capital.

**Look for the difference behind the ratios.** Ali owns his shop, which has a book value of $200,000 inside his capital employed, and his expenses include $4,000 of depreciation on it. Bina rents a similar shop for $24,000 a year, which is in her expenses.

**Adjust Ali's figures as if he rented, like Bina.** Profit = 40,000 + 4,000 depreciation - 24,000 rent = $20,000. Capital employed = 250,000 - 200,000 = $50,000.

| Adjusted for Ali | Result |
|---|---|
| Profit margin | 20,000 / 400,000 x 100 = 5.00% |
| ROCE | 20,000 / 50,000 x 100 = 40.00% |

On a like-for-like basis, both shops earn a 40% return on the capital used in trading, and Bina's profit margin (8%) is higher than Ali's (5%). The first conclusion, that Ali's shop is more profitable on sales, reverses once the treatment of premises is matched. Ali's lower gross profit margin with higher revenue also suggests a pricing policy of selling more at lower prices.

## How an accounting policy can create a difference

Two delivery firms each buy an identical van for $30,000 on the first day of the year. Firm A depreciates it at 20% a year on the straight line basis; Firm B uses 40% on the reducing balance.

| | Firm A | Firm B |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation, year 1 | 30,000 x 20% = 6,000 | 30,000 x 40% = 12,000 |
| Book value at year end | 24,000 | 18,000 |

With identical trading, Firm B's profit is $6,000 lower in year 1, and in later years its lower book value and lower charges can make its ROCE the higher of the two. Neither firm has done anything wrong; consistency only requires each to keep its own method. But a comparison of their ratios compares their policies as well as their performance.

## A model evaluation

*Question: evaluate whether Bina's shop is performing better than Ali's.*

"Bina's ROCE of 40% is far above Ali's 16%, but this is mainly because Ali owns his premises, which adds $200,000 to his capital employed. When Ali's figures are adjusted as if he rented, both shops earn 40%, so neither uses its capital better. Bina's profit margin of 8% is higher than Ali's adjusted 5%, so she keeps more of each sale. However, Ali's revenue is $100,000 higher, and the figures cover one year only and may follow different accounting policies. On balance, Bina's shop is slightly more profitable on sales, but the difference is smaller than the unadjusted ratios suggest."

## Problems of inter-business comparison

- **Different accounting policies**: depreciation, inventory valuation and allowances differ, so the same trading produces different figures.
- **Businesses are never identical**: size, trade, location, product mix and pricing policy all vary.
- **Different year-end dates**: one business's statement of financial position may fall in a busy season and the other's in a quiet one.
- **Limited information**: statements available to outsiders may not show credit sales, credit purchases or opening inventory, so some ratios cannot be calculated exactly.
- **Historic cost**: assets bought years apart are recorded at very different prices.
- **One year only**: a single year may include one-off events; trends need several years.
- **Non-financial factors**: staff skill, reputation and customer loyalty are not in the ratios.
- **Different financing**: a sole trader may take drawings instead of a salary, while a company pays directors' salaries as an expense, so profits are not directly comparable.

## Common mistakes

- Concluding that one business is "better" from one ratio.
- Listing factors that are not linked to the ratio in the question.
- Naming "different accounting policies" without saying which policy or which ratio it affects.
- Forgetting that a lower gross profit margin can be a deliberate pricing policy, not a weakness.

## Exam technique

A 6.3 question often follows ratio calculations for two businesses and asks you to **compare**, **explain** or **evaluate**. "Compare" in the syllabus list means to "identify/comment on similarities and/or differences", so use comparative words: "higher than", "both". An **explain** answer on a factor should name it, say which ratio it changes and in which direction, using the case: "Ali owns his premises, so his capital employed is $200,000 higher and his ROCE is lower." For **evaluate**, finish with a judgement that recognises the comparison's limits.
