Condensed notes for **5.5 Budgets** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Worked examples on building a budget, zero budgeting and flexing variances are in the [Budgets study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-budgets-cambridge-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [Budgets practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-budgets-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 5.5 asks you to handle

- **5.5.1** Measuring performance; benefits and drawbacks; incremental, flexible and zero budgeting; budgets for measuring performance, allocating resources, controlling and monitoring.
- **5.5.2** Adverse and favourable variances; calculating and interpreting them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget | A financial plan for a future period, agreed in advance |
| Forecast | A prediction of what is likely to happen |
| Budget holder | Manager responsible for keeping to a budget |
| Incremental budget | Last period's budget adjusted by a percentage |
| Flexible budget | Budget adjusted to the actual level of output |
| Zero budgeting | Every item starts at zero and must be justified |
| Variance | Difference between budgeted and actual figures |
| Favourable variance | Raises profit: revenue above budget or costs below |
| Adverse variance | Lowers profit: revenue below budget or costs above |
| Budget slack | Padding a budget so targets are easy to meet |

## Building a budget

Sales budget → production/purchasing budget → cost budgets → department budgets → overall budget and planned profit.
The **sales forecast** drives everything: if it is wrong, every budget is wrong.

## Four uses

1. **Measuring performance**: actual against plan.
2. **Allocating resources**: who gets how much.
3. **Controlling**: spending limits.
4. **Monitoring**: progress checks during the period.

## Benefits

Direction and coordination · early warning of overspending · motivation if managers take part · accountability through budget holders.

## Drawbacks

Time and cost · inaccurate forecasts · rigidity (missed opportunities) · budget slack · "spend it or lose it" · demotivation if imposed and unrealistic.

## Choosing a method

| Method | Best for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Incremental | Stable activities | Carries forward waste |
| Flexible | Output that varies | Needs fixed/variable split; more complex |
| Zero | Cost reviews, new projects | Slow; needs training |

## Variance rules

- Revenue: actual **above** budget → **F**; below → **A**.
- Costs: actual **below** budget → **F**; above → **A**.
- Profit variance = sum of revenue and cost variances (F adds, A subtracts).
- Always label F or A.

## Worked reminder: a variance table

A shop budgets revenue $40 000, stock $22 000 and wages $9 000. Actual: revenue $38 500, stock $21 200, wages $9 400.

| Item | Variance |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $1 500 A |
| Stock | $800 F |
| Wages | $400 A |
| Profit | $1 100 A |

Check: budgeted profit $9 000; actual profit $7 900; difference $1 100 A. The profit variance always equals the favourable variances minus the adverse ones.

## Flexing in three steps

1. Variable cost per unit = original budget ÷ budgeted output.
2. Flexed budget = variable cost per unit × actual output (fixed costs unchanged).
3. Variance = flexed budget − actual (label F or A).

*Quick check:* materials budget $24 000 for 8 000 units = $3 a unit. Actual output 9 000 units, actual materials $28 500. Flexed = $27 000. Variance = $1 500 A (not $4 500 A).

## Interpreting variances

- **Favourable ≠ good**: cheaper materials may lower quality.
- **Adverse ≠ bad**: more sales need more materials.
- **Flex first** when output differs from plan.
- **Size matters**: compare with the budget (as a %); act on large or growing variances.
- **Controllable?** Do not blame managers for external causes such as national energy prices.

## Causes and actions

| Variance | Causes | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue A | Price cut, lower volume, rival | Review price, promotion |
| Materials A | Supplier price, waste, volume | Negotiate, reduce waste |
| Labour A | Overtime, pay rise, inefficiency | Rotas, training |
| Overheads A | Energy, repairs | Investigate controllability |

## Exam traps

- Higher cost than budget is **adverse**, never favourable.
- Do not judge costs without flexing if output changed.
- Budget ≠ forecast.
- Incremental budgets can hide waste.
- Zero budgeting is thorough but slow.
- State whether you are comparing with the original or the flexed budget.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between a budget and a forecast.
2. Give two uses of budgets.
3. Why is the sales budget usually prepared first?
4. Give one drawback of incremental budgeting.
5. When is zero budgeting most useful?
6. Budgeted revenue is $60 000; actual revenue is $57 000. State the variance.
7. Budgeted wages are $12 000; actual wages are $11 400. State the variance.
8. Explain why a favourable materials variance may be bad news.
9. Labour budget is $10 000 for 5 000 units. Actual output is 6 000 units and labour costs $12 600. Calculate the flexed budget and the variance.
10. What is budget slack?
11. Give one way to reduce "spend it or lose it" behaviour.
12. Why should managers not be blamed for every adverse variance?

**Answers**

1. A budget is an agreed plan with targets and limits for which someone is responsible; a forecast is a prediction of what is likely to happen.
2. Any two: measuring performance, allocating resources, controlling, monitoring.
3. Most other budgets depend on the expected level of sales.
4. For example, it carries forward past inefficiencies.
5. When spending needs a full review, such as in a cost-cutting drive or for a new project.
6. $3 000 adverse.
7. $600 favourable.
8. It may come from cheaper, lower-quality materials, leading to complaints and lost sales.
9. $2 per unit × 6 000 = $12 000 flexed; variance $600 adverse.
10. Padding a budget, such as overstating costs or understating sales, so targets are easy to meet.
11. For example, zero budgeting, or allowing unspent money to be carried forward.
12. Some variances have external causes outside their control, such as a national rise in energy prices.
