This practice set covers the individual oral in IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and
Literature: choosing a global issue, pairing a literary and a non-literary extract, structuring the
oral and preparing with your learner portfolio. It draws on the areas of exploration **Readers,
writers and texts** and **Intertextuality: connecting texts**, and applies at both SL and HL. It
follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL 2021).

## Questions

### Passage A -- poem (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Below the waterline**
>
> They told us the valley would make a lake,\
> that it would fill the taps of towns we'd never seen.\
> My father walked the lanes the week before,\
> touching each gate as if to wake it.\
> Now, when the summer is dry, the church\
> lifts its grey shoulder out of the water\
> and I row out to it, and say nothing,\
> the way you say nothing at a grave.

### Passage B -- press release (original text written for Marlbridge; fictional authority)

> **Ferrindale Reservoir opens on schedule**
>
> The Ferrindale Reservoir will supply clean water to 240,000 homes from next spring. The project was
> completed on time and within budget. A small number of properties in the former Ferrindale valley
> were acquired, and residents were offered relocation support. "This is a proud day for the
> region," said the authority's chief engineer.

### Passage C -- campaign leaflet (original text written for Marlbridge; fictional residents' group)

> **FERRINDALE WAS HERE**
>
> 63 homes. 1 school. 1 church. 400 years.
>
> They call it a small number. We call it home.
>
> Join us at the dam wall on 12 June. Bring a name. Say it out loud.

**1.** Using the subject brief's description of the individual oral:

**(a)** State the weighting of the individual oral in the final grade at SL and at HL. **[2]**
**(b)** State which two kinds of studied text the oral connects, and what they must have in common. **[2]**

**2.** Which of these is a workable global issue for an oral, rather than a topic? Choose one and
explain your choice: (i) "Water"; (ii) "Nature in poetry and advertising"; (iii) "How official
language minimises the human cost of large building projects"; (iv) "Rural life". **[3]**

**3.** Narrow the topic "the environment" into an angle that would suit an oral on Passages A and B.
Show the steps and justify your final angle with one reference to each passage. **[3]**

**4.** In Passage A, identify the technique in "the church / lifts its grey shoulder out of the
water" and explain its effect. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how the final two lines of Passage A present the speaker's grief. **[3]**

**6.** Identify two language choices in Passage B that make the loss of the valley seem minor, and
explain the effect of each. **[4]**

**7.** Passage B quotes one person. Explain whose voice is included, whose is missing, and the
effect of this choice. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how Passage C uses numbers and imperatives to present the loss of the valley. **[4]**

**9.** Compare how Passages B and C use numbers to present the same event. Refer to the phrase
Passage C borrows from Passage B. **[4]**

**10.** Describe three learner portfolio entries you would make to prepare an oral on Passages A and
B. **[3]**

**11.** Extended task. Treat Passage A as an extract from a studied poetry collection and Passage B
as one text from a studied body of work of the authority's press releases. Write the spoken
introduction to an individual oral on these two texts. **[6]**

**12.** Extended task. Using the same pairing, write a plan for the rest of the oral: the analysis
of each extract, a link to each whole work, the comparison and the conclusion. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **30%** at SL [1] and **20%** at HL [1].
**(b)** **One literary work and one non-literary body of work** [1], which have approached **a
common global issue** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Two texts" is too vague: the non-literary side is a body of work.

**2.** **(iii)** [1]. It names a tension ("minimises the human cost") that you can argue for or
against [1]. It can be traced in specific language choices in both kinds of text, while (i), (ii)
and (iv) are topic labels with nothing to prove [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say why the issue is arguable, not just that it is "more specific".

**3.** Topic "the environment" → issue: **communities displaced by large water projects** [1] →
angle: **how official and personal voices present the loss of a village: one as progress, the other
as grief** [1]. Justification: Passage B turns homes into "A small number of properties", while
Passage A shows the father "touching each gate as if to wake it" [1].
*Examiner insight:* The angle scores only when it is tested against both texts; a well-worded angle
with evidence from one passage only is incomplete.

**4.** **Personification**: the church "lifts its grey shoulder" like a body [1]. The drowned
village is presented as a person half-buried, still present under the water [1]. Because it
surfaces "when the summer is dry", the loss is shown as unfinished: the past keeps rising back into
view [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming "personification" earns little alone; the marks sit in the effect.

**5.** The repetition of "say nothing" stresses the speaker's silence [1]. The simile "the way you
say nothing at a grave" turns the valley into a death that is mourned, not a project that is
finished [1]. This silence contrasts with the confident promise in the opening lines that the lake "would fill
the taps of towns we'd never seen" [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers connect the ending to the opening; weak answers treat the last
lines as if they stood alone.

**6.** Choice 1: the vague quantifier and noun in "A small number of properties" [1] reduce
people's homes to an unspecified count of assets, so the loss sounds small and impersonal [1].
Choice 2: the passive "were acquired" [1] hides who took the homes and how, so the authority's
responsibility disappears from the sentence [1]. (Also creditworthy: "former Ferrindale valley",
which writes the valley into the past.)
*Examiner insight:* Each choice needs its own quotation and its own effect, not one shared comment.

**7.** Only the official voice is quoted: the chief engineer's "This is a proud day for the region"
[1]. The residents are named only as "residents" and never speak [1]. The effect is that the event
is framed as a shared success, and "the region" absorbs the valley's loss into a wider gain [1].
*Examiner insight:* Absence counts as analysis only if you say what it does to the reader's view.

**8.** The list "63 homes. 1 school. 1 church. 400 years." makes the loss exact and countable [1].
Its short fragments read like an inscription on a memorial, so the leaflet mourns as well as
informs [1]. The imperatives "Join us", "Bring a name" and "Say it out loud" call readers to act
together [1]. The final command turns silence into public speech [1].
*Examiner insight:* In non-literary texts, full stops and line breaks are choices to analyse too.

**9.** Passage B uses a large figure, "240,000 homes", to show the scale of the benefit [1], while
the loss gets no figure at all: "A small number of properties" [1]. Passage C supplies the missing
numbers, "63 homes", and adds time, "400 years" [1]. By borrowing B's phrase in "They call it a
small number", C answers the release directly: the same event reads as progress or loss depending
on which numbers are printed [1].
*Examiner insight:* Comparison earns most when both texts meet in the same sentence.

**10.** An **issue log** entry noting that both passages deal with displacement, with the key
quotations located [1]. A **technique bank** entry recording, for example, the passive "were
acquired" and the personified church, each with its effect in one line [1]. A **pairing trial or
rehearsal reflection**: a draft thesis for this pair, or notes after a timed run-through on what ran
long or stayed vague [1].
*Examiner insight:* Entries must be specific to the texts; "revise techniques" earns nothing.

**11.** The real oral is assessed holistically against the IB criteria, which your teacher will
share, not point by point. Model introduction:

> "My global issue is how large building projects are presented to the public, and how that
> presentation can hide the people who lose their homes. I am looking at an extract from the poetry
> collection and a press release from the authority's series on the reservoir. My argument is that the press release uses scale and the
> passive voice to make the valley vanish, while the poem uses silence and the image of a rising
> church to keep it present. I will start with the poem."

- The global issue is stated as an angle, not a topic [1].
- Both texts are named with their form (poetry collection; press releases) [1].
- The thesis makes a comparison between the two texts [1].
- The thesis names a specific method in each text (scale and passive; silence and imagery) [1].
- A route is previewed ("I will start with the poem") [1].
- The register is spoken and clear, with a signpost the listener can follow [1].

*Examiner insight:* A strong opening commits to an argument at once; a weak one gives background
facts about reservoirs.

**12.** The real oral is assessed holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point. A
strong plan contains these indicative points.

- Poem: "They told us" -- the unnamed authority speaks and "us" only listens [1].
- Poem: the father "touching each gate as if to wake it" treats the village as asleep, not dead [1].
- A precise link to a named later poem in the collection [1].
- Release: "240,000 homes" set against "A small number of properties" -- benefit counted, loss left
  vague [1].
- Release: passive "were acquired" and "former Ferrindale valley" remove the agent and write the
  valley into the past [1].
- A precise link to another release in the series showing the same pattern [1].
- Comparison: the engineer's "proud day" against a speaker who can only "say nothing" [1].
- Comparison: official voices dominate both texts, but the poem exposes the imbalance while the
  release repeats it [1].
- Conclusion answers the thesis: grammar and scale make the loss vanish; the poem makes it
  resurface [1].
- Balanced time and clear signposts between sections [1].

*Examiner insight:* In strong plans every point returns to the global issue; otherwise the oral
reads as two separate commentaries.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a topic ("water", "the environment") instead of an angle you can argue.
- Giving facts or opinions about the real-world issue that are not tied to a quotation.
- Naming a technique without explaining what it does to the reader.
- Ignoring non-literary form: fragments, line breaks, headlines and layout are all choices.
- Treating one text in depth and the other briefly.
- Comparing only in the final sentence, so the texts never meet.
- Vague links to the whole work ("this is typical of the collection") with no named moment.
