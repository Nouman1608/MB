## 2.6 Communication: definitions

- **Communication**: passing information, ideas or instructions from a sender to a receiver so that the message is understood.
- **Formal communication**: through official channels, usually planned and recorded.
- **Informal communication**: outside official channels, unplanned and rarely recorded.
- **Grapevine**: the unofficial network through which news and rumour travel.
- **Vertical communication**: up or down the hierarchy. **Horizontal communication**: between people at the same level.
- **One-way**: no reply expected. **Two-way**: feedback is built in.
- **Barrier**: anything that stops a message being sent, received or understood as intended.

## Formal versus informal: what to weigh

| | Formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Meetings with minutes, emails, memos, reports, intranet, notice boards, appraisals, presentations | Corridor chat, lunch talk, messaging between colleagues, social events, the grapevine |
| Strengths | Record, consistency, accountability, clear authority | Speed, flexibility, builds relationships, quick feedback |
| Limits | Slow, impersonal, often one-way, can be rigid | No record, rumour and distortion, uneven reach |
| Best for | Safety, legal, contractual or policy messages; large audiences | Quick questions, morale, testing reactions, small teams |

### Method in steps: choosing a method (AO3)

1. Identify the message: urgent, sensitive, complex, routine?
2. Identify the audience: how many, where, and on what shifts?
3. Decide whether a record is needed.
4. Decide whether feedback is needed (two-way) or not (one-way).
5. Pick a main method, and name a supporting one that covers its gap.
6. Judge: say which is best in this case and why the other is weaker here.

## Barriers to communication

- Language and jargon.
- Cultural differences in norms about directness, status and gestures.
- Noise and distraction.
- Information overload.
- Many levels of hierarchy, which delay or filter messages.
- Status and attitude, such as staff afraid to speak up.
- Poor channel choice.
- Technology problems or lack of access.
- Lack of feedback.
- Distance and time zones.

Method for AO2 answers: name the barrier, link it to a fact in the case, explain the effect on the business (errors, delay, low morale, lost sales), then say what could reduce it.

## 2.7 Industrial/employee relations (HL only)

### Sources of conflict

- Pay and benefits.
- Working conditions, hours and rotas.
- Job security, redundancies and closure fears.
- Change in technology or working practices.
- Management style and poor communication.
- Differences in aims between owners or managers and employees.

### Approaches to conflict

| Action | Who | Meaning | Effect and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collective bargaining | Employees and employers | Negotiation between employer and employee representatives | Stronger voice for staff and a settled outcome, but talks take time and may fail |
| Work-to-rule | Employees | Do only what the contract and rules require | Output falls, pay continues, but goodwill is lost |
| Strike action | Employees | Stop work completely | Heavy pressure on employer, but lost pay for staff |
| Threats of redundancies | Employers | Warn that jobs may go | Pressure on staff, but trust and skilled staff may be lost |
| Changes of contract | Employers | Alter terms such as hours or pay | Can force change, but may provoke strikes |
| Closure | Employers | Shut a site | Drastic and costly, with large effects on the community |
| Lockouts | Employers | Stop staff entering until terms are accepted | Output stops for both sides, so very costly |

### Conflict resolution

| Approach | What it is | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conciliation | Neutral third party helps the sides reach their own agreement | Keeps control with the sides | No outcome is forced |
| Arbitration | Neutral third party rules on the dispute | A definite result | Sides may feel the ruling was imposed |
| Employee participation and industrial democracy | Staff take part in decisions through consultation, councils or representation | Staff feel heard, so less conflict | Slower decisions |
| No-strike agreement | Union agrees not to strike, usually in exchange for an agreed way of settling disputes | Continuity of output | Removes staff's strongest tool |
| Single-union agreement | Employer bargains with one union for all staff | Simpler, quicker bargaining | One union may not represent every group |

## Must-know distinctions

- Conciliation helps the sides agree. Arbitration produces a ruling.
- Work-to-rule: staff keep working to the letter of the rules. A strike: staff stop work.
- Strike: employee action. Lockout: employer action.
- Collective bargaining appears on both lists: employees and employers both take part.
- Industrial democracy is about involvement in decisions. A no-strike agreement is about not stopping work.
- Formal is not always better than informal. Fit to the situation decides.

## Worked reminder: pay gap

A firm has 80 staff at an average of 30,000 each, so payroll is 80 × 30,000 = 2,400,000. A claim of 4% costs 96,000 and an offer of 2.5% costs 60,000. The gap is 36,000, or 450 per employee. Compare this gap with the cost of a stoppage before judging how far each side should move.

## Building an AO3 answer on these topics

For a question that asks you to evaluate, justify or recommend, a reliable shape is:

1. A short judgement in your first sentence, so the reader knows your position.
2. Two or three reasons for it, each tied to a fact or figure in the case.
3. The strongest counter-point, with the reason you still hold your view.
4. A closing sentence that names the conditions under which you would change your mind, such as a longer dispute, a larger workforce or a more sensitive message.

The guide's command-term glossary defines evaluate as weighing up strengths and limitations, and justify as giving valid reasons or evidence to support a conclusion. Use those meanings to decide what your answer must contain.

### Linking the two sections

Poor communication is itself a source of conflict. A rumour on the grapevine about closure can start a dispute before any official decision exists. Equally, employee participation and industrial democracy are communication tools as well as resolution tools, because they create two-way channels between staff and managers. When a case mixes both topics, say how better communication could prevent the dispute or shorten it.

## Quick self-test

1. Define informal communication.
2. State two formal methods of communication.
3. Give two barriers to communication that can arise on a multi-site business.
4. Distinguish a lockout from a strike.
5. State two sources of conflict in the workplace.
6. Outline what work-to-rule involves.
7. Distinguish conciliation from arbitration.
8. Explain one reason an employer might prefer a single-union agreement.
9. Explain one risk of a no-strike agreement for employees.
10. A union claims 4% and the employer offers 2.5% on a payroll of 2,400,000. Calculate the cost gap.

### Answers

1. Communication outside official channels, such as chat in the corridor or on a messaging app, which is not planned or usually recorded.
2. Any two: meetings with minutes, emails, memos, reports, notice boards, intranet, appraisals, presentations.
3. Any two: time zones and distance, language, cultural differences, technology access, many levels of hierarchy.
4. A strike is employees stopping work. A lockout is the employer keeping employees out of the workplace until terms are accepted.
5. Any two: pay, hours or conditions, job security, change, management style, poor communication.
6. Staff do exactly what the contract and rules require and give up extras such as unpaid overtime, so output falls while they are still at work and paid.
7. In conciliation a neutral party helps the sides reach their own agreement. In arbitration a neutral party gives a ruling.
8. Bargaining with one union is simpler and quicker than dealing with several, which can mean fewer conflicting demands.
9. Staff give up their strongest form of pressure, so if the agreed procedure is slow or unfair they have limited ways to push for change.
10. 96,000 minus 60,000 is **36,000**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a method of communication without judging whether it suits the situation.
- Listing barriers with no link to facts in the case.
- Writing that informal communication is unreliable and stopping there, with no benefit named.
- Mixing up conciliation and arbitration.
- Describing a lockout as something unions do.
- Saying a strike harms only the employer, when staff lose pay.
- Giving one side only in an evaluate or recommend answer.
- Ending without a clear judgement tied to the business in the case.
