## Section A

**1.** Explain, in terms of electronegativity, why the H–F bond is polar. **[2]**

**2.** Which molecule has no overall dipole moment?

A: NH₃

B: CHCl₃

C: BF₃

D: H₂O **[1]**

**3.** Name the only type of intermolecular force between molecules of iodine, I₂. **[1]**

**4.** State the general relationship between the strength of covalent bonds and the strength of intermolecular forces. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why there is no hydrogen bonding between molecules of methane, even though methane contains hydrogen. **[2]**

**6.** State what is meant by the statement that hydrogen bonding is a "special case" of permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Fluorine, F₂, and hydrogen chloride, HCl, each have 18 electrons per molecule. Their boiling points are −188 °C and −85 °C.

**(a)** Explain why the comparison of these two substances is a fair test of the effect of pd-pd forces. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the difference in boiling points. **[3]**

**8.** Pentane and 2,2-dimethylpropane are isomers of C₅H₁₂. Pentane boils at 36 °C and 2,2-dimethylpropane at about 10 °C.

**(a)** Name the intermolecular forces present in both. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the difference in boiling points. **[2]**

**9.** Draw, or describe in words, a hydrogen bond between two ammonia molecules. Your answer should include partial charges, any lone pairs involved and the bond angle at the hydrogen atom. **[3]**

**10.** The boiling points of the Group 16 hydrides are H₂O 100 °C, H₂S −60 °C, H₂Se −41 °C, H₂Te −2 °C.

**(a)** Explain the trend from H₂S to H₂Te. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why water does not follow the trend. **[2]**

**11.** Ice floats on liquid water.

**(a)** Describe the arrangement of water molecules in ice. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why this makes ice less dense than liquid water. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why water has a relatively high surface tension. **[1]**

**12.** Water boils at 100 °C and ammonia at −33 °C.

**(a)** State the number of hydrogen atoms and lone pairs available for hydrogen bonding in each molecule. **[2]**

**(b)** Use your answer to explain the difference in boiling points. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Fluorine is much more electronegative than hydrogen [1], so the bonding pair is pulled towards F, making F δ− and H δ+ [1].

**2.** C [1].

**3.** Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id / London dispersion) forces [1].

**4.** In general, covalent (and ionic and metallic) bonds are stronger than intermolecular forces [1].

**5.** The C–H bond is only slightly polar because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities [1]; hydrogen bonding needs H bonded to N or O, and methane has no lone pairs on its carbon atom [1].

**6.** It is an attraction between permanent dipoles [1], occurring specifically when hydrogen is bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N or O), which makes it the strongest type of pd-pd force [1].

**7. (a)** Both have the same number of electrons, so their id-id forces are similar [1].

**(b)** F₂ is non-polar, with id-id forces only [1]. HCl is polar (Cl more electronegative than H) [1], so it also has pd-pd forces, and more energy is needed to separate its molecules [1].

**8. (a)** id-id (London dispersion) forces [1].

**(b)** Both have the same number of electrons, but pentane molecules are long and can touch over a larger surface area [1], so the id-id forces are stronger and more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1].

**9.** δ+ on an H atom of one NH₃ and δ− on N of the other [1]; hydrogen bond (dashed line) from the H to the lone pair on N of the second molecule [1]; N–H···N angle 180° (linear) [1].

**10. (a)** The number of electrons increases from H₂S to H₂Te [1], so the id-id forces become stronger and more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1].

**(b)** Water forms hydrogen bonds between molecules (H bonded to O, lone pairs on O) [1]; these are much stronger than the forces between the other hydride molecules, so much more energy is needed to boil water [1].

**11. (a)** Each water molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others [1] in a regular, ordered three-dimensional (tetrahedral) arrangement [1].

**(b)** The hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open structure, further apart than in the liquid [1]; when ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules move closer, so the same mass of liquid occupies less volume [1].

**(c)** Surface molecules are pulled inwards and sideways by hydrogen bonds to neighbouring molecules, with no balancing pull from above [1].

**12. (a)** H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and two lone pairs [1]; NH₃: three hydrogen atoms and one lone pair [1].

**(b)** Each hydrogen bond needs one δ+ H and one lone pair, so water can form two hydrogen bonds per molecule on average but ammonia only one [1]; water has more hydrogen bonding, so more energy is needed to separate its molecules [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "non-polar molecules have no intermolecular forces".
- Leaving the lone pair out of a hydrogen-bond diagram.
- Explaining a boiling point difference without naming the forces in each substance.
- Ignoring shape when deciding whether a molecule is polar.
- Explaining ice's density with "stronger hydrogen bonds" instead of an open, ordered structure.
- Saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecular liquid boils.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are fluorine compounds tested for hydrogen bonding?**
The syllabus limits hydrogen bonding to molecules containing N–H and O–H groups, so questions focus on those, such as ammonia, water and alcohols.
