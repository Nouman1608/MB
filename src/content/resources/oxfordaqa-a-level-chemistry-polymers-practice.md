Section 3.3.12, Polymers, is the focus of this set, taken from OxfordAQA's International AS and A-level Chemistry specification (9620), Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams from May/June 2020: 3.3.12.1 Condensation polymers and 3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers. The whole section is **International A-level only**. It belongs to the International A2 organic content assessed in Unit 4, and Unit 5 may test anything in the course.

If the ideas are new, start with the [polymers study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-polymers/) and the [polymers revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-polymers-revision-notes/). Where a question needs relative atomic masses, use Cl = 35.5, C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0 and O = 16.0. C₆H₄ in a formula means a benzene ring bonded at positions 1 and 4.

## Questions

**1.** Which of these monomer pairs forms a polyester?

- A benzene-1,4-diamine and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid
- B propene and but-1-ene
- C butane-1,4-diol and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid
- D hexane-1,6-diamine and propene

**[1]**

**2.** Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid reacts with pentane-1,5-diol, HO–(CH₂)₅–OH, to form a condensation polymer.

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit of the polymer. **[2]**
**(b)** Complete the equation for n molecules of each monomer, showing the inorganic product and its amount. **[1]**

**3.** A section of a polymer chain is

`–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₈–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₈–NH–`

**(a)** Draw the repeating unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Draw the structures of the two monomers and name them. **[2]**

**4.** A section of a polyamide made from two amino acids is

`–NH–CH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)–CO–NH–(CH₂)₃–CO–NH–CH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)–CO–NH–(CH₂)₃–CO–`

**(a)** Draw the structures of the two amino acids. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why an amino acid can form a polymer without a second type of monomer. **[1]**

**5.** State one typical use of each of Terylene, nylon 6,6 and Kevlar. **[3]**

**6.** Kevlar fibres are much stronger than Terylene fibres. Explain this difference in terms of the intermolecular forces between the polymer chains. **[4]**

**7.** This question is about the hydrolysis of polymers.

**(a)** The polyester in question 2 is refluxed with dilute hydrochloric acid. Give the names of both organic products. **[2]**
**(b)** The polyamide in question 3 is boiled with aqueous sodium hydroxide. What are the two organic products? **[2]**
**(c)** Poly(phenylethene) is not affected by either treatment. Explain why. **[2]**

**8.** This question is about the polyamide in question 3.

**(a)** Calculate the Mr of its repeating unit. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** One sample has an average chain Mr of 6.35 × 10⁴. How many repeating units does an average chain contain? **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in the polymer. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[1]**

**9.** Polymer X is heated with dilute hydrochloric acid. The only products are benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and a salt Y, with formula C₁₀H₂₆N₂Cl₂, made from an unbranched diamine with an –NH₂ group at each end.

**(a)** Give the structure and the name of the diamine that Y is made from. **[2]**
**(b)** Draw the repeating unit of X. **[1]**
**(c)** State the type of condensation polymer that X is. **[1]**
**(d)** Name the two organic products formed if X is heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide instead. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the Mr of the repeating unit of X. **[1]**

**10.** A sportswear firm collects worn-out Terylene shirts and used poly(ethene) packaging film from its shops. Evaluate landfill, incineration and recycling as ways of dealing with this waste. **[6]**

**11.** 11-aminoundecanoic acid, H₂N–(CH₂)₁₀–COOH, forms a polyamide.

**(a)** Write an equation for the polymerisation, showing the repeating unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Chains in a sample have a mean Mr of 3.66 × 10⁴. Work out the mean number of repeating units in one chain. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of monomer needed to make 50.0 kg of the polymer, assuming complete conversion. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why this polyamide is biodegradable but poly(ethene) is not. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A diol with a dicarboxylic acid is the only pair that gives ester links; A gives a polyamide, and B and D cannot form ester links at all.

**2. (a)** One residue of each monomer joined by an ester link, with a second link split across the brackets [1]; bonds through both brackets with n outside: **–[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–(CH₂)₅–O]ₙ–** [1]
**(b)** n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH + n HO–(CH₂)₅–OH → –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–(CH₂)₅–O]ₙ– + **2n H₂O** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the count of CH₂ groups in the diol residue against the name; a unit with four or six CH₂ groups is drawn from the wrong monomer and is unlikely to score.

**3. (a)** **–[CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₈–NH]–** with bonds through the brackets [1]
**(b)** **HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH, hexanedioic acid** [1]; **H₂N–(CH₂)₈–NH₂, octane-1,8-diamine** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each structure with its correct name, and count the carbons in each residue before naming, including the two carbonyl carbons of the acid.

**4. (a)** **H₂N–CH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)–COOH** [1]; **H₂N–(CH₂)₃–COOH** [1]
**(b)** Each molecule has both an –NH₂ group and a –COOH group, so the amine end of one molecule reacts with the acid end of another, and the chain can grow at both ends [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Drawing the residues (–NH–...–CO–) rather than complete amino acids does not answer the question; add H to the N and OH to the C.

**5.** Terylene: clothing fibres or drinks bottles [1]. Nylon 6,6: clothing, carpets or ropes [1]. Kevlar: body armour or bulletproof vests [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a specific use for each polymer; "making plastics" or "materials" is too vague to credit.

**6.** Terylene chains are held by van der Waals forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces between polar C=O groups [1]. No hydrogen in the ester link is attached to an O or N atom, so Terylene chains cannot hydrogen bond to each other [1]. Kevlar chains form hydrogen bonds: the δ+ hydrogen of an N–H group is drawn to a lone pair on a carbonyl oxygen in the next chain [1]. Kevlar's flat, rigid chains line up in parallel, so there are very many of these stronger hydrogen bonds, and more energy is needed to separate the chains [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that talks about breaking covalent bonds in the chain contradicts itself; the comparison must be about forces between chains.

**7. (a)** **Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid** [1]; **pentane-1,5-diol** [1]
**(b)** **Sodium hexanedioate** [1]; **octane-1,8-diamine** [1]
**(c)** Its chain holds only C–C and C–H bonds, which are essentially non-polar [1]; with no δ+ carbon present, neither water nor OH⁻ has a site to attack, so there is no link to hydrolyse [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the free acid is not the product in alkali; it is the sodium salt. In (c), saying the bonds are "too strong" without mentioning polarity misses the key idea.

**8. (a)** Repeating unit formula C₁₄H₂₆N₂O₂ [1]; Mr = 14(12.0) + 26(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = **254.0** [1]
(check: 146.0 + 144.0 − 2 × 18.0 = 254.0)
**(b)** 6.35 × 10⁴ ÷ 254.0 = **250** [1]
**(c)** % N = 28.0 ÷ 254.0 × 100 = **11.0%** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula of the repeating unit before the Mr, so your method is clear and any slip in the arithmetic is easy to spot.

**9. (a)** **H₂N–(CH₂)₁₀–NH₂** [1]; **decane-1,10-diamine** [1]
**(b)** **–[CO–C₆H₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₁₀–NH]–** with bonds through the brackets [1]
**(c)** **Polyamide** [1]
**(d)** **Sodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate** [1]; **decane-1,10-diamine** [1]
**(e)** C₁₈H₂₆N₂O₂: 18(12.0) + 26(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = **302.0** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* For (a), Y is the diammonium chloride, ⁺H₃N–(CH₂)₁₀–NH₃⁺ 2Cl⁻; work back by removing the two HCl units (C₁₀H₂₆N₂Cl₂ − 2HCl = C₁₀H₂₄N₂) before drawing the diamine.

**10.** Landfill: low cost, and the two materials need not be separated [1]; against this, it occupies land, and poly(ethene) film is non-biodegradable, so it persists almost unchanged [1].
Incineration: shrinks the waste and the heat given out can be used to generate electricity [1]; but it produces CO₂, a greenhouse gas, and the raw materials are lost [1].
Recycling: conserves crude oil, uses less landfill and recovers the material [1]; however, the Terylene and poly(ethene) must be collected, separated and cleaned first, which costs energy and money, and quality can fall; Terylene could instead be hydrolysed back to its monomers, which needs energy and specialist plant [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs both sides for each method, and each point should be tied to these polymers; generic statements such as "bad for the environment" earn little.

**11. (a)** n H₂N–(CH₂)₁₀–COOH → **–[NH–(CH₂)₁₀–CO]ₙ–** [1] **+ n H₂O** [1]
**(b)** Repeating unit C₁₁H₂₁NO, Mr = 11(12.0) + 21(1.0) + 14.0 + 16.0 = 183.0 [1]; 3.66 × 10⁴ ÷ 183.0 = **200** [1]
**(c)** Each repeating unit (183.0 g mol⁻¹) comes from one monomer molecule (Mr 201.0) [1]; mass = 50.0 × 201.0 ÷ 183.0 = **54.9 kg** [1]
**(d)** The amide links contain a polar C=O bond, so the carbonyl carbon is δ+ [1]; water (or OH⁻ or H⁺/H₂O) can attack it and hydrolyse the link, breaking the chain into monomers [1]. Poly(ethene) contains just C–C and C–H bonds, which are essentially non-polar, so a nucleophile finds no target and the polymer is inert [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), one monomer gives n H₂O, not 2n; in (c), using the polymer Mr for both masses gives 50.0 kg, which ignores the water lost.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing a two-monomer repeating unit with a single residue, or with the same monomer's residue twice.
- Unit ends left with –H or –OH instead of bonds through the brackets.
- 2n H₂O written for a single amino acid, or n H₂O for a diacid with a diol.
- Monomer names that miscount carbons, especially forgetting the two carbonyl carbons of a diacid.
- Hydrogen bonds claimed between polyester chains.
- Hydrogen bonds drawn from N–H to N instead of to the C=O oxygen.
- Free acid given as an alkaline-hydrolysis product, or free diamine as an acid-hydrolysis product.
- Polyalkene inertness explained by "strong bonds" with no mention of non-polar bonds or the lack of a δ+ carbon.
- Disposal answers that give only advantages, or only problems.
