## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by economies of scale. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between internal and external economies of scale. **[3]**

**3.** Kelsby Cycles has fixed costs of £200,000 a year and variable costs of £3.50 a unit. Calculate its average cost when it makes 40,000 units. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** marketing economy of scale. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an external economy of scale?

A: A discount for buying frames in bulk.

B: A local college training skilled assemblers.

C: A new, larger paint-spraying machine.

D: A lower interest rate on a bank loan.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Kelsby Cycles makes 100,000 cycles a year. Its fixed costs are £200,000 and its variable cost is £3.50 a unit.

**(a)** Calculate the average cost per unit at 100,000 units. **[2]**

**(b)** A supplier offers a discount that reduces the variable cost to £3.20 a unit at 100,000 units. Calculate the new average cost and the percentage fall from the figure in part (a). Give your answer to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Kelsby is considering expanding to 250,000 units. Fixed costs would rise to £320,000 and the variable cost would stay at £3.20. Each unit sells for £6.00. Evaluate whether Kelsby should expand. **[8]**

**7.** Ferrin Foods has three factories. At 1,000,000 units its total cost is £2,200,000. At 1,500,000 units its total cost is £3,600,000.

**(a)** Calculate the average cost at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse **two** possible causes of the change in average cost. **[4]**

**8.** Dunmere is a small town where several firms making electronic parts have opened in the last ten years. A new training college and a faster rail link have been built. Rents for factory units have since doubled.

Evaluate the external economies and diseconomies of scale for a firm that is thinking of locating there. **[8]**

**9.** Two printing firms, Alder Print and Bastow Press, plan to merge. The merged firm expects to save 4% on a combined paper bill of £800,000 a year and £45,000 a year by combining administration. It will need extra managers costing £55,000 a year. One-off merger costs are £96,000.

**(a)** Calculate the net annual benefit and the time taken to recover the one-off costs. Give the time in years to one decimal place. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether the merger is likely to benefit the stakeholders of the two firms. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Economies of scale are falls in the average (unit) cost of production [1] that result from an increase in the scale of output, for example because fixed costs are spread over more units or bulk discounts are earned [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Internal economies come from the growth of the business itself [1]. External economies come from the growth of the industry or the area in which the business operates [1]. The business has some control over internal economies, but little or none over external ones [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Total cost = £200,000 + (£3.50 × 40,000) = £340,000 [1]. Average cost = £340,000 ÷ 40,000 = **£8.50** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The cost of advertising is mainly fixed, so a national campaign costs the same whatever the sales [1]. If output and sales are higher, the cost is spread over more units, so the marketing cost per unit falls [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Total cost = £200,000 + (£3.50 × 100,000) = £550,000 [1]. Average cost = £550,000 ÷ 100,000 = **£5.50** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Total cost = £200,000 + (£3.20 × 100,000) = £520,000, so average cost = **£5.20** [1]. Fall = £0.30 [1]. Percentage fall = 0.30 ÷ 5.50 × 100 = **5.5%** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Average cost at 250,000 units = (£320,000 + £3.20 × 250,000) ÷ 250,000 = £1,120,000 ÷ 250,000 = £4.48, which is £0.72 lower than the £5.20 at 100,000 units because fixed costs are spread over more units [1].
Profit now = £600,000 − £520,000 = £80,000. Profit at 250,000 units = £1,500,000 − £1,120,000 = £380,000 [1] [1].
Expansion therefore raises profit by £300,000, a large gain, if all the extra units sell at £6.00 [1].
Risks: demand may not support 150,000 more units at that price, so unsold stock or lower prices would cut profit [1]. The fixed cost rises by £120,000, and this must be financed and is paid even if sales are weak [1]. Larger output may bring diseconomies such as communication problems, which the figures ignore [1].
Judgement: Kelsby should expand if market research shows that demand will reach near 250,000 units, perhaps in stages so that the risk is limited; without that evidence a smaller step would be safer [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** At 1,000,000 units: £2,200,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = **£2.20** [1]. At 1,500,000 units: £3,600,000 ÷ 1,500,000 = **£2.40** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Average cost has risen by £0.20, which shows diseconomies of scale. Award 2 marks for each of two causes, one for identifying it and one for explaining it. Communication problems across three sites [1], so errors and waste increase and decisions are slow [1]. Lack of coordination or control of the larger workforce [1], leading to rework and delay [1]. Lack of motivation as employees feel remote from managers [1], leading to absence and poorer quality [1]. **[4]**

**8.** External economies: the training college provides a pool of skilled workers, cutting training and recruitment costs [1]. The rail link lowers transport costs and delivery times [1].
External diseconomies: doubled rents raise fixed costs for the firm [1]. Competition for skilled workers may push up wages [1]. More firms may cause congestion and strain local services [1].
Evaluation: the net effect depends on the size of the firm's rent bill compared with its labour and transport savings [1]. A firm that needs skilled labour and rail access would probably gain more than it loses [1]. Judgement: the site is worth choosing for a labour-intensive firm that values the skills pool, but not for a firm needing a large, cheap site, since rents may rise further as more firms arrive [1]. **[8]**

**9. (a)** Paper saving = 4% × £800,000 = £32,000 [1]. Net annual benefit = £32,000 + £45,000 − £55,000 = **£22,000** [1]. Time to recover = £96,000 ÷ £22,000 [1] = **4.4 years** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Owners: net saving of £22,000 a year increases profit, but it takes over four years to recover the one-off costs [1]. Employees: the administration saving probably means redundancies, which harms the staff affected, though a stronger firm may protect other jobs [1]. Suppliers: a larger paper order gives a bigger customer, but the supplier must accept a 4% lower price and loses bargaining power [1]. Customers: they may gain if savings are passed on in prices, or may lose the personal service of a small firm [1]. Managers: extra management cost of £55,000 suggests more posts and promotion opportunities [1].
Evaluation: the saving is modest relative to the combined paper bill of £800,000 and the figures are estimates [1]. Merging two cultures could cause diseconomies such as poor communication and falling morale, which would reduce the saving [1]. The case is stronger if the merger also wins larger contracts [1]. Judgement: the merger is likely to benefit owners in the long run, but the effect on employees and customers is uncertain, so it depends on how well the integration is managed [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving labels (such as "technical economies") without explaining why unit cost falls.
- Mixing up internal and external economies.
- Calculating total cost but forgetting to divide by output.
- Assuming that lower average cost always means higher profit, without checking demand.
- Evaluating with only the benefits and not the costs and risks of growth.
