This guide covers subtopic **1.2 Classification of businesses** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series). The combined [Business Activity and Classification study guide](/resources/business-activity-and-classification/) introduces the three sectors and the private and public sectors. This page goes further: classifying awkward cases, measuring how important each sector is, explaining why that importance changes in developing and developed economies, and using the private and public sector distinction in a mixed economy.

## What the syllabus asks for

- **1.2.1** Economic sectors in terms of primary, secondary and tertiary sectors:
  - basis of business classification, using examples to illustrate the classification
  - reasons for the changing importance of business classification, for example in developed and developing economies
- **1.2.2** Classify business enterprises between private sector and public sector in a mixed economy.

## The basis of classification

Businesses are classified by **what they do** at each stage of turning natural resources into something a customer uses.

- **Primary sector**: extracting or growing natural resources (farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil extraction).
- **Secondary sector**: turning raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods (manufacturing, processing, construction).
- **Tertiary sector**: providing services to businesses and consumers (retail, transport, banking, insurance, tourism, education, health care).

### Following a product through the sectors

A cotton shirt shows how the sectors link together in a **chain of production**:

| Stage | Activity | Sector |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton farm | Grows and picks cotton | Primary |
| Spinning mill | Spins cotton into yarn | Secondary |
| Garment factory | Cuts and sews shirts | Secondary |
| Lorry firm | Delivers shirts to shops | Tertiary |
| Clothing shop | Sells to customers | Tertiary |
| Bank | Lends to every firm in the chain | Tertiary |

Each stage depends on the others. A poor cotton harvest raises costs for the mill and factory; a fall in shop sales cuts orders all the way back to the farm. This **interdependence** is a useful point when a question asks how a change in one sector affects another.

### Awkward cases

Classify the **activity** in the question, not the business name.

| Business activity | Sector | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A fish farm raising fish in tanks | Primary | It grows a natural resource, even though it uses technology |
| A fish-canning factory | Secondary | It processes a raw material into a product |
| A house builder | Secondary | Construction is part of the secondary sector |
| A software developer selling apps | Tertiary | It provides a service, even though it is "high-tech" |
| A farmer selling vegetables at her own roadside stall | Primary and tertiary | Growing is primary; selling to the public is tertiary |

## Measuring the importance of each sector

The importance of a sector is usually measured by its share of a country's **employment** or its share of total **output**.

**Worked example 1 (invented figures).** A country has the following employment, in millions of workers:

| Sector | Year 1 | Year 20 |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 12.0 | 8.0 |
| Secondary | 5.0 | 9.0 |
| Tertiary | 3.0 | 8.0 |
| Total | 20.0 | 25.0 |

```
Primary share:   Year 1: 12 ÷ 20 × 100 = 60%    Year 20: 8 ÷ 25 × 100 = 32%
Secondary share: Year 1:  5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%    Year 20: 9 ÷ 25 × 100 = 36%
Tertiary share:  Year 1:  3 ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%    Year 20: 8 ÷ 25 × 100 = 32%
```

The primary sector is still large but has shrunk from 60% to 32%, while the secondary sector has grown. This pattern suggests a country that is **industrialising**. The number of tertiary workers almost tripled, from 3 million to 8 million, so by Year 20 the tertiary sector employs as many people as the primary sector. The number of primary workers fell, but in many countries a sector's share falls while its number of workers stays the same or rises, so check which measure the question uses.

## Why the importance of the sectors changes

### In developing economies

- **Farming becomes more mechanised**, so fewer workers are needed on the land and many move to towns.
- **Manufacturing grows**, often helped by investment from foreign businesses attracted by lower wage costs.
- **Rising incomes** from factory jobs increase spending on manufactured goods and, later, on services.
- Natural resources such as minerals may be exported raw at first; over time more processing is done at home, adding value in the secondary sector.

### In developed economies

- **Incomes are high**, so a larger part of spending goes on services such as travel, restaurants, health and leisure.
- **Manufacturing faces competition** from countries with lower costs, so some factories close or move abroad. A fall in the importance of the secondary sector is called **deindustrialisation**.
- **Automation and technology** mean fewer workers are needed to make the same factory output.
- **Natural resources may run out**, or become too costly to extract compared with imports, shrinking the primary sector further.

### Why this matters to businesses

A business in a growing sector can expect more customers and easier access to skilled workers and suppliers. A business in a shrinking sector may face falling demand and must find new markets, cut costs or change what it produces. Examiners reward answers that link the national trend to the business in the question.

## Private sector and public sector in a mixed economy

A **mixed economy** has both a private sector and a public sector.

- **Private sector**: businesses owned by individuals or groups of private shareholders, such as sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies. Most aim to make a profit.
- **Public sector**: organisations owned and controlled by the government, such as state schools, state hospitals and public corporations.

**Worked example 2: classifying by ownership.**

| Organisation | Sector | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A public limited company whose shares are traded on a stock exchange | Private | Owned by private shareholders, despite the word "public" |
| A state-owned railway | Public | Owned and controlled by the government |
| A family-owned private hospital | Private | Owned by private individuals |
| A charity running a school | Private | Not owned by the government |

### Why some activities are in the public sector

Governments often keep activities in the public sector when the service is essential and should be available to everyone whatever their ability to pay (basic health care, education), when private businesses might not provide enough of it (rural transport), or when the activity is of national importance (water supply, defence). The exact mix differs between countries and changes over time, so always use the details given in the question.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying a whole business when the question asks about one activity.
- Calling construction tertiary, or calling high-technology services secondary.
- Treating a fall in the **share** of a sector as a fall in the **number** of workers in it.
- Confusing a public limited company with the public sector.
- Explaining sector change without a reason, such as rising incomes or automation.

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Identify**: name the sector for the activity given.
- **Calculate**: show the division and the percentage, as in worked example 1.
- **Explain**: give the reason, then its effect, such as "rising incomes increase spending on services, so tertiary businesses expand".
- **Consider / Justify**: weigh whether a sector trend helps or harms the business described and give a judgement.
