These notes condense one content point of key development 3.1.3.1 of the 9236 specification:
"Muhammad bin Qasim, the expansion of the Umayyad Caliphate in the subcontinent, and its religious
impact". The full explanation is in the
[Muhammad bin Qasim study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-pakistan-studies-9236-muhammad-bin-qasim/);
the optional topic as a whole is in the
[Islam in the Subcontinent revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-islam-in-the-subcontinent-revision-notes/).

## Who's who

| Person | Role |
|---|---|
| Al-Walid I | Umayyad Caliph, 705-715; his reign saw expansion on many fronts |
| Hajjaj bin Yusuf | Governor of Iraq and the east; sent the expedition |
| Muhammad bin Qasim | Young commander from the Thaqif tribe, a relative of Hajjaj |
| Raja Dahir | Ruler of Sindh, capital Alor; killed in 712 |
| Sulaiman | Caliph from 715; recalled Muhammad bin Qasim |

## Causes of the invasion

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Pirates near Debal seized ships carrying gifts and Muslim families from Sri Lanka; Dahir said he could not control them |
| Prestige | Earlier, smaller expeditions had failed and their commanders were killed |
| Political | Dahir sheltered Arab rebels |
| Economic | Control of the sea route and the wealth of the lower Indus |
| Expansion | Part of the wider Umayyad expansion under al-Walid |

## Campaign timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 711-712 | Army marches through Makran; siege equipment by sea; Debal captured |
| 712 | Nerun surrenders on terms; Sehwan taken; Indus crossed |
| 712 | Dahir defeated and killed near Raor |
| 712-713 | Brahmanabad and Alor captured |
| 713 | Multan taken, with great wealth |
| 714 | Death of Hajjaj |
| 715 | Death of al-Walid; Sulaiman recalls Muhammad bin Qasim; he dies in prison in Iraq |

## Why he succeeded

- Well-equipped army, with cavalry by land and siege engines by sea.
- Strong backing from Hajjaj.
- Divisions in Sindh: some groups, including Buddhist communities, accepted terms.
- Generous terms for towns that surrendered, which reduced resistance.
- Death of Dahir removed the centre of resistance.

## How he governed

- Hindus and Buddhists given **dhimmi** (protected) status.
- **Jizya** graded by wealth; some groups exempt.
- **Local officials**, including Brahmins, kept in revenue and administration.
- Temples could be repaired and worship continued (Brahmanabad settlement).
- **Arab garrisons**, settlers and **mosques** in key towns.

## Religious impact and its limits

| Impact | Limit |
|---|---|
| Sindh the first Muslim-ruled region: *Bab-ul-Islam* | Conversion gradual; many stayed Hindu or Buddhist |
| Sindh linked to the caliphate's trade, law and learning | Conquest stopped at Sindh and Multan |
| Tolerant dhimmi model for later rulers | Arab rule later broke into smaller states |
| Mosques and Arab settlers introduced Islamic worship | Wider spread came later through Ghaznavids, the Sultanate and Sufis |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Caliphate | The state ruled by a caliph, successor to the Prophet as head of the Muslim community |
| Umayyad | Dynasty of caliphs ruling from Damascus, 661-750 |
| Dhimmi | Protected non-Muslim subject under Muslim rule |
| Jizya | Tax paid by dhimmis in return for protection and exemption from military service |
| Bab-ul-Islam | "Gateway of Islam", a name for Sindh |
| Debal | Port near the mouth of the Indus captured in 711-712 |
| Chachnama | Persian history of the conquest, written in the early thirteenth century |
| Siege engine | Catapult or similar machine used against city walls |

## Why the conquest stopped at Multan

- **Political change:** Hajjaj died (714), al-Walid died (715), and the commander was recalled.
- **Distance:** Sindh was the far eastern edge of an empire ruled from Damascus.
- **Strong neighbours:** powerful kingdoms to the east and north resisted later Arab advances.
- **Other frontiers:** armies were also needed in Central Asia, North Africa and Spain.

## Linking to the rest of the topic

- **Sufism:** the later spread of Islam owed more to Sufi saints than to conquest; use this contrast
  in judgement questions.
- **Al-Ghazali and Sirhindi:** their influence came through ideas and teaching, another contrast
  with military expansion.
- **Command words:** "explain why he succeeded" needs reasons with evidence; "how far was the
  religious impact limited" needs both the foundation and the limits.

## Exam traps

- Saying Sindh was converted to Islam by Muhammad bin Qasim.
- Calling Hajjaj the Caliph.
- Giving the pirate incident as the only cause.
- Claiming he was defeated; he was recalled for political reasons.
- Treating every detail of the *Chachnama* as certain.

## Self-test

1. Which dynasty ruled the caliphate in 711, and from which city?
2. Who sent Muhammad bin Qasim to Sindh?
3. What was the trigger for the invasion?
4. Give two longer-term causes.
5. Which port was captured first?
6. Where was Raja Dahir killed?
7. Which city in southern Punjab was taken in 713?
8. What status were Hindus and Buddhists given?
9. Why did keeping local officials help Muhammad bin Qasim?
10. Why was he recalled in 715?
11. Why is Sindh called *Bab-ul-Islam*?
12. Give one limit of the conquest's religious impact.

## Answers

1. The Umayyads, from Damascus.
2. Hajjaj bin Yusuf, governor of Iraq.
3. Pirates near Debal seized ships carrying gifts and Muslim families, and Dahir said he could not
   control them.
4. Any two: earlier failed expeditions; Dahir sheltering rebels; trade and wealth; Umayyad
   expansion.
5. Debal.
6. Near Raor, in 712.
7. Multan.
8. Dhimmi, protected status, in return for jizya.
9. Revenue and local government continued smoothly, so the change of rulers caused less disruption
   and resistance.
10. The new Caliph Sulaiman was hostile to Hajjaj and his family.
11. It was the first part of the subcontinent where Islam was established as a ruling faith.
12. Any one: conversion was gradual; the conquest stopped at Sindh and Multan; the wider spread came
    later through other rulers and Sufis.
