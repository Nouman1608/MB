> **Original questions written for Marlbridge** on data analysis and interpretation (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2027). This strand is not assessed at SL; at HL it is tested in Paper 3. They are not past-paper questions. All data are invented (constructed for practice). Questions 7 to 10 follow the pattern of the four Paper 3 questions (3, 6, 6 and 15 marks); the others use practice tariffs.

Related: [Data analysis and interpretation study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-data-analysis-and-interpretation/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-data-analysis-and-interpretation-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Hours of sleep reported by nine students were 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 9 and 12. Calculate the mean (to one decimal place), the median and the range. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why the median may be a better measure of the centre than the mean for the data in Question 1. **[2]**

**3.** A scatterplot shows that as hours of daily screen time rise from 2 to 9, scores on a well-being scale fall. The correlation coefficient is r = -0.94. Explain what this tells us and what it does not tell us. **[4]**

**4.** State what is meant by a type I error and a type II error. **[2]**

**5.** A researcher reports that a difference between two groups was significant at p = 0.03. Explain what this means. **[2]**

**6.** Identify two stages of thematic analysis and say what is done in each. **[4]**

## Section B

Read the sources (constructed for practice; data are invented). They concern human relationships and motivation at work.

> **Source A (experiment).** Forty employees were randomly allocated to two groups for a month. Group 1 (n = 20) received a weekly message of thanks from their manager. Group 2 (n = 20) received no message. At the end of the month, job satisfaction was rated on a scale from 1 to 10.
>
> | | Group 1 (thanks) | Group 2 (no message) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Mean | 7.1 | 6.2 |
> | Median | 7 | 6 |
> | Standard deviation | 1.2 | 1.4 |
> | Lowest to highest | 4 to 9 | 3 to 9 |
>
> An independent-samples t-test gave t(38) = 2.17, p = 0.04.
>
> **Source B (interviews).** The researcher interviewed 10 of the employees from Group 1. Using thematic analysis, she coded 120 extracts and reported three themes: "feeling noticed", "pressure to perform" and "doubts about sincerity". She was the manager's assistant and read all transcripts alone.

**7.** Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of Source A. **[3]**

**8.** Analyse the findings in Source A and state a conclusion. **[6]**

**9.** Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings in Source B. **[6]**

## Section C

**10.** Using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the role of motivation, discuss the claim that "praise from managers increases job satisfaction". **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Sum = 67, divided by 9 gives a mean of 7.4 **[1]**. Median = 7 (the fifth value) **[1]**. Range = 12 minus 5 = 7 **[1]**.

**2.** The value of 12 is an outlier that raises the mean above most values **[1]**. The median (7) is not affected by the size of the extreme value, so it describes the typical student better **[1]**. For example, without 12 the mean falls to 6.9 but the median remains 7.

**3.** It tells us there is a strong negative relationship in this sample: higher screen time goes with lower well-being **[1]**. Squared, r gives about 0.88, so about 88 per cent of the variation is shared in this sample **[1]**. It does not show cause, because low well-being could lead to more screen time (bidirectional ambiguity) or a third variable could affect both **[1]**. The sample size is not given, and a small sample may not generalise to other students **[1]**.

**4.** Type I error: rejecting a null hypothesis that is true, so concluding there is an effect when there is none (false positive) **[1]**. Type II error: failing to reject a null hypothesis that is false, so missing a real effect (false negative) **[1]**.

**5.** If the null hypothesis (no difference) were true, a difference this large or larger would occur about 3 per cent of the time **[1]**. As this is below the usual 0.05 threshold, the result is called statistically significant, which does not mean the difference is large or important **[1]**.

**6.** Any two stages, two marks each: familiarisation, reading and rereading the data **[1]** to gain an overview and note first impressions **[1]**; coding, labelling segments of text relevant to the aim **[1]** so that data can be organised **[1]**; searching for themes, grouping related codes **[1]** into candidate themes **[1]**; reviewing themes, checking them against the coded extracts and the whole data set **[1]**, and merging, splitting or dropping them **[1]**; defining and naming themes **[1]** with a clear description of each **[1]**; reporting, writing up with extracts as evidence **[1]** linked to the research aim **[1]**.

**7.** Any one, three marks: the mean difference (0.9 points) is modest compared with the standard deviations (1.2 and 1.4), so the distributions overlap and many employees in Group 2 were as satisfied as those in Group 1 **[1]**; job satisfaction was probably self-reported, so participants may have answered to please the manager or because they knew they had received messages (demand characteristics) **[1]**; only one month was studied, so lasting change is not shown **[1]**. Also creditable: the sample is from one workplace.

**8.** Analysis, up to 4: Group 1 had a higher mean (7.1 against 6.2) and a higher median (7 against 6), a mean difference of 0.9 points on a 10-point scale **[1]**. The standard deviations (1.2 and 1.4) show similar spread in both groups, and the ranges (4 to 9 and 3 to 9) overlap heavily **[1]**. The t-test result, p = 0.04, is below 0.05, so the difference is statistically significant, with a risk of a type I error of less than 5 per cent **[1]**. Random allocation supports internal validity, although the groups are small (20 each) **[1]**. Conclusion, up to 2: in this workplace, employees who received weekly thanks reported higher job satisfaction than those who did not **[1]**; the difference is modest, comes from one organisation and a month, so it cannot be generalised without caution **[1]**. Maximum **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 5 to 6 for detailed discussion with explicit reference to the source; 3 to 4 for some reference; 1 to 2 for generic points. Indicative content, up to two marks each:
- Reflexivity: the researcher worked as the manager's assistant, so she should record how that role may shape what employees say and how she interprets it **[2]**.
- A second independent coder should code a sample of transcripts, and agreement (inter-rater reliability) should be reported; she read all transcripts alone **[2]**.
- Triangulation: compare the themes with the satisfaction scores and with another source such as observation **[2]**.
- Member checking: ask interviewees whether the themes reflect their views **[2]**.
- Sampling and detail: explain how the 10 interviewees were chosen, include employees with different views, and keep an audit trail of how 120 extracts led to three themes with quotations **[2]**.
Maximum **[6]**.

**10.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for detailed, well-developed critical analysis that interprets both sources, uses own knowledge, evaluates different points of view and reaches a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good but less developed analysis. Only two sources are given, so a full-mark answer must use both in depth and make the limits of the evidence clear. Indicative content, up to the markband:
- Source A supports the claim: higher mean and median for the thanked group and p = 0.04, with random allocation **[3]**.
- Limits of Source A: modest difference (0.9), overlap, small groups, one month, self-report, a single workplace **[3]**.
- Source B adds depth and complexity: "feeling noticed" supports the claim, but "pressure to perform" and "doubts about sincerity" show that praise can have mixed effects **[3]**.
- Limits of Source B: only 10 interviewees, a researcher with a close link to the manager and no second coder, so credibility and transferability are limited **[2]**.
- Own knowledge: self-determination theory (competence and relatedness) predicts that feedback that supports competence increases intrinsic motivation, while controlling praise can reduce it; goal-setting and reward research show that effects depend on the type of praise **[2]**.
- Different points of view and a conclusion: the claim is supported in these data but depends on the type of praise and context; "increases" may overstate a modest effect **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** forgetting to give the mean to one decimal place or confusing range with highest value.
- **Question 8:** repeating the table without a conclusion tied to the figures, or not mentioning the t-test result.
- **Question 9:** general advice such as "use a bigger sample" without any reference to the researcher's role in the source.
- **Question 10:** treating Source A as proof, or ignoring the qualitative themes.
