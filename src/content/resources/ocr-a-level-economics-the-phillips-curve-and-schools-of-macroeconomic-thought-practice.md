## Questions

**1.** Define the natural rate of unemployment. **[2]**

**2.** Country C has a labour force of 40 million, of whom 37.6 million are employed. Its natural rate of unemployment is 5.5%. Calculate the unemployment rate, and the number of workers whose unemployment is cyclical. **[3]**

**3.** In Country C, wage growth = 3 + 0.5 × (5.5 − U), where U is the unemployment rate in %.

**(a)** Calculate wage growth when U = 4.5% and when U = 6.5%. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the short-run Phillips Curve slopes downward. **[2]**

**4.** Wage growth is 4.5% and productivity growth is 2.5%. Calculate the growth of unit labour costs, and state what this implies for price inflation. **[3]**

**5.** Inflation = expected inflation + 0.6 × (5 − U), with expected inflation equal to last year's actual inflation. Inflation is 2% and U = 5% in year 0. The government holds U at 3% in years 1 to 3, then returns it to 5% in year 4. Calculate inflation in years 1, 2, 3 and 4. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why the long-run Phillips Curve may be vertical, using the adaptive-expectations hypothesis. **[5]**

**7.** Explain why the natural rate of unemployment can be called the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU). **[3]**

**8.** Country D (illustrative): the table shows unemployment U (%) and wage growth (%).

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| U | 7 | 6 | 5 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Wage growth | 1.5 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 5.0 | 5.5 | 6.0 |

Assume wage growth = expected inflation + 0.5 × (6 − U). Evaluate whether the Phillips Curve accurately explains the relationship between wage growth and changes in unemployment. **[8]**

**9.** Expected inflation is 3%, the slope of the curve is 0.5, and the policymaker believes the natural rate is 5.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate needed for wage growth of 3.5%. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate wage growth at that rate if the true natural rate is 4.5%, and explain what this shows about the curve's usefulness to policymakers. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how Keynesian and neo-classical economists differ on whether the economy returns to full employment after a fall in aggregate demand. **[6]**

**11.** Over six years, bank credit in Country E grows by 10% a year and real income by 2.5% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative growth of credit and of income, and how many times larger the first is than the second (to one decimal place). **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate the Austrian explanation of a recession that follows such a period. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The rate of unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium [1], consisting of frictional and structural unemployment with no cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "the normal level of unemployment" is thin; the answer gains credit by saying what it includes and what it leaves out.

**2.** Unemployed = 40 − 37.6 = 2.4 million [1]. Rate = 2.4 ÷ 40 = **6.0%** [1]. Cyclical = 6.0 − 5.5 = 0.5 points = 0.005 × 40 = **0.2 million** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the unemployed count before the percentage, and keep units (million, percentage points) with each result.

**3. (a)** U = 4.5: 3 + 0.5 × 1 = **3.5%** [1]. U = 6.5: 3 + 0.5 × (−1) = **2.5%** [1].
**(b)** Low unemployment means labour shortages, so firms bid up pay [1]; high unemployment weakens workers' bargaining power, so wage growth falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explanations earn credit when they run as a chain from the labour market to wages, not just restate that the curve slopes down.

**4.** Unit labour cost growth = 1.045 ÷ 1.025 − 1 [1] = **1.95%** (about 2.0%) [1]. Wage growth overstates cost pressure because productivity rises, so price inflation is likely to be well below wage growth [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A subtraction (4.5 − 2.5 = 2.0) is a close approximation; showing which method you use avoids confusion.

**5.** Each year at U = 3%, inflation rises by 0.6 × 2 = 1.2 [1]. Year 1 = **3.2%**, year 2 = **4.4%**, year 3 = **5.6%** [2]. Year 4 at U = 5%: the gap is zero, so inflation stays at **5.6%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show that expected inflation is last year's actual figure each time; it makes errors in later years easier to trace.

**6.** Expected inflation depends on past inflation [1]. If unemployment is held below the natural rate, wages and prices rise faster than expected [1]. People raise their expectations, so the short-run curve shifts up [1]. Only at the natural rate does actual equal expected inflation, so inflation is stable [1]. Joining those points gives a vertical line, so no lasting trade-off exists [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say that the vertical line is at the natural rate and say why, not merely that expectations "change".

**7.** At the natural rate actual inflation equals expected inflation [1]. Below it, labour shortages push wages above expectations, expectations rise and inflation accelerates [1]. So the natural rate is the lowest unemployment consistent with non-accelerating inflation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Non-accelerating" is not "zero": inflation can be stable at any level there, which is what the answer should make clear.

**8.** Years 1 to 3: unemployment falls as wage growth rises, which fits an inverse relationship [1]. Years 4 to 6: unemployment rises while wage growth also rises, which a single curve cannot explain [1]. With U* = 6 and slope 0.5, implied expected inflation is 2.0% throughout years 1 to 3 but 4.5%, 5.5% and 6.5% in years 4 to 6 [1]. So the short-run curve shifted up as expectations rose, consistent with the expectations-augmented curve [1]. A supply shock (for example higher import costs) could also raise wage claims [1]. Other limits are mismeasured unemployment and the gap between wage growth and price inflation caused by productivity [1]. The curve still explains demand-driven movements when expectations are stable [1]. Overall it is a good short-run description but incomplete: accuracy depends on the period and the type of shock, and it cannot say what causes shifts [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that use the table figures (here, the implied expectations) and then reach a supported judgement tend to do better than lists of generic criticisms.

**9. (a)** 3.5 = 3 + 0.5 × (5.5 − U) [1], so U = **4.5%** [1].
**(b)** If U* = 4.5%, wage growth = 3 + 0.5 × (4.5 − 4.5) = **3.0%** [1], which undershoots the target by 0.5 points [1]. The natural rate is unobservable, so targeting it risks policy errors, limiting the curve's usefulness [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking a calculation to a stated implication, rather than leaving the number alone, is what turns it into analysis.

**10.** Keynesians: wages and prices are sticky downward [1], so after a fall in AD output and employment can stay below full employment for a long time [1]; government demand management is justified [1]. Neo-classical: wages and prices are flexible [1], so falling prices and wages restore equilibrium at full-employment output (vertical long-run AS) [1], so intervention is unnecessary and supply-side policy matters more [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the two schools point by point (wages, time, policy) usually reads better than two separate summaries.

**11. (a)** Credit: 1.1⁶ − 1 = **77.2%** [1]. Income: 1.025⁶ − 1 = **16.0%** [1]. Ratio = 77.2 ÷ 16.0 = **4.8** times [1].
**(b)** Austrians say low interest rates and credit expansion misdirect investment into unsustainable projects (malinvestment) [1]. Credit growing about 4.8 times faster than income fits that imbalance [1]. The recession is then a necessary correction that liquidates bad investment [1]. However, causes other than credit, such as supply shocks, may explain slumps [1]. Keynesians argue stimulus can limit long-lasting output and job losses, so letting failures run has costs [1]. The judgement depends on whether evidence shows credit drove the boom and how severe the bust is [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from part (a) in part (b); evaluating a school using only general knowledge ignores the data given.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not stating the natural rate excludes cyclical unemployment.
- Mixing wage growth with price inflation, and ignoring productivity.
- Calling the vertical curve "inflation is constant" instead of explaining expectations catching up.
- Forgetting that expected inflation is last year's actual inflation in the adaptive model.
- Quoting calculation results without units or percentage points.
- Listing criticisms of the Phillips Curve without a judgement or a time frame.
- Treating NAIRU as a target of zero inflation.
- Describing three schools separately and never comparing them.
- Judging a school without using the data in the question.
