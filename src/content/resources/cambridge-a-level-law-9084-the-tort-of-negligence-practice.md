## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Explain the difference between personal liability and vicarious liability. **[3]**

**2.** State the three parts of the Caparo test. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the importance of policy considerations in deciding whether a duty of care exists, with one case. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how the standard of care differs for a child defendant, with an authority. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the Bolam test and how it was qualified in Bolitho. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why the claimant failed in Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee (1969). **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by a novus actus interveniens, with one example. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why pure economic loss is generally not recoverable in negligence, and state the main exception. **[3]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Owen, a trainee driving instructor, is giving a lesson to Pia. He takes a bend too fast and the car hits a wall. Pia suffers a fractured wrist, which is worse than expected because she has brittle bones. A passing cyclist, Rob, swerves to avoid debris and falls; he is not hurt but his bicycle is damaged. An hour later Rob's sister, who had been told by phone, arrives and develops anxiety. Advise Owen on his liability in negligence. **[12]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Imani asks her neighbour Dan, who works as a financial adviser, at a barbecue whether a local company is a safe investment. Dan says, "Definitely, I've checked its accounts." Imani invests £20,000 and loses it all when the company collapses. Dan had not checked the accounts. Separately, Dan's firm sends a report on the company to its client, who passes it to her brother; he also invests and loses money. Advise Imani and the brother. **[10]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* A hospital negligently fails to diagnose Tom's fractured ankle. On the way home Tom, unable to walk properly, accepts a lift from a friend who drives dangerously and crashes, causing Tom serious injuries. Advise the hospital on whether it is liable for all of Tom's injuries. **[8]**

**12.** "The rules on liability for nervous shock are arbitrary and in need of reform." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Personal liability is the defendant's liability for their own tort (1). Vicarious liability makes one person, usually an employer, liable for a tort committed by another, without personal fault (1), where the relationship is employment or akin to it and the tort is closely connected with the work (Mohamud v WM Morrison Supermarkets, 2016) (1). **[3]**

**2.** Reasonable foreseeability of damage to the claimant (1); a relationship of sufficient proximity (1); that it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty (1) (Caparo Industries v Dickman, 1990). **[3]**

**3.** Policy allows the court to refuse a duty even where harm is foreseeable and the parties are proximate (1), through the "fair, just and reasonable" stage (1). Concerns include floodgates and defensive practices (1). In Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire (1989) no duty was owed to a potential victim of a serial killer, partly because a duty could lead to defensive policing (1). **[4]**

**4.** A child is judged against a reasonable child of the same age, not a reasonable adult (1). In Mullin v Richards (1998) two fifteen-year-olds fencing with rulers were not negligent (1). **[2]**

**5.** A professional is not in breach if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of professional opinion (1), even if others disagree (1) (Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee, 1957). Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority (1997) added that the opinion must be capable of withstanding logical analysis (1), so the court has the final say on whether a practice is reasonable (1). **[4]**

**6.** The doctor's failure to examine the patient was a breach (1), but the patient would have died from arsenic poisoning even with proper treatment, so the breach failed the but for test (1). **[2]**

**7.** A new intervening act, by a third party, the claimant or nature, that breaks the chain of causation between the breach and the harm (1), so the defendant is not liable for harm after it (1). Example: Knightley v Johns (1982), where a police officer's negligent order to ride against the traffic broke the chain from the original accident (1). **[3]**

**8.** Courts fear indeterminate liability to an indeterminate class (1), and economic loss is regarded as better protected by contract (1). The main exception is negligent misstatement where a special relationship exists (Hedley Byrne v Heller, 1964) (1). **[3]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Duty to Pia: driver to passenger is an established duty; Caparo is not needed (Robinson) (1).
- Breach: Owen is judged as a reasonably competent driver; inexperience is no excuse (Nettleship v Weston) (1). Taking a bend too fast falls below that standard (1).
- Causation: but for the speed, no crash (1). A fracture is a foreseeable kind of harm (1).
- Thin skull rule: Owen is liable for the full extent of Pia's injury despite her brittle bones (Smith v Leech Brain) (1).
- Rob: a road user is a foreseeable claimant (1); swerving to avoid debris is a reasonable reaction, not a novus actus (1); damage to the bicycle is foreseeable property damage (1).
- Rob's sister: no physical danger, so she is at most a secondary victim (1). Being told by phone fails the requirement of perception with her own unaided senses (Alcock) (1); anxiety may not be a recognised psychiatric illness (1). **[12]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Imani suffers pure economic loss, recoverable only for negligent misstatement (Hedley Byrne) (1).
- Special skill: Dan is a financial adviser (1).
- Social occasions usually do not create a special relationship (1), but Dan claimed to have checked the accounts, inviting reliance (1), and a friend can be liable where he assumes responsibility (Chaudhry v Prabhakar) (1).
- Reliance by Imani was arguably reasonable because of his profession and the assurance given (1).
- Breach: a reasonably competent adviser would not claim to have checked accounts without doing so (1).
- Brother: under Caparo, a duty is owed only where the adviser knew the statement would be communicated to the claimant for a specific purpose (1). The report was made for the client, not the brother (1).
- Conclusion: Imani has a reasonable claim; the brother is unlikely to succeed (1). **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Breach: failure to diagnose falls below the standard of a reasonably competent doctor (Bolam) (1).
- Factual causation: but for the misdiagnosis, Tom would have had treatment and might not have needed a lift (1).
- The ankle injury itself is worsened by the breach, so the hospital is liable for that additional harm (1).
- Intervening act: the friend's dangerous driving is a free, deliberate and reckless act of a third party (1), which is likely to break the chain of causation (Knightley v Johns) (1).
- Accepting a lift is not an unreasonable act by Tom of the kind in McKew v Holland (1).
- Remoteness: injury in a crash is arguably a different kind of harm from that risked by misdiagnosis (Wagon Mound) (1).
- Conclusion: the hospital is liable for the worsening of the ankle, not the crash injuries (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): recognised psychiatric illness; primary victims (Dulieu v White; Page v Smith); secondary victims and the Alcock control mechanisms; McLoughlin v O'Brian on the aftermath; White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire on rescuers; Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust (2024) on medical negligence.
- Analysis (up to 3): explain why the courts impose controls (floodgates, fraudulent claims, proportionality between fault and liability).
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, the controls produce arbitrary distinctions (live perception against later news; siblings and friends having to prove a close tie); Page v Smith treats primary victims generously while secondary victims face strict hurdles; White left police officers without a remedy while spectators in Alcock were also refused. Against, clear lines provide certainty and prevent unlimited liability, and the courts have limited room to reform without Parliament. Reform: the Law Commission's 1998 recommendations; a reasoned conclusion on whether legislative reform is needed. **[15]**
