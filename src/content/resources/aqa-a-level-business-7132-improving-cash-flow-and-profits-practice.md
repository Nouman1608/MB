These questions cover section 3.5.4 Making financial decisions: improving cash flow and profits of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [improving cash flow and profits study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-cash-flow-and-profits/) and the [improving cash flow and profits revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-cash-flow-and-profits-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following would improve a business's cash flow in the short term? **[1]**

A: Giving customers longer to pay

B: Paying suppliers earlier

C: Reducing the amount of inventory held

D: Buying new equipment for cash

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a measure of profitability rather than profit? **[1]**

A: Operating profit of £80,000

B: Operating profit margin of 8%

C: Revenue of £1,000,000

D: A bank balance of £50,000

**3.** Define the term "credit control". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** drawback of delaying payments to suppliers in order to improve cash flow. **[3]**

**5.** Calder Cycles is owed £80,000 by customers. It offers a 2.5% discount for immediate payment, and customers owing £32,000 accept.

**(a)** Calculate the cash received from these customers. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost of the discount. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why offering the discount may still be worthwhile. **[2]**

**6.** A bakery can buy an oven for £48,000 cash or lease it for £1,200 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the cash cost of leasing in the first year. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate how much more cash the bakery keeps in year one by leasing. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate how much more leasing costs than buying over five years. **[2]**

**7.** Marren Mugs sells 5,000 mugs at £12. Variable cost is £7 per mug and fixed costs are £15,000.

**(a)** Calculate the current profit. **[2]**

**(b)** Marren raises the price to £13 and sales fall to 4,600 mugs. Calculate the new profit. **[2]**

**(c)** State what happens to revenue. **[1]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** ways in which a retailer could improve its cash flow. **[6]**

**9.** A restaurant chain's profit has fallen. Assess whether it should cut costs or raise prices. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse why selling assets is unlikely to solve a business's long-term cash flow problems. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** difficulties a business may face when trying to improve profit by cutting costs. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the methods available to a fast-growing manufacturer to improve its cash flow. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Cutting inventory releases cash tied up in stock; the other options either delay cash coming in or bring it forward as payments. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A margin compares profit with revenue; the other options are amounts. **[1]**

**3.** Managing the credit a business gives to customers [1], including checking customers, setting terms and chasing late payments [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Suppliers may lose trust in a business that pays late [1], so they may cut credit limits, demand cash on delivery or raise prices [1]. The business could also lose discounts for prompt payment, or find it hard to get supplies when it is short of stock, which could hurt sales [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** £32,000 × (1 − 0.025) [1] = **£31,200** [1].

**(b)** £32,000 × 0.025 = **£800** [1].

**(c)** The business receives cash earlier [1], which may avoid expensive overdraft interest or allow it to pay its own bills on time, so £800 may cost less than the alternative [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** £1,200 × 12 = **£14,400** [1].

**(b)** £48,000 − £14,400 = **£33,600** [1].

**(c)** Five years of leasing = £1,200 × 60 = £72,000 [1]; £72,000 − £48,000 = **£24,000** more [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Contribution = £12 − £7 = £5; total contribution = 5,000 × £5 = £25,000 [1]; profit = £25,000 − £15,000 = **£10,000** [1].

**(b)** Contribution = £13 − £7 = £6; 4,600 × £6 = £27,600 [1]; profit = £27,600 − £15,000 = **£12,600** [1].

**(c)** Revenue falls slightly, from £60,000 to £59,800 (4,600 × £13), even though profit rises [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Way 1: tighter credit control [1]. Shortening the time customers have to pay, or chasing late payers, brings money in sooner [1], although it may lose customers who prefer longer credit [1]. Way 2: reducing inventory [1]. Selling slow-moving stock, perhaps with a discount, and ordering smaller amounts frees cash that was tied up [1], but there is a risk of running out of popular items and losing sales [1]. Accept also: delaying supplier payments; leasing. **[6]**

**9.** Cutting costs: it raises profit without risking sales if the savings do not affect customers, for example through less waste, better buying or energy savings [1]; savings are within the firm's control [1]. But cutting staff or quality may reduce customer satisfaction and sales [1]. Raising prices: it increases revenue per customer quickly [1], and profit rises if the fall in sales is smaller than the gain in contribution [1]. But customers may switch to rival restaurants if demand is elastic [1]. Judgement: start with cost savings that customers will not notice, such as waste and purchasing [1], then make a modest price rise if the brand is strong and rivals charge similar prices [1]; the choice depends on how price-sensitive diners are [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Selling an asset brings in cash only once [1], so the shortage returns when the money has been spent [1]. The asset may have earned income or helped production, so selling it can lower future revenue and profit [1]. If the asset is rented back (sale and leaseback), regular payments add to future outflows [1]. The real cause, such as poor credit control or low margins, is untouched [1], so the business needs lasting changes to inflows and outflows [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Difficulty 1: effect on quality and customers [1]. Cheaper materials or fewer staff may reduce quality or service, so sales and reputation fall and any saving is lost [1]. Difficulty 2: reactions and time [1]. Suppliers may refuse to cut prices or staff may resist cuts and leave; redundancy payments or new equipment cost money before savings appear [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit control: chasing customers and shortening terms speeds up inflows [1]; but customers may move to rivals [1]. Early payment discounts and factoring: quick cash on invoices [1]; but they cost profit [1]. Supplier credit: negotiating longer terms keeps cash in the business [1]; but suppliers may refuse or raise prices [1]. Leasing equipment: avoids large outlays during growth [1]; but costs more over time [1]. Reducing inventory: releases cash tied up in stock [1]; but a fast-growing firm risks stock-outs that lose orders [1]. Judgement: a combination is best, with credit control and supplier terms for lasting improvement and leasing to cover rapid expansion [1]; external finance should be arranged in advance as a safety net [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* State the size of the cash effect with figures from the case, then say what it costs the business to get it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating cash flow and profit as the same problem.
- Offering a loan as a way of improving trading cash flow.
- Forgetting that a higher price may lower sales.
- Treating a one-off asset sale as a lasting solution.
- Ignoring how customers and suppliers will react.
- Listing methods with no judgement of which suits the business.
