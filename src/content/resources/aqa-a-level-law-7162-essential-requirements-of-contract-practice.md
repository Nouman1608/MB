These questions test the section 3.4 row on the essential requirements of contract: offer and acceptance, consideration including privity, and intention to create legal relations. All sums of money are invented for practice.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between an offer and an invitation to treat, with **one** example of an invitation to treat. **[3]**

**2.** State when a revocation of an offer takes effect, with an authority. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a counter-offer and a request for information. **[4]**

**4.** State the rule on past consideration and **one** exception to it. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the rule in *Foakes v Beer* (1884). **[2]**

**6.** State the presumption about intention to create legal relations in commercial agreements and how it may be rebutted. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain when the performance of an existing duty can be good consideration, referring to **three** cases. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 changed the doctrine of privity. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the decision in *Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O* (1979) and what it shows about acceptance. **[4]**

**10.** Ola puts a notice in a café: "£50 reward to anyone who finds my lost cat, Biscuit." Pete, who has not seen the notice, finds Biscuit and returns her. Rana sees the notice, starts searching, and on her second day of searching Ola tears the notice down. Rana finds Biscuit the next day. Advise Pete and Rana. **[6]**

**11.** Sam's aunt promises to pay him £2,000 if he passes his exams. He passes, but she refuses to pay. Separately, Sam's landlord agrees in writing to reduce his rent by half while Sam is unemployed; three months later the landlord demands the full rent for those months. Advise Sam. **[7]**

**12.** On 1 June, Lark Ltd posts an offer to sell steel to Mill Ltd. On 3 June, Lark posts a revocation. On 4 June, Mill receives the offer and posts an acceptance. On 5 June, Mill receives the revocation. Advise whether there is a contract. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** An offer is a statement of terms made with the intention of being bound as soon as it is accepted [1]. An invitation to treat only invites others to make offers [1]. Example: goods displayed on a shop shelf (*Pharmaceutical Society v Boots*, 1953), goods in a window (*Fisher v Bell*, 1961) or most advertisements (*Partridge v Crittenden*, 1968) [1].

**2.** A revocation is effective only when it is communicated to the offeree, not when it is posted [1]: *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880) [1].

**3.** A counter-offer introduces new terms [1] and ends the original offer, which cannot then be accepted (*Hyde v Wrench*, 1840) [1]. A request for information merely asks about the terms [1] and leaves the original offer open (*Stevenson v McLean*, 1880) [1].

**4.** Past consideration is not good consideration (*Re McArdle*, 1951) [1]. Exception: where the act was done at the promisor's request, both parties understood it would be paid for, and payment would have been enforceable (*Lampleigh v Braithwait*, 1615; *Pao On*, 1980) [1].

**5.** Payment of a smaller sum on the due date is not consideration for a promise to forgive the rest of a debt [1], so the creditor can still recover the balance, unless something different is provided or promissory estoppel applies [1].

**6.** Commercial agreements are presumed to be intended to be legally binding (*Edwards v Skyways*, 1964) [1]. The presumption can be rebutted by clear words, such as an honour clause (*Rose & Frank v Crompton*, 1925) [1].

**7.** Performing an existing public duty is not consideration (*Collins v Godefroy*, 1831), but doing more than the duty requires is [1]: in *Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC* (1925) police who provided extra protection at a mine could recover payment [1]. Performing an existing contractual duty is not consideration (*Stilk v Myrick*, 1809) [1], unless the promisee does more than the contract requires, as in *Hartley v Ponsonby* (1857) where so many crew deserted that the remaining sailors' duties changed [1]. In *Williams v Roffey Bros* (1991) a practical benefit to the promisor, such as avoiding a penalty for late completion, was good consideration [1], provided the promise was not obtained by economic duress or fraud [1].

**8.** Before 1999, only a party to a contract could enforce it (*Dunlop v Selfridge*, 1915) [1]. Section 1 allows a third party to enforce a term if the contract expressly provides that they may [1], or if the term purports to confer a benefit on them, unless it appears the parties did not intend it to be enforceable by them [1]. The third party must be identified by name, class or description (s1(3)) [1].

**9.** A seller quoted a price on its terms, which included a price variation clause [1]. The buyer ordered on its own terms, without the clause, which was a counter-offer [1]. The seller signed and returned the buyer's acknowledgment slip, which accepted the buyer's terms [1]. The case shows the mirror image rule: the last set of terms accepted, here by signing the slip, governs the contract [1].

**10.** The notice is a unilateral offer to the world, accepted by performance (*Carlill*, 1893) [1]. **Pete:** he did not know of the offer when he found the cat, so he cannot be said to have accepted it [1], and is unlikely to be entitled to the reward [1]. **Rana:** she knew of the offer and had begun performance by searching [1]. Following *Errington v Errington* (1952), an offeror cannot revoke a unilateral offer once the offeree has started to perform [1]. Ola's revocation was ineffective, so Rana can claim the £50 when she finds the cat [1].

**11.** **Aunt's promise:** the presumption for family agreements is that there is no intention to create legal relations (*Balfour v Balfour*, 1919; *Jones v Padavatton*, 1969) [1]. Nothing suggests it is rebutted, so Sam probably cannot enforce it [1]. Even if intention could be shown, the aunt could argue that there was no consideration, since Sam was taking the exams for his own benefit anyway, although passing was the act she asked for, so this point is arguable [1]. **Rent:** part payment of rent is not consideration for forgiving the rest (*Foakes v Beer*) [1]. However, the landlord made a clear promise in writing, which Sam relied on [1]. Under promissory estoppel (*High Trees*, 1947) the landlord cannot go back on it for the period of reliance [1], so Sam need not pay the arrears for the three months, though full rent can be restored on reasonable notice for the future [1].

**12.** The offer takes effect when received by Mill on 4 June [1]. Mill's acceptance by post is effective when posted on 4 June, as post is a reasonable method where the offer came by post (*Adams v Lindsell*, 1818; *Henthorn v Fraser*, 1892) [1]. Lark's revocation was posted on 3 June, but a revocation takes effect only when received [1] (*Byrne v Van Tienhoven*, 1880), which was 5 June [1]. The acceptance was effective before the revocation, so a contract was formed on 4 June [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the postal rule to revocation.
- Treating a request for information as a counter-offer.
- Forgetting that a promise to keep an offer open is not binding without consideration.
- Ignoring the presumption against intention in family agreements.
