## 1.1.1 The economic problem

**The economic problem** is that resources are finite (limited) while wants are unlimited, so choices have to be made about how resources are used. This situation is called **scarcity**.

Two parts need explaining.

**Why resources are finite.** At any moment an economy has a limited amount of land, labour, capital and enterprise (the factors of production, section 1.2). There are only so many workers, so many hours in a day, so much fertile land and so many machines.

**Why wants are unlimited.** When one want is satisfied, people want something else or something better. Rising incomes create new wants, and firms keep developing new products that people did not want before because they did not exist. Wants are not the same as needs: needs are what is essential to survive, such as food, water and shelter; wants include everything people would like to have.

Because the gap between the two never closes, every decision maker must **choose**. That is why economics is often described as the study of choice.

### The problem for each decision maker

The syllabus names four contexts. Learn one clear example for each and be ready to apply the idea to a new one.

| Decision maker | Finite resource | Unlimited wants | Choice forced on them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Income and time | Goods, services, leisure | Which goods to buy with a limited budget |
| Worker | Time, skills and energy | Higher pay, leisure, a better job | How many hours to work; which job or training to choose |
| Producer (firm) | Land, labour, capital and finance available to it | More output, new products, growth | What to make, how much, and with which resources |
| Government | Tax revenue and borrowing | Spending on health, education, defence, roads, welfare | Which services to fund and by how much |

**Worked example 1: a consumer.** Amara earns 800 cu a month after paying rent. Her wants this month are:

| Want | Cost (cu) |
|---|---|
| Food and bills | 450 |
| A new phone | 300 |
| A weekend trip | 220 |
| A course in graphic design | 180 |
| Total | 1,150 |

Her wants cost 1,150 cu but her income is 800 cu, a gap of 350 cu. She cannot have everything, so she must rank her wants and choose. If she pays for food and bills (450 cu) and the course (180 cu), she has 170 cu left, which is not enough for either the phone or the trip.

**Worked example 2: a producer.** A farmer has 20 hectares of land. She could grow vegetables, keep goats, or plant fruit trees. Every hectare used for one purpose cannot be used for another at the same time. The land is finite; the possible uses are many.

**Worked example 3: a worker.** Dev has 60 waking hours a week outside sleep and travel. A local firm will pay him for up to 48 hours. He also wants time to study and to see his family. More hours at work means more income but fewer hours for everything else. His time is the scarce resource.

**Worked example 4: a government.** A government's budget is 50 billion cu. Ministries ask for 72 billion cu in total. It must decide which requests to meet in full, which to cut, and which to refuse, or else raise taxes or borrow, which creates choices of its own.

## 1.1.2 Economic goods and free goods

**An economic good** is a good that is scarce: it takes scarce resources to produce, so producing it means giving up something else. It has an opportunity cost (section 1.3). Almost all goods and services are economic goods.

**A free good** is a good that is not scarce: there is enough of it to satisfy everyone's wants at a zero price, and no scarce resources are needed to provide it. Using it involves no opportunity cost. Examples usually given are the air we breathe, sunlight, and sea water in the open sea.

### The test to apply

Ask: *does providing more of this mean that something else must be given up?*

| Item | Economic or free? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Air in an open field | Free | Unlimited relative to wants; no resources used |
| Bottled oxygen in a hospital | Economic | Has to be produced, stored and transported |
| Sea water on the open coast | Free | Plentiful; taking some leaves no less for others |
| Desalinated drinking water | Economic | Needs a plant, energy and workers |
| A school place provided at no charge by the government | Economic | Teachers, buildings and books are scarce resources paid for by taxes |
| A free sample of shampoo in a shop | Economic | Resources were used to make it; the firm bears the cost |

The last two rows are the classic trap. A good with **no price to the user** is not necessarily a free good. If scarce resources were used to provide it, someone has given something up, so it is an economic good.

### Goods can change category

Whether a good is free depends on the situation, not on the good itself. Clean air can become scarce in a heavily polluted city, where people pay for air filters. Water from a river may be a free good where the river is large and few people live, but an economic good during a drought or where it must be treated before use.

## Common errors

- Defining the economic problem as "people do not have enough money". Money is not a resource in this sense; the problem is finite **resources**.
- Giving an example for consumers only, when the question names workers, producers or governments.
- Calling anything with a zero price a free good.
- Saying that wants are limited by income. Income limits what people can **buy**, not what they want.
- Forgetting to say that scarcity forces **choice**.

## Exam technique

- **Define** (Paper 1 and Paper 2): give the precise meaning. "The economic problem is that resources are finite but wants are unlimited, so choices must be made."
- **Give** or **identify** an example: name the decision maker, the scarce resource and the choice. "A government has limited tax revenue so must choose between building hospitals and roads."
- **Explain** why something is a free good or an economic good: use the test above and mention whether resources are used and whether there is an opportunity cost.
- In multiple-choice questions, watch for options that describe a good with no price as a free good; check whether resources were used.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the basic economic problem in Cambridge O Level Economics?**
Resources are finite while wants are unlimited, so consumers, workers, producers and governments must all make choices about how to use what they have.

**Is a free school place a free good?**
No. Teachers, buildings and equipment are scarce resources funded through taxes, so a school place has an opportunity cost and is an economic good, even if the student pays nothing.
