## Questions

**1.** State three assumptions of oligopoly. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how economies of scale and sunk costs each act as a barrier in an oligopoly. **[4]**

**3.** Firms C and D each choose to hold price or cut price. Profits in millions are (C, D).

| | D holds | D cuts |
|---|---|---|
| **C holds** | (18, 18) | (6, 26) |
| **C cuts** | (26, 6) | (12, 12) |

**(a)** Identify C's dominant strategy, with reasons. **[2]**
**(b)** State the likely outcome and explain why it is not the best joint result. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why co-operation may be easier to sustain if the game is repeated. **[2]**

**4.** Market demand is P = 130 − Q. Two firms form a cartel and each has constant marginal cost of 30.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising cartel output and price, and each firm's profit if output is shared equally. **[4]**
**(b)** One firm secretly raises output to 35 while the other keeps to 25. Calculate its profit and compare. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what this shows about the stability of a cartel. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how price leadership operates and why it may avoid a price war. **[4]**

**6.** Price is 20 and a firm sells 5,000 units. Market demand has price elasticity of demand of −0.6. The firm cuts price by 8% and its rivals match.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity and the new quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in the firm's revenue. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what this suggests about price wars when demand is inelastic. **[2]**

**7.** A dominant firm has average cost of 12 and sells 300 units a period. It cuts price to 9 to drive out a rival. The war lasts 4 periods, then price rises to 16.

**(a)** Calculate the loss per period and the total loss. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit per period after the rival leaves and the number of periods needed to recover the loss. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why the strategy might fail. **[2]**

**8.** An incumbent has average cost of 10 at 200 units. A possible entrant would have average cost of 16 at 30 units. The current price is 22.

**(a)** Calculate the entrant's profit at a price of 22 and at a price of 14. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why 14 is a limit price and what it costs the incumbent. **[3]**

**9.** Explain two costs and one benefit of collusion, naming the stakeholder affected in each case. **[6]**

**10.** **(a)** Explain two forms of non-price competition other than advertising. **[4]**
**(b)** An advertising campaign costs 1.8 million. Each extra unit sold adds 5 to profit before the campaign cost. Current sales are 1,200,000 units. Calculate the extra sales needed to break even and express them as a percentage of current sales. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether non-price competition benefits consumers more than price competition in an oligopoly. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three: a few large firms hold most of the market [1]; there are significant barriers to entry [1]; firms are interdependent, so each must consider rivals' reactions [1]. Also acceptable: price makers, identical or differentiated products. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating "few firms" alone is thin; adding what it means for behaviour usually shows understanding.

**2.** Economies of scale: large incumbents have lower average cost [1], so a small entrant cannot match their price profitably [1]. Sunk costs: spending such as specialised equipment cannot be recovered on exit [1], so entry is risky and firms stay longer or never enter [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition of the barrier gains less than the chain from the barrier to the entrant's decision.

**3. (a)** If D holds, C earns 26 by cutting against 18 by holding [1]; if D cuts, C earns 12 by cutting against 6 by holding. Cutting is best either way, so it is dominant [1].
**(b)** The game is symmetrical, so both cut and the outcome is (12, 12) [1]. Total profit is 24, against 36 if both hold [1]. Each firm has an incentive to cut if the other holds, so (18, 18) is not stable [1].
**(c)** In a repeated game a firm that cuts can be punished in later rounds [1], so the short-term gain may be outweighed by lost future profit [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the best reply for each rival choice before naming the dominant strategy, rather than only the final matrix cell.

**4. (a)** MR = 130 − 2Q = 30 [1], so Q = 50 [1] and P = 130 − 50 = 80 [1]. Total profit = (80 − 30) × 50 = 2,500, so 1,250 each [1].
**(b)** Total output = 35 + 25 = 60 and P = 130 − 60 = 70 [1]. Profit = (70 − 30) × 35 = 1,400 [1], which is more than 1,250 [1].
**(c)** Each member can gain by cheating [1]. If both cheat, price falls towards 30 and the cartel breaks down [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the steps (MR equal to MC, then price from demand, then profit): a clear method lets the reader see your reasoning even if a number slips.

**5.** One firm, often the largest, sets the price [1] and others follow it [1]. Rivals expect any undercutting to be matched, so there is little to gain from it [1]. Price stays stable and a price war is avoided without a formal agreement [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming dominant or barometric leadership helps only if you also explain why followers choose to follow.

**6. (a)** Quantity change = 0.6 × 8% = 4.8% [1]; new quantity = 5,000 × 1.048 = 5,240 [1].
**(b)** Revenue before = 20 × 5,000 = 100,000 [1]. New price = 20 × 0.92 = 18.40, so revenue after = 18.40 × 5,240 = 96,416 [1]. Revenue falls by 3,584 [1].
**(c)** Demand is inelastic, so the rise in quantity is proportionally smaller than the fall in price [1]. With rivals matching, no firm gains share, so a price war lowers revenue for everyone [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the elasticity value in the working; an answer that only says "revenue falls" does not show how it was reached.

**7. (a)** Loss per period = (9 − 12) × 300 = −900, a loss of 900 [1]. Over 4 periods the loss is 3,600 [1].
**(b)** Profit after the rival leaves = (16 − 12) × 300 = 1,200 a period [1]. Periods to recover = 3,600 ÷ 1,200 = 3 [1].
**(c)** Any two: the rival may not leave, for example if it has funds to survive [1]; new firms may enter when price rises [1]; competition authorities may act against predatory pricing. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Commenting on the length of recovery relative to the length of the war strengthens an evaluation of whether the strategy pays.

**8. (a)** At 22: (22 − 16) × 30 = 180 [1]. At 14: (14 − 16) × 30 = −60 [1].
**(b)** At 14 the entrant makes a loss, so it is deterred [1], while 14 is still above the incumbent's average cost of 10, so it earns 4 per unit [1]. The cost is the lower margin: 4 per unit instead of 12 at a price of 22 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A limit price sits between the two firms' costs; saying it is "low" without that comparison is incomplete.

**9.** Consumers: higher prices and less choice [1] because competition is restricted and output falls [1]. Government: costs of detecting and prosecuting collusion [1], and reduced welfare [1]. Benefit to producers: higher and more stable profit [1], because uncertainty over rivals' actions falls [1]. Other valid stakeholders include workers. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that name the stakeholder and then give a reasoned effect tend to do better than lists of costs and benefits.

**10. (a)** Quality: better design or reliability [1] supports loyalty or a higher price [1]. After-sales service: warranties and repairs [1] encourage repeat purchase [1]. Endorsement or product placement are also valid.
**(b)** Break-even extra sales = 1,800,000 ÷ 5 = 360,000 units [1] (campaign cost divided by extra profit per unit [1]). As a percentage of current sales: 360,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 30% [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving the unit with the answer and a comment on whether 30% is realistic usually adds to a calculation.

**11.** For non-price competition: consumers gain wider choice, better quality and service [1]; firms avoid costly price wars, which can protect investment and jobs [1]; loyalty may create stable supply and better information [1]. Against: spending on advertising can be wasteful and raise prices [1]; brand loyalty can create barriers to entry, so less competition over time [1]. For price competition: lower prices raise consumer surplus at once [1]; but a war can squeeze margins, cutting quality, jobs and supplier prices [1], and may drive out smaller firms, leaving fewer sellers [1]. Evaluation: the answer depends on elasticity, the time horizon and whether rivals can copy [1]. Judgement: non-price competition usually gives more lasting benefits when markets are stable, while price competition helps more when firms are colluding [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced list of points is weaker than a judgement that is supported and says which conditions would change it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing assumptions without linking them to interdependence
- Naming the dominant strategy without checking each rival choice
- Treating the cartel outcome as stable
- Forgetting to share cartel output between firms before calculating profit each
- Not using the elasticity value when judging a price cut
- Confusing predatory pricing with limit pricing
- Giving costs and benefits without naming the stakeholder
- Evaluation with no judgement or no condition
