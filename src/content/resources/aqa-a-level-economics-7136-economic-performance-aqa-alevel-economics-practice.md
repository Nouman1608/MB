## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a positive and a negative output gap. **[2]**

**2.** Country J's potential output is 1,250 billion and its actual output is 1,200 billion. Calculate the output gap
as a percentage of potential output. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between the claimant count and the Labour Force Survey measure of unemployment. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between voluntary and involuntary unemployment. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between deflation and disinflation. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** causes of cyclical instability. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** The table shows data for Country K over four years.

| Year | Real GDP growth (%) | Unemployment (%) | Inflation (%) | Investment growth (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.8 | 3.9 | 3.6 | 6.0 |
| 2 | 1.1 | 4.1 | 3.9 | −2.5 |
| 3 | −1.4 | 5.2 | 2.1 | −8.0 |
| 4 | 0.9 | 5.6 | 1.4 | 1.5 |

**(a)** Identify the phase of the economic cycle in Year 3 and in Year 4, using the data. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why unemployment continued to rise in Year 4. **[2]**

**8.** In Country L, M = 600, V = 5 and Q = 1,500.

**(a)** Calculate the price level. **[1]**

**(b)** The money supply rises by 20% while V and Q are unchanged. Calculate the new price level. **[1]**

**(c)** State the assumption of the Quantity Theory of Money that makes the price level rise in proportion to the
money supply, and give **one** criticism of it. **[2]**

**9.** A region's car factories close as production moves abroad. Explain the type of unemployment this creates and
why raising aggregate demand may not reduce it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in world oil prices can affect inflation in the UK. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why, according to the long-run Phillips curve, a government cannot permanently reduce unemployment
below the natural rate by raising aggregate demand. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that deflation is more harmful to an economy than moderate inflation. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A positive output gap is when **real GDP is above** the economy's productive potential [1]; a negative gap is
when **real GDP is below** potential, so resources are underused [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (1,200 − 1,250) ÷ 1,250 × 100 = **−4.0%** [1].

**3.** The claimant count measures people **claiming unemployment-related benefits** [1]. The survey measure uses a
**household survey** and counts people without a job who have actively sought work in the past four weeks and are
available to start in the next two weeks, whether or not they claim [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Voluntary unemployment is when people **choose not to accept work at the going wage** [1]. Involuntary
unemployment is when people are **willing to work at the going wage but cannot find a job** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Deflation is a **falling price level**, a negative inflation rate [1]. Disinflation is a **fall in the rate of
inflation** while prices are still rising [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two [2]: excessive growth in credit and debt; asset price bubbles; destabilising speculation; animal
spirits or herding behaviour; global or domestic shocks.

**7. (a)** Year 3: **recession**, since real GDP fell by 1.4% and investment fell sharply [1]. Year 4: **recovery**,
since real GDP growth turned positive (0.9%) and investment began to grow again [1].

**(b)** Unemployment **lags** output [1]: firms wait until they are confident the recovery will last before hiring,
and growth of 0.9% may be below trend, so the negative output gap was still widening or only beginning to close
[1].

**8. (a)** P = (600 × 5) ÷ 1,500 = **2.0** [1].

**(b)** M = 720; P = (720 × 5) ÷ 1,500 = **2.4** [1].

**(c)** V and Q are **assumed stable**, or determined independently of M, in the long run [1]. Criticism: velocity
can change, for example falling in a crisis when people hold on to money, so a rise in M need not raise P in
proportion [1].

**9.** This is **structural unemployment** [1]: the workers' skills and location no longer match the jobs available
[1]. Raising AD creates jobs in growing sectors and regions, but the car workers may lack the **skills** or be
unable to **move** to them [1], so policies such as retraining and support for mobility are needed instead [1].
**[4]**

**10.** Oil is an input for transport, energy and many products, so a price rise increases **firms' costs** [1].
SRAS shifts left, causing **cost-push inflation** [1]. Fuel and energy prices in the consumer price basket rise
directly, adding to measured inflation [1]. If workers expect higher inflation, they may demand **higher wages**,
spreading the effect further [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Raising AD pushes unemployment below the natural rate and moves the economy up the **short-run Phillips
curve**, with higher inflation [1] [1]. Workers come to **expect** the higher inflation [1] and demand higher money
wages to restore their real wages [1]. Firms' real labour costs return to their previous level, so employment falls
back and unemployment returns to the **natural rate** [1], now with higher inflation. Repeating the process raises
inflation further, so the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate [1]. **[6]**

**12.** *Harms of deflation:* consumers may **delay purchases** expecting lower prices, reducing AD [1]; the **real
burden of debt** rises, squeezing borrowers and firms [1]; firms cut output and jobs, and money wages are hard to cut,
so unemployment may rise [1]. *Harms of moderate inflation:* it erodes savings and fixed incomes [1] and may reduce
competitiveness if faster than in trading partners [1]. *Counter-arguments:* deflation caused by **rising
productivity** (good deflation) can coexist with growth [1]; moderate inflation allows real wages to adjust and gives
room for negative real interest rates [1]. *Judgement:* demand-led deflation is usually more harmful than moderate,
stable inflation because it can become self-reinforcing, but the cause matters [2]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling any fall in growth a recession; check whether real GDP actually fell.
- Confusing a negative output gap with negative growth.
- Forgetting that unemployment lags the cycle.
- Treating structural unemployment as curable by higher AD alone.
- Drawing the long-run Phillips curve as downward sloping.
- Evaluating deflation without asking what caused it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I identify a phase of the cycle from data?**
Look at real GDP growth first, then check unemployment, inflation and investment. Remember that unemployment and
inflation often respond after output.

**Do I need to use T or Q in the Fisher equation?**
The specification allows either, but using Q makes PQ nominal national income and avoids the problem of intermediate
transactions.
