## Two lenses, kept in separate revision banks

Paper 2 Section A offers a choice of essay on **diversity** (variation
across social groups, regions, contexts at a point in time) or
**change** (shift in language over time, 1600 to present). Build and
revise two genuinely separate banks of examples and terminology —
blending them costs precision in the exam when a question asks for one
specifically.

## Sociolect vs dialect — the foundational distinction

| Term | Basis of variation |
|---|---|
| **Sociolect** | Social group (occupation, gender, ethnicity) |
| **Dialect** | Geography (regional, national, international) |

Conflating these two is one of the most common and most costly errors on
diversity essays. A specialist workplace jargon term is a sociolect
feature; a regional pronunciation or vocabulary difference is a dialect
feature — never the reverse.

## A five-lens analytical framework (a revision aid, not the specification's own term)

Apply this systematically to any unfamiliar text, in this order:

1. **Personal** context — who produced it?
2. **Social** context — what group or setting shaped it?
3. **Geographical** context — where, relevant to dialect?
4. **Temporal** context — when, relevant to change?
5. **Audience, purpose, genre, mode** — who is it for, why, in what
form, and where does it sit on the spectrum from spoken to written?

Working through all five before analysing individual language features
prevents the common failure of jumping straight to isolated features
without establishing context first.

## Linking a feature to the bigger picture

A feature identified in isolation ("uses jargon") earns less than one
explicitly linked to the specification's own concepts:

```
Feature:  specialist lexis/abbreviations within an occupational group
Concept:  occupational sociolect
Function: creates in-group identity and efficient communication,
          while potentially excluding outsiders
Link:     connects to how language is used to enact relationships and
          construct identity -- the exact framing the specification
          names
```

Naming the concept and its social function, not just the feature
itself, is what an evaluative essay requires.

## Key term precision

- **Mode** — a spectrum from spoken to written, with digital forms
between, that interacts with audience and purpose to shape language
choices.
- **Representation** — how language constructs an image or portrayal of
a group, potentially reinforcing or challenging social attitudes.
- **Language change** — observable shift in vocabulary, grammar,
pronunciation or usage from 1600 to the present, the specification's
defined timeframe for this content.

## Timeframe discipline for change essays

A change-focused essay must draw on the **1600-to-present** timeframe
the specification names. Using only present-day examples when a
question specifically addresses change is a common and avoidable error
— at least one historical comparison point is expected.

## Where this sits within the full A-level

4.2 Language Diversity and Change is one of two major content areas,
alongside 4.1 Language, the Individual and Society (assessed on Paper
1). Revising 4.2 in isolation from 4.1 misses a useful overlap: several
of the analytical concepts introduced for Paper 1 — audience, purpose,
identity construction — reappear here applied to diversity and change
specifically, so a candidate who has already built fluency with these
concepts for Paper 1 has a head start on applying them to this
content, rather than learning them from scratch.

## Exam traps

- Treating diversity and change as one blended topic rather than
preparing separate example banks.
- Analysing a text's features before establishing its audience,
purpose, genre and mode.
- Naming a language feature without explaining its social function or
what it reveals about identity or relationships.
- Confining a change essay to present-day examples only.
- Confusing sociolect (social-group basis) with dialect (geographic
basis).

## Building a named-debate bank

An evaluative essay is strengthened by referencing a specific, named
attitude or debate about language variation or change (for example,
prescriptivist versus descriptivist attitudes to change, or debates
about whether a dialect or sociolect is being unfairly stigmatised),
rather than only analysing given texts in isolation. Prepare two or
three such named debates in advance so at least one is always available
to bring into an essay, regardless of which specific texts or data the
exam provides.

- **Aitchison's Damp Spoon / Crumbling Castle / Infectious Disease** —
three prescriptivist metaphors for change, which Aitchison herself
rejects.
- **The S-curve model** — a new form's adoption spreads slowly, then
rapidly, then slowly again as it reaches the last holdouts.
- **Functional theory** — change meets new communicative needs (new
technology, new social contexts) rather than reflecting decline.
- **Random fluctuation theory** — some changes have no single
identifiable social or functional cause.

## Working with research data, not just texts

The specification also names collections of language data — dictionaries,
online resources, language corpora — and research findings such as
tables, graphs and statistics as legitimate material for this content
area. Practise interpreting a simple data set (for example, frequency
figures for a word's usage across decades) alongside text-based
analysis, since a question may present quantitative evidence rather
than a prose extract, and the same diversity/change lenses apply to
interpreting it.

## Self-test

1. What is the key distinguishing test between sociolect and dialect?
2. Name the five lenses in this guide's analytical framework.
3. Why should a feature always be linked to a named concept (identity,
relationships, representation) rather than just identified?
4. What timeframe must a language-change essay draw examples from?
5. Why should diversity and change be revised as separate example
banks?

**Answers:** 1. Sociolect is based on social-group membership
(occupation, gender, ethnicity); dialect is based on geography
(regional, national, international variation). 2. Personal, social,
geographical and temporal context, plus audience/purpose/genre/mode. 3.
Because an evaluative essay is assessed on explaining a feature's
social function and significance, not merely spotting and naming it. 4.
1600 to the present day. 5. Because Paper 2 Section A offers a choice
of essay on either diversity or change, so a candidate needs a
separately organised, ready set of examples and terminology for
whichever option they choose, rather than one blended, less precise
set.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between sociolect and dialect?**
A sociolect is variation based on social-group membership, such as occupation, gender or ethnicity. A dialect is variation based on geography, whether regional, national or international.

**What timeframe does language change cover in AQA A-level English Language?**
The AQA A-level English Language (7702) specification defines language change from 1600 to the present. A change essay should include at least one historical comparison point, not only present-day examples.

**Do I answer on diversity or change in AQA Paper 2 Section A?**
Paper 2 Section A offers a choice of essay on either language diversity or language change, so you answer one. Revising them as two separate banks of examples and terminology helps you write precisely on whichever you choose.

**What theories of language change can I use in an essay?**
Useful named ideas include Aitchison's damp spoon, crumbling castle and infectious disease metaphors for prescriptivist attitudes (which Aitchison rejects), the S-curve model of how a new form spreads, functional theory, and random fluctuation theory.

**Can language data appear in the exam instead of a text?**
Yes. The specification names collections of language data, such as dictionaries and corpora, and research findings such as tables, graphs and statistics, so practise interpreting simple data sets with the same diversity and change lenses.
