This guide covers subtopic **8.3 Absorption and assimilation** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to state that the small intestine is the region where nutrients are absorbed, to understand that absorption (by diffusion, osmosis and active transport) is the movement of nutrients from the intestines into the cells lining the digestive system and then into the blood, and that assimilation is the uptake and use by cells of nutrients from the blood. You must describe the structure of a villus and the roles of capillaries and lacteals, explain the significance of villi and microvilli in increasing the internal surface area of the ileum, understand that most water absorption occurs in the small intestine, and state the function of the hepatic portal vein.

This page follows the digested nutrients from the gut to the cells that use them, with a worked surface area calculation. ## Absorption and assimilation

These two words describe different steps and are often confused.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Absorption | The movement of nutrients from the intestines into the cells lining the digestive system, and then into the blood |
| Assimilation | The uptake and use of nutrients from the blood by the cells of the body |

Absorption happens in the **gut**. Assimilation happens in the **body cells**, such as muscle, liver and brain cells.

### Processes involved in absorption

Three processes move the products of digestion across the gut wall.

- **Diffusion**: molecules such as glucose and amino acids move down a concentration gradient from the gut into the blood, which is kept low in glucose because the blood carries it away.
- **Osmosis**: water moves across the gut lining from a higher water potential to a lower water potential.
- **Active transport**: when the concentration of a nutrient (for example glucose) in the gut falls below its concentration in the cells or blood, cells use energy from respiration to take it in against the gradient. The cells lining the ileum contain many mitochondria.

## The small intestine

The **small intestine** is where nutrients are absorbed. Most absorption occurs in the **ileum**, the lower part. By the time food reaches the ileum, digestion has produced small soluble molecules: glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, together with mineral ions, vitamins and water.

### Adaptations of the ileum

The ileum is long and its inner surface is greatly increased in area by folds, **villi** and **microvilli**.

- It has a very large internal surface area, so a lot of absorption can take place at the same time.
- Its lining is very thin, so the distance for diffusion is short.
- It has a very good blood supply, which carries absorbed nutrients away and keeps the concentration gradient steep.

## The villus

A **villus** (plural villi) is a finger-like projection of the wall of the ileum, about a millimetre long. There are huge numbers of them.

| Part | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Epithelium | A wall only **one cell thick** | Short diffusion distance for absorbed molecules |
| Microvilli | Tiny folds on the surface of the epithelial cells | Further increase the surface area |
| Capillaries | A network of thin-walled blood vessels | Absorb glucose, amino acids, mineral ions and water-soluble vitamins; carry them away in the blood |
| Lacteal | A blind-ended lymph vessel in the centre | Absorbs **fatty acids and glycerol** (which are re-formed into fats and carried in the lymph) |

The constant movement of blood in the capillaries keeps the concentration of absorbed molecules low in the blood next to the villus. This maintains a steep concentration gradient, so diffusion continues.

### Worked example: how much do villi add?

*Model: a flat section of gut wall measures 10 mm by 10 mm, so its area is 100 mm squared. Now suppose it carries 2000 villi, each a cylinder 1 mm long and 0.2 mm in diameter. Estimate how much the villi increase the surface area.*

1. The curved side of one villus has area = pi x diameter x length = 3.14 x 0.2 x 1 = **0.63 mm squared**.
2. For 2000 villi: 2000 x 0.63 = **1257 mm squared** of extra area. (The tip of each villus replaces the base it stands on, so this is the net addition.)
3. Total area = 100 + 1257 = **1357 mm squared**.
4. Increase = 1357 divided by 100 = about **13.6 times** larger.

This is only a model, and it ignores the folds and the microvilli. The microvilli increase the area many times more. The real ileum has a surface area far greater than a smooth tube would have.

**Significance**: a larger internal surface area means that more nutrients can be absorbed in a given time, so the body gets the nutrients it needs from food efficiently.

## Absorption of water

Water is absorbed from the **lumen** (the central space) of the small intestine and the **colon**. Most water is absorbed in the **small intestine**, by osmosis. The water in the gut comes from food and drink, and from the digestive secretions (saliva, gastric juice, bile and pancreatic juice). The colon absorbs most of the remaining water, so that the faeces are semi-solid.

*Illustrative data*: if 9.0 dm cubed of water enters the gut in a day and 8.5 dm cubed is absorbed in the small intestine, the percentage absorbed there is 8.5 divided by 9.0 x 100 = 94 per cent. This supports the statement that most absorption of water takes place in the small intestine. In diarrhoea, water is not absorbed, so too much is lost.

## The hepatic portal vein

Blood from the capillaries in the villi flows into veins that join to form the **hepatic portal vein**. Its function is to carry **most of the molecules and ions absorbed from the ileum to the liver**. (Fatty acids and glycerol enter the lacteals and reach the blood by a different route.)

The liver acts on the blood before it passes to the rest of the body. For example, it:

- removes excess glucose from the blood and stores it as glycogen
- processes amino acids, and breaks down excess amino acids to make urea
- removes many harmful substances from the blood

This is why the blood in the hepatic portal vein is rich in nutrients after a meal, and why the liver is described as controlling the content of the blood.

## Assimilation in the cells

Once nutrients have entered the blood, they are carried to all the tissues. Cells then use them.

| Nutrient | Use after assimilation |
|---|---|
| Glucose | Used in respiration to release energy; stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles |
| Amino acids | Built into proteins (enzymes, muscle, haemoglobin) |
| Fatty acids and glycerol | Made into lipids for cell membranes and energy storage; used in respiration |
| Mineral ions and vitamins | Used for specific jobs, such as iron in haemoglobin |

## Two routes out of the gut

The products of digestion leave the villus by two different routes. Keeping them separate is a common exam point.

| Route | Nutrients | Where they go |
|---|---|---|
| Capillaries | Glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, water-soluble vitamins, most water | Into the blood, then the **hepatic portal vein** to the liver |
| Lacteal | Fatty acids and glycerol | Into the lymph, which later joins the blood system |

If a question asks "which nutrients are carried by the hepatic portal vein", answer with the first row. Fat products travel by the lacteal and do not take the liver route first.

## Why the ileum and not the stomach?

Questions sometimes ask why most absorption happens in the ileum. Use features of the ileum, not just "it is the small intestine".

- By the time food reaches the ileum, digestion is nearly complete, so there are many small soluble molecules ready to be absorbed.
- The ileum is long, which gives a large area, and it is lined with villi and microvilli.
- Its wall is thin and has a rich blood supply, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
- The stomach has a thick muscular wall, no villi and a smaller surface area, and its contents are still mostly partly digested food.

Alcohol and some drugs can cross the stomach wall, which is why they act quickly, but nutrients are mainly absorbed in the ileum.

## Common mistakes

- Using absorption and assimilation as if they meant the same thing.
- Saying that villi absorb into the "stomach" or that digestion happens in the villi.
- Saying that the lacteals absorb glucose. Glucose enters the capillaries.
- Saying that most water is absorbed in the colon.
- Saying that the hepatic portal vein carries blood to the heart or from the liver.
- Writing "large surface area" without saying why this helps absorption.
- Forgetting that the villus wall is only one cell thick.

## Command words

- **State**: give the region or function in a few words.
- **Describe**: give the structure of a villus.
- **Explain**: give the significance of villi and microvilli.
- **Understand** terms: be able to give the precise meaning of absorption and assimilation.
