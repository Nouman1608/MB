This guide covers section 21.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: why bacteria are so useful in biotechnology and genetic modification. It is the short opening section of topic 21, but every later idea in the topic depends on it. Core statement 21.1.1 is examined on all theory and multiple-choice papers. Supplement statement 21.1.2 is examined only on the Extended papers (Papers 2 and 4) and is labelled "Extended only" below.

Once you have read this page, test your recall with the [section 21.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-biotechnology-and-genetic-modification-revision-notes/) and then try the [section 21.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-biotechnology-and-genetic-modification-practice/). For the whole of topic 21 in one place, see the [topic 21 study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-biotechnology-and-genetic-modification/).

## What the syllabus asks for

| Statement | Wording you must be able to use | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 21.1.1 | State that bacteria are useful in biotechnology and genetic modification due to their **rapid reproduction rate** and their **ability to make complex molecules** | Core |
| 21.1.2 | Discuss why bacteria are useful, limited to (a) **few ethical concerns** over their manipulation and growth and (b) **the presence of plasmids** | Extended only |

That is the whole list. Four features, two for Core and two more for Extended. Questions on this section rarely ask for anything outside these four, but they often ask you to *apply* them, for example to explain why bacteria rather than sheep are used to make a human protein.

## The bacterial cell: what you need from topic 2

You cannot explain plasmids without knowing where they sit. From topic 2, a bacterial cell has:

- a **cell wall** (not made of cellulose)
- a **cell membrane**
- **cytoplasm**
- **ribosomes**, where proteins are made
- a single loop of **circular DNA**, which is not enclosed in a nucleus
- one or more **plasmids**: small, separate circular loops of DNA.

Bacteria have no nucleus. A common error is to write that a gene is "inserted into the nucleus of the bacterium". There is no nucleus to insert it into; in genetic modification the gene goes into a plasmid.

## Core feature 1: rapid reproduction rate

Bacteria reproduce **asexually**: one cell divides to form two. In good conditions (enough nutrients, a suitable temperature and pH, and wastes removed) this can happen again and again in a short time, so the number of cells doubles at regular intervals.

Why this matters in biotechnology:

1. **A large population quickly.** Starting from a small sample, a fermenter can hold a huge number of cells within hours or days, so a large amount of product is made in a short time.
2. **Low cost per unit of product.** Less time and fewer resources are needed than for growing plants or animals that take months or years to mature.
3. **Every new cell is genetically identical.** Asexual reproduction produces offspring that are genetically identical to the parent. If one bacterium contains a useful gene, all of its descendants contain the same gene and can all make the same product. This is the link to genetic modification in section 21.3.

### Worked example: doubling

*The figures are illustrative.* A fermenter is started with 2000 bacteria. In the conditions used, the population doubles every 30 minutes. How many bacteria are present after 4 hours, assuming no cells die?

```
number of doublings = 4 hours ÷ 0.5 hours = 8
number of bacteria  = 2000 × 2⁸ = 2000 × 256 = 512 000
```

**Answer: 512 000 bacteria.** Notice that the population does not rise by the same amount each half-hour; it doubles each time, so the increase gets bigger and bigger. A good written answer would add that, in a real fermenter, growth would eventually slow as nutrients are used up or wastes build up, which is why fermenter conditions are controlled (section 21.2).

## Core feature 2: ability to make complex molecules

Bacteria contain ribosomes and the enzymes needed to build large molecules from small ones. The most important complex molecules here are **proteins**, including enzymes and, after genetic modification, human proteins such as **insulin**.

The key idea for an answer is that a bacterium will use any gene it carries as an instruction to make a protein. From topic 17, a gene is a length of DNA whose base sequence determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein. So if a bacterium is given a human gene, its ribosomes can build the human protein. Bacteria are therefore useful twice over: they make useful molecules naturally, and they can be made to produce molecules that they would not normally make.

Be careful not to overclaim. Ordinary bacteria do not make human insulin; only bacteria that have been genetically modified with the human insulin gene do. And penicillin is made by a fungus, not a bacterium, so do not use it as an example of a complex molecule made by bacteria.

## Extended only: few ethical concerns

Using living organisms raises ethical questions: is it right to change an organism's genes, keep it in a vessel, or kill it to collect a product? For bacteria these concerns are much smaller than for animals:

- Bacteria are single cells with no nervous system, so there is no question of pain or suffering of the kind that arises when animals are kept or modified.
- They are not kept in the way farm or laboratory animals are, so animal welfare rules and objections do not apply in the same way.
- Very large numbers can be grown, modified and destroyed without the objections raised by doing the same to mammals.

The syllabus says "few" ethical concerns, not "none". Some people still object to any genetic modification, and there are concerns about modified microorganisms escaping. That is why fermenters are sealed and sterilised. In an answer, "few ethical concerns" earns credit; "no ethical concerns" is an overstatement that a careful examiner may not accept.

## Extended only: the presence of plasmids

**Plasmids** are small circular loops of DNA found in bacterial cells, separate from the main circular DNA. They make bacteria useful in genetic modification because:

- **they can be cut open and joined again.** A plasmid can be cut with a restriction enzyme, a new gene inserted and the DNA rejoined with DNA ligase, forming a recombinant plasmid (the full process is in section 21.3).
- **they can be put back into bacteria.** The recombinant plasmid acts as a carrier, taking the new gene into a bacterial cell.
- **they are copied when the bacterium divides.** Each new cell receives the plasmid, so the inserted gene passes to the whole population as it grows.
- **the bacterium uses the genes they carry.** Genes on a plasmid are expressed, so the bacterium makes the protein coded for by the inserted gene.

The syllabus asks you to *discuss* these features. In practice, exam questions on this point usually use the command words **explain** or **suggest**, which in the 0610 command word list mean giving reasons or applying your knowledge to a new situation. Either way, a bare list ("plasmids, ethics") is not enough: each feature needs a "because" or "so".

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Extended):** *Explain why bacteria, rather than mammals, are used to produce large quantities of a human protein.* [4]

> Bacteria reproduce very rapidly ✔, so a large population, and therefore a large amount of the protein, can be produced in a short time ✔. Bacteria contain plasmids ✔, into which the human gene can be inserted, and the bacteria then make the human protein ✔. Growing and modifying bacteria also raises fewer ethical concerns than using mammals.

*Commentary:* the first two ticks come from linking rapid reproduction to the amount of product. The third and fourth come from naming plasmids and saying what they allow. The final sentence is correct and would earn a mark in place of either pair if one were weaker; with four marks available, one well-developed point per mark is the target. Notice the answer compares with mammals, which the question asked for.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "bacteria reproduce quickly" with no consequence. Add "so a large amount of product is made in a short time".
- Putting the inserted gene into a nucleus. Bacteria have no nucleus; the gene goes into a plasmid.
- Describing plasmids as "chromosomes" or "the main DNA". They are small and separate from the main circular DNA.
- Saying there are "no" ethical concerns. The syllabus wording is "few".
- Using penicillin as an example of a product made by bacteria. It comes from a fungus.
- In Core answers, giving Extended reasons only. A Core question will usually expect rapid reproduction and the ability to make complex molecules.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (Core statement 21.1.1): a short answer is enough. "They reproduce rapidly; they can make complex molecules" gains both marks.
- **Explain**: give the feature, then the reason it helps. One feature with a reason is usually worth two marks.
- **Suggest**: apply the four features to an unfamiliar context, such as a new product or a comparison with plants or animals.
- **Describe**: when shown a diagram of a bacterial cell, describe the plasmid's features (small, circular, separate from main DNA) before saying why it is useful.
