This study guide teaches Criterion D: Thinking critically in IB MYP Individuals and Societies. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Individuals and societies guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion D is where you move beyond knowing what happened to asking how we know, who says so and what follows. It carries the most marks in the on-screen examination, so it repays careful practice.

Use it with the [Criterion D revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-d-thinking-critically-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion D practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-d-thinking-critically-practice/). The wider course is on the [IB MYP Individuals and Societies course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/).

## What the guide says about Criterion D

The objective is "Thinking critically". Students use critical thinking skills to develop and apply their understanding of individuals and societies and the process of investigation. At the end of Year 5 the four strands are:

1. **Discuss** concepts, issues, models, visual representation and theories.
2. **Synthesize** information to make valid, well-supported arguments.
3. **Analyse and evaluate** a range of sources and data in terms of origin and purpose, examining value and limitations.
4. **Interpret** different perspectives and their implications.

Criterion D has a maximum of 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Band | i Discuss | ii Arguments | iii Sources and data | iv Perspectives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | analyses to a limited extent | summarises to a limited extent to make arguments | describes a limited number of sources; nominal value and limitations | identifies perspectives; minimal implications |
| 3-4 | analyses | summarises to make arguments | analyses and/or evaluates; some value and limitations | interprets perspectives and some implications |
| 5-6 | discusses | synthesises to make valid arguments | effectively analyses and evaluates a range; usually recognises value and limitations | interprets perspectives and their implications |
| 7-8 | detailed discussion | synthesises to make valid, well-supported arguments | effectively analyses and evaluates a range; consistently recognises value and limitations | thoroughly interprets a range of perspectives and their implications |

In Year 3 the strand verbs are "analyse", "summarize", "analyse" and "recognize" different perspectives and "explain" their implications. In Year 1 the strands are to identify main points, use information to justify an opinion, identify and analyse sources, and identify different views and their implications. The same skills deepen from year to year.

## Command terms

| Term | Guide meaning |
|---|---|
| Analyse | break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure (identify parts and relationships, and interpret information to reach conclusions) |
| Discuss | offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses; opinions or conclusions are presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence |
| Evaluate | make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations |
| Interpret | use knowledge and understanding to recognise trends and draw conclusions from given information |
| Synthesize | combine different ideas in order to create new understanding |
| Justify | give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion |
| Summarize | abstract a general theme or major point(s) |
| Explain | give a detailed account including reasons or causes |

"Discuss" asks for a balanced review of several arguments, so a one-sided answer is incomplete. "Synthesize" asks you to combine ideas from more than one source into something new.

## Strand iii first: analysing and evaluating sources

Every source was made by someone, at some time, for some reason. Ask four questions, often abbreviated in classrooms as OPVL.

| Letter | Question |
|---|---|
| Origin | who made it, when, where and what type of source is it? |
| Purpose | why was it made, and for whom? |
| Value | what is useful about it for this question? |
| Limitations | what does it leave out, or how might it mislead? |

### Worked example: two invented sources

The question is: *How far did factory work improve the lives of families in the town of Hartwell in the 1840s?*

**Source A** (invented for this guide). Extract from a report by a government inspector, 1842.
"In the weaving sheds of Hartwell I counted 140 children under thirteen. Shifts begin at five in the morning and end at seven in the evening. Many looked pale and tired. Wages are paid weekly, and I am told that families who work in the mills are better fed than those who depend on farm work, though several workers complained of coughs."

**Source B** (invented for this guide). Extract from a letter by a mill owner to a local newspaper, 1843.
"Our mill gives work to hundreds who would otherwise have none. Our workers receive regular wages and live in cottages built by the company. Those who speak against us are idle agitators who know nothing of honest business."

**Analysis of Source A.**

- *Origin*: a government inspector, writing a report in 1842. He visited and counted, so this is a primary source based on observation.
- *Purpose*: to inform the government about conditions so that laws could be considered.
- *Value*: it gives specific, checkable detail (140 children, hours of work) and mentions both better food and ill health, so it is balanced. The writer is not employed by the mill.
- *Limitations*: it records one visit, so conditions on other days may differ. Some information is reported ("I am told"), so it is second-hand. The inspector may have expected to find poor conditions.

**Analysis of Source B.**

- *Origin*: the mill owner, writing to a newspaper in 1843. A primary source from someone with a direct interest.
- *Purpose*: to defend the mill and its reputation, and to persuade readers that critics are wrong.
- *Value*: it shows how employers justified their business and what they claimed they offered (regular wages and housing).
- *Limitations*: it gives no figures and no evidence. The owner profits from the mill, so he has a reason to present it favourably. It insults critics, and does not address the issue of children or long hours.

**Comparing them.** Both agree that the mill provided regular wages. They disagree about conditions. Source A is more reliable on conditions because it is based on observation by someone who gains nothing from the mill, and it is more detailed. Source B is useful for showing the employer's point of view.

## Strand iv: perspectives and implications

A perspective is the way someone looks at something, shaped by their position and interests. To **interpret** perspectives you identify them, explain why they hold them and say what follows.

| Perspective | Why they hold it | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Mill owner | profit depends on cheap labour and long hours | resists regulation; stresses jobs and wages |
| Government inspector | responsible for reporting on the law and public welfare | recommends limits on hours and child labour |
| Working family | needs wages to eat; children's earnings are important | may welcome work but suffer ill health, and may fear losing income if laws change |
| Doctor or reformer | sees illness and injury | calls for safer conditions and schooling |

"Implications" are the results of a view for people or events, for example: if child labour is limited, factory output may fall in the short term and families may lose income, but children's health and schooling could improve in the long term.

## Strand ii: synthesising information into an argument

A strong argument has a clear **claim**, **evidence from more than one source**, **reasoning** that links evidence to the claim, and an acknowledgement of **counter-views**.

**Worked paragraph (for the Hartwell question).**

"Factory work improved some aspects of family life in Hartwell, but at a high cost to health. Both the inspector and the owner agree that mill families received regular wages, and the inspector adds that these families were better fed than those depending on farm work (Source A). This suggests a real gain in income and food security. However, the inspector also found 140 children working shifts of fourteen hours and reported coughs among workers, which shows that the gains came with serious harm. The owner's claim that workers were well housed (Source B) has no evidence, and he benefits from presenting the mill well, so it should be treated with caution. Taken together, the evidence suggests that factory work raised incomes but harmed health, especially for children, so the improvement was partial and unequal."

Notice how it **synthesises**: it combines two sources to reach a new conclusion, weighs their reliability, and ends with a judgment that is qualified ("partial and unequal").

## Strand i: discussing concepts, issues, models and theories

Criterion D also asks you to discuss ideas, not only sources. A concept (for example significance, sustainability or scarcity), an issue (for example whether to ban cars from a city centre), a model (for example the demographic transition) or a visual representation (a map, graph or cartoon) can be discussed.

**Method.**

1. **Define** the idea in your own words.
2. **Explain** the arguments for it, with evidence.
3. **Explain** the arguments against, or its limits, with evidence.
4. **Weigh up** the arguments and reach a supported conclusion.

**Visual sources.** For a cartoon or a map, describe what it shows, then analyse the message, origin and purpose, the audience and the techniques used, and comment on its value and limitations as evidence.

**Data sources.** For a graph or table, quote figures, describe the trend, give a reason, and say what the data does not show (for example the causes, or years outside the range).

## How Criterion D is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for individuals and societies, the Thinking critically task is worth 36 marks: 20 for Criterion D, 8 for Criterion C and 8 for Criterion A. The guide says the task assesses the ability to think about and discuss issues, arguments and perspectives through structured questions culminating in an extended piece of writing. Sources can include primary and secondary texts, images, maps, graphs, data tables, charts, videos and animations. Criterion D is worth 20 of the 80 marks in the whole examination.

## Common errors

- Describing a source and not evaluating it.
- "It is biased" with no explanation of how the bias affects the evidence.
- Treating a source as worthless because it is biased, instead of saying what it is still useful for.
- Using one source when the question asks for a range.
- Listing views with no reasons or implications.
- Summarising each source in turn instead of synthesising them.
- A conclusion that does not follow from the evidence, or that ignores counter-views.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion D in IB MYP Individuals and Societies?**
Criterion D is Thinking critically. It assesses how well you discuss concepts and issues, synthesise information into valid arguments, analyse and evaluate sources for origin, purpose, value and limitations, and interpret different perspectives and their implications.

**What is the difference between analyse and evaluate?**
Analyse breaks something down to bring out its essential elements and relationships. Evaluate weighs up strengths and limitations to reach an appraisal.

**What does synthesise mean in practice?**
It means combining ideas from more than one source or point of view to create new understanding, for example a conclusion that neither source states on its own.

**How many marks is Criterion D worth in the on-screen examination?**
Twenty of the 80 marks, in the Thinking critically task.
