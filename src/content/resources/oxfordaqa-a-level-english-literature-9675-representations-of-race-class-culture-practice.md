## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain what the specification means by saying the four areas "are not topics which are
prescribed for detailed study". **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between an insider's and an outsider's viewpoint in representing a
culture, and one effect of each. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how a writer can use a stereotype in order to subvert it. **[2]**

**4.** A 4b student plans to write on a poetry collection linked to class and a novel linked to class.
Explain the problem and how to fix it. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a contextual point about the date of an unseen text should be linked to a specific
word or image. **[2]**

## Section B: original texts and essay skills

**Poem (original).**

> **Sunday Best**
>
> My father ironed his one good shirt
> on the kitchen table, Sundays,
> pressing the collar flat as a promise.
> At church the bank manager's sons
> wore theirs creased, carelessly,
> the way you wear a thing you'll never lose.

**6.** Analyse how the poem represents class through clothing. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the final line changes the poem's significance. **[3]**

**Passage (original).** In this invented passage, Leila, born in Britain to parents from abroad, visits
her grandmother's town for the first time.

> In the market everyone knew I was foreign before I opened my mouth. It was the shoes, my cousin
> said later, and the way I waited to be served. When I did speak, in the language my mother had
> taught me at the kitchen table, the stallholder laughed and answered in English, kindly, to save me
> the trouble. I paid too much for the apricots and did not mind. At home they called me by my
> grandmother's name. Here they called me "the English one", and I did not know which of us was
> wrong.

**8.** Analyse how the passage represents culture and belonging. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how the gaze operates in the passage. **[3]**

**10.** A student writes: "The passage shows that people from abroad are rude to British visitors."
Explain two problems with this reading and offer a better one. **[4]**

**11.** Write a topic sentence for a 4a answer on the poem that directly addresses representation of
class and culture. **[2]**

**12.** List four elements of a plan for a 4b essay on a prose text you have chosen, linked to race,
class or culture. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The areas are frameworks for exploring representation in the texts [1], not subjects to study
in depth, so answers are rewarded for analysis rather than for knowledge about the topic [1]. **[2]**

**2.** An insider is a member of the group represented [1]; their view may show pride, shame or
divided loyalty, giving intimacy and authority [1]. An outsider observes from beyond the group [1];
their view may reveal curiosity or prejudice, often exposing the observer as much as the observed
[1]. **[4]**

**3.** The writer sets up a familiar image so the reader expects it [1], then complicates or reverses
it through a character's actions, voice or viewpoint, exposing the stereotype as false or limited
[1]. **[2]**

**4.** Each text must be linked to a different one of the four areas [1]; the student should link one
text to another area, such as women/men, childhood or war, or choose a different text [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Context is rewarded only when it helps explain how the text makes meaning [1]; a general
historical point that does not change the reading of a line shows knowledge but not analysis [1].
**[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- "His one good shirt" establishes scarcity, so clothing carries the family's limited means.
- Ironing "on the kitchen table" shows domestic space doubling as workspace.
- The simile "flat as a promise" turns the pressed collar into a vow of respectability.
- "Sundays" places the effort in a weekly ritual of public appearance.
- The bank manager's sons are identified by their father's job, so class is inherited.
- "Creased, carelessly" uses alliteration to make their ease sound effortless and slightly
  dismissive.
- Church is the shared space where class differences are on display.
- The contrast between the two sentences structures the poem as a comparison of effort
  and ease.

**7.** Any three, [1] each: "the way you wear a thing you'll never lose" moves from clothing to
security, so class becomes a matter of confidence; it implies the father fears loss; the
generalising "you" draws the reader into the sons' privilege; the line turns care into a sign of
precariousness rather than pride alone. **[3]**

**8.** Any six, [1] each:

- "Everyone knew I was foreign before I opened my mouth" shows identity read from the body.
- The shoes and the way she "waited to be served" are cultural markers she cannot hide.
- The language learned "at the kitchen table" links heritage to private, domestic transmission.
- The stallholder's switch to English "kindly" is generous but also denies her belonging.
- Paying "too much" and not minding suggests a wish to be accepted, even at a cost.
- The two names, her grandmother's at home and "the English one" here, show a split identity.
- The final clause, "I did not know which of us was wrong", leaves belonging unresolved and
  questions both communities' labels.
- The retrospective first person ("my cousin said later") gives a reflective, uncertain tone.

**9.** Any three, [1] each: the market's observers read Leila before she speaks, so she is the one
looked at; their gaze defines her as foreign; the cousin's explanation shows Leila learning how she
is seen; Leila does not look back with judgement, but turns the question on herself. **[3]**

**10.** Problem one [1]: the passage presents the stallholder as kind, not rude. Problem two [1]: the
reading generalises about a group instead of analysing the representation of one experience. Better
reading [2] for an argued reading tied to the text, [1] for a partial one, for example: "The passage
represents belonging as a matter of how others read Leila, so that the market's friendliness itself
marks her as an outsider." **[4]**

**11.** A topic sentence that names representation, class and culture and makes an argument [2]; one
that names the area without an argument [1]. Example: "Through two ways of wearing a shirt, the poem
represents class as the difference between labouring to appear respectable and inheriting the ease
to look careless." **[2]**

**12.** Any four, [1] each: a task framed around representation (for example, "Explore the
significance of the representation of..."); a text not on any exam set-text list; a central
argument that allows debate; key passages from the beginning, middle and end; critical views to weigh;
methods to analyse (viewpoint, voice, markers); context used only where it explains a choice; a
conclusion on what the representation suggests. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about a society instead of analysing the text.
- Attributing a character's views to the writer.
- Covering race, class and culture together when the question names one aspect.
- Listing markers without explaining what they are made to mean.
