## Definitions in the field of mental health

**Statistical infrequency.** A characteristic is unusual if it is rare in the population. On a normal distribution, scores far from the mean, such as an IQ below 70 (about 2% of people), are statistically infrequent.
*Evaluation.* It is objective and useful for diagnosing conditions defined by a score. But some rare characteristics are desirable, such as very high IQ, and some common conditions, such as depression, would be missed.

**Deviation from social/cultural norms.** Behaviour is unusual if it breaks the unwritten rules a society or culture holds about acceptable behaviour.
*Evaluation.* It considers the social effect of behaviour. But norms vary across cultures and change over time, so behaviour judged abnormal in one place or era may be accepted in another; it also risks labelling people who simply choose to be different.

**Failure to function adequately.** A person cannot cope with the ordinary demands of daily life, such as holding a job, maintaining relationships or caring for themselves. Signs suggested by Rosenhan and Seligman (1989) include personal distress, irrational or dangerous behaviour, and no longer following interpersonal rules such as keeping eye contact or personal space.
*Evaluation.* It recognises the person's own experience and distress. But judging what counts as "adequate" is subjective, and some people with serious disorders continue to function well.

**Deviation from ideal mental health.** Jahoda (1958) listed criteria for ideal mental health: a positive attitude towards oneself, self-actualisation, autonomy, resistance to stress, an accurate perception of reality, and mastery of the environment. Falling short of these indicates a problem.
*Evaluation.* It focuses on what is needed for good mental health, which helps target support. But the standard is so high that most people would fall short, and the criteria, especially autonomy, reflect individualist Western values.

No single definition is adequate alone; in practice, clinicians use several together.

## Characteristics of the three disorders

| Disorder | Behavioural | Emotional | Cognitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phobias | Avoidance; panic; freezing or fainting | Anxiety and fear out of proportion to the danger | Selective attention to the feared object; irrational beliefs; recognising the fear is excessive |
| Depression | Reduced energy and activity; disrupted sleep and eating; possible aggression or self-harm | Persistent low mood; anger; low self-esteem | Focusing on the negative; absolutist "all-or-nothing" thinking |
| OCD | Compulsions, repeated to reduce anxiety; avoiding triggers | Anxiety and distress; guilt and disgust | Intrusive obsessive thoughts; insight that the thoughts are excessive; hypervigilance |

## The behavioural approach to phobias

**The two-process model** (Mowrer, 1960) uses two forms of learning.

1. **Acquisition by classical conditioning.** A neutral stimulus becomes associated with an unconditioned stimulus that causes fear, so it becomes a conditioned stimulus producing a conditioned fear response. In Watson and Rayner's (1920) study, Little Albert came to fear a white rat after it was repeatedly paired with a loud noise, and the fear generalised to similar furry objects.
2. **Maintenance by operant conditioning.** Avoiding the phobic stimulus reduces anxiety. The removal of an unpleasant feeling is **negative reinforcement**, so avoidance is repeated. Because the person never meets the stimulus without harm, the fear is never unlearned.

*Evaluation.* The model explains why phobias persist and led to effective exposure therapies. However, not everyone with a phobia remembers a frightening experience, and some fears, such as of snakes, may reflect an evolved readiness to fear things that threatened our ancestors, which conditioning alone does not explain.

**Systematic desensitisation (SD)**, developed by Wolpe, gradually reduces the conditioned fear through counterconditioning.
- The patient learns a **relaxation** technique, such as breathing exercises or progressive muscle relaxation.
- Patient and therapist build an **anxiety hierarchy**: a list of situations involving the phobic stimulus, ranked from least to most frightening.
- The patient works up the hierarchy, moving on only when relaxed at each step. Fear and relaxation cannot exist together (**reciprocal inhibition**), so the stimulus becomes associated with relaxation.

**Flooding** exposes the patient immediately to the most frightening situation, without a gradual build-up, for a long session. They cannot avoid it, so the fear response peaks and then subsides through **extinction**, and they learn the stimulus is harmless.

*Evaluation of treatments.* SD suits a wide range of patients, including those with learning difficulties, and patients can control the pace. Flooding is quicker and so cheaper, but it is highly distressing; patients must give fully informed consent, and some drop out, making the phobia worse.

## The cognitive approach to depression

**Beck's negative triad** (1967). Depressed people have **faulty information processing**, attending to the negative and ignoring the positive, and **negative self-schemas** formed in childhood. These produce a triad of negative views about:
- **the self** ("I am a failure")
- **the world** ("people are against me")
- **the future** ("nothing will ever get better").

**Ellis's ABC model** (1962). Depression results from irrational thinking, not events themselves.
- **A**: an **activating event**, such as failing a driving test.
- **B**: the **belief** about it. Irrational beliefs include **"musturbation"**, such as "I must always succeed", and "I-can't-stand-it-itis".
- **C**: the emotional and behavioural **consequences**, such as depression.

*Evaluation.* Research supports a link between negative cognitions and depression, and the approach has led to successful therapy. However, it is hard to establish whether negative thinking causes depression or results from it. Focusing on beliefs also risks overlooking real circumstances such as poverty or abuse.

**Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT)** identifies irrational, negative thoughts and **challenges** them.
- In Beck's approach, the client acts like a scientist, testing beliefs against reality. Homework, such as recording occasions when people were kind, provides evidence against negative beliefs.
- In Ellis's approach, the therapist extends ABC to **ABCDE**: **D**ispute irrational beliefs (asking where the evidence is, or whether the belief is logical) leading to an **E**ffective new belief.
- **Behavioural activation** encourages the client to return to rewarding activities.

*Evaluation.* CBT addresses causes rather than symptoms and has long-lasting effects for many clients. It needs motivation and energy, so severely depressed clients may need medication first.

## The biological approach to OCD

**Genetic explanations.** OCD tends to run in families. Lewis (1936) found that 37% of his OCD patients had a parent with OCD and 21% had a sibling with it. Twin studies find higher concordance for identical (MZ) than non-identical (DZ) twins; a review by Nestadt et al. (2010) reported 68% for MZ and 31% for DZ twins. Many genes are likely to be involved (**polygenic**), including candidate genes that help regulate serotonin.

**Neural explanations.** Low levels of **serotonin** may disrupt the transmission of mood-relevant information. Abnormal activity in frontal brain circuits, including high activity in the **orbitofrontal cortex**, which is involved in worry and decision-making, may produce the sense that something is wrong and cannot be resolved.

*Evaluation.* Concordance below 100% in MZ twins shows environmental factors also matter, supporting a diathesis-stress view. The effectiveness of serotonin-based drugs supports the neural explanation, but it does not prove low serotonin causes OCD; brain differences might result from the disorder.

**Drug therapy.** **SSRIs** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors), such as fluoxetine, block the reuptake of serotonin at the synapse, so more remains to stimulate the postsynaptic neuron. If SSRIs fail, alternatives include tricyclics such as clomipramine, and drugs are often combined with CBT.

*Evaluation.* Drugs are cost-effective and need little effort from the patient, and reviews of controlled trials show SSRIs reduce symptoms compared with placebo for many patients. However, they can cause side effects such as nausea and headaches, symptoms often return when medication stops, and not all patients respond.

## Applied model answer

*Original scenario.* Malik has been terrified of lifts since he was stuck in one for an hour as a child. He now climbs twelve floors to his office every day. *Use the two-process model to explain Malik's phobia.* [4]

> Being stuck caused fear, an unconditioned response to an unconditioned stimulus, the frightening experience. The lift, a neutral stimulus, was associated with it and became a conditioned stimulus that triggers fear. This is acquisition by classical conditioning. Malik now avoids lifts by using the stairs, which reduces his anxiety; this negative reinforcement strengthens avoidance, so he never learns lifts are safe and the phobia is maintained.

## Common mistakes

- Saying negative reinforcement is punishment; it is the removal of something unpleasant.
- Mixing up flooding and systematic desensitisation.
- Writing Beck's triad as "self, others, past" instead of self, world and future.
- Describing SSRIs as increasing serotonin production; they block reuptake.
- Giving characteristics without labelling them behavioural, emotional or cognitive.
- Evaluating definitions with the same point for all four.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** or **describe**: precise content; for treatments, give the steps in order.
- **Apply**: name the concept, then tie it to the scenario detail.
- **Explain one limitation**: point, reason or evidence, consequence for the explanation or treatment.
- **Discuss** or **evaluate**: compare with alternative approaches and end with a judgement.
