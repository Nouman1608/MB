This guide covers section 3.2 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Low unemployment, one of the four economic objectives in topic 3 and part of Component 02. It extends the wider [guide to economic growth, GDP and unemployment](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-economic-growth-gdp-and-unemployment/) with harder calculations, classification practice and deeper evaluation. All figures are invented.

Then use the [low unemployment revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/) and the [low unemployment practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-low-unemployment-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by employment and unemployment | Define both and know who is outside the labour force |
| Explain how unemployment is measured using the Claimant Count | Describe the measure and its strengths and weaknesses |
| Calculate the unemployment rate | Use the labour force, not the population |
| Analyse recent and historical unemployment figures | Read trends, compare rates and numbers, link to the economy |
| Explain the types of unemployment | Cyclical, frictional, seasonal, structural |
| Evaluate causes and consequences | For individuals, regions and the government |

## Who counts as what

Every adult falls into one of three groups:

- **Employed**: in paid work, full-time, part-time or self-employed.
- **Unemployed**: without a job, but available for work and actively looking.
- **Economically inactive**: neither working nor looking, such as full-time students, retired people, full-time carers and those too ill to work.

The **labour force** (or workforce) is the employed plus the unemployed. Inactive people are not in it, so a student who stops studying and starts job-hunting **adds** to unemployment even though nobody lost a job.

A government objective of "low unemployment" does not mean zero. Some people will always be between jobs, so a small amount of frictional unemployment is normal and even useful.

## The Claimant Count

The Claimant Count is the number of people claiming benefits principally because they are unemployed.

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Quick and cheap: the records already exist | Misses jobless people who are not entitled to claim, for example because a partner earns or they have savings |
| Published often, so changes are seen fast | Misses people who choose not to claim |
| Can be broken down by area, age and length of claim | Changes to benefit rules change the count even if joblessness has not changed |
| A direct measure of a cost to the government | Can include some people who do a little work while claiming |

**Analysis point.** Because the count depends on benefit rules, a fall in the Claimant Count after rules are tightened is not evidence that more people found work. Always ask what else changed.

## Calculating the unemployment rate

Unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100

### Worked example 1: the rate can fall while the number rises

Country T has a labour force of 32.0 million, of whom 1.6 million are unemployed. A year later the labour force is 34.0 million (more young people and migrants seeking work) and 1.65 million are unemployed.

- Year 1 rate = 1.6 ÷ 32.0 × 100 = **5.0%**.
- Year 2 rate = 1.65 ÷ 34.0 × 100 = **4.9%** (4.85%).
- The number unemployed **rose** by 50,000, a 3.1% increase.
- The rate **fell** by about 0.15 percentage points.

Both statements are true. The rate fell because the labour force grew faster than unemployment. In the exam, say which measure you are using.

You can also work backwards. If a region has a labour force of 400,000 and an unemployment rate of 9%, the number unemployed is 400,000 × 0.09 = **36,000**.

## Analysing unemployment figures

When you are given a table or chart:

1. Quote the highest and lowest values and when they occurred.
2. Distinguish rates (percentages) from numbers (people).
3. Describe the change in **percentage points** when comparing rates.
4. Link to the economy: unemployment usually rises after output falls and drops slowly in recovery, because firms wait to be sure demand is back before hiring.
5. Compare groups or regions if the data allows.

**Historical context.** In the UK, unemployment rose sharply in the early 1980s as many coal mines, steelworks and factories closed, and the job losses were concentrated in former industrial areas. This is the classic example of structural unemployment hitting particular regions. When an exam gives you real figures, use those figures rather than memorised ones.

## The four types

| Type | Cause | Typical length | Who or where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Falling total demand in a downturn | As long as the downturn | Across the economy, especially construction, manufacturing and retail |
| Frictional | Time spent moving between jobs | Short | School leavers, graduates, people changing jobs |
| Seasonal | Demand changes with the season | Months | Tourism, farming, winter sports |
| Structural | Long-term decline of an industry, or new technology, leaves skills or locations unmatched to jobs | Long | Regions that relied on one industry |

### Worked example 2: classifying cases

- A seaside café closes from November to March and lays off three staff: **seasonal**.
- Car sales fall during a recession and a dealer cuts jobs: **cyclical**.
- A nurse leaves one hospital and starts at another three weeks later: **frictional**.
- A bank closes branches as customers move online, and cashiers' skills are not wanted elsewhere in the town: **structural**.

**Trap.** Job losses caused by new technology are structural, not cyclical, even if they happen during a recession. Ask: will the jobs come back when demand recovers? If not, it is structural.

## Consequences

### Worked example 3: the cost to a region and the government

Region V has 36,000 unemployed people. Each would earn £24,000 a year in work and pay 20% of that in tax. Each receives £5,000 a year in benefits.

- Lost income in the region = 36,000 × £24,000 = **£864m** a year.
- Lost tax revenue = 20% × £864m = **£172.8m**.
- Benefit spending = 36,000 × £5,000 = **£180m**.
- Total cost to the government = 172.8 + 180 = **£352.8m** a year.

The opportunity cost is what £352.8m could fund instead. The lost £864m of income also means less spending in local shops, so other firms in the region lose sales.

| Group | Consequences |
|---|---|
| Individuals | Lower income and living standards; skills go out of date; loss of confidence and worse health; longer spells make finding work harder |
| Regions | Less local spending, so shops and services close; young people move away; the area becomes less attractive to new firms |
| Government | Higher benefit spending, lower tax revenue, possible pressure to borrow; lost output below what the economy could produce |

## Evaluating causes and consequences

The seriousness depends on:

- **Type and length**: frictional unemployment lasting weeks does little harm; structural unemployment lasting years does a lot.
- **Concentration**: 5% unemployment spread evenly is less damaging than 15% in one region.
- **Who is affected**: long spells early in a career can reduce earnings for years.
- **Benefits and retraining**: support can soften the impact on individuals but adds to government costs.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain why structural unemployment may be more serious than cyclical unemployment.*

"Cyclical unemployment is caused by a fall in demand, so when the economy recovers, firms need workers again and many are rehired. **[cause and why it ends]** Structural unemployment comes from the long-term decline of an industry, so the jobs do not come back. **[contrast]** Workers' skills no longer match available jobs, and they may need retraining or to move area, which takes years. **[why it lasts]** The longer they are out of work, the more their skills and confidence fall, so the costs to the individual and to the government's benefit bill are larger." **[consequence that justifies "more serious"]**

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the population or by the employed rather than the labour force.
- Saying "unemployment fell 0.5%" when the rate fell 0.5 percentage points.
- Counting retired people or students as unemployed.
- Calling technology-driven job losses cyclical.
- Discussing consequences without saying who suffers them.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** types: give the cause **and** an example from the case study.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, state the labour force you used, and give the answer to one decimal place.
- **Analyse** data: quote figures and separate rates from numbers.
- **Evaluate** consequences: cover individuals, regions and government as the question asks, then judge which type or group is most affected and why.
