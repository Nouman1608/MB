This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.10.3 The ionic product of water, Kw** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification states that "water is slightly dissociated", that "Kw is derived from the equilibrium constant for this dissociation", that Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] and that "the value of Kw varies with temperature". Students should be able to "use Kw to calculate the pH of a strong base from its concentration". It is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

The [acids and bases study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/) introduces Kw with one strong-base example. This page shows the derivation step by step, works through bases with one and two hydroxide ions, and handles temperatures other than 25 °C. Condense it with the [3.1.10.3 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-10-3-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.10.3 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-10-3-practice/).

## Deriving Kw

Water ionises very slightly:

```
H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
```

Its equilibrium constant (from [3.1.7.2](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-7-2/)) is

```
Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]
```

So few water molecules ionise that [H₂O] is effectively **constant**. One dm³ of water (about 1000 g) contains 1000 ÷ 18.0 = 55.6 mol, so [H₂O] ≈ 55.6 mol dm⁻³. Combining the two constants:

```
Kc × [H₂O] = [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw
```

**Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]**, units **mol² dm⁻⁶**. At 298 K (25 °C), Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.

To see how slight the ionisation is: Kc = Kw ÷ [H₂O] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 55.6 = 1.80 × 10⁻¹⁶ mol dm⁻³. In pure water at 25 °C, [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, so only about 1 molecule in every 5.6 × 10⁸ is ionised.

## Kw applies to every aqueous solution

Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] holds in **any** aqueous solution at a given temperature, not only in pure water. Adding acid raises [H⁺], and [OH⁻] must fall so the product stays the same.

**Worked example 1: [OH⁻] in an acid.** A solution has pH 2.40 at 25 °C.

```
[H⁺] = 10^(−2.40) = 3.98 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³
[OH⁻] = Kw / [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 3.98 × 10⁻³ = 2.51 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³
```

Even a strongly acidic solution contains a small concentration of OH⁻.

## Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]

In pure water, each ionisation gives one H⁺ and one OH⁻, so [H⁺] = [OH⁻] and

```
[H⁺] = √Kw
```

A solution is **neutral** when [H⁺] = [OH⁻], **acidic** when [H⁺] > [OH⁻] and **alkaline** when [H⁺] < [OH⁻]. pH 7 is neutral **only at 25 °C**.

## How Kw varies with temperature

Values of Kw (mol² dm⁻⁶):

| Temperature / °C | 10 | 25 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kw | 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁵ | 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ | 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴ |
| pH of pure water | 7.27 | 7.00 | 6.77 |

**Deducing the sign of ΔH.** Kw increases with temperature, so the equilibrium H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ moves to the right on heating. By Le Chatelier's principle, heating favours the endothermic direction, so **the ionisation of water is endothermic**. The reverse reaction, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, is the exothermic neutralisation you met in energetics.

**Worked example 2: pure water at 40 °C.**

```
[H⁺] = √(2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.71 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³
pH = −log₁₀(1.71 × 10⁻⁷) = 6.77
```

The pH is below 7, yet the water is **neutral**, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. At 10 °C, the same method gives pH 7.27, and the water is still neutral.

## pH of a strong base

A strong base is completely dissociated, so [OH⁻] comes straight from its concentration. Kw then gives [H⁺]:

1. [OH⁻] = concentration × number of OH⁻ per formula unit.
2. [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻].
3. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

**Worked example 3: one OH⁻ per formula unit.** 0.0320 mol dm⁻³ KOH at 25 °C.

```
[OH⁻] = 0.0320 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0320 = 3.13 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³
pH = 12.51
```

**Worked example 4: two OH⁻ per formula unit.** 0.0125 mol dm⁻³ Sr(OH)₂ at 25 °C.

```
[OH⁻] = 2 × 0.0125 = 0.0250 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0250 = 4.00 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³
pH = 12.40
```

**Worked example 5: from a mass.** 0.600 g of NaOH (Mr = 40.0) is dissolved and made up to 250 cm³ at 25 °C.

```
n(NaOH) = 0.600 / 40.0 = 0.0150 mol;  [OH⁻] = 0.0150 / 0.250 = 0.0600 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0600 = 1.67 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³;  pH = 12.78
```

**Worked example 6: working backwards from pH.** A KOH solution has pH 11.40 at 25 °C. Find its concentration.

```
[H⁺] = 10^(−11.40) = 3.98 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³
[OH⁻] = Kw / [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 3.98 × 10⁻¹² = 2.51 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ = [KOH]
```

For Ba(OH)₂ with the same pH, the concentration would be half this, 1.26 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³.

## Strong bases at other temperatures

The concentration of OH⁻ does not change with temperature, but Kw does, so the **pH of a strong base depends on temperature**.

**Worked example 7.** 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 40 °C (Kw = 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶).

```
[H⁺] = 2.92 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0100 = 2.92 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³
pH = 11.53
```

At 25 °C the same solution has pH 12.00. The solution is just as alkaline in terms of [OH⁻], but its pH is lower because Kw is larger. Always use the Kw value for the stated temperature.

## Dilution and mixing with bases

- Diluting a strong base **tenfold** lowers [OH⁻] tenfold, raises [H⁺] tenfold and so **lowers the pH by 1**.
- When a strong base is mixed with a strong acid and the base is in excess, find the leftover moles of OH⁻, divide by the **total** volume, then use Kw. For example, 30.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH with 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl leaves 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol OH⁻ in 50.0 cm³: [OH⁻] = 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ and pH = 12.30 at 25 °C.

## Common mistakes

- Including [H₂O] in the expression for Kw.
- Giving Kw units of mol dm⁻³ instead of mol² dm⁻⁶.
- Forgetting to double [OH⁻] for Ba(OH)₂ or Sr(OH)₂.
- Taking −log₁₀[OH⁻] and calling it the pH.
- Using 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at a temperature other than 25 °C.
- Saying pure water above 25 °C is acidic because its pH is below 7.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Derive** or **explain** Kw: write Kc, state that [H₂O] is effectively constant because so little water ionises, and combine the constants.
- **Calculate** the pH of a strong base: [OH⁻] → [H⁺] = Kw/[OH⁻] → pH, showing each step.
- **Deduce** whether ionisation is endothermic: Kw rises with temperature → equilibrium moves right on heating → endothermic.
- **Explain** whether water at a given pH is neutral: compare [H⁺] and [OH⁻], not the pH with 7.
