This guide covers **Paper 4 Language Topics**, the second of the two A Level-only papers in Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093), sitting alongside Paper 3 Language Analysis to complete the full A Level.

## Where this fits in 9093

Paper 4 asks candidates to discuss two broad, discursive fields — the global spread of English, and the relationship between language and personal or social identity — using unseen stimulus material as the starting point for each discussion. Unlike Paper 3, which tests defined bodies of factual knowledge, Paper 4 rewards genuine wider reading and the ability to bring outside knowledge to bear on unfamiliar texts.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (9093) — PAPER 4 LANGUAGE TOPICS**

The syllabus does not break its subject content into numbered subsections like 4.1 or 4.2; the two
groupings below are an editorial summary of the named content for each section, not syllabus numbering.

- **Section A: English in the World** — the history of English's spread as a global language, often organised using **Kachru's Three Circles model** (the Inner Circle of countries where English is a first language, the Outer Circle of countries where it holds official or institutional status as a second language, and the Expanding Circle of countries where it is learned as a foreign language); the development of standard and nonstandard forms; varieties of English used by first-language speakers outside the UK; **creolisation** (the process by which a pidgin — a simplified contact language with no native speakers — develops into a creole, a fully-fledged first language of a speech community, as English-lexicon creoles did through historical contact and colonisation); hybrid varieties that combine English with other languages (such as Hinglish, Singlish and Spanglish); and the ethical dimensions of English's global spread, including cultural imperialism and equality of access to language use.
- **Section B: Language and the Self** — three linked strands. First, **innateness and learning**: how far language is an innate human capacity versus a learned behaviour, covering behaviourism, innatism/nativism and empiricism. Second, **language and thought**: how far the language a person speaks shapes or limits how they think, covering linguistic relativity and determinism (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis), universalism (the view that thought is independent of and prior to language), and the language of thought hypothesis. Third, **language and social identity**: how language reflects and shapes individual and social identity; the distinction between idiolect (an individual's distinctive speech pattern) and sociolect (a variety of language shared within a social group, such as a class, occupation or age group, as distinct from a regionally defined dialect), including the idea of a **sociolect continuum** — social varieties shading into one another along a spectrum (for example between a broad, informal in-group style and a more standard, formal one) rather than falling into wholly discrete, sharply bounded categories; language and gender, ethnicity, age, occupation and social group; the language of inclusion and exclusion, slang and jargon; and accent and speech sounds as markers of identity.

Total mark for the paper: 50, split evenly between the two sections. Each question presents unseen stimulus material (typically a news article, blog extract, or set of online comments) and asks candidates to discuss the issues it raises, drawing on wider study of the topic as well as the material itself. No dictionaries allowed.

## How to approach Section A: English in the World

Because Section A questions are built around unseen stimulus texts, the skill being tested is not simply reciting facts about global English but engaging with what a specific text says and linking it outward to broader knowledge. Build a working vocabulary of key terms and examples: **Kachru's Three Circles** (Inner, Outer, Expanding) as a way of framing where and how a variety developed; "zero translation" (using an English word or acronym without translating it, common in contexts like China's use of "WiFi" or "CEO"); **creolisation** as the process by which contact between English and other languages produced new first-language varieties; hybrid varieties like Hinglish, Singlish and Spanglish, the standard/nonstandard distinction, and the ethical debate over whether the global spread of English represents opportunity (wider communication, economic access) or a form of cultural imperialism that displaces local languages. A strong answer references specific details in the given text before widening out to this broader knowledge, rather than ignoring the text and writing a prepared essay regardless of what it actually says.

## How to approach Section B: Language and the Self

A Section B stimulus can draw on any of the three strands, so prepare all three rather than only the identity strand. For **innateness and learning**, know the competing positions: **behaviourism** explains language as learned through imitation and reinforcement; **innatism/nativism** (associated with Chomsky) argues for an innate language acquisition device, evidenced by children producing rule-governed forms ("goed", "sheeps") they cannot have imitated; **empiricism** holds that language is learned primarily from environmental input rather than an innate structure. For **language and thought**, know **linguistic relativity and determinism** (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis: the language you speak shapes, or in its strong form determines, how you think), **universalism** (thought is independent of and precedes language), and the **language of thought hypothesis** (there is an underlying, language-independent "mentalese" that language merely expresses). For **language and social identity**, keep the idiolect/sociolect distinction sharp: idiolect is what makes one individual's speech distinctive, while sociolect is shared within a group defined by class, occupation, age or another social category — a stimulus text about a youth variety like Multicultural London English (MLE) is evidence of sociolect, not idiolect, even though it may be illustrated through one speaker's words. Build a working knowledge of the named influences on identity — gender, ethnicity, age, occupation, region and social group — along with at least one concrete named theory or example for each (for gender, theories such as the deficit, dominance, difference and diversity approaches are commonly cited). As with Section A, ground every point in the specific stimulus text first, then widen out to named theories or examples from your broader study.

## Worked example: responding to a Section A-style stimulus

A stimulus text describes English loanwords appearing in Chinese media without translation, alongside a set of online comments debating whether this damages Chinese language and culture or simply reflects normal linguistic exchange. A strong response first identifies the specific tension the text presents (the "purity of language" argument against normal linguistic borrowing), quotes or closely references a specific detail from the text or comments, and then widens out — noting, for example, that English itself has absorbed vast numbers of loanwords from other languages throughout its history, which complicates any claim that borrowing uniquely threatens a language's integrity. The response should conclude with a considered position on the specific issue the text raises, not a generic essay about "English becoming a global language" that could have been written without reading the stimulus at all.

## Worked example: responding to a Section B-style stimulus on identity

A stimulus text describes the rise of a youth sociolect such as Multicultural London English, noting its origins in inner-city, multicultural communities and its spread beyond its city of origin. A strong response identifies this as sociolect (not idiolect, since it is shared across a social group, not unique to one speaker), links the described features (loanwords from other languages, distinctive grammar or vocabulary) to the specific social identity the text associates them with, and widens out to named concepts from wider study — for example, the idea that a sociolect can signal group belonging and be adopted as "cool" and prestigious by speakers outside its original community, spreading its features more widely over time.

## Worked example: responding to a Section B-style stimulus on thought or acquisition

A stimulus text reports a study claiming that speakers of languages with no dedicated future tense save more money, and quotes online comments debating whether language really shapes behaviour this way. A strong response identifies this as a language-and-thought question, references the specific claim in the text, and widens out to named positions — linguistic relativity/determinism as the position the study seems to support, set against universalism's objection that the correlation could reflect cultural factors rather than language shaping thought, and the language of thought hypothesis's challenge that underlying cognition may be language-independent regardless of grammar. A stimulus on how a child began combining words, or on a viral clip of a toddler over-regularising a verb, calls instead on the innateness-and-learning strand: identify the specific behaviour described, then weigh behaviourist (imitation/reinforcement), nativist (innate rule-forming capacity) and empiricist (input-driven) explanations against it.

## Common mistakes

Writing a prepared, generic essay on the topic without engaging with the specific stimulus text given. Confusing idiolect (individual) with sociolect (shared, group-level). Naming an influence on identity (gender, ethnicity, region) without a concrete named example or theory to support it. Treating the global spread of English as straightforwardly positive or negative rather than engaging with the genuine ethical tension the syllabus expects. Preparing only the identity strand of Section B and being caught out by a stimulus on innateness/learning or language and thought instead. Running out of time on one section because the other was allowed to overrun.

## Quick revision checklist

- Ground every response in specific detail from the given stimulus text before widening out to broader knowledge.
- Keep the idiolect/sociolect distinction precise.
- Build a bank of named examples and theories for both sections -- for Section B, across all three strands (innateness/learning, language and thought, and social identity), not identity alone -- ready to deploy against unfamiliar stimulus material.
- Engage genuinely with the ethical tension in English's global spread, rather than taking only one side.
- Divide time evenly between the two compulsory, equally-weighted sections.
