This guide covers **Paper 4 Language Topics**, the second of the two A Level-only papers in Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093), sitting alongside Paper 3 Language Analysis to complete the full A Level.

## Where this fits in 9093

Paper 4 asks candidates to discuss two broad, discursive fields — the global spread of English, and the relationship between language and personal or social identity — using unseen stimulus material as the starting point for each discussion. Unlike Paper 3, which tests defined bodies of factual knowledge, Paper 4 rewards genuine wider reading and the ability to bring outside knowledge to bear on unfamiliar texts.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (9093) — PAPER 4 LANGUAGE TOPICS**

- **4.1 Section A: English in the World** — the history of English's spread as a global language; the development of standard and nonstandard forms; varieties of English used by first-language speakers outside the UK; hybrid varieties that combine English with other languages (such as Hinglish, Singlish and Spanglish); and the ethical dimensions of English's global spread, including cultural imperialism and equality of access to language use.
- **4.2 Section B: Language and the Self** — how language reflects and shapes individual and social identity; the distinction between idiolect (an individual's distinctive speech pattern) and sociolect (a style of speech shared within a region or social group); language and gender, ethnicity, age, occupation and social group; the language of inclusion and exclusion, slang and jargon; and accent and speech sounds as markers of identity.

Total mark for the paper: 50, split evenly between the two sections. Each question presents unseen stimulus material (typically a news article, blog extract, or set of online comments) and asks candidates to discuss the issues it raises, drawing on wider study of the topic as well as the material itself. No dictionaries allowed.

## How to approach Section A: English in the World

Because Section A questions are built around unseen stimulus texts, the skill being tested is not simply reciting facts about global English but engaging with what a specific text says and linking it outward to broader knowledge. Build a working vocabulary of key terms and examples: "zero translation" (using an English word or acronym without translating it, common in contexts like China's use of "WiFi" or "CEO"), hybrid varieties like Hinglish, Singlish and Spanglish, the standard/nonstandard distinction, and the ethical debate over whether the global spread of English represents opportunity (wider communication, economic access) or a form of cultural imperialism that displaces local languages. A strong answer references specific details in the given text before widening out to this broader knowledge, rather than ignoring the text and writing a prepared essay regardless of what it actually says.

## How to approach Section B: Language and the Self

Keep the idiolect/sociolect distinction sharp: idiolect is what makes one individual's speech distinctive, while sociolect is shared within a group defined by region, class, age or another social category — a single stimulus text about, for example, a youth dialect like Multicultural London English (MLE) is really evidence of sociolect, even though it may be illustrated through one speaker's words. Build a working knowledge of the named influences on identity that this section covers — gender, ethnicity, age, occupation, region and social group — along with at least one concrete named theory or example for each (for gender, theories such as the deficit, dominance, difference and diversity approaches are commonly cited). As with Section A, ground every point in the specific stimulus text first, then widen out to named theories or examples from your broader study.

## Worked example: responding to a Section A-style stimulus

A stimulus text describes English loanwords appearing in Chinese media without translation, alongside a set of online comments debating whether this damages Chinese language and culture or simply reflects normal linguistic exchange. A strong response first identifies the specific tension the text presents (the "purity of language" argument against normal linguistic borrowing), quotes or closely references a specific detail from the text or comments, and then widens out — noting, for example, that English itself has absorbed vast numbers of loanwords from other languages throughout its history, which complicates any claim that borrowing uniquely threatens a language's integrity. The response should conclude with a considered position on the specific issue the text raises, not a generic essay about "English becoming a global language" that could have been written without reading the stimulus at all.

## Worked example: responding to a Section B-style stimulus

A stimulus text describes the rise of a youth sociolect such as Multicultural London English, noting its origins in inner-city, multicultural communities and its spread beyond its city of origin. A strong response identifies this as sociolect (not idiolect, since it is shared across a social group, not unique to one speaker), links the described features (loanwords from other languages, distinctive grammar or vocabulary) to the specific social identity the text associates them with, and widens out to named concepts from wider study — for example, the idea that a sociolect can signal group belonging and be adopted as "cool" and prestigious by speakers outside its original community, spreading its features more widely over time.

## Common mistakes

Writing a prepared, generic essay on the topic without engaging with the specific stimulus text given. Confusing idiolect (individual) with sociolect (shared, group-level). Naming an influence on identity (gender, ethnicity, region) without a concrete named example or theory to support it. Treating the global spread of English as straightforwardly positive or negative rather than engaging with the genuine ethical tension the syllabus expects. Running out of time on one section because the other was allowed to overrun.

## Quick revision checklist

- Ground every response in specific detail from the given stimulus text before widening out to broader knowledge.
- Keep the idiolect/sociolect distinction precise.
- Build a bank of named examples and theories for both sections, ready to deploy against unfamiliar stimulus material.
- Engage genuinely with the ethical tension in English's global spread, rather than taking only one side.
- Divide time evenly between the two compulsory, equally-weighted sections.
