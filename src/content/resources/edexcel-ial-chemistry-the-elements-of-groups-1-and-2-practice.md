**Use these values:** molar masses (g mol⁻¹) CO₂ 44.0, MgCO₃ 84.3, HCl 36.5, Ca(OH)₂ 74.1,
H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O 126.0. At room temperature and pressure, one mole of gas occupies 24.0 dm³.

## Questions

**1.** Explain why the first ionisation energy of strontium is lower than that of calcium. **[3]**

**2.** Reactions of Group 1 metals.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction of lithium with chlorine. **[1]**
**(b)** Sodium is burnt in excess oxygen. Write an equation for the main product and give the
oxidation number of oxygen in it. **[2]**
**(c)** A small piece of potassium is dropped into water. Give two observations and write an equation
with state symbols. **[3]**

**3.** Cold water attacks magnesium only very slowly, but heated magnesium reacts readily with steam.

**(a)** Give the equation for magnesium heated in steam, and one thing you would see. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why barium reacts much faster than magnesium with cold water. **[2]**

**4.** Oxides and hydroxides.

**(a)** Strontium oxide is added to water containing universal indicator. Write an equation and state
the final colour of the indicator. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an ionic equation for solid magnesium hydroxide reacting with dilute nitric acid. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why lumps of calcium oxide stop reacting with dilute sulfuric acid before dissolving.
**[1]**

**5.** You are given two unlabelled colourless solutions: magnesium chloride and barium chloride. Name
one reagent that distinguishes them and give the result for each. **[3]**

**6.** Thermal decomposition.

**(a)** Write equations for the thermal decomposition of sodium nitrate and of calcium nitrate. **[2]**
**(b)** Give one observation that would differ between the two reactions in (a). **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why lithium carbonate decomposes on heating with a Bunsen burner but sodium carbonate
does not. **[3]**

**7.** A 2.50 g sample of impure magnesium carbonate is heated to constant mass. The mass falls by
1.10 g. The impurity does not decompose.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage by mass of magnesium carbonate in the sample. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the sample is heated to constant mass. **[1]**

**8.** Flame tests.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of electrons, how a compound produces a flame colour. **[3]**
**(b)** An unknown metal chloride gives a pale green flame. Identify the metal ion. **[1]**
**(c)** Why is the wire first dipped in concentrated hydrochloric acid and heated? **[1]**

**9.** Two white solids, A and B, are tested.

- A gives a brick-red flame. It fizzes with dilute hydrochloric acid, giving a gas that turns
  limewater milky.
- B gives no flame colour. Warmed with sodium hydroxide solution, it gives a gas that turns damp red
  litmus blue. A solution of B gives a white precipitate with dilute hydrochloric acid and barium
  chloride solution.

**(a)** Identify A and B. **[2]**
**(b)** Write ionic equations for: A with the acid; B with hydroxide ions; the formation of the white
precipitate. Write a full equation for the limewater reaction. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why hydrochloric acid is added before the barium chloride. **[1]**

**10.** 10.0 cm³ of concentrated hydrochloric acid is diluted to 250 cm³ with distilled water. The
diluted acid is run from a burette into 25.0 cm³ portions of 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ sodium carbonate solution
with methyl orange indicator. Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + CO₂ + H₂O. Titres (cm³): rough 23.40; then
22.90, 22.80, 22.85.

**(a)** State the indicator colour change you would see at the end point. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean titre. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the concentration of the diluted acid. **[3]**
**(d)** Calculate the concentration of the concentrated acid in mol dm⁻³ and in g dm⁻³. **[2]**

**11.** 1.58 g of ethanedioic acid crystals, H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O, is used to make 250 cm³ of standard
solution. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean titre of 24.10 cm³ of sodium hydroxide, with phenolphthalein.
H₂C₂O₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂C₂O₄ + 2H₂O. Use these uncertainties: balance ±0.005 g per reading (two readings),
burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading, pipette ±0.06 cm³, volumetric flask ±0.3 cm³.

**(a)** Describe how the standard solution is prepared from the weighed solid. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide. **[3]**
**(c)** Estimate the overall percentage uncertainty and the absolute uncertainty in your answer to
(b). **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest one change that would reduce the largest uncertainty. **[1]**

**12.** A 25.0 cm³ portion of filtered, saturated calcium hydroxide solution is neutralised by
21.60 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Calculate the solubility of calcium hydroxide in mol dm⁻³ and in g dm⁻³. **[3]**
**(b)** Predict, with a reason, how the titre would change if saturated magnesium hydroxide solution
were used instead. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Strontium has an extra occupied shell, so its outermost electrons are more distant from the
nucleus [1]. Inner electrons screen them more [1]. Although the nuclear charge is larger, the net
attraction falls, so removing an electron takes less energy [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Bigger atom" alone is often not enough; name distance, shielding and the
weaker attraction.

**2. (a)** **2Li + Cl₂ → 2LiCl** [1]
**(b)** **2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂** [1]; oxygen **−1** [1]
**(c)** Any two of: floats, fizzes, moves about, burns with a lilac flame [1][1].
**2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming hydrogen says what forms, not what you see; "fizzing" or "bubbles" is
the observation that gains credit.

**3. (a)** **Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)** [1]; bright white glow or white solid forms [1]
**(b)** Barium's two outer electrons are more distant from the nucleus and better screened, so they
are lost more easily [1]; barium is therefore the stronger reducing agent and reacts faster [1].
*Examiner insight:* Giving Mg(OH)₂ as the product with steam usually costs the equation mark.

**4. (a)** **SrO + H₂O → Sr(OH)₂** [1]; **blue or purple** (alkaline) [1]
**(b)** **Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2H₂O(l)** [1]
**(c)** Poorly soluble calcium sulfate coats the solid, so the acid cannot reach it [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b), keep the solid as Mg(OH)₂(s); OH⁻(aq) implies it had dissolved.

**5.** Add dilute sulfuric acid (or sodium sulfate solution) [1]. Barium chloride: white precipitate
(BaSO₄) [1]. Magnesium chloride: no visible change, as MgSO₄ is soluble [1].
*Examiner insight:* Describe what you would see for both solutions; "no visible change" is safer
than "no reaction".

**6. (a)** **2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂** [1]; **2Ca(NO₃)₂ → 2CaO + 4NO₂ + O₂** [1]
**(b)** Brown gas is seen only with calcium nitrate [1].
**(c)** Li⁺ is smaller than Na⁺ (same charge), so it has a higher charge density [1]. It polarises the
carbonate ion's electron cloud more [1], weakening its C–O bonds so it breaks down at a lower
temperature [1].
*Examiner insight:* Referring to "lithium atoms" rather than ions usually loses the first mark in (c).

**7. (a)** n(CO₂) = 1.10 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]. Mass MgCO₃ = 0.0250 × 84.3 = 2.11 g [1].
Percentage = 2.11 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **84.3%** [1]
**(b)** To make sure all the magnesium carbonate has decomposed [1].
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 1.10 by 2.50 gives the mass lost, not the purity; convert CO₂ back to
MgCO₃ first.

**8. (a)** Heat energy promotes electrons to higher energy levels [1]. They fall back to lower levels,
releasing energy as light [1]. The energy gaps are fixed for each element, so specific frequencies
(colours) are emitted [1].
**(b)** **Ba²⁺** [1]
**(c)** To remove traces of other compounds that would add their own colour [1].
*Examiner insight:* The energy comes from the flame; light is given out as electrons return, not
absorbed.

**9. (a)** A: **calcium carbonate, CaCO₃** [1]. B: **ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄** [1]
**(b)** CaCO₃(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) [1].
NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + NH₃(g) [1].
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) [1].
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) [1]
**(c)** Carbonate ions would otherwise react with Ba²⁺ to form white BaCO₃, a false positive [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each identification needs both ions; "a calcium compound" alone is incomplete.

**10. (a)** **Yellow to orange** [1]
**(b)** Concordant titres 22.90, 22.80, 22.85; mean = **22.85 cm³** [1]
**(c)** n(Na₂CO₃) = 0.0800 × 0.0250 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. n(HCl) = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1].
c = 4.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02285 = **0.175 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(d)** Dilution factor 250 ÷ 10.0 = 25, so c = 0.17505 × 25 = **4.38 mol dm⁻³** [1];
4.376 × 36.5 = **160 g dm⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Missing the 1 : 2 ratio or the dilution factor are the usual slips; showing each
step keeps method credit available.

**11. (a)** Weigh the crystals by difference, then dissolve them in a little distilled water in a
beaker [1]. Pour into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, washing the beaker and stirring rod into it [1].
Add water until the bottom of the meniscus touches the graduation mark, then stopper and invert [1].
**(b)** n(acid) = 1.58 ÷ 126.0 = 0.01254 mol, so 1.254 × 10⁻³ mol in 25.0 cm³ [1].
n(NaOH) = 2 × 1.254 × 10⁻³ = 2.508 × 10⁻³ mol [1].
c = 2.508 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02410 = **0.104 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** Mass 0.63% + titre 0.41% + pipette 0.24% + flask 0.12% = **1.4%** [1];
1.4% of 0.104 = **±0.0015 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(d)** Weigh a larger mass, or use a three-decimal-place balance [1].
*Examiner insight:* A titre uses two readings (±0.10 cm³), and the overall value adds percentages,
not absolute uncertainties.

**12. (a)** n(HCl) = 0.0500 × 0.02160 = 1.08 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. n(Ca(OH)₂) = 5.40 × 10⁻⁴ mol; c =
5.40 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0216 mol dm⁻³** [1]. × 74.1 = **1.60 g dm⁻³** [1]
**(b)** Much smaller titre [1], because magnesium hydroxide is far less soluble [1].
*Examiner insight:* The 2 : 1 ratio between HCl and Ca(OH)₂ is easy to miss; write the equation
before you calculate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the nuclear charge as the reason for a fall in ionisation energy.
- Writing Na₂O for sodium in excess oxygen, or K₂O for potassium.
- Treating Group 1 and Group 2 nitrates as if they decompose the same way.
- Saying the cation "attracts" the anion, instead of polarising its electron cloud.
- Averaging non-concordant titres or the rough titre.
- Forgetting to scale up for a dilution or for titrating 25.0 cm³ of a 250 cm³ solution.
