These notes condense **3.2.2.2 Human resource data** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 2). For a full two-site comparison and trend example, see the [human resource data study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-human-resource-data-9725/). Practise with the [human resource data practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-human-resource-data-9725-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Recall in one minute

Calculate and interpret: labour turnover; employee retention rates (including the % still employed one year after recruitment); labour productivity; employee costs as % of turnover; labour costs per unit; sales and profit per employee. Know that measures such as productivity vary in different contexts.

## Formulas (9725 appendix)

| Measure | Formula | Answer in |
|---|---|---|
| Labour turnover | leavers in a year ÷ average total employees × 100 | % |
| Employee retention rate | employees at end of period ÷ average employees at start of period × 100 | % |
| % still employed one year after recruitment | recruits still employed after one year ÷ number recruited × 100 | % |
| Labour productivity | output in a period ÷ average employees in the period | units per employee |
| Employee costs as % of turnover | total employee costs ÷ total revenue × 100 | % |
| Labour costs per unit | total labour costs ÷ units produced | $ per unit |
| Sales per employee | total sales revenue ÷ average employees | $ |
| Profit per employee | total profit ÷ average employees | $ |

**Average employees = (start + end) ÷ 2** unless the question gives the average.

The appendix retention rate counts new joiners, so it can exceed 100% in a growing business. The one-year measure tracks only new recruits. State which one you are using.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover | The proportion of the workforce leaving in a year |
| Employee retention | The extent to which the business keeps its employees |
| Labour productivity | Output per employee |
| Employee costs | Total spending on employees: wages, salaries and related costs |
| Turnover (revenue) | Total income from sales in the period |
| Labour cost per unit | The labour cost carried by each unit produced |
| Sales per employee | Revenue generated per employee |
| Profit per employee | Profit generated per employee |

## Interpretation rules

- **Turnover high** → recruitment and training costs, lost experience, possible service problems; some turnover brings new ideas.
- **Turnover rising, productivity falling** → experienced staff leaving; newer staff less productive.
- **Low one-year retention** → look at selection and induction.
- **Employee costs % rising** → pay rising faster than revenue; margins under pressure.
- **Higher pay can pay for itself** if it lowers turnover and raises productivity enough: check labour cost per unit.
- **Productivity up** may come from machinery, not effort.

## Context warnings

- Capital-intensive operations show higher productivity than labour-intensive ones.
- Services have higher employee costs as % of turnover than retailers of goods.
- Sectors with many part-time or seasonal staff show higher turnover.
- Compare like with like: same business over time, similar sites, same-sector rivals.

## Mini comparison: two shops

| | Shop X | Shop Y |
|---|---|---|
| Average employees | 20 | 25 |
| Leavers in year | 6 | 3 |
| Revenue | $900,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Employee costs | $270,000 | $350,000 |

```
Labour turnover      X: 6 ÷ 20 × 100 = 30%      Y: 3 ÷ 25 × 100 = 12%
Sales per employee   X: 900,000 ÷ 20 = $45,000  Y: 1,000,000 ÷ 25 = $40,000
Employee costs %     X: 270 ÷ 900 × 100 = 30%   Y: 350 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 35%
```

Reading: X earns more per employee and spends a smaller share of revenue on staff, but loses almost a third of its staff a year; Y pays more and keeps people. Before calling X "better", ask whether its turnover is harming service and whether Y's higher pay buys loyalty worth having.

## Exam traps

- Using end headcount instead of the average.
- Dividing leavers by start or end headcount.
- Missing × 100 on percentages.
- Mixing up employee costs % of turnover with labour cost per unit.
- Interpreting a figure without a comparison or a cause.
- Rounding too early: keep full figures until the final answer.

## Self-test

A firm has 50 employees at the start of the year and 70 at the end. 9 employees left during the year. Output was 21,000 units, revenue $1,680,000, employee costs $420,000 and profit $210,000. Last year it recruited 25 people, of whom 20 were still employed a year later.

1. Calculate the average number of employees.
2. Calculate labour turnover.
3. Calculate labour productivity.
4. Calculate employee costs as a percentage of turnover.
5. Calculate labour cost per unit.
6. Calculate sales per employee.
7. Calculate profit per employee.
8. Calculate the percentage of recruits still employed one year after recruitment.
9. Calculate the employee retention rate using the appendix formula. Why is it above 100%?
10. Give one reason labour productivity may be lower in a craft workshop than a factory.
11. Give one cost to a business of high labour turnover.
12. Labour productivity rose by 10% but labour cost per unit also rose. Suggest why.

### Answers

1. (50 + 70) ÷ 2 = **60**.
2. 9 ÷ 60 × 100 = **15%**.
3. 21,000 ÷ 60 = **350 units per employee**.
4. 420,000 ÷ 1,680,000 × 100 = **25%**.
5. 420,000 ÷ 21,000 = **$20** per unit.
6. 1,680,000 ÷ 60 = **$28,000**.
7. 210,000 ÷ 60 = **$3,500**.
8. 20 ÷ 25 × 100 = **80%**.
9. 70 ÷ 50 × 100 = **140%**; it counts new joiners in the end figure, and the firm grew.
10. Work is done by hand, so each employee produces fewer units (the measure depends on context).
11. Recruitment and training costs, or lost experience (any one).
12. Pay per employee rose by more than 10%, so labour costs grew faster than output.
