## Core recall in eight lines

1. Ozone is a storage in dynamic equilibrium: UV makes it (from O₂) and UV breaks it, and the absorbed energy heats the stratosphere.
2. Most ozone lies roughly 15–35 km up, at only a few parts per million.
3. The layer absorbs most UV-B and all UV-C; UV-A mostly passes through.
4. Human chlorine and bromine add a faster output, so the storage settles at a lower level.
5. A chlorine atom is regenerated each cycle, so it is a catalyst.
6. Damage depends on lifetime and on the halogen carried; bromine is far more destructive per atom.
7. Antarctic losses peak in September–October because of the vortex, polar stratospheric clouds and returning sunlight.
8. Prevention was the only strategy: ODS cannot be cleaned out of the stratosphere.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dynamic equilibrium | Inputs and outputs balance, so the storage stays steady while molecules are constantly made and destroyed |
| Dobson unit (DU) | Column ozone; 1 DU = a 0.01 mm layer at standard temperature and pressure, so 300 DU = 3 mm |
| Ozone hole | Area where column ozone is below 220 DU |
| Ozone-depleting substance (ODS) | Long-lived human-made gas that carries chlorine or bromine to the stratosphere |
| Ozone depletion potential (ODP) | Ozone damage per tonne relative to CFC-11 (ODP 1) |
| ODP tonnes | Mass × ODP; lets different ODS be added together |
| Reservoir compound | Chlorine held in an unreactive form, such as hydrogen chloride or chlorine nitrate |
| Polar vortex | Ring of strong winds that isolates polar stratospheric air in winter |
| Polar stratospheric clouds | Clouds that form at very low temperatures; reactions on them release chlorine from reservoirs |
| Banks | ODS still held in old equipment and foams, which leak over time |
| Precautionary principle | Acting to prevent serious harm before the science is complete |

## Substances to know

| Substance | Former use | Point to remember |
|---|---|---|
| CFCs (CFC-11, CFC-12) | Refrigerants, aerosol propellants, foam blowing | Lifetimes about 50 and 100 years |
| Halons (halon-1211, halon-1301) | Fire extinguishers | Contain bromine; high ODP (3 and 10) |
| Methyl bromide | Soil fumigant, pesticide | Bromine source |
| Carbon tetrachloride | Solvent, chemical feedstock | Chlorine source |
| HCFCs (HCFC-22) | Transitional replacement for CFCs | Low ODP (0.055), being phased out |
| HFCs | Replacement for HCFCs | No ozone damage but strong greenhouse gases; Kigali Amendment |
| Nitrous oxide | Fertiliser use, industry | Depletes ozone; not covered by the Montreal Protocol |

## The Antarctic spring sequence

Vortex isolates the air → temperatures fall → polar stratospheric clouds form → reservoirs converted to chlorine molecules in the dark → sunlight returns (September–October) → UV releases chlorine atoms → rapid catalytic loss → vortex breaks up, ozone-rich air mixes in.

**Arctic contrast:** warmer stratosphere and a weaker, less stable vortex, so fewer clouds and smaller, more variable losses.

## Discovery and agreements timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1974 | Molina and Rowland propose CFCs destroy ozone |
| 1985 | British Antarctic Survey publishes Antarctic spring losses; Vienna Convention |
| 1987 | Montreal Protocol |
| 1990 | London Amendment sets up the Multilateral Fund |
| 1995 | Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Crutzen, Molina and Rowland |
| 2016 | Kigali Amendment adds an HFC phase-down |
| 2018 | Slowed fall in CFC-11 reveals illegal production |
| About 2060s | Projected Antarctic recovery to 1980 levels |

## Evaluation points on the Montreal Protocol

**Why it worked:** universal ratification; schedules tightened as science improved; Multilateral Fund paid developing countries' switching costs; trade controls with non-parties; few producers and available substitutes; clear human health harm.

**Limits:** substitutes caused new problems (HCFCs still deplete, HFCs warm); depends on monitoring and enforcement; banks keep leaking; nitrous oxide is uncontrolled; recovery takes decades.

**Judgement frame:** successful on its own aim, but helped by conditions that climate agreements lack.

## Calculation methods

```
Mass to ODP tonnes:   mass (t) × ODP
DU to thickness:      DU × 0.01 = mm of pure ozone
Percentage change:    (new − original) ÷ original × 100
Mean rate of change:  (final − initial) ÷ years
Half-lives:           amount left = start × 0.5^(years ÷ half-life)
```

## Exam traps

- "The ozone hole causes global warming." No: it lets in more UV-B, not more infrared trapping.
- "UV-C reaches the ground under the hole." No: it is still absorbed.
- "CFCs react with ozone." Chlorine atoms released by UV do.
- Mixing ODP (versus CFC-11) with GWP (versus CO₂).
- Percentage decrease divided by the final value instead of the starting value.
- Calling HFCs ozone-depleting. They are a climate problem.
- Calling the layer "repaired". It is recovering.
- Leaving out DU or ODP t units.

## Quick self-test

1. State the band of UV whose arrival at the surface increases most as ozone thins.
2. Convert a column of 250 DU into the thickness of pure ozone.
3. Explain why the ozone storage is described as a dynamic equilibrium.
4. Calculate the ODP tonnes of 50 t of halon-1211 (ODP 3).
5. Calculate the ODP tonnes of 400 t of HCFC-22 (ODP 0.055).
6. Give two properties that let CFCs reach the stratosphere.
7. Why do halons cause more damage per tonne than CFC-11?
8. A column falls from 320 DU to 180 DU. Calculate the percentage decrease and state whether it counts as an ozone hole.
9. Give the role of polar stratospheric clouds.
10. Why are Arctic losses usually smaller than Antarctic losses?
11. An ODS has a half-life of 40 years. What fraction of a release remains after 120 years?
12. Give one reason the Montreal Protocol's success is hard to repeat for CO₂.

### Answers

1. UV-B.
2. 250 × 0.01 = **2.5 mm**.
3. Ozone is constantly formed and broken down by UV at matching rates, so the amount stays steady although molecules keep changing.
4. 50 × 3 = **150 ODP t**.
5. 400 × 0.055 = **22 ODP t**.
6. Any two: unreactive; insoluble, so not washed out by rain; long lifetime.
7. They carry bromine, which destroys ozone far more efficiently per atom than chlorine.
8. (180 − 320) ÷ 320 × 100 = **−43.75%**, a decrease of about 43.8%; 180 DU is below 220 DU, so yes.
9. Reactions on their particles convert chlorine reservoir compounds into chlorine molecules, which sunlight splits into reactive atoms in spring.
10. The Arctic stratosphere is warmer and its vortex weaker, so fewer polar stratospheric clouds form.
11. 120 ÷ 40 = 3 half-lives; 0.5³ = **one-eighth (12.5%)**.
12. Any one: CO₂ comes from fossil fuels used across the whole economy; there are millions of sources, not a few producers; low-carbon substitutes require large-scale change; the harm is less direct.
