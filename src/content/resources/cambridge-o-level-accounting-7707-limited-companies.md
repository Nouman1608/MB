This guide goes deeper into section **5.3 Limited companies** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus asks for an understanding of how a limited company can be "a trading, service, manufacturing business or a combination of these", its advantages and disadvantages, "the term 'limited liability'", "the term 'equity'", a capital structure made of "preference share capital, ordinary share capital, general reserve and retained earnings", "the features of ordinary share capital and loan capital (debentures)", share capital that is "issued, called-up, and paid-up", and how to prepare "statements of profit or loss, statements of changes in equity and statements of financial position" with the adjustments from 5.1.

The shared [limited companies study guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-financial-statements-of-limited-companies/) works through a full trial balance. This page concentrates on the parts O Level candidates most often lose marks on: tracing what changes each part of equity, separating finance costs from dividends, the three share capital figures, and following one error through all three statements. For quick recall use the [limited companies revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limited-companies-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [limited companies practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-limited-companies-practice/). ## The company as a separate legal person

A limited company is a separate legal person from the shareholders who own it. It owns its assets, owes its debts and can make contracts in its own name. **Limited liability** means that if the company fails, a shareholder can lose only the amount paid, or still unpaid, on the shares held. Personal savings, homes and cars are not used to pay the company's debts.

A trading company has a cost of sales section built from opening inventory, purchases and closing inventory. A service company, such as a firm of surveyors, earns fees and has no cost of sales, so it moves straight from revenue to expenses. A manufacturing company first prepares a manufacturing account, and its cost of sales uses the factory cost of production instead of purchases. A company doing more than one combines the sections.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Shareholders have limited liability, so investors are more willing to put money in | Setting up costs more and involves more legal formalities |
| More capital can be raised by issuing shares to many people | Financial statements must follow legal requirements and may have to be made available to the public, so there is less privacy |
| The company continues if a shareholder dies or sells shares | Profit is shared among many shareholders, and owners who are not directors have little say in day-to-day decisions |

An "advise" question expects a recommendation tied to the case. Suppose Farah runs a printing business as a sole trader and wants to borrow heavily to buy a large press. A strong answer says: "Farah should form a limited company. The new press needs a large loan, and if the business failed she could lose her house as a sole trader. As a shareholder, her loss would be limited to her shares. The extra legal cost and the need to make accounts available are smaller than this risk." It weighs an advantage against a disadvantage using the facts and gives a decision.

## Equity and what changes each part

**Equity** is the shareholders' interest in the company: total assets less total liabilities. In 7707 it has four parts, and each changes for different reasons.

| Part of equity | Increased by | Decreased by |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary share capital | Issuing more ordinary shares | Not normally reduced while trading |
| Preference share capital | Issuing more preference shares | Not normally reduced while trading |
| General reserve | Transfers from retained earnings | Transfers back to retained earnings |
| Retained earnings | Profit for the year; transfers back from general reserve | Loss for the year; dividends paid; transfers to general reserve |

A transfer to the general reserve moves profit between two parts of equity, so total equity does not change and no cash moves. Dividends reduce retained earnings, not profit, so they never appear in the statement of profit or loss.

## Ordinary shares compared with debentures

Ordinary shareholders own the company, normally have votes and receive a dividend that the directors may raise, lower or not pay. Preference shareholders receive a fixed rate of dividend, paid before any ordinary dividend, and usually have no vote. Debenture holders are lenders. They receive fixed interest whether or not a profit is made, and the debentures are repaid on an agreed date.

The accounting treatment follows from that difference. Debenture interest is a **finance cost** in the statement of profit or loss, deducted from profit from operations to reach profit for the year. Dividends on both classes of share go to the statement of changes in equity. Debentures sit under non-current liabilities until they are due within twelve months.

## Issued, called-up and paid-up share capital

Linwood Glass Ltd issues 150,000 ordinary shares of $2 each. The company asks shareholders for $1.50 a share now and the rest later. Holders of 4,000 shares have not yet paid.

| Figure | Working | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Issued share capital | 150,000 x $2 | $300,000 |
| Called-up share capital | 150,000 x $1.50 | $225,000 |
| Unpaid calls | 4,000 x $1.50 | $6,000 |
| Paid-up share capital | 225,000 - 6,000 | $219,000 |

Issued capital uses the nominal value of every share issued, called-up capital the amount asked for, and paid-up capital the amount received. Give only the figure the question names.

## Worked example: Ashgrove Packaging Ltd

At 1 January 2028 the equity of Ashgrove Packaging Ltd was: ordinary shares of $1, $200,000; 5% preference shares of $1, $50,000; general reserve, $18,000; retained earnings, $41,000. The company also had $60,000 of 8% debentures.

During the year ended 31 December 2028:

- On 1 July, 40,000 ordinary shares of $1 were issued at $1 each, fully paid.
- Profit from operations was $72,500.
- Debenture interest of $2,400 was paid; the second half-year's interest was unpaid at the year end.
- $10,000 was transferred to the general reserve.
- The full preference dividend was paid, and an ordinary dividend of $0.06 a share was paid on 31 December on all ordinary shares then in issue.

**Step 1: profit for the year.** Interest for the year is 8% x $60,000 = $4,800, so $2,400 is accrued. Profit for the year = $72,500 - $4,800 = **$67,700**. The accrued $2,400 is an other payable.

**Step 2: dividends.** Preference dividend = 5% x $50,000 = $2,500. Ordinary shares in issue at 31 December = 240,000, so the ordinary dividend = 240,000 x $0.06 = $14,400.

## Following one change through the statements

Section 5.1 asks for "the effect of a change in an account balance on other account balances", and 5.3 applies the 5.1 list to companies. Suppose Ashgrove's closing inventory was overstated by $3,000. Gross profit, profit from operations and profit for the year are each $3,000 too high. Retained earnings at the year end are $3,000 too high, so total equity is $3,000 too high. On the other side, current assets are $3,000 too high, so the statement still balances.

The same chain works for any adjustment. An unrecorded accrued expense of $900 lowers profit for the year by $900, lowers retained earnings by $900 and adds $900 to other payables.

## Common mistakes

- Putting dividends in the statement of profit or loss, or debenture interest in the statement of changes in equity.
- Charging only the interest paid, instead of the full year's interest on the debentures.
- Working out the ordinary dividend on the shares in issue at the start of the year when the question says the shares in issue at the date of payment.
- Treating a transfer to the general reserve as an expense or as cash.
- Giving issued capital when the question asks for paid-up capital.

## Exam technique

Paper 1 is 40 multiple-choice questions and often tests one link, such as which part of equity a dividend reduces. Paper 2 has five structured questions of 20 marks each, and the syllabus says candidates may have to "complete an incomplete statement of accounts". For limited companies this often means filling in a statement of changes in equity or the equity section.

- **Calculate**: show the working line, such as 240,000 x $0.06, so a slip in arithmetic still earns method marks.
- **Prepare**: use a full heading with the company name, statement name and date, and label every line.
- **Explain**: give the reason and its effect, for example "debenture interest must be paid even in a loss-making year, which reduces profit for the year".
- **Advise**: make a recommendation and support it with facts from the case.

Before leaving a statement of changes in equity, check that the closing totals match the equity section of the statement of financial position.
