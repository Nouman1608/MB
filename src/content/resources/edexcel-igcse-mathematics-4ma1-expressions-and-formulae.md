This guide covers section 2.3, Expressions and formulae, in topic 2 (Equations, formulae and identities) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2. You will learn to write algebra in the accepted way, substitute any kind of number into a formula, build formulae from words and diagrams, and rearrange them.

The topic-level [Expressions, formulae and linear equations study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-expressions-formulae-and-linear-equations/) covers 2.3 and 2.4 together. This page concentrates on 2.3, with different examples and a step-by-step method for each kind of rearrangement. Afterwards, use the [expressions and formulae revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-expressions-and-formulae-revision-notes/) and the [expressions and formulae practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-expressions-and-formulae-practice/).

## What the specification says

| Statement | Students should be taught to | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation 2.3 A | understand that a letter may represent an unknown number or a variable | Both |
| Foundation 2.3 B | use correct notational conventions for algebraic expressions and formulae | Both |
| Foundation 2.3 C | substitute positive and negative integers, decimals and fractions for words and letters in expressions and formulae | Both |
| Foundation 2.3 D | use formulae from mathematics and other real-life contexts expressed initially in words or diagrammatic form and convert to letters and symbols | Both |
| Foundation 2.3 E | derive a formula or expression | Both |
| Foundation 2.3 F | change the subject of a formula where the subject appears once | Both |
| Higher 2.3 A | change the subject where the subject may appear twice or a power of the subject occurs | Higher only |

The specification's own examples include making r the subject of A = πr² and t the subject of v = u + at (Foundation), and cases such as T = 2π√(l/g) for the Higher Tier.

## Notation conventions (2.3 B)

Examiners expect algebra written in a standard way:

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| 3ab (number first, letters usually in alphabetical order) | a × b × 3, ba3 |
| x²y | x × x × y |
| y | 1y |
| −x | −1x |
| n/4 | n ÷ 4 |
| 3(m + n) | (m + n)3 |

Conventional notation also avoids misreading: "ab²" means a × b², and "(ab)²" means a²b².

## Substitution (2.3 C)

Put each value in brackets, especially negatives and fractions, and keep fractions exact until the end.

**Worked example 1.** (a) E = ½mv². Find E when m = 0.4 and v = −6. (b) T = (a − b)/(ab). Find T when a = 1/2 and b = −1/3.

```
(a) E = ½ × 0.4 × (−6)² = 0.2 × 36 = 7.2

(b) a − b = 1/2 − (−1/3) = 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6
    ab = (1/2) × (−1/3) = −1/6
    T = (5/6) ÷ (−1/6) = −5
```

In (b), subtracting a negative fraction becomes an addition. Writing the numerator and denominator separately earns method marks even if the division goes wrong.

## Formulae from words and diagrams (2.3 D and 2.3 E)

To turn words into a formula:

1. Choose a letter for each quantity and say what it stands for, with units.
2. Identify the fixed part and the part that changes with each unit.
3. Write the formula with the subject on the left.

**Worked example 2.** A bike-hire company charges a fixed fee of £8 plus £2.50 for each hour. (Prices invented for this example.) Write a formula for the cost, £C, of hiring a bike for h hours.

```
fixed part = 8;  changing part = 2.5 × h
C = 8 + 2.5h
```

"Derive" (2.3 E) means you build the expression yourself from a description or a diagram, rather than being given it.

**Worked example 3.** A rectangle is x cm wide and (x + 4) cm long. A square of side 2 cm is cut from one corner. Derive expressions for the perimeter P and the area A of the shape that is left.

```
Area:      A = x(x + 4) − 2 × 2 = x² + 4x − 4
Perimeter: cutting a corner square removes two 2 cm edges and adds two 2 cm edges,
           so the perimeter is the same as the rectangle's:
           P = 2x + 2(x + 4) = 4x + 8
```

A sketch with every edge labelled stops you from missing a side. Here, the step that a corner cut does not change the perimeter is the reasoning examiners look for.

## Changing the subject: subject appears once (2.3 F)

Treat the formula like an equation and undo the operations on the new subject in reverse order. Whatever you do to one side, do to the whole of the other side.

**Worked example 4.** Make (a) t the subject of v = u + at (b) b the subject of K = (a − 3b)/4 (c) r the subject of A = πr².

```
(a) v = u + at
    v − u = at
    t = (v − u)/a

(b) K = (a − 3b)/4
    4K = a − 3b
    3b = a − 4K          (add 3b to both sides, subtract 4K from both sides)
    b = (a − 4K)/3

(c) A = πr²
    A/π = r²
    r = √(A/π)
```

In (b), moving the 3b term to the other side keeps it positive, which avoids dividing by −3. In (c), the square root is taken of the whole of A/π; for a length, take the positive root.

## Changing the subject: Higher cases (Higher tier only)

### The subject appears twice

The method is always the same:

1. Clear any fractions by multiplying.
2. Expand any brackets.
3. Collect every term containing the subject on one side and everything else on the other.
4. Factorise the subject out.
5. Divide by the bracket.

**Worked example 5 (Higher).** Make k the subject of 2k + 7 = (3 − k)/w.

```
w(2k + 7) = 3 − k              clear the fraction
2kw + 7w = 3 − k               expand
2kw + k = 3 − 7w               k terms on the left
k(2w + 1) = 3 − 7w             factorise
k = (3 − 7w)/(2w + 1)          divide
```

Without step 4 you cannot finish: an answer such as k = (3 − 7w − k)/(2w) still has k on both sides, so k is not the subject.

### A power or root of the subject

Isolate the power or root first, then undo it last.

**Worked example 6 (Higher).** The period T seconds of a pendulum of length l metres is T = 2π√(l/g). (a) Make l the subject. (b) Find l when T = 2.4 and g = 9.8.

```
(a) T/(2π) = √(l/g)
    T²/(4π²) = l/g             square both sides (the 2π is squared too)
    l = gT²/(4π²)

(b) l = 9.8 × 2.4² ÷ (4π²) = 56.448 ÷ 39.478... = 1.4298...
    l = 1.43 m (3 s.f.)
```

Squaring T/(2π) gives T²/(4π²), not T²/(2π). This is the most common slip in this type of question.

## Common mistakes

- Substituting −6 into v² without brackets and getting −36.
- Writing a formula with no subject, such as "8 + 2.5h" instead of C = 8 + 2.5h.
- Dividing only one term by a number: from 4K = a − 3b, writing b = a − 4K/3.
- Taking the square root of each term separately: √(a² + b²) is not a + b.
- When the subject appears twice, leaving it on both sides of the final answer.
- Forgetting to square a coefficient: (2π)² = 4π².

## Exam technique

- **"Write down a formula"** or **"Write a formula for C in terms of h"**: the answer must start "C =". An expression alone often loses a mark.
- **"Make ... the subject"**: write one operation per line, and finish with the new subject alone on the left.
- **"Show that"** with a derived formula: start from the diagram or words, not from the given answer.
- **"Find the value of"** after a rearrangement: use your rearranged formula, but you can check by substituting back into the original.
- If a part says **"Hence"**, use the formula from the previous part.
