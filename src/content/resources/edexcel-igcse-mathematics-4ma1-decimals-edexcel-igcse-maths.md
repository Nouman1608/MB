## Decimal place value (1.3 A, 1.3 B)

Columns to the right of the decimal point are tenths, hundredths, thousandths and so on; each is one tenth of the column to its left.

| Tens | Units | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths | Ten-thousandths |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | . | 0 | 3 | 5 | 8 |

In 12.0358 the 5 is worth 5 thousandths, 0.005. The 0 in the tenths column is a placeholder: without it the number would be 12.358, a different value.

**Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1000** moves the digits, not the point: 0.47 × 100 = 47 and 3.2 ÷ 1000 = 0.0032. Zeros fill empty places.

## Ordering decimals (1.3 C)

Compare whole-number parts first, then tenths, then hundredths, and so on. Padding with zeros so every number has the same number of decimal places makes the comparison mechanical.

**Worked example 1.** Order 2.07, 2.7, 2.071, 2.0071 and 2.17, smallest first.

```
pad to 4 d.p.: 2.0700  2.7000  2.0710  2.0071  2.1700
compare as whole numbers of ten-thousandths:
20 071 < 20 700 < 20 710 < 21 700 < 27 000
order: 2.0071, 2.07, 2.071, 2.17, 2.7
```

"More digits" does not mean "bigger": 2.0071 has the most digits and is the smallest. With negative decimals, the order flips: −0.405 < −0.4 < −0.35 < −0.04, because −0.405 is furthest left on the number line.

## Calculating with decimals (1.3 A)

A calculator may be used on every 4MA1 paper, but quick written methods let you estimate and check.

**Multiplying.** Multiply as whole numbers, then give the answer as many decimal places as the two numbers have altogether. 0.06 × 0.4: 6 × 4 = 24, and there are 2 + 1 = 3 decimal places, so 0.024.

**Dividing.** Multiply both numbers by the same power of 10 so you divide by a whole number. 7.2 ÷ 0.03 = 720 ÷ 3 = 240. Dividing by a number between 0 and 1 makes the answer bigger, a useful check.

**Using a known fact.** If 37 × 64 = 2368, then 3.7 × 6.4 = 23.68 (two decimal places in total), and 2368 ÷ 0.64 = 236 800 ÷ 64 = 3700.

## Terminating decimals as fractions and percentages (1.3 D, 1.3 E)

A **terminating** decimal stops. Its last digit sits in a known column, so it can be written over 10, 100, 1000, …, which proves that every terminating decimal is a fraction.

**Worked example 2.** Write as fractions in their simplest form and as percentages: (a) 0.84, (b) 0.045, (c) 3.125.

```
(a) 0.84  = 84/100  = 21/25           percentage: 84%
(b) 0.045 = 45/1000 = 9/200           percentage: 4.5%
(c) 3.125 = 3 + 125/1000 = 3 + 1/8 = 3 1/8   percentage: 312.5%
```

The denominator is set by the last decimal place: two places, hundredths, over 100; three places, thousandths, over 1000. To get a percentage, multiply the decimal by 100.

Because the denominator starts as a power of 10, and 10 = 2 × 5, the simplified denominator of a terminating decimal only ever has prime factors 2 and 5. So 21/25 and 9/200 terminate, but a fraction such as 5/6 (denominator contains 3) gives a recurring decimal.

## Recurring decimals into fractions (Higher tier only)

A **recurring** decimal repeats a digit or block forever. Dots go over the first and last digits of the repeating block only: 0.405405405… is written 0.4̇05̇, and 0.8333… is written 0.83̇.

**The method.**

1. Let x equal the decimal, written out with several repeats.
2. Multiply by 10ⁿ, where n is the length of the repeating block, so the repeating parts line up. If there is a non-repeating part, first multiply so it sits before the point.
3. Subtract to remove the repeating tail, then solve for x and simplify.

**Worked example 3.** Write 0.4̇05̇ as a fraction in its simplest form.

```
x       = 0.405405405…
1000x   = 405.405405…
999x    = 405
x       = 405/999 = 15/37        (HCF 27)
```

**Worked example 4.** Prove algebraically that 1.26̇ = 19/15.

```
x      = 1.2666…
10x    = 12.666…
100x   = 126.666…
90x    = 114            (100x − 10x)
x      = 114/90 = 19/15
```

When the repeat starts straight after the point, the shortcut "block over that many nines" works: 0.6̇3̇ = 63/99 = 7/11. A "prove algebraically" or "show that" question needs the full subtraction, not the shortcut.

The same method shows a surprising fact: if x = 0.999…, then 10x = 9.999…, so 9x = 9 and x = 1. So 0.9̇ and 1 are the same number.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking 0.45 > 0.5 because 45 > 5. Pad first: 0.45 < 0.50.
- Placing the point by "lining up" in multiplication: 0.3 × 0.07 is 0.021, not 0.21.
- Writing 0.045 as 45/100. Three decimal places means thousandths.
- Forgetting to simplify: 84/100 must become 21/25.
- In recurring decimals, multiplying by 10 when the block has two digits, so the tails do not cancel.
- Writing dot notation on the wrong digits, or putting a dot on every digit of a long block.

## Exam technique

- **Write down** the value of a digit: give it as a number (0.005) or in words (5 thousandths).
- **Write in order of size**: use the original numbers in your answer, not the padded versions.
- **Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form**: cancel fully; a percentage is not accepted in place of a fraction.
- **Show that** or **Prove algebraically** (Higher): set x equal to the decimal, write two multiples with matching tails, subtract, and finish on exactly the fraction given.
- Check a conversion with your calculator after you have written the method: 15 ÷ 37 should display 0.405405…
