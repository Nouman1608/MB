This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.4.2 Receptors**, in Unit 4, Control. Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only.

A receptor is a cell or nerve ending that detects a change in the environment (a stimulus) and converts it into a nerve impulse. The section uses two examples: the Pacinian corpuscle, to show how a stimulus becomes an electrical signal, and the retina, to show how the arrangement of receptors and their connections explains what we see. ## Receptors as transducers

A receptor works as a **transducer**: it converts energy from a stimulus (pressure, light, chemical energy) into the electrical energy of a nerve impulse. Two features apply to all receptors.

1. **Specificity.** Each type of receptor responds only to a specific stimulus. Pacinian corpuscles respond to pressure, not to light. Rods and cones respond to light, not to pressure.
2. **A generator potential.** Stimulation of a receptor changes the permeability of its membrane to ions, which changes the potential difference across the membrane. This change is called a **generator potential**.

## The Pacinian corpuscle

### Structure

A Pacinian corpuscle is a pressure receptor found deep in the skin, for example in the fingers, and in joints. It consists of the ending of a sensory neurone surrounded by many layers of connective tissue (lamellae), separated by a viscous gel. The membrane of the neurone ending contains **stretch-mediated sodium ion channels**.

### How a stimulus becomes a generator potential

1. At rest, the stretch-mediated sodium ion channels are too narrow for sodium ions to pass through, so they are closed. The inside of the neurone is negative relative to the outside.
2. Pressure on the corpuscle deforms the lamellae. The deformation is passed to the membrane of the neurone ending, which stretches.
3. Stretching widens the sodium ion channels, so they open.
4. Sodium ions diffuse into the neurone ending down their electrochemical gradient.
5. The inside of the neurone becomes less negative. This depolarisation is the **generator potential**.
6. If the generator potential is large enough to reach the **threshold**, voltage-gated channels in the neighbouring region open and an **action potential** is produced and travels along the sensory neurone.
7. If the generator potential does not reach the threshold, no action potential is produced. The response is all-or-nothing at the action potential level.

### Size of stimulus

A stronger stimulus causes more stretching, more sodium channels open, and the generator potential is larger. A larger generator potential produces **more frequent action potentials**, not larger ones, because action potentials are all of the same size. This is how the brain recognises the strength of the stimulus. The relationship levels off when all of the channels are open.

**Worked example 1: threshold and frequency.** Assume a resting potential of -70 mV and a threshold of -55 mV. The generator potential measured at different pressures was: 1 kPa, +4 mV; 2 kPa, +9 mV; 3 kPa, +15 mV; 4 kPa, +21 mV; 5 kPa, +22 mV.

1. The depolarisation needed to reach the threshold is -55 - (-70) = +15 mV.
2. At 1 kPa and 2 kPa the depolarisation (+4 and +9 mV) is below 15 mV, so no action potential is produced.
3. At 3 kPa the depolarisation reaches exactly 15 mV, so the threshold is reached and action potentials begin.
4. At 4 kPa the generator potential is larger (+21 mV), so the action potentials are more frequent.
5. From 4 kPa to 5 kPa the increase is only +1 mV, so the response is close to a maximum. This is because nearly all of the sodium channels are already open.

## The human retina

The retina is the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. It contains two types of photoreceptor: **rods** and **cones**. In the light, a pigment in the photoreceptor absorbs light and breaks down, which changes the membrane potential and, through a **bipolar neurone**, leads to an impulse in a neurone of the **optic nerve**.

You need to explain three differences in the way we see: sensitivity to light, colour and visual acuity. Each depends on the **optical pigments** and on the **connections** the receptors make.

| Feature | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| Optical pigment | One type (rhodopsin) | Three types, each sensitive to a different range of wavelengths (red, green and blue) |
| Sensitivity to light | High; function in dim light | Low; need bright light |
| Colour vision | None; give black and white vision | Yes; the combination of cones stimulated gives the colour |
| Acuity | Low | High |
| Connections | Many rods connect to one bipolar neurone | One cone connects to one bipolar neurone |
| Distribution | Mainly at the edge of the retina | Concentrated at the fovea |

### Sensitivity

In dim light there may be only a little energy in each receptor. The pigment in a rod is broken down by low-intensity light. Many rods connect to one bipolar neurone, so the small generator potentials from several rods add up (**spatial summation**). Together they may reach the threshold and cause an impulse in the neurone. A cone connects individually, so a small generator potential does not reach the threshold, and cones respond only to brighter light.

**Worked example 2.** Each rod produces a generator potential of 2 mV in dim light. A bipolar neurone needs a total of 10 mV to be depolarised to threshold.

1. Rods needed to stimulate the neurone together = 10 / 2 = 5.
2. If 30 rods connect to the bipolar neurone, light that stimulates at least 5 of them will trigger an impulse.
3. A single cone connected to its own neurone would produce only 2 mV, which is below the threshold, so the neurone is not stimulated in dim light.

### Visual acuity

**Visual acuity** is the ability to distinguish between two points that are close together. Each cone has its own connection to a bipolar neurone, so light falling on two neighbouring cones results in two separate impulses, and the brain sees two points. Many rods share one neurone, so light on two neighbouring rods produces one signal, and the points are seen as one. The fovea has many closely packed cones, so acuity is highest there.

### Colour

There are three types of cone, each with a different form of the pigment, sensitive to different wavelengths of light. A wavelength stimulates the types in different proportions, and the brain interprets the combination as a colour. Rods have only one pigment, so they cannot distinguish wavelengths.

**Worked example 3: applying the ideas.** A person has trouble reading in a dim room but reads well in bright light. Which receptors are most likely to be involved?

1. In bright light, cones with high acuity are stimulated, and fine detail can be resolved.
2. In dim light, only rods are stimulated, and they have low acuity because of their shared connections.
3. The person's problem is a result of using rods in dim light, not a fault in cones.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the Pacinian corpuscle produces an action potential directly. It produces a generator potential, which may lead to an action potential.
- Writing that stretch-mediated channels open when the cell "senses" pressure. The deformation of the membrane opens them.
- Saying that a stronger stimulus produces a bigger action potential. It produces a larger generator potential and more frequent action potentials.
- Saying that rods "are more sensitive because they are bigger". The explanation is the pigment and the summation of signals.
- Confusing sensitivity and acuity. Rods are more sensitive and cones have greater acuity.
- Writing that rods connect to "the brain". They connect to bipolar neurones.
- Leaving out the pigments when explaining colour vision.

## Exam technique

- **Sequence the steps** in a Pacinian corpuscle: stimulus, deformation, sodium channels open, sodium enters, generator potential, threshold, action potential.
- **For retina questions, pair every difference with a cause**: pigment or connection.
- **Interpret data** with values: state the threshold, compare it with the generator potential and then state whether an action potential occurs.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name the pigments rhodopsin and iodopsin?**
The specification refers to "optical pigments" and to "differences" between the pigments of rods and cones. Naming rhodopsin for rods is useful, but the explanation matters more than the names.

**Why are cones concentrated in the fovea?**
The fovea is the part of the retina on which an image is focused when we look straight at an object. Cones there give the highest acuity and colour vision in the centre of the field of view.
