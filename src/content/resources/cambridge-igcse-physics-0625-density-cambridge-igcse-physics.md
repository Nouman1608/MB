This guide teaches section 1.4, Density, of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Core statements 1 to 3 cover the definition, the three experiments and deciding whether an object floats. Supplement statement 4, deciding whether one liquid floats on another, is examined only on Papers 2 and 4 and is labelled **Extended only**. ## Density and its units

**Density is mass per unit volume:**

```
ρ = m / V        m = ρ V        V = m / ρ
```

ρ (the Greek letter rho) is in kg/m³ when m is in kg and V in m³, or in g/cm³ when m is in g and V in cm³.

**Converting.** 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³, because 1 g = 0.001 kg and 1 cm³ = 0.000 001 m³, so 1 g/cm³ = 0.001 ÷ 0.000 001 = 1000 kg/m³. Water has a density of about 1.0 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.

Density is a property of the **material**, not of the object. Cut a copper block in half and each half has half the mass and half the volume, so the density is unchanged. That is why density can help identify a material.

**Worked example 1 (large object).** A concrete paving slab measures 0.60 m × 0.40 m × 0.050 m. Concrete has a density of 2400 kg/m³. Find the slab's mass and its weight (g = 9.8 N/kg).

```
V = 0.60 × 0.40 × 0.050 = 0.012 m³
m = ρ V = 2400 × 0.012 = 28.8 kg
W = m g = 28.8 × 9.8 = 282 N   (about 280 N)
```

Keep the units consistent: lengths in metres give a volume in m³, which pairs with kg/m³.

## The three experiments

Each experiment needs a **mass** (from a balance) and a **volume** (found differently each time).

### A regularly shaped solid

1. Find the mass on a balance.
2. Measure the dimensions with a ruler (or, for small objects, more precisely with the multiples method from section 1.1 where possible). Measure each dimension in two or three places and average.
3. Calculate the volume: a cuboid is length × width × height; a cylinder is π × radius² × length.
4. ρ = m ÷ V.

**Worked example 2.** A brass cuboid is 5.0 cm × 3.0 cm × 2.0 cm and has a mass of 255 g.

```
V = 5.0 × 3.0 × 2.0 = 30 cm³
ρ = 255 / 30 = 8.5 g/cm³  =  8500 kg/m³
```

### A liquid

1. Find the mass of an empty, dry measuring cylinder.
2. Pour in some liquid and read the volume at the bottom of the meniscus, eye level, cylinder on a level bench.
3. Find the mass of the cylinder and liquid.
4. Mass of liquid = second mass − first mass; ρ = m ÷ V.

For example: empty cylinder 86.4 g; with 50.0 cm³ of liquid, 126.4 g. Mass of liquid = 40.0 g, so ρ = 40.0 ÷ 50.0 = **0.800 g/cm³**. Many electronic balances can be "zeroed" (tared) with the empty cylinder on them, so the display then shows the liquid's mass directly.

### An irregularly shaped solid that sinks (displacement)

**Method A: measuring cylinder.** Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and read the volume. Tie the object to a thread and lower it gently until it is completely covered. Read the new volume. The object's volume is the **rise** in the reading.

**Method B: displacement (overflow) can.** Fill the can until water runs out of the spout; wait for it to stop. Place an empty measuring cylinder under the spout and lower the object in. The volume of water collected equals the object's volume. Use this for objects too wide for a measuring cylinder.

Precautions: lower the object gently so no water splashes out; make sure it is fully under water; tap away any air bubbles clinging to it (they add to the apparent volume); find the mass **before** putting it in water, or dry it first.

The method only works for objects that sink: a floating object is not fully under water, so it displaces less than its own volume.

## Floating and sinking

An object **floats** in a liquid if its density is **less** than the liquid's density, and **sinks** if its density is **greater**.

| Object | Density / g/cm³ | In water (1.0 g/cm³) |
|---|---|---|
| ice | 0.92 | floats |
| a pine block | 0.50 | floats |
| brass cuboid above | 8.5 | sinks |
| plastic with density 1.2 | 1.2 | sinks |

You may be given mass and volume instead of density: calculate the density first, then compare.

### Liquids in layers (Extended only)

When liquids that do **not mix** are poured into the same container, they settle into layers with the **densest at the bottom** and the least dense at the top.

**Worked example 3.** Three liquids that do not mix are poured into a tall jar: liquid X, 1.26 g/cm³; liquid Y, 0.86 g/cm³; water, 1.00 g/cm³. A small bead of density 0.95 g/cm³ is dropped in.

```
order from the top:  Y (0.86)  →  water (1.00)  →  X (1.26)
bead (0.95): denser than Y, so it sinks through Y;
             less dense than water, so it floats on the water
bead rests at the boundary between Y and water
```

Two liquids that **do** mix (such as water and alcohol) do not form layers, which is why the syllabus states "given that the liquids do not mix".

## Common mistakes

- Writing ρ = V/m.
- Mixing units: grams with m³, or kilograms with cm³.
- Using the total mass of cylinder and liquid as the liquid's mass.
- Taking the final water level as the object's volume, rather than the rise in level.
- Converting 1 g/cm³ to 100 kg/m³ (it is 1000 kg/m³).
- Saying heavy objects sink: a large log is heavy but floats, because its density is low.
- *(Extended)* Saying the "heavier" liquid goes to the bottom. A large volume of a less dense liquid can be heavier but still floats on top.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define** density: "mass per unit volume". "Mass ÷ volume" is accepted only if you name both quantities.
- **Describe** an experiment: list the measurements, the instruments, how volume is found, at least one precaution, and the calculation. Number your steps.
- **Calculate**: show the volume calculation, the substitution into ρ = m/V, and the unit.
- **Determine / State and explain** whether something floats: quote the two densities and compare them in a sentence ("0.92 g/cm³ is less than 1.00 g/cm³, so it floats").
- *(Extended)* For layers, give the order **and** the reason (density) for each position.
