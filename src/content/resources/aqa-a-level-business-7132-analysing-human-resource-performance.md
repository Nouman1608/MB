This guide goes deeper into section **3.6.2 Analysing human resource performance** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **calculating and interpreting human resource data**, where you should be able to calculate and interpret **labour turnover, labour productivity, employee costs as a percentage of turnover and labour cost per unit**
- **the use of data for human resource decision making and planning**.

The wider [human resource management study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-human-resource-management/) covers the whole topic. Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why measure HR performance

HR objectives (see the [HR objectives guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-human-resource-objectives/)) are only useful if progress can be checked. HR data allows managers to see whether the workforce is stable and productive, whether labour costs are under control, and where problems are building. It also supports planning: how many people to recruit, whether to train, and whether pay rises are affordable.

## The four calculations

### Labour turnover

**Labour turnover** measures the proportion of the workforce that leaves in a period.

> labour turnover (%) = number of employees leaving in the period ÷ average number of employees × 100

Its reverse is **labour retention**: the proportion of staff who stay (100% minus turnover, approximately, if no new joiners are counted among leavers).

A high rate is costly: recruitment, induction and training of replacements, lost knowledge, and lower service quality while new staff learn. It may signal low pay, poor management, weak engagement or a better-paying competitor. However, some turnover is healthy because it brings fresh ideas and allows promotion. What counts as "high" depends on the industry: seasonal, hospitality and retail businesses usually have higher rates than, for example, engineering firms.

### Labour productivity

> labour productivity = total output ÷ number of employees

It can also be measured per hour worked. Rising productivity means each worker produces more. See the [operational performance guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-operational-performance/) for the calculation in operations terms. In HR terms, productivity reflects skills, training, motivation, leadership and technology.

### Employee costs as a percentage of turnover

**Turnover** here means sales revenue.

> employee costs as a percentage of turnover = total employee costs ÷ turnover × 100

Employee costs include wages, salaries, employer's national insurance, pensions and benefits. The ratio shows how much of each pound of sales is spent on staff. It is especially important in service businesses, where labour is the main cost. A lower percentage can mean better efficiency or higher prices; a higher percentage may mean over-staffing, rising wages or falling sales.

### Labour cost per unit

> labour cost per unit = total labour cost ÷ total output

This shows how much labour cost is built into each unit of output. It links productivity and pay: if wages rise faster than productivity, labour cost per unit rises. It is affected by overtime, bonuses and the mix of skilled and unskilled labour.

## Worked example: Fairlight Stores

Fairlight Stores makes and sells garden furniture. Data for two years:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Average number of employees | 200 | 208 |
| Employees who left during the year | 46 | 31 |
| Output (units) | 480,000 | 520,000 |
| Total employee costs | £4,800,000 | £5,200,000 |
| Turnover (sales revenue) | £24,000,000 | £28,600,000 |
| Cost of recruiting and training each replacement | £3,500 | £3,500 |

**Labour turnover**

- Year 1: 46 ÷ 200 × 100 = **23%**
- Year 2: 31 ÷ 208 × 100 = **14.9%** (to one decimal place)

**Cost of turnover** (leavers × £3,500): Year 1 = 46 × £3,500 = **£161,000**; Year 2 = 31 × £3,500 = **£108,500**, a saving of £52,500.

**Labour productivity**

- Year 1: 480,000 ÷ 200 = **2,400 units per employee**
- Year 2: 520,000 ÷ 208 = **2,500 units per employee**, a rise of 4.2%.

**Employee costs as a percentage of turnover**

- Year 1: 4,800,000 ÷ 24,000,000 × 100 = **20%**
- Year 2: 5,200,000 ÷ 28,600,000 × 100 = **18.2%**

**Labour cost per unit**

- Year 1: £4,800,000 ÷ 480,000 = **£10.00**
- Year 2: £5,200,000 ÷ 520,000 = **£10.00**

**Interpretation.**

- Turnover fell sharply and saved £52,500 of replacement costs. This suggests that something improved, such as pay, working conditions or management, though the data does not say what.
- Productivity rose 4.2%, but labour cost per unit stayed at £10. This is because average cost per employee also rose 4.2% (from £24,000 to £25,000), so higher pay absorbed the productivity gain.
- Employee costs fell as a share of turnover (from 20% to 18.2%), because sales revenue grew faster (19%) than employee costs (8.3%). This could reflect higher selling prices as well as efficiency.
- Overall, Fairlight's HR performance improved, but the business has not yet turned higher productivity into lower labour cost per unit. If competitors cut costs, it could be vulnerable.

## A second example: comparing two stores

A retailer compares two stores for one year.

| | Store A | Store B |
|---|---|---|
| Average employees | 40 | 25 |
| Leavers | 10 | 3 |
| Items sold (output) | 96,000 | 50,000 |
| Employee costs | £960,000 | £700,000 |
| Revenue | £4,800,000 | £2,500,000 |

- Labour turnover: A = 10 ÷ 40 = **25%**; B = 3 ÷ 25 = **12%**.
- Productivity: A = 96,000 ÷ 40 = **2,400**; B = 50,000 ÷ 25 = **2,000** items per employee.
- Employee costs as a percentage of revenue: A = **20%**; B = **28%**.
- Labour cost per unit: A = £960,000 ÷ 96,000 = **£10**; B = £700,000 ÷ 50,000 = **£14**.

Store B keeps its staff much better, but it sells fewer items per employee and spends 28p of every pound of revenue on staff, against 20p at Store A. A manager should ask why. Perhaps Store B is too lightly used for its staffing, or its location draws less trade, or Store A's higher turnover hides a harder working culture that may not last. The sensible response is to investigate (for example, with staff surveys and exit interviews at A and a staffing review at B), not to copy one store's approach without evidence.

## Using the data

| Question | Data to use | Possible decision |
|---|---|---|
| Is the workforce stable? | Labour turnover over time and against competitors | Improve pay, engagement, management or induction; investigate exit interviews |
| Do we have the right number of people? | Productivity and forecast output | Recruit, train, redeploy or automate |
| Can we afford a pay rise? | Employee costs % of turnover; labour cost per unit | Link rises to productivity gains |
| Is a training programme working? | Productivity before and after | Continue, expand or change the programme |
| Which store or team is struggling? | Compare turnover and productivity between units | Targeted support or management change |
| Should we move production? | Labour cost per unit in different locations | Relocate or outsource |

## Causes and responses

**High labour turnover:** low pay; limited promotion; poor management; stress; poor induction; strong local labour market. Responses: better pay and benefits, career paths, flexible working, better selection and induction, listening to staff.

**Low or falling productivity:** poor morale, lack of training, out-of-date equipment, poor scheduling, new inexperienced staff. Responses: training, motivation methods, investment in technology, better organisation.

**High employee costs as % of turnover:** over-staffing, high wages relative to sales, low prices, falling sales. Responses: raise productivity, adjust staffing, raise prices, increase sales.

## Limits of HR data

- Figures are **averages** and hide differences between departments or groups.
- Labour turnover does not distinguish good leavers from poor ones, or planned from unplanned departures (such as retirements).
- Productivity measures quantity, not quality.
- Employee costs as a percentage of turnover are affected by prices and sales as well as by the workforce.
- Data tells you *what* is happening, not *why*; surveys and exit interviews are needed.
- Small samples (a small business with few staff) may produce misleading percentages.
- Changes in technology and the labour market mean past data may not predict the future.

## Technique for exam questions

- Write the formula, show the substitution and state the unit (% or £ per unit).
- Interpret: say what the figure means for the business, compare it with another year or benchmark, and suggest a cause.
- For "assess" or "evaluate" questions, discuss the limits of the data and what other information is needed.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing leavers by the wrong figure instead of the average number of employees.
- Mixing up employee costs as a percentage of turnover with labour cost per unit.
- Saying all labour turnover is bad.
- Giving the figure without any interpretation.
- Forgetting that pay rises can cancel out productivity gains.
