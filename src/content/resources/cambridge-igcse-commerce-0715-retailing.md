Retailing is the last stage of the chain of distribution: selling goods to the final consumer. Section 2.3 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has four parts: **types of retailers**, **retail selling techniques**, **digital retail trends** and the **effects of ecommerce on traditional commerce**. The lists are long, so examiners often test whether you can tell similar items apart, such as a supermarket and a hypermarket, or omnichannel and multi-channel retailing. This page goes deeper than the retailing section of the [Commercial Operations study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-commerce-commercial-operations/).

## Which types of retailer does the syllabus name? (2.3.1)

The syllabus lists ten types, small-scale and large-scale.

| Type | Features | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Itinerant retailer | No fixed shop; travels to customers on foot, by bicycle or van, or between markets | Low costs; brings goods to the customer's door | Small stock; income can be irregular; affected by weather |
| Vending machine | Automatic machine selling snacks, drinks or tickets | Sells 24 hours with no staff | Limited range; breakdowns; theft or damage |
| Independent retailer | A single shop, or very few, run by the owner | Personal service; flexible hours; knows customers | Higher prices; little capital; small range |
| Street market | Many stalls in an open or covered space, often on set days | Low rent; lively atmosphere; customers compare prices easily | Weather; limited trading days; few customer facilities |
| Speciality shop | Sells a deep range of one type of product, such as sports gear or cameras | Expert advice; wide choice within one product type | Narrow appeal; higher prices |
| Online retailer | Sells through a website or app and delivers to customers | Sells 24/7 to a wide area; no shop rent | Delivery and returns costs; customers cannot handle goods |
| Multiple chain store | Many branches under one name, with central buying and similar layout | Bulk buying lowers costs; familiar brand | Less flexibility for local tastes; impersonal |
| Supermarket | Large self-service store selling mainly food and household goods | Wide range; low prices from bulk buying | Little personal service; needs a large site |
| Hypermarket | Very large store, often out of town, with food and non-food goods and large car parks | Huge range under one roof; free parking | Hard to reach without a car; long walks inside |
| Department store | Many departments under one roof, each like a separate shop, usually in a city centre | High service; many types of goods in one visit | High costs and prices; expensive city-centre sites |

The usual pairing is **small-scale** (itinerant retailers, independent retailers, street markets, many speciality shops) and **large-scale** (multiple chains, supermarkets, hypermarkets, department stores). Vending machines and online retailers can be either.

## Why does the small-scale retailer often survive? (2.3.1)

The syllabus gives three examples: **personal service**, **flexible opening hours** and **additional services provided**. Make each one an explanation:

- **Personal service**: the owner knows regular customers, remembers what they buy and gives advice, which large stores cannot match.
- **Flexible opening hours**: a corner shop can open early, late or on holidays, when large stores are closed.
- **Additional services**: delivery to elderly customers, selling mobile phone credit, bill payment points, or **informal credit** to trusted customers.

Convenience (being close to homes) and stocking local products are further reasons you can add.

## What selling techniques do retailers use? (2.3.2)

| Technique | Meaning | How it helps sales |
|---|---|---|
| Branding | Giving a product a name and identity that customers recognise | Builds trust and repeat purchases |
| Own brands | Products sold under the retailer's own name, made for it by manufacturers | Cheaper than national brands; higher profit margin for the retailer |
| Logos | A symbol that identifies the business or brand | Quick recognition on shops, packaging and online |
| Packaging | The wrapping and container of the product | Protects goods, gives information and attracts attention on the shelf |
| Self service | Customers choose goods themselves and pay at a checkout | Fewer staff; customers browse and buy more |
| After-sales service | Help after purchase: delivery, installation, repairs, guarantees | Gives confidence for expensive items such as fridges |
| Barcoding | A machine-readable code on each item | Fast, accurate checkout and automatic stock records |
| Direct mail | Advertising sent straight to customers' homes or inboxes | Targets known customers with offers |
| Loyalty programmes | Points or rewards for repeat purchases | Encourages customers to return; gives data on buying habits |
| Informal credit | Trusted customers pay later without a formal agreement | Keeps regular customers loyal to small shops |
| Cash on delivery | Customer pays when the goods arrive | Builds trust for online buyers who fear fraud or have no card |

## What are the digital retail trends? (2.3.3)

| Trend | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Omnichannel retailing | All channels (shop, website, app, social media) are joined up, sharing stock and customer accounts, so a customer can move between them seamlessly |
| Multi-channel retailing | The retailer sells through several channels, but they may operate separately |
| Cashless stores | Shops that accept only card, phone or other digital payments |
| Electronic Point of Sale (EPoS) | Tills linked to a computer that record each sale and update stock automatically |
| Click and collect | Customers order online and pick up from a store or collection point |
| Self-service checkouts | Customers scan and pay for items themselves |
| Subscription commerce | Customers pay regularly to receive products automatically, such as a monthly box |
| QR codes | Scannable codes linking to product information, payment or offers |
| Virtual Reality (VR) showrooms | Customers view products in a virtual space, such as walking round a virtual furniture room |
| Dropshipping | The retailer sells goods it does not stock; the supplier sends them directly to the customer |
| Mobile phone or smartphone apps | The retailer's own app for browsing, buying, loyalty points and offers |

**Omnichannel versus multi-channel** is the pair most often confused. In multi-channel retailing a customer might find that the website and the shop have different prices or stock. In omnichannel retailing the customer can check stock in the app, reserve online, collect in store and return by post, all within one account.

## How has ecommerce affected traditional commerce? (2.3.4)

| Positive impacts | Negative impacts |
|---|---|
| 24/7 selling opportunities | Increased delivery costs |
| More potential customers beyond the local area | Staff training in new systems |
| Increased revenue | Larger warehousing space for online stock |
| Targeted marketing using customer data | Website maintenance costs |
| Reduced operating store costs, such as fewer or smaller shops | |

Traditional retailers that also sell online gain the benefits but take on the costs, so the effect depends on the business.

## Worked example: an annotated model answer

*Question: Corner Fresh is an independent grocery shop near a new hypermarket. Explain two reasons why Corner Fresh may survive.* **[4]**

> Corner Fresh can offer **personal service** [1]: the owner knows regular customers, can order special items for them and may give trusted customers informal credit, which a hypermarket's staff are unlikely to do [1]. It can also offer **flexible opening hours** [1], opening early in the morning and late at night when the hypermarket, which is out of town and needs a car journey, is less convenient for a quick purchase [1].

Why it scores: each reason is named (1 mark) and then explained by contrast with the hypermarket in the question (1 mark).

## What are the common mistakes in 2.3?

- Describing a hypermarket as "a big supermarket" without its features: out-of-town site, food and non-food, large car parks.
- Confusing a multiple chain store with a department store.
- Treating omnichannel and multi-channel retailing as the same.
- Saying own brands are made by the retailer; they are usually made for it by manufacturers.
- Saying dropshippers hold stock; they do not.
- Giving only positive impacts of ecommerce in a "discuss" question.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify**: name the type of retailer or trend from the scenario.
- **Describe**: give features, for example of a department store.
- **Explain**: give the technique and how it raises sales or loyalty for the retailer named.
- **Discuss** or **evaluate**: weigh positive and negative impacts, such as of adding self-service checkouts, and conclude.
