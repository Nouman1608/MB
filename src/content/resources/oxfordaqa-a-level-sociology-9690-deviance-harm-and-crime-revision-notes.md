Section **3.1.2.5 Deviance, harm and crime** of OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690)
is the last content section of 3.1.2 Socialisation and social control, assessed in Unit 2 Section A.
It covers how crime and deviance are socially constructed and differ between societies and over
time; the main types of crime; social harms that are not treated as crimes; the problems of
measuring and researching crime; and patterns of offending and victimisation by gender, social class,
age and ethnicity.

Full overview: [Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

## Specification coverage

3.1.2.5 lists:

- Social construction of crime and deviance, including differences between societies and over time.
- Types of crimes and examples: violent crimes; property crimes; white collar crimes; corporate
  crimes; state crimes; green/environmental crimes; "victimless" crimes; cybercrimes.
- Social harms, including examples of actions which cause harm but are not considered crimes.
- Issues in measuring and researching crime and deviance, including official/police statistics,
  self-report studies and victim surveys.
- Patterns of offending and victimisation by gender, social class, age and ethnicity.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Crime** | An act that breaks the criminal law |
| **Deviance** | An act that breaks social norms |
| **Social construction** | Defined by society; varies across cultures and time |
| **White-collar crime** | Crime by people in respectable, high-status occupations in the course of their work (Sutherland, 1939) |
| **Corporate crime** | Crime committed by or for a company |
| **State crime** | Illegal or harmful acts by governments or state agencies |
| **Green crime** | Crime and harm against the environment and other species |
| **"Victimless" crime** | Offence with no obvious direct victim, e.g. some drug offences (the inverted commas show the label is disputed) |
| **Cybercrime** | Crime committed using computers and the internet |
| **Social harm** | Serious harm caused by actions or policies that may not be illegal |
| **Dark figure** | Crime that is unreported or unrecorded |
| **Victimisation** | Experience of being a victim of crime |

## Social construction

- Acts are defined as crime or deviance by those with the power to make and enforce rules.
- Differences **between societies** — alcohol, gambling or forms of dress are legal in some states
  and criminal in others.
- Differences **over time** — behaviours have been criminalised (new cyber offences) or
  decriminalised as attitudes change.
- Moral panics (Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*, 1972) show how media and public
  reaction help define deviance.

## Types of crime — examples to learn

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Violent | Assault, robbery, homicide |
| Property | Burglary, theft, criminal damage |
| White-collar | Embezzlement, insider trading by an employee |
| Corporate | Breaches of health and safety law; price-fixing; false accounting |
| State | Torture, genocide, corruption by officials |
| Green | Illegal logging, wildlife trafficking, toxic dumping |
| "Victimless" | Personal drug use, some gambling offences |
| Cyber | Hacking, online fraud, ransomware |

## Social harm

The **social harm** (zemiology) approach — associated with Paddy Hillyard and Steve Tombs, *Beyond
Criminology: Taking Harm Seriously* (2004) — argues that focusing on legally defined crime ignores
many of the most serious harms. Examples of harmful but often legal acts: deaths and injuries from
unsafe but legal working conditions, pollution within legal limits, poverty caused by policy, and
aggressive marketing of harmful products. Stanley Cohen's *States of Denial* (2001) explored how
states and societies deny atrocities and suffering.

## Measuring and researching crime

| Source | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Official / police statistics** | National, regular, cheap; show trends in recorded crime | Many crimes unreported; recording depends on police discretion and priorities; counting rules change |
| **Self-report studies** | Reveal hidden offending; show offenders never caught | Respondents may lie or forget; usually limited to minor offences; sampling problems |
| **Victim surveys** (e.g. Crime Survey for England and Wales) | Capture unreported crime; large samples | Miss crimes without individual victims (corporate, green, state); rely on memory; exclude some groups such as homeless people |

Researching powerful offenders is especially difficult: access is limited and corporate and state
crimes are often hidden.

## Patterns of offending and victimisation

- **Gender:** men are responsible for most recorded crime, especially violence; women are more often
  victims of domestic and sexual violence.
- **Social class:** working-class people are over-represented in recorded crime and prisons; crimes
  of higher classes (white-collar, corporate) are under-recorded.
- **Age:** recorded offending peaks in late teens and early twenties; young people are also at high
  risk of victimisation.
- **Ethnicity:** in some societies certain minority groups are over-represented in arrests and
  imprisonment — debated as evidence of discrimination, deprivation or both — and minorities may
  suffer hate crime.

Always note that **patterns depend on the data**: official statistics reflect reporting and policing.

## Worked example: an 8-mark answer outline

*Explain two problems with using victim surveys to measure crime.* (practice)

1. **Missing crimes** — victim surveys ask individuals about crimes they experienced, so they miss
   corporate, state and green crimes where victims may not know they have been harmed, and crimes
   against organisations. (4)
2. **Memory and honesty** — respondents may forget, misremember dates ("telescoping") or not disclose
   sensitive crimes such as domestic abuse, especially if the abuser is present; this reduces
   validity. (4)

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Confusing **white-collar** (individual, in the course of work) with **corporate** (for the company)
  crime.
- Treating official statistics as a true measure of crime.
- Writing about social harm without giving **examples of harmful but legal acts**, which the
  specification asks for.
- Describing patterns without considering **victimisation** as well as offending.
- Ignoring differences **between societies** when explaining social construction.

## Quick self-test

1. Give an example of an act that is a crime in one society but not another.
2. Define state crime and give an example.
3. What is meant by social harm?
4. Give one strength and one limitation of self-report studies.
5. Describe the age pattern of offending.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the specification put "victimless" in inverted commas?** Because whether such crimes
really have no victims is disputed — families or communities may still be harmed.

**Is social harm the same as crime?** No. It is a wider concept including legal actions and policies
that cause serious harm.

**Which data source is best?** Each has strengths and limitations; combining sources gives a fuller
picture.
