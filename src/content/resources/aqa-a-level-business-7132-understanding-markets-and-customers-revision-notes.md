Condensed notes on section **3.3.2 Understanding markets and customers** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and worked examples are in the [markets and customers study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-understanding-markets-and-customers/); exam-style questions are in the [markets and customers practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-understanding-markets-and-customers-practice/).

## Specification content

- The value of primary and secondary marketing research; qualitative and quantitative data; calculating market and sales growth, market share and market size.
- The value of sampling: random, stratified, quota.
- Interpreting marketing data: positive and negative correlation and strength; confidence intervals; extrapolation.
- Interpreting price and income elasticity of demand data and their impact on revenue (calculation not required).
- The value of elasticity to decision makers; the use of data in marketing decision making and planning.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary research | New data collected first-hand for the purpose |
| Secondary research | Existing data collected by others |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data (how many, how much) |
| Qualitative data | Opinions and reasons (why) |
| Population | The whole group being studied |
| Sample | The part of the population actually asked |
| Correlation | A relationship between two sets of data |
| Confidence interval | The range in which the true value probably lies, at a stated confidence level |
| Extrapolation | Extending a past trend to forecast the future |
| PED | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price |
| YED | Responsiveness of demand to a change in incomes |

## Primary versus secondary research

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation | Government statistics, trade reports, sales records |
| For | Specific, up to date, owned by the firm | Cheap, quick, wide view |
| Against | Slow, costly, bias risk | May be dated or not fit the question |

Use **both** quantitative (patterns) and qualitative (reasons) data.

## Sampling

| Method | Key feature | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Random | Equal chance for everyone | May miss small groups; needs a full list |
| Stratified | Groups sampled in proportion to their size | Needs information to form groups |
| Quota | Set numbers of each type, chosen by interviewer | Interviewer bias; not random |

**Stratified calculation:** group size ÷ population × sample size.

Bigger samples are more reliable but cost more. A big biased sample is still unreliable.

## Interpreting data

- **Positive correlation:** both rise together. **Negative:** one rises as the other falls. **Strength:** points close to a line mean a strong relationship.
- Correlation is **not** proof of cause. Look for other factors.
- **Confidence interval:** estimate ± margin. Larger sample means narrower interval and more precision. A wide interval means more caution.
- **Extrapolation:** quick and useful, but assumes conditions stay the same; weaker the further ahead it goes.

## Elasticity

| | Value | Meaning | Price rise effect on revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| PED | Above 1 (ignoring sign) | Elastic | Revenue falls |
| PED | Below 1 | Inelastic | Revenue rises |
| YED | Above 1 | Luxury | Demand rises faster than income |
| YED | Between 0 and 1 | Necessity | Demand rises slower than income |
| YED | Negative | Inferior | Demand falls as income rises |

- PED of −1.5: a 10% price rise leads to about a 15% fall in quantity.
- Inelastic demand is more likely with few substitutes, necessities, strong brands, small share of income.
- Use of elasticity: pricing, promotion, forecasting in recession or boom, product range.
- Limits: based on past data; rivals react; other factors change.

## Using data in decisions

Spot trends, choose target segments, set prices, forecast sales, judge campaigns. Risks: small or biased samples, confusing correlation with cause, ethical limits on personal data. Data supports judgement; it does not replace it.

## Exam traps

- A quota sample is **not** random.
- Strong correlation does not prove one thing causes the other.
- A wider confidence interval means **less** precision.
- Elastic demand: raise price and revenue **falls**.
- PED concerns price; YED concerns income. Do not mix them.
- Say how far ahead an extrapolation is being made and why that matters.

## Self-test

1. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of secondary research.
2. Why should a business use both qualitative and quantitative data?
3. A shop has 3,000 customers: 1,800 regular and 1,200 occasional. It takes a stratified sample of 150. How many of each type should it ask?
4. Give one disadvantage of quota sampling.
5. Sales and advertising spend show a strong positive correlation. Explain why this does not prove advertising caused the sales.
6. A survey finds 48% of customers like a new flavour, ±4 percentage points. What is the confidence interval?
7. Would a larger sample make the interval wider or narrower?
8. Sales (£000) for four months were 20, 22, 24 and 26. Forecast month 7 by extrapolation.
9. Demand for a product has a PED of −0.4. What happens to revenue if the price rises?
10. Demand for a product has a PED of −2. A 5% price cut is made. What happens to quantity demanded and to revenue?
11. A product has a YED of −0.3. What type of good is it, and what happens to demand in a boom?
12. Give two ways elasticity data helps a marketing manager.

### Answers

1. Advantage (any one): cheaper; quicker; wide view. Disadvantage (any one): may be out of date; not designed for the question; rivals can see it.
2. Quantitative data shows patterns and numbers; qualitative data explains the reasons behind them.
3. Regular: 1,800 ÷ 3,000 × 150 = **90**; occasional: 1,200 ÷ 3,000 × 150 = **60**.
4. Interviewers may choose people who are easiest to approach, so bias is possible and the sample is not random.
5. Another factor (such as season, a rival's problems or a price cut) may have caused the rise; correlation shows association only.
6. 44% to 52%.
7. Narrower.
8. Rise of 2 a month; month 7 is three months after month 4: 26 + 3 × 2 = **£32k**.
9. Demand is inelastic, so quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises; revenue rises.
10. Quantity rises by about 10%; because demand is elastic, revenue rises.
11. An inferior good. In a boom, as incomes rise, demand falls.
12. Any two: setting prices; deciding whether to discount; forecasting demand in a recession or boom; deciding whether to spend on branding.
