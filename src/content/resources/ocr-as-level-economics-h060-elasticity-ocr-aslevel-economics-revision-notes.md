These notes condense topic 2.6 **Elasticity** in Component 01, Microeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to "explain and calculate" PED, YED, XED and PES, show their values on diagrams, link PED to a firm's total revenue, and evaluate the determinants and the usefulness of each measure. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [elasticity study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-elasticity-ocr-aslevel-economics/); test yourself with the [elasticity practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-elasticity-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## The four formulas

| Measure | Formula | Usual sign | Read the |
|---|---|---|---|
| PED | % change in Qd ÷ % change in own price | Negative | Size |
| YED | % change in Qd ÷ % change in real income | Positive (normal) or negative (inferior) | Sign, then size |
| XED | % change in Qd of A ÷ % change in price of B | Positive (substitutes) or negative (complements) | Sign, then size |
| PES | % change in Qs ÷ % change in price | Positive | Size |

Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The responding variable always goes on top.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Elastic | Coefficient above 1 in size: the response is proportionately larger than the cause |
| Inelastic | Coefficient between 0 and 1 in size |
| Unitary elasticity | Coefficient of exactly 1: proportionate response |
| Perfectly inelastic | Coefficient 0: no response at all (vertical curve) |
| Perfectly elastic | Coefficient infinite: any price change removes all demand or supply (horizontal curve) |
| Inferior good | Negative YED: demand falls as income rises |
| Normal good | Positive YED |
| Luxury (income elastic) good | YED above +1 |
| Substitutes | Positive XED: goods used instead of each other |
| Complements | Negative XED: goods used together |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity sold |

## Values and diagrams

| Value | Demand curve | Supply curve |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Vertical | Vertical |
| Inelastic | Steep (around the point compared) | Straight line starting on the quantity axis |
| 1 | Rectangular hyperbola | Any straight line through the origin |
| Elastic | Shallow | Straight line starting on the price axis |
| Infinite | Horizontal | Horizontal |

Along a straight-line demand curve PED changes: elastic near the top, unitary at the midpoint, inelastic near the bottom. A constant slope does not mean a constant elasticity.

## PED and total revenue

| Demand | Price up | Price down |
|---|---|---|
| Inelastic | TR up | TR down |
| Unitary | No change | No change |
| Elastic | TR down | TR up |

**Worked reminder.** A gym raises its monthly fee from £30 to £33 (+10%). Members fall from 1,500 to 1,410 (−6%). PED = −6 ÷ 10 = **−0.6**. TR rises from £45,000 to 33 × 1,410 = **£46,530**.

**Worked reminder.** A train operator cuts an off-peak fare from £20 to £16 (−20%). Journeys rise from 3,000 to 4,200 (+40%). PED = **−2.0**. TR rises from £60,000 to **£67,200**.

## Determinants in one line each

- **PED:** substitutes (the main one), share of income, necessity or habit, time, breadth of market definition.
- **YED:** necessity versus luxury, and the income level of the buyers.
- **XED:** how closely goods substitute or complement in use; brand loyalty weakens it.
- **PES:** spare capacity, stocks, mobility of factors, length of production process, time.

## Usefulness and significance

| User | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Firm | Pricing (PED), planning output over the cycle (YED), tracking rivals and complements (XED) | Estimates come from past data and rivals react |
| Government | Choosing goods to tax for revenue (low PED), predicting effects on consumption | A tax on an inelastic good raises revenue but barely cuts consumption |
| Producers in a market | PES explains price volatility in farming and housing | Short-run and long-run values differ |

## Exam traps

- Swapping the numerator and denominator.
- Using absolute changes instead of percentages.
- Writing "elastic" for a value of −0.6 because the number is negative.
- Saying a price rise always raises revenue.
- Treating revenue as profit: costs of extra units still matter.
- Labelling a positive XED as complements.
- Giving determinants without saying whether they make the value larger or smaller.
- Ignoring time: almost every elasticity rises in size as the time period lengthens.

## Self-test

1. The price of a bus pass rises from £50 to £55 and sales fall from 800 to 760. Calculate PED.
2. Is demand in question 1 elastic or inelastic, and what happens to total revenue?
3. Incomes rise by 5% and demand for taxi rides rises by 8%. Calculate YED and classify the good.
4. Incomes rise by 2% and demand for a value range of tinned soup falls by 1%. Calculate YED.
5. The price of coffee rises 10% and sales of tea rise 4%. Calculate XED and name the relationship.
6. The price of games consoles falls 20% and sales of games rise 10%. Calculate XED.
7. The price of timber rises 25% and quantity supplied rises 5% in a month. Calculate PES.
8. State two reasons why the PES of timber is likely to be greater after five years.
9. State the shape of a demand curve with unitary elasticity throughout.
10. Why might a firm with inelastic demand still not raise its price?
11. Explain why governments often tax goods with inelastic demand.
12. Give one limitation of using a PED estimate to set price.

### Answers

1. % change in price = +10%; % change in quantity = −5%; PED = **−0.5**.
2. Inelastic; total revenue rises from £40,000 to 55 × 760 = **£41,800**.
3. YED = 8 ÷ 5 = **+1.6**: a normal, income elastic (luxury) good.
4. YED = −1 ÷ 2 = **−0.5**: an inferior good.
5. XED = 4 ÷ 10 = **+0.4**: substitutes, though not close ones.
6. XED = +10 ÷ −20 = **−0.5**: complements.
7. PES = 5 ÷ 25 = **0.2**: inelastic.
8. Any two of: new trees can be planted and grow, more land can be used, firms can invest in more sawmills and machinery, workers can be trained.
9. A rectangular hyperbola: every price × quantity rectangle under it has the same area.
10. Any one of: rivals may not follow and buyers switch, so demand becomes more elastic at the higher price; long-run demand is more elastic; the firm may fear regulation or bad publicity; it may aim for market share.
11. Inelastic demand means quantity falls little after the tax, so the tax base stays large and revenue is high and predictable.
12. Any one of: the estimate is from past data and tastes change; PED varies along the curve; other factors also change (ceteris paribus rarely holds); rivals may react.
