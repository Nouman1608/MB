## Section A

**1.** Name the **four** social functions of language this unit's Section A assesses. **[4]**

**2.** State which assessment objective, AO1 or AO3, is weighted more heavily in Unit 2 than in Unit 1, and explain what this signals for revision priorities. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain what is meant by "group rapport and shared perspectives" as a social function of language, giving **one** linguistic feature that could signal it. **[3]**

**4.** A transcript shows a manager using closed questions, topic control and formal register with an employee. Explain which social function this demonstrates, and how. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why Section B of this unit is described as an academic essay rather than a creative or transformed writing task, and how this differs from Unit 1's Section B. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why a Section B essay that only restates the Section A analysis is likely to lose marks, and describe what it should do instead. **[5]**

**7.** Explain why revision for Section A should include spoken and multimodal texts, not only written prose. **[4]**

**8.** A piece of online banter between friends uses in-jokes and playful misspelling. Explain which social function this best demonstrates, and identify **two** specific language features that support your answer. **[5]**

**9.** Evaluate the claim that "identity and relationship" and "power and status" are simply the same social function viewed from two different angles. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** **Group rapport and shared perspectives**; **power and status**; **identity and relationship**; **play and performance** [4 — 1 mark each].

**2.** **AO1** (understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and issues) is weighted more heavily in Unit 2 than in Unit 1, where AO3 (using English in different ways) dominates [1] [1]. This signals that **precise linguistic terminology matters at least as much as fluent writing style** for this unit — vague description of a text costs marks that named, precise terminology would earn [1].

**3.** **Group rapport and shared perspectives** is language that **builds solidarity around a shared interest or affiliation** between speakers or writers [1] [1]. A signalling feature could be **shared in-group vocabulary or references that assume common knowledge**, marking the audience as insiders [1].

**4.** This demonstrates **power and status** [1]. **Closed questions and topic control let the manager restrict what is discussed and when the subject changes**, while the employee can only respond within that frame [1] [1]; **formal register maintained by the more powerful speaker reinforces the institutional hierarchy** of the interaction [1].

**5.** Unit 2's Section B is an **academic essay that uses the Section A analysis directly as evidence** for an argument about language and social group behaviour [1] [1]. This differs from **Unit 1's Section B**, which is a **transformed or creative writing task**, not an evidence-based academic argument [1] [1].

**6.** A Section B essay that only restates the Section A analysis **fails to build an academic argument**, since **description alone is not the assessed skill** — the task specifically asks for argument, connected directly to the analysed data [1] [1]. Instead, the essay should **use the observations from Section A as evidence to support a wider claim** about language and social group behaviour, going beyond what was already said in the analysis [1] [1].

**7.** Section A's source material is **explicitly drawn from spoken, written and multimodal texts** [1] [1]. Revision confined only to written prose **leaves a genuine gap**, since an unfamiliar spoken transcript or multimodal text in the exam would then come as a surprise rather than a format the candidate has already practised identifying language features in [1] [1].

**8.** This best demonstrates **play and performance** [1]. Supporting features: **in-jokes**, which rely on **shared knowledge only the group possesses**, creating an exclusionary, performative effect [1] [1]; and **playful misspelling**, which is a **deliberate, creative deviation from standard orthography rather than an error**, used for humour or group identity [1] [1].

**9. Case that they are the same function:** both power/status and identity/relationship can involve **word choices that construct how a speaker is perceived by others**, and a single feature (such as formal register) can sometimes be read as doing both at once [1] [1].
**Case that they are genuinely distinct:** the specification **separates them as distinct social functions**, and a text may draw on one **without** the other — a piece of playful in-group banter can construct identity and relationship **without any claim to power or status** being made at all [1] [1], while a legal document can assert power and status through instrumental authority **without expressing anything about personal identity or relationship** [1]. Treating them as interchangeable also **loses the precision AO1 explicitly rewards** in this unit, since naming the correct specific function is part of what is assessed [1].
**Judgement:** the two functions **can overlap in a single text**, but they are **not the same function under two names** — power/status concerns **who has authority or control**, while identity/relationship concerns **how a speaker presents themselves or connects with others**, and a text can do either, both, or neither depending on its purpose [1] [1]. A strong answer identifies **which function (or functions) a specific feature demonstrates**, rather than treating the two as freely interchangeable labels [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing what a text says without naming the specific language feature and the social function it demonstrates.
- Treating "power and status" and "identity and relationship" as interchangeable rather than distinct, separately assessable functions.
- Writing a Section B essay that restates the Section A analysis instead of using it as evidence for an argument.
- Confusing Unit 2's academic-essay Section B with Unit 1's transformed or creative Directed Writing task.
- Revising only written prose and being caught out by an unfamiliar spoken or multimodal text in the exam.
