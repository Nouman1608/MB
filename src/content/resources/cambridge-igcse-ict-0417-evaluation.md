Evaluation is the final stage of the systems life cycle in Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417, and it is the one most often answered vaguely. Section 7.6 is specific about what an evaluation includes: "the efficiency of the solution, the ease of use of the solution, and the appropriateness of the solution"; comparing "the solution with the original task requirements"; identifying "any limitations and necessary improvements to the system"; and evaluating "the users' responses to the results of testing the system". Each of those phrases can be a mark point, so learn them and know how each is actually carried out.

The [systems life cycle study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-the-systems-life-cycle-study-guide/) lists what evaluation checks. This page explains how each check is done and what evidence it uses. For recall, see the [evaluation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-evaluation-revision-notes/), and practise with the [evaluation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-evaluation-practice/).

## What evaluation is for

Evaluation answers one question: **does the new system do what it was meant to do, and how well?** It takes place after the system has been in use for long enough for users to be familiar with it. The result is a judgement, supported by evidence, and a list of improvements. That list often becomes the starting point for the next analysis, which is why the stages form a cycle.

## The three qualities of the solution

### Efficiency

Efficiency is about how well the system uses **time and resources** to do its job. The evaluator looks for measurable evidence:

- How long tasks take now compared with the old system, such as the time to process an order or to produce a report.
- How many staff are needed for a task.
- Whether processing, searching and printing are fast enough at busy times.
- Use of materials such as paper or storage space.

An efficient solution does the work faster, with fewer resources and fewer errors than before.

### Ease of use

Ease of use is about how easily **the intended users** can operate the system. Evidence comes from:

- user feedback, through questionnaires or interviews;
- observing users carrying out real tasks;
- how much training users needed and how often they ask for help;
- the number of input errors users make.

A system can be efficient but hard to use, for example if it needs many unclear menu steps. The two qualities are separate.

### Appropriateness

Appropriateness is about whether the solution **suits the organisation, its users and its purpose**. Is it the right kind of solution for the problem? For example, a complex system needing specialist staff may not be appropriate for a small charity, even if it works. Appropriateness also covers whether the hardware and software fit the working environment and whether outputs meet the users' needs.

## Comparing with the original task requirements

The requirements specification, written during analysis, lists what the new system must do. Evaluation goes through it point by point and records whether each requirement has been **met fully, partly or not at all**. This is the most objective part of the evaluation, because the requirements were agreed before the system was built.

### Worked example: a requirements check

A school library introduced a new system. Part of the evaluation might look like this:

| Original requirement | Evidence | Met? |
|---|---|---|
| Issue a book in under 30 seconds | Observed average of 12 seconds per issue | Met |
| Pupils can search the catalogue by author, title or subject | Search by author and title works; subject search not provided | Partly met |
| Produce a weekly list of overdue books | Report is produced, sorted by form | Met |
| Send automatic reminder emails to borrowers | Emails send, but go only to pupils, not to parents as requested | Partly met |
| Work on the existing library computers | Runs, but searches are slow on the oldest computer | Partly met |

The "partly met" rows lead directly to the next part of the evaluation.

## Limitations and necessary improvements

A **limitation** is something the system cannot do or does poorly. An **improvement** is a specific change that would remove it. Each limitation should have a matching improvement.

From the library example:

| Limitation | Necessary improvement |
|---|---|
| No subject search | Add a subject field to the book records and to the search screen |
| Reminder emails do not go to parents | Add a parent email field and include it in the reminder |
| Slow on the oldest computer | Upgrade or replace that computer, or increase its memory |

Writing "make it better" or "make it faster" is not an improvement. Say what would change.

## Evaluating users' responses to the results of testing

During and after testing, users see the system working with test data and live data. Their responses are evidence about whether the system meets their needs in practice. The evaluator:

- collects users' comments on the results of testing, for example through questionnaires, interviews or feedback forms;
- notes problems users report, such as confusing screens or outputs that do not match what they expected;
- checks whether faults found in testing were fixed and whether users are satisfied with the fixes;
- weighs this against the measurable evidence, since one person's dislike of a feature is not the same as a failure to meet a requirement.

In the library example, librarians testing the overdue report might say it is correct but hard to use because it lists pupils by form rather than by date overdue. The report meets the requirement, yet the users' response identifies a useful improvement: an option to sort by date.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: a garden centre has used its new stock system for three months. Describe how the system should be evaluated.* **[4]**

"The evaluator should compare the system with the original task requirements, checking each one to see if it has been met **[comparison with requirements]**. The efficiency could be judged by measuring how long stock checks take now compared with the old system **[efficiency, with method]**. Staff could be given a questionnaire to find out how easy they find the system to use **[ease of use, with evidence source]**. Any limitations, such as not handling seasonal plants that have no barcode, should be identified and improvements suggested, such as adding picture buttons for them **[limitation with matching improvement]**."

Each sentence uses a syllabus phrase and applies it to the garden centre.

## Common mistakes

- "Check that it works." Testing did that. Evaluation compares the system with its requirements.
- Treating efficiency and ease of use as the same thing.
- Naming a limitation without an improvement, or giving a vague improvement.
- Forgetting the users. The syllabus explicitly includes evaluating users' responses to the results of testing.
- Placing evaluation before implementation. It happens after the system has been in use.

## Exam technique

**Describe** how a system is evaluated: use the syllabus phrases (efficiency, ease of use, appropriateness, compare with requirements, limitations, improvements, users' responses) and attach each to the scenario. **Explain** why evaluation is needed: give a reason and a consequence, such as finding limitations so the system can be improved. **Evaluate** a given system: weigh evidence on both sides and reach a judgement.
