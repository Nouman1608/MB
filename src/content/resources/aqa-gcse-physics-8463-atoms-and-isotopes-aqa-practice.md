## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Give the approximate radius of an atom, in metres. **[1]**

**2.** State where most of the mass of an atom is found. **[1]**

**3.** An atom of potassium is written ³⁹₁₉K. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in the atom. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why an atom has no overall electrical charge. **[2]**

**5.** Describe what happens to an electron in an atom when the atom absorbs electromagnetic radiation. **[2]**

**6.** An atom of aluminium (atomic number 13) loses three outer electrons. State the charge on the ion formed and the number of electrons it has. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Oxygen has three naturally occurring isotopes: ¹⁶₈O, ¹⁷₈O and ¹⁸₈O.

**(a)** State what is meant by isotopes. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, using the symbols, what the three isotopes have in common and how they differ. **[3]**

**8.** An atom has a radius of 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Its nucleus has a radius of 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.

**(a)** Calculate how many times larger the radius of the atom is than the radius of the nucleus. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether this agrees with the specification's statement about nuclear size, and explain. **[1]**

**9.** Before the discovery of the electron, scientists thought atoms were tiny spheres that could not be divided.

**(a)** Describe the model of the atom that followed the discovery of the electron. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the discovery of the electron meant the earlier model had to change. **[1]**

**10.** In the alpha particle scattering experiment, alpha particles were directed at very thin gold foil.

**(a)** State what was observed for most of the alpha particles and what this showed. **[2]**
**(b)** A very small number of alpha particles bounced back. Explain why this result could not be explained by the plum pudding model. **[3]**

**11.** Describe **two** differences between the plum pudding model and the nuclear model of the atom. **[2]**

**12.** The nuclear model was later developed further.

**(a)** Describe Niels Bohr's contribution and why it was accepted. **[2]**
**(b)** State what later experiments showed about the positive charge of a nucleus. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the scientist whose experimental work provided evidence for the neutron, and state roughly how long after the nucleus was accepted this happened. **[2]**

**13.** A student says that ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₁₉K must be isotopes because they have the same mass number.

**(a)** Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of neutrons in each atom. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** About 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m [1]

**2.** In the nucleus [1]

**3.** Protons: 19 [1]; neutrons: 39 − 19 = 20 [1]; electrons: 19 [1]

**4.** The number of electrons equals the number of protons [1]; so the negative charge of the electrons cancels the positive charge of the protons [1]

**5.** The electron moves further from the nucleus [1]; to a higher energy level [1]

**6.** Charge +3 (3+) [1]; 13 − 3 = 10 electrons [1]

**7. (a)** Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons [1]
**(b)** They all have atomic number 8, so the same number of protons (8), and are all oxygen [1]; their mass numbers differ (16, 17, 18) [1]; so they have different numbers of neutrons: 8, 9 and 10 [1]

**8. (a)** (1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰) ÷ (6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵) [1]; = 2.5 × 10⁴, so **25 000 times** larger [1]
**(b)** Yes: the nucleus radius is 1/25 000 of the atom's, which is less than 1/10 000 [1]

**9. (a)** The plum pudding model [1]; a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it [1]
**(b)** Electrons are smaller particles inside atoms, so atoms could be divided and were not solid indivisible spheres [1]

**10. (a)** Most passed straight through the foil [1]; showing that most of the atom is empty space [1]
**(b)** In the plum pudding model the positive charge and mass are spread through the whole atom [1]; so at no point is there enough concentrated charge or mass to turn an alpha particle back [1]; bouncing back requires a tiny centre where the mass and positive charge are concentrated: a nucleus [1]

**11.** Any two from: in the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread through the atom, but in the nuclear model it is concentrated in the nucleus [1]; in the plum pudding model the mass is spread through the atom, but in the nuclear model most of it is in the nucleus [1]; the nuclear model is mostly empty space, while the plum pudding model has no empty space [1]

**12. (a)** He suggested electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances (energy levels) [1]; his theoretical calculations agreed with experimental observations [1]
**(b)** It could be subdivided into a whole number of smaller particles each with the same positive charge (protons) [1]
**(c)** James Chadwick [1]; about 20 years after the nucleus became accepted [1]

**13. (a)** Isotopes must be atoms of the same element, with the same number of protons [1]; these atoms have different atomic numbers (18 and 19), so they are different elements (argon and potassium) and are not isotopes [1]
**(b)** Argon: 40 − 18 = **22** neutrons [1]; potassium: 40 − 19 = **21** neutrons [1]
*Examiner insight:* The mass number alone never decides whether atoms are isotopes; always compare the bottom number first.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading the mass number as the number of protons.
- Saying isotopes have different numbers of protons.
- Writing that a positive ion has gained protons.
- Describing electrons moving closer to the nucleus when radiation is absorbed.
- Describing the scattering observations without the conclusions, or the conclusions without the observations.
