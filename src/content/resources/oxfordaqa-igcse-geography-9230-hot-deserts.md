This guide covers section 3.1.2.3, Hot deserts, in Section B (The living world) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. It has two key ideas: "Hot desert ecosystems have a range of distinctive characteristics" and "Areas on the fringe of hot deserts are at risk of desertification." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). This page goes into more depth on the desert ecosystem, and on desertification using the Sahel as an example. For desert landforms and processes, see the separate [hot desert landscapes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-hot-desert-landscapes/).

## What the specification lists

- The physical characteristics of a hot desert.
- The interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people.
- How plants and animals adapt to the physical conditions.
- Issues related to biodiversity.
- **Examples** to illustrate the causes of desertification (climate change, population growth, removal of fuel wood, overgrazing, over-cultivation and soil erosion).
- **Examples** of strategies to reduce the risk of desertification (water and soil management, tree planting and use of appropriate technology).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hot desert | A dry region, usually with less than about 250 mm of rain a year and high daytime temperatures |
| Aridity | Dryness; a lack of available water |
| Desertification | The spread of desert-like conditions into areas that were not desert, because the land degrades |
| Fringe (margin) | The edge of a desert, where land is semi-arid and used by people |
| Overgrazing | Too many animals eating the vegetation faster than it can recover |
| Over-cultivation | Growing crops so intensively that the soil is exhausted |
| Fuel wood | Wood cut for cooking and heating |
| Appropriate technology | Simple, low-cost methods that suit local needs, skills and resources |
| Biodiversity | The variety of living things |

## Physical characteristics

**Location.** Hot deserts lie mostly between about 15° and 30° north and south of the equator, on the western sides of continents and in continental interiors. Examples are the Sahara, the Arabian Desert, the Thar, the Namib and Kalahari, the Australian deserts and the Sonoran Desert. Air that rose at the equator sinks around these latitudes, creating a belt of high pressure with few clouds and little rain. This is part of the atmospheric circulation.

**Climate.** Rainfall is low, usually below 250 mm a year, and unreliable. When it comes, it often falls in short, heavy storms. Daytime temperatures can reach well over 40°C in summer. The sky is clear, so heat escapes quickly at night, and the temperature can fall sharply, sometimes close to freezing in winter. The daily temperature range is therefore large.

**Soils.** Soils are thin, sandy or stony and low in organic matter, because there is little vegetation to supply dead leaves. They drain quickly and may contain salts left behind when water evaporates.

**Water.** Surface water is rare. Many rivers flow only after rain. Oases form where groundwater reaches the surface. A few "exotic" rivers, such as the Nile, flow through deserts from wetter regions.

**Vegetation.** Plant cover is sparse. Plants are spread widely so that each one can reach enough water.

## The interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people

- The dry climate limits plant growth, so soils have little humus and stay poor.
- Poor soils hold little water, so even when it rains much runs off, and plants cannot take it up.
- Sparse plants provide limited food and shelter, so animals are few and often small, nocturnal or burrowing.
- Plant roots bind the soil. Where plants are removed, wind and flash floods erode the surface.
- People depend on scarce water and on the plants and animals the land supports. Nomadic herders move their livestock to follow the rains, which allows plants time to recover.

If one part is disturbed, for example by removing the plants, soil, water supply and animals all suffer.

## Adaptations

| Organism | Adaptation | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Cactus | Stem stores water; waxy skin; spines instead of leaves | Water is kept and loss is reduced; spines also deter animals |
| Cactus | Wide, shallow roots | Catch water quickly after rare rain |
| Acacia and mesquite | Very long tap roots | Reach deep groundwater |
| Many desert plants | Small leaves or leaf loss in drought; seeds that wait for rain | Reduce water loss; survive dry years |
| Camel | Fat stored in hump; wide feet; thick eyelashes | Energy reserve; walks on sand; protects eyes from sand |
| Fennec fox | Large ears; nocturnal; pale fur | Loses heat; avoids midday heat; reflects the sun |
| Kangaroo rat | Gets water from seeds; concentrated urine; burrows | Needs no drinking water; avoids heat |
| Reptiles | Burrow; are active at dawn and dusk; thick skin | Avoid heat; reduce water loss |

## Biodiversity

Hot deserts have fewer species than rainforests, but many species are highly specialised and found nowhere else. Biodiversity is vulnerable because:

- conditions are extreme, so small changes can push species past their limits;
- vegetation recovers slowly after damage, if at all;
- water sources such as oases are small and heavily used by people and animals;
- hunting, off-road vehicles, mining and farming damage habitats;
- climate change may raise temperatures further and alter the rainfall.

Desert species can also be valuable. Plants and animals provide food, medicines and materials, and research on their adaptations helps scientists.

## Desertification

Desertification is not the natural growth of existing deserts. It is **land degradation** in dry areas on the fringe of deserts, which makes the land less able to support plants, animals and people. The Sahel, a belt of semi-arid land south of the Sahara, stretching across Africa from Senegal to Sudan, is a well-known area at risk. Other fringe areas include parts of northern China and central Asia.

### Causes

| Cause | How it leads to desertification |
|---|---|
| Climate change | Less or more erratic rain and higher temperatures dry the soil and kill plants; long droughts, such as in the Sahel in the 1970s and 1980s, have been severe |
| Population growth | More people need food, fuel and water, putting pressure on limited land |
| Removal of fuel wood | Trees and shrubs are cut for cooking, so the soil loses cover and roots |
| Overgrazing | Too many livestock eat vegetation faster than it regrows and trample the soil |
| Over-cultivation | Crops are grown continuously with no rest, removing nutrients and leaving the soil bare and dusty |
| Soil erosion | Bare soil is blown away by wind or washed away by storms, removing the fertile topsoil |

These causes link up. Population growth leads to more grazing and more fuel wood cutting, which leads to erosion, which lowers yields, which pushes people to farm more land: a downward cycle.

### Strategies to reduce the risk

| Strategy | How it works |
|---|---|
| Water and soil management | Build stone lines or small earth walls (bunds) along the contour to slow runoff and trap water and soil; dig planting pits (known as zaï in parts of West Africa) that collect water and compost; use drip irrigation to deliver water to roots with little waste |
| Tree planting | Shelterbelts and rows of trees reduce wind speed, bind the soil and provide shade, fuel and fodder; farmers can protect and manage young trees that regrow naturally in their fields |
| Appropriate technology | Fuel-efficient stoves and solar cookers cut the need for wood; hand-dug wells, small dams and simple pumps are cheap and can be maintained locally; rotating the animals' grazing areas allows grass to recover |

### Examples from the Sahel

- In parts of **Burkina Faso and Niger**, farmers used stone lines, planting pits and the protection of naturally regrowing trees on their land. These low-cost methods have improved yields and tree cover over large areas.
- The **Great Green Wall** is an African-led initiative, launched in 2007, to restore degraded land across the Sahel. Early ideas pictured a continuous belt of trees, but the programme now focuses on a patchwork of restored land, trees, water harvesting and farming practices. Progress has been slower than hoped, partly because of cost, insecurity and difficulties in keeping trees alive.

Always check these against the examples in your own notes. Exam answers can use any suitable example, as long as it is specific.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how overgrazing can lead to desertification. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "On the edge of a desert, too many cattle or goats feed on the same area. They eat the plants faster than they can regrow, so the vegetation cover is lost. Without plant roots to bind it, the soil is dry and loose, and wind and rain remove the topsoil. The remaining soil is less fertile and holds less water, so plants cannot grow back and the land becomes desert-like."

The answer follows a chain: cause, vegetation loss, soil exposure, erosion, consequence.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing desertification as deserts naturally growing.** It is land degradation, caused partly by human activity.
- **Writing "no rain" for hot deserts.** They get low, unreliable rainfall.
- **Naming a cause without a mechanism.** "Overgrazing" alone earns little.
- **Giving a strategy without saying why it works.** Say what the stone lines or trees do.
- **Mixing up hot desert and rainforest adaptations.** Drip tips are for heavy rain; waxy skins in cacti are to hold water.
- **Using large-scale expensive strategies as the only answer.** Appropriate technology that local people can maintain is part of the specification.
