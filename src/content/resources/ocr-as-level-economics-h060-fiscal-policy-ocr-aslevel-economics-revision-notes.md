## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy |
| Direct tax | Tax on income or wealth, such as income tax |
| Indirect tax | Tax on spending, such as VAT |
| Progressive tax | Average rate rises with income |
| Proportional tax | Average rate is constant |
| Regressive tax | Average rate falls as income rises |
| Average tax rate | Total tax / total income |
| Marginal tax rate | Extra tax / extra income |
| Current expenditure | Day-to-day spending, used up within the year |
| Capital expenditure | Spending on long-lasting assets such as roads |
| Budget deficit | Spending exceeds revenue |
| Structural deficit | Deficit that would remain at normal output |
| Cyclical deficit | Deficit caused by a downturn; disappears in recovery |
| National debt | Total accumulated government borrowing |
| Automatic stabilisers | Tax and benefit changes that happen automatically with the cycle |
| Discretionary policy | Deliberate changes in tax rates or spending |
| Crowding out | Government borrowing or spending reducing private spending |
| Laffer curve | Curve linking tax rates to tax revenue |

## Formulas

- **ATR** = total tax / total income x 100.
- **MTR** = change in tax / change in income x 100.
- **Structural position** = actual position - cyclical position.
- **New debt** = old debt + deficit (or - surplus).
- **Debt ratio** = debt / nominal GDP x 100.

### Worked reminder: ATR and MTR

Invented bands: 0% to 10,000; 25% from 10,001 to 40,000; 45% above 40,000.
Income 60,000: tax = 25% x 30,000 + 45% x 20,000 = 7,500 + 9,000 = **16,500**. ATR = **27.5%**; MTR = **45%**.
Income 20,000: tax = 25% x 10,000 = **2,500**. ATR = **12.5%**; MTR = **25%**. The ATR rises with income, so the system is progressive.

### Worked reminder: MTR from two incomes

Income rises from 45,000 to 46,000 and tax from 10,500 to 10,950. MTR = 450 / 1,000 = **45%**.

### Worked reminder: structural deficit

Actual deficit 4.0% of GDP, cyclical part 1.5% of GDP: structural deficit = **2.5%**. In a boom the cyclical position might be a surplus of 1.0%; then a 2.5% structural deficit gives an actual deficit of **1.5%**.

### Worked reminder: debt ratio

Debt 600, nominal GDP 1,000: ratio **60%**. A deficit of 30 and nominal GDP growth of 6%: debt 630, GDP 1,060, ratio **59.4%**. The ratio falls even though the government borrowed, because GDP grew faster than debt.

## Stabilisers versus discretionary policy

| | Automatic stabilisers | Discretionary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a decision? | No | Yes |
| Speed | Immediate | Subject to recognition and implementation lags |
| Size | Limited | Can be large |
| Example | Falling income tax receipts in a recession | A temporary VAT cut |

## Crowding out and the Laffer curve

- **Financial crowding out:** government borrowing raises interest rates, cutting private investment.
- **Resource crowding out:** at full capacity, public use of resources leaves fewer for the private sector.
- Crowding out is **weak in a deep recession**, **strong near full capacity**.
- **Laffer curve:** revenue is zero at 0% and near zero at 100%; above the peak, a rate cut raises revenue. The position of the peak is uncertain.

## Evaluating effectiveness

- Time lags mean policy may arrive too late.
- Spare capacity decides whether higher AD raises output or prices.
- Crowding out can weaken the effect.
- High debt limits room for stimulus and may raise borrowing costs.
- Expansion can worsen the current account.
- Capital spending and tax reform can raise LRAS as well as AD.

## Exam traps

- Do not apply the top rate to the whole income.
- "Regressive" is judged by share of income, not the total paid.
- A deficit is a flow; debt is a stock.
- A rising deficit in a recession is mostly cyclical and need not mean policy has loosened.
- Transfer payments are current expenditure but not part of G in AD.
- The Laffer curve does not show that every tax cut pays for itself.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between direct and indirect taxes, with an example of each.
2. A tax takes 10% of a 20,000 income and 6% of a 60,000 income. Classify it.
3. Using the invented bands in the worked reminder, calculate the tax and ATR for an income of 50,000.
4. Give one example each of current and capital expenditure.
5. Distinguish the budget deficit from the national debt.
6. Actual deficit 5% of GDP; structural deficit 3%. What is the cyclical deficit?
7. Explain how unemployment benefits act as an automatic stabiliser.
8. Give one cause of financial crowding out.
9. When is crowding out least likely?
10. What does the Laffer curve show above the revenue-maximising rate?
11. Debt 900, GDP 1,200, deficit 60, nominal GDP growth 5%. Calculate the new debt ratio.
12. Give two reasons why discretionary fiscal policy may be ineffective.

### Answers

1. Direct taxes fall on income or wealth (income tax); indirect taxes fall on spending (VAT).
2. **Regressive**: the average rate falls as income rises.
3. 25% x 30,000 + 45% x 10,000 = 7,500 + 4,500 = **12,000**; ATR = **24%**.
4. Current: public sector wages or medicines. Capital: a new hospital or road.
5. The deficit is the shortfall of revenue in one year (flow); the debt is the total owed from past deficits (stock).
6. 5 - 3 = **2% of GDP**.
7. In a downturn more people claim benefits, so spending rises automatically, supporting incomes and AD.
8. Government borrowing raising interest rates.
9. In a deep recession with high saving and idle resources.
10. Raising the rate reduces revenue; cutting it could raise revenue.
11. Debt 960; GDP 1,260; ratio = 960 / 1,260 = **76.2%** (from 75%).
12. Any two: time lags; crowding out; temporary tax cuts saved; little spare capacity; high debt limiting scope; imports leaking demand abroad.
