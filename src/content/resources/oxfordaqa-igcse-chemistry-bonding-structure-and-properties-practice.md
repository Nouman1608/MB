## Questions

**1.** Molten lithium bromide conducts electricity. Explain why. **[2]**

**2.** Three structure diagrams are described below. For each one, state the type of structure shown.

- Diagram A: groups of four phosphorus atoms, each group joined by lines into a small tetrahedron, with empty space between the groups.
- Diagram B: a regular grid of particles labelled Li⁺ and F⁻, alternating along every row and column.
- Diagram C: silicon atoms, each joined by lines to four other silicon atoms, with bonds running off every edge of the drawing.

**[3]**

**3.** Use these values.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C | Conducts as solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| J | −95 | 69 | No | No |
| K | 772 | 1935 | No | Yes |
| L | 1538 | 2862 | Yes | Yes |
| M | 1650 | 2230 | No | No |

**(a)** Identify the type of structure of each substance J, K, L and M. **[4]**
**(b)** State the physical state of J at 25 °C. **[1]**

**4.** A student writes: "Water boils at 100 °C because that is the temperature at which the O–H bonds in the water molecules break." Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[3]**

**5.** Use these values for ammonia, NH₃. Energy needed to boil 1 mol of liquid ammonia: 23 kJ. Energy needed to break one mole of N–H bonds: 391 kJ.

**(a)** Calculate the energy needed to break all the covalent bonds in 1 mol of ammonia molecules. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times larger your answer to (a) is than the energy needed to boil 1 mol of ammonia. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what your answer to (b) shows about what happens when ammonia boils. **[2]**

**6.** This question is about metals.

**(a)** Explain why a copper wire conducts electricity. **[2]**
**(b)** An iron pan heats up quickly all over when placed on a cooker. Explain, in terms of particles, why iron is a good conductor of heat. **[2]**
**(c)** Molten lithium chloride and molten lithium metal both conduct electricity. Name the particles that carry the current in each. **[2]**

**7.** Sodium chloride is a white crystalline solid that melts at about 801 °C.

**(a)** Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. **[3]**

**8.** A student uses a battery, two electrodes and an ammeter to test five samples. Her results are shown.

| Sample | Ammeter reading / A |
|---|---|
| Solid potassium iodide | 0.00 |
| Potassium iodide dissolved in water | 0.35 |
| Glucose dissolved in water | 0.00 |
| Strip of zinc | 1.20 |
| Liquid hexane | 0.00 |

**(a)** Explain the difference between the two potassium iodide results. **[2]**
**(b)** Glucose and hexane are both simple molecular substances. Explain why neither conducts. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the zinc strip conducts. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest one variable the student should keep the same for every sample so that the readings can be compared fairly. **[1]**

**9.** An engineer has four materials. Use these values.

| Material | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C | Conducts as solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | 1455 | 2913 | Yes | Yes |
| V | −13 | 197 | No | No |
| W | 1610 | 2230 | No | No |
| X | 714 | 1412 | No | Yes |

**(a)** Identify the type of structure of each material T, V, W and X. **[4]**
**(b)** The engineer needs an electrically insulating container that stays solid while holding a molten salt at 900 °C. Choose the best material and explain your choice in terms of its structure and bonding. **[2]**
**(c)** Choose the best material for electrical wiring. Explain your choice in terms of the particles in its structure. **[2]**

**10.** Use these values. Potassium chloride, KCl, melts at 770 °C. Chlorine, Cl₂, melts at −101 °C and boils at −34 °C.

**(a)** Use ideas about structure and bonding to explain why the melting point of potassium chloride is so much higher than that of chlorine. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why molten potassium chloride conducts electricity but liquid chlorine does not. **[2]**
**(c)** State the physical state of chlorine at 25 °C. **[1]**

**11.** Use these values.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| P | −182 | −162 |
| Q | −78 | −33 |
| R | 0 | 100 |
| S | 114 | 184 |

**(a)** Which substance or substances are gases at −50 °C? **[1]**
**(b)** Which substance or substances are liquids at −50 °C? **[1]**
**(c)** Which substance or substances are liquids at 120 °C? **[1]**
**(d)** All four are simple molecular substances. Explain how their melting and boiling points support this. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Lithium bromide is ionic; when molten, its ions are free to move [1]; the moving ions carry the charge, so it conducts [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that says "electrons move" in a molten ionic compound usually scores nothing for the mechanism, so name the ions as the moving particles.

**2.** A: **simple molecular** (separate P₄ molecules) [1]. B: **giant ionic** (alternating oppositely charged ions) [1]. C: **giant covalent** (atoms joined by covalent bonds that continue in all directions) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For C, the clue is the bonds running off the edges; a diagram of covalently bonded atoms is not automatically "molecular".

**3. (a)** J: **simple molecular** (low melting and boiling points, no conduction) [1]. K: **giant ionic** (high melting point, conducts only when molten) [1]. L: **metallic** (conducts as a solid and when molten) [1]. M: **giant covalent** (very high melting point, never conducts) [1].
**(b)** 25 °C lies between −95 °C and 69 °C, so J is a **liquid** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Where a question asks you to identify from data, quoting the property that decides it (for K, conducting only when molten) makes the identification secure.

**4.** The O–H covalent bonds are **not** broken when water boils; the water molecules stay whole [1]. Boiling overcomes the **intermolecular forces** between water molecules [1]. These forces are **weak** compared with covalent bonds, which is why the boiling point is relatively low [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "The bonds are weak" is ambiguous; say which forces are weak (between molecules) and which are strong (covalent bonds within molecules).

**5. (a)** Each NH₃ molecule has three N–H bonds, so energy = 3 × 391 = **1173 kJ** [1].
**(b)** 1173 ÷ 23 = **51 times** [1].
**(c)** Boiling needs far less energy than breaking the covalent bonds, so the N–H bonds are not broken when ammonia boils [1]; only the weak intermolecular forces between ammonia molecules are overcome [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (a), forgetting that there are three bonds per molecule gives 391 kJ and carries the slip into (b), so count the bonds from the formula first.

**6. (a)** Copper contains delocalised electrons [1] that are free to move through the whole structure and carry charge [1].
**(b)** The delocalised electrons move freely through the metal [1] and carry energy quickly from the hot part to the rest of the pan [1].
**(c)** Molten lithium chloride: **ions** (Li⁺ and Cl⁻) [1]. Molten lithium: **delocalised electrons** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) is testing whether you can keep two mechanisms apart; writing "ions" for both, or "electrons" for both, loses a mark.

**7. (a)** A giant ionic lattice, a regular repeating arrangement of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions [1]; every ion has six nearest neighbours of opposite charge [1].
**(b)** There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions [1]; there are many of these forces, acting in all directions through the lattice [1]; overcoming them takes a lot of energy, so the melting point is high [1].
*Examiner insight:* The third mark in (b) depends on the energy link; "strong bonds so high melting point" leaves it out.

**8. (a)** In solid potassium iodide the ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move [1]; when dissolved, the ions are free to move and carry the current [1].
**(b)** The molecules have no overall electric charge, so there are no charged particles to carry a current [1].
**(c)** Zinc is a metal with delocalised electrons that are free to move and carry charge [1].
**(d)** Any one of: the same battery voltage, the same distance between the electrodes, or the same depth the electrodes are dipped into the sample [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (d), "keep it fair" alone gains nothing; credit goes to a named variable that would actually change the reading.

**9. (a)** T: **metallic** [1]. V: **simple molecular** [1]. W: **giant covalent** [1]. X: **giant ionic** [1].
**(b)** **W**: it is giant covalent, with strong covalent bonds between all its atoms, so its melting point (1610 °C) is far above 900 °C [1]; it has no ions or delocalised electrons free to move, so it does not conduct [1].
**(c)** **T**: it is a metal, so it has delocalised electrons [1]; these drift through the metal when a voltage is applied, carrying the current [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b), T and X are wrong for different reasons (T conducts; X would melt at 900 °C), so check every condition in the question against the data before choosing.

**10. (a)** Potassium chloride is a giant ionic lattice with strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in all directions [1]; melting it means overcoming many of these strong forces, which needs a large amount of energy [1]. Chlorine is made of simple Cl₂ molecules [1]; melting it overcomes only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules, which needs little energy, and the Cl–Cl covalent bonds are not broken [1].
**(b)** In molten KCl the K⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are free to move and carry the current [1]; Cl₂ molecules have no overall charge, so liquid chlorine has no charged particles to carry a current [1].
**(c)** 25 °C is above −34 °C, so chlorine is a **gas** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs a point about each substance; an answer that describes only potassium chloride cannot reach full marks in (a).

**11. (a)** **P** only (−50 °C is above its boiling point of −162 °C) [1].
**(b)** **Q** only (−78 < −50 < −33) [1].
**(c)** **S** only (114 < 120 < 184) [1].
**(d)** They all have relatively low melting and boiling points, showing that only weak intermolecular forces are overcome [1].
*Examiner insight:* Questions with negative temperatures are a common source of slips; writing the inequality, as in (b), shows your reasoning and catches errors.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that the covalent bonds inside molecules break on melting or boiling.
- Naming electrons as the charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds.
- Explaining a high melting point without saying that a large amount of energy is needed.
- Missing "oppositely charged" or "in all directions" when describing forces in an ionic lattice.
- Writing "strong intermolecular forces" for a giant covalent substance such as silicon dioxide.
- Answering only one side of a comparison question.
- Identifying a structure from data without quoting the property that decides it.
- Misordering negative temperatures when deciding states.
