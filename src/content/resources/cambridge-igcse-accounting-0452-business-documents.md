This guide covers sub-topic **2.2 Business documents**. The syllabus names eight documents: "invoice, debit note, credit note, cheque counterfoil, paying-in slip, receipt, bank statement, statement of account". You need to know how each is "used as sources of information" and that documents may be "produced and recorded manually or digitally".

The examples follow two invented businesses: Corvo Kitchenware, a wholesaler, and its credit customer Linden Café. Amounts are in dollars. Use this guide with the [2.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-business-documents-revision-notes/) and [2.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-business-documents-practice/). The documents feed the [books of prime entry](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-books-of-prime-entry/), and the broader [business documents and books of prime entry guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-business-documents-and-books-of-prime-entry/) covers both sub-topics together.

## 1. Why source documents matter

A **source document** is the first written or digital evidence of a transaction. Nothing should be entered in the books without one, because it:

- gives the facts to record: date, parties, quantities, prices and amounts;
- proves that the transaction happened, which matters if a customer disputes a bill or the tax authority asks questions;
- lets the records be checked later, by tracing an entry back to its document.

## 2. The eight documents

| Document | Prepared by | Given to | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Invoice | Seller | Credit customer | Goods or services supplied, prices, trade discount, amount owed, credit terms |
| Debit note | Buyer | Seller | Goods returned or an overcharge, asking for the amount owed to be reduced |
| Credit note | Seller | Buyer | The reduction in the amount owed, for returns or an overcharge |
| Cheque counterfoil | Payer, when writing a cheque | Kept by the payer | Date, payee and amount of the cheque |
| Paying-in slip | Business paying money into its bank | Bank, with a counterfoil kept by the business | Cash and cheques deposited |
| Receipt | Person receiving money | Person paying | Evidence that payment was made |
| Bank statement | Bank | Account holder | Every entry on the account from the bank's records, and the balance |
| Statement of account | Seller, usually monthly | Credit customer | Opening balance, invoices, credit notes, payments and the balance owed |

## 3. How each document is used as a source

The same document gives rise to different entries for the two parties.

| Document | Seller's book of prime entry | Buyer's book of prime entry |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice | Sales journal | Purchases journal |
| Credit note | Sales returns journal | Purchases returns journal |
| Debit note | Supports the return; the seller records the credit note it issues | Purchases returns journal (from its copy of the debit note or the credit note received) |
| Receipt | Copy supports cash received in the cash book | Evidence for a payment in the cash book or petty cash book |
| Cheque counterfoil | | Credit side of the cash book, bank column |
| Paying-in slip counterfoil | Debit side of the cash book, bank column | |
| Bank statement | Updates the cash book for items not yet recorded, such as bank charges and direct debits; used for bank reconciliation | Same |
| Statement of account | Not a book of prime entry source | Checked against the supplier's account in the purchases ledger; prompts payment |

Two documents are **checking documents** rather than sources of new entries in the usual way. The **statement of account** repeats transactions already recorded, so the buyer compares it with its own records and investigates differences. The **bank statement** is compared with the cash book; only items the business did not know about, such as bank charges, are then entered from it (see [bank reconciliation](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-bank-reconciliation/)).

## 4. Worked example 1: an invoice, a credit note and a statement

On 4 March 2027 Corvo Kitchenware supplies Linden Café with 40 frying pans at a list price of 28 each and 25 knife sets at 16 each. Linden is allowed 15% trade discount. The invoice states "Terms: 2.5% cash discount if paid within 14 days".

| Line | Working | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Frying pans | 40 x 28 | 1,120.00 |
| Knife sets | 25 x 16 | 400.00 |
| Total at list price | 1,120 + 400 | 1,520.00 |
| Less trade discount 15% | 1,520 x 15% | 228.00 |
| **Invoice total** | 1,520 - 228 | **1,292.00** |

Corvo enters **1,292.00** in its sales journal and Linden enters 1,292.00 in its purchases journal. The trade discount of 228 is never entered in either business's books: it is a price reduction for trade customers, not a transaction.

If Linden paid within 14 days, the cash discount would be 1,292 x 2.5% = 32.30 and the payment 1,259.70. The cash discount *is* recorded, but only when payment is made, in the discount columns of the cash book.

**The credit note.** On 9 March Linden returns four damaged pans. It sends a debit note; Corvo replies with a credit note. The credit note must deduct the same trade discount as the invoice:

4 x 28 = 112.00, less 15% (16.80) = **95.20**.

A common error is to credit 112.00, which would reduce Linden's debt by more than it was charged for the pans.

**The statement of account.** On 1 March Linden already owed 640 from February. It paid that 640 by cheque on 10 March. Corvo's statement at 31 March shows:

| Date | Details | Debit | Credit | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Mar | Balance | | | 640.00 |
| 4 Mar | Invoice | 1,292.00 | | 1,932.00 |
| 9 Mar | Credit note | | 95.20 | 1,836.80 |
| 10 Mar | Cheque received | | 640.00 | 1,196.80 |

Linden owes **1,196.80**. If it pays this on 5 April, it is outside the 14 days from 4 March, so no cash discount can be taken. Linden checks each line against Corvo's account in its own purchases ledger before paying.

## 5. Worked example 2: from Linden Café's documents to its records

| Document Linden holds | Entry it supports |
|---|---|
| Invoice from Corvo, 1,292.00 | Purchases journal; then debit Purchases, credit Corvo |
| Copy of debit note and Corvo's credit note, 95.20 | Purchases returns journal; then debit Corvo, credit Purchases returns |
| Cheque counterfoil, 640.00 to Corvo | Cash book, credit side, bank column; then debit Corvo |
| Paying-in slip counterfoil, 2,150 of café takings | Cash book, debit side, bank column |
| Receipt from a plumber paid 85 in cash | Cash book or petty cash book payment, depending on who paid |
| Bank statement showing a 24 bank charge | Cash book, credit side, bank column; then debit Bank charges |

The paying-in slip deserves care. If the 2,150 had already been recorded as cash sales in the cash column, banking it is a **contra**: debit bank column, credit cash column. If the takings went straight to the bank, the paying-in slip is the source for recording them as sales.

## 6. Manual and digital documents

Documents can be paper or digital, and the syllabus expects you to recognise both.

| Manual | Digital equivalent |
|---|---|
| Printed invoice posted to the customer | Invoice emailed as a file or sent from accounting software |
| Cheque and counterfoil | Online bank transfer, with an onscreen confirmation |
| Paying-in slip | Card takings and transfers paid directly into the bank account |
| Paper receipt | Emailed receipt or card terminal slip |
| Printed bank statement | Online statement downloaded from the bank |

**Benefits of digital documents:** they arrive instantly; software can record an invoice without retyping it; they are easy to search, copy and store; and less paper is used.

**Limitations:** they depend on equipment, power and internet access; files can be lost, deleted or accessed by hackers; and an emailed invoice can be recorded twice if it is also posted. Whatever the form, the document must still be kept as evidence, and the accounting entry is the same.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the buyer sends the credit note. The **seller** issues credit notes; the buyer sends debit notes.
- Recording trade discount in the books, or failing to deduct it on a credit note.
- Deducting cash discount on the invoice total recorded in the sales journal.
- Treating a statement of account as a new transaction and recording it again.
- Confusing the cheque counterfoil (kept by the payer) with a receipt (given by the person paid).

## Exam technique

- **Identify** ("name/select/recognise") questions often give a situation and ask for the document. Decide first who issues it, then what it records.
- **Explain** the use of a document by naming the book of prime entry, the side and the column, then the ledger posting it leads to.
- **Calculate** invoice and credit note totals step by step: quantity x price, total, trade discount, net. Show the discount amount separately.
- When asked about digital documents, give a benefit or limitation linked to the business in the question, for example the café's internet connection.
