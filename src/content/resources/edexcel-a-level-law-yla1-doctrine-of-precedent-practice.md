These questions cover content area 1.2.7, "The doctrine of precedent in the court hierarchy", including the process of appeal and the 1966 House of Lords Practice Statement.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the meaning of *stare decisis*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between ratio decidendi and obiter dicta. **[4]**

**3.** Identify **three** sources of persuasive precedent. **[3]**

**4.** State the **three** exceptions in *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co*. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between overruling and reversing. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe the route of appeal for a defendant convicted in the Crown Court who wishes to take the case as far as the Supreme Court. **[4]**

**7.** A High Court judge is deciding a negligence claim. There is an earlier High Court decision on almost identical facts, a Court of Appeal decision on similar facts, and a Privy Council decision that takes a different view from the Court of Appeal.

Explain which of these the judge must follow and which are only persuasive. **[5]**

**8.** Explain, using **two** decided cases, how the House of Lords used the 1966 Practice Statement. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) may depart from its own previous decisions more readily than the Civil Division. **[3]**

**10.** In 2019 the Court of Appeal (Civil Division) decided case P. In 2025 the Supreme Court decided case Q, laying down a principle that conflicts with P. A new case on the same issue now reaches the Court of Appeal.

Advise the Court of Appeal on whether it is bound by P. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a judge can use distinguishing to avoid following a binding precedent, using **one** pair of decided cases. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the use of the 1966 Practice Statement by the House of Lords and the Supreme Court. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** *Stare decisis* means "stand by what has been decided" [1]: courts must follow the legal principles of earlier decisions of higher courts (and in some cases their own) where the material facts are the same, so like cases are treated alike [1].

**2.** The **ratio decidendi** is the legal principle on which the decision is based, applied to the material facts [1], and it is **binding** on lower courts [1]. **Obiter dicta** are other things said by the judge, such as comments on hypothetical facts or dissenting views [1]; they are only **persuasive**, though they may later be followed, as in *R v Gotts* [1992] adopting obiter from *R v Howe* [1987] [1].

**3.** Any three [1] each: obiter dicta; decisions of lower courts; decisions of the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council; decisions of courts in other common law countries; dissenting judgments; decisions of the European Court of Human Rights (taken into account under s 2 HRA 1998).

**4.** One mark each: two **conflicting** Court of Appeal decisions, when it may choose [1]; a previous decision **inconsistent with a later House of Lords or Supreme Court** decision [1]; a decision made ***per incuriam*** [1].

**5.** **Overruling** happens when a court decides that the legal rule in an **earlier, different case** was wrong [1]. **Reversing** happens when a higher court on appeal changes the decision **in the same case** [1].

**6.** Appeal from the Crown Court to the **Court of Appeal (Criminal Division)** [1], with **leave** [1]. Further appeal to the **Supreme Court** only if the Court of Appeal certifies a **point of law of general public importance** [1] and leave is granted by the Court of Appeal or the Supreme Court [1].

**7.** The judge is **bound by the Court of Appeal** decision, because the Court of Appeal is higher in the hierarchy [1], provided its ratio applies to the material facts [1]. The **earlier High Court decision is persuasive only**, because High Court judges are not bound by each other [1]. The **Privy Council decision is persuasive** [1], unless the Privy Council expressly directed English courts to follow it, as permitted by *Willers v Joyce* [2016], in which case it would take priority over the conflicting Court of Appeal decision [1].

**8.** Any two cases, three marks each (case and what was overruled [1], explanation [1], reason it was right to depart [1]):

- ***BRB v Herrington* [1972]** overruled *Addie v Dumbreck* [1929] [1]: occupiers now owed a duty of common humanity to child trespassers [1], because social conditions and attitudes had changed since 1929 [1].
- ***R v Shivpuri* [1986]** overruled *Anderton v Ryan* [1985] [1]: attempting the impossible can be a criminal attempt [1]; the earlier decision had misread the Criminal Attempts Act 1981 and was corrected within a year [1].
- ***R v G* [2003]** overruled *MPC v Caldwell* [1982] [1]: recklessness requires the defendant personally to foresee the risk [1], because the objective test was unjust to young or vulnerable defendants [1].
- ***Pepper v Hart* [1993]** [1]: courts may consult *Hansard* [1], to identify Parliament's intention where legislation is ambiguous [1].

**9.** The Criminal Division deals with a person's **liberty** [1], so it will not follow a previous decision where the law was **misapplied or misunderstood** if doing so would cause injustice to the defendant [1], as recognised in *R v Taylor* [1950] and *R v Spencer* [1985] [1].

**10.** The Court of Appeal is normally **bound by its own previous decisions** [1] (*Young v Bristol Aeroplane* [1944]). However, the second exception applies: where a previous Court of Appeal decision is **inconsistent with a later Supreme Court decision** [1], the Court of Appeal must not follow it [1]. It should therefore **follow case Q**, not case P [1].

**11.** Distinguishing means a judge finds that the **material facts** of the present case are sufficiently different from those of the precedent [1], so its ratio does not apply and need not be followed [1]. In *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] an agreement between a married couple living together had no intention to create legal relations [1]; in *Merritt v Merritt* [1970] the court distinguished it because the couple had **separated**, so the agreement was binding [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: the position before 1966 (*London Street Tramways* [1898]); the terms of the Statement; its application to the Supreme Court (*Austin v Southwark* [2010]); cases where it was used (*Conway v Rimmer*, *Herrington*, *Miliangos*, *Shivpuri*, *Pepper v Hart*, *R v G*) and refused (*Jones*, *Knuller*).
- **Strengths**: allows errors to be corrected (*Shivpuri*); lets the law keep pace with social change (*Herrington*) and economic change (*Miliangos*); avoids waiting years for Parliament.
- **Weaknesses**: reduces certainty; retrospective effect on parties who relied on the old law; unelected judges changing the law; used rarely, so bad precedents may survive (*Jones*); cases reach the Supreme Court only if a party can afford to appeal.
- **Evaluation**: weigh flexibility against certainty and conclude, for example, that its sparing use has struck a sensible balance, though its effectiveness depends on suitable cases reaching the court.

Top-level answers use cases accurately, consider both sides and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the Supreme Court is bound by its own decisions.
- Treating obiter dicta as binding.
- Confusing overruling and reversing.
- Naming Practice Statement cases without explaining what changed and why.
- Describing appeal routes without the permission or point-of-law requirements.
