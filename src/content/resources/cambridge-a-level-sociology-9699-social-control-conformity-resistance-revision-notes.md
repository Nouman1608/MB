Condensed notes for section 1.2, *Social control, conformity and resistance*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 1. The [1.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-control-conformity-resistance/) has the full explanations and a worked answer; test yourself with the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-control-conformity-resistance-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Structure and agency, including the differences between structuralist and interactionist views.
- Why individuals conform: sanctions, social pressure, self-interest and social exchange.
- How order is maintained: power, ideology, force and consensus.
- Explanations of deviance and non-conformity: subcultures, under-socialisation, marginalisation, cultural deprivation and social resistance.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Structure | Patterned social arrangements (institutions, class, norms) that constrain individuals |
| Agency | People's capacity to make choices and act on them |
| Structuralist view | Society shapes the individual; theories such as functionalism and Marxism |
| Interactionist view | Individuals create society through meanings formed in interaction |
| Structuration | Giddens (1984): structures constrain action and are reproduced by it |
| Social control | Processes that encourage conformity to norms |
| Sanction | A reward (positive) or punishment (negative) for behaviour |
| Authority | Power accepted as legitimate |
| Hegemony | Ruling-class leadership based on winning consent through ideas |
| False consciousness | Accepting ideas that work against your own class interests |
| Deviance | Behaviour that breaks a group's norms |
| Marginalisation | Being pushed to the edge of society with little voice or stake |
| Relative deprivation | Feeling deprived compared with others or with expectations |
| Label | A definition applied to a person that can shape how others treat them and how they see themselves |

## Structure versus agency

- **Structuralist**: Durkheim (1895): social facts are external and constraining. Society comes first; methods tend to be large-scale.
- **Interactionist**: Blumer (1969): people act on meanings created in interaction. The individual comes first; methods tend to be qualitative.
- **Bridges**: Weber's social action theory; Giddens' structuration and the "duality of structure".
- **Critique each**: structuralists make people passive; interactionists ignore inequality and power.

## Sanctions grid

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | Promotion, award, certificate | Fine, prison, exclusion |
| Informal | Praise, acceptance | Gossip, ridicule, ostracism |

## Four reasons for conformity

1. **Sanctions**: rewards and punishments.
2. **Social pressure**: from equals and from authority; Milgram's obedience studies (1963).
3. **Self-interest**: people weigh what they would lose by deviating.
4. **Social exchange**: Blau (1964), people conform to keep valued exchanges of help, approval and status; dependence creates power.

Functionalist addition: most conformity comes from internalised values, not calculation.

## Mechanisms of order

| Mechanism | Argument | Theory |
|---|---|---|
| Power and authority | Weber: traditional, charismatic, rational-legal authority make power legitimate | Weberian |
| Ideology | Ruling ideas make inequality seem fair; false consciousness | Marxist |
| Force | The state claims a monopoly of legitimate force (Weber, 1919) | Weberian, Marxist |
| Consensus | Shared values; Durkheim's collective conscience | Functionalist |
| Surveillance | Foucault (1975): the panopticon; people discipline themselves | Post-structuralist |

Evaluation: societies combine mechanisms; heavy use of force usually signals weak consent.

## Explaining deviance

| Factor | Core idea | Name |
|---|---|---|
| Under-socialisation | Weak socialisation, often blamed on family breakdown | New Right underclass arguments |
| Cultural deprivation | Group values that clash with mainstream institutions | Miller (1958) focal concerns |
| Subcultures | Alternative values, often from blocked opportunities | Merton (1938) strain; Cloward and Ohlin (1960) |
| Marginalisation | Exclusion and lack of voice | Lea and Young (1984) |
| Social resistance | Deviance as symbolic challenge | Hall and Jefferson (1976) |
| Labelling (interactionist) | Deviance is created by others' reactions | Becker (1963) |

**Merton's adaptations**: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion.

## Exam traps

- **Deviance is not crime.** Many deviant acts are legal and some crimes are widely tolerated.
- **Missing the interactionist side.** The syllabus asks directly for structuralist *and* interactionist views.
- **Treating the four reasons for conformity as one.** Sanctions and pressure act on people; self-interest and exchange involve calculation.
- **Force alone.** Order mostly rests on consent; say so.
- **Romanticising resistance.** Resistance theories can overlook harm to victims; evaluate.
- **One theory of deviance for every case.** Match the explanation to the group or behaviour in the question.

## Self-test

1. Define *agency*.
2. What did Durkheim mean by social facts?
3. What is the "duality of structure"?
4. Give an example of an informal negative sanction.
5. What did Milgram's experiments suggest about conformity?
6. How does social exchange explain conformity?
7. Name Weber's three types of authority.
8. What is false consciousness?
9. What does Foucault's panopticon illustrate?
10. Name Merton's five adaptations to strain.
11. What three factors did Lea and Young combine to explain crime?
12. What did Becker argue about deviance in *Outsiders* (1963)?

### Answers

1. The capacity of individuals to make choices and act on them.
2. Ways of acting and thinking that exist outside individuals and constrain them.
3. Giddens' idea that structures both limit action and are reproduced, and changed, by it.
4. Gossip, ridicule or being ignored by friends.
5. Many people obey an authority figure even when asked to harm others, so pressure from authority produces conformity.
6. People conform to keep up valued exchanges of help, approval and status; those who depend on others' rewards become subject to their power (Blau).
7. Traditional, charismatic, rational-legal.
8. Accepting ideas, such as the fairness of inequality, that work against your own class interests.
9. Control through constant possible surveillance, so people discipline themselves.
10. Conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion.
11. Relative deprivation, marginalisation and subculture.
12. Deviance is not a quality of the act but results from others applying the label "deviant".
