## Half-equations

In the IUPAC convention, an electrode reaction is written as a **reduction**, with the electrons on the left:

```
oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form
Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s)
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s)
Fe³⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺(aq)
MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)
```

The electrode potential, E, is the potential of the half-cell written in this direction. A more positive E means that the oxidised form is a stronger oxidising agent, and the reduced form is a weaker reducing agent.

## A half-cell and a cell

A **half-cell** is a metal in contact with a solution of its ions (or an inert platinum electrode in a solution containing two oxidation states of an ion, such as Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺). Two half-cells joined by a **salt bridge** (filter paper soaked in potassium nitrate solution) and a **high-resistance voltmeter** make a cell. The salt bridge completes the circuit by allowing ions to flow, without mixing the solutions. The voltmeter has a high resistance so that almost no current flows, so the reading is the maximum potential difference, the EMF.

### Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)

The potential of a single half-cell cannot be measured alone, so each is compared with the standard hydrogen electrode, which is given the value **0.00 V**. The SHE consists of:

- a platinum electrode coated with platinum black,
- hydrogen gas at **100 kPa**,
- a solution containing H⁺ ions at **1.00 mol dm⁻³** (for example, hydrochloric acid),
- a temperature of **298 K**.

The half-equation is H⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ ½H₂(g).

The standard electrode potential, **E⦵**, is the EMF of a cell in which the half-cell under test, with all ions at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ and at 298 K (and gases at 100 kPa), is connected to the SHE.

### Why the conditions matter

An electrode potential depends on the concentrations of the ions, the temperature and, for gases, the pressure. It follows from Le Chatelier's principle: for Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s), a lower concentration of Cu²⁺ shifts the position to the left, making the electrode less positive. The Nernst equation is not required, but the direction of the change should be predicted.

## The electrochemical series

A table of standard electrode potentials, from the most negative to the most positive:

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg | −2.37 |
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn | −0.76 |
| Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe | −0.44 |
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ | 0.00 |
| Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn²⁺ | +0.15 |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu | +0.34 |
| I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻ | +0.54 |
| Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ | +0.77 |
| Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag | +0.80 |
| Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻ | +1.07 |
| Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻ | +1.36 |
| MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O | +1.51 |

Reading the table: species on the left of a more positive half-equation are better oxidising agents; species on the right of a more negative half-equation are better reducing agents.

## Cell diagrams

The conventional representation puts the **oxidation** half-cell (the negative electrode) on the **left** and the **reduction** half-cell (the positive electrode) on the right. A single vertical line shows a phase boundary. A double vertical line shows the salt bridge. Where two species are in the same solution, a comma separates them. An inert platinum electrode is shown at the end.

```
Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)
Pt(s) | Fe²⁺(aq), Fe³⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)
```

### The EMF of a cell

**EMF = E⦵(right-hand electrode) − E⦵(left-hand electrode)**

This is the same as E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode), taking each potential as written for reduction. If the right-hand electrode is the more positive, the EMF is positive.

### Worked example 1: the zinc–copper cell

```
Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)
EMF = E⦵(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E⦵(Zn²⁺/Zn) = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = +1.10 V
```

At the left electrode (negative), zinc is oxidised: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. At the right (positive), copper(II) is reduced: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The overall reaction is Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Electrons flow in the external circuit from zinc to copper.

### Worked example 2: a cell with a platinum electrode

```
Pt(s) | Fe²⁺(aq), Fe³⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)
EMF = (+0.80) − (+0.77) = +0.03 V
```

The iron(II)/iron(III) electrode needs a platinum wire because there is no metal to conduct.

### Worked example 3: choosing which side

A cell uses the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺/Cu half-cells. Which is the positive electrode, and what is the EMF?

Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ is more positive (+0.77 against +0.34), so it is the positive electrode, and copper is the negative electrode. Written conventionally:

```
Cu(s) | Cu²⁺(aq) || Fe³⁺(aq), Fe²⁺(aq) | Pt(s)
EMF = (+0.77) − (+0.34) = +0.43 V
```

The overall reaction is Cu + 2Fe³⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Fe²⁺.

## Predicting the direction of a reaction

A reaction is feasible if the EMF of the cell formed from its two half-reactions is **positive**. The half-reaction with the more positive E⦵ is reduced (it is the oxidising agent); the other is reversed and oxidised.

### Worked example 4: predictions

| Question | Working | Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Does copper reduce silver ions? Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag | E⦵(Ag⁺/Ag) − E⦵(Cu²⁺/Cu) = 0.80 − 0.34 = +0.46 V | Feasible |
| Does Fe³⁺ oxidise iodide? 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂ | 0.77 − 0.54 = +0.23 V | Feasible |
| Does I₂ oxidise Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺? | 0.54 − 0.77 = −0.23 V | Not feasible |
| Does acidified manganate(VII) oxidise chloride? | 1.51 − 1.36 = +0.15 V | Feasible |
| Does zinc react with dilute acid? Zn + 2H⁺ → Zn²⁺ + H₂ | 0.00 − (−0.76) = +0.76 V | Feasible |
| Does copper react with dilute acid? | 0.00 − 0.34 = −0.34 V | Not feasible |

Note that the stoichiometry does not change E⦵: the electrode potential does not depend on how many moles are in the equation.

### Worked example 5: the manganate(VII) and iron(II) reaction

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺. EMF = 1.51 − 0.77 = +0.74 V, so the reaction is feasible. This is why manganate(VII) is used in redox titrations of iron(II).

### Limits to the predictions

A positive EMF shows that a reaction is **feasible**, not that it is fast. Some predicted reactions have a high activation energy and do not occur noticeably. Also, the values are for standard conditions; with other concentrations or temperatures the actual EMF differs, and for small standard EMFs the prediction may be reversed.

## Practical: measuring an EMF (Required practical 8)

1. Prepare each half-cell: a clean metal strip in a solution of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ of its ions at the same temperature.
2. Make a salt bridge from filter paper soaked in saturated potassium nitrate solution.
3. Connect the metals to a high-resistance voltmeter and record the reading.
4. Repeat and average; clean the metal first (emery paper) to remove oxide.

Precautions: use fresh solutions, avoid contamination of the salt bridge, and take the reading at a steady value.

## Common mistakes

- Writing half-equations with electrons on the right.
- Reversing the subtraction (left minus right).
- Multiplying E⦵ by the number of electrons or by the coefficients.
- Leaving out the platinum electrode for ion/ion half-cells.
- Putting the double line in the wrong place or omitting it.
- Forgetting the conditions in the definition of E⦵: 298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³.
- Saying that a positive EMF shows a fast reaction.
- Using a low-resistance voltmeter, or a salt bridge made of a chloride that reacts.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** E⦵: mention the SHE, the conditions and "EMF".
- **Write** a cell diagram: the oxidation half-cell on the left, the reduction half-cell on the right.
- **Calculate** the EMF: right minus left, with sign and units (V).
- **Predict** a reaction: calculate the EMF for the proposed reaction and say whether it is positive.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why a salt bridge and not a piece of wire?**
A wire cannot carry ions. The salt bridge completes the circuit by allowing ions to move.

**Why is the voltmeter of high resistance?**
So that almost no current flows, and the reading is the EMF.

**Does the stoichiometry change E⦵?**
No. E⦵ is an intensive property.
