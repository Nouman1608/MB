This study guide teaches section **9.2 Interpreting statistical data** from Topic 9 Statistics of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The syllabus has three learning outcomes:

1. "Read, interpret and draw inferences from tables and statistical diagrams."
2. "Compare sets of data using tables, graphs and statistical measures."
3. "Appreciate restrictions on drawing conclusions from given data."

The outcomes are the same for Core (C9.2) and Extended (E9.2). Only the note to outcome 2 differs: Core compares "averages and ranges", while Extended compares "averages and measures of spread", which includes the interquartile range. Sections marked **(Extended)** below are for Extended candidates only.

The [classifying and interpreting data study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-0580-classifying-and-interpreting-data/) shows how to read a two-way table and a dual bar chart. This page adds four skills that questions test often: judging statements as true, false or impossible to tell; choosing the right average when one value is extreme; spotting a misleading diagram; and separating proportions from numbers. Practise them with the [interpreting data revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-interpreting-statistical-data-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [interpreting data practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-interpreting-statistical-data-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## Read, interpret, infer: three different jobs

- **Read**: take a value straight from the table or diagram.
- **Interpret**: say what a value or pattern means in the context of the question.
- **Infer**: make a statement the data support but do not show directly.

A strong answer always says *what the data show*, not only a number. "The class 4 ≤ h < 6 has frequency 14" is reading. "More students do 4 to 6 hours of homework than any other amount" is interpreting.

## Judging statements: true, false or cannot tell

A favourite question type gives a table and a list of statements. Every statement is true, false, or impossible to decide from the data. "Cannot tell" is a correct answer, and examiners use it to test whether you know what a table does **not** contain.

### Worked example 1

The table shows the time 40 Year 10 students spent on homework last week.

```
Time h (hours)    Frequency
0 ≤ h < 2             3
2 ≤ h < 4             8
4 ≤ h < 6            14
6 ≤ h < 8            10
8 ≤ h < 10            5
Total                40
```

Decide whether each statement is true, false, or cannot be told from the data.

**(a)** "Most of the students did at least 4 hours of homework." 14 + 10 + 5 = 29 out of 40, which is more than half. **True.**

**(b)** "Three students did no homework." The class 0 ≤ h < 2 contains 3 students, but any of them could have done 1.5 hours. **Cannot tell.**

**(c)** "More than a quarter of the students did less than 4 hours." 3 + 8 = 11, and 11/40 = 27.5%, which is more than 25%. **True.**

**(d)** "Nobody did 10 hours or more." The classes stop at 10 and the frequencies add to 40, so every student is in a class below 10 hours. **True.**

**(e)** "Year 10 students do more homework than Year 11 students." There are no Year 11 data. **Cannot tell.**

Statements (b) and (e) fail for different reasons. In (b) the grouping has hidden the individual values. In (e) the data are about a different group from the claim.

## Comparing two data sets

The syllabus asks you to compare using "statistical measures". A full comparison has two parts:

1. compare an **average** (mean, median or mode), and
2. compare a **spread** (range, or for Extended candidates the interquartile range),

and each part must say which data set is higher and what that means in the context.

### Worked example 2: when the mean misleads

Two online shops each made 11 deliveries to the same town. The delivery times, in days, are:

```
Shop A:  1  2  2  3  3  3  3  4  4  5  12
Shop B:  2  2  3  3  3  4  4  4  4  5   5
```

**Core comparison: averages and ranges.**

```
Shop A: mean = 42 ÷ 11 = 3.82 days (3 s.f.)   median = 3 days   range = 12 − 1 = 11 days
Shop B: mean = 39 ÷ 11 = 3.55 days (3 s.f.)   median = 4 days   range = 5 − 2 = 3 days
```

The mean says Shop A is slower; the median says Shop A is faster. Which is right? Shop A's mean is pulled up by one delivery of 12 days. Ten of Shop A's eleven deliveries took 5 days or less, and its typical delivery took 3 days. When one value is far from the rest, the **median** gives the better picture of a typical value.

A good written comparison:

- "Shop A's median delivery time (3 days) is lower than Shop B's (4 days), so a typical delivery from Shop A is quicker."
- "Shop A's range (11 days) is much bigger than Shop B's (3 days), so Shop A's delivery times are less consistent."

**(Extended) Using the interquartile range.** With 11 ordered values, the lower quartile is the 3rd value and the upper quartile is the 9th.

```
Shop A: LQ = 2, UQ = 4, IQR = 2 days
Shop B: LQ = 3, UQ = 4, IQR = 1 day
```

The IQR ignores the single 12-day delivery. It still shows Shop B is more consistent, but the gap (2 against 1) is far smaller than the ranges suggest (11 against 3). The range uses only the two most extreme values, so a single unusual delivery can change it a lot.

## Misleading diagrams

Reading a diagram includes checking whether it gives a fair picture.

### Worked example 3

A company's bar chart shows sales of 420 units in January and 480 units in February. The vertical axis starts at 400, not at 0. The February bar looks four times as tall as the January bar.

**(a)** Explain why the chart is misleading. The bars show only the amount above 400: 20 for January and 80 for February. So the February bar is four times as tall even though February's sales are nowhere near four times January's.

**(b)** Find the actual percentage increase.

```
increase = 480 − 420 = 60
percentage increase = 60 ÷ 420 × 100 = 14.3% (3 s.f.)
```

Other features that make diagrams misleading:

- a frequency axis with an uneven scale;
- pictogram symbols of different sizes, or no key;
- a pie chart drawn in 3D, which makes the front sectors look bigger;
- no labels or units, so you cannot tell what is being measured.

## Proportions and numbers

A percentage, a fraction or a pie chart shows a **proportion** of a group. To compare actual numbers you need the size of each group.

### Worked example 4

At School X, 30% of the 240 students walk to school. At School Y, 15% of the 600 students walk. A newspaper says "Students at School X are more likely to walk, so more students walk to School X."

```
School X: 30% of 240 = 72 walkers
School Y: 15% of 600 = 90 walkers
```

The first part of the claim is correct: a higher proportion walk at School X. The second part is wrong: more students walk to School Y. Always ask whether a claim is about a proportion ("more likely", "a greater percentage") or about a number ("more students").

## Restrictions on drawing conclusions

Before agreeing with any conclusion, test it against these questions.

- **Is the sample large enough?** A pattern in 10 people is weak evidence.
- **Is it representative?** Asking only one class, one shop or one time of day can bias the results.
- **Does the claim go beyond the data?** Predicting next year from three months of figures, or applying data from one town to a whole country, is unreliable.
- **Has information been lost?** Grouped tables hide individual values; an average alone hides the spread.
- **Are like and like being compared?** A mean from one set and a median from another, or numbers from groups of different sizes, do not compare fairly.

When a question says "Give a reason why this conclusion may not be correct", one clear reason linked to the context earns the mark. A general phrase such as "the data might be wrong" does not.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "true" or "false" when the data cannot settle the statement.
- Comparing only an average, or only a spread, when asked to compare two data sets.
- Giving numbers with no comparison words and no context: "3 and 4" is not a comparison.
- Using the mean to describe a typical value when one value is far from the rest.
- Saying a bigger range means "higher values". A bigger range means more spread.
- Comparing proportions as if they were numbers, or numbers from groups of different sizes as if they were proportions.
- Reading a bar chart without checking where the axis starts.

## Exam technique

- **"Compare"**: write two sentences, one on average and one on spread, each naming both data sets and the context.
- **"Is this statement correct? Give a reason."**: say yes or no first, then give a reason with a number from the data.
- **"Explain why the diagram is misleading"**: name the feature (axis not starting at 0, unequal symbols) and say what effect it has.
- **"Give a reason why the conclusion may not be reliable"**: refer to sample size, representativeness or going beyond the data, in the words of the question.
- On the non-calculator papers, percentages such as 27.5% and 11/40 are chosen to be worked by hand. On the calculator papers, give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures.
