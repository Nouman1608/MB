This guide goes deeper into section **3.10.1 Managing change** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.10 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **causes of and pressures for change**, where types of change include **internal, external, incremental and disruptive** change, and managing change includes **Lewin's force field analysis**
- **the value of change**
- **the value of a flexible organisation**, including **restructuring, delayering, flexible employment contracts, organic versus mechanistic structures, and knowledge and information management**
- **the value of managing information and knowledge**
- **barriers to change**, using **Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for resistance to change**
- **how to overcome barriers to change**, using **Kotter and Schlesinger's six ways of overcoming resistance to change**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Causes of and pressures for change

Change is constant. Businesses change because something inside or outside them has changed.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Internal change | Triggered by events inside the business | New owner or chief executive; poor performance; new strategy; merger; growth; staff turnover |
| External change | Triggered by the environment | New competitors; technology; economic slump; new law; shift in customer tastes |
| Incremental change | Small, gradual adjustments over time | Improving a process each month; rolling out new software team by team |
| Disruptive change | Sudden, major change that breaks with the past | A new technology that makes a product obsolete; a pandemic; a major takeover |

The same event can be classified in several ways. A new law is external, but if the business reacts by gradually altering its methods, the response is incremental.

**Pressures for change** include: competitors (see section 3.7.7), technology (3.7.6), customer expectations, legislation (3.7.4), the economy (3.7.5), shareholders demanding better results, and internal pressures such as poor morale or falling productivity.

## The value of change

- Survival and competitiveness in a changing market
- Chance to exploit new opportunities and technology
- Greater efficiency and lower costs
- Better fit with customer needs
- Renewal of staff energy and ideas

But change is costly: training, disruption, lost productivity during the transition, resistance, and the chance of getting it wrong. Change is worthwhile only if benefits outweigh costs and the risks can be managed. Failing to change can be the riskier option.

## Lewin's force field analysis

Kurt Lewin saw every situation as a balance of two sets of forces:

- **Driving forces** push towards change (competition, cost savings, customer demand).
- **Restraining forces** push against it (fear of job losses, cost, habit, lack of skills).

The situation stays stable when the forces are balanced. To bring about change, a manager can **strengthen the driving forces**, **weaken the restraining forces**, or both. Lewin warned that raising only the driving forces often increases resistance, so reducing restraining forces is often more effective.

### Worked example: introducing a new booking system

| Driving forces | Score (1 to 5) | Restraining forces | Score (1 to 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitors already offer online booking | 4 | Staff fear job losses | 4 |
| Savings on administration | 5 | Time needed for training | 3 |
| Customers ask for it | 3 | Cost of installation | 2 |
| **Total** | **12** | **Total** | **9** |

Driving forces (12) exceed restraining forces (9), so change is feasible. The most powerful restraining force is the staff's fear of job losses (4). The manager's best action is to reduce this: promise that no one will be made redundant because of the system, and offer retraining. If that lowers the score to 2, the restraining total falls to 7 and the net balance to +5.

**Strengths of the method:** simple; makes forces visible; identifies where to act; encourages involvement.
**Limits:** scores are subjective; it is a snapshot; it does not explain how to carry out change; forces interact (reducing one may raise another).

## The value of a flexible organisation

A **flexible organisation** can adapt quickly to changes in its environment. Features include:

### Restructuring

Changing the structure of the organisation, for example merging departments, creating project teams or moving to customer-based units. *Value:* fits the structure to the new strategy and can cut costs. *Limits:* disruption, uncertainty and redundancy costs.

### Delayering

Removing one or more levels of management, so the hierarchy is flatter and spans of control are wider.

**Example.** Dunnock Insurance has 900 operatives. With a span of control of 5, the number of managers needed is 180 + 36 + 8 + 2 + 1 = **227**, across 5 levels. Widening the span to 9 gives 100 + 12 + 2 + 1 = **115** managers across 4 levels. The reduction is 112 managers. At an average cost of £45,000, the saving is **£5.04m** a year.

*Value:* lower cost; faster decisions and communication; more empowerment. *Limits:* managers' workloads rise; less supervision and support; redundancy and loss of experience; promotion routes disappear, which can lower motivation.

### Flexible employment contracts

Part-time, temporary, zero-hours, freelance and annualised-hours contracts allow the workforce to expand and shrink with demand. *Value:* lower costs in quiet periods; access to skills as needed. *Limits:* lower commitment and loyalty; higher turnover and training costs; less job security can harm morale and reputation.

### Organic against mechanistic structures

| | Mechanistic | Organic |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Tall, hierarchical, rigid | Flat, fluid, team-based |
| Decisions | Centralised | Decentralised |
| Roles | Narrow, clearly defined | Broad, changing |
| Communication | Top-down | Open, in all directions |
| Suits | Stable, predictable environments | Fast-changing environments |

An organic structure adapts faster and encourages innovation, but may lack clear control and accountability. A mechanistic one gives control and efficiency in stable conditions but adapts slowly.

### Knowledge and information management

Organisations collect large amounts of information and also hold knowledge in employees' heads. **Managing information and knowledge** means capturing, storing, sharing and using it, through databases, shared systems, communities of practice and training.

*Value:* faster, better decisions; avoids repeating mistakes; keeps know-how when staff leave; supports innovation and customer service. *Limits:* cost of systems; information overload; staff may hoard knowledge; security and data protection risks.

## Barriers to change

Kotter and Schlesinger identified four main reasons why people resist change:

1. **Parochial self-interest:** people fear losing something they value, such as status, pay, power or jobs.
2. **Misunderstanding:** people do not understand the reasons or implications of the change, often because of poor communication or lack of trust.
3. **Low tolerance for change:** people fear they will not be able to develop the new skills or behaviours, or simply prefer security and familiarity.
4. **Different assessments of the situation:** people disagree with managers about the need for change or its likely benefits and costs, and may see more costs than advantages.

Other barriers include a lack of resources, a culture that resists change, and change fatigue after too many initiatives.

## How to overcome barriers: Kotter and Schlesinger's six ways

| Approach | Use when | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Education and communication** | Resistance is from lack of information or misunderstanding | Once persuaded, people help | Time-consuming for many people |
| **Participation and involvement** | Managers lack information and others have power to resist | People feel ownership and commit | Slow; may produce an unsuitable change |
| **Facilitation and support** | People resist because of adjustment problems | Reduces fear; training and counselling help | Time-consuming, costly, may fail |
| **Negotiation and agreement** | Some groups will lose out and have power to resist | Can avoid major resistance | Can be expensive and encourage others to ask for deals |
| **Manipulation and co-optation** | Other tactics are too costly or do not work | Quick and cheap | Can cause future problems if people feel manipulated |
| **Explicit and implicit coercion** | Speed is essential and the managers hold power | Fast; overcomes resistance | Risky; creates resentment, harms trust |

The best approach depends on how fast change is needed, how strong the resistance is, who holds power, and the relationship between managers and staff. Often a combination is used: communicate first, involve staff, support them, and use harder methods only as a last resort.

## Assessing a change programme

- **Need:** is there a real driver, and is the change in line with the strategy?
- **Scale and speed:** incremental changes are easier to manage; disruptive change may be unavoidable but riskier.
- **People:** who gains and who loses? Where will resistance come from?
- **Capability:** do leaders have the credibility and the resources to see it through?
- **Alternatives:** can the outcome be achieved by a less disruptive route?

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** trace the effect of a type of change or a method, such as delayering, on costs, speed and staff.
- **Evaluate:** weigh benefits against costs and resistance, and decide what the success depends on (communication, trust, speed, the size of the gain).
- Use the models precisely: name Lewin's forces and the four reasons or six ways by name, and apply them to the case.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all change as positive.
- Listing Kotter and Schlesinger's approaches with no choice for the case.
- Describing delayering as only cost cutting.
- Mixing up organic and mechanistic structures.
- Ignoring the cost of poorly managed change.
