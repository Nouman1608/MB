## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between above the line and below the line promotion. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by drip marketing. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** limit of viral marketing for a business. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Daybreak Tea has a launch budget and is comparing three methods.

| Method | Cost | People reached | Purchase rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local radio | £12,000 | 300,000 | 0.5% |
| In-store coupons | £9,000 | 15,000 shoppers | 8% |
| Social media | £15,000 | 500,000 | 0.6% |

**(a)** Calculate the number of sales from each method. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost per sale for each method. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate which method Daybreak Tea should use. **[8]**

**5.** Daybreak spends £20,000 a year on advertising and sells 50,000 packs. The contribution is £4 per pack. It raises spending in two steps.

| Step | Advertising spend | Sales (packs) |
|---|---|---|
| Start | £20,000 | 50,000 |
| 1 | £25,000 | 56,000 |
| 2 | £30,000 | 58,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the advertising elasticity of demand for step 1. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the extra contribution minus the extra advertising cost for step 1. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the advertising elasticity of demand for step 2, to two decimal places. **[3]**

**(d)** Evaluate the usefulness of advertising elasticity calculations to Daybreak Tea in deciding how much to spend. **[9]**

**6.** Corbel Software sells accounting software to small businesses, with a price of £900 a year. Its marketing director proposes a large television advertising campaign. Recommend and justify a promotional mix for Corbel Software. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Above the line promotion is paid-for advertising in mass media such as television, radio and press [1]. Below the line promotion is directly controlled by the business, such as coupons, free samples and displays [1], and it is usually more targeted and easier to measure [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Sending a planned series of regular messages to the same customers over time [1], such as emails or letters, to keep the brand in mind and encourage purchase [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The business cannot control whether content is shared [1], so a campaign may reach few people if viewers do not pass it on [1], and the money spent on creating it may be wasted; it may also spread in ways the business did not intend [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Radio: 300,000 × 0.005 = **1,500** [1]. Coupons: 15,000 × 0.08 = **1,200** [1]. Social: 500,000 × 0.006 = **3,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Radio: 12,000 ÷ 1,500 = **£8.00** [1]. Coupons: 9,000 ÷ 1,200 = **£7.50** [1]. Social: 15,000 ÷ 3,000 = **£5.00** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Social media has the lowest cost per sale (£5.00) and the most sales (3,000) [1]; it can target tea drinkers and results can be measured [1]. Coupons give a high purchase rate (8%) and encourage trial, but reach only 15,000 shoppers [1]. Radio reaches many people and builds awareness, but has the highest cost per sale at £8.00 [1]. Cost per sale ignores brand building and the chance that coupon buyers do not buy again [1]; the response rates are estimates [1]. Recommendation: use social media as the main method, with coupons to encourage trial among shoppers [1]. Judgement: a mix supports each other, but Daybreak should measure repeat purchase, not only the first sale [1]. **[8]**

**5. (a)** % change in spend = (25,000 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 25% [1]. % change in sales = (56,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 12% [1]. AED = 12 ÷ 25 = **0.48** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Extra contribution = 6,000 × £4 = £24,000 [1]; less extra spend of £5,000 = **+£19,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** % change in spend = (30,000 − 25,000) ÷ 25,000 × 100 = 20% [1]. % change in sales = (58,000 − 56,000) ÷ 56,000 × 100 = 3.57% [1]. AED = 3.57 ÷ 20 = **0.18** [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** Useful: it shows that sales respond less than proportionately (0.48 and 0.18), so advertising has a positive effect [1]; the falling value shows diminishing returns [1]; combined with the contribution per pack, it shows that step 1 adds £19,000 and step 2 adds £3,000 (2,000 × £4 − £5,000), so spending is still worthwhile but with a falling gain [1]; it helps to decide where to stop [1].
Limits: sales may also change because of price, seasons or rivals, so the rise may not be due only to advertising [1]; there may be a delay between spending and sales [1]; the result from one campaign may not apply next year [1].
Judgement: AED is a helpful guide, since the next step is likely to add very little, but Daybreak should test further spending on a small scale and use other evidence [2]. **[9]**

**6.** Corbel sells a complex, high-value product to a small number of business buyers, so personal selling or telephone demonstration is likely to be effective [1]. The internet is important: search advertising when owners search for accounting software, a website with free trials, and a clear price [1]. Drip marketing by email to leads, with guides and reminders, builds trust over time [1]. Trade press and accountants' recommendations reach the target market [1].
Television: it reaches a mass audience, most of whom do not buy accounting software, so cost per sale would be high [1]; it might help brand awareness for a small firm, but the budget is better used on targeted methods [1].
Measure results: cost per lead and cost per customer, using the £900 yearly price to judge what is worth paying [1]. Risks: personal selling is costly per contact; online adverts are competitive [1]. Judgement: a targeted mix of internet, drip marketing and selling, with television rejected, suits the product and the budget [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the change in spend or sales in pounds and packs, not percentages, in AED.
- Judging a method only by cost per sale.
- Describing a method without applying it to the product and audience.
- Recommending a mix without a reason or a risk.
