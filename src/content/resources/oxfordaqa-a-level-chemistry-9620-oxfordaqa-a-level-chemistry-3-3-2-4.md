## What the specification asks for

- The reaction of methane with chlorine.
- Explain this reaction as a free-radical substitution mechanism involving initiation, propagation and termination steps.

From 3.3.1.2: show the unpaired electron of a radical as a dot; curly arrows are not required; write a balanced equation for each step.

## The reaction

Methane and chlorine do not react in the dark at room temperature. In **ultraviolet light** (including sunlight), they react:

```
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl        chloromethane and hydrogen chloride
```

- **Substitution**: a chlorine atom takes the place of a hydrogen atom.
- **Free-radical**: the reactive species that carry the reaction are radicals.

### Why the Cl–Cl bond breaks, not C–H

UV light supplies the energy to break a bond. The Cl–Cl bond (bond enthalpy about 243 kJ mol⁻¹) is much weaker than a C–H bond in methane (mean bond enthalpy about 413 kJ mol⁻¹), so it is the Cl–Cl bond that breaks. It breaks **homolytically**: one electron goes to each chlorine atom, giving two chlorine radicals.

## The mechanism, step by step

### Initiation

```
Cl₂ → 2Cl•        (UV light)
```

Zero radicals on the left, two on the right. This step starts the chain.

### Propagation

```
CH₄ + Cl• → •CH₃ + HCl
•CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
```

Each step uses one radical and makes one. The Cl• made in the second step can start the first step again, so the cycle repeats many times from one initiation: it is a **chain reaction**. Adding the two propagation steps gives the overall equation, with Cl• and •CH₃ cancelling.

### Termination

```
Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
•CH₃ + Cl• → CH₃Cl
•CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆
```

Two radicals combine and none are left, so the chain stops. Termination is rare compared with propagation while radical concentrations are low, which is why many molecules of product form for each initiation step.

### Worked example 1: an annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why the reaction of methane with chlorine is described as a free-radical substitution, and why only a little ultraviolet light is needed for a large amount of product. (4 marks)*

> A hydrogen atom in methane is replaced by a chlorine atom, so it is substitution [1]. The reaction is carried by radicals, species with an unpaired electron, such as Cl• and •CH₃ [1]. UV light breaks Cl–Cl homolytically in the initiation step [1]. In the propagation steps each radical used produces another radical, so one initiation leads to many cycles before termination stops the chain [1].

## Further substitution

Chloromethane still has C–H bonds, and a chlorine radical can attack any of them. So substitution continues, one hydrogen at a time. Each new product forms by a pair of propagation steps like the first.

### Worked example 2: dichloromethane

*Write the two propagation steps that convert chloromethane into dichloromethane.*

```
CH₃Cl + Cl• → •CH₂Cl + HCl
•CH₂Cl + Cl₂ → CH₂Cl₂ + Cl•
```

The pattern repeats:

| Product | Formula | Overall equation from methane | Boiling point / °C (approximate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| chloromethane | CH₃Cl | CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl | −24 |
| dichloromethane | CH₂Cl₂ | CH₄ + 2Cl₂ → CH₂Cl₂ + 2HCl | 40 |
| trichloromethane | CHCl₃ | CH₄ + 3Cl₂ → CHCl₃ + 3HCl | 61 |
| tetrachloromethane | CCl₄ | CH₄ + 4Cl₂ → CCl₄ + 4HCl | 77 |

The products have different boiling points, so the mixture can be separated by fractional distillation (the method from 3.3.2.1).

### Controlling the product

- **Excess methane**: a chlorine radical is far more likely to meet a methane molecule than a chloromethane molecule, so the main product is **chloromethane**.
- **Excess chlorine**: substitution continues until all hydrogen atoms are replaced, giving mainly **tetrachloromethane**.

### Termination products

Termination between two carbon radicals makes C–C bonds. •CH₃ + •CH₃ gives ethane; •CH₃ + •CH₂Cl gives chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl. Traces of these two-carbon compounds in the product are evidence for the radical mechanism, because there is no other way for a C–C bond to form from methane.

## Calculations

### Worked example 3: percentage yield and atom economy

*4.00 g of methane reacts with chlorine and 7.07 g of chloromethane is collected. Calculate the percentage yield, and the percentage atom economy for making chloromethane by CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl. (Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, Cl = 35.5)*

```
n(CH₄)          = 4.00 ÷ 16.0 = 0.250 mol
theoretical mass = 0.250 × 50.5 = 12.6 g      (1 : 1)
% yield          = 7.07 ÷ 12.625 × 100 = 56.0 %

atom economy     = Mr(CH₃Cl) ÷ total Mr of reactants × 100
                 = 50.5 ÷ (16.0 + 71.0) × 100 = 58.0 %
```

The yield is low because other chlorinated products and termination products form. The atom economy is low because HCl is a by-product of every substitution step.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out UV light, or calling it a catalyst. It provides the energy for initiation.
- Writing CH₄ + Cl• → CH₃Cl + H•. The hydrogen leaves as HCl; H• is not formed.
- Putting the dot on hydrogen (CH₃• drawn with the dot on an H). The unpaired electron is on carbon.
- Drawing curly arrows on radical steps.
- A termination step with a radical on the right, or a propagation step with no radical on the right.
- Saying the reaction gives only chloromethane. It gives a mixture unless methane is in excess.
- Describing homolytic fission as "the bond breaks evenly into ions". It gives radicals, not ions.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Name the mechanism**: free-radical substitution.
- **Write equations for the steps**: label initiation, propagation and termination; one equation per step; dots on radicals; conditions (UV) on initiation.
- **Explain** (step types): count radicals on each side.
- **Suggest** (products): use further substitution and termination to account for unexpected products, with equations.
- **Calculate**: yields and atom economy, as in section 3.1.2.
