It walks through a real-life task strand by strand, using three full examples. All prices, rates and measurements in the examples are invented for practice.

## What Criterion D assesses

Criterion D is marked out of 8. In Year 5 its five strands ask you to:

1. identify relevant elements of authentic real-life situations;
2. select appropriate mathematical strategies when solving authentic real-life situations;
3. apply the selected mathematical strategies successfully to reach a solution;
4. justify the degree of accuracy of a solution;
5. justify whether a solution makes sense in the context of the authentic real-life situation.

All five strands apply in every year; Years 1 and 3 ask you to describe or explain where Year 5 asks you to justify. The tasks are open situations, so you decide what matters and which mathematics to use.

## A routine for real-life tasks

| Stage | Strand | Questions to ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Understand the situation | i | What is the real question? What information matters, and what can be ignored? |
| State assumptions | i | What must I assume to make the problem solvable? |
| Choose a strategy | ii | Which model fits: linear, exponential, geometric, statistical? |
| Carry it out | iii | Is each step correct, with units? |
| Check accuracy | iv | How accurate is the input data, and so the answer? How should I round? |
| Judge the answer | v | Does it make sense? What are the model's limits? |

## Strand i: identifying relevant elements

Real situations contain information you do not need and leave out information you do. Sort it explicitly:

- **Relevant and given**: values you will use.
- **Irrelevant**: values that do not affect the question (the colour of a car, a room's height when only the floor is tiled).
- **Missing**: values you must estimate or assume, stated as assumptions.

Writing a short list of assumptions is one of the clearest signs of strand i. Good assumptions are realistic and checkable: "fuel is not included in either price", "the walls are flat rectangles".

## Strand ii: selecting strategies

Match the behaviour of the situation to a model:

| Behaviour | Strategy |
|---|---|
| A fixed amount plus a steady rate | Linear function; compare lines; find where they meet |
| A percentage change repeated each period | Exponential function with a multiplier |
| Space covered or contained | Area, surface area or volume |
| Heights, distances and angles | Trigonometry or Pythagoras' theorem |
| Variation between people or items | Statistics: mean, median, range, a graph |
| A choice between options | Calculate each option on the same basis and compare |

Say why the strategy fits. "Each company charges a daily rate plus a rate per kilometre, so each total is a linear function of distance" is a selection with a reason.

## Worked example 1: comparing two offers

A family needs a car for 3 days. Company A charges 45 per day plus 0.20 per km. Company B charges 30 per day plus 0.35 per km. They expect to drive about 250 km. Which company should they use?

**Identify.** Relevant: the daily rates, the per-km rates, 3 days, 250 km. Assumption: there are no other charges, and fuel costs the same in either car.

**Select.** Each total cost is linear in the distance d km:

```
Cost A = 3 × 45 + 0.20d = 135 + 0.20d
Cost B = 3 × 30 + 0.35d =  90 + 0.35d
```

**Apply.** At d = 250: A = 135 + 50 = 185 and B = 90 + 87.50 = 177.50. Company B is cheaper by 7.50.

**Go further.** The costs are equal when 135 + 0.20d = 90 + 0.35d, so 45 = 0.15d and d = 300 km. Below 300 km Company B is cheaper; above it, Company A is.

**Makes sense?** The answer depends on the 250 km estimate, which is uncertain. Because the break-even distance is 300 km, Company B stays cheaper unless the family drives 50 km more than planned. That is a justified recommendation: it gives the answer, the condition under which it changes, and the evidence.

## Strand iv: justifying the degree of accuracy

Accuracy has two parts. First, the answer should be rounded in a way the context needs: money to the cent, people and tins to whole numbers, measurements to the precision of the data. Second, the answer can be no more accurate than the information it came from. To *justify* the accuracy, show how much the answer could change if the inputs were at the edges of their accuracy.

## Worked example 2: paint for a room

A room is 4.2 m long, 3.6 m wide and 2.5 m high, measured to the nearest 0.1 m. It has a door 0.9 m by 2.0 m and a window 1.5 m by 1.2 m. The walls need two coats. One tin covers 30 m² with one coat. How many tins are needed?

**Identify and assume.** The ceiling and floor are not painted. The door and window are not painted. The walls are flat rectangles.

**Apply.**

```
Wall area   = 2 × (4.2 + 3.6) × 2.5 = 39.0 m²
Openings    = 0.9 × 2.0 + 1.5 × 1.2 = 1.8 + 1.8 = 3.6 m²
To paint    = 39.0 − 3.6 = 35.4 m²
Two coats   = 70.8 m²
Tins        = 70.8 ÷ 30 = 2.36, so 3 tins
```

**Justify the accuracy.** Each length could be up to 0.05 m out. Using the smallest possible lengths (4.15, 3.55 and 2.45 m) the two-coat area is 68.3 m², needing 2.28 tins. Using the largest (4.25, 3.65 and 2.55 m) it is 73.4 m², needing 2.45 tins. Both round up to 3 tins, so the measurement accuracy does not change the answer. Rounding up is required because 2 tins would leave part of the wall unpainted.

**Makes sense?** Three tins leave roughly 0.6 of a tin spare, which also covers some waste or a third coat on a patch. The answer is sensible for the situation.

## Strand v: does the solution make sense?

Check the answer against reality in three ways:

1. **Size**: is it a believable value? A room needing 300 tins, or a car hire costing 2 in total, signals an error.
2. **Form**: is it the right kind of number? Whole tins, positive lengths, money to two decimal places.
3. **Limits of the model**: over what range is the model realistic? Which assumptions, if wrong, would change the answer?

## Worked example 3: the limits of a model

A patient takes a 200 mg dose of a medicine. Model: the amount in the body falls by 20% each hour. After how many whole hours is it below 50 mg?

```
A = 200 × 0.8ⁿ
n = 6: 200 × 0.8⁶ = 52.4 mg   (above 50)
n = 7: 200 × 0.8⁷ = 41.9 mg   (below 50)
```

The amount first falls below 50 mg after **7 hours**. Justifying whether this makes sense means discussing the model, not just the arithmetic. The model treats the decrease as happening in steps each hour, when the real decrease is continuous, so the amount actually crosses 50 mg somewhere between 6 and 7 hours. It also assumes the same percentage rate for every patient and at every concentration. The answer is a reasonable estimate, but a real dosing decision would need measured data.

## Common mistakes

- Using every number in the question, including irrelevant ones.
- Giving no assumptions, so the reader cannot judge the model.
- Comparing options on different bases (one for 3 days, one for 1 day).
- Rounding tins, buses or boxes down.
- Writing "the answer is accurate" without saying why.
- Saying "it makes sense" without comparing the answer with the situation.
- Using a model outside its realistic range.

## Exam technique

Real-life tasks reward reasoning in words as much as calculation. Finish each task with a short paragraph that (1) answers the question, (2) states the accuracy and why, and (3) judges whether the answer makes sense, naming at least one limitation. In Year 5 back each claim with evidence, such as the range of possible areas or the break-even distance. Criterion C is often assessed in the same task, so keep the structure clear.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "authentic real-life situation" mean in Criterion D?**
It means a situation that could genuinely occur, where you must decide what information matters and which mathematics to use. The task is open rather than a disguised textbook exercise.

**How do I justify the degree of accuracy?**
Show how the answer changes when the inputs are at the edges of their accuracy, and explain how you rounded. If the answer stays the same across that range, the accuracy is justified.

**What should I write to show a solution makes sense?**
Compare the answer with the real situation: is it a believable size, the right kind of number, and within the range where the model works? Name a limitation and say how it could affect the answer.
