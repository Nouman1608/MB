## Core idea

For every type of particle there is an antiparticle with the **same mass and rest energy** and the **opposite charge**. E = mc² is not needed: rest energies are given in MeV.

## The four pairs you must know

| Particle | Antiparticle | Symbol | Charge of antiparticle | Rest energy / MeV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electron | Positron | e⁺ | +e | 0.511 |
| Proton | Antiproton | p̄ | −e | 938.3 |
| Neutron | Antineutron | n̄ | 0 | 939.6 |
| Neutrino | Antineutrino | ν̄ | 0 | negligible |

Neutral particles still have distinct antiparticles. Ratio of rest energies = ratio of masses.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Antiparticle | Particle with the same mass and rest energy and opposite charge |
| Rest energy | Energy a particle has because of its mass, when at rest |
| Annihilation | Particle + antiparticle → photons; both particles cease to exist |
| Pair production | Photon → particle + antiparticle, close to a nucleus |
| Threshold energy | Minimum photon energy for pair production: 2 × one rest energy |
| Electron volt | Energy gained by an electron moving through 1 V: 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J |

## Energy rules

**Annihilation:** total photon energy = 2 × rest energy + total kinetic energy.

**Pair production:** photon energy = 2 × rest energy + total kinetic energy of the pair.

| Process | At rest / threshold value |
|---|---|
| e⁻ + e⁺ annihilation at rest | Two photons of 0.511 MeV |
| p + p̄ annihilation at rest | Two photons of 938.3 MeV |
| n + n̄ annihilation at rest | Two photons of 939.6 MeV |
| e⁻ e⁺ pair production threshold | 1.022 MeV |
| p p̄ pair production threshold | 1876.6 MeV |
| n n̄ pair production threshold | 1879.2 MeV |

## Conservation checklist

- **Charge:** pair total is zero; photons have none.
- **Energy:** use the rules above.
- **Momentum:** annihilation at rest gives at least two photons in opposite directions; pair production needs a nucleus nearby to take up momentum.

## Converting

| From | To | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| MeV | J | × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ |
| J | MeV | ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ |
| Photon energy E (J) | frequency | f = E/h |
| Photon energy E (J) | wavelength | λ = hc/E |

A minimum energy corresponds to a maximum wavelength.

## Method for any energy question

1. List the rest energy of each particle in MeV.
2. Add or subtract kinetic energies according to the process.
3. Share between two equal photons or two equal particles only if the situation is symmetrical.
4. Convert to joules last, and only once.

## Worked reminders

**Annihilation to wavelength.** An electron and a positron, each with 0.400 MeV of kinetic energy, meet head-on. Each photon carries 0.511 + 0.400 = 0.911 MeV = 1.46 × 10⁻¹³ J, so λ = hc/E = 1.36 × 10⁻¹² m.

**Threshold to frequency.** An electron-positron pair needs at least 1.022 MeV = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J, so the lowest photon frequency that can produce one is f = E/h = 2.47 × 10²⁰ Hz.

**Too little energy.** A 1.50 GeV photon (1500 MeV) cannot produce a proton-antiproton pair, because 1500 MeV is less than the 1876.6 MeV threshold; it can produce an electron-positron pair with 1499 MeV of kinetic energy to spare.

## Exam traps

- Threshold taken as one rest energy, not two.
- Kinetic energy of only one particle included.
- MeV used directly in E = hf.
- "A neutral particle is its own antiparticle" (not for the neutron).
- "Antimatter has negative mass": it has the same, positive mass.
- Two photons explained by energy rather than momentum.
- Pair production described as happening in empty space.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the antiparticle of the proton and state its charge.
2. State one property that is the same and one that is different for a particle and its antiparticle.
3. Convert 0.511 MeV to joules.
4. An electron and a positron annihilate at rest. State the energy of each photon in MeV.
5. Calculate the minimum photon energy, in joules, needed to produce a proton and an antiproton.
6. A 2.50 MeV photon produces an electron-positron pair. Find the kinetic energy of each particle if shared equally.
7. Why must pair production occur close to a nucleus?
8. An electron and a positron, each with 0.350 MeV of kinetic energy, collide head-on. Find the energy of each photon.
9. Find the longest photon wavelength that can produce a proton-antiproton pair.
10. State the charge of the antineutron and explain why it is not the same particle as the neutron.
11. Use the rest energies to show that the neutron is slightly more massive than the proton.
12. Use rest energies to find how many times more massive a neutron is than an electron.

### Answers

1. The antiproton, charge −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (relative charge −1).
2. Same: mass or rest energy. Different: charge, which is opposite.
3. 0.511 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 8.18 × 10⁻¹⁴ J.
4. 0.511 MeV each.
5. 2 × 938.3 = 1876.6 MeV = 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ J.
6. 2.50 − 1.022 = 1.478 MeV shared, so 0.739 MeV each.
7. A nucleus nearby takes up some momentum, allowing both momentum and energy to be conserved.
8. 0.511 + 0.350 = 0.861 MeV each.
9. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 6.62 × 10⁻¹⁶ m.
10. Zero. It is the neutron's antiparticle: a neutron and an antineutron that meet annihilate, which identical particles would not do.
11. Its rest energy (939.6 MeV) is 1.3 MeV larger than the proton's (938.3 MeV), and the ratio of rest energies equals the ratio of masses.
12. 939.6 / 0.511 = 1839 times.
