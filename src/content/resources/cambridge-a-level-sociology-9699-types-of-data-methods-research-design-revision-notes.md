Condensed notes for section 2.1, *Types of data, methods and research design*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 1. Fuller explanations and a worked research design are in the [2.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-types-of-data-methods-research-design/); test yourself with the [2.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-types-of-data-methods-research-design-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Primary and secondary sources; quantitative and qualitative data.
- Secondary sources: official statistics, personal documents, digital content, media sources.
- Quantitative methods: questionnaires, structured interviews, experiments, content analysis.
- Qualitative methods: overt and covert participant and non-participant observation; unstructured, semi-structured and group interviews.
- Research design: strategy, questions and hypotheses, sampling frames, sampling techniques, pilot studies, operationalisation, conducting research, interpreting results.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary data | Collected by the researcher for the current study |
| Secondary data | Already exists, produced by others for another purpose |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data showing patterns, size and frequency |
| Qualitative data | Words, images or observations showing meanings and experiences |
| Hard statistics | Fairly complete official counts, such as births and deaths |
| Soft statistics | Official figures that depend on reporting and recording, such as crime |
| Coding frame | Categories used to count features in content analysis |
| Interviewer effect | The interviewer's characteristics or manner influencing answers |
| Hawthorne effect | People changing behaviour because they know they are studied |
| Going native | A participant observer over-identifying with the group studied |
| Hypothesis | A testable prediction about a relationship between variables |
| Operationalisation | Turning a concept into measurable indicators |
| Sampling frame | A list of all members of the population |
| Pilot study | A small-scale trial of the research before the main study |

## Secondary sources at a glance

| Source | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Official statistics | Large scale, free, trends over time and between places | Soft statistics are socially constructed (Douglas, 1967, on suicide); categories set by the state |
| Personal documents | Rich insight into meanings and past experience | Test with Scott's (1990) criteria: authenticity, credibility, representativeness, meaning |
| Digital content | Plentiful, cheap, spontaneous, current | Unrepresentative users; fake identities; consent for public posts |
| Media sources | Show framing and agenda | Selected and shaped to attract audiences |

## Quantitative methods at a glance

| Method | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Large samples; standardised so reliable; quick to analyse; anonymous | Low response rates; closed questions impose the researcher's categories; no chance to clarify |
| Structured interview | Higher response; can clarify | Interviewer effect; social desirability; limited depth |
| Laboratory experiment | Control of variables; cause and effect | Artificial; Hawthorne effect; ethical limits |
| Field experiment | Real setting | Less control; consent problems |
| Content analysis | Systematic; repeatable; cheap | Coding is subjective; counts frequency, not meaning |

## Qualitative methods at a glance

| Method | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Overt participant observation | Ethical consent; can ask questions openly (Barker, 1984) | Group may change behaviour |
| Covert participant observation | Access to closed groups; natural behaviour (Patrick, 1973) | Deception; danger; hard to record; going native |
| Non-participant observation | Less influence on the group; can use a schedule | Little insight into meanings |
| Unstructured interview | Depth, rapport, high validity | Time; small samples; hard to compare |
| Semi-structured interview | Comparable core plus flexibility | Still small samples; interviewer effect |
| Group interview | Shows how views form in interaction | Dominant voices; peer pressure |

## Research design in eight steps

1. **Strategy**: survey, case study, ethnography, experiment or mixed.
2. **Question or hypothesis**: open question (interpretivist) or testable prediction (positivist).
3. **Operationalisation**: define indicators, such as measuring religiosity by attendance, prayer and belief.
4. **Sampling frame**: school registers, membership lists, electoral rolls.
5. **Sampling technique**: random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball, opportunity.
6. **Pilot study**: tests wording, timing, access and ethics.
7. **Conducting**: consent, anonymity, consistent procedures.
8. **Interpreting**: patterns, alternative explanations, limits of the sample.

**Sampling techniques compared.**
- Random and systematic need a full frame.
- Stratified mirrors key characteristics of the population.
- Quota needs no frame but is chosen by the researcher, so bias can enter.
- Snowball reaches hidden groups but is unrepresentative.
- Opportunity is quick but the least representative.

## Exam traps

- **"All secondary data is numbers."** Diaries and websites are secondary and qualitative.
- **Frame versus sample.** The frame is the list; the sample is drawn from it.
- **Vague method names.** Specify overt or covert, participant or non-participant.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Say so when interpreting survey results.
- **Generic evaluation.** Apply every strength and limitation to the context in the question.
- **Hypothesis versus question.** A hypothesis must be testable.

## Self-test

1. Give an example of secondary qualitative data.
2. Why are crime figures called "soft" statistics?
3. Name Scott's four criteria for assessing documents.
4. Give one ethical problem with using social media posts as data.
5. What is the interviewer effect?
6. Why do sociologists use laboratory experiments less than psychologists?
7. What is a coding frame?
8. Give one strength and one limitation of covert participant observation.
9. How does a group interview differ from an unstructured interview?
10. Operationalise the concept "poverty" in two ways.
11. When would a snowball sample be useful?
12. What is the purpose of a pilot study?

### Answers

1. Diaries, letters, memoirs, blog posts or newspaper articles.
2. They depend on crimes being noticed, reported and recorded, so many go uncounted.
3. Authenticity, credibility, representativeness, meaning.
4. Authors have not given informed consent, and quoting posts may identify them.
5. The interviewer's characteristics or manner influencing the answers given.
6. Laboratory settings are artificial, social behaviour has many causes, and manipulating people raises ethical problems.
7. A set of categories used to count features of media or documents systematically.
8. Strength: access to closed groups and natural behaviour. Limitation: deception, risk to the researcher or difficulty recording data.
9. A group interview involves several participants together and reveals interaction between them; an unstructured interview is usually one-to-one.
10. Any two: household income below a set level; lacking items most people regard as necessities; receiving means-tested benefits.
11. When studying hidden or hard-to-reach groups with no sampling frame, such as undocumented workers.
12. To test the research tools and procedures on a small scale so problems can be fixed before the main study.
