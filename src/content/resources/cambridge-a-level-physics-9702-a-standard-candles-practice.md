## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the luminosity of a star. **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by a standard candle. **[1]**

**3.** A star of luminosity 2.0 × 10²⁸ W is at a distance of 4.0 × 10¹⁸ m from the Earth. Calculate the radiant flux intensity at the Earth. **[2]**

**4.** The distance from a star to an observer is trebled. State the factor by which the radiant flux intensity changes. **[1]**

**5.** A standard candle of luminosity 5.0 × 10³⁰ W has a measured radiant flux intensity of 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ W m⁻². Calculate its distance from the Earth. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the difference between luminosity and radiant flux intensity, including their units. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Mars orbits the Sun at a mean distance of 2.3 × 10¹¹ m. The Earth orbits at 1.5 × 10¹¹ m.

**(a)** Calculate the radiant flux intensity of the Sun's radiation at the distance of Mars. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio (flux at Mars) ÷ (flux at Earth) without using the luminosity. **[1]**

**8.** In this question, assume that a certain type of supernova always reaches a peak luminosity of 1.0 × 10³⁶ W.

**(a)** A supernova of this type in a distant galaxy has a peak radiant flux intensity of 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ W m⁻². Calculate the distance to the galaxy. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why supernovae of this type, rather than Cepheid variable stars, are used for the most distant galaxies. **[2]**

**9.** Cepheid A is at a known distance of 6.0 × 10¹⁸ m and has a radiant flux intensity of 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ W m⁻². Cepheid B, in a distant galaxy, has the same period of variation as A and a radiant flux intensity of 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ W m⁻².

**(a)** Explain why A and B can be assumed to have the same luminosity. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the luminosity of Cepheid A. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the distance to the galaxy containing B. **[2]**

**10.** Dust between a standard candle and the Earth absorbs 30 % of the radiation travelling towards the Earth. An astronomer ignores the dust.

**(a)** State whether the calculated distance will be too large or too small. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio (calculated distance) ÷ (true distance). **[2]**

**11.** State **two** assumptions made when the inverse square law F = L/(4πd²) is used to find the distance to a star. **[2]**

**12.** The radiant flux intensity of a standard candle is measured with an uncertainty of ±6 %. Its luminosity is known exactly. Determine the percentage uncertainty in the calculated distance. **[2]**

**13.** Explain how standard candles are used to determine the distance to a galaxy. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The total power of radiation emitted by the star [1].

**2.** An astronomical object of known luminosity [1].

**3.** F = L/(4πd²) = 2.0 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × (4.0 × 10¹⁸)²) [1] = 9.9 × 10⁻¹¹ W m⁻² [1].

**4.** It becomes one ninth of its original value [1].

**5.** d = √(L/(4πF)) [1] = √(5.0 × 10³⁰ ÷ (4π × 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶)) = 2.2 × 10²² m [1].

**6.** Luminosity is the total power emitted by the star, in W, and does not depend on the observer [1]; radiant flux intensity is the power received per unit area at the observer, in W m⁻², and decreases with distance from the star [1].

**7. (a)** F = 3.8 × 10²⁶ ÷ (4π × (2.3 × 10¹¹)²) [1] = 570 W m⁻² [1].

**(b)** F ∝ 1/d², so ratio = (1.5 ÷ 2.3)² = 0.43 [1].

**8. (a)** d = √(1.0 × 10³⁶ ÷ (4π × 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)) [1] = 1.4 × 10²⁴ m [1].

**(b)** These supernovae have a far greater luminosity than Cepheids [1], so their radiant flux intensity is still large enough to be measured at distances where Cepheids are too faint to be detected (or to be identified individually) [1].

**9. (a)** For Cepheid variables, the period of variation is related to the luminosity, so stars with the same period have the same luminosity [1].

**(b)** L = 4πd²F = 4π × (6.0 × 10¹⁸)² × 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ [1] = 2.3 × 10²⁹ W [1].

**(c)** d = √(L/(4πF)) = √(2.26 × 10²⁹ ÷ (4π × 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁷)) [1] = 3.0 × 10²² m [1]. (Or by ratio: 6.0 × 10¹⁸ × √(5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 3.0 × 10²² m.)

**10. (a)** Too large [1].

**(b)** Measured F = 0.70 × true F, and d ∝ 1/√F [1]; ratio = 1/√0.70 = 1.2 [1].

**11.** Any two [1 each]: the star radiates equally in all directions; no radiation is absorbed or scattered between the star and the observer; the star can be treated as a point source.

**12.** d ∝ F^(−½) [1], so the percentage uncertainty in d is half that in F: ±3 % [1].

**13.** A standard candle, an object of known luminosity, is identified in the galaxy [1]. Its radiant flux intensity at the Earth is measured [1]. The distance is calculated from F = L/(4πd²), d = √(L/(4πF)), and this is taken as the distance to the galaxy [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up luminosity and radiant flux intensity, or their units.
- Using πd² or 4πd instead of 4πd².
- Forgetting the square root when calculating a distance.
- Inverting the ratio of fluxes so that the fainter object appears closer.
- Saying dust makes a galaxy appear nearer than it is.
- Describing a standard candle as "a bright star" rather than an object of known luminosity.
