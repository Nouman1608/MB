Sub-topic 6.1 is the longest part of Topic 6 in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029). It has ten outcomes, running from the structure of the Earth and the names of the continents, through the processes at plate boundaries and the features of earthquakes and eruptions, to their impacts, how those impacts are managed, and the opportunities volcanoes give people. This guide takes each outcome in turn, adding the explanations and connections that turn short recall into full-mark answers.

For an overview of all four hazards, see the [natural hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-natural-hazards-study-guide/).

## The structure of the Earth (6.1.1) and the continents (6.1.2)

The syllabus limits structure to three layers.

| Layer | Description |
|---|---|
| **Crust** | The thin, rigid outer layer. Oceanic crust is thinner and denser; continental crust is thicker and less dense. The crust and the top of the mantle are broken into tectonic plates. |
| **Mantle** | The thickest layer, made of hot rock. Although it is mostly solid, it flows very slowly, which allows convection currents. |
| **Core** | The centre of the Earth, made mainly of iron and nickel and extremely hot; its heat drives the convection currents in the mantle. |

You must be able to identify the seven continents on a map: **Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania and South America**. Questions may ask which continent a plate boundary or volcano lies in, so practise on a blank world map.

## Distribution: plates and hotspots (6.1.3)

Earthquakes and volcanoes are not spread evenly. Most lie in narrow belts along **plate boundaries**, because that is where plates move against each other. The belt around the edge of the Pacific Ocean is the clearest example on a world map. A smaller number of volcanoes lie far from any boundary, over **hotspots**: places where a plume of unusually hot mantle rises beneath a plate and melts through it. Because the plate moves while the hotspot stays roughly still, a hotspot can produce a chain of volcanoes, with the oldest furthest from the hotspot.

When describing a distribution from a map, give the overall pattern (in belts or lines), name a location using the continents or oceans, and mention an exception (scattered hotspot volcanoes).

## How convection moves plates (6.1.4)

Heat from the core warms the rock at the base of the mantle. The warmer rock is less dense, so it rises slowly, spreads out beneath the crust, cools, becomes denser and sinks again. These **convection currents** drag the plates above them. The syllabus names three types of boundary:

- **Divergent (constructive):** plates move apart. Magma rises into the gap, causing volcanic activity and earthquakes and forming new crust.
- **Convergent (destructive):** plates move towards each other. The denser plate is forced down beneath the other (**subduction**). Friction causes earthquakes, and the sinking plate melts, so magma rises to form a **line of volcanoes** parallel to the boundary.
- **Conservative:** plates slide past each other. They lock, stress builds up, and the sudden slip causes earthquakes. No crust is made or destroyed, so there are no volcanoes.

## Features of earthquakes (6.1.5)

An earthquake is the sudden **release of energy** stored in rocks as stress builds up and the rocks slip. The **focus** is the point underground where the slip begins; the **epicentre** is the point on the surface directly above the focus. **Magnitude** is the amount of energy released, measured on the **Moment Magnitude Scale**.

The scale is logarithmic: each increase of 1 in magnitude means roughly 32 times more energy released.

### Worked example 1: comparing magnitudes

How much more energy does a magnitude 7.0 earthquake release than a magnitude 5.0 earthquake?

- Difference = 7.0 - 5.0 = 2 steps.
- Energy ratio ≈ 32 x 32 = **about 1 000 times** more energy.

So a magnitude 7.0 earthquake is not "40% bigger" than a 5.0; it is enormously more powerful. Damage, however, also depends on depth of focus, distance from the epicentre, building quality and population density.

## Features of volcanic eruptions (6.1.6)

- **Magma rising to the surface to form lava:** magma is molten rock underground; once it erupts it is called lava.
- **Ash:** tiny fragments of rock and glass blasted into the air; it can collapse roofs, ruin crops and affect breathing.
- **Gases:** water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.
- **Acid rain:** sulfur dioxide dissolves in water in the atmosphere to form acid rain, which damages vegetation and acidifies soils and lakes.
- **Volcanic bombs:** lumps of lava thrown out that cool and harden in the air before landing.
- **Pyroclastic flows:** fast-moving currents of hot gas, ash and rock that flow down the side of the volcano, destroying everything in their path.

## Impacts on people and the environment (6.1.7)

The ten impacts in the syllabus group into four kinds.

- **Physical damage:** damage to buildings and infrastructure; fire (from broken gas pipes and electrical cables after earthquakes, or from lava); landslides on slopes shaken by earthquakes or loaded with ash; tsunamis when an undersea earthquake or eruption displaces water.
- **Farming and nature:** loss of crops, livestock and habitats.
- **People:** evacuation of people and livestock; human health (injuries, breathing problems from ash and gas, stress).
- **Water and money:** contamination of drinking-water supplies when pipes and sewers break; water-related disease spreading in crowded camps without clean water; financial losses from rebuilding, lost business and lost tourism.

Explain impacts as chains: earthquake → broken water and sewage pipes → contaminated drinking water → water-related disease in temporary camps.

## Managing impacts before, during and after (6.1.8 and 6.1.9)

| Stage | Strategy | How it reduces impacts | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | Monitoring and warning | Volcanoes: ground swelling, small earthquakes and gas changes can warn of eruption | Earthquakes cannot be predicted with an exact time; false alarms reduce trust |
| Before | Land use zoning | Keeps housing and key services away from fault lines and volcano slopes | Land may be scarce; people may live there already for fertile soil |
| Before | Structure of buildings | Flexible frames, cross-bracing, deep foundations, light roofs to resist ash load | Expensive; older buildings are hard to strengthen |
| Before | Disaster preparation | Plans, drills, emergency supplies and emergency rescue teams mean people know what to do | Needs funding and regular practice |
| During | Evacuation | Removes people and livestock from the danger zone | Needs warning time and transport; people may refuse to leave |
| During/after | Shelters | Safe places to stay | Overcrowding can spread disease |
| After | Rebuilding of damaged areas | Restores homes and services; a chance to build better | Slow and costly |
| After | International aid | Money, equipment, rescue teams, food and medicine | Can arrive late; may not reach those most in need |

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of monitoring and warning as a strategy for managing the impacts of tectonic events. **[6]**

**Answer:** For volcanoes, monitoring ground swelling, small earthquakes and gas emissions can show that magma is rising *(benefit, 1)*, which gives time to evacuate people and livestock *(benefit, 1)*. Warnings also allow emergency services to prepare *(benefit, 1)*. However, earthquakes cannot be predicted to an exact time and place, so monitoring cannot give enough warning *(limitation, 1)*. Equipment is expensive for low-income countries *(limitation, 1)*, and false alarms can make people ignore later warnings *(limitation, 1)*. Overall, monitoring is very useful for volcanoes but must be combined with building structure and preparation for earthquakes *(judgement)*.

## Opportunities from volcanoes (6.1.10)

- **Fertile soils:** weathered lava and ash release minerals, so soils on volcano slopes can be very productive.
- **Extraction of minerals:** valuable minerals and sulfur can be mined in volcanic areas.
- **Building materials:** volcanic rock such as basalt is quarried for building and roads.
- **Geothermal power:** heat in rocks near the surface is used to make steam to generate electricity and to heat buildings.
- **Tourism:** visitors come to see volcanoes, hot springs and geysers, bringing jobs and income.

These opportunities explain why many people live close to active volcanoes despite the danger.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping focus and epicentre.
- Saying volcanoes form at conservative boundaries.
- Saying earthquakes "can be predicted". Volcanic eruptions can often be forecast; the exact time of an earthquake cannot.
- Calling magma "lava" while it is still underground.
- Listing management strategies without saying whether they act before, during or after the event when the question asks.

## Exam technique

**Identify** questions need a name (a continent, a layer, a boundary). **Describe** a boundary: say which way the plates move and what happens. **Explain** distribution: link the pattern to plate boundaries and hotspots. **Discuss** strategies: give a benefit and a limitation for each and a judgement that depends on the type of hazard and the wealth of the country.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three layers of the Earth. *Crust, mantle and core.*
2. What happens at a conservative boundary? *Plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes.*
3. Name the three volcanic gases in the syllabus. *Water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.*
4. Roughly how much more energy does a magnitude 6.0 earthquake release than a 5.0? *About 32 times.*
5. Give two opportunities volcanoes provide. *Fertile soils and geothermal power.*
