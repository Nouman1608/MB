## Unit 4 Section B in one box

- Four debates are printed in section 3.4.2: free will and determinism; nature-nurture; holism and reductionism; psychology and science.
- Unit 4 is labelled a synoptic unit. The specification expects you to use "knowledge and understanding from other areas of the course" to illustrate Section A and B answers.
- Approximate Unit 4 weightings (share of the whole A-level): AO1 9%, AO2 8%, AO3 13%. Evaluation carries the most weight.
- Command words and mark schemes are not printed in the specification; your teacher will confirm them.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Free will | People can make genuine choices that are not fully caused by internal or external forces |
| Determinism | Every behaviour has prior causes and could in principle be predicted |
| Hard determinism | All behaviour is caused; free will is an illusion |
| Soft determinism | Behaviour is caused, but conscious choice still operates within those limits |
| Biological determinism | Behaviour caused by genes, brain structures, hormones or neurochemistry |
| Environmental determinism | Behaviour caused by the environment, mainly through learning |
| Causal explanation | An account that identifies what produces a behaviour; tested by manipulating an IV and measuring a DV |
| Heredity | Characteristics passed from parents to offspring through genes |
| Environment | Every non-genetic influence, from conditions before birth to culture |
| Interactionist approach | Nature and nurture influence each other, so behaviour results from both together |
| Reductionism | Explaining behaviour by breaking it into smaller, more basic parts |
| Holism | Studying the person or group as a whole, because the whole has properties the parts lack |
| Levels of explanation | Biological, basic behavioural, cognitive, and social or cultural accounts of the same behaviour |
| Biological reductionism | Explaining behaviour at the physical level: genes, neurotransmitters, brain areas |
| Stimulus-response (environmental) reductionism | Explaining behaviour as learned links between stimuli and responses |
| Objectivity | Findings not affected by the researcher's expectations or feelings |
| Empirical method | Gaining knowledge through observation and measurement |
| Replicability | A study can be repeated with the same method and give similar results |
| Falsifiability | A theory makes predictions that could, in principle, be shown false |
| Hypothetico-deductive method | Theory produces a hypothesis; data test it; the theory is supported, revised or rejected |

## Studies and theories at a glance

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skinner (1971), *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* | Behaviourist view of freedom | Behaviour is shaped by reinforcement; the "autonomous" person is an illusion | Hard to falsify: any act can be called "reinforced" after the event |
| Bandura (1986), reciprocal determinism | Social cognitive theory | Behaviour, environment and personal factors influence each other | Soft determinism; less precise to test than single causes |
| Rotter (1966) | Locus of control | Beliefs about control lie on a continuum from internal to external | Links beliefs about choice to real behaviour, such as resisting pressure |
| Vohs and Schooler (2008) | Reading a determinist passage | Participants were more likely to cheat on a later test | Later studies have reported contradictory findings |
| Baillargeon (1987) | Violation of expectation | Some infants as young as 3.5 months looked longer at impossible events | Looking time may reflect novelty, not knowledge |
| Maguire et al. (2000) | London taxi drivers, brain scans | Larger posterior hippocampus; volume correlated with time as a driver | Correlational, but the years-of-driving link supports plasticity |
| Frith (1992) | Cognitive account of schizophrenia | A fault in central monitoring of action | A cognitive level of explanation alongside the dopamine hypothesis |
| Tolman and Honzik (1930) | Rats in a maze | Rats rewarded only from day 11 quickly matched always-rewarded rats | Challenges stimulus-response reductionism: learning without reinforcement |
| Janis (1972) | Groupthink | Drive for agreement in a close group overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives | Supports holism, but group-level claims are hard to test |
| Watson (1913) | Behaviourist manifesto | Psychology should be objective natural science aiming at prediction and control | Rejected introspection; narrowed what psychology could study |
| Popper (1934; English 1959) | Falsifiability | A scientific theory must be open to refutation | Criticised theories that could explain any outcome |
| Baddeley and Hitch (1974); Baddeley (2000) | Working memory model | Episodic buffer added to fit new evidence | Shows theory revision through hypothesis testing |
| Open Science Collaboration (2015) | Replication of 100 studies | 97% of originals significant; 36% of replications significant | Failed replications do not always mean the original was false |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Hard vs soft determinism.** Both say behaviour has causes. Only soft determinism says conscious choice still operates within those causes.
- **Biological vs environmental determinism.** The cause is inside the body versus outside it, through learning.
- **Determinism vs reductionism.** Determinism is about *whether* behaviour is caused. Reductionism is about *which level* you explain it at. A theory can be one without the other: Bandura's reciprocal determinism is deterministic but not reductionist.
- **Heredity vs environment vs interaction.** Nature, nurture, or both changing each other (plasticity, endogenous pacemakers reset by zeitgebers).
- **Biological vs stimulus-response reductionism.** Physical level versus learned stimulus-response links. Both ignore the cognitive level.
- **Falsifiable vs falsified.** Falsifiable means it *could* be shown wrong. Falsified means it *has* been.
- **Replication vs reliability.** Replication repeats a whole study, often by other researchers. Reliability is consistency of a measure, such as test-retest.
- **Objective vs empirical.** Objective is free of researcher bias. Empirical is based on observation. A study can be empirical but not objective, if the observer's expectations shape the record.

## Mini essay plan: "Discuss the view that psychology is a science." (extended response)

1. **Define (AO1).** Objectivity, the empirical method, replicability, falsifiability, theory construction and hypothesis testing.
2. **Point for (AO1 + example).** Behaviourism set out to be objective (Watson, 1913) and produced testable laws of learning.
3. **Point for (AO2 + example).** The working memory model was revised in 2000 after new evidence: the hypothetico-deductive cycle working as it should.
4. **Point for (AO3).** Baillargeon's looking-time research tested and challenged Piaget's timing, so developmental theory is falsifiable.
5. **Point against (AO3).** The Open Science Collaboration (2015) found significant results in 36% of replications, against 97% of originals.
6. **Point against (AO3).** Participants react to being studied (demand characteristics), and measures such as looking time still need interpretation.
7. **Counter-point (AO3).** The replication problem was found and is being corrected by scientific methods.
8. **Judgement.** Psychology uses scientific methods well in some areas (biological, cognitive) and less in others; "is it a science?" depends on which part you mean.

## Quick self-test

1. What does soft determinism claim?
2. Which type of determinism does Skinner's view of behaviour represent?
3. Why does the experimental method assume determinism?
4. What does "relative importance" of heredity and environment ask you to do?
5. How does the sleep/wake cycle illustrate the interactionist approach?
6. Name the two kinds of reductionism printed in the specification.
7. What did Tolman and Honzik (1930) show that challenges stimulus-response reductionism?
8. What is the difference between "falsifiable" and "falsified"?
9. What did the Open Science Collaboration (2015) find?
10. Why was the episodic buffer added to the working memory model?
11. Give one reason looking-time studies may not show what infants know.

### Answers

1. Behaviour has causes, but people can still make conscious choices within those limits.
2. Hard, environmental determinism.
3. It manipulates an IV to find its effect on a DV, which only makes sense if behaviour has regular causes.
4. Weigh how much each contributes, rather than choosing one.
5. An inherited endogenous pacemaker sets a rhythm, but exogenous zeitgebers such as light reset it.
6. Biological reductionism and stimulus-response (environmental) reductionism.
7. Rats that were not rewarded for 10 days had still learned the maze, which showed once food was added on day 11.
8. Falsifiable means it could be shown false; falsified means evidence has shown it false.
9. Of 100 studies, 97% of the originals had significant results, but only 36% of the replications did.
10. Some amnesic patients recalled more of a story in the short term than the phonological loop could hold.
11. Longer looking may reflect novelty or perceptual interest rather than understanding.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "determinism" with no type. Say whether it is hard or soft, biological or environmental.
- Mixing up determinism and reductionism. One concerns causes; the other concerns levels of explanation.
- Claiming Piaget is "the nature side". His theory needs experience to drive accommodation, so it is interactionist.
- Calling Maguire et al. (2000) proof that experience changes the brain. It is correlational; say so and then use the years-of-driving link.
- Listing levels of explanation without applying them to one behaviour.
- Defining falsifiability as "proved wrong".
- Using the replication figures without saying what they show about objectivity or scientific status.
- Giving a debate answer with no example from the rest of 9685, when the specification asks you to use one.
- Ending an extended answer without a judgement. AO3 has the largest Unit 4 weighting.
