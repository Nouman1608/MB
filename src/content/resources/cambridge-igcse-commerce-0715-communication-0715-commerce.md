Communication is the first aid to trade named in Topic 1: buyers and sellers cannot trade until they can exchange information. Section 2.6 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has three parts: the **types of communication** (written, oral, electronic and visual), the **methods** (letter, telephone, face to face, videoconferencing, email, web-based chat including chatbots, social media and posters) and the **factors** that decide which method is appropriate (urgency, cost, security, distance, availability of networks and importance of the message). Paper 2 questions usually describe a situation and ask you to recommend a method, so the skill is matching. This page goes further than the communication section of the [Commercial Operations study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-commerce-commercial-operations/), with more on each method and worked recommendations.

## What are the four types of communication? (2.6.1)

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Written | Words set down on paper or screen, giving a permanent record | Letter, email, report, contract |
| Oral | Spoken communication, in person or at a distance | Face-to-face meeting, telephone call |
| Electronic | Communication sent through digital devices and networks | Email, web-based chat, social media, videoconferencing |
| Visual | Information given through images, signs, charts or video | Poster, chart, product video, logo |

The types overlap. An email is both **written** and **electronic**; a videoconference is **oral**, **visual** and **electronic**. If a question asks you to distinguish types, give the main feature of each type and an example that fits it best.

## What methods of communication are there? (2.6.2)

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Letter | Formal; permanent record; can include signatures and enclosures; suits people without internet | Slow; postage cost; no instant reply |
| Telephone | Instant two-way conversation; tone of voice helps understanding; questions answered at once | No written record unless recorded; both people must be available; time zones |
| Face to face | Body language and tone show meaning; builds trust; best for sensitive or complex issues | Travel time and cost; must meet in one place; no record unless notes are taken |
| Videoconferencing | Face-to-face contact over distance; several people in different places; screens and documents shared | Needs reliable internet and equipment; delays or dropped connections; harder to read body language |
| Email | Fast and cheap; written record; attachments; one message to many people | Can be ignored or lost among other emails; risk of hacking or sending to the wrong person |
| Web-based chat (including chatbots) | Instant help on a website; chatbots answer common questions 24 hours a day at low cost | Chatbots cannot handle unusual or complex problems; customers may find them frustrating |
| Social media | Reaches very large audiences cheaply; two-way, so customers can reply and share | Negative comments spread quickly; messages are public; hard to control |
| Posters | Visual and eye-catching; seen by everyone passing; cheap to produce | One-way; limited information; only seen in one place |

A **chatbot** is a computer program that answers customer questions automatically in a chat window, using set answers or artificial intelligence. Businesses use chatbots to handle simple, repeated questions (opening times, order tracking) and pass complex ones to human staff.

## Which factors affect the choice of method? (2.6.3)

The syllabus names six factors. Each one pushes the choice in a particular direction:

| Factor | Question to ask | Methods it favours |
|---|---|---|
| Urgency | How quickly must the message arrive, or a reply come back? | Telephone, web-based chat, videoconferencing, email |
| Cost | What can the business afford, per message and in equipment? | Email, social media, chat; avoid travel for face to face |
| Security | Is the information confidential? | Face to face, registered letter, encrypted email; avoid social media |
| Distance | How far apart are sender and receiver? | Videoconferencing, email, telephone for long distances |
| Availability of networks | Does the receiver have internet or phone coverage? | Letter or telephone where internet is poor |
| Importance of the message | Does it need a record, signature or personal touch? | Letter or email for a record; face to face for serious matters |

Most situations involve two or three factors, sometimes pulling in opposite directions. A strong answer names the factors in the scenario, recommends a method and says why it beats an alternative.

## Worked example: matching method to situation

| Situation | Key factors | Recommended method | Why not the obvious alternative? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A factory's machine has broken and it needs an engineer today | Urgency | Telephone | Email may not be read in time |
| A bank must send a customer a new PIN | Security, importance | Letter by post or a secure app message | Social media or ordinary email could be seen by others |
| A sales team in four countries needs to plan a product launch | Distance, cost, need for discussion | Videoconferencing | Face to face would cost a lot in flights and time |
| A shop wants to tell local people about a weekend sale | Cost, audience | Posters in the shop window and social media | A letter to every household is slow and costly |
| A supplier must send a signed contract to a buyer in a rural area with weak internet | Importance, availability of networks | Letter by recorded post | Email depends on a reliable connection |
| A manager must tell an employee that their job is changing | Importance, sensitivity | Face to face, followed by a letter | Email feels impersonal and cannot respond to the employee's reaction |
| An online shop receives hundreds of "where is my order?" questions daily | Cost, urgency, 24-hour demand | Web-based chatbot, with option to reach a person | Telephone staff for every query would be expensive |

## Annotated model answer

*Question: TravelEase, a travel agency, wants to confirm flight bookings for its customers. Recommend a method of communication and justify your choice.* **[4]**

> TravelEase should use **email** [1]. Bookings need a **written record** of flight times, prices and booking references that the customer can keep and show at the airport, which a telephone call does not give [1]. Email is also **cheap and fast**, so many confirmations can be sent in seconds, and attachments such as **etickets** can be included [1]. Although email could be hacked, the importance of a permanent record and its low cost make it more suitable than a letter, which would be too slow for customers travelling soon [1].

Why it scores: a clear recommendation, two factors from the syllabus applied to TravelEase, and a comparison with alternatives.

## What are the common mistakes in 2.6?

- Confusing **types** (written, oral, electronic, visual) with **methods** (letter, email and so on).
- Recommending a method without naming the factor that makes it suitable.
- Saying email is always the best because it is cheap; security, urgency or importance may point elsewhere.
- Treating videoconferencing as having no costs; it needs equipment and reliable networks.
- Forgetting that a chatbot cannot deal with complex or sensitive queries.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify**: name the type or method.
- **Explain**: give one advantage or disadvantage and link it to the situation.
- **Recommend** or **justify**: choose one method, apply two factors from the scenario, and explain why it beats an alternative.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the benefits and drawbacks of a method, such as replacing call-centre staff with a chatbot, and reach a judgement.
