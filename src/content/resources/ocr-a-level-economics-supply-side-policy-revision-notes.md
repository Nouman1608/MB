## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply-side policy | Measures that raise the quality or quantity of factors of production, or make markets work better, so potential output grows |
| Marginal tax rate | Share of each extra unit of income taken in tax |
| Effective marginal rate | Tax plus benefits lost on each extra unit earned |
| National Minimum Wage | Legal wage floor |
| Living wage | Wage linked to the cost of living, promoted by government or others, which employers may choose to pay |
| Human capital | Skills and knowledge of the workforce |
| Geographical and occupational mobility | Ease of moving place or job |
| Privatisation | Transfer of ownership from public to private sector |
| Deregulation | Removing rules that restrict entry or add costs |
| Crowding in | Public investment that encourages more private investment |

## The twelve measures at a glance

| Measure | Factor changed | Main benefit | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education and training | Quality of labour | Higher productivity, mobility | Slow, costly, may not match needs |
| Tax and benefit reform | Quantity of labour | Work pays more | Revenue cost, inequality |
| Lower marginal tax rates | Labour supply, enterprise | Reward for effort | Income effect, revenue loss |
| NMW, living wage | Incentive to work | Raises pay of the low-paid | Possible job losses, costs |
| Labour market flexibility, housing | Mobility | Fewer vacancies and unemployed | Insecurity, slow house building |
| Immigration | Quantity and quality of labour | Fills shortages | Pressure on services, per-head effects |
| Privatisation | Efficiency of capital | Lower costs, innovation | Private monopoly, social losses |
| Deregulation | Entry, costs | More competition | Weaker protection |
| Trade union reform | Labour cost and flexibility | Fewer strikes | Lower voice and morale |
| Infrastructure, transport | Quantity and quality of capital | Lower business costs | Large cost, long delay |
| R&D incentives | Technology | Innovation, spillovers | Cost, uncertain results |
| Subsidies | Cost of production | More output, lower price | Cost to taxpayers, inefficiency |

## The diagram

Describe it in words. LRAS1 shifts right to LRAS2. The price level falls from P1 to P2 and real output rises from Y1 to Y2. Say that potential output and the trend rate of growth rise. Policies that mainly raise demand shift AD, not LRAS.

## Formulas and methods

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Tax on income | Apply each rate to the slice of income in that band |
| Effective marginal rate | 1 − (take-home from extra pay ÷ extra pay) |
| Employment change | Wage elasticity of demand for labour × % change in wage |
| Net present value | Present value of benefits − cost |
| GDP per head | GDP ÷ population |
| Subsidy cost | Subsidy per unit × new quantity |

### Method in steps: effective marginal rate

1. Take the extra pay, for example 100.
2. Subtract tax at the marginal rate.
3. Subtract benefit lost on the net amount.
4. Compare what is kept with the 100.

Example: tax 30%, benefit loss 45% of net. Net = 70. Benefit lost = 31.5. Kept = 38.5. Effective rate = 61.5%.

## Small worked reminders

- A worker with a 30% marginal rate gets a 4,000 pay rise and keeps 2,800.
- NMW up 8%, elasticity −0.5: employment falls 4%.
- Training costs 1,500 and adds 400 a year for 5 years at 5%: present value = 400 × 4.3295 = 1,732; net present value = +232.
- Trend growth of 3% against 2% for 5 years: index 115.9 against 110.4, about 5% higher.
- Subsidy 6 per unit and output 50: cost 300.
- A state firm cuts unit cost from 40 to 36: down 10%.
- A worker gains 2,000 from moving but pays 2,600 more rent: net −600, so does not move. If the rent gap falls to 1,200, net = +800.

## Must-know distinctions

- Supply-side policy shifts LRAS; demand-side policy shifts AD.
- Quality of factors (skills, technology) against quantity (more workers, more capital).
- NMW (compulsory) against a living wage (voluntary or promoted).
- GDP (total) against GDP per head (average).
- Private return against social return, since spillovers justify R&D support.
- Market-based policies (deregulation, tax cuts) against interventionist ones (subsidies, training funded by the state).

## Subsidies and R&D: the logic

A subsidy shifts the supply curve down or right by the amount per unit. Equilibrium quantity rises, the price consumers pay falls, and producers receive more than before, because they get the price plus the subsidy. Who gains more depends on the slopes of demand and supply: the steeper curve side gets the bigger share. Cost to government = subsidy per unit × new quantity.

R&D incentives work when the private return is below the cost but the social return, including spillovers, is above it. Example: cost 8m, private return 6.5m, tax credit 25% cuts the cost to 6m, so the firm goes ahead. If the social return is 11m the credit costing 2m is worth it.

## Effectiveness against each objective

| Objective | How supply-side policy helps | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Raises potential output and the trend rate | Long lags |
| Low inflation | Lower costs, more capacity | Weak if costs rise elsewhere |
| Low unemployment | Better skills and mobility reduce structural unemployment | Does not cure demand-deficient unemployment |
| Balance of payments | Better competitiveness, more exports | Depends on rivals and exchange rates |
| Income distribution | Higher employment and pay at the bottom | Tax cuts and weaker unions may widen gaps |
| Government finances | Higher output means more tax revenue | Upfront cost of spending and subsidies |

## Chains of reasoning to learn

1. Lower marginal tax → more reward for extra work → more hours and effort, more people enter work → more labour supply → higher potential output. Limit: the income effect, and lost revenue.
2. Training → higher skills → higher productivity → lower unit labour costs → better competitiveness and room for growth without inflation. Limit: delay and poor match.
3. Privatisation → profit motive → lower costs → lower prices, more investment. Limit: private monopoly power.
4. Infrastructure → lower transport costs → firms more profitable → more investment (crowding in). Limit: long lags and big fiscal cost.
5. Immigration → more workers → LRAS right → growth. Limit: per-head output may not rise.

## Short-run and long-run view

In the short run, some measures raise costs or cut demand: a sharp rise in the minimum wage may raise prices, and job losses follow privatisation when firms cut staff. In the long run the gains come through higher productivity and capacity. Say which time period your point refers to, and use it to justify a final judgement.

## Evaluation prompts

- Time lag: how long before the policy works?
- Size of cost against size of benefit.
- Elasticity: how responsive are workers and firms?
- Spare capacity: is demand strong enough to use the new capacity?
- Who gains and who loses, and what happens to inequality?
- Which objective matters most in the context given?

## Quick self-test

1. Say what is meant by supply-side policy.
2. A worker with a 30% marginal tax rate gets a 4,000 pay rise. How much is kept?
3. Wage elasticity of demand for labour is −0.5 and the NMW rises 8%. Find the change in employment.
4. Tax is 30% and 45% of net earnings is lost in benefits. Find the effective marginal rate on an extra 100.
5. Training costs 1,500 and adds 400 a year for 5 years (annuity factor 4.3295 at 5%). Find the net present value.
6. State two ways immigration may raise potential output.
7. A subsidy of 6 per unit raises output to 50. Find the cost.
8. Distinguish a National Minimum Wage from a living wage.
9. Why might privatisation not improve efficiency?
10. State one reason the housing market matters for labour flexibility.

### Answers

1. Policy that raises the quality or quantity of factors of production, shifting LRAS right.
2. 4,000 × 0.70 = 2,800.
3. −0.5 × 8% = −4%.
4. Net 70; benefit lost 31.5; kept 38.5; rate 61.5%.
5. 400 × 4.3295 = 1,732 (rounded); minus 1,500 = +232.
6. More workers (quantity); skills of migrants raise quality; fills shortages.
7. 6 × 50 = 300.
8. NMW is a legal floor; a living wage relates to cost of living and is promoted, not compelled.
9. A private monopoly with no competition may raise prices and has no pressure to cut costs.
10. High rents and housing shortages stop workers moving to jobs, leaving vacancies and regional unemployment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating supply-side policy as demand-side, with the wrong curve shifted.
- Listing measures without a chain of reasoning to an objective.
- Saying a policy "will" work, with no judgement of size, time or cost.
- Giving the NMW effect without mentioning elasticity.
- Mixing up average and marginal tax rates.
- Forgetting that GDP per head can fall when GDP rises.
- Ignoring spare capacity and weak demand.
- A conclusion that repeats points without choosing between them.
