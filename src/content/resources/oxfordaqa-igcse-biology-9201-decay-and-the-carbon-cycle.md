## Decay: materials return to the environment

Organisms take materials from their surroundings for growth and other processes, such as carbon, nitrogen and water. These materials
are returned to the environment in two ways: in **waste materials** (such as faeces and urine) and when living things
**die and decay**.

**Materials decay because they are broken down (digested) by microorganisms.** The microorganisms release enzymes onto the
dead material and absorb the soluble products. Some animals feed on the dead bodies and waste, and they are called
detritus feeders. Together with the microorganisms, they are responsible for the break down of dead organisms and waste.

The decay process **releases substances that plants need to grow**, such as mineral ions. Without decay, those substances
would stay locked in dead bodies, and eventually the living world would run out of nutrients.

### Conditions for decay

Microorganisms are more active and digest materials faster in **warm, moist, aerobic** conditions.

| Condition | Why it speeds up decay |
|---|---|
| Warm | Enzymes work faster, and microorganisms grow and reproduce faster; too hot and enzymes are damaged |
| Moist | Microorganisms need water to be active and to absorb the soluble products |
| Aerobic | Oxygen is needed for the aerobic respiration that supplies the energy they need to grow and reproduce |

If any of these is missing, decay is slower. A frozen, dry or waterlogged environment preserves dead material for a
long time. Peat bogs are waterlogged and acidic with little oxygen, so decay is very slow and plant remains build up.

### Worked example 1: comparing four composting conditions

Invented data. Four sealed bags each held 40.0 g (dry mass) of identical grass clippings. After 28 days the dry mass remaining
was measured.

| Condition | Dry mass left after 28 days (g) |
|---|---|
| Warm, moist, with air | 12.0 |
| Warm, moist, waterlogged (little oxygen) | 31.0 |
| Cold, moist, with air | 29.0 |
| Warm, dry, with air | 34.0 |

```
mass lost = 40.0 − mass left
warm, moist, air:  40.0 − 12.0 = 28.0 g   → 28.0 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = 70.0 %   → 28.0 ÷ 28 = 1.0 g per day
waterlogged:       40.0 − 31.0 = 9.0 g    → 22.5 %                       → 0.32 g per day
cold, moist, air:  40.0 − 29.0 = 11.0 g   → 27.5 %                       → 0.39 g per day
warm, dry, air:    40.0 − 34.0 = 6.0 g    → 15.0 %                       → 0.21 g per day
```

Interpretation: the fastest decay is in the warm, moist, aerobic bag, and each missing condition slows decay. The driest
bag decays slowest, which shows that water is a limiting factor, and the waterlogged bag shows that oxygen matters. Because the
only difference between the bags is the condition tested, the comparison is fair. The loss of mass is mostly carbon
leaving as carbon dioxide from the respiration of the microorganisms, which links decay to the carbon cycle.

### Worked example 2: heat from decay

A compost heap rises from 20 °C to 55 °C in 4 days.

```
rise = 55 − 20 = 35 °C
mean rate = 35 ÷ 4 = 8.75 °C per day
```

The warming is evidence that the microorganisms are respiring, and some of the energy transferred is released as
heat. The specification says that by the time the microorganisms and detritus feeders have broken down the waste and
the dead bodies, **all the energy originally absorbed by green plants and algae has been transferred**.

## A stable community: balance

In a stable community, the processes that remove materials are **balanced** by the processes that return them. The
materials are part of a constant cycle. If the removal exceeds the return, the supply of a material runs down. If the
return exceeds the removal, it builds up.

## The carbon cycle

The constant cycling of carbon is called the **carbon cycle**. The specification lists these steps.

1. Carbon dioxide is removed from the environment by green plants and algae during **photosynthesis**.
2. The carbon is used to make **carbohydrates, fats and proteins**, which make up the body of plants and algae.
3. When green plants and algae **respire**, some of the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and is released into the atmosphere.
4. When plants and algae are **eaten** by animals, and those animals are eaten by other animals, some carbon becomes
   part of the **fats and proteins** in the consumers.
5. When animals **respire**, some of the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and is released.
6. When plants, algae and animals **die**, some animals and microorganisms **feed** on their bodies.
7. Carbon is released as carbon dioxide when the **microorganisms respire**.
8. By the time microorganisms and detritus feeders have broken down the waste and dead bodies, all the energy originally absorbed
   has been transferred.
9. **Combustion** of wood and fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

The key idea is that carbon moves in two forms. In the air it is **carbon dioxide**. In living things it is in
**carbohydrates, fats and proteins**. Photosynthesis moves carbon from the air into organisms, and respiration and
combustion return it.

| From | To | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide in the air | Carbohydrates, fats and proteins in plants and algae | Photosynthesis, then making other compounds |
| Plants and algae | Fats and proteins in animals | Feeding |
| Plants, algae and animals | Dead bodies and waste | Death and waste materials |
| Dead bodies and waste | Microorganisms and detritus feeders | Feeding, decay |
| Plants, algae, animals and microorganisms | Carbon dioxide in the air | Respiration |
| Wood and fossil fuels | Carbon dioxide in the air | Combustion |

### Worked example 3: a carbon budget for a stable forest

Invented data, in arbitrary units of carbon per year.

| Process | Carbon (units per year) |
|---|---|
| Removed by photosynthesis | 640 |
| Returned by plant respiration | 250 |
| Returned by animal respiration | 40 |
| Returned by respiration of microorganisms in decay | 330 |
| Returned by combustion of wood | 20 |

```
total returned = 250 + 40 + 330 + 20 = 640 units
net change in atmospheric carbon = 640 returned − 640 removed = 0
```

Interpretation: the forest is stable, because removal equals return. Now suppose combustion increases by 30 units
because of fossil fuel burning, with nothing else changing.

```
returned = 250 + 40 + 330 + 50 = 670 units
net change = 670 − 640 = +30 units added to the atmosphere each year
```

The extra 30 units is not balanced by removal, so carbon dioxide builds up in the atmosphere. The link to human
effects on the environment is developed in 3.3.4.

### Worked example 4: tracing a carbon atom

Invented stimulus. "A carbon atom in carbon dioxide in the air ends up as carbon dioxide again after passing through a
grass plant, a rabbit, a fox and decomposers. Describe the route."

1. Photosynthesis in the grass: the carbon in carbon dioxide is used to make carbohydrates.
2. The rabbit eats the grass: the carbon becomes part of the fats and proteins that make up the rabbit.
3. The fox eats the rabbit: some carbon becomes part of the fox.
4. The fox dies: microorganisms and detritus feeders feed on its body.
5. The microorganisms respire: carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere.

At every step there is a shorter route that returns carbon dioxide directly to the air, because the grass, rabbit and
fox all respire. A complete answer states the route and notes that respiration returns carbon dioxide at each stage.

## Applying the principles

The specification says students should be able to **apply the principles of the carbon cycle**. This means a question
may give a new situation, such as a forest being cleared, a peat bog, an aquarium or a sealed jar, and ask what will
happen to carbon dioxide levels. Work through the cycle and ask whether photosynthesis, respiration, decay or combustion
changes in rate.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that decay "makes energy". Decay transfers energy through the respiration of microorganisms.
- Writing that microorganisms "eat" using a mouth; they digest materials with enzymes.
- Forgetting that plants also respire and release carbon dioxide.
- Listing only photosynthesis and respiration, and leaving out decay and combustion.
- Saying carbon dioxide is "removed" by animals.
- Confusing "aerobic" with "airtight". Aerobic conditions have oxygen.
- Writing "the carbon is used up". Carbon is never used up, only moved.

## Exam technique

- When asked **why** decay is slower in a given condition, name the missing condition (warm, moist or aerobic) and
  link it to microorganism activity.
- In carbon cycle questions, name the **form** of the carbon at each stage (carbon dioxide, carbohydrates, fats and
  proteins).
- For budget calculations, add the returns, subtract the removals and state whether the amount in the air rises, falls
  or stays the same.
- Link decay to plant growth by saying that it **releases substances that plants need**.
