## Section A

**1.** Within what period must an application be made to the European Court of Human Rights after the final domestic decision? A: one month. B: three months. C: four months. D: one year. **[1]**

**2.** Which body supervises the execution of judgments of the European Court of Human Rights? A: the European Commission. B: the Committee of Ministers. C: the Grand Chamber. D: the Supreme Court. **[1]**

**3.** State **two** requirements an applicant must satisfy before an application to Strasbourg is admissible. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the three grounds of judicial review set out in Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service (1985). **[3]**

**5.** State what section 7 of the Human Rights Act 1998 requires of a claimant and the normal time limit it sets. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the difference between individual measures and general measures in the execution of a Strasbourg judgment, giving an example of a general measure taken by the United Kingdom. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by a "hybrid" public authority under section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998, referring to YL v Birmingham City Council (2007). **[4]**

**8.** Explain how courts approach awards of damages under section 8 of the Human Rights Act 1998. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the stages a claimant must pass through to bring a claim for judicial review. **[6]**

**10.** Explain the relationship between domestic courts and the European Court of Human Rights under section 2 of the Human Rights Act 1998, referring to two cases. **[4]**

**11.** *Nadia's local council refuses to let her hold a vigil in a park, giving no reasons and without considering her Article 11 rights. Seven weeks later she asks what she can do.* Advise Nadia on how she could challenge the decision in the domestic courts. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss how effective the European Court of Human Rights is in enforcing Convention rights against the United Kingdom. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: four months. **[1]**

**2.** B: the Committee of Ministers. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: the applicant must have **exhausted domestic remedies**; applied **within four months** of the final decision; be a **victim**; the application must not be anonymous, substantially the same as an earlier one, manifestly ill-founded or an abuse; the applicant must have suffered a **significant disadvantage** [1] [1].

**4.** **Illegality** [1], **irrationality** [1] and **procedural impropriety** [1].

**5.** The claimant must be a **victim** of the unlawful act, in the same sense as Article 34 of the Convention [1]. Proceedings must normally be brought within **one year** of the act complained of, or a longer period the court considers equitable [1].

**6.** **Individual measures** put right the violation for the claimant [1], such as paying just satisfaction or reopening proceedings. **General measures** prevent similar violations in future, usually by **changing law or practice** [1]. The **Committee of Ministers** supervises both [1]. Example: after **Malone v UK (1984)** Parliament passed the **Interception of Communications Act 1985** (or Contempt of Court Act 1981 after Sunday Times; Gender Recognition Act 2004 after Goodwin; Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 after S and Marper) [1].

**7.** Core public authorities such as the police must act compatibly in everything they do, but a **hybrid** body is bound only when performing **functions of a public nature** [1]. In **YL v Birmingham City Council** the House of Lords held that a **private care home** providing care arranged and paid for by a council was **not performing a public function** [1], so residents could not rely on section 6 against it [1]. Parliament **reversed** this for publicly arranged care in **section 73 of the Care Act 2014** [1].

**8.** Damages are awarded only where **necessary to afford just satisfaction** [1]. In **R (Greenfield) v SSHD (2005)** the House of Lords held that courts should be guided by the **principles applied by the Strasbourg Court** under Article 41 [1], and that often a **finding of a violation is itself sufficient**, so awards tend to be modest [1].

**9.** Send a **pre-action protocol letter** to the public body [1]. File a claim form in the **Administrative Court** under **CPR Part 54** [1], **promptly and in any event within three months** of the grounds arising [1]. Show **sufficient interest** (Senior Courts Act 1981 s31(3)), or victim status for HRA grounds [1]. Obtain **permission** by showing an **arguable case** [1]. At the **full hearing**, argue the grounds (illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety, proportionality) and seek a discretionary remedy such as a quashing order [1].

**10.** Section 2 requires courts to **take into account** Strasbourg case law, not to follow it as binding precedent [1]. In **R (Ullah) v Special Adjudicator (2004)** Lord Bingham said courts should keep pace with Strasbourg, "**no more, but certainly no less**" [1]. In **R v Horncastle (2009)** the Supreme Court **declined to follow** a Chamber judgment on hearsay [1], and the Grand Chamber later took account of its reasoning, showing **judicial dialogue** [1].

**11.** The council is a **core public authority** under section 6 and must act compatibly with **Article 11** [1]. Nadia is directly affected, so she is a **victim** under section 7 and has sufficient interest [1]. She should seek **judicial review**: after a pre-action letter, file a claim **promptly** and within **three months**; at seven weeks she must act quickly [1]. Grounds: **illegality** (acting incompatibly with Article 11 contrary to section 6) [1] and failure to show the refusal is **proportionate** (Daly), plus possible **procedural impropriety** for giving no reasons [1]. Remedies: a **quashing order** and possibly a **mandatory order** or declaration; damages under section 8 only if necessary [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- Judgments have produced real **legislative change** in the UK: Contempt of Court Act 1981, Interception of Communications Act 1985, Gender Recognition Act 2004, Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 [1].
- The **Committee of Ministers** gives political pressure for compliance, and infringement proceedings now exist [1].
- But the Court **cannot quash** UK law or decisions; compliance depends on the state [1].
- **Hirst (No 2)** shows that political resistance can delay compliance for over a decade [1].
- **Admissibility filters** (exhaustion, four months, significant disadvantage) mean most applications never reach a judgment [1].
- Applications take **years**, so remedies come long after the harm [1].
- Since the HRA, most issues are resolved **domestically**, so Strasbourg's role is now mainly supervisory, which reflects **subsidiarity** [1].
- **Reasoned conclusion**: effective as a long-term influence and backstop, less effective as a quick remedy for individuals [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Sending the claimant to Strasbourg before domestic remedies are used.
- Quoting the old six-month limit.
- Mixing up the HRA one-year limit and the judicial review three-month limit.
- Describing judicial review as an appeal on the merits.
- Claiming the European Court can strike down UK legislation.
