## 1. The ten ratios

The syllabus says the formulas in its ratio list are the only ones accepted in answers, so learn them exactly.

| Ratio | Formula | Show the answer as |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | gross profit / revenue x 100 | % |
| Mark-up | gross profit / cost of sales x 100 | % |
| Profit margin | profit for the year / revenue x 100 | % |
| ROCE (return on capital employed) | profit for the year before interest / capital employed x 100 | % |
| Current (working capital) ratio | current assets : current liabilities | a ratio, such as 2.50 : 1 |
| Acid test (liquid) ratio | (current assets - inventory) : current liabilities | a ratio |
| Rate of inventory turnover | cost of sales / average inventory | times |
| Inventory turnover | average inventory / cost of sales x 365 | days |
| Trade receivables turnover | trade receivables / credit sales x 365 | days |
| Trade payables turnover | trade payables / credit purchases x 365 | days |

- **Average inventory** is opening inventory plus closing inventory, divided by 2. Both inventory ratios use it.
- **Credit figures.** If a question gives total revenue and cash sales, subtract to find credit sales. Do the same for purchases.
- **Capital employed** is issued shares plus reserves plus non-current liabilities. Reserves include a general reserve and retained earnings. As a check, it equals total assets less current liabilities.
- **ROCE** adds interest on the non-current liabilities back to profit for the year, because the lenders' capital is in the denominator.
- **Margin and mark-up** share one gross profit but divide by different figures. Cost of sales is smaller than revenue, so mark-up is the larger percentage.

This guide gives every answer to 2 decimal places.

## 2. Worked example: Ferndale Paints Limited

Ferndale Paints Limited is a paint wholesaler with a 30 September year end. From October 2025 it won lower prices from its main supplier, kept selling prices unchanged, opened an online shop and tightened inventory control. It allows trade customers 45 days to pay and receives 60 days' credit from suppliers. Credit sales were 480,000 (2025) and 552,000 (2026); credit purchases were 296,000 and 330,000. In 2026 it borrowed a further 40,000 at 7%, bought vehicles and equipment, and paid dividends of 25,000. Depreciation of 45,000 is included in the 2026 expenses. All figures are in $.

```
Statement of profit or loss, year ended 30 September
                                      2025        2026
Revenue                            600,000     690,000
Opening inventory                   45,000      55,000
Purchases                          370,000     396,000
Closing inventory                  (55,000)    (48,000)
Cost of sales                      360,000     403,000
Gross profit                       240,000     287,000
Other income                         6,000       6,000
Expenses                          (168,000)   (214,000)
Loan interest                       (5,600)     (8,400)
Profit for the year                 72,400      70,600

Statement of financial position at 30 September
Non-current assets                 330,000     411,200
Inventory                           55,000      48,000
Trade receivables                   62,000      96,000
Other receivables                    3,000       4,000
Bank                                14,000       9,000
Current assets                     134,000     157,000
Trade payables                      34,000      51,000
Other payables                       6,400       8,000
Current liabilities                 40,400      59,000
Net current assets                  93,600      98,000
7% bank loan (non-current)          80,000     120,000
Net assets                         343,600     389,200
Ordinary shares                    250,000     250,000
General reserve                     40,000      40,000
Retained earnings                   53,600      99,200
Equity                             343,600     389,200
```

The ratios, with the working shown:

```
Ratio                 2025                                2026
Gross profit margin   240,000/600,000 x 100 = 40.00%      287,000/690,000 x 100 = 41.59%
Mark-up               240,000/360,000 x 100 = 66.67%      287,000/403,000 x 100 = 71.22%
Profit margin         72,400/600,000 x 100 = 12.07%       70,600/690,000 x 100 = 10.23%
ROCE                  (72,400+5,600)/423,600 x 100        (70,600+8,400)/509,200 x 100
                      = 78,000/423,600 = 18.41%           = 79,000/509,200 = 15.51%
Capital employed      250,000+93,600+80,000 = 423,600     250,000+139,200+120,000 = 509,200
Current ratio         134,000 : 40,400 = 3.32 : 1         157,000 : 59,000 = 2.66 : 1
Acid test             79,000 : 40,400 = 1.96 : 1          109,000 : 59,000 = 1.85 : 1
Average inventory     (45,000+55,000)/2 = 50,000          (55,000+48,000)/2 = 51,500
Inventory turns       360,000/50,000 = 7.20 times         403,000/51,500 = 7.83 times
Inventory days        50,000/360,000 x 365 = 50.69        51,500/403,000 x 365 = 46.64
Receivables days      62,000/480,000 x 365 = 47.15        96,000/552,000 x 365 = 63.48
Payables days         34,000/296,000 x 365 = 41.93        51,000/330,000 x 365 = 56.41
```

## 3. Comparing different years

Put the years side by side with the change in each line.

| Item | 2025 | 2026 | Change | % change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 600,000 | 690,000 | +90,000 | +15.00% |
| Cost of sales | 360,000 | 403,000 | +43,000 | +11.94% |
| Gross profit | 240,000 | 287,000 | +47,000 | +19.58% |
| Other income | 6,000 | 6,000 | 0 | 0.00% |
| Expenses | 168,000 | 214,000 | +46,000 | +27.38% |
| Loan interest | 5,600 | 8,400 | +2,800 | +50.00% |
| Profit for the year | 72,400 | 70,600 | -1,800 | -2.49% |

Profit for the year fell by 1,800 even though gross profit rose by 47,000: 47,000 + 0 - 46,000 - 2,800 = -1,800.

## 4. Interpreting the ratios

Write each comment in three parts: the ratio with both figures, the reason from the scenario, and what it means for the business.

- **Gross profit margin and mark-up** rose from 40.00% to 41.59% and from 66.67% to 71.22%. Selling prices were unchanged but purchase prices fell, so each $100 of revenue left more gross profit.
- **Profit margin** fell from 12.07% to 10.23%. Expenses rose from 28.00% to 31.01% of revenue, which absorbed the better gross profit.
- **ROCE** fell from 18.41% to 15.51%. Profit before interest rose by only 1,000 while capital employed rose by 85,600 (20.21%). The new loan and retained profit have not yet earned a matching return.
- **Current ratio and acid test** fell from 3.32 : 1 to 2.66 : 1 and from 1.96 : 1 to 1.85 : 1. Current liabilities rose by 46.04% and current assets by less. Both still cover current liabilities, but the direction is downward.
- **Inventory turnover** rose from 7.20 to 7.83 times, and inventory days fell from 50.69 to 46.64. Inventory is sold faster, so less cash is tied up. Tighter control is working.
- **Trade receivables days** rose from 47.15 to 63.48, against 45 allowed. Customers are 18.48 days over the terms.
- **Trade payables days** rose from 41.93 to 56.41, against 60 allowed. Ferndale is paying later, which helps cash, but it is now close to the limit.

## 5. Suggestions and recommendations

Match each suggestion to the weak ratio and say what it should change.

- **Profitability.** Expenses grew 27.38% against revenue growth of 15.00%. Review the online shop's advertising and delivery costs, charge for delivery and keep the supplier's lower prices. Raising prices on popular lines lifts the gross profit margin.
- **Liquidity.** Collect from customers within 45 days: send statements promptly, chase accounts that are overdue and offer a cash discount for early payment. At 45 days, trade receivables would be about 68,055, which is 27,945 less than 96,000.
- **Working capital.** Keep inventory low by ordering what sells. Pay suppliers within the 60 days, not earlier, but not so late that they withdraw credit.

## 6. Profit indicators: what changes gross profit and profit for the year

The **gross profit margin** shows the trading result before expenses; the **profit margin** shows what remains after expenses, other income and interest. A steady first margin with a falling second points to expenses. A falling first points to prices, purchase costs or inventory.

Starting from Ferndale's 2026 figures, four causes change gross profit as follows.

| Cause | Revenue | Cost of sales | Gross profit | Gross profit margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 as reported | 690,000 | 403,000 | 287,000 | 41.59% |
| Closing inventory overvalued by 4,000 | 690,000 | 399,000 | 291,000 | 42.17% |
| Selling prices 5% higher, same units | 724,500 | 403,000 | 321,500 | 44.38% |
| Purchases cost 3% more (inventory values unchanged) | 690,000 | 414,880 | 275,120 | 39.87% |
| 10% more units, same prices and costs | 759,000 | 443,300 | 315,700 | 41.59% |

Closing inventory reduces cost of sales, so an overvalued figure inflates gross profit and profit for the year equally. More units raise gross profit, not the margin. Profit for the year = gross profit + other income - expenses - interest, so it can fall while gross profit rises, as at Ferndale.

## 7. Why cash and profit differ

Ferndale made a profit for the year of 70,600, but its bank balance fell from 14,000 to 9,000.

```
Profit for the year                                70,600
Depreciation (expense, no cash paid)              +45,000
Non-current assets bought (cash, not an expense) -126,200
Decrease in inventory                               +7,000
Increase in trade receivables                      -34,000
Increase in other receivables                       -1,000
Increase in trade payables                         +17,000
Increase in other payables                          +1,600
Dividends paid                                     -25,000
New loan received (cash in, not income)            +40,000
Change in bank balance                              -5,000
```

Other reasons include capital introduced, drawings, irrecoverable debts and expenses or income paid or received in a different year.

## Common errors

- Giving a current ratio as a percentage, or without the "to 1".
- Using profit for the year after interest in ROCE.
- Using closing inventory instead of average inventory.
- Using total revenue for receivables days when credit sales are given.
- Stating a change with no reason from the scenario.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is average inventory calculated for Cambridge IGCSE Accounting 0452 ratios?**
Average inventory is opening inventory plus closing inventory, divided by 2. Both the rate of inventory turnover and inventory turnover in days use it, so using closing inventory alone is an error.

**How do you find credit sales if a question gives total revenue and cash sales?**
Subtract cash sales from total revenue to find credit sales, and do the same for purchases. Trade receivables and trade payables turnover in days use the credit figures.

**Why is mark-up always higher than gross profit margin?**
Both ratios use the same gross profit, but mark-up divides by cost of sales and margin divides by revenue. Cost of sales is smaller than revenue, so mark-up is the larger percentage.

**How should you write a comment on a ratio?**
Write it in three parts: the ratio with both years' figures, the reason from the scenario, and what it means for the business. For example, a rise in trade receivables days above the credit period allowed means customers are paying late.

**Why can a business make a profit but have less cash in the bank?**
Profit and cash differ because of items such as depreciation, buying non-current assets, loan repayments, changes in inventory, receivables and payables, and drawings or dividends. A profitable business can therefore see its bank balance fall.
