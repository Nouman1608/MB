This guide covers subtopic **8.3, Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts**, from Topic 8, Reaction kinetics, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

## What 8.3 asks you to do

The syllabus outcome is to explain and use the terms **catalyst** and **catalysis**, which means you must:

- (a) explain that, in the presence of a catalyst, a reaction has a **different mechanism, i.e. one of lower activation energy**;
- (b) explain this catalytic effect in terms of the **Boltzmann distribution**;
- (c) **construct and interpret a reaction pathway diagram** for a reaction in the presence and absence of an effective catalyst.

The subtopic title also uses the words homogeneous and heterogeneous, so you should be able to classify a catalyst by phase. Describing **how** each type works step by step (adsorption, bond weakening and desorption; or a catalyst used in one step and reformed in a later one) is A Level content in Topic 26, covered in [Reaction Kinetics: Rate Equations and Catalysis](/resources/a-reaction-kinetics-rate-equations-and-catalysis/).

## Catalyst and catalysis

A **catalyst** is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is chemically unchanged at the end. **Catalysis** is the process of speeding up a reaction in this way.

The key phrase is *different mechanism*. A catalyst does not push the reactants over the same barrier more easily. It takes part in the reaction, often forming an intermediate or a bond to the reactants, and so opens a new route. That route has a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed one. Because the catalyst is regenerated, a small amount can convert a large amount of reactant.

What a catalyst does **not** change:

- ΔH: the reactants and products are the same, so the enthalpy change is the same.
- The position of equilibrium and the equilibrium yield. The forward and reverse barriers are lowered by the same amount, so both rates increase by the same factor.
- The temperature, and so the shape of the Boltzmann distribution.

## The catalytic effect on the Boltzmann distribution

At a fixed temperature, the distribution of molecular energies does not change when a catalyst is added. What changes is the energy a collision needs. Draw one curve and **two** vertical lines: Eₐ for the uncatalysed route, and Eₐ(cat) further to the left.

The area beyond Eₐ(cat) is larger than the area beyond Eₐ. So a greater proportion of molecules have enough energy to react by the catalysed route, a greater proportion of collisions are effective, and the frequency of effective collisions increases.

| | Raising the temperature | Adding a catalyst |
|---|---|---|
| Boltzmann curve | Changes: lower, broader, peak moves right | **Unchanged** |
| Eₐ line | Unchanged | **Moves left** (new route) |
| Area beyond the relevant Eₐ | Increases | Increases |
| Collision frequency | Increases slightly | Unchanged |
| ΔH | Unchanged | Unchanged |

Examiners often ask you to compare these two. The short version: temperature moves the curve; a catalyst moves the line.

## Constructing a reaction pathway diagram

A pathway diagram has enthalpy on the vertical axis and progress of reaction on the horizontal axis. For the catalysed and uncatalysed routes on one diagram:

1. Draw reactants and products at **the same two levels** for both routes.
2. Draw the uncatalysed route as one high hump.
3. Draw the catalysed route as a lower path. If the catalyst forms an intermediate, the catalysed route has **two smaller humps** with a trough between them. The trough is the intermediate.
4. Mark Eₐ (uncatalysed) and Eₐ (catalysed) as arrows from the **reactants' level** to the top of the **highest** hump of each route.
5. Mark ΔH once, from reactants to products. It applies to both routes.

**Worked example (invented values).** A reaction has ΔH = −90 kJ mol⁻¹. The uncatalysed route has Eₐ = +240 kJ mol⁻¹. With a catalyst, the route passes through an intermediate. The first hump is 130 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants and the second hump is 95 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants.

(a) The catalysed activation energy is the height of the **highest** point above the reactants: **+130 kJ mol⁻¹**, not 95 and not 130 + 95.

(b) The reduction in Eₐ is 240 − 130 = **110 kJ mol⁻¹**.

(c) Reverse barriers: uncatalysed 240 − (−90) = 330 kJ mol⁻¹; catalysed 130 − (−90) = 220 kJ mol⁻¹. Both are lower by the same 110 kJ mol⁻¹. This is why the catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally and leaves the position of equilibrium unchanged.

### Interpreting a diagram you are given

Read these four things from any pathway diagram:

- Whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic (are the products lower or higher than the reactants?).
- Which route is catalysed (the lower maximum).
- Whether the catalysed route involves an intermediate (a trough between humps).
- The values of Eₐ for each route, and ΔH, if a scale is given.

## Homogeneous and heterogeneous: the phase test

- A **homogeneous** catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants.
- A **heterogeneous** catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants.

"Phase" is more precise than "state". Two liquids that do not mix, such as an organic layer and an aqueous layer, are different phases although both are liquids. Ask whether the catalyst and the reactants form one uniform mixture or not.

**Worked example: classify each catalyst.**

| Reaction (all met in 9701) | Catalyst | Phases | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) | Iron, Fe(s) | Solid catalyst, gaseous reactants | Heterogeneous |
| Hydrogenation of an alkene with H₂(g) | Nickel or platinum, solid | Solid catalyst, gaseous or liquid reactants | Heterogeneous |
| Removal of CO and NO from car exhaust | Platinum, palladium, rhodium on a solid support | Solid catalyst, gaseous reactants | Heterogeneous |
| Esterification of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol | Concentrated H₂SO₄ | Liquid mixture, all one phase | Homogeneous |
| Oxidation of atmospheric SO₂ | NO₂(g) | Gas catalyst, gaseous reactants | Homogeneous |
| Iodide ions with peroxodisulfate ions | Fe²⁺(aq) or Fe³⁺(aq) | All in aqueous solution | Homogeneous |

For the nitrogen dioxide example, the catalyst is used in one step and regenerated in the next:

SO₂ + NO₂ → SO₃ + NO

NO + ½O₂ → NO₂

Add the two steps and NO₂ and NO cancel, leaving SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃. Adding the steps is a quick check that a species really is a catalyst: it must appear on the left in an early step and be regenerated in a later one, so it does not appear in the overall equation.

## Common mistakes

- **"A catalyst lowers the activation energy" with nothing else.** Credit needs the idea of a different route or mechanism with a lower Eₐ.
- **Drawing a new Boltzmann curve for the catalysed reaction.** At constant temperature the curve stays the same.
- **Catalysed and uncatalysed routes ending at different levels.** That would mean a different ΔH.
- **Measuring the catalysed Eₐ to the lower hump, or adding the humps together.** Eₐ is to the highest point.
- **"A catalyst is not involved in the reaction."** It takes part and is regenerated.
- **Classifying by state alone.** Use phase.
- **"A catalyst increases the yield."** It increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached, not the equilibrium yield.

## Exam technique: command words in 8.3

- **Explain** (catalysis): "provides an alternative route / different mechanism" + "lower activation energy" + "greater proportion of molecules have E ≥ Eₐ" + "more frequent effective collisions".
- **Construct** (a pathway diagram): axes labelled, same start and end levels, catalysed maximum lower, Eₐ arrows from the reactants, ΔH shown.
- **Interpret**: read off values with signs and units, and say which route is catalysed and why.
- **State** (type of catalyst): give the type and the phase evidence, for example "heterogeneous: the solid catalyst is in a different phase from the gaseous reactants".
