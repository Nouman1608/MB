This guide covers the "Structuring and presenting the Cambridge Research Report" section of Component 4 in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for 2026-2028. The syllabus says: "The report should contain an introduction, main body and conclusion. It may include sub-headings and must include references. The report needs to be organised so that the argument is well communicated and easy to follow." The matching assessment point is to "structure their report effectively, use accurate terminology and use complete and appropriate citation and referencing (AO3)". For the component as a whole, read the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/) first. This page goes further into planning, writing and presenting the report.

## The rules the syllabus prints

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Length | up to 5000 words; "work beyond the 5000 word maximum will not be marked" |
| Word count | an accurate word count "must be clearly stated" |
| Not counted | the title and the bibliographical references |
| Form | continuous prose |
| Title | takes the form of a question |
| Audience | a non-specialist reader |
| Structure | introduction, main body, conclusion; sub-headings allowed |
| References | a bibliography is required; full references for any quotations |
| Referencing system | appropriate to the discipline; Harvard, Chicago or APA are common; use it consistently |
| Research log | a separate document, not part of the 5000 words |

AO3 (Communication) is 15% of the Component 4 marks. That is a smaller share than AO1 and AO2, but poor structure and poor referencing also make it harder to show the AO1 and AO2 skills, so the effect on the whole mark is larger than the weighting suggests.

## Planning the structure

The syllabus names three parts, but each part has a job to do. A useful plan is below. The proportions are advice, not syllabus wording, and your subject may suggest a different balance.

| Section | Job | Suggested words |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | the question, why it matters, how it developed, key concepts, outline of the report | 500 |
| Methods | the research methods, why they suit the question, limits | 600 |
| Perspective A | argument, evidence and assumptions, with assessment of sources | 1000 |
| Perspective B | the same for the contrasting view | 1000 |
| Comparison and judgement | compare the perspectives and reach a supported judgement | 1100 |
| Conclusion and reflection | answer the question, reflect on conclusions, limits, further research | 800 |
| Total | | 5000 |

Check that the numbers add up to 5000, and then aim to write **below** the limit, around 4700 to 4900 words, so that edits do not take you over.

### Introduction

A strong introduction does four things:

1. States the **research question** and why it is worth asking.
2. Explains **how and why the question was developed** (the syllabus requires this).
3. Defines the **key concepts** in plain language.
4. Gives a **short route map**: what each part of the report will do.

Because the audience is non-specialist, define technical terms the first time you use them.

### Main body

You can organise the body in two main ways.

| Plan | How it works | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| By perspective | one section per perspective, then a comparison | the debate has clear sides |
| By theme | sections on themes (for example economic, social, environmental), each comparing perspectives | the debate has several dimensions |

Whichever you choose, make each paragraph do one job and follow a clear order. A reliable pattern is a topic sentence stating the point, evidence with a citation, an assessment of the evidence, and a link back to the question.

Sub-headings help the reader, and the syllabus allows them. Use them to show the structure (for example "Perspective A: Supporters of a ban"), not to break up one argument.

### Conclusion

The conclusion should not introduce new evidence. It should:

- answer the **research question** directly;
- state the **judgement** and the main reasons for it;
- **reflect on the conclusion**: how certain it is, what limits the research had and what might change it (the syllabus lists "reflect on their conclusions (AO2)");
- suggest what **further research** would be useful.

## Writing for a non-specialist reader

The syllabus says the report "should be written for a non-specialist audience". Aim to be clear without simplifying the argument.

- Define terms and avoid unexplained jargon.
- Use accurate terminology: precise words show that you understand the concepts.
- Keep sentences short enough to follow.
- Give a reason after each claim.
- Use signposts such as "First", "However" and "This suggests that" to guide the reader.

## Citation and referencing

The syllabus requires you to "acknowledge where specific ideas and information come from" and to use "an appropriate referencing and citation system related to the academic discipline in which they are working". Many reports use Harvard, Chicago or APA, and "whatever referencing system candidates choose, they must use it consistently".

| Element | What to do |
|---|---|
| In-text citation | give the source briefly where you use it |
| Bibliography | list every source cited, in one agreed format |
| Quotations | use quotation marks; give full bibliographical references |
| Paraphrase | put the idea in your own words and still cite the source |
| Consistency | use one system throughout |

### Harvard-style example (fictional source)

In the text: "Air quality improved by 18% after the ban (Rivera and Chan, 2023)."

In the bibliography: Rivera, A. and Chan, L. (2023) *Traffic restrictions and urban air quality*. Metro University Press.

A candidate using Chicago or APA would use a different format for the same source. The important thing is that the format is **correct and consistent**.

## Authenticity and academic honesty

The syllabus requires that the report is the candidate's own work: "A candidate taking someone else's work or ideas and passing them off as his or her own is an example of plagiarism." Cambridge International uses plagiarism detection software, and a Declaration of Authenticity is completed for each candidate. Teachers should not correct or edit drafts; they may give only brief summative comments.

To protect yourself:

- note the source of every idea as you read;
- use quotation marks for exact words;
- paraphrase in your own words and still cite;
- keep your research log up to date, because it shows how your work developed.

## Managing the word count

- Write a plan with a budget for each section.
- Count as you go, and state the final count clearly on the report.
- The syllabus excludes only the title and the bibliographical references from the count. Treat everything else, including sub-headings and in-text quotations, as counting unless your teacher confirms otherwise.
- If you must cut, remove repetition first, then description, and keep argument and evidence.
- Do not use the research log to hold material that belongs in the report; the log "should not be seen as a way to extend that word limit".

## Presentation

- Number pages and use a consistent font and layout.
- Use sub-headings sparingly and clearly.
- Include a short contents list if the report is long.
- If you use a table or chart, refer to it in the text and cite its source.
- Proofread for spelling, grammar and punctuation.

## Common mistakes

- **Going over the word limit**, so the end of the report is not marked.
- **A missing or inaccurate word count.**
- **No clear introduction**, so the reader does not know what the question is.
- **A conclusion that adds new evidence** or simply summarises.
- **Mixing referencing styles**, or citing without a bibliography entry.
- **Long quotations in place of analysis.**
- **Bullet points in place of continuous prose.**
- **Technical language that a non-specialist cannot follow.**
- **Last-minute assembly**, which causes errors and missed references.

## Final checklist

- The title is a question.
- The introduction, body and conclusion are clear.
- The word count is accurate and within 5000.
- Every citation has a bibliography entry, in one consistent system.
- Terms are defined and accurate.
- The conclusion answers the question.
- The research log is a separate document.
- The proposal form and the Declaration of Authenticity are ready.
