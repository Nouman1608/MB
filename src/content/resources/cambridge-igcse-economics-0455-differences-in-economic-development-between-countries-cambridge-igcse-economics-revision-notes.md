These notes condense section 5.4 of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: the causes and impacts of differences between countries in income, productivity, population growth, sector sizes, saving and investment, education and healthcare. All figures are invented. Full explanations, worked examples and a model answer are in the [development differences study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-differences-in-economic-development-between-countries-cambridge-igcse-economics/); test yourself with the [development differences practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-differences-in-economic-development-between-countries-cambridge-igcse-economics-practice/). The combined unit on [population and differences in development](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-population-and-differences-in-development-between/) links this section to population.

## Core recall

- **Economic development:** a sustained rise in living standards, covering income, health, education and opportunities.
- **Economic growth:** a rise in real GDP. Growth can happen without development if gains reach few people.
- For each of the seven factors, learn one **cause** of the difference and one **impact**.

## The seven factors at a glance

| Factor | Why it differs (cause) | What the difference does (impact) |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Productivity, resources, capital, type of output | Low spending power, low saving, small tax base |
| Productivity | Capital per worker, technology, skills, health, sector | Sets wages: low output per worker → low pay |
| Population growth | High birth rates, falling death rates | Income per head rises slowly; more schools and jobs needed |
| Sector sizes | Little capital keeps many in small-scale farming | Low average productivity; exposure to primary price swings |
| Saving and investment | Low income leaves little to save; weak banks | Little capital per worker → low productivity |
| Education | Few schools and teachers; child labour | Low skills, slow take-up of technology |
| Healthcare | Little funding, few staff, poor sanitation | Short lives, days lost to illness, weaker learning |

## Sectors

- **Primary:** farming, fishing, forestry, mining.
- **Secondary:** manufacturing, construction.
- **Tertiary:** services such as retail, banking, tourism, education, health.
- Pattern: as countries develop, the share of workers in the primary sector falls and the tertiary share rises.

### Weighted average productivity

```
Average output per worker = sum of (share of workers x output per worker) for each sector
Shares 70 / 10 / 20, output per worker 1,500 / 7,000 / 5,000:
0.7 x 1,500 + 0.1 x 7,000 + 0.2 x 5,000 = 1,050 + 700 + 1,000 = 2,750
```

Moving workers from the low-productivity sector to a higher one raises the average even if no one's productivity changes.

## Key chains

- Low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income.
- Low income → low tax revenue → few schools and clinics → poor skills and health → low productivity.
- Low income → children valued as labour and old-age support → high birth rate → fast population growth → slow rise in income per head.
- Better education → higher productivity **and** lower birth rates → faster rise in income per head.
- Better healthcare → fewer days lost to illness → more output per worker per year.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic development | Sustained improvement in living standards, not just output |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per unit of input) |
| Primary sector | Extraction of natural resources and farming |
| Secondary sector | Manufacturing and construction |
| Tertiary sector | Services |
| Saving | Income not spent on consumption |
| Investment | Spending on capital goods such as machinery and infrastructure |
| Capital per worker | The quantity of capital each worker has to work with |

## Evaluation points

- Which factor matters most depends on the country: where disease is widespread, healthcare may come first; where there is no power or roads, investment may come first.
- Education is slow but long-lasting.
- Investment needs skilled workers to use it, and skilled workers need capital.
- Faster growth of output does not raise income per head if population grows as fast.
- Averages hide differences within a country.

## Exam traps

- Treating growth and development as the same.
- Giving a cause but no impact, or an impact but no cause.
- Saying "a large primary sector is bad" without the productivity or price-swing link.
- Comparing total GDP instead of per-head figures.
- Writing about one factor in isolation when the question invites links.
- Ending a "Discuss" answer without saying what the judgement depends on.

## Quick self-test

1. Define economic development.
2. Give an example of an activity in each of the three sectors.
3. Why is output per worker often low in the primary sector of a low-income country?
4. Shares of workers are 50 / 20 / 30 with output per worker of 2,000 / 9,000 / 6,000. Calculate average output per worker.
5. Explain one link between saving and productivity.
6. State two impacts of poor healthcare on development.
7. How can education affect population growth?
8. Real GDP grows by 4% and population by 3.5%. Roughly how much does income per head rise?
9. Give one reason why low-income countries have low saving.
10. Give one impact of relying on primary exports.
11. Why might investment in machinery fail to raise productivity in a country with low education?
12. A worker loses 20 of 250 days to illness; better healthcare cuts this to 10 days. Calculate the percentage increase in days worked.

### Answers

1. A sustained improvement in living standards, including income, health, education and opportunities.
2. Primary: farming or mining; secondary: manufacturing or construction; tertiary: banking, retail or tourism.
3. Little capital and simple technology, and small plots of land.
4. 0.5 × 2,000 + 0.2 × 9,000 + 0.3 × 6,000 = 1,000 + 1,800 + 1,800 = 4,600.
5. Saving funds investment in capital; more capital per worker raises output per worker.
6. Any two: shorter working lives; days lost to illness; children learn less; spending on treatment rather than investment.
7. Educated women tend to marry later and have fewer children, so the birth rate and population growth fall.
8. About 0.5% (exactly 1.04 ÷ 1.035 − 1 = 0.48%).
9. Most income is spent on necessities, so little is left to save (or weak banks and low confidence).
10. Export earnings swing with world prices, so income and tax revenue are unstable.
11. Workers may lack the skills to operate or maintain it.
12. Days worked rise from 230 to 240: 10 ÷ 230 × 100 = 4.3%.
