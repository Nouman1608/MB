## Exothermic and endothermic changes (3.1)

In an **exothermic** change, heat energy is given out to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings rises. In an **endothermic** change, heat energy is taken in from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings falls. The word "surroundings" matters: the thermometer measures the surroundings, usually the water or solution, not the chemicals themselves.

| Type | Examples | Temperature of surroundings | Sign of ΔH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exothermic | Combustion, neutralisation, most displacement reactions, respiration | Rises | Negative |
| Endothermic | Dissolving ammonium nitrate, thermal decomposition, photosynthesis | Falls | Positive |

A common error is to say that an exothermic reaction "makes energy". Energy is not created. It is transferred from the chemicals to the surroundings, because the chemicals end with less stored energy than they started with.

## Simple calorimetry (3.2 and 3.8)

A **calorimeter** is any apparatus used to measure the heat energy transferred. The simplest kind for a reaction in solution is a polystyrene cup with a lid, a thermometer and a stirrer. For combustion, a spirit burner heats a metal can containing a known mass of water.

**Reactions in solution (neutralisation, displacement, dissolving).**

1. Measure a known volume of one reagent (for example 25 cm³ of acid) into a polystyrene cup. Record its starting temperature.
2. Add the other reagent (a measured volume of alkali, a known mass of metal powder or a known mass of salt).
3. Stir, and record the highest temperature (or the lowest for an endothermic change).
4. Calculate the temperature change, ΔT.

Polystyrene is used because it is a good insulator, which reduces heat loss, and a lid reduces losses further. Stirring ensures the temperature is the same throughout.

**Combustion.** Weigh a spirit burner containing the fuel. Place it under a metal can containing a measured mass of water (for example 100 g), and record the water's starting temperature. Light the fuel, stir, and extinguish the flame when the temperature has risen by about 20 °C. Record the highest temperature and re-weigh the burner to find the mass of fuel burned.

**Sources of error in combustion:** heat is lost to the air and the can, the fuel burns incompletely (soot on the can) and some heat is absorbed by the can itself. All of these make the measured temperature rise too small, so the measured energy released is lower than the true value. Insulating the can, adding a draught shield and placing the flame closer to the can all help.

## Calculating heat energy change: Q = mcΔT (3.3)

```
Q = m x c x ΔT
```

- **Q** is the heat energy transferred, in joules (J).
- **m** is the mass of the substance being heated, in grams (g). For dilute solutions use the mass of water: 1 cm³ is taken as 1 g.
- **c** is the specific heat capacity, in J/g/°C. For water (and dilute aqueous solutions) it is 4.2 J/g/°C. In an exam it is given in the question.
- **ΔT** is the temperature change in °C: final temperature minus starting temperature, written as a positive number for a temperature rise.

**Worked example 1.** Calculate the heat energy needed to warm 50 g of water by 7.5 °C.

- Q = 50 x 4.2 x 7.5 = **1,575 J** (or 1.575 kJ).

**A very common mistake:** using the mass of the reactant instead of the mass of the water. The mass in Q = mcΔT is always the mass of the substance whose temperature changed, normally the water or the solution.

## Calculating molar enthalpy change, ΔH (3.4)

The **molar enthalpy change** is the heat energy change per mole of a stated substance. It is calculated in three steps.

1. Calculate Q in joules, then convert to kilojoules (divide by 1,000).
2. Calculate the moles of the substance that reacted.
3. Divide: ΔH = -Q / moles for a temperature rise (exothermic) or +Q / moles for a temperature fall (endothermic). The unit is kJ/mol.

**Worked example 2: neutralisation.** 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid is mixed with 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide. The temperature rises by 6.8 °C.

- Mass of solution = 25 + 25 = 50 g.
- Q = 50 x 4.2 x 6.8 = 1,428 J = 1.428 kJ.
- Moles of acid = 1.0 x 25 / 1,000 = 0.025 mol (the alkali is also 0.025 mol, so there is no excess).
- ΔH = -1.428 / 0.025 = **-57.1 kJ/mol** (to three significant figures).

The negative sign shows that the reaction is exothermic.

**Worked example 3: combustion of ethanol.** 0.46 g of ethanol (Mr = 46) is burned and heats 100 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.5 °C.

- ΔT = 45.5 - 20.0 = 25.5 °C.
- Q = 100 x 4.2 x 25.5 = 10,710 J = 10.71 kJ.
- Moles of ethanol = 0.46 / 46 = 0.010 mol.
- ΔH = -10.71 / 0.010 = **-1,071 kJ/mol**.

The accepted value is about -1,367 kJ/mol. The experimental figure is smaller in size because of the heat losses described above.

**Worked example 4: dissolving an endothermic salt.** 4.0 g of ammonium nitrate (Mr = 80) is dissolved in 50 g of water. The temperature falls by 5.5 °C.

- Q = 50 x 4.2 x 5.5 = 1,155 J = 1.155 kJ.
- Moles = 4.0 / 80 = 0.050 mol.
- ΔH = +1.155 / 0.050 = **+23.1 kJ/mol**.

The positive sign shows that it is endothermic.

## Energy level diagrams (3.5C, Paper 2 only)

An energy level diagram plots the energy of the chemicals against the progress of the reaction.

- **Exothermic:** the products are lower in energy than the reactants. The difference between them is the energy given out, and ΔH is negative. The arrow for ΔH points downwards.
- **Endothermic:** the products are higher in energy than the reactants, and ΔH is positive. The arrow points upwards.

Draw a horizontal line for the reactants, a horizontal line for the products, a vertical arrow between them labelled ΔH, and label the axes "energy" and "progress of reaction". Label the species on each line. Examination answers often need the sign of ΔH and a short explanation: "the products have less energy than the reactants, so energy is released to the surroundings and the reaction is exothermic".

## Bond breaking and bond making (3.6C, Paper 2 only)

Energy must be supplied to **break** bonds, so bond breaking is **endothermic**. Energy is released when new bonds are **made**, so bond making is **exothermic**. The overall energy change depends on which is bigger.

- If more energy is released making bonds than is needed to break bonds, the reaction is exothermic.
- If more energy is needed to break bonds than is released making bonds, the reaction is endothermic.

## Using bond energies (3.7C, Paper 2 only)

A **bond energy** is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond (in kJ/mol). The method has four steps.

1. Draw out the molecules showing all the bonds.
2. Add the energies of all bonds **broken** in the reactants.
3. Add the energies of all bonds **made** in the products.
4. ΔH = energy in (bonds broken) - energy out (bonds made).

**Worked example 5: burning methane.** CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Bond energies (kJ/mol): C-H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O-H 464.

- Bonds broken: 4 x C-H + 2 x O=O = 4 x 413 + 2 x 498 = 1,652 + 996 = **2,648 kJ**.
- Bonds made: 2 x C=O + 4 x O-H = 2 x 805 + 4 x 464 = 1,610 + 1,856 = **3,466 kJ**.
- ΔH = 2,648 - 3,466 = **-818 kJ/mol**, so the reaction is exothermic.

**Worked example 6: hydrogen and chlorine.** H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Bond energies: H-H 436, Cl-Cl 243, H-Cl 432.

- Broken: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ.
- Made: 2 x 432 = 864 kJ.
- ΔH = 679 - 864 = **-185 kJ/mol**.

Check each time that you have counted every bond: water has two O-H bonds per molecule, so two molecules have four.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the reactant, not the water, in Q = mcΔT.
- Forgetting to convert joules to kilojoules before dividing by moles.
- Leaving out the negative sign for an exothermic ΔH, or giving a positive sign when the temperature fell.
- Dividing by the moles of the wrong reagent. Use the limiting reagent.
- Saying bond breaking releases energy.
- Subtracting the wrong way round in bond-energy calculations. It is bonds broken minus bonds made.
- Blaming only "human error" for a low experimental result. Name heat loss, incomplete combustion or the heat absorbed by the can.

## Exam technique

State the formula, substitute the values and show every step, because most of the marks are for working. Give units (J or kJ, and kJ/mol for ΔH) and the correct sign. For a "state one improvement" question, link the improvement to its effect: "add a lid, so less heat escapes and the temperature rise is closer to the true value". Draw energy level diagrams with labelled axes, reactant and product levels, and the ΔH arrow. Remember that 3.5C to 3.7C are Paper 2 only.
