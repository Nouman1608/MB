## Section A

**1.** State one economic and one environmental indicator that could be used to judge the sustainability of a tourist destination. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the purpose of the core zone and the transition zone in a zoned protected area. **[2]**

**3.** Outline one criticism of carbon offsetting as a response to tourism emissions. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by last-chance tourism and why it is described as a paradox. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why timed entry may not reduce the environmental impact of a site. **[2]**

**6.** Suggest two ways in which rising temperatures may affect major outdoor sporting events. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An invented island has 40,000 residents. In its peak month of 30 days it records 1,500,000 visitor-days.

**(a)** Calculate the average number of visitors present on the island each day in the peak month. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio of visitors to residents on an average peak-month day. **[1]**

**(c)** Visitor-days rise by 20%. Calculate the new ratio and comment on what it suggests. **[3]**

**8.** A coastal reef destination is considering three strategies: a daily entry fee, a cap on dive-boat permits and a programme of visitor education.

**(a)** For each strategy, state one strength. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest which stakeholder is most likely to oppose the permit cap, and explain why. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how a low-altitude ski resort might adapt to climate change, and outline one drawback of your chosen adaptation. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how international sporting events are being redesigned to be more sustainable. Refer to one example. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the effectiveness of strategies used to manage tourism in one or more named destinations. **[10]**

**12.** To what extent can tourism become sustainable while international tourist numbers continue to grow? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Economic: for example the share of tourism jobs that are year-round, or the share of spending retained locally [1]. Environmental: for example water use per visitor-night, or habitat condition such as reef cover [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The core zone gives strict protection to the most sensitive habitats, with little or no tourism [1]; the transition zone contains settlements, accommodation and visitor services, where most tourism and local livelihoods are concentrated [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Offsets pay for emission reductions elsewhere but do not reduce the emissions of the flight itself [1], and the reductions they claim can be hard to verify or may have happened anyway [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Travel to see places or features, such as glaciers or coral reefs, before they decline [1]; it is a paradox because the travel adds emissions and visitor pressure that contribute to the decline the tourists come to see [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Timed entry spreads visitors through the day, reducing crowding at any one moment [1], but the total number of visitors, and so the total erosion, waste and disturbance, may be unchanged [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two, for example: events moved to cooler months [1]; events moved to cooler locations [1]; heat risk to athletes and spectators requiring changed start times or cooling measures [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 1,500,000 / 30 = **50,000 visitors per day** [1].

**(b)** 50,000 / 40,000 = **1.25** visitors per resident [1].

**(c)** New visitor-days = 1,500,000 x 1.2 = 1,800,000; 1,800,000 / 30 = 60,000 per day [1]; 60,000 / 40,000 = **1.5** [1]. Visitors already outnumber residents, and this rise suggests growing pressure on services and a greater risk of resident hostility, so the island may be moving beyond its social carrying capacity [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Entry fee: raises money for reef conservation and may reduce demand [1]. Permit cap: directly limits the number of boats and divers at the reef [1]. Education: low cost and reduces damaging behaviour such as touching coral [1].

**(b)** Dive operators [1], because a cap limits how many trips they can sell and therefore their income, especially for new businesses unable to obtain permits [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Adaptation, for example artificial snowmaking [1], which extends the season when natural snow is unreliable [1]. Alternative adaptations: moving lifts and runs to higher ground, or diversifying into year-round activities such as hiking and mountain biking. Drawback of snowmaking: it uses large amounts of water and energy [1], so it adds to emissions and competes with other water users, and becomes ineffective if temperatures are too high [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Hosts are encouraged to reuse existing venues and build temporary ones rather than new permanent stadiums [1], reducing cost, construction emissions and the risk of underused facilities [1]. Events may also be rescheduled or relocated to reduce heat risk, and compact venue plans with public transport cut spectator travel emissions [1]. Example: the International Olympic Committee's Olympic Agenda 2020 reforms of 2014 promoted existing and temporary venues, an approach used by Paris 2024 [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content; credit accurate use of named destinations:

- **Clear criteria:** effectiveness, equity, cost, enforcement, long-term viability [1].
- **Visitor management example:** licensed guides, approved itineraries and entry fees in the Galapagos protect sites and fund conservation [1]; but land-based tourism and island population growth add pressure [1].
- **Pricing example:** Bhutan's daily fee limits mass tourism and funds services [1]; but excludes lower-income visitors [1].
- **Overtourism example:** Venice's restrictions on large cruise ships and day-tripper fee [1]; critics argue short-term rentals remain the main cause of resident loss [1].
- **Community-based example:** Namibia's conservancies link wildlife income to local communities [1].
- **Comparison:** strategies are most effective where several tools are combined and enforced [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion about where and for whom management has worked [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Definition:** sustainability balances economic, social and environmental goals over the long term [1].
- **Arguments that it can:** management tools such as zoning, quotas and planning controls keep use within carrying capacity at site scale [1]; local ownership and supply chains reduce leakage [1]; certification and community-based tourism spread benefits [1].
- **Arguments that it cannot:** aviation emissions grow with numbers and are hard to cut [1]; spreading demand displaces pressure to new places [1]; enforcement is weak in many destinations [1].
- **Climate feedback:** tourism contributes to climate change that damages its own primary resources [1].
- **Scale:** individual destinations may become more sustainable while the global system does not [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, tourism can become more sustainable locally, but global growth makes full sustainability unlikely without changes in transport [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers separate the local scale, where management can work, from the global scale, where total growth and emissions dominate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating visitor-days without converting them into visitors present per day.
- Naming a strategy's strength but not its weakness.
- Treating sustainability as environmental only.
- Ignoring tourism's contribution to climate change when discussing the future.
