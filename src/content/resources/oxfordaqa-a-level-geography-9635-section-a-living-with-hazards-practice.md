These questions cover section 3.1.1 Section A: Living with hazards of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2, including 3.1.1.1 to 3.1.1.7. The specification assesses this content in Section A of Unit 1: Physical geography 1. Revise first with the [living with hazards study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-section-a-living-with-hazards/) and the [living with hazards revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-living-with-hazards-revision-notes/).

The places Varnia, Port Amara and Kessin, and all data about them, are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between a natural hazard and a disaster. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by risk-sharing as a response to natural hazards, and give one example. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how magma plumes can produce volcanoes far from plate margins. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows two fictional earthquakes in Varnia.

| | Earthquake X | Earthquake Y |
|---|---|---|
| Moment magnitude | 7.4 | 5.4 |
| Depth of focus | 60 km | 8 km |
| Distance of epicentre from nearest city | 80 km | Beneath a city of 2 million |
| Building codes enforced | Yes | No |
| Local time of event | 14:00 | 03:00 |
| Deaths | 14 | 960 |

**(a)** Approximately how many times more energy did Earthquake X release than Earthquake Y? **[1]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain **two** reasons why Earthquake Y caused more deaths. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how a tropical storm generates a storm surge. **[3]**

**6.** Outline **two** forms of volcanic hazard other than lava flows. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows a quality-of-life index (pre-event level = 100) for two fictional coastal towns struck by the same cyclone in week 0.

| Week | -2 | 0 | 2 | 8 | 26 | 52 | 104 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Port Amara | 100 | 40 | 45 | 60 | 80 | 95 | 105 |
| Kessin | 100 | 15 | 18 | 25 | 40 | 55 | 70 |

**(a)** Calculate the fall in the index at each town in week 0. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare the recovery of the two towns, using the Park model. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons for the differences. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the hazard management cycle can reduce the impacts of future wildfires. **[4]**

**9.** With reference to a recent volcanic event, assess the effectiveness of the short-term and long-term responses. **[9]**

**10.** With reference to a multi-hazardous environment you have studied, explain how human responses allow people to continue living there. **[6]**

**11.** "Prediction is the most effective way of reducing the impacts of natural hazards." To what extent do you agree with this view? **[12]**

**12.** With reference to a local-scale place in a hazardous setting, analyse how the economic, social and political character of its community reflects the presence of the hazard. **[9]**

## Answers

Questions 9 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, judging knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation together; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** A natural hazard is a natural event or process with the potential to cause loss of life or damage [1]. A disaster occurs when the event actually overwhelms a community's ability to cope, causing major losses [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the contrast must turn on "potential" against "actual impact beyond coping capacity".

**2.** Spreading the cost of hazard losses across many people, so no individual bears all of it [1], for example household insurance or a government disaster fund [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A magma plume is a column of hot mantle material rising from deep in the mantle [1]. Where it reaches the base of a plate, melting produces magma that rises to form a volcano [1]. Because the plume stays roughly fixed while the plate moves over it, a chain of volcanoes forms that become older with distance from the plume, as in the Hawaiian islands [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** About 1,000 times (two magnitude units, each about 32 times the energy) [1].
**(b)** Earthquake Y's focus was only 8 km deep, so seismic waves lost little energy before reaching the surface and shaking was more intense [1], whereas X's 60 km focus was far deeper [1]. Y occurred directly beneath a city of 2 million without enforced building codes [1], so many buildings collapsed onto residents, and at 03:00 most people were indoors asleep [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason must quote the table and then explain the effect; "Y was shallower" alone earns only the first mark of the pair.

**5.** Very low air pressure at the storm's centre lets the sea surface rise beneath it [1]. Strong onshore winds push this water towards the coast [1], and where the coast is shallow or funnel-shaped the water piles up and floods low-lying land, especially if the surge coincides with high tide [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Two marks for each form: one for the hazard, one for its development. Pyroclastic flows: fast-moving currents of hot gas and rock fragments that flow down slopes [1], destroying and burning everything in their path [1]. Lahars (mudflows): ash mixed with rain, meltwater or lake water [1], which flow down valleys and can bury settlements far from the vent [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* ash fallout and tephra (collapsing roofs, damaging crops, disrupting aviation) or volcanic gases (suffocation, acid rain) earn the same credit in place of either form above.

**7. (a)** Port Amara: 100 - 40 = 60 points [1]. Kessin: 100 - 15 = 85 points [1].
**(b)** Port Amara's fall was smaller and its rehabilitation faster, rising from 45 to 80 between weeks 2 and 26, while Kessin rose only from 18 to 40 [1]. Port Amara reached 95 by week 52 and finished above its pre-event level at 105 [1], whereas Kessin was still well below normal at 70 after two years, suggesting reconstruction was incomplete [1].
**(c)** Port Amara may have had stronger buildings, defences or warnings, so its fall was smaller [1], meaning fewer homes and services were destroyed [1]. Port Amara may have had more funding, insurance or effective government, allowing rapid reconstruction [1], possibly with improved buildings that explain its finish above 100 [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* in (b) use phase names from the Park model and quote values from the table at named weeks.

**8.** Preparedness: public warnings, evacuation plans and community education mean people leave before fires arrive [1]. Response: trained fire services and aerial fire-fighting reduce the area burned [1]. Recovery: rebuilding with fire-resistant materials and defensible space around homes [1]. Mitigation: controlled burning and firebreaks reduce fuel, so the next fire is less intense; each event feeds lessons into the next round of preparation [1]. **[4]**

**9.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Soufrière Hills, Montserrat, began erupting in 1995 at a destructive margin [1]. Short term: residents of the south were evacuated to the north and abroad, and emergency shelters were set up [1]. Evacuation was largely effective, but some people returned to farm in the danger zone, and 19 were killed by pyroclastic flows in June 1997 [1]. Long term: the Montserrat Volcano Observatory monitors activity and sets hazard zones [1], an effective prediction and land-use response [1]. The capital, Plymouth, was abandoned and new housing and services were built in the north [1]. However, many residents left the island permanently, so the economy and population have not fully recovered [1]. Effectiveness differed by group: those with family abroad adapted more easily than farmers who lost land [1]. Judgement: responses were effective in reducing deaths, less effective in sustaining the island's economy [1]. **[9]**

**10.** *Indicative content.* Japan faces earthquakes, tsunami, volcanoes and typhoons [1]. Strict building codes and base-isolated structures reduce collapse in earthquakes (mitigation) [1]. Earthquake early warning and tsunami alerts give seconds to minutes of warning (prediction and preparedness) [1]. Regular national drills build community resilience [1]. Sea walls and evacuation towers protect some coasts, although the 2011 tsunami overtopped many defences [1]. People stay because fertile land, coastal economies and strong recovery funding outweigh the perceived risk [1]. **[6]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Define prediction and set it alongside other responses [1]. For: volcanic prediction from seismicity, ground deformation and gas output allows timely evacuation [1]. Storm track forecasts give days of warning, so evacuation can save many lives [1]. Prediction allows early warning systems to work, for example tsunami alerts [1]. Against: earthquakes cannot be predicted in time, so building codes and land-use planning matter more [1]. A prediction only helps if warnings reach people and they can act on them [1], as Cyclone Nargis showed, when many villagers in the delta had little effective warning [1]. Prediction does not reduce property damage, which needs mitigation [1]. Wealthy places can act on predictions; poorer places may lack transport and shelters [1]. Risk-sharing through insurance speeds recovery, which prediction cannot do [1]. The best results come from combining prediction with preparedness and mitigation in the management cycle [1]. Judgement: prediction is most effective for volcanoes and storms, least for earthquakes, and never sufficient alone [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that treat all hazards as equally predictable stay in the middle levels; distinguish by hazard type.

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Locate and describe the place and its hazard precisely [1]. Explain the physical nature of the hazard there [1]. Economic: hazard affects property values, insurance costs or business location [1]. Economic: rebuilding and protection spending shape local employment [1]. Social: community groups, drills and shared memory of past events build resilience [1]. Social: some residents leave after events, changing the age or income structure [1]. Political: zoning rules, building codes and the allocation of recovery funds reveal who holds power [1]. Political: disagreement over rebuilding or relocation creates local conflict [1]. Judgement on how far the hazard, rather than other factors, explains the community's character [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* local scale means one town or district; a national overview cannot reach the upper levels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting table values without explaining their effect, or explaining without quoting them.
- Treating magnitude as a linear scale.
- Describing the Park model without naming its phases or reading the shape of a curve.
- Case studies with no dates, or an event that is not recent.
- Extended answers that describe responses without judging how well they worked, for whom and over what timescale.
