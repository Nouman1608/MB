Like the exam's stimulus material, this pack contains several texts on one modern-world issue,
including a brief text, a visual text and a fiction extract. Some questions are on single texts and
some on pairs.

## The source pack: cycling in the city (all original)

**Text A — from a local news report**

> *The city council has approved plans for twelve kilometres of protected cycle lanes along the main
> ring road. Work will begin in the spring. Councillor Ana Ruiz said the lanes would "give families the
> confidence to cycle". Some business owners have raised concerns about the loss of parking spaces
> outside shops. The council says a public consultation will run until the end of the month.*

**Text B — from an opinion column by a taxi driver**

> *Twenty years I have driven these streets. I know every pothole by name. And now, apparently, I am
> the problem. The council wants to paint my road green and hand it to a few hundred cyclists who will
> vanish the moment it rains. Who will carry your grandmother to hospital at two in the morning? Not a
> bicycle.*

**Text C — a campaign poster (described)**

> *The poster is mostly white. In the centre, a photograph shows a girl of about ten on a red bicycle,
> laughing, with her school bag on her back. Behind her, slightly out of focus, is a queue of grey
> traffic. Across the top, in large black capitals: "SHE'S ALREADY THERE." At the bottom, in small green
> text: "Safe lanes. Faster journeys. Cleaner air. Support the cycle plan."*

**Text D — from a short story**

> *Mira's father checked her helmet twice, then a third time. "Straight to school," he said. "No
> racing the buses." She nodded and pushed off. At the junction the cars stood in a long, breathing
> line, engines muttering. She slipped past them in the painted lane, and for the first time that year
> she arrived before the bell, cheeks burning, with nothing to apologise for.*

**Text E — a social media post**

> *Day 1 of the new lanes: 14 mins to work instead of 40. Day 2: rain. Day 3: rain. Still faster.
> #NoRegrets*

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** From Text A, give **four** facts about the cycle lane plan. **[4]**

**2.** In Text B, what does "I know every pothole by name" suggest about the writer? **[2]**

**3.** Explain the effect of the rhetorical question in the last two sentences of Text B. **[3]**

**4.** In Text C, explain the effect of the headline "SHE'S ALREADY THERE." **[3]**

**5.** How does Text E use structure to make its point? **[3]**

**6.** In Text D, what does the father's behaviour suggest about his feelings? **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Analyse how presentational features are used in Text C to persuade the viewer. **[8]**

**8.** How does the writer of Text D use language to present Mira's journey? **[6]**

**9.** Using Texts A and E, write a summary of the effects of the new lanes, according to these texts,
on people who travel in the city. **[6]**

**10.** Compare how the writers of Text B and Text E present their attitudes to cycling. **[10]**

**11.** Text A is a news report. To what extent does it present the cycle lane plan neutrally? **[6]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff. Other valid points of the same quality earn the
same credit.

**1.** Any four, [1] each: twelve kilometres of lanes; protected lanes; along the main ring road;
approved by the city council; work begins in the spring; a consultation runs until the end of the
month; some businesses worry about losing parking. Maximum 4.

**2.** He has driven the roads for so long that he knows them intimately [1]; the personification
suggests affection and ownership, as if the streets were his own [1].

**3.** It invites readers to imagine a real emergency [1], creating sympathy by mentioning "your
grandmother", which makes it personal [1]; the blunt answer "Not a bicycle" dismisses cycling as
impractical and leaves the reader agreeing with him [1].

**4.** It is short and confident, in large capitals, so it is the first thing the viewer reads [1];
"already" implies the cyclist has beaten the traffic [1], creating a slightly teasing tone that makes
car drivers feel left behind and curious to read on [1].

**5.** The diary-like sequence "Day 1… Day 2… Day 3" builds a mini story [1]; the repetition of
"rain" sets up an expectation of complaint [1], which is reversed by the short final phrase "Still
faster", so the point lands with humour and the hashtag confirms it [1].

**6.** He is anxious for her safety [1], shown by checking her helmet three times and giving
warnings, which also suggests he is reluctant to let her go [1].

**7.** Credit up to 8: the photograph of a laughing girl is central, making cycling seem joyful and
safe for children [2]; the contrast between her bright red bicycle and the "grey" queue of traffic
behind, out of focus, makes cars seem dull and slow [2]; the mostly white background gives a clean,
calm look that links to "cleaner air" [2]; the large capital headline draws attention first, while the
small green tricolon at the bottom gives reasons and a call to action, with green suggesting nature
and "go" [2].

**8.** Credit up to 6: the cars are personified as "breathing" and "muttering", making the traffic
seem like a restless, slightly threatening creature [2]; the verb "slipped" suggests ease and
freedom compared with the stationary cars [2]; the ending "cheeks burning, with nothing to apologise
for" shows physical effort and pride, implying she is usually late and that cycling has changed this
[2].

**9.** Credit up to 6, [2] per synthesised point: Text A suggests the lanes will make families more
confident to cycle, and Text E shows a real journey time cut from forty to fourteen minutes, so
together they suggest cycling becomes safer and quicker [2]; Text A notes that business owners worry
about losing parking, so drivers and shoppers may find it harder to stop outside shops [2]; Text E shows
that bad weather does not cancel the benefit, since the journey was "still faster" in the rain, which
suggests the change could last [2].

**10.** Credit up to 10: Text B is hostile and Text E enthusiastic: a clear overview of the difference
[2]; Text B uses a personal, authoritative opening, "Twenty years I have driven these streets", while
Text E uses personal evidence of journey times, so both rely on experience but draw opposite
conclusions [2]; Text B dismisses cyclists as fair-weather, who "vanish the moment it rains", whereas
Text E directly contradicts this by reporting cycling through two days of rain [2]; Text B's tone is
bitter and sarcastic ("apparently, I am the problem"), while Text E's is light and humorous, with the
hashtag "#NoRegrets" [2]; Text B uses an emotive rhetorical question to appeal to readers' fears,
whereas Text E persuades through brevity and a twist ending, inviting readers to try cycling [2].

**11.** Credit up to 6: a judgement [2], for example "mostly neutral but with a slight positive
framing"; neutral features such as factual details and the inclusion of business owners' concerns
[2]; less neutral features such as quoting only the councillor directly, with a positive phrase about
"confidence", while the businesses' view is summarised, and opening with the approval rather than the
objections [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- In Question 7, describing the poster without explaining effects.
- In Question 9, copying details instead of combining them into points.
- In Question 10, writing about Text B and then Text E in separate halves.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the real exam include a visual text?**
The specification says some texts may be brief or solely visual, so practise with posters, adverts
and infographics.

**Can I use these texts for writing practice too?**
Yes. Section B writing is linked to the reading sources, so try a writing task on cycling using ideas
from this pack in your own words.
