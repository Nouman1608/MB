## Section A

**1.** State the two age ranges included in the specification's definition of learning language, and the **three** kinds of data that may be provided. **[5]**

**2.** Name and briefly describe Kroll's four stages of writing development. **[4]**

**3.** Identify the spelling stage each example best represents: (a) "bk" for "book", (b) "becos" for "because", (c) "rane" for "rain". **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between BICS and CALP, and why it matters when analysing an EAL learner's writing. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what Selinker meant by interlanguage. **[2]**

## Section B

**Data 1** (original, invented). A story written by a child aged about 6.

```
Wen I went to the beech I saw a big crab and it was red and it
nipt my toe and I cryd and then my dad got me a ice creem.
```

**6.** Analyse **three** features of Data 1 that show the child's stage of writing development, referring to Kroll's model. **[6]**

**7.** Analyse what the spellings in Data 1 reveal about the child's phonological knowledge. **[5]**

**Data 2** (original, invented). Two 15-year-old friends messaging.

```
AMIR:  bro that test was actually long 😭
JOSH:  fr i didnt even finish the last page
AMIR:  same. lowkey think i failed
JOSH:  nah ur good ur always saying that 😂
```

**8.** Analyse how Data 2 shows features of adolescent language and identity. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the view that messaging like Data 2 shows declining literacy among teenagers. **[6]**

**Data 3** (original, invented). An adult EAL learner, recently arrived, speaking to a teacher.

```
LEARNER:  yesterday I go to the bank (.) for open account (.) but the man
          he say I need (.) how you say (.) proof of address
TEACHER:  ah you needed proof of address (.) did you have a letter
LEARNER:  yes I have letter (.) from school (.) is okay he say
```

**10.** Analyse **three** features of the learner's interlanguage in Data 3, explaining possible reasons for each. **[6]**

**11.** Analyse how the teacher's response supports the learner, referring to **one** theory. **[4]**

**12.** "Language learning is mostly complete by the age of five." Discuss this view with reference to **two** of the data sets above. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **Early language acquisition in young children** [1] and **later language development in teenagers** [1]. Data may be **speech** [1], **writing** [1] or **multimodal texts** [1] (produced by native or EAL speakers).

**2.** **Preparation**: mastering letter formation and basic spelling [1]. **Consolidation**: writing much as one speaks [1]. **Differentiation**: distinguishing writing from speech and adapting to genre and audience [1]. **Integration**: a controlled personal voice across forms [1].

**3.** (a) **Semi-phonetic** [1]. (b) **Phonetic** [1]. (c) **Transitional** (uses a known spelling pattern, silent "e") [1].

**4.** **BICS** are basic interpersonal communicative skills used in everyday conversation [1]; **CALP** is cognitive academic language proficiency needed for academic tasks, which takes longer to develop [1]. It matters because a learner who **speaks fluently may still struggle with academic writing** [1], so writing data should not be used to judge overall competence [1].

**5.** Interlanguage is the learner's **developing, rule-governed language system** [1] that lies **between the first language and the target language** and changes as learning proceeds [1].

**6.** **"and... and... and then"**: a long chain of coordinated clauses mirrors speech, typical of **consolidation** [1] [1]. **Chronological recount** without paragraphing or a crafted opening shows writing that has not yet **differentiated** from spoken narrative [1] [1]. **A capital at the start, a full stop at the end and a consistent past-tense narrative** show basic control, so the **preparation** stage has been passed [1] [1]. Alternatively, the absence of a closing reflection or audience awareness.

**7.** **"Wen" and "beech"** show the child represents sounds accurately but has not learned the conventional spelling patterns ("wh", "ea") [1] [1]. **"nipt" and "cryd"** represent the past tense ending as it sounds (/t/, /d/), showing **phonological awareness** of the ending but not yet the morphological spelling "-ed" [1] [1]. **"creem"** uses a plausible pattern for the vowel, showing knowledge that "ee" spells /iː/ [1].

**8.** **In-group address "bro"** marks friendship and solidarity [1]. **Slang and discourse markers** ("fr", "lowkey", "actually long") mark adolescent identity and a shared peer style [1] [1]. **Emoji** (😭, 😂) convey exaggerated emotion and humour, replacing facial expression [1]. **Mutual reassurance** ("nah ur good") shows relationship maintenance through peer-group norms [1] [1].

**9.** Against the view: abbreviations and lower-case style are **deliberate choices suited to the mode** [1], and research associated with Crystal and with Clare Wood and colleagues found **no evidence that textism use harms spelling** [1] [1]. Using forms such as "ur" requires **knowing the standard form** being altered [1]. For the view, a limitation: the data show informal style only, so they **cannot show what the writers can do in formal writing** [1]. Judgement: the data show **register control in one mode**, not declining literacy [1].

**10.** **Present tense for past events** ("yesterday I go", "he say"): tense and third-person marking still developing, possibly reflecting a first language that marks time with adverbs rather than verb endings [1] [1]. **Article omission** ("for open account", "I have letter"): possibly **transfer** from a first language without articles [1] [1]. **Resumptive pronoun** ("the man he say"): a topic-comment structure, possibly from first-language patterns or a common learner strategy [1] [1]. Alternatively, the communication strategy "how you say", used to bridge a lexical gap.

**11.** The teacher **recasts** "I need... proof of address" as "you needed proof of address", modelling the past tense without correcting directly [1] [1]. The **follow-up question** keeps the interaction going and gives further **comprehensible input** at a manageable level, consistent with **Krashen's** view that acquisition depends on understandable input [1] [1].

**12.** Against: Data 2 shows teenagers acquiring **new peer-group vocabulary and pragmatic norms** [1] [1]; Data 1 shows a 6-year-old still developing **spelling and written structure** (Kroll's later stages lie ahead) [1] [1]. Later grammar such as **passives and nominalisation**, and **academic vocabulary**, develop through schooling [1]. For: by five most children have **core grammar and phonology** in place, as early acquisition research shows [1]. Data 3 shows learning continues in **adulthood** for EAL speakers [1]. Judgement: **foundations** are largely in place by five, but language learning, especially **literacy, register and identity**, continues through adolescence and beyond [1] [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating children's phonetic spellings as careless errors.
- Analysing teenage messaging only for "mistakes".
- Labelling EAL forms wrong without suggesting a reason.
- Applying Kroll's stages as fixed age bands.
- Essays that ignore the data and recite theory.
