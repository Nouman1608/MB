This guide covers section 12.3 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists "pollution (land, air and water): nature, causes, solutions"; "demand for and supply of water and issues of water quality"; "factors in the degradation of rural environments (e.g. overpopulation, poor agricultural practices, deforestation)"; "factors in the degradation of urban environments (e.g. urbanisation, industrial development, inadequate waste management)"; "constraints on improving the quality of degraded environments"; and "the protection of environments at risk at the local or regional scale: needs, measures and outcomes". It is part of Topic 12, Environmental management, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 12 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-environmental-management/) introduces each statement with London's Ultra Low Emission Zone, Cape Town's water crisis and the Great Barrier Reef. This page adds the measures used to judge pollution and water stress, the processes that link causes to degradation, a debate about population and land, and a second protected environment. Condense it with the [12.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-environmental-degradation-revision-notes/) and test it with the [12.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-environmental-degradation-practice/).

## Pollution: nature, causes, solutions

| Type | Main pollutants | Typical sources | Effects | Solutions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Air | Fine particulates (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, ground-level ozone | Vehicles, power stations, industry, burning of waste and crop residues | Respiratory and heart disease; acid rain; smog | Emission standards, cleaner fuels, filters and scrubbers, public transport, low-emission zones |
| Water | Sewage, nitrates and phosphates, heavy metals, oil, warm water | Untreated sewage, farm run-off, industrial effluent, power-station cooling | Disease; eutrophication; fish deaths; unsafe drinking water | Sewage treatment, discharge permits, buffer strips, reduced fertiliser use |
| Land | Solid waste, plastics, heavy metals, pesticides, mining spoil | Open dumps, industry, mining, agriculture | Contaminated soils and food; leachate into groundwater | Sanitary landfill, recycling, remediation of contaminated sites |

**Three ways to classify a source:**

- **Point source:** a single identifiable outlet, such as a factory pipe; easier to monitor and regulate.
- **Diffuse (non-point) source:** many scattered sources, such as fertilised fields or traffic; hard to control.
- **Transboundary:** pollution that crosses borders, such as acid rain or smoke haze from forest fires; needs international agreement.

**Measuring water quality:** dissolved oxygen (low values stress fish), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD: high values mean much organic pollution), nitrate and phosphate levels, and faecal coliform bacteria (a sign of sewage).

### Does pollution fall as countries develop?

The **environmental Kuznets curve** suggests that some pollution rises during industrialisation and then falls as incomes rise, because richer societies demand cleaner air and can afford controls. It fits local pollutants such as sulphur dioxide in many HICs. It fits carbon dioxide and waste less well, and HICs may simply move polluting industry to other countries. Use it as a hypothesis to test, not a law.

## Water: demand, supply and quality

**Demand** comes from agriculture (the largest user globally, mainly irrigation), industry and domestic use. It rises with population, urbanisation, irrigation and higher living standards.

**Measuring stress.** One widely used indicator compares renewable water with population: below about 1 700 m³ per person per year is **water stress**, below 1 000 m³ is **water scarcity**, and below 500 m³ is **absolute scarcity**.

- **Physical scarcity:** too little water for demand, typical of arid regions.
- **Economic scarcity:** water exists but people lack the money or infrastructure to use it, common in parts of sub-Saharan Africa.

**Supply options** and their drawbacks:

| Option | Drawback |
|---|---|
| Dams and reservoirs | Displacement, evaporation, sediment trapping |
| Groundwater pumping | Falling water tables, subsidence, saltwater intrusion at coasts |
| Water transfer between basins | High cost; conflict with source regions |
| Desalination | Energy-intensive; brine waste |
| Recycling wastewater | Treatment cost; public reluctance |

**Demand management** includes metering and pricing, fixing leaks, efficient irrigation (drip instead of flood) and water-saving appliances. It is usually cheaper than new supply.

**Quality issues** include sewage contamination where treatment is inadequate, salinisation of irrigated land and aquifers, nitrate pollution from farming, and natural contaminants. In Bangladesh, millions of tube wells dug to avoid contaminated surface water were later found to draw groundwater with high natural arsenic levels, which shows how a solution to one quality problem can create another.

## Factors in the degradation of rural environments

- **Overpopulation:** more people than the land can support with current technology shortens fallow periods, pushes farming onto steep or dry land and increases fuelwood cutting.
- **Poor agricultural practices:** overgrazing removes vegetation cover; overcultivation exhausts nutrients; ploughing up and down slopes channels run-off; irrigation without drainage causes waterlogging and salinisation.
- **Deforestation:** removes the canopy that intercepts rain and the roots that bind soil, so erosion, flooding and siltation of rivers increase, and biodiversity falls.

These factors interact in a **downward spiral**: degraded land yields less, so people clear more land, which degrades further.

**A debate to use.** A Malthusian view sees population growth as a direct cause of degradation. Ester Boserup argued instead that population pressure can drive farmers to intensify and innovate. A study of the Machakos district in Kenya, published as *More People, Less Erosion* (1994), found that as population grew over several decades, farmers built terraces, planted trees and raised output per hectare. The lesson for evaluation: population pressure degrades land where farmers lack secure tenure, capital and markets, but may not where they have them.

## Factors in the degradation of urban environments

- **Urbanisation:** when cities grow faster than infrastructure, informal settlements lack sewerage, drainage and waste collection; green space is built over.
- **Industrial development:** emissions, effluent and contaminated land, often concentrated in poorer districts near factories.
- **Inadequate waste management:** open dumping and burning release smoke and toxic chemicals; leachate pollutes groundwater; rubbish blocks drains, worsening floods; dumps attract disease vectors. Informal waste pickers recover materials but work in dangerous conditions.
- **Traffic:** congestion and older vehicles concentrate air pollution at street level.

## Constraints on improving degraded environments

- **Financial:** clean-up, treatment works and landfill cost more than many cities or households can afford.
- **Institutional:** weak enforcement, unclear responsibilities between authorities, corruption.
- **Economic interests:** polluting industries provide jobs and tax revenue, so controls meet resistance.
- **Physical:** soils, aquifers and ecosystems may take decades to recover, or may not recover at all.
- **Social:** poverty forces people to use land or fuel unsustainably; low awareness of risks.
- **Spatial:** transboundary pollution needs cooperation between countries or regions.
- **Data:** without monitoring, problems are not measured, so they are not prioritised.

## Protecting environments at risk: the Galápagos Islands

**Needs.** The Galápagos Islands, part of Ecuador, have many species found nowhere else. They are threatened by introduced species such as goats and rats, by growing tourism and resident population, and by illegal fishing.

**Measures.**

- Most of the land became a national park in 1959.
- The islands were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1978.
- A large marine reserve was created in 1998, restricting industrial fishing.
- Visitors must go to most sites with licensed guides, and cargo is inspected to stop new species arriving.
- Eradication programmes in the 2000s removed feral goats from several islands.

**Outcomes.** Vegetation recovered on islands where goats were removed, showing that targeted local measures work. However, pressure from tourism and migration continued, and UNESCO placed the islands on its List of World Heritage in Danger from 2007 to 2010. Protection reduced some threats but depends on controlling growth that is driven by the islands' economic value.

**Evaluation framework for any protected environment:** Was the **need** clearly identified? Were **measures** enforced and funded? Do **outcomes** show measurable improvement? Who bears the costs (local fishers, residents)? Can local measures handle threats that come from outside, such as climate change?

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why diffuse pollution is harder to control than point-source pollution. [4]*

> Point-source pollution comes from a single outlet, such as a factory pipe, so it can be measured and the polluter identified (**definition and consequence**). Diffuse pollution comes from many small sources, such as fertiliser washing off thousands of fields (**definition**), so no single polluter can be held responsible or fined (**responsibility**). Controls must change the behaviour of many land users, which is costly to enforce (**enforcement**).

## Common mistakes

- Naming a pollutant without its source or effect.
- Confusing physical and economic water scarcity.
- Blaming population alone for rural degradation; consider tenure, capital and markets.
- Listing constraints without linking them to a named place.
- Describing protection measures without outcomes, which the syllabus names.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** water-quality data: quote values at named sites and identify where change is greatest.
- **Explain** degradation: give the process chain from cause to effect.
- **Assess** constraints or protection: rank factors, use a place, and conclude on what limits improvement most.
