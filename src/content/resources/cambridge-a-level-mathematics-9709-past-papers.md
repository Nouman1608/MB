This page is a guide to past papers for Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics (9709). It covers eleven series, from March 2023 to June 2026, across Pure Mathematics, Mechanics and Probability & Statistics. The grade thresholds are copied from Cambridge's grade-threshold documents, and the examiner notes are our own summaries of Cambridge's Principal Examiner Reports for Teachers. We do not host any papers: every paper, mark scheme and report comes from Cambridge's own website.

## Where to get the official papers

- [Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics (9709) syllabus page](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/programmes-and-qualifications/cambridge-international-as-and-a-level-mathematics-9709/)
- [9709 past papers page](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/programmes-and-qualifications/cambridge-international-as-and-a-level-mathematics-9709/past-papers/)

Cambridge publishes recent papers, mark schemes, examiner reports and grade thresholds on the syllabus's past-papers page; older series are available to schools through the School Support Hub.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which shows where each mark comes from. Then read the examiner report for that paper, which explains why answers lost marks. The notes on this page give you the short version of each report.

The March and June 2026 papers were set on the current syllabus, which Cambridge publishes for examination in 2026 and 2027. Papers from 2023 to 2025 come from before that, so check the syllabus version on the paper's front cover against the current syllabus before you use an older paper. Topic names on this page follow the current syllabus.

## What examiners keep saying

These errors come back across the eleven series summarised here, grouped by syllabus topic.

- **Quadratics.** Show a method: factorise, complete the square or substitute into the formula. Read the discriminant condition carefully (equal to zero, positive, or a perfect square). (March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, November 2025, June 2026)
- **Functions.** Name transformations exactly, with a vector or a scale factor and direction, and apply them in the right order. Let the domain fix the sign of an inverse; never swap domain and range. (March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025, June 2026)
- **Trigonometry.** Solutions go missing: the negative root of a squared ratio, negative or second angles, roots lost by cancelling a factor. Use radians when the question does. (every series from March 2023 to June 2026)
- **Differentiation.** The chain-rule factor gets dropped, rates of change are tried without it, and the product rule is missed in implicit and parametric work. (June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025, November 2025)
- **Integration.** Show the integral and the substituted limits. Rewrite first: squared trig terms with an identity, improper fractions by division, fractions as powers. For a volume, square each function. (March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, March 2025, November 2025, March 2026, June 2026)
- **Logarithmic and exponential functions.** The log of a sum or difference cannot be split, and brackets matter with the power law. Reject any root that makes a log invalid. (June 2023, June 2024, November 2024, March 2025, June 2025, November 2025, March 2026, June 2026)
- **Numerical solution of equations.** A sign-change argument needs both values and a conclusion. Iterate in radians and keep going until the accuracy is settled. (June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, June 2025, November 2025, June 2026)
- **Complex numbers.** Draw Argand diagrams with a ruler, equal scales and labels. Many loci are arcs or half-lines, not full circles or lines. Check the quadrant of an argument. (March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, March 2024, June 2024, November 2024, March 2026)
- **Vectors.** A line equation starts r = ...; a single vector such as AB is not a line. Never assume lines meet, and keep position, direction and displacement vectors apart. (March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, November 2024, March 2025, June 2025, November 2025, June 2026)
- **Kinematics of motion in a straight line.** Distance is not displacement when velocity changes sign: split at v = 0. Constant-acceleration formulae do not apply when acceleration varies. (November 2023, March 2024, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025, November 2025, March 2026, June 2026)
- **Forces and equilibrium; Newton's laws of motion.** The normal reaction includes any component of an applied force; it is not always the weight. Check friction's direction. When the set-up changes, find the new acceleration. (March 2023, November 2023, November 2024, June 2025, November 2025, June 2026)
- **Energy, work and power; Momentum.** Use energy when told to, with every term: friction, resistance, driving force, vertical height. In collisions, use velocities just before impact and conserve momentum, not kinetic energy. (June 2023, November 2023, March 2024, June 2024, June 2025, November 2025, March 2026, June 2026)
- **Permutations and combinations.** List the scenarios before counting. Divide for repeated letters, multiply independent choices rather than adding them, and count the possible orders. (every series from March 2023 to June 2026)
- **Discrete random variables; The normal distribution.** Read the success criterion exactly and spot a geometric model. A z-value is not a probability: write the standardisation and sketch the curve. (every series from March 2023 to June 2026)
- **Linear combinations of random variables; Sampling and estimation; Hypothesis tests.** Square coefficients and add variances, even for a difference. Use a tail probability, not one term, and conclude in context without certainty. (every series from March 2023 to June 2026)

Exam technique the reports repeat:

- **Show the method.** The reports say unsupported calculator answers can score nothing. Write the factorisation, the limits and the standardisation.
- **Carry four significant figures.** Round only at the end, keep exact answers exact, and know 3 s.f. from 3 d.p.
- **Follow *hence*.** Build on the earlier part. Stem information holds throughout, but data given in one part belongs to that part.
- **Derive a given answer.** Write every step of a *show that*; checking by substitution is not a proof. If it will not come out, find the slip.
- **Draw a diagram.** Force diagrams, normal-curve sketches, and Argand diagrams with a ruler and equal scales all appear in the best scripts.
- **Present one attempt.** Cross out rather than overwrite, label any additional page, and reread the question at the end.

## June 2026

Routine calculus, binomial expansions, quadratics and standard statistics were mostly secure. The best scripts stood out for precision: a visible method for quadratics, limits and standardising, earlier work reused after *Hence*, answers in the exact form or accuracy asked for, and every step of a *show that*. On Pure Mathematics 3, vector notation, sketches used to locate roots and radian mode in iteration were weak. Mechanics went wrong when the situation changed partway through a question or when the normal reaction was taken as the weight. In statistics, success criteria, continuity corrections and test wording cost marks.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: the wrong radius in arc and sector formulae; functions subtracted before squaring for a volume of revolution.
- Papers 13, 15 and 31-33: trigonometric solutions dropped from one factor or missing from the interval.
- Papers 31-33 and 35: position, direction and displacement vectors confused; a line not written as r = ... (Paper 33).
- Paper 42: an earlier acceleration or reaction reused after the set-up changed; friction's work left out.
- Papers 52 and 55: an extreme case missed and counts added, not multiplied; continuity correction in the wrong direction.
- Papers 61 and 63: one item's distribution used for a total; coefficients not squared; a z-value compared with an area.

## March 2026

This series favoured candidates who linked the parts of a question and wrote out each step. On the pure papers, *hence* and *use your answer* were often ignored, given results were checked by substitution instead of derived, and less familiar forms caught people out: fractions to rewrite as powers, products of trig functions, and complex loci that are arcs. In Mechanics, energy methods and motion in several phases split the entry. In both statistics papers, careful reading of success criteria, accurate use of normal tables and four significant figures before rounding marked the strongest scripts. Test conclusions still needed context and non-definite wording.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: a fraction integrated top and bottom separately instead of rewritten as powers; only the positive root taken in a trig equation.
- Paper 32: logs taken term by term instead of spotting a disguised quadratic; an arc locus drawn as a full circle.
- Paper 42: only three of four phases of motion used for total distance or time; potential energy counted twice, or a force given for work done.
- Paper 52: no division for repeated letters; cumulative frequency plotted at upper class values, not boundaries.
- Paper 52: a probability from the tables used as a z-value; a rounded value used as a conditional-probability denominator.
- Paper 62: the estimated variance used when the true value was given; no continuity correction for a normal approximation to a Poisson.

## November 2025

One theme ran through every strand: calculator answers with no working. Quadratic and trig equations, definite integrals and probabilities simply written down lost marks under the rubric. On the pure papers, strong scripts used earlier parts after "hence", kept answers exact and found every angle in range; weaker ones slipped on brackets, improper fractions and the chain rule, and the report found Paper 32 challenging for many. In Mechanics, the normal reaction with an applied force at an angle, friction direction and energy equations for several particles separated scripts. Statistics stressed success criteria, 4 s.f. working, continuity corrections and careful test conclusions.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: discriminant inequalities solved on a calculator or left as equations; rates of change without the chain rule.
- Papers 12, 22, 31 and 33: angles outside the calculator's principal range missed.
- Papers 31 and 32: improper fractions not spotted before partial fractions; iteration stopped too soon (Paper 32).
- Papers 41-43: the normal reaction taken as the weight alone; friction reversed; an instructed energy method ignored.
- Papers 51, 52 and 55: repeated or grouped items mishandled in arrangements; success criteria misread (Paper 52).
- Papers 62 and 65: definite or context-free conclusions; standard deviations added (Paper 62); a one-tailed comparison in a two-tailed test (Paper 65).

## June 2025

The June 2025 report weighs method and presentation as heavily as knowledge. Pure papers rewarded secure algebra, visible working for quadratic and trigonometric equations, and earlier parts used in later ones. Weaker scripts struggled with transformations, domain and range, complex numbers and vectors, and left trig solution sets incomplete. In Mechanics, force diagrams, calculus for variable motion and energy methods when instructed separated candidates. In statistics, the strongest scripts showed standardisation, applied continuity corrections, kept four significant figures, read success criteria exactly and wrote test conclusions in context, without claiming certainty.

**Where marks were lost**

- Papers 11, 13, 15 and 35: trig solution sets incomplete, with negative or second angles missing.
- Papers 12 and 15: the wrong square-root sign in an inverse; domain and range confused.
- Papers 31-33: a vector such as AB given where a line equation was needed; complex transformations described informally (Papers 31 and 35).
- Papers 42 and 45: constant-acceleration formulae where energy was required; friction assumed up the plane only (Paper 45).
- Papers 51, 52 and 55: standardisation not shown, or set equal to a probability; repeated letters mishandled (Paper 51).
- Papers 61-63: faulty continuity corrections for a Poisson approximated by a normal; a variance multiplied by a coefficient, not its square (Paper 61).

## March 2025

The report keeps returning to method and accuracy rather than gaps in knowledge. Pure papers penalised calculator-only answers for equations, cubic factorisation and definite integrals, and careless handling of negative values in series and expansions. Strong scripts carried at least four significant figures, left exact answers exact and set out *show that* steps in full. Weak scripts misread the demand, rounded too early and lost signs on crowded pages. In statistics, reading of success criteria improved, but normal and geometric work, cumulative frequency graphs and hypothesis-test conclusions still divided the entry.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: minus signs dropped in a binomial expansion; a valid negative common ratio rejected for a convergent progression.
- Paper 32: the log of a difference split; complex loci missing modulus signs or a second inequality.
- Paper 32: lines called skew without showing the direction vectors are not multiples; integration by parts done once, not twice.
- Paper 42: constant-acceleration formulae for a particle at constant speed; g missing from a weight component on a slope.
- Paper 52: cumulative frequency plotted at class values or midpoints; a switch to the geometric distribution not spotted.
- Paper 62: √n missing from a test, or a definite conclusion; Type II probabilities found from the original distribution.

## November 2024

Every component came back to visible working: quadratic and trig solutions, definite integrals and normal standardisations done only on a calculator scored little or nothing. Pure papers leaned on functions and inverses, series, trig identities, iteration, implicit and parametric differentiation, vectors and complex numbers. Mechanics centred on resolving forces, calculus for variable acceleration and multi-stage motion; statistics on arrangements, binomial, geometric and normal work and hypothesis tests. Strong scripts read how a question was built, kept exact values and enough figures, and wrote out every step of a given result. Weak scripts rounded early, misread inequality wording and lost signs.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: a bracketed product raised to a power without powering its coefficient, an error the report sees every year.
- Paper 13: the negative root of a squared trig ratio dropped, halving the solutions; transformations in the wrong order.
- Paper 33: complex loci drawn as full circles or regions, not arcs; the cosine rule used where a scalar product was required (Papers 32 and 33).
- Papers 41-43: constant-acceleration formulae for variable acceleration; integration constants set to zero (Paper 42).
- Papers 51-53: missed cases, wrong divisors or adding instead of multiplying in arrangements; continuity corrections misapplied.
- Papers 62 and 63: √n omitted or the wrong critical value; definite test conclusions.

## June 2024

The June 2024 report puts evidence first: unsupported calculator answers earned nothing, from quadratics and cubics to Poisson and normal probabilities. Pure papers leaned on graph transformations, trig equations, circles, iteration, partial fractions, complex numbers and vectors, and the last two were often left blank on Paper 31. Mechanics rewarded clear force diagrams, connected-particle work and complete work-energy equations. Statistics rewarded organised lists of scenarios and test conclusions in context. Stronger scripts linked parts, carried more than 3 s.f. and gave the form asked for; weaker ones rounded early, misread demands or skipped steps in given results.

**Where marks were lost**

- Papers 11-13: a translation with no direction, or applied before the stretch; quadratics solved by calculator.
- Papers 22, 23, 31 and 32: log laws misapplied to sums, differences or exponentials; the wrong partial-fraction form for an improper fraction (Paper 32).
- Papers 31-33: complex arguments in the wrong quadrant (Papers 31 and 32); a half-line locus drawn as a full line (Paper 33).
- Papers 41-43: work-energy terms missing or wrongly signed; constant-acceleration formulae for a time-varying velocity (Paper 41).
- Papers 51-53: repeats not divided out in arrangements; 3 d.p. given for 3 s.f. and success criteria misread (Paper 52).
- Papers 61 and 63: a single term used instead of a tail; definite conclusions (Paper 61).

## March 2024

Routine technique was secure: calculus, binomial expansion, progressions, histograms, single-distribution probabilities and standard connected particles were generally well done. The less familiar separated strong from weak: transformed trig graphs in radians, complex arguments, conditions on implicit curves, energy for linked bodies, collisions with a time delay, multi-case probability and hypothesis tests. Equation solvers and calculator integration again could not replace written working, accuracy had to be carried beyond the final figure needed, and conclusions needed explicit comparisons in context. Quick sketches and Argand diagrams drawn with instruments featured in the best answers.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: the period or minimum of a transformed trig graph in radians misread; zero-factor or negative-root solutions missed.
- Paper 32: an argument in the wrong quadrant; Argand sketches without scales or a labelled centre and radius.
- Paper 32: a rectangle claimed from parallel or equal sides alone, with no right angle shown.
- Paper 42: displacement found instead of total distance when velocity changes sign; work-energy equations missing a term.
- Paper 52: a standardised value equated to a probability; selections added instead of multiplied.
- Paper 62: a single-value probability used in a significance test; an approximation justified without values from the context.

## November 2023

Routine calculus went well on the pure papers, but lost marks kept tracing back to weak algebra and trig manipulation, answers copied from a calculator, and ignored instructions on exact form, "Hence" or accuracy. Pure Mathematics 1 needed visible methods for quadratics and visible substitution of limits. Pure Mathematics 3 exposed gaps in complex numbers, vectors, differential equations and integrating awkward fractions. In Mechanics, strong scripts drew force diagrams and wrote a separate equation for each particle; weak ones reused values from another stage of the motion. In statistics, misread success criteria, early rounding and missing standardisation or continuity corrections cost marks.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: the discriminant set equal to zero when it had to be positive; rates of change without the chain rule.
- Papers 22 and 32: a compound angle split into separate functions (Paper 22) or its formula misquoted; roots lost by dividing through (Paper 32).
- Paper 32: an improper fraction not split before integrating; partial fractions not used after separating variables.
- Papers 42 and 43: a tension or acceleration from an earlier stage reused, not recalculated once one particle stops.
- Papers 51 and 52: success wording misread; a binomial model used where a geometric one fitted (Paper 51).
- Paper 62: a point probability compared instead of a tail; a confidence interval centred on a total, not a mean.

## June 2023

The report returns again and again to unsupported calculator answers: quadratics, definite integrals, given-answer equations and binomial or Poisson probabilities scored little without method. Pure papers centred on functions and transformations, circles, trig identities, every differentiation rule and integration, with Paper 3 adding complex numbers, vectors, differential equations and iteration. Strong scripts reused earlier parts, kept exact values, worked to four significant figures and showed every step of a "show that". Weaker scripts left later parts blank, answered a different demand (range for domain, angle for length), slipped on signs and brackets, used degrees for radians and gave test conclusions without context.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: a common trig factor cancelled, losing solutions; a completed-square result not used in the next part.
- Paper 21: brackets missing when taking logs; a negative-angle solution missed with the R-alpha form.
- Papers 31 and 33: false critical values kept after squaring a modulus inequality (Paper 31); two lines assumed to meet (Paper 33).
- Papers 41 and 42: kinetic energy conserved instead of momentum (Paper 41); forces given where work done was needed (Paper 42).
- Papers 51 and 53: combinations added, not multiplied (Paper 51); a geometric distribution not recognised (Paper 53).
- Papers 61-63: conclusions not in context or too definite; textbook conditions quoted instead of checked (Paper 61).

## March 2023

Routine calculus, standard integration and textbook distributions were handled with confidence. What lifted the strongest scripts was Argand-diagram loci, polynomial division with unknown constants, multi-stage mechanics and unfamiliar probability settings. The report keeps coming back to presentation: calculator answers earn little, quadratics need visible working, and values rounded too early spoil final accuracy in every strand. Stronger candidates drew diagrams, set out one clear method, read success criteria exactly and kept exact values exact. Weaker ones left several half-finished attempts and lost marks to sign and arithmetic slips.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: a volume about the y-axis with functions unsquared or x-limits used; both square-root signs kept for an inverse.
- Paper 32: half-lines with no scales or the wrong starting point; division with unknown constants where equating coefficients was cleaner.
- Paper 42: driving force put in both component equations, or tension in the whole-system equation; a thrust treated as a tension.
- Paper 52: cumulative frequency drawn with line segments or started at the origin; no division for identical letters.
- Paper 62: a variance coefficient not squared, or variances subtracted; tests with no explicit comparison or context.

## How to use this page

1. **Find your gaps first.** Take the free [A Level diagnostic](/practice/9709/diagnostic/a-level/) or [AS diagnostic](/practice/9709/diagnostic/as/) (all our diagnostics are on the [diagnostics page](/diagnostics/)), and tick off the [9709 topic checklist](/checklists/cambridge/a-level/mathematics/).
2. **Sit a full paper under timed conditions.** Get it from Cambridge's past-papers page (older series are available to schools through the School Support Hub) and check exam dates on the [exam calendar](/exam-calendar/).
3. **Mark it strictly with the mark scheme.** Check each command word against our [command words guide](/command-words/), because "show that", "hence" and "find" expect different answers.
4. **Read the notes for that series on this page.** Compare your lost marks with the "Where marks were lost" bullets and the recurring errors above.
5. **Fix one topic at a time.** Use the [9709 Mathematics hub](/boards/cambridge/a-level/mathematics/), for example [Pure Mathematics 1 trigonometry notes](/resources/a-level-maths-9709-pure-mathematics-1-trigonometry-revision-notes/), [Mechanics notes](/resources/a-level-maths-9709-mechanics-revision-notes/) or [Probability & Statistics 1 notes](/resources/a-level-maths-9709-probability-statistics-1-revision-notes/), then practise in the [9709 self-check question bank](/practice/9709/).
6. **Re-test after a week.** Sit a paper from a different series and check that the same errors have gone. For the June 2026 thresholds of our main syllabuses, see the [grade thresholds page](/grade-thresholds/).
7. **Get help if a topic will not stick.** Book a [free trial lesson](/trial/) with one of our maths teachers.

The grade thresholds on this page are copied from Cambridge's grade-threshold tables, and the examiner notes are written in our own words from Cambridge's Principal Examiner Reports for Teachers for March 2023 to June 2026, the eleven series listed above.
