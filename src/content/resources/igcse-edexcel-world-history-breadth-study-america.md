Breadth Study B1, "America: from new nation to divided union, 1783–1877", is one of eight breadth
studies in change in Section B of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It
covers almost a century: the making of the Constitution, westward expansion and the removal of Native
Americans, the conflict over slavery that led to secession, the Civil War, and Reconstruction. Because
it is a breadth study, questions focus on change and continuity across long periods, not only on single
events.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) names five **themes** running through B1 — the
relationship of the separate states to the whole; slavery; the problems of an expanding nation; the role
of government policy; and financial and economic factors. The (c) essay focuses on these themes over a
period of at least 25 years, normally drawing on at least two key topics. The five **key topics** are:

1. **Building a new nation, 1783–1809** — large and small states, slavery, Shays' Rebellion, the
   Connecticut Compromise and the Constitutional Convention (1787), Federalists and Anti-federalists, the
   Bill of Rights, strict and loose constructionists, Jefferson's presidency, the "1800 Revolution" and
   states' rights.
2. **Westward expansion and Native American removal, 1803–49** — opposition from Spain, Britain, Mexico and
   Native Americans; the Louisiana Purchase, the Transcontinental Treaty and the annexation of Texas; the
   Missouri Compromise; the Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears; Manifest Destiny, Texas and Oregon
   and the California Gold Rush.
3. **Slavery, the South and the causes of secession, 1850–61** — the Compromise of 1850, the
   Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854), Bleeding Kansas and Dred Scott; the economic division between North and
   South; John Brown, Lincoln, Fort Sumter and the secession of South Carolina.
4. **Civil War and the end of slavery, 1861–65** — reasons for Union victory, Lee, Sherman and Grant, the
   naval blockade, Lincoln and Davis, the Emancipation Proclamation and Gettysburg Address, Gettysburg and
   Vicksburg (1863), the March through Georgia (1864) and the destruction of the Southern economy.
5. **A More Perfect Union? 1865–77** — Johnson and the Reconstruction Act (1867), the Freedmen's Bureau,
   the Black Codes, the Civil Rights Acts of 1866 and 1875, the 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments, and the
   failure of Grant's Peace Policy, Custer's Last Stand and its aftermath.

## How was the new nation built, 1783–1809?

After independence the states were loosely joined under the Articles of Confederation. **Shays'
Rebellion** (1786–87), an armed revolt by indebted farmers in Massachusetts, showed that the central
government was too weak to keep order. At the **Constitutional Convention** in Philadelphia (1787) large
states wanted representation by population and small states wanted equality; the **Connecticut
Compromise** created a Senate with two members per state and a House of Representatives based on
population. The clash over **slavery** produced the three-fifths clause for counting enslaved people.

**Federalists** such as Hamilton and Madison argued for ratification; **Anti-federalists** feared a
powerful central government. Ratification was secured with the promise of a **Bill of Rights** (1791).
Debate continued between **loose constructionists** (Hamilton, who used implied powers to create a
national bank) and **strict constructionists** (Jefferson). Jefferson's election in 1800 — the "**Revolution
of 1800**" — was the first peaceful transfer of power between parties, and he championed **states' rights**.

## How did the nation expand west, 1803–49?

The **Louisiana Purchase** (1803) from France doubled the country's size. The **Transcontinental Treaty**
(1819) with Spain gained Florida and fixed the western border. Expansion raised the question of slavery:
the **Missouri Compromise** (1820) admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state and banned
slavery north of 36°30' in the rest of the Louisiana territory.

**Native Americans** resisted. Under Andrew Jackson the **Indian Removal Act** (1830) forced eastern nations
west of the Mississippi; on the **Trail of Tears** (1838–39) thousands of Cherokee died. Americans moved
into Texas, which won independence from Mexico (1836) and was **annexed** in 1845, and settlers took the
Oregon Trail. The idea of **Manifest Destiny** — that Americans were destined to spread across the
continent — justified war with Mexico (1846–48), and the **California Gold Rush** (from 1848) brought a
flood of migrants.

## Why did the South secede in 1860–61?

The North was industrialising and relied on free labour; the South's economy depended on cotton grown
by enslaved people. The **Compromise of 1850** admitted California as a free state but imposed a stricter
Fugitive Slave Act, angering Northerners. The **Kansas–Nebraska Act** (1854) let settlers decide on slavery,
overturning the Missouri line and producing violence in **Bleeding Kansas**. In **Dred Scott** (1857) the
Supreme Court ruled that black people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the
territories. **John Brown**'s raid on Harpers Ferry (1859) terrified Southerners. When **Abraham Lincoln**,
of the anti-slavery-expansion Republican Party, won the 1860 election, **South Carolina** seceded, followed by
other Southern states, which formed the Confederacy. The war began when Confederates fired on **Fort
Sumter** (April 1861).

## Why did the Union win the Civil War?

The **Union** had far more people, industry and railways, and its **naval blockade** strangled Southern
trade. The Confederacy had skilled generals, above all **Robert E. Lee**, and fought on home ground. 1863 was
the turning point: Lee was defeated at **Gettysburg** and **Vicksburg** surrendered to **Grant**, giving the Union
control of the Mississippi. Lincoln's **Emancipation Proclamation** (1863) made the war a fight against
slavery and discouraged European recognition of the Confederacy; the **Gettysburg Address** defined the war
as a struggle for a "new birth of freedom". **Sherman**'s **March through Georgia** (1864) destroyed railways,
farms and morale. Lincoln's political leadership proved stronger than **Jefferson Davis**'s. Lee surrendered
in April 1865, leaving the Southern economy in ruins.

## How far did Reconstruction create "a more perfect union", 1865–77?

The **13th Amendment** (1865) abolished slavery. President **Andrew Johnson** let Southern states return
quickly, and they passed **Black Codes** restricting freed people. Congress responded with the **Freedmen's
Bureau**, the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, the **Reconstruction Act** (1867), which placed the South under
military rule until states accepted the **14th Amendment** (citizenship and equal protection), and the **15th
Amendment** (1870), which barred racial discrimination in voting. The **Civil Rights Act of 1875** banned
discrimination in public places but was poorly enforced. White Southern resistance, including the Ku Klux
Klan, steadily reversed gains. In the West, **Grant's Peace Policy** towards Native Americans failed; the
defeat of **Custer** at the Little Bighorn (1876) was followed by the forced removal of the Sioux to
reservations.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best way to revise a breadth study?**
Track each theme across the whole period — for
example, how the slavery question changed from 1787 to 1877.

**Was slavery the main cause of the Civil War?**
Strong answers link slavery to states' rights, economic
difference and expansion rather than treating them as separate causes.

**Did Reconstruction succeed?**
Legally it transformed the Constitution; in practice many gains were lost
after 1877.
