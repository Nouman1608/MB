## W1–W4 in one line each

- **W1 — communicate:** clear, effective, imaginative; form and vocabulary fit the task; reader engaged.
- **W2 — sentences:** a range of structures, chosen for clarity, purpose and effect.
- **W3 — organise:** sentences, paragraphs and the whole text hold together (cohesion and coherence).
- **W4 — accuracy:** grammar, punctuation and spelling.

## Choosing the task: three checks

1. Can I see the **ending** already?
2. Does it suit my **strengths**?
3. Have I read the **instruction word**: describe, write a story, argue, discuss?

## The four types at a glance

| Type | Core move | Structure that works | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Render one moment precisely | Camera movement; time shift; mood change | A list of sights across a whole day |
| Imaginative | Small story with a planned ending | Start late; slow down at the key moment; deliberate ending | Too many characters and events |
| Argumentative | Persuade from a clear position | Hook → 3–4 points → rebut → call to action | Ranting without evidence |
| Discursive | Explore views with balance | Introduce issue → views for → views against → judgement | Pretending to be balanced but taking one side throughout |

## Descriptive toolkit

- Focus: **one place, one moment**.
- **Nouns and verbs first**, adjectives sparingly.
- **Senses**: select one or two precise details per sense.
- **Figurative language**: one fresh image beats three clichés.
- **Structure**: wide → close → wide; outside → inside; dawn → dusk; calm → storm.
- **Motif**: an object or sound that returns.

## Imaginative toolkit

- **One** character, setting, problem and short time span.
- **Opening**: dialogue, action or a striking detail, close to the moment of change.
- **Middle**: build tension; slow time at the climax.
- **Ending**: realisation, twist, cyclical return or deliberate open ending.
- **Show feelings** through action and detail.
- **Dialogue**: short, purposeful, correctly punctuated.

## Argument and discussion toolkit

```
HOOK        anecdote / bold claim / question / scenario
POINT       one idea per paragraph
EVIDENCE    realistic example, observation, expert view you know to be accurate
EXPLAIN     why it matters to the reader
LINK        however / consequently / admittedly / above all
COUNTER     argue: raise and rebut; discuss: weigh fairly
CLOSE       argue: call to action; discuss: considered judgement
```

**Rhetorical devices**: direct address, rhetorical question, rule of three, emotive language, contrast,
repetition. Use each where it adds force.

## Sentence menu (W2)

| Form | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Short simple | It stopped. | Emphasis, shock |
| Multi-clause | As the rain thickened, the street emptied, and the lights came on one by one. | Build-up, flow |
| Fronted adverbial | Without warning, the door opened. | Varied opening, suspense |
| Subordinate clause first | Although she smiled, her hands shook. | Contrast, tension |
| Minor sentence | Silence. | Isolation (use sparingly) |
| Question | Who decides what is fair? | Engages the reader |

## Cohesion (W3)

- Paragraph for each change of **time, place, speaker, focus or argument**.
- Link with pronouns, synonyms, discourse markers and repeated motifs.
- The **ending answers the opening**.

## Accuracy checklist (W4)

- Full stops, not commas, between sentences (no comma splices).
- Apostrophes for possession and contraction only.
- Homophones: there/their/they're, your/you're, its/it's, where/were.
- One tense, unless you deliberately shift.
- Speech punctuation: new speaker, new line.
- Capital letters for names and sentence starts.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cohesion | Links that hold sentences and paragraphs together |
| Coherence | The whole text making sense as a planned piece |
| Discourse marker | A word or phrase that signals the logic of the next point |
| Motif | A repeated image or idea |
| Cyclical structure | Ending that returns to the opening |
| Rebuttal | Answering and overturning an opposing point |
| Register | The level of formality |

## Exam traps

- Changing task after starting.
- Describing with a plot, or narrating when asked to describe.
- Treating "discuss" as "argue" or the reverse.
- Endings that stop rather than finish ("then I woke up").
- Invented statistics in arguments.
- Over-decorated prose with every noun given an adjective.
- No time left to proofread.

## Self-test

1. How many composition tasks are offered, and how many do you answer?
2. Name the task types listed in the specification.
3. Which objective covers sentence structures?
4. What does W3 mean by cohesion and coherence?
5. Give one reason to narrow a descriptive piece to a single moment.
6. Suggest two ways to structure a descriptive piece.
7. Why should you plan the ending of an imaginative piece first?
8. State two differences between argumentative and discursive writing.
9. Give three discourse markers and the logic each signals.
10. Why are invented statistics a weakness?
11. Name four accuracy errors to check for.
12. What is a cyclical structure, and why is it effective?

**Answers:** 1. Three; one. 2. Descriptive, imaginative, argumentative and/or discursive. 3. W2.
4. Cohesion is the linking of sentences and paragraphs; coherence is the whole text making sense as a
planned piece. 5. It allows precise detail instead of a thin list. 6. Any two: wide view to close
detail; outside to inside; the same place at two times; a change of mood. 7. So that the story has a
shape and every paragraph moves towards a deliberate conclusion. 8. Argumentative takes a position and
rebuts counter-arguments; discursive explores views fairly and ends with a considered judgement.
9. For example: however (contrast), consequently (result), admittedly (concession). 10. They are not
credible, and a realistic example is more persuasive. 11. Any four: comma splices, apostrophes,
homophones, tense shifts, speech punctuation, capital letters. 12. An ending that returns to the
opening image or idea; it gives a sense of completion and shows how things have changed.
