## Section A: Short questions

**1.** In a market economy, what mainly determines which goods are produced? **[1]**

A: Output targets set by the government

B: Consumers' spending decisions and firms' search for profit

C: The number of workers available in each industry

D: Decisions of trade unions

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be a feature of a planned economy? **[1]**

A: Prices that change daily in response to demand

B: Firms competing to attract customers through new products

C: Queues and rationing for goods in short supply

D: Private ownership of most factories

**3.** Which **one** of the following best describes a mixed economy? **[1]**

A: An economy that produces both goods and services

B: An economy in which both private firms and the government allocate resources

C: An economy that trades with both market and planned economies

D: An economy with both agricultural and manufacturing sectors

**4.** Distinguish between decentralised and centralised decision-making. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a market economy answers the question "for whom to produce?". **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why a planned economy may produce goods of low quality. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** In an invented planned economy, planners allocate 500 thousand tonnes of cement between housing and road building. They allocate 350 to housing and 150 to roads. Builders would in fact use 280 on housing and 220 on roads.

**(a)** Calculate the surplus or shortage of cement in each sector. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the planners' allocation did not match the builders' needs. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how a market for cement would have reallocated it between the two sectors. **[4]**

**8.** Country Velo moves from a planned economy towards a market economy. Explain **two** changes this would bring to how resources are allocated. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a market economy may under-provide health care, and how a mixed economy might respond. **[4]**

**10.** Invented Countries Arno and Brisa are both mixed economies. Government spending is 45% of GDP in Arno and 22% in Brisa.

**(a)** Explain what these figures suggest about how resources are allocated in each country. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** possible advantage and **one** possible disadvantage of Arno's approach. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a rise in consumers' wants for electric cars would lead to a reallocation of resources in a market economy. **[4]**

**12.** Assess whether a market economy allocates resources better than a planned economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Spending signals what consumers want; profit motivates firms to supply it. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Planners set prices and quantities, so shortages lead to queues and rationing. A, B and D are market features. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. A mixed economy combines private and public sector allocation. **[1]**

**4.** Decentralised decision-making means many separate consumers and firms each decide for themselves, co-ordinated by prices [1]. Centralised decision-making means a single authority, such as a planning agency, decides for the whole economy [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Goods go to those who are willing and able to pay the market price [1], so the distribution of output follows the distribution of income and wealth [1]. **[2]**

**6.** State enterprises are judged on meeting output targets rather than on satisfying customers [1], and there is no competition or profit motive, so managers have little incentive to improve quality [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Housing: 350 − 280 = a **surplus of 70 thousand tonnes** [1]. Roads: 220 − 150 = a **shortage of 70 thousand tonnes** [1].

**(b)** Planners must estimate the needs of every user [1], but they lack full and up-to-date information about builders' plans and costs, so their estimates were wrong [1].

**(c)** Road builders, short of cement, would be willing to pay more for it, so the price of cement would rise [1]. House builders, with more than they need, would sell their surplus or buy less [1]. Cement suppliers would find road builders the more profitable customers and direct more cement to them [1]. Cement would move from housing to roads until the extra benefit of switching disappeared, without any central instruction [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Change 1: prices would be set by demand and supply instead of by planners [1], so they would signal shortages and surpluses and guide firms' output decisions [1]. Change 2: factories would move into private ownership and be run for profit [1], so firms would choose their own methods to cut costs and respond to consumers rather than meet targets [1]. Other valid changes: wider consumer choice; more inequality in incomes. **[4]**

**9.** In a market, health care goes to those who can pay, so people on low incomes may not afford treatment [1]. Consumers may also underestimate its private benefit because of imperfect information, so it is under-consumed as a merit good [1]. A mixed economy might provide health care directly, free at the point of use and funded by taxes [1], or subsidise it and provide information about its benefits [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** A larger share of resources in Arno is allocated by the government, through state provision and spending [1], while in Brisa more resources are allocated through markets [1].

**(b)** Advantage: more services such as education and health care can be provided free, improving access for low-income households [1] and increasing consumption of merit goods [1]. Disadvantage: higher taxes are needed to fund the spending [1], which may reduce incentives to work and invest, and government decisions may respond less closely to individual preferences [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Consumers spend more on electric cars, so demand rises and their price rises [1]. Higher prices and profits signal to firms that electric cars are wanted [1]. Firms expand production and bid for the factors needed, such as batteries, engineers and factory space [1]. Resources move away from less profitable uses, such as petrol cars, towards electric cars, without any central instruction [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 8 marks across knowledge, analysis and evaluation.
- Market: prices transmit consumers' wants quickly and profit rewards firms that satisfy them [1].
- Market: competition encourages efficient use of resources and innovation [1].
- Planned: the information problem leads to shortages and surpluses; weak incentives lower quality [1].
- Planned: can share basic goods more equally and direct resources to priorities [1].
- Market: allocation follows ability to pay, so inequality may result [1].
- Market: public goods are not provided and merit goods are under-consumed [1].
- "Better" depends on the criterion: efficiency favours the market, equity may favour planning, and weighting them is a value judgement [1].
- Judgement: markets generally allocate more efficiently, but most societies choose a mixed economy to address equity and market failure, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing ownership without explaining who makes decisions.
- Asserting that plans cause shortages without explaining the information problem.
- Explaining market reallocation without the role of price and profit.
- Treating all mixed economies as identical.
- Concluding without stating the criterion used to judge "better".
