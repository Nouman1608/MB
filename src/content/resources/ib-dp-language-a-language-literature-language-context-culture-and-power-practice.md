## Questions

**1.** Define *representation*, and state the difference between *perspective* and *bias*. **[2]**

### Passage A -- two news openings (original texts written for Marlbridge; the town and people are invented)

> **Text A1.** LIBRARY SIT-IN: ACTIVISTS TAKE OVER COUNCIL BUILDING
> A group of activists has occupied Brinmouth Central Library for a second day, forcing staff to
> cancel children's reading sessions. Council leader Mark Ellery said the protest was "unlawful and
> irresponsible". The library is due to close next month as part of savings of £2 million.

> **Text A2.** "This is the only warm place many of us have": residents refuse to leave library
> Around forty residents, some in their eighties, spent a second night inside Brinmouth Central
> Library yesterday. The library, which lends 90,000 books a year, is to be closed by the council next
> month. "My grandchildren learned to read here," said retired bus driver Joyce Amadi, 78.

**2.** Identify how Text A1 and Text A2 each name the people inside the library. Give one quotation
from each. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the choice of who is quoted in Text A1 positions the reader. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how Text A2 uses details about the people to build the reader's sympathy. Refer to two
quotations. **[4]**

**5.** Compare how Text A1 and Text A2 present who is responsible for closing the library. **[3]**

### Passage B -- Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), Chapter 4 (public domain; (…) marks a cut)

> (…) if fear in girls, instead of being cherished, perhaps, created, were treated in the same manner
> as cowardice in boys, we should quickly see women with more dignified aspects. It is true, they
> could not then with equal propriety be termed the sweet flowers that smile in the walk of man; but
> they would be more respectable members of society, and discharge the important duties of life by
> the light of their own reason. "Educate women like men," says Rousseau, "and the more they resemble
> our sex the less power will they have over us." This is the very point I aim at. I do not wish them
> to have power over men; but over themselves.

**6.** Explain how the phrase "the sweet flowers that smile in the walk of man" represents women, and
why Wollstonecraft uses it. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how Wollstonecraft uses Rousseau's words to redefine *power*. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how a reader in 1792 and a reader today might respond differently to Passage B. **[4]**

### Passage C -- workplace email (original text written for Marlbridge; the company is invented)

> **To:** All colleagues
> **From:** The Executive Team
> **Subject:** Our next chapter
>
> We are excited to share that Brackwater Foods is entering a new phase of growth. Following a
> careful review, the Leeming Road site will be consolidated into our central hub from 1 March. A
> small number of roles will be impacted by this transition. Colleagues affected will be contacted in
> due course. We know change can be unsettling, and we thank you for your continued flexibility.
> Together, we will emerge stronger.

**9.** Identify two euphemisms in Passage C and explain what each one hides. **[4]**

**10.** Guided analysis: *How does Passage C use language to present a decision as positive and
unavoidable?* Write a plan with a thesis and three analytical points, each with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Comparison plan: *Compare how Text A1 and Passage C give authority to an institution's point
of view.* Give a thesis, three points of comparison and a closing sentence on what the comparison
shows. **[8]**

## Answers

For questions 10 and 11 the points listed are **indicative**. The real exam is marked holistically
against the IB assessment criteria, not point by point.

**1.** Representation is the way a text presents people, groups, places or events through its
choices of language, selection and framing. [1] Every text has a perspective (the position it is
written from); bias is a stronger claim -- a one-sided treatment presented as fair -- that must be
shown with evidence. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Credit goes to answers that separate the two terms; saying "bias means
perspective" treats a universal feature as a fault.

**2.** Text A1 calls them "activists" (or "A group of activists"). [1] Text A2 calls them "residents"
(or "Around forty residents"). [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Identify" needs the exact word from each text; a paraphrase such as
"protesters" for both misses the contrast.

**3.** Only the council leader, "Mark Ellery", is quoted, so official authority speaks. [1] His
words "unlawful and irresponsible" frame the protest as a breach of law, not a response to a closure.
[1] No occupier speaks, so the reader hears only the institution and sees the sit-in as a problem. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers note who is **not** quoted; the occupiers' silence is evidence.

**4.** The detail "some in their eighties" stresses age and vulnerability. [1] The occupiers seem frail, not
threatening, so removing them would seem harsh. [1] Naming "retired bus driver Joyce Amadi, 78" turns a group
into an ordinary, named individual. [1] Her words "My grandchildren learned to read here" link the
library to family and learning, so the closure reads as a community loss. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each quotation must be followed by its effect on the reader; two quotations
dropped in without comment earn little.

**5.** Text A1 says the library "is due to close", which has no agent: the closure seems to happen
by itself. [1] The phrase "as part of savings of £2 million" frames it as a sensible budget measure.
[1] Text A2 says it "is to be closed by the council", naming the council as the actor and so making
it responsible. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Comparison rewards a point that holds both texts together; describing A1, then
A2, without the contrast in agency misses it.

**6.** The metaphor presents women as decorative and passive -- flowers exist to be looked at. [1]
The phrase "smile in the walk of man" places women along a man's path, for his enjoyment. [1] Wollstonecraft
cites the image to reject it: she wants women to be "more respectable members of society", so the
flattery is exposed as a way of keeping women weak. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The best answers see that the image is used critically; treating it as her own
view reverses the passage's meaning.

**7.** Rousseau warns that educating women "like men" will reduce their "power" over men. [1] For
him, women's power is influence over men through charm and difference. [1] Wollstonecraft accepts
his prediction -- "This is the very point I aim at" -- so his warning becomes her goal. [1] She
redefines power as self-command, "not ... over men; but over themselves", guided by "the light of
their own reason". [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong responses show the quotation turned against its speaker; summarising
Rousseau without the reversal stays at description.

**8.** In 1792 the claim that girls should be raised like boys challenged common views of women's
nature, so many readers would have found it radical. [1] Readers who shared Rousseau's view would
have read it oppositionally. [1] A reader today, where equal education is common, may find the main
claim obvious and read the passage as history. [1] That reader may instead notice limits: measuring
girls against "cowardice in boys" still treats male behaviour as the standard. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Answers that name a specific difference between readers, tied to a phrase in the
passage, score higher than general claims that "times have changed".

**9.** "consolidated into our central hub" [1] hides that the Leeming Road site is closing. [1] "A
small number of roles will be impacted" [1] hides job losses and turns people into "roles". [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming a euphemism is only half the answer; the mark for effect comes from saying
exactly what harsh fact the phrase replaces.

**10.** Model plan:

*Thesis:* The email frames a site closure as good news by using the language of growth, hiding the
actors behind passive verbs and binding readers into a shared "we". [1]

*Point 1 -- positive framing.* "Our next chapter" and "entering a new phase of growth" present the
change as progress [1]; the closure comes only after "We are excited to share" has set the mood [1].

*Point 2 -- hidden decision-makers.* "Following a careful review" and "will be consolidated" give no
agent [1]; the decision sounds like the result of a process, not a choice that could be reversed [1].

*Point 3 -- pronouns and power.* "We" is the Executive Team, yet "Together, we will emerge stronger"
folds staff into the same group [1]; the affected staff are "Colleagues affected" who "will be
contacted", always acted upon, never acting [1].

*Linking sentence:* The email serves the company's version of events: by the end, the reader is
thanked for "continued flexibility", which asks for acceptance before any objection is voiced. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Plans score well when every point answers the guiding question with a
quotation; features listed in text order, not tied to "positive and unavoidable", read as description.

**11.** Model plan:

*Thesis:* Both texts give authority to an institution by letting it speak while the people affected
are summarised or silenced, but Text A1 does this through news selection while Passage C does it
through the institution's own voice. [1]

*Point 1 -- who speaks.* In Text A1 only the council leader is quoted ("unlawful and
irresponsible") [1]; in Passage C the whole text is the voice of "The Executive Team", and the staff
never speak [1].

*Point 2 -- hiding the decision.* Text A1's "is due to close" and Passage C's "will be consolidated"
both remove the agent from the key decision [1], so in both texts the closure seems inevitable rather
than chosen [1].

*Point 3 -- framing the cost.* Text A1 frames the closure as "savings of £2 million", and Passage C
as "a new phase of growth" [1]; both use the language of good management to hide a loss to the
people affected [1].

*Closing sentence:* In both texts authority comes less from argument than from control of voice and
grammar: the institution names the event, and the people affected are left without words. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The strongest plans keep both texts in every point; covering Text A1 in full and
then Passage C in full is block comparison and weakens the argument.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating Text A2 as the "true" version when it also selects (it omits the council's budget case).
- Spotting that "is due to close" has no agent without saying who is hidden.
- Reading "the sweet flowers that smile in the walk of man" as Wollstonecraft's own view.
- Writing about 1792 as general history with no link to a phrase in Passage B.
- Naming a euphemism but not the harsh fact it replaces.
- Missing how "we" in Passage C shifts from the executives to everyone.
- Comparing block by block in question 11 instead of pairing evidence in each point.
