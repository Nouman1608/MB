These notes cover section 3.1.1.3, Weather hazards, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. Key ideas: "Global atmospheric circulation helps to determine patterns of weather and climate"; "Tropical storms develop as a result of particular physical conditions"; "Tropical storms have significant effects on people and the environment." For full explanations, read the [weather hazards study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-weather-hazards/). For the whole unit, see the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/).

## Specification checklist

- **Atmospheric circulation:** pressure belts and surface winds.
- **Distribution** of tropical storms and the link to circulation.
- **Causes and sequence** of formation; **structure** and features.
- Effect of **climate change** on distribution, frequency and intensity.
- **Primary and secondary effects**; **immediate and long-term responses**.
- A **named example**.
- **Monitoring, prediction, protection and planning.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tropical storm | Large rotating storm over warm tropical seas with very strong winds and heavy rain |
| Hurricane / typhoon / cyclone | Regional names for the same storm |
| Trade winds | Surface winds blowing towards the equator |
| Westerlies | Surface winds blowing from the west at mid-latitudes |
| Coriolis effect | Deflection of moving air by the Earth's rotation |
| Latent heat | Heat released when water vapour condenses |
| Eye | Calm, clear centre |
| Eyewall | Ring of tall clouds with the strongest winds |
| Storm surge | Raised sea level pushed onto the coast |

## The circulation model

| Latitude | Pressure | Surface wind | Weather |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equator (0°) | Low (air rising) | Trade winds blow in from both sides | Heavy rain, rainforests |
| About 30° | High (air sinking) | Winds flow out, as trade winds and westerlies | Dry, hot deserts |
| About 60° | Low | Westerlies meet polar easterlies | Unsettled, changeable |
| Poles (90°) | High | Polar easterlies | Cold, dry |

- Three cells per hemisphere: **Hadley** (0-30°), **Ferrel** (30-60°), **Polar** (60-90°).
- Winds are deflected right in the north and left in the south.
- Trade winds: north-east in the north, south-east in the south.

## Distribution

- Between about **5° and 30° north and south** of the equator.
- Over oceans with warm water; on the **western** sides of oceans.
- Hurricanes: Atlantic and east Pacific. Typhoons: north-west Pacific. Cyclones: Indian Ocean, South Pacific, Australia.
- Hardly any in the South Atlantic or south-east Pacific (cool water).
- **Link to circulation:** the trade winds steer storms westwards, the Earth's spin turns them poleward, and the westerlies carry them eastwards as they weaken.

## Conditions needed

1. Sea temperature at least about **27°C**, to a depth of about 50 m.
2. More than about **5° from the equator** (enough Coriolis spin).
3. **Light upper winds** (low wind shear).
4. An existing area of low pressure and thunderstorms.

## Sequence of formation

1. Warm, moist air rises from the sea.
2. It cools and condenses into huge storm clouds, releasing latent heat, which makes more air rise.
3. Pressure at the surface falls; air rushes in.
4. Coriolis effect makes the air spiral.
5. Winds strengthen above about 120 km/h; an eye forms as air sinks in the middle.
6. The storm moves west with the trade winds.
7. It weakens over land (no moisture or heat, more friction) or cool water.

## Structure

- Several hundred kilometres across.
- **Eye:** 30-60 km, calm, clear, sinking air, lowest pressure.
- **Eyewall:** strongest winds, heaviest rain, tallest clouds.
- **Rainbands** spiral outwards.
- **Storm surge** often causes the most deaths.
- Spins anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south.

## Climate change and tropical storms

| Aspect | Likely change |
|---|---|
| Intensity | Strongest storms probably stronger, with heavier rainfall |
| Distribution | Warm water may extend further from the equator |
| Frequency | Uncertain; total may not rise, but the share of very strong storms may |
| Sea level | Rising sea level makes surges reach further inland |

Use cautious language ("might", "is likely to").

## Effects and responses

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary | Wind damage, heavy rain, storm surge flooding, deaths and injuries |
| Secondary | River floods, landslides, disease, lost crops and income, homelessness, disrupted power and transport |
| Immediate response | Evacuation, shelters, rescue, food, water, medical care, clearing roads |
| Long-term response | Rebuilding, restoring livelihoods, defences, better warning systems and education |

## Named example: Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines, 2013 (approximate)

- North-west Pacific typhoon belt; one of the strongest storms recorded at landfall, with winds around 300 km/h.
- Storm surge of several metres in the Tacloban area; more than 6 000 deaths.
- Millions displaced; over a million homes damaged or destroyed; crops and coconut plantations lost.
- Hundreds of thousands evacuated before landfall; international aid arrived, but damaged roads and airports slowed delivery.
- Long term: stronger homes, mangrove replanting, better evacuation planning, new coastal safety zones.

If you studied another storm (for example Hurricane Katrina in 2005), use the same headings with its own facts.

## Managing the risk

- **Monitoring:** satellites, aircraft, weather buoys.
- **Prediction:** tracks forecast days ahead; intensity is less certain.
- **Protection:** shelters, reinforced buildings, sea walls, mangroves.
- **Planning:** evacuation routes, emergency stocks, education, land-use zoning.

## Exam traps

- Storms form over warm oceans, not land.
- Hurricane, typhoon and cyclone are the same type of storm.
- No formation at the equator because the Coriolis effect is too weak.
- The eye is calm; the eyewall has the strongest winds.
- Climate change: intensity is more certain than frequency.
- Name your example and give specific facts.
- Explain distribution with sea temperature **and** the Coriolis effect **and** circulation.

## Self-test

1. At roughly which latitudes is the Hadley cell?
2. Where is surface pressure low and where is it high?
3. Name the trade winds' direction in the northern hemisphere.
4. What name is given to tropical storms in the north-west Pacific?
5. State **three** conditions needed for a tropical storm to form.
6. Why do tropical storms weaken over land?
7. Describe the structure of a tropical storm.
8. Give **two** primary and **two** secondary effects.
9. How might climate change affect tropical storms?
10. Give **three** ways to reduce the effects of a tropical storm.

**Answers**

1. From the equator to about 30° north and south.
2. Low at the equator and about 60°; high at about 30° and the poles.
3. From the north-east.
4. Typhoon.
5. Any three: sea temperature of at least 27°C; more than 5° from the equator; light upper winds; an existing low-pressure area.
6. They lose their supply of warm, moist air, and friction with the land slows the wind.
7. A calm eye with sinking air at the centre; an eyewall of tall clouds with the strongest winds and rain; spiral rainbands outside; a spin that is anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere.
8. Primary: strong winds; storm surge; heavy rain. Secondary: river floods; landslides; disease; lost income.
9. Warmer seas might make the strongest storms more intense with more rain, extend the area where storms can form, and rising sea levels would make surges worse; the effect on frequency is uncertain.
10. Any three: forecasting and warnings; evacuation; shelters; strong buildings; sea walls; mangroves; land-use planning; education.
