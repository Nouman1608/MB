## What the syllabus lists

- Global distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes related to plate tectonics.
- Earthquake hazards: shaking, landslides, soil liquefaction, tsunami.
- Volcanic hazards: eruption types and products (nuées ardentes, lava flows, lahars, volcanic landslides, pyroclastic flows, ash fallout).
- Primary and secondary impacts on lives and property.
- Prediction, hazard mapping, preparedness, monitoring, perception of risk.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Focus / epicentre | Point of rupture underground / point on the surface directly above |
| Benioff zone | Sloping band of earthquakes along a subducting plate |
| Moment magnitude (Mw) | Energy released; each unit about 32 times more energy |
| P, S and surface waves | Fast compressional, slower shear, slowest and most damaging |
| Liquefaction | Saturated loose sediment loses strength as pore-water pressure rises |
| Tsunami | Waves from sudden displacement of the water column |
| Viscosity | Resistance to flow; higher with more silica and lower temperature |
| Nuée ardente | Glowing avalanche of hot gas and ash, often from dome collapse |
| Lahar | Volcanic mudflow of debris and water |
| Primary impact | Direct result of the event |
| Secondary impact | Result of the primary impacts |
| Vulnerability | How easily people and property are harmed |
| Risk | Hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity to cope |

## Distribution recall

- **Subduction:** deepest and largest earthquakes; explosive volcanoes (Andes, Japan).
- **Divergent:** shallow, moderate earthquakes; effusive basalt (Iceland).
- **Conservative:** shallow, large earthquakes; no volcanoes (San Andreas, North Anatolian).
- **Collision:** wide belt of shallow earthquakes; few volcanoes (Himalaya).
- **Hotspot:** shield volcanoes, small earthquakes (Hawaii).

## Hazard mechanisms

- **Shaking:** worse with shallow focus, nearby epicentre, long duration, soft sediment, resonance in buildings.
- **Landslides:** shaking lowers friction on steep weathered slopes; can dam rivers.
- **Liquefaction:** tilting buildings, sand boils, broken pipes.
- **Tsunami:** speed about √(gd); about 710 km per hour at 4,000 m depth; slows and grows near the coast; sea may draw back first.
- **Eruption style:** low viscosity, low gas = effusive; high viscosity, high gas = explosive.

## Examples at a glance

| Event | Point to remember |
|---|---|
| Indian Ocean tsunami, 26 December 2004 (Mw 9.1) | About 230,000 deaths in 14 countries; no regional warning system at the time |
| Haiti, 12 January 2010 (Mw 7.0) | Vulnerability: informal housing, weak construction |
| Turkey–Syria, 6 February 2023 (Mw 7.8) | More than 50,000 deaths; buildings below code |
| L'Aquila, 6 April 2009 | Over 300 deaths; risk communication; 2012 convictions, scientists acquitted 2014 |
| Montserrat, 25 June 1997 | Pyroclastic flows killed 19; Plymouth abandoned |
| Eyjafjallajökull, 2010 | Small eruption, ash closed European airspace |
| Kīlauea, 2018 | Lava flows destroyed hundreds of homes; few lives at risk |
| Parkfield, California | Expected about 1988, occurred 2004: limits of prediction |

## Monitoring and preparedness

- **Volcanoes:** seismometers (swarms), tiltmeters, GPS, satellite radar (deformation), gas sensors (sulphur dioxide), thermal imaging.
- **Earthquakes:** probability forecasts only; early warning gives seconds after rupture.
- **Hazard maps:** past deposits, faults, ground type, liquefaction zones.
- **Preparedness:** building codes, base isolation, cross-bracing, drills, tsunami routes and vertical evacuation.

## Mini essay plan

*"Volcanic hazards are easier to manage than earthquake hazards." How far do you agree?*

1. **Define** management: prediction, monitoring, hazard mapping, preparedness.
2. **For:** volcanoes give warning signs (swarms, swelling, gas), so evacuation is possible; hazard zones can be mapped from past deposits; Montserrat's exclusion zone protected life once enforced.
3. **Against:** some eruptions give little warning; lahars and ash reach far from the vent; evacuation may last years and destroy livelihoods.
4. **Earthquakes:** no precise prediction (Parkfield), but building codes and early warning can greatly reduce deaths; L'Aquila shows communication problems.
5. **Judgement:** volcanoes are easier to forecast, but earthquakes can be mitigated through construction; the answer depends on wealth and governance.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Prediction** gives a time and place; **forecasting** gives probabilities.
- **Magnitude** measures energy; **intensity** measures shaking at a place.

## Exam traps

- Magnitude given as the only cause of death toll.
- Lava flows and pyroclastic flows confused.
- Claims that earthquakes can be predicted.
- Impacts not classified as primary or secondary.
- Tsunami explained without displacement of the water column.
- Perception of risk reduced to "people are unaware".

## Self-test

1. Why are the largest earthquakes found at subduction zones?
2. Why do conservative margins have no volcanoes?
3. How much more energy does an Mw 8.0 earthquake release than an Mw 7.0?
4. Explain liquefaction in one sentence.
5. Why do tsunami slow and grow near the coast?
6. What controls magma viscosity?
7. How does a lahar differ from a pyroclastic flow?
8. Give two secondary impacts of an earthquake.
9. Name three volcano monitoring methods.
10. What did the Parkfield experiment show?
11. Give two reasons why people live near active volcanoes.
12. Why did the 2010 Haiti earthquake cause so many deaths?

### Answers

1. Large areas of locked plate interface can store strain over long periods and release it in one rupture.
2. No crust is created or destroyed and no plate subducts, so no magma is generated.
3. About 32 times more.
4. Shaking raises pore-water pressure in saturated loose sediment, so grains lose contact and the ground behaves like a liquid.
5. Speed depends on depth, so the wave slows in shallow water and its energy is compressed into a higher, shorter wave.
6. Mainly silica content (more silica, more viscous) and temperature (cooler, more viscous); gas content affects explosiveness.
7. A lahar is a water-rich mudflow of volcanic debris; a pyroclastic flow is a current of hot gas, ash and rock.
8. Any two: fires, disease, homelessness, loss of income, blocked transport, landslide-dammed lakes bursting.
9. Seismometers, tiltmeters or GPS, gas sensors (also satellite radar, thermal imaging).
10. Earthquakes do not recur at regular intervals, so they cannot be predicted from past timing.
11. Any two: fertile soils, jobs in tourism or geothermal energy, family ties, cost of moving, low perceived risk.
12. A shallow earthquake close to a dense city, with weakly built informal housing and limited emergency capacity.
