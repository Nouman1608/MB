This guide covers **4.2.2.5 Polymers** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from
September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of matter,
examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only), and there is no
required practical. How polymers are made from alkenes and from other monomers belongs to the organic chemistry
topic; this page is about what a polymer is and why it behaves as it does.

For quick recall use the [polymers revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-polymers-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [polymers practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-polymers-practice/).

| Statement or skill | What to be ready for |
|---|---|
| Polymers have very large molecules | Describing size |
| The atoms in the polymer molecules are linked by strong covalent bonds | Naming the bonds inside the molecule |
| The intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are relatively strong | Comparing with small molecules |
| So polymers are solids at room temperature | Explaining the state |
| Recognise polymers from diagrams showing their bonding and structure | Picking a polymer out from a set of diagrams |

## What is a polymer?

A **polymer** is a substance made of **very large molecules**. Each polymer molecule is a long chain made of the
same small unit repeated many times. The small unit is the **repeat unit**. Thousands of repeat units may be
joined in one chain.

You can write a polymer using the repeat unit in square brackets, with the letter *n* afterwards to show that
the unit repeats a large number of times. For poly(ethene), the repeat unit has two carbon atoms and four
hydrogen atoms:

```
   H   H
   |   |
 [ C - C ]n
   |   |
   H   H
```

The bond lines at the two ends of the unit pass through the brackets. They show that the unit is joined to the
next one at each end. The *n* is a very large number.

Other polymers have side groups. In poly(propene) one hydrogen on each repeat unit is replaced by a CH₃ group.
In poly(chloroethene) it is replaced by a chlorine atom.

## Bonding in a polymer

Inside a polymer molecule, every atom is joined to its neighbours by **strong covalent bonds**. The backbone
is a chain of carbon atoms. Two kinds of attraction are present, just as in small molecules.

| Type | Where | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Covalent bonds | Between atoms along and within a polymer molecule | Strong |
| Intermolecular forces | Between neighbouring polymer molecules | Weak for a small molecule, but **relatively strong** here |

The intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are described in the specification as **relatively strong**.
Compare them with the forces between small molecules such as methane. The reason is size: a polymer molecule is
so long that it lies alongside many other molecules. Many forces add up along its length, so the total attraction
between two chains is much greater than between two small molecules.

## Why polymers are solids at room temperature

To melt a polymer, the chains must be moved past each other, which means overcoming the intermolecular forces.
Because these forces are relatively strong, a lot more energy is needed than for small molecules. So polymers are
**solids at room temperature**, unlike most small-molecule substances, which are gases or liquids.

The covalent bonds are not overcome when a polymer melts or softens. If you heated it enough, the bonds along
the chain would eventually break, and the polymer would decompose or burn rather than boil as one substance.

| Feature | Small molecules (for example ethene) | Polymer (poly(ethene)) |
|---|---|---|
| Size of molecule | A few atoms | Thousands of atoms |
| Covalent bonds inside | Strong | Strong |
| Forces between molecules | Weak | Relatively strong |
| State at room temperature | Gas | Solid |

## Polymers compared with other covalent substances

There are three kinds of covalent substance in the specification.

| Type | Example | What makes it solid or gas |
|---|---|---|
| Small molecules | Methane, water | Weak intermolecular forces; mostly gases or liquids |
| Polymers (very large molecules) | Poly(ethene) | Long molecules with relatively strong intermolecular forces; solids |
| Giant covalent structures | Diamond, silicon dioxide | A network of atoms all joined by covalent bonds; solids with very high melting points |

The key difference between a polymer and a giant covalent structure is this. A polymer is made of **separate
long molecules** held together by intermolecular forces. A giant covalent structure is **one continuous network**
of covalent bonds. So a polymer softens or melts at a far lower temperature than diamond.

## Recognising polymers from diagrams

The specification asks you to recognise polymers from diagrams showing their bonding and structure. Look for
these features.

1. **Square brackets** around a short section of a chain.
2. **Bond lines passing through the brackets** to show it continues.
3. The letter ***n*** after the closing bracket.
4. A **long chain** of repeated atoms, with the same pattern repeating.
5. Side groups such as H, Cl or CH₃ attached along a carbon backbone.

Diagrams that show a single small group of atoms, a network that goes on in all directions, or a pattern of
ions are **not** polymers.

### Worked example 1: recognising a polymer

*Three diagrams are shown. Diagram 1: two oxygen atoms joined by a double bond. Diagram 2: a repeat unit
containing two carbon atoms, in square brackets, with bonds extending through the brackets and the letter n
after the bracket. Diagram 3: a network of carbon atoms, each bonded to four others, continuing in all
directions. Which diagram shows a polymer?*

```
Diagram 1: a small molecule (O2)
Diagram 2: brackets, bonds through the brackets and n -> a polymer
Diagram 3: giant covalent network (diamond)
Answer: Diagram 2
```

### Worked example 2: counting atoms in a polymer chain

*A polymer chain of poly(ethene) has 250 repeat units. Each repeat unit is C₂H₄. How many carbon atoms and
hydrogen atoms are in the chain?*

```
Carbon atoms:   250 x 2 = 500
Hydrogen atoms: 250 x 4 = 1000
```

So one such chain has 500 carbon atoms and 1000 hydrogen atoms: a very large molecule. A real chain can be
longer.

### Worked example 3: explaining the state

*Poly(ethene) is a solid at room temperature but ethene, C₂H₄, is a gas. Explain the difference.*

```
Ethene:  small molecules, weak intermolecular forces, little energy to separate -> gas
Poly(ethene): very large molecules; the forces between neighbouring chains
              add up along the length and are relatively strong
              more energy is needed to separate the chains -> solid at room temperature
In both cases the covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken.
```

### Worked example 4: choosing the right description

*Which description fits a polymer: (A) a lattice of ions, (B) a network of atoms with covalent bonds in all
directions, (C) very large molecules with strong covalent bonds along the chains?*

The answer is **C**. Option A is an ionic compound and option B is a giant covalent structure.

### Worked example 5: finding a repeat unit formula

*A repeat unit in poly(chloroethene) has two carbon atoms. One carbon has two hydrogen atoms. The other has one
hydrogen atom and one chlorine atom. Give the formula of the repeat unit and the number of each atom in 120
units.*

```
Carbon:   2
Hydrogen: 2 + 1 = 3
Chlorine: 1
Formula of repeat unit: C2H3Cl

In 120 units:  C = 120 x 2 = 240
               H = 120 x 3 = 360
               Cl = 120 x 1 = 120
```

## Common mistakes

- Calling a polymer a giant covalent structure. A polymer is made of separate very large molecules.
- Saying the intermolecular forces in a polymer are weak. The specification says they are relatively strong.
- Writing that covalent bonds are broken when a polymer melts.
- Forgetting the bond lines through the brackets, or the *n*, when drawing a repeat unit.
- Writing a repeat unit with a double bond between the carbon atoms. In the polymer the carbon atoms are
  joined by single bonds.
- Saying a polymer is a solid because it contains "strong covalent bonds". The reason is the relatively strong
  forces between the large molecules.

## Exam technique

- **Recognise / identify:** point to the feature: brackets, bonds through them and *n*.
- **Explain why it is a solid:** very large molecules, relatively strong intermolecular forces, more energy
  needed than for small molecules.
- **Compare:** name the particles and the forces for each substance.
- **Draw:** include the brackets, the bonds through them and the *n*.
