These notes condense learning outcomes 3.1.3(a) to (f) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification (H420, Version 4.1, April 2026, first assessment 2025), including the practical work linked to PAG1, PAG2, PAG5 and PAG11. ## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transpiration | Loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through stomata |
| Transpiration stream | Movement of water from roots to leaves in the xylem |
| Apoplast pathway | Water moves through cell walls and intercellular spaces |
| Symplast pathway | Water moves through cytoplasm, from cell to cell via plasmodesmata |
| Cohesion | Attraction between water molecules by hydrogen bonds |
| Adhesion | Attraction between water molecules and the xylem walls |
| Xerophyte | Plant adapted to dry conditions |
| Hydrophyte | Plant adapted to living in water |
| Translocation | Energy-requiring movement of assimilates in the phloem from source to sink |
| Source / sink | Where assimilates are loaded into phloem / where they are removed and used or stored |

## Need for transport: 3.1.3(a)

- Large plants have a small overall SA:V and long distances between roots and leaves.
- Roots need sugars from leaves; leaves need water and ions from roots.
- Diffusion is too slow over these distances.

## Vascular tissue: 3.1.3(b)

| | Xylem vessel | Sieve tube element | Companion cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Living? | No | Yes | Yes |
| End walls | None | Sieve plates | Normal |
| Nucleus | No | No | Yes |
| Special features | Lignin rings or spirals; pits | Few organelles | Many mitochondria; plasmodesmata to sieve tube |
| Carries | Water, mineral ions; upwards | Sucrose and other assimilates; up or down | Loads sucrose using ATP |

| Organ | Position of xylem and phloem |
|---|---|
| Root | Xylem central (star or X); phloem between the arms |
| Stem | Bundles in a ring; xylem inside, phloem outside |
| Leaf | Xylem uppermost in the midrib and veins; phloem below |

Practicals: stained sections (PAG1); stem dissected lengthways and across, for example celery in dye (PAG2).

## Transpiration: 3.1.3(c)

- A consequence of gas exchange: stomata open for CO₂, so water vapour escapes.
- Faster with: more light, higher temperature, lower humidity, more air movement.
- Slower when soil water is short, because stomata close.

**Potometer checklist**

- Cut the shoot under water, at an angle.
- Assemble under water; seal joints with petroleum jelly.
- Dry the leaves; allow time to adjust.
- One air bubble; measure distance moved in a set time; reset with the reservoir.
- Volume = πr²l; rate = volume ÷ time.
- Measures water uptake, not exactly transpiration.

## Water movement: 3.1.3(d)

1. Soil water → root hair cell by osmosis (higher to lower water potential).
2. Across the cortex by apoplast and symplast pathways.
3. Endodermis: waterproof band blocks the apoplast, so water enters the symplast; ions pumped into xylem lower its water potential.
4. Up the xylem: transpiration pull, cohesion, adhesion; water column under tension.
5. Leaf: mesophyll cell walls → evaporation into air spaces → diffusion out through stomata.

## Adaptations: 3.1.3(e)

| Plant | Key features |
|---|---|
| Marram grass | Rolled leaf; sunken stomata on the inner surface; hairs; thick cuticle outside |
| Cactus | Spines instead of leaves; water-storing stem that photosynthesises; thick cuticle; wide roots |
| Water lily | Stomata on the upper surface; large air spaces for buoyancy; thin cuticle; little lignin |

Xerophyte logic: trap humid air or cut the evaporating surface, so the water vapour gradient or the area is reduced.

## Translocation: 3.1.3(f)

1. Companion cells pump H⁺ out (active transport, ATP).
2. H⁺ diffuses back with sucrose through co-transporter proteins.
3. Sucrose enters the sieve tube via plasmodesmata.
4. Water potential falls; water enters from xylem by osmosis; pressure rises.
5. Sink removes sucrose (respiration or starch); water leaves; pressure falls.
6. Mass flow from high to low pressure.

## Worked reminders

- Bubble moves 18 mm in 3.0 min in a tube of radius 0.40 mm: volume = π × 0.16 × 18 = 9.0 mm³; rate = 3.0 mm³ min⁻¹.
- If total leaf area is 60 cm², rate = 3.0 ÷ 60 = 0.050 mm³ min⁻¹ cm⁻².

## Exam traps

- "Transpiration is water loss" with no mention of water vapour or stomata.
- Xylem described as living or with end walls.
- Apoplast and symplast swapped.
- Cohesion described as water sticking to the xylem wall.
- A potometer claimed to measure transpiration exactly.
- Translocation described as passive or as one-way.
- Radius and diameter confused in πr²l.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is transpiration described as a consequence of gas exchange?
2. Give two ways in which a xylem vessel is adapted to its function.
3. State the role of companion cells.
4. Where is the xylem in a root?
5. Explain why high humidity reduces the rate of transpiration.
6. Why must a shoot be cut under water for a potometer?
7. Name the two pathways water takes across the root cortex.
8. Explain how cohesion and adhesion help water move up the xylem.
9. Give two adaptations of marram grass and explain one.
10. Why do water lilies have stomata on the upper surface of their leaves?
11. What is meant by a sink? Give an example.
12. Explain why translocation needs energy.

### Answers

1. Stomata must open to let carbon dioxide in, so water vapour diffuses out at the same time.
2. No end walls, forming a continuous tube; lignin strengthens walls and stops collapse under tension (also pits for sideways movement).
3. They provide ATP and actively load sucrose into the sieve tube elements.
4. In the centre, in a star or X shape.
5. The air outside holds more water vapour, so the gradient from the air spaces to the air is less steep and diffusion is slower.
6. To stop air entering the xylem and breaking the water column.
7. Apoplast and symplast.
8. Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together (cohesion), so the column is pulled up as a whole; water also clings to the xylem walls (adhesion).
9. Rolled leaves, sunken stomata, hairs, thick cuticle. For example, sunken stomata trap humid air, reducing the water vapour gradient.
10. The lower surface is in water; stomata on the upper surface are in contact with air for gas exchange.
11. A region where assimilates are removed from the phloem and used or stored, such as a root, meristem or developing fruit.
12. Active loading at the source uses ATP from companion cells to pump hydrogen ions, which drives sucrose uptake into the sieve tubes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is root pressure the main way water rises?**
No. The transpiration pull, with cohesion and adhesion, is the main mechanism. Ion pumping at the endodermis helps draw water into the xylem.

**Why does the water lily need little lignified tissue?**
Water supports the plant, so it needs little strengthening tissue. Large air spaces keep the leaves floating at the surface.

**Can phloem move sugar upwards?**
Yes. Assimilates move from source to sink in whichever direction the sink lies, for example upwards to growing shoot tips.
