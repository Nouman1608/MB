These questions test the section 3.3 row on the rules of tort law: what a tort claim is, where the rules come from, the burden of proof, the role of fault across the torts, and how defences and remedies complete an answer.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain **two** differences between a tort claim and a criminal prosecution. **[2]**

**2.** State the burden and standard of proof in a negligence claim. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the source of the rules on liability to visitors and to trespassers on premises. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by res ipsa loquitur, giving an authority. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between contributory negligence and consent as defences. **[3]**

**6.** State the aim of compensatory damages and explain what is meant by mitigation of loss. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Lena's doctor carelessly fails to diagnose her illness. Expert evidence shows that, even with a correct diagnosis, the illness could not have been treated. Explain whether Lena can succeed in negligence. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how far fault is required for liability in negligence and under the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why vicarious liability is described as liability without fault. **[4]**

**10.** Mo is injured when a lorry driver for HaulCo Ltd crashes into his car after driving for 14 hours without a break. Mo was not wearing a seat belt. Explain the rules that will decide whom Mo can sue and how much he will recover. **[7]**

**11.** Nell's neighbour runs a noisy car repair business from his garage late at night in a quiet residential street. Explain the rules on fault and remedies that would apply to Nell's claim in private nuisance. **[5]**

**12.** "Tort law should impose liability only where the defendant is at fault." Discuss with reference to negligence, occupiers' liability, nuisance, Rylands v Fletcher and vicarious liability. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: a tort claim is brought by the claimant, a prosecution usually by the state [1]; tort uses the balance of probabilities, crime the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt; tort leads to liability and a remedy such as damages, crime to conviction and sentence [1].

**2.** The claimant bears the burden of proving each element of negligence [1] on the balance of probabilities, that is, that it is more likely than not [1].

**3.** Liability to visitors is governed by the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 [1]; liability to trespassers by the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 [1].

**4.** "The thing speaks for itself": the court may infer negligence without direct proof of what went wrong [1] where the thing that caused the harm was under the defendant's control and the accident would not normally happen without negligence [1] (*Scott v London and St Katherine Docks*, 1865) [1].

**5.** Contributory negligence is a partial defence: the claimant's damages are reduced to reflect his share of responsibility under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 [1]. Consent is a complete defence, so the claimant recovers nothing [1]; it requires that the claimant knew of the risk and freely agreed to it (*Smith v Baker*, 1891; *Morris v Murray*, 1991) [1].

**6.** Compensatory damages aim to put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position he would have been in had the tort not been committed [1]. Mitigation means the claimant must take reasonable steps to reduce his loss [1], and cannot recover for loss he could reasonably have avoided [1].

**7.** Lena must prove duty, breach and damage caused by the breach on the balance of probabilities [1]. A doctor owes a duty to a patient, and a careless failure to diagnose is likely to be a breach [1]. But she must prove factual causation: but for the breach, would the harm have occurred? [1] As the illness could not have been treated anyway, the breach did not cause her harm, so her claim fails, as in *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC* (1969) [1].

**8.** Negligence is fault-based: the defendant is liable only if he fell below the standard of the reasonable person (*Vaughan v Menlove*, 1837) [1], judged by risk factors such as the probability of harm (*Bolton v Stone*, 1951) [1]. *Rylands v Fletcher* is strict liability: the claimant need not prove the defendant was careless [1], only that he brought something likely to do mischief onto his land in a non-natural use and it escaped [1]. Fault is not wholly absent, because the type of damage must be foreseeable (*Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather*, 1994) [1].

**9.** The employer is liable for a tort committed by its employee [1], even though the employer itself did nothing wrong [1]. The claimant must prove the employee committed a tort and that it was in the course of employment, or closely connected with it (*Lister v Hesley Hall*, 2001) [1]. It is justified because the employer profits from the work, controls the risk and is usually insured [1].

**10.** Mo can sue the driver in negligence: road users owe a duty of care to other road users, so *Caparo* is not needed (*Robinson*, 2018) [1]. Driving for 14 hours without a break falls below the standard of the reasonable driver, so there is a breach [1], and the crash caused foreseeable injury [1]. HaulCo is vicariously liable, as the driver is an employee acting in the course of employment [1], so Mo is likely to sue HaulCo, which is insured [1]. His damages will be reduced for contributory negligence because he was not wearing a seat belt (1945 Act) [1], by 25% if a belt would have prevented the injuries or 15% if it would have reduced them (*Froom v Butcher*, 1976) [1].

**11.** Private nuisance is an unlawful, indirect interference with the use or enjoyment of land [1]. Liability depends on whether the neighbour's use of land is unreasonable, not on carelessness [1]. Relevant factors include the quiet residential locality and the time of night [1]. Nell may claim damages for the interference already suffered [1] and seek an injunction, a discretionary remedy, to stop or limit the late-night work [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Fault-based torts:** negligence requires breach of the objective standard of care (*Vaughan v Menlove*, 1837) [1], and occupiers' liability requires reasonable care in all the circumstances under the 1957 and 1984 Acts [1]. This links liability to blame and does not discourage useful activity [1]. **Limits of fault:** nuisance focuses on the reasonableness of the use of land rather than carelessness [1], *Rylands v Fletcher* imposes strict liability [1] though limited by foreseeability of the type of damage (*Cambridge Water*, 1994) [1], and vicarious liability makes an employer liable without personal fault [1]. **For liability without fault:** it ensures victims are compensated by those best able to pay or insure [1], and encourages employers to select and train staff carefully [1]. **For fault:** the objective standard can already be harsh, as for learner drivers (*Nettleship v Weston*, 1971) [1], and liability without fault can seem unjust to the defendant [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that fault is the general rule but limited exceptions are justified by policy [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using criminal vocabulary in a tort answer.
- Forgetting causation when breach is obvious.
- Treating contributory negligence as a complete defence.
- Saying vicarious liability needs fault by the employer.
