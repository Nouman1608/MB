## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between hard and soft adaptation, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Define carbon offsetting. **[1]**

**3.** State two factors that cause people in the same city to differ in their vulnerability to a climate hazard. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by greenwashing. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one ecosystem-based measure that acts as both adaptation and mitigation. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Two power stations each emit 200 tonnes of carbon dioxide. A cap requires total emissions to fall by 60 tonnes. Station X can cut emissions at 15 dollars a tonne and Station Y at 45 dollars a tonne.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost if each station must cut 30 tonnes. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total cost if Station X makes all 60 tonnes of cuts. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why permit trading would encourage the outcome in (b). **[2]**

**7.** Explain why the places most affected by climate change often have the least power over global decisions. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how risk perception can increase vulnerability to a climate hazard. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest two limitations of voluntary corporate targets as a response to climate change. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** With reference to two or more societies you have studied, examine why their vulnerability to climate change differs. **[10]**

**11.** Evaluate the role of non-governmental stakeholders in responding to climate change in one country you have studied. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Hard adaptation uses engineered structures to resist a hazard, such as a sea wall or storm-surge barrier [1]; soft adaptation changes behaviour, land use, knowledge or institutions, such as an early-warning system or zoning that keeps homes off floodplains [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Compensating for emissions by paying for an equivalent reduction or removal of emissions elsewhere, such as forest protection [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Income and ownership of resources such as a car or insurance [1]; location and quality of housing [1]. (Also credit age, disability, or risk perception and access to warnings.) **[2]**

**4.** Greenwashing is when a company or organisation makes environmental claims that exaggerate or misrepresent what it actually does [1], for example advertising a net-zero target with no credible plan or no verification of progress [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, mangrove restoration, which stores carbon and reduces storm-surge damage [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** X: 30 x 15 = 450 dollars; Y: 30 x 45 = 1,350 dollars [1]. Total = **1,800 dollars** [1].

**(b)** 60 x 15 = **900 dollars** [1].

**(c)** Station X can sell its spare permits to Station Y at any price between 15 and 45 dollars a tonne, so X earns more than its cutting costs and Y pays less than its own cutting costs [1]; both gain, so the cuts move to where they are cheapest and the same reduction is achieved at half the total cost [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Many of the most exposed places, such as small island states and low-lying deltas, have small economies and low emissions [1], so they have little economic or political leverage over the large emitters whose actions decide how much warming occurs [1]. Decisions also depend on finance and negotiating capacity, which wealthier countries have more of, although coalitions such as the Alliance of Small Island States increase their collective voice [1]. **[3]**

**8.** People who have not experienced a severe event, or who came through earlier ones without harm, may judge the danger to be lower than it is [1]. They may then ignore warnings, fail to evacuate or not take out insurance [1], so their exposure to harm increases, as with some residents who stayed in New Orleans as Hurricane Katrina approached in 2005 [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Targets may cover only a firm's own operations and not its supply chain, where most emissions occur [1], so the real reduction is small [1]. Targets may not be checked by an independent body [1], so claims can amount to greenwashing [1]. (Also credit, as a developed point, that voluntary targets can be dropped when profits fall because nothing legally enforces them.) **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines vulnerability as susceptibility to harm, distinct from the size of the hazard [1].
- Selects contrasting societies, for example Bangladesh and the Netherlands, or contrasting groups within one city [1].
- Location: both deltas are low-lying, but exposure differs with cyclones, storm surges and subsidence [1].
- Wealth: the Netherlands can fund engineered defences and maintenance; many Bangladeshi households cannot [1].
- Institutions and planning: long-standing Dutch water boards against more limited state capacity, though Bangladesh's volunteer warning system shows effective preparedness [1].
- Social differences: age, gender and education shape who receives and acts on warnings [1].
- Risk perception influences behaviour, as shown by some residents' decisions before Hurricane Katrina [1].
- Livelihoods: dependence on farming exposed to salinisation increases sensitivity [1].
- Compares point by point rather than describing each society separately [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that wealth and institutions explain more of the difference than physical exposure [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Identifies the country and its main non-governmental stakeholders, such as NGOs, businesses, investors and community groups [1].
- Litigation: in the Netherlands, the Urgenda Foundation's case led the Supreme Court in 2019 to uphold an order requiring the state to cut emissions [1].
- Corporate litigation: Milieudefensie's case against Shell won in 2021 but was overturned on appeal in 2024, showing the limits of courts [1].
- Protest and youth movements raise public pressure and shift political debate [1].
- Businesses and investors can move capital towards renewable energy faster than regulation alone [1].
- Local organisations and communities deliver adaptation projects suited to local needs [1].
- Limits: voluntary actions can be small in scale or reversed [1].
- Opposition from other non-governmental groups, such as farmers' protests against environmental rules, shows that stakeholders do not act in one direction [1].
- Non-governmental action often works by forcing government action rather than replacing it [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on how far non-governmental stakeholders changed outcomes in that country [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" asks for a judgement against criteria. State what success would look like, then weigh strengths and limitations with dated evidence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to add both stations' costs in trading calculations, or misreading which station is cheaper.
- Answering a non-governmental question with government policies.
- Describing two societies one after the other with no direct comparison.
- Mentioning risk perception without explaining how it changes behaviour.
