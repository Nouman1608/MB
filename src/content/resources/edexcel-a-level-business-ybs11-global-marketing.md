This guide covers section **4.3.3 Global marketing** in Unit 4 (Global business) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. It asks how a business should market its products across borders: how much to keep the same everywhere, how much to change for each market, and how to avoid cultural mistakes. All businesses, countries and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Global business study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-global-business/). Condensed notes are in the [Global marketing revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-marketing-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Global marketing practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-marketing-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Marketing strategy:** global marketing strategy and global localisation (glocalisation); marketing approaches (domestic or ethnocentric, mixed or geocentric, international or polycentric); applying and adapting the marketing mix (4Ps) to global markets; applying Ansoff's matrix and Porter's matrix to global marketing decisions.
2. **Niche markets:** cultural diversity (groups across the globe have different interests and values); features of global niche markets; applying and adapting the 4Ps to global niches.
3. **Cultural and social factors:** cultural differences; different tastes and preferences; language and unintended meanings; inappropriate branding and promotion.

## Global marketing strategy and glocalisation

A **global marketing strategy** treats the world, or large parts of it, as a single market, with a consistent brand, product and message. The attraction is economies of scale in design, production and advertising, and a recognisable brand wherever customers travel. The risk is that one message may not suit every culture.

**Glocalisation** ("think globally, act locally") keeps a global brand and core product but adapts some elements, such as flavour, language, size, price or advertising, to suit each local market. It balances the cost savings of standardisation against the sales gains of local relevance.

**Worked example 1: cost of standardising or adapting.** A drinks company markets in 10 countries. One standard campaign costs $2m in total. Adapting the campaign for each country costs $0.4m per country: 10 x 0.4m = **$4m**. The extra cost of adapting is $2m. In one country, adaptation is expected to raise sales by 12% on current revenue of $5m, with a contribution margin of 40%.

- Extra revenue = 12% x 5m = **$600,000**.
- Extra contribution = 40% x 600,000 = **$240,000**.
- If the adaptation for this country costs an extra $150,000 compared with the standard version, net gain = 240,000 - 150,000 = **$90,000**.

So it pays to adapt in this country, but this will not be true everywhere. Adaptation is most worthwhile where the market is large and tastes differ greatly from the home market.

## Marketing approaches

| Approach | Meaning | Example | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic or ethnocentric | Home-country methods and product used abroad; the home market is the model | Selling the same product with home advertising everywhere | Low cost; consistent brand | Ignores local needs; may offend or fail |
| Mixed or geocentric | A global approach that treats the world as one market, using the best ideas from any country, with regional variations | A global brand with a common core and some regional changes | Economies of scale with some local fit | Needs strong co-ordination |
| International or polycentric | Each country's market treated separately, with its own marketing mix | A separate product and campaign in each country | Strong local fit | High cost; inconsistent brand |

Most large businesses use a mixture. The best approach depends on how different the markets are, the firm's resources, the nature of the product (technology products are easier to standardise than food) and how important a consistent brand is.

## Adapting the marketing mix (4Ps)

| P | Questions for a global market | Examples of adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Does it meet local needs, standards and tastes? | Smaller packs, different flavours, a local language on the label, voltage and safety rules |
| Price | What can customers afford? What do tariffs, transport and exchange rates add? | Lower-priced versions in low-income markets; premium pricing for status brands |
| Place | How do customers buy? What distribution exists? | Online selling, local distributors, street kiosks or large supermarkets |
| Promotion | Which media and messages work locally? | Local celebrities, local-language slogans, festivals and religious calendars |

**Worked example 2: building the overseas price.** A product leaves the factory at $20. Shipping costs $2, giving a cost, insurance and freight value of $22. The importing country charges a 10% tariff on this value: 0.10 x 22 = **$2.20**, so the landed cost is **$24.20**. The distributor adds a 30% margin: 24.20 x 1.30 = **$31.46**, and the retailer adds 40%: 31.46 x 1.40 = **$44.04**.

A local rival sells a similar product at $38. Customers see the imported product at $44.04, which is $6.04 (16%) dearer. Options: absorb part of the cost, use a shorter distribution chain, assemble locally to avoid the tariff, or stress quality or brand to justify the premium. This shows why price in a global market is not just the home price converted into a different currency.

## Ansoff and Porter in global marketing

**Ansoff's matrix** (Unit 3, section 3.3.1) shows the risk of different global strategies: selling more of the same product in countries where the firm already operates is *market penetration*; taking existing products to new countries is *market development* (the most common global strategy); adapting or creating products for overseas customers is *product development*; and new products for new countries is *diversification* (highest risk).

**Porter's matrix** helps to decide how to compete: a *cost leadership* strategy sells standard, low-priced products to a mass global market; a *differentiation* strategy sells a distinctive brand worldwide, often with premium prices; and *focus* strategies target a particular segment or niche in selected countries. A global differentiator needs a consistent brand image, while a cost leader needs scale and low-cost supply chains.

## Niche markets

A **niche market** is a small, specialised segment of the market with particular needs. **Cultural diversity** means people in different parts of the world have different interests, values and traditions, so there are many niches that are global in reach but small in each country, such as vegan food, adventure sports or religious products.

**Features of global niche markets:** a distinct group with shared interests; often loyal customers who pay premium prices; limited competition; and the internet makes it possible to reach the niche across borders at low cost. They suit small firms that cannot compete on scale.

**Adapting the 4Ps for a niche:** the *product* is specialised and high quality; the *price* is often premium because customers value the fit; *place* tends to be online or through specialist retailers; and *promotion* is targeted through communities, influencers, specialist magazines or events rather than mass media. The risk is dependence on a small group of customers, and larger rivals may enter if the niche proves profitable.

## Cultural and social factors

| Consideration | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural differences | Values, religion, customs and attitudes to family, gender or authority vary | An advertisement showing alcohol may be unacceptable in some markets |
| Tastes and preferences | Food, fashion, colour and design preferences differ | A cereal that is too sweet for local taste may fail |
| Language and unintended meanings | Translations and brand names can have a different or embarrassing meaning | A product name that sounds like an offensive word in another language |
| Inappropriate branding and promotion | Symbols, colours, images or humour may offend | A colour linked with mourning used for a celebration |

Businesses reduce these risks through market research in each country, local staff or partners, testing translations and names with native speakers, and adapting campaigns for the culture. Mistakes can lead to boycotts, withdrawn products and long-term reputation damage, which cost far more than the research would have done.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "global" and "standardised" as the same thing. Glocalisation is global brand plus local adaptation.
- Mixing up ethnocentric, geocentric and polycentric: say which country or world view drives the decisions.
- Converting a price without allowing for transport, tariffs and distributor margins.
- Saying a niche market is "small, so unattractive". Niche markets can be profitable.
- Listing cultural problems without a link to the firm and market in the question.
- Claiming that adaptation is always better: it costs money and can weaken the brand.

## Exam technique

For **explain**, choose one element of the mix and apply it to the country in the source with a specific example. For **analyse**, set out the chain, such as "language error, offence, boycott, loss of sales". For **discuss** and **assess**, compare standardising and adapting on cost, brand consistency and sales, and give a judgement that depends on how different the cultures are and how large the markets are.
