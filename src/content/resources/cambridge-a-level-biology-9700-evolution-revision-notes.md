## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Gene pool | All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time |
| Allele frequency | The proportion of all copies of a gene in a population that are one particular allele |
| Evolution | Formation of new species from pre-existing species over time, as a result of changes to gene pools from generation to generation |
| Species (biological concept) | A group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring |
| Gene flow | Movement of alleles between populations through interbreeding |
| Genetic isolation | A barrier that stops gene flow between two populations |
| Speciation | Formation of a new species |
| Allopatric speciation | Speciation after geographical separation (different areas) |
| Sympatric speciation | Speciation in the same area, after ecological or behavioural separation |
| Common ancestor | The species from which two or more later species descended |
| Molecular clock | The idea that sequence differences build up at a roughly constant rate, so they estimate time since divergence |

## 17.3.1 The theory of evolution

- It is **populations** that evolve, not individuals.
- Mutation adds new alleles; **natural selection, genetic drift and the founder effect** change allele frequencies (17.2).
- Change is measured **between generations**.
- Enough change, plus isolation, gives a **new species** from a **pre-existing** one.

**Outline in four ticks:** pre-existing species → gene pool / allele frequencies change → over many generations → new species that cannot interbreed with the original to give fertile offspring.

## 17.3.2 DNA sequence data

**Logic chain**

1. Two species inherit the same sequence from their common ancestor.
2. After the split, mutations build up separately in each lineage.
3. Fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → more closely related.

**Method in steps for a difference table**

1. Find the pair with the smallest number (or percentage) of differences: join them first.
2. Find the species with the next smallest differences to that pair: add it next.
3. The species with the largest differences to all others branched off earliest.
4. Percentage difference = (number of differing positions ÷ total positions compared) × 100.

*Worked reminder:* 6 differences in a 120-base section = 6 ÷ 120 × 100 = **5.0%**.

**Molecular clock:** if one difference builds up per t years, divergence time ≈ number of differences × t. Only an estimate.

**Limitations for "discuss"**

- Short sections may not represent the genome.
- Mutation rates differ between genes and lineages.
- A position can mutate twice, hiding a change.
- One individual per species ignores variation within a species.
- Degenerate code: protein sequences hide some DNA differences, so DNA gives finer detail.

## 17.3.3 Speciation

**Allopatric (geographical separation)**

1. Geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains, habitat fragmentation).
2. No gene flow.
3. Different mutations in each population.
4. Different conditions → different selection pressures → different alleles selected.
5. Genetic drift, especially in small populations.
6. Many generations → cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring → new species.

**Sympatric (same area)**

- **Ecological**: different host plants, food, microhabitats or breeding seasons (e.g. flowering at different times).
- **Behavioural**: different courtship, songs or mate preferences.
- Then the same chain: reduced gene flow → mutation, selection, drift → reproductive isolation.

| | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Populations live | In different places | In the same place |
| What stops gene flow | Physical barrier | Ecology or behaviour |
| Example to quote | Island populations separated by sea | Flies switching to a new host plant |

## Exam traps

- "The barrier caused mutations." No: mutations are random.
- "The animals adapted to their new environment." Say "alleles that gave an advantage were selected for, so their frequency increased".
- "They can no longer breed." Add "**to produce fertile offspring**".
- Calling breeding-time differences "geographical". Same area = sympatric.
- Using "proves". Sequence data **suggest** relationships.
- Comparing different genes, or sections of different lengths without converting to percentages.

## Quick self-test

1. Define gene pool.
2. Why can an individual not evolve?
3. Name three processes that change allele frequencies in a gene pool.
4. Two species differ at 9 of 150 positions in a gene. Give the percentage difference.
5. Species A differs from B at 3 positions and from C at 14 positions. Which pair has the more recent common ancestor?
6. State one reason why amino acid sequences may show fewer differences than DNA sequences for the same gene.
7. One difference builds up every 2 million years in a gene section. Two species differ at 7 positions. Estimate when they diverged.
8. What is meant by gene flow?
9. Give the type of speciation when two populations of frogs in one pond breed in different months.
10. List the steps of allopatric speciation after the barrier forms.
11. Give two limitations of using one short DNA section to work out relationships.
12. What test shows that two populations have become separate species?

### Answers

1. All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time.
2. Its alleles are fixed at fertilisation; evolution is a change in allele frequencies in a **population** over generations.
3. Any three: natural selection, genetic drift, the founder effect (with mutation supplying new alleles).
4. 9 ÷ 150 × 100 = **6.0%**.
5. **A and B** (fewer differences).
6. The genetic code is **degenerate**: some base substitutions code for the same amino acid.
7. 7 × 2 = about **14 million years ago**.
8. The movement of alleles between populations when their members interbreed.
9. **Sympatric** (ecological separation by breeding season).
10. No gene flow; different mutations; different selection pressures select different alleles; drift; over many generations, gene pools diverge until interbreeding cannot produce fertile offspring.
11. Any two: not representative of the whole genome; mutation rates vary; repeated mutation at one position hides changes; variation within a species ignored.
12. Individuals from the two populations cannot interbreed to produce **fertile** offspring.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answers that never mention **alleles**, **allele frequency** or **gene pool**.
- Explaining speciation without the words "no gene flow".
- Skipping "different selection pressures" and jumping straight to "new species".
- Choosing the wrong pair from a difference table by reading the largest number instead of the smallest.
- Giving a molecular-clock date as exact rather than an estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between allopatric and sympatric speciation?**
In allopatric speciation a geographical barrier separates the populations. In sympatric speciation they live in the same area but are separated by ecology (habitat, food, breeding time) or behaviour (courtship, song).

**Does the 9700 syllabus require polyploidy for speciation?**
No. For 2025 to 2027, section 17.3 limits sympatric speciation to ecological and behavioural separation.

**How do DNA sequences show evolutionary relationships?**
Species with fewer differences in the base sequence of the same gene are likely to share a more recent common ancestor, because mutations have had less time to accumulate since they diverged.
