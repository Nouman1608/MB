This guide covers subtopic **7.1 The characteristic properties of acids and bases** in Topic 7, Acids, bases and salts, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The [acids, bases and salts study guide](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/) summarises the whole topic in one place. This page stays with 7.1 and adds what that overview leaves out: full balanced equations with observations for each reaction type, how to use pH and universal indicator to compare solutions, proton transfer in reactions that are not simple H⁺ + OH⁻ neutralisations, and how strong and weak acids of the same concentration behave differently in experiments. Then work through the [acids and bases revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-the-characteristic-properties-of-acids-and-bases-revision-notes/) and the [acids and bases practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-the-characteristic-properties-of-acids-and-bases-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates):

- Describe acids by their reactions with metals, bases and carbonates, and their effect on litmus, thymolphthalein and methyl orange.
- State that bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals and that alkalis are soluble bases.
- Describe bases by their reactions with acids and with ammonium salts, and alkalis by their effect on the three indicators.
- State that aqueous acids contain H⁺ ions and aqueous alkalis contain OH⁻ ions.
- Compare hydrogen ion concentration, neutrality, relative acidity and relative alkalinity using the colour and pH of universal indicator paper.
- Describe neutralisation as H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- Define acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors.
- Define strong acids as completely dissociated and weak acids as partially dissociated in aqueous solution, using HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) and CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq).

## Reactions of acids: equations with observations

"Describe" questions reward what you would *see* as well as the products. Learn each reaction as a set: equation, products, observations.

**Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen**

```
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)
```

Observations: bubbles (effervescence); the metal gets smaller and disappears if the acid is in excess; the mixture warms. The gas gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint. Very unreactive metals such as copper do not react with dilute acids.

**Acid + base → salt + water**

```
CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l)
NaOH(aq) + HNO3(aq) → NaNO3(aq) + H2O(l)
```

With copper(II) oxide: the black solid dissolves (warming speeds this up) and the solution turns blue. No gas is produced. With an alkali there is usually no visible change, which is why an indicator is needed to see the end point.

**Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide**

```
Na2CO3(s) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
CuCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
```

Observations: effervescence; the solid dissolves. Copper(II) carbonate is green and gives a blue-green solution. The gas turns limewater milky.

**Naming the salt.** The metal (or ammonium) gives the first word; the acid gives the second: hydrochloric → chloride, sulfuric → sulfate, nitric → nitrate.

## Bases and alkalis

A **base** is an oxide or hydroxide of a metal, such as copper(II) oxide, magnesium oxide or iron(III) hydroxide. An **alkali** is a base that dissolves in water, such as sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide. Calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble, but its solution (limewater) is alkaline.

**Base + ammonium salt → salt + water + ammonia** (on warming)

```
NaOH(aq) + NH4Cl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + NH3(g)
Ca(OH)2 + 2NH4Cl → CaCl2 + 2H2O + 2NH3
```

Ammonia has a sharp smell and turns damp red litmus paper blue. This reaction is the basis of the test for ammonium ions in Topic 12.

## Indicators and the pH scale

| Indicator | Acid | Alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Litmus | Red | Blue |
| Thymolphthalein | Colourless | Blue |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |

Litmus, thymolphthalein and methyl orange only tell you *whether* a solution is acidic or alkaline. **Universal indicator** has a range of colours, so it shows *how* acidic or alkaline a solution is, as a pH value.

| Universal indicator colour | Approximate pH | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 0–2 | Strongly acidic: highest H⁺ concentration |
| Orange / yellow | 3–6 | Weakly acidic |
| Green | 7 | Neutral |
| Blue | 8–10 | Weakly alkaline |
| Purple | 11–14 | Strongly alkaline: highest OH⁻ concentration |

The rule to apply: **the lower the pH, the higher the hydrogen ion concentration and the more acidic the solution.** Above 7, the higher the pH, the more alkaline.

### Worked example: comparing solutions

Universal indicator paper gives these colours: solution P, purple; Q, green; R, orange; S, red.

- Most acidic: **S** (red, lowest pH, highest H⁺ concentration).
- Neutral: **Q** (green, pH 7).
- Arrange in order of increasing pH: **S, R, Q, P**.
- Solution that could be aqueous sodium hydroxide: **P**.

## Neutralisation

Whatever the acid and alkali, the reaction that happens is the same:

```
H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l)
```

The other ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in NaOH + HCl, for example) are spectator ions; they stay in solution and form the salt when the water is evaporated. As alkali is added to acid, the pH rises towards 7; adding more alkali after neutralisation takes the pH above 7.

## Proton donors and acceptors (Supplement)

H⁺ is a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, which leaves just a proton. So:

- An **acid** is a **proton donor**: it gives H⁺ away.
- A **base** is a **proton acceptor**: it takes H⁺.

This definition covers reactions the H⁺/OH⁻ picture does not:

```
HCl(g) + NH3(g) → NH4Cl(s)
```

HCl donates a proton to NH₃, so HCl is the acid and NH₃ is the base, even though no water or OH⁻ is involved. Similarly, in CuO + 2H⁺ → Cu²⁺ + H₂O, the oxide ion accepts protons.

## Strong and weak acids (Supplement)

```
HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)                    strong: completely dissociated
CH3COOH(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)            weak: partially dissociated
```

The single arrow and the reversible arrow carry the meaning. For a weak acid, most molecules stay undissociated at any moment.

### Comparing acids of the same concentration

Take hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid, both at the same concentration.

| Test | Hydrochloric acid | Ethanoic acid | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| pH | Lower | Higher (still below 7) | HCl gives a higher H⁺ concentration |
| Reaction with magnesium ribbon | Faster, vigorous effervescence | Slower effervescence | More frequent collisions with H⁺ ions |
| Total volume of hydrogen from excess magnesium (same volume of acid) | Same | Same | The same amount of acid; as H⁺ is used up, more ethanoic acid dissociates |

The last row catches many candidates. A weak acid reacts *more slowly*, not *less completely*.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving out carbon dioxide** from acid + carbonate.
- **Writing that methyl orange is orange in alkali.** It is yellow.
- **Calling copper(II) oxide an alkali.** It is a base but insoluble.
- **Saying a higher pH means more H⁺ ions.** It is the reverse.
- **Treating "weak" as "dilute".** Weak is about dissociation; dilute is about concentration.
- **Forgetting that ammonia is released only when a base reacts with an ammonium salt**, usually on warming; acids do not release ammonia.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** a reaction: name the products, and give observations if asked "what would you see".
- **State**: give the fact only, such as "acids contain H⁺ ions".
- **Define** (Extended): "proton donor" and "proton acceptor"; for strong and weak, include "in aqueous solution" and "completely" or "partially dissociated".
- **Compare**: make a point about each solution, using comparative words (lower, faster, higher).
- Include state symbols in the neutralisation ionic equation; they are part of the syllabus statement.
