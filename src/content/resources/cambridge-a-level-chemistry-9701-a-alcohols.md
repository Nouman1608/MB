This guide covers subtopic **32.1, Alcohols**, from Topic 32, Hydroxy compounds, of Cambridge International AS &
A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The AS chemistry of alcohols (combustion,
substitution, oxidation, dehydration and esterification with carboxylic acids) is in
[Alcohols: Reactions and Oxidation](/resources/as-alcohols-reactions-and-oxidation/). At A Level the syllabus
adds **one** reaction, and the broader page [Phenol: Reactions and Acidity](/resources/a-phenol-reactions-and-acidity/)
gives it a single equation. This page treats that reaction in full: conditions, mechanism, naming, comparison
with the AS method, and the calculations and structure questions built on it. Afterwards, use the
[alcohols revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-alcohols-revision-notes/) and the
[alcohols practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-alcohols-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

One learning outcome: **describe the reaction with acyl chlorides to form esters using ethyl ethanoate.**

"Describe" means you should be able to give the reagents, conditions, equation, products and observations, and
the syllabus's mechanism requirement for acyl chlorides (33.3) means you should also be able to outline how the
reaction happens.

## The reaction

    CH₃COCl + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + HCl
    ethanoyl chloride + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + hydrogen chloride

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Conditions | room temperature; no catalyst; no heating |
| Rate | fast, and exothermic |
| Extent | goes to completion (not an equilibrium) |
| By-product | HCl, seen as **steamy (white) fumes** in moist air |
| Precaution | carry out in a fume cupboard; keep reagents dry |

**Why dry?** Acyl chlorides react with water at room temperature to give the carboxylic acid and HCl. Any water
present competes with the alcohol and lowers the yield of ester.

**Which alcohols?** The reaction is general for alcohols: any R–OH can act as the nucleophile. Ethyl ethanoate is
the syllabus example, and the same reaction with phenol gives phenyl esters.

## Mechanism: addition–elimination

The carbonyl carbon of ethanoyl chloride is bonded to two electronegative atoms, O and Cl, so it carries a large
δ+ charge. The oxygen atom of ethanol has two lone pairs and acts as the **nucleophile**.

**Step 1: addition.** A curly arrow from a lone pair on the ethanol oxygen to the carbonyl carbon. At the same
time a curly arrow from the C=O π bond to the carbonyl oxygen. The intermediate carries O⁻ on the former
carbonyl carbon and a positive charge on the ethanol oxygen, which now has three bonds.

**Step 2: elimination.** A curly arrow from the O⁻ lone pair re-forms the C=O bond; a curly arrow from the C–Cl
bond to Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻. Finally the positively charged oxygen loses H⁺, which combines with Cl⁻.

Overall, the –Cl of the acyl chloride is replaced by –OCH₂CH₃ from the alcohol, and the H of the alcohol's –OH
leaves with the Cl as HCl. See the [characteristic reactions study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-characteristic-organic-reactions/)
for how to name and classify this mechanism.

## Comparing the two routes to an ester

| | Alcohol + carboxylic acid (AS) | Alcohol + acyl chloride (A Level) |
|---|---|---|
| Equation (ethyl ethanoate) | CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O | CH₃COCl + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + HCl |
| Conditions | heat under reflux with concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst | room temperature, no catalyst |
| Rate | slow | fast |
| Reversible? | yes: an equilibrium | no |
| Yield | limited by the position of equilibrium | high |
| By-product | water | hydrogen chloride |
| Main drawback | slow, incomplete | acyl chloride is expensive, reacts with moisture, and HCl fumes are corrosive |

## Naming the ester you make

The ester name has two parts:

1. the **alkyl group from the alcohol**, named first (ethanol gives *ethyl*);
2. the **acid part from the acyl chloride**, ending "-oate" (ethanoyl chloride gives *ethanoate*).

| Alcohol | Acyl chloride | Ester |
|---|---|---|
| ethanol | ethanoyl chloride | ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ |
| methanol | propanoyl chloride | methyl propanoate, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ |
| propan-1-ol | ethanoyl chloride | propyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| butan-1-ol | butanoyl chloride | butyl butanoate, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ |

**Working backwards:** split the ester at the single-bonded O. The side with C=O came from the acyl chloride; the
side on the single-bonded O came from the alcohol. Pentyl ethanoate, CH₃COOC₅H₁₁, is made from ethanoyl chloride
and pentan-1-ol.

## Worked example 1: a quantity calculation

*2.30 g of ethanol reacts with excess ethanoyl chloride. Calculate (a) the maximum mass of ethyl ethanoate and
(b) the volume of HCl formed at room temperature and pressure. Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0; molar gas volume
at r.t.p. = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.*

    Mr(C₂H₅OH) = 46.0        n(ethanol) = 2.30 / 46.0 = 0.0500 mol
    1 : 1 : 1 ratio          n(ester) = n(HCl) = 0.0500 mol
    Mr(CH₃COOC₂H₅) = 88.0    (a) mass = 0.0500 × 88.0 = 4.40 g
                              (b) volume = 0.0500 × 24.0 = 1.20 dm³

If a student obtains 3.52 g of ester, the percentage yield is 3.52 / 4.40 × 100 = **80.0%**. With the
carboxylic-acid method, the equilibrium would make a yield this high harder to reach without removing a
product.

## Worked example 2: an alcohol with two –OH groups

*Predict the organic product when ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂CH₂OH, reacts with excess ethanoyl chloride.*

Each –OH group reacts independently, so both are converted into ester groups:

    HOCH₂CH₂OH + 2CH₃COCl → CH₃COOCH₂CH₂OOCCH₃ + 2HCl

**Key point:** count the –OH groups to find the moles of acyl chloride used and HCl formed. The same idea, with
a molecule that has an acyl chloride group at **each** end (a dioyl chloride), is how polyesters can be made
from diols in Topic 35.

## Recognising the reaction in a structure question

You may be told only that "compound F, C₃H₈O, reacts with ethanoyl chloride at room temperature, giving steamy
fumes and a sweet-smelling liquid G". Reason it out:

- steamy fumes = HCl, so F has an –OH group that reacted;
- sweet-smelling liquid = an ester;
- C₃H₈O with an –OH: propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol;
- G is propyl ethanoate (from propan-1-ol) or the ester of propan-2-ol, CH₃COOCH(CH₃)₂.

Further information, such as the result of oxidation from AS Level, would decide between them.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding a catalyst or heating**: neither is needed with an acyl chloride.
- **Water as the by-product**: with an acyl chloride it is HCl.
- **Reversible arrows** in the acyl chloride equation: the reaction goes to completion.
- **Ester names the wrong way round**: "ethanoate ethyl" or "ethyl ethanoyl".
- **Mechanism arrows from the H** of the alcohol instead of a lone pair on O.
- **Forgetting to balance** for diols: two –OH groups need two acyl chloride molecules.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** the reaction: reagent, room temperature, no catalyst, equation, product named, HCl fumes.
- **Compare** the two methods: give at least two contrasting points (conditions, reversibility, by-product,
  yield) in matched pairs.
- **Suggest** why the acyl chloride method is chosen: faster, no catalyst, not reversible, higher yield.
- **Calculate**: state the mole ratio before using it.
