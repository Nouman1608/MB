This guide goes deeper into section 3.2.2 Ethical and environmental considerations of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. It sits in Topic 2, Influences on business, which can be assessed on either written paper. The combined page, [Ethical and environmental considerations and the economic climate](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-and-the-economic-climate-on-businesses/), introduces the content. Here the focus is on applying it: deciding whether a choice is ethical, judging who bears the costs, and writing trade-off answers that reach a judgement.

## What the specification asks for

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Ethical considerations | "identify and analyse where there may be a possible trade off between ethics and profit"; know "relevant examples of ways in which a business can behave ethically and the benefits and drawbacks of ethical behaviour" |
| Environmental considerations: impact on traffic congestion, recycling, disposing of waste, noise and air pollution | Show "how business and consumers accept greater environmental responsibility in their decision making and the costs and benefits of businesses behaving this way" |
| Sustainability: global warming, using scarce resources | "identify and analyse where there may be a possible trade-off between sustainability and profit" |

The specification defines the key idea: "Ethical behaviour requires businesses to act in ways that stakeholders consider to be both fair and honest."

Like every part of Topic 2, these influences should be linked to the four functional areas: operations, human resources, marketing and finance.

## Ethics: fair and honest in the eyes of stakeholders

Ethics is about what is right, not only what is legal. A business can obey every law and still act in a way its stakeholders think unfair. Because the specification's test is what stakeholders consider fair and honest, start every ethics answer by naming the stakeholder affected.

| Stakeholder | Ethical behaviour towards them | Unethical behaviour towards them |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | Fair pay, safe conditions, respect, honest communication about changes | Pressure to work unpaid overtime, unsafe shortcuts |
| Customers | Honest labelling and advertising, fair prices, prompt refunds | Exaggerated claims, hidden charges |
| Suppliers | Paying a fair price, paying invoices on time | Delaying payment to boost the business's own cash |
| Workers in the supply chain | Checking overseas factories treat staff fairly; buying certified fair trade goods | Ignoring poor conditions to keep costs low |
| Local community | Being a considerate neighbour, supporting local causes | Ignoring complaints about noise or litter |

### Benefits of ethical behaviour

- A stronger reputation, which can attract customers who care about how products are made.
- Customer loyalty, giving more stable sales.
- Easier recruitment and lower staff turnover, because people prefer to work for a business they respect.
- Good supplier relationships, which can bring priority treatment when supplies are short.
- A point of difference in marketing.

### Drawbacks of ethical behaviour

- Higher costs: fair prices to suppliers, better pay, checks on overseas factories.
- Higher prices for customers, which may reduce sales if they choose mainly on price.
- Lower profit, at least in the short run, which may disappoint owners.
- Risk of criticism if the business claims to be ethical and is found not to be.

### The trade-off with profit

A trade-off exists when acting ethically raises costs or reduces sales enough to lower profit. To analyse it:

1. Identify the ethical choice and who benefits.
2. State the cost or lost revenue it causes.
3. State the gain it might bring, such as loyalty or a premium price.
4. Judge which is larger for this business, using the case study's evidence about its customers and finances.

Sometimes there is no real trade-off. Paying suppliers on time costs little but builds goodwill. Treating staff well can reduce the cost of replacing those who leave. Point this out when the evidence supports it.

## Environmental considerations

The specification names four impacts. For each, think about what a business causes, what it could do instead, and who pays.

- **Traffic congestion:** delivery lorries, customer car journeys and staff commuting all add to local traffic. A business can schedule deliveries outside rush hours, combine deliveries into fewer journeys, or encourage staff to car-share or cycle.
- **Recycling:** reusing materials reduces the need for new ones. A business can recycle its own waste, use recycled materials in its products, or offer take-back schemes for old products.
- **Disposing of waste:** businesses must get rid of packaging, offcuts, food waste and sometimes hazardous materials. Careful disposal costs money; cutting waste at source can save it.
- **Noise and air pollution:** machinery, vehicles and late-night activity affect neighbours. Quieter equipment, restricted operating hours and cleaner vehicles reduce the impact but cost money.

### Business and consumers accepting responsibility

The specification asks how both businesses and consumers accept greater environmental responsibility. Consumers do this by choosing products with less packaging, buying second-hand, recycling at home and paying more for greener options. Businesses respond to that demand, but many also act because it lowers costs, protects their reputation or meets the expectations of employees and the local community. The two drive each other: when consumers reward greener choices, businesses that make them gain sales.

### Costs and benefits of acting responsibly

| Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|
| New equipment, vehicles or packaging | Lower energy, fuel and disposal bills over time |
| Higher prices for greener inputs | Better image, which can attract customers and staff |
| Staff time and training | Better relations with the local community |
| Possible loss of convenience, such as fewer delivery slots | A marketing message that can differentiate the business |

## Sustainability

Sustainability means operating in a way that does not damage the ability of future generations to meet their needs. The specification names two areas.

- **Global warming:** burning fossil fuels for energy and transport releases gases linked to rising temperatures. A business can cut energy use, switch to renewable energy, or reduce transport miles.
- **Using scarce resources:** some materials are limited or slow to replace. A business can use less, switch to renewable or recycled materials, or design products to last longer.

The trade-off with profit usually involves timing. Sustainable choices often cost more now and pay back later, or protect the business against rising prices for scarce materials.

## Worked example: a sustainability trade-off

Figures invented. Brackwater Coffee roasts beans and delivers to 60 cafés using two diesel vans. It is considering replacing them with electric vans.

| | Diesel (current) | Electric |
|---|---|---|
| Extra cost to switch | — | £18,000 after selling the old vans |
| Fuel or charging per year | £9,600 | £3,600 |
| Servicing per year | £1,800 | £1,200 |

Yearly saving = (£9,600 + £1,800) − (£3,600 + £1,200) = £11,400 − £4,800 = £6,600.

Time to recover the extra cost = £18,000 ÷ £6,600 ≈ 2.7 years.

In the first two years profit is lower once the cost of the switch is counted, so there is a trade-off in the short term. After about 2.7 years the switch has paid for itself, and the business also cuts air pollution on its delivery routes and can promote this to cafés. A judgement depends on whether Brackwater has the cash now, how long the owners can wait for a return, and whether its café customers value lower-emission deliveries.

## Annotated model paragraph

Question: *Analyse whether a clothing retailer faces a trade-off between ethics and profit if it only buys from factories that pay workers fairly.*

"Buying only from audited factories means the retailer pays more per garment (cost), so if it keeps prices the same its profit margin falls (effect on profit). However, customers who care about how clothes are made may switch to it from rivals (benefit), and the risk of a damaging news story about poor factory conditions is reduced (further benefit). The trade-off is greatest if its customers are mainly price-driven, because higher prices would then lose sales (judgement linked to the case)."

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name an ethical action, an environmental impact or a sustainability issue.
- **Explain:** link the action to a cost or benefit for the business.
- **Analyse:** build the trade-off chain: action → cost → benefit → net effect on profit.
- **Evaluate:** weigh short-term cost against long-term benefit, refer to the business's customers and finances, and decide.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing ethical with legal. Obeying the law is the minimum; ethics goes further.
- Assuming ethical always means lower profit. Sometimes it raises it.
- Ignoring the consumer side of environmental responsibility.
- Writing about environmental issues without naming one of the specification's impacts.
- Forgetting timing: a cost now can be a saving later.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does ethical behaviour mean in AQA GCSE Business?**
The specification says it requires businesses to act in ways that stakeholders consider to be both fair and honest.

**Is there always a trade-off between sustainability and profit?**
No. Some choices cost more now but save money later or attract customers, so the trade-off may only be short term.

**Which environmental impacts does the specification name?**
Traffic congestion, recycling, disposing of waste, and noise and air pollution, with sustainability covering global warming and using scarce resources.
