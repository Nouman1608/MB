This study guide teaches Criterion C: Processing and evaluating in IB MYP Sciences. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Sciences guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion C starts when the data has been collected. You turn raw numbers into a table and graph, explain what they show, and then judge how far you can trust your own hypothesis and method. The worked example continues the investigation planned in the [Criterion B study guide](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-b-inquiring-and-designing/): the effect of water temperature on the time an effervescent tablet takes to dissolve.

Use it with the [Criterion C revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-c-processing-and-evaluating-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion C practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-criterion-c-processing-and-evaluating-practice/). ## What the guide says about Criterion C

The objective is "Processing and evaluating". Students collect, process and interpret qualitative or quantitative data and explain conclusions that have been appropriately reached. MYP Sciences helps students to develop analytical thinking skills that they use to evaluate the method and discuss possible improvements or extensions.

At the end of Year 5 the five strands are:

1. **Present** collected and transformed data.
2. **Interpret** data and **explain** results using scientific reasoning.
3. **Evaluate** the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of the investigation.
4. **Evaluate** the validity of the method.
5. **Explain** improvements or extensions to the method.

Criterion C has a maximum of 8. The top band (7-8) asks you to correctly collect, organise, transform and present data in numerical or visual forms, accurately interpret data and explain results using correct scientific reasoning, and evaluate and explain as above.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Strand | 1-2 | 3-4 | 5-6 | 7-8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| i Data | collect and present | correctly collect and present | correctly collect, organise and present | correctly collect, organise, transform and present |
| ii Interpret | interpret data | accurately interpret data and explain results | explain results using scientific reasoning | explain results using correct scientific reasoning |
| iii Hypothesis | state validity | outline validity | discuss validity | evaluate validity |
| iv Method | state validity | outline validity | discuss validity | evaluate validity |
| v Improvements | state | outline | describe | explain |

Reading across, the verbs climb from state, to outline, to discuss, to evaluate. For the top band you must weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgment, not just list them.

## Key words from the guide's glossary

- **Numerical forms**: may include calculations such as averaging or finding values from a graph or table.
- **Visual forms**: may include graphs of various types suitable to the data (line graphs, bar graphs, histograms, pie charts).
- **Transforming data**: processing raw data into a form suitable for visual representation, for example adding, subtracting, squaring or dividing, finding a value of a physical quantity, or averaging several measurements.
- **Validity of the method**: whether the method allows the collection of sufficient valid data to answer the question. It covers whether the instrument measures what it should, the conditions of the experiment and the manipulation of variables (fair testing).
- **Extensions to the method**: developments for further inquiry that follow from the outcome of the investigation.

## Strand i: present collected and transformed data

### Step 1: the raw results table

Give the table a title, headings with units, and equal precision down each column.

| Water temperature (°C) | Trial 1 (s) | Trial 2 (s) | Trial 3 (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 142 | 150 | 146 |
| 20 | 95 | 91 | 99 |
| 30 | 61 | 64 | 58 |
| 40 | 41 | 40 | 66 |
| 50 | 27 | 25 | 29 |

### Step 2: spot the anomaly

At 40 °C, trials 1 and 2 are 41 s and 40 s but trial 3 is 66 s. At the other temperatures, the trials differ by at most 8 s. The 66 s result is an **anomaly**. Do not delete it silently. Mark it in the table, say you have excluded it from the mean, and give a possible cause, such as the tablet sticking to the side of the beaker or a late start of the stopwatch.

### Step 3: transform the data

The mean time for each temperature is the sum of the usable trials divided by their number.

- 10 °C: (142 + 150 + 146) ÷ 3 = 146 s
- 20 °C: (95 + 91 + 99) ÷ 3 = 95 s
- 30 °C: (61 + 64 + 58) ÷ 3 = 61 s
- 40 °C: (41 + 40) ÷ 2 = 40.5 s, with 66 s excluded as an anomaly
- 50 °C: (27 + 25 + 29) ÷ 3 = 27 s

A short time means a fast change. To show the **rate**, calculate 1 ÷ mean time, which rises when the tablet dissolves faster. The answers are small (for example 1 ÷ 146 = 0.00685 s⁻¹), so multiply by 1000 and give the unit as 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

| Temperature (°C) | Mean time (s) | Rate (10⁻³ s⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 146 | 6.85 |
| 20 | 95 | 10.5 |
| 30 | 61 | 16.4 |
| 40 | 40.5 | 24.7 |
| 50 | 27 | 37.0 |

Each rate is 1000 ÷ the mean time, for example 1000 ÷ 40.5 = 24.7 to three significant figures.

### Step 4: draw the graph

A good graph has these features.

- The independent variable (temperature) is on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis.
- Each axis has a label with a unit and an even scale that uses more than half the grid.
- Points are plotted accurately as small crosses or dots.
- A best-fit line or smooth curve is drawn through the points. It does not join the dots with straight segments unless the data call for it.
- There is a title.

For this data, a line graph of mean time against temperature gives a curve that falls steeply and then flattens. A graph of rate against temperature gives a curve that rises more steeply as the temperature goes up.

## Strand ii: interpret data and explain results

Interpreting has three parts: **describe the pattern, use numbers, explain with science**.

**Worked example.**

- Pattern: as the temperature rose from 10 °C to 50 °C, the mean time to dissolve fell from 146 s to 27 s, so the tablet dissolved faster at higher temperatures.
- Numbers: between 10 °C and 30 °C the mean time fell from 146 s to 61 s, a drop of 85 s. Between 30 °C and 50 °C it fell from 61 s to 27 s, a drop of 34 s. The rate rose from 6.85 to 37.0 (10⁻³ s⁻¹), which is about 5.4 times larger.
- Explanation: at higher temperatures the water particles have more kinetic energy. They move faster, hit the tablet more often and with more energy, and so the reaction and dissolving happen more quickly. This agrees with the collision idea used in the hypothesis.

The 5.4 comes from 37.0 ÷ 6.85 = 5.40. Always check your arithmetic.

## Strand iii: evaluate the hypothesis

The hypothesis was: if the water temperature is raised from 10 °C to 50 °C, then the time for the tablet to dissolve will decrease, because the water particles have more kinetic energy.

An evaluation has three moves.

1. **Verdict**: the data support the hypothesis.
2. **Evidence**: the time fell at every step of 10 °C, and the rate increased by about 5.4 times over the range.
3. **Limits**: the data support the trend between 10 and 50 °C only. The investigation does not show what happens above 50 °C or whether the relationship is exactly proportional. A single anomaly at 40 °C suggests the result there is less certain than the others.

Avoid the word "proves". Evidence supports or does not support a hypothesis.

## Strand iv: evaluate the method

Evaluating the method is about **validity**: did it collect enough good data to answer the question?

| Aspect | Strength | Limitation | Effect on the result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeats | three trials at each temperature allowed means and spotted an anomaly | the anomaly shows that human timing and judging the end point vary | the 40 °C mean is based on two results |
| Controls | the same volume of water, same brand of tablet and beaker, no stirring | tablets may differ slightly in mass | some of the spread is from the tablets, not the temperature |
| Temperature | the water was set to the target before starting | water at 10 °C warms and at 50 °C cools during the run | the true temperature was not constant, so the points are not exact |
| Timing | stopwatch to the nearest second | reaction time of about 0.2 to 0.5 s and judging when the solid has gone | at 27 s this is a larger percentage error than at 146 s |
| Range | five values gave a clear trend | only five values, and no values between 30 and 40 | the shape of the curve is less certain |

Link every limitation to an **effect** on the data. "There were errors" earns very little.

## Strand v: improvements and extensions

For each improvement, say **what**, **how** and **why it helps**.

- Measure the temperature at the start and the end and use the mean, or use an insulated container, because the water temperature drifted.
- Use a light sensor or a conductivity sensor with a data logger to detect the end point, because judging by eye is subjective.
- Weigh each tablet and choose ones of the same mass, because mass affects how long it takes to dissolve.
- Repeat the 40 °C trial a further three times, because one anomaly left only two usable results.
- Add temperatures at 5 °C intervals, because that gives a better curve.

An **extension** is a new question that follows from the result. For example: does crushing the tablet change the time at each temperature? Or does a different tablet brand show the same pattern?

## How Criterion C is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for sciences, the Investigation skills task has 50 marks, 25 for Criterion B and 25 for Criterion C. You should expect to collect data from simulations, present data appropriately, and interpret and evaluate data and hypotheses. The response tools include a graph plotter, a table drawing tool and an on-screen calculator. The guide's conventions ask for final answers to three significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Common errors

- Missing units in the table headings, or mixed numbers of decimal places in one column.
- Joining plotted points with a ruler when a smooth curve is expected.
- Axes the wrong way round, or a scale that squashes the data into a corner.
- Deleting an anomaly without saying so.
- Describing the graph but not explaining it with science.
- Saying the data "prove" the hypothesis.
- A list of vague "errors" with no effect on the result.
- Improvements such as "be more careful" or "use better equipment" with no detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion C in IB MYP Sciences?**
Criterion C is Processing and evaluating. It assesses how you present and transform data, interpret results with scientific reasoning, evaluate the hypothesis and the method, and explain improvements or extensions.

**What does "transform data" mean?**
It means processing raw data into a form that is suitable for visual representation, for example by averaging or by calculating a quantity such as a rate.

**What is the difference between validity of the hypothesis and validity of the method?**
Validity of the hypothesis is how well the results support the prediction and reasoning. Validity of the method is whether the method collected enough good data to answer the question.

**How many marks is Criterion C worth in the on-screen examination?**
The Investigation skills task is worth 50 marks, of which 25 are assessed against Criterion C.
