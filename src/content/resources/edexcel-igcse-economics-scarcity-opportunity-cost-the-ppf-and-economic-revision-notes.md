## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Wants are unlimited but resources are finite, so choices must be made |
| Opportunity cost | The value of the best alternative sacrificed by making a choice |
| Production possibility frontier (PPF) | A curve showing the most of one good an economy can make for each level of the other good, with all resources in use |
| Economic growth | An increase in the economy's productive potential, shown as an outward shift of the PPF |
| Maximise benefit | A consumer aims for the greatest satisfaction from spending |
| Maximise profit | A business aims for the greatest gap between revenue and cost |

## 1.1.1 a-b: Scarcity and opportunity cost

- Scarcity comes from unlimited wants and finite resources. It affects rich and poor countries alike.
- Scarcity forces a choice, and every choice has an opportunity cost.
- Opportunity cost is one item: the best alternative forgone.

**Method for opportunity cost.**

1. List the options.
2. Rank them.
3. Find the choice made.
4. Name the next best option as the cost, with its value if given.

| Agent | Typical choice | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Spend limited income on one item | The next best item that income could have bought |
| Producer | Invest limited funds in one project | The return from the next best project |
| Government | Spend a limited budget in one area | The next best spending area or a tax cut |

**Worked reminder (consumer).** Lena has 60 units. Her ranking is headphones (60), a deposit on a weekend trip (60), or two pairs of jeans (30 each). She buys the headphones. The opportunity cost is the trip deposit, not the jeans as well.

**Worked reminder (producer).** A farm has 12,000 units. A tractor adds 1,800 a year and irrigation adds 1,200 a year. Return on the tractor is 1,800 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 15%, on irrigation 1,200 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 10%. Choosing the tractor gives up 1,200 a year.

**Worked reminder (government).** A budget of 150 million can fund five clinics at 30 million each (5 × 30 = 150) or 25 km of rail at 6 million per km (25 × 6 = 150). Choose rail and five clinics are forgone.

## 1.1.1 c-d: The PPF

Country C makes laptops and wheat (thousands).

| Laptops | Wheat | Opportunity cost of extra laptops |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 60 | -- |
| 5 | 56 | 4 wheat for 5 laptops = 0.8 each |
| 10 | 48 | 8 for 5 = 1.6 each |
| 15 | 30 | 18 for 5 = 3.6 each |
| 20 | 0 | 30 for 5 = 6.0 each |

Opportunity cost per laptop rises, so the curve bows outwards. A straight line means a constant opportunity cost.

**Sketch.** Wheat on the vertical axis, laptops on the horizontal axis, a smooth curve from (0, 60) to (20, 0), both axes labelled, the curve labelled PPF.

**What each position means**

| Position | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| On the curve | Maximum productive potential, resources fully employed | (10, 48) |
| Inside the curve | Possible, but resources unemployed or underused | (10, 40) |
| Outside the curve | Unobtainable with present resources | (15, 45) |

For (10, 40): the curve gives 48 wheat at 10 laptops, so 48 − 40 = 8 wheat of potential is unused. That is 8 ÷ 48 × 100 = 16.7% below potential.

**Shifts**

- Outward shift = positive growth. A 10% rise in capacity moves the intercepts from 60 wheat to 66 and from 20 laptops to 22.
- Inward shift = negative growth. Capacity falls.
- Moving along the curve is a change of mix. Moving from inside to the curve is using idle resources, not growth.
- One-sided change: better wheat technology raises the wheat intercept only, and the laptop intercept stays at 20.

## 1.1.1 e: Causes of growth and decline

| Positive (outward) | Negative (inward) |
|---|---|
| More labour, land or capital | Fewer workers, depleted resources |
| Better education and training | Loss of skills |
| Investment in machinery and infrastructure | War, flood or earthquake damaging capital |
| Better technology | Lost access to technology or trade |

**Chain of reasoning.** Cause, then what changes in resources (quantity or quality), then output of both goods rises or falls, then the PPF shifts.

## 1.1.2: Assumptions

- Consumers aim to maximise their benefit.
- Businesses aim to maximise their profit.

**Consumers may not maximise benefit because of:**

- poor calculation of benefits and costs, such as ignoring unit prices
- habits that are hard to give up, such as a daily purchase
- copying others, such as buying what friends or influencers buy

**Producers may not maximise profit because of:**

- managers who maximise revenue or sales
- caring for customers, for example by keeping prices fair or giving free support
- charitable work, for example donating goods

**Worked reminder (profit versus revenue).** A market stall sells T-shirts.

| Output | Revenue | Cost | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 80 | 60 | 20 |
| 4 | 140 | 90 | 50 |
| 6 | 180 | 120 | 60 |
| 8 | 200 | 190 | 10 |

Profit is highest at 6 shirts (60). Revenue is highest at 8 (200) but profit is only 10. A revenue or sales-maximising manager picks 8 and gives up 50 of profit.

## Evaluation prompts

The specification lists Evaluate as a command word that asks for a supported conclusion. For this unit, practise short lines like these.

- **Is the profit assumption reasonable?** Yes for a firm facing tough rivals, since survival needs profit. Less so for a manager paid on sales, or a firm with a social aim. The judgement depends on who owns and runs the business.
- **Are consumers rational?** Often close to it for big, rare purchases where people compare prices. Less so for small, repeated or fashion-led purchases. The assumption is a simplification that still predicts general patterns.
- **Which cause of growth matters most?** Investment and technology tend to raise output for many years, while a discovery of resources may be one-off. Whether growth lasts depends on whether the extra output is used and kept.
- **Does growth remove scarcity?** No. The curve moves out, but wants rise too, so choices and opportunity costs remain.

**Command words to recognise.** The specification says What is meant by needs two separate parts to the definition, Calculate expects workings, and Draw expects a diagram with separate awardable points. Always label axes and curves.

## Must-know distinctions

- Scarcity versus shortage: scarcity is permanent, a shortage is at one price.
- Opportunity cost versus money cost: the price paid is not the opportunity cost.
- Inside the PPF versus outside: inside is wasteful but possible, outside is unobtainable.
- Movement versus shift: choice moves you along, growth shifts the curve.
- Revenue versus profit: revenue is price times quantity, profit is revenue minus cost.

## Quick self-test

1. What is meant by scarcity?
2. A student ranks a game (45 units), a cinema trip (45) and shoes (45) in that order, and buys the game. What is the opportunity cost?
3. In the Country C table, how much wheat is lost moving from 10 to 15 laptops, and what is the cost per laptop?
4. Country C produces 10 laptops and 40 wheat. Is this possible, and what does it show?
5. Country C capacity rises by 10%. State the new maximum wheat output.
6. Give two causes of an inward shift of the PPF.
7. A government has a budget of 150 million and chooses rail instead of clinics. What is the opportunity cost?
8. State two reasons why consumers may not maximise their benefit.
9. In the T-shirt table, which output maximises profit?
10. Give two ways a firm might not maximise profit.

### Answers

1. Wants are unlimited but resources are finite, so choices must be made.
2. The cinema trip, as the next best alternative.
3. 48 − 30 = 18 wheat, which is 18 ÷ 5 = 3.6 wheat per laptop.
4. It is possible but inside the curve (48 wheat is possible at 10 laptops), so resources are unemployed or underused, by 8 wheat or 16.7%.
5. 60 × 1.1 = 66.
6. For example war or a natural disaster destroying capital, or workers leaving the country.
7. The five clinics.
8. For example habits that are hard to give up, and copying others.
9. 6 shirts, with profit of 60.
10. For example managers maximising revenue, or charitable work.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a list of all alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Stating the price instead of the thing given up.
- Drawing a PPF with a straight line when the question describes rising costs, or leaving axes unlabelled.
- Describing a point inside the curve as unobtainable.
- Showing growth as a move along the curve.
- Naming a cause of growth without saying what it does to resources or output.
- Writing "consumers are irrational" without a specific reason such as habit.
- Stating a numerical answer with no working or no units where the question says "Calculate".
