## The carbonyl group

Aldehydes and ketones both contain C=O. In an aldehyde the carbonyl carbon carries a hydrogen, so the group ends a chain (–CHO). In a ketone it is joined to two carbons, inside the chain (–CO–).

Examples on this page: octanal, CH₃(CH₂)₆CHO, and benzaldehyde, C₆H₅CHO (aldehydes); 4-methylpentan-2-one, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂COCH₃, and heptan-4-one, CH₃CH₂CH₂COCH₂CH₂CH₃ (ketones).

Two features explain this section:

- **The C=O bond is polar.** Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon is δ+ and the oxygen δ−. Electron-pair donors (nucleophiles) are drawn to the δ+ carbon.
- **The C=O double bond contains a π bond**, which can break while the σ bond stays, so a nucleophile can add across C=O. Compare alkenes: non-polar C=C is attacked by electrophiles.

## 6.1.2(a): Oxidising aldehydes

Acidified dichromate, Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺ (K₂Cr₂O₇ with dilute H₂SO₄), oxidises an aldehyde to a carboxylic acid. Reflux with excess oxidising agent so the volatile aldehyde cannot escape unreacted. As the aldehyde is oxidised, the mixture turns from orange (Cr₂O₇²⁻) to green (Cr³⁺).

Write the oxidising agent as [O]. One oxygen atom is inserted into the C–H bond of the –CHO group:

RCHO + [O] → RCOOH

Ketones have no H on the carbonyl carbon, so they are not oxidised and the reagent stays orange. The same reagent oxidised alcohols in 4.2.1; this colour change is also a PAG7 test (6.3.1(c)(vi)).

### Worked example 1: making octanoic acid

3.84 g of octanal is heated under reflux with excess acidified potassium dichromate. 3.37 g of pure octanoic acid is obtained. Write the equation and calculate the percentage yield. (Mᵣ: C₈H₁₆O 128.0, C₈H₁₆O₂ 144.0)

1. CH₃(CH₂)₆CHO + [O] → CH₃(CH₂)₆COOH
2. n(octanal) = 3.84 ÷ 128.0 = 0.0300 mol
3. One mole of octanal gives one mole of acid: at most 0.0300 mol
4. Theoretical mass = 0.0300 × 144.0 = 4.32 g
5. Percentage yield = 3.37 ÷ 4.32 × 100 = **78.0%**

## 6.1.2(b): Nucleophilic addition

### (b)(i) Reduction with NaBH₄

Sodium tetrahydridoborate(III), NaBH₄, reduces carbonyl compounds to alcohols. It is used in aqueous solution (or in methanol) and supplies hydride ions, H⁻. In equations write the reducing agent as [H]; two [H] are added across C=O.

- An aldehyde: RCHO + 2[H] → RCH₂OH, a **primary** alcohol
- A ketone: RCOR′ + 2[H] → RCH(OH)R′, a **secondary** alcohol

### Worked example 2: two reductions

Benzaldehyde gives phenylmethanol:

C₆H₅CHO + 2[H] → C₆H₅CH₂OH

4-Methylpentan-2-one gives 4-methylpentan-2-ol:

(CH₃)₂CHCH₂COCH₃ + 2[H] → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH(OH)CH₃

### (b)(ii) Addition of HCN

Adding HCN across C=O produces a **hydroxynitrile**: one carbon ends up carrying both an –OH group and a –C≡N group. HCN is very toxic and volatile, so the specification gives the reagent as NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq): sodium cyanide supplies CN⁻ ions and the acid supplies H⁺ ions.

RCHO + HCN → RCH(OH)CN

The new bond is between carbon atoms, so the chain grows by one carbon. This is one of the C–C bond-forming reactions used in synthesis (6.2.4(b)(ii)), where the nitrile group can then be converted further.

**Naming the product.** The –C≡N carbon is carbon 1 of the nitrile chain, so the former carbonyl carbon becomes carbon 2 and the name starts "2-hydroxy…".

### Worked example 3: a hydroxynitrile from benzaldehyde

5.30 g of benzaldehyde reacts with NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq). 4.79 g of product is isolated. Name the product and find the percentage yield. (Mᵣ: C₇H₆O 106.0, C₈H₇NO 133.0)

1. C₆H₅CHO + HCN → C₆H₅CH(OH)CN, **2-hydroxy-2-phenylethanenitrile**
2. n(benzaldehyde) = 5.30 ÷ 106.0 = 0.0500 mol
3. Maximum n(product) = 0.0500 mol (1 : 1)
4. Theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 133.0 = 6.65 g
5. Percentage yield = 4.79 ÷ 6.65 × 100 = **72.0%**

## 6.1.2(c): The two mechanisms

Both mechanisms have two stages: a nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon, then the O⁻ is protonated. Curly arrows start at a lone pair or a bond.

### NaBH₄ with 4-methylpentan-2-one

1. Show the C=O dipole: δ+ on C, δ− on O.
2. The hydride ion, H⁻, uses its lone pair to attack the carbonyl carbon (curly arrow from that lone pair to C).
3. The π-bond electrons of C=O move onto O, shown by a curly arrow that starts on the double bond and ends on the O atom.
4. The intermediate is (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH(CH₃)–O⁻, with the negative charge on oxygen.
5. The O⁻ uses a lone pair to take H from a water molecule (arrow to that H), while the water's O–H bonding pair moves back onto its oxygen.
6. The products are 4-methylpentan-2-ol and OH⁻.

### HCN with benzaldehyde

1. Show the C=O dipole.
2. The nucleophile is the cyanide ion, :C≡N⁻. Its carbon atom forms the new bond, so the curly arrow begins at the **carbon's** lone pair.
3. As before, the C=O π-bond pair moves onto O.
4. The intermediate is C₆H₅CH(CN)–O⁻.
5. Protonate the oxygen with H⁺ (arrow from the O⁻ lone pair to H⁺) or with H₂O (as in step 5 above); the specification allows either.
6. The product is 2-hydroxy-2-phenylethanenitrile.

Nothing leaves in either mechanism: two groups add across C=O, which is why both are nucleophilic additions.

## 6.1.2(d): 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNP)

### (d)(i) Detecting a carbonyl group

Add a few drops of the unknown to a solution of 2,4-DNP. An **orange (or yellow-orange) precipitate** shows a C=O group in an aldehyde or ketone. No precipitate means no aldehyde or ketone is present. Carboxylic acids and esters also contain C=O, but they do not give this precipitate. You do not need the equation or the structure of the precipitate (the derivative).

### (d)(ii) Identifying the compound by melting point

The orange solid is a derivative of the particular carbonyl compound, and each derivative has its own melting point. The method, which is part of PAG7:

1. Filter off the orange precipitate.
2. Recrystallise it: dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent, cool so crystals form, filter and wash with a little cold solvent.
3. Dry the crystals.
4. Measure the melting point.
5. Compare with a table of known derivative melting points.

An impure solid starts melting too low and melts gradually, so a crude sample can match the wrong entry in the table. A pure sample melts sharply.

### Worked example 4: identifying a C₇H₁₄O ketone

With 2,4-DNP, compound K (C₇H₁₄O) forms an orange precipitate; with Tollens' reagent it forms no silver mirror. Its recrystallised derivative melts at 74–75 °C. Use these values for the derivatives: heptanal 108 °C, heptan-2-one 89 °C, heptan-3-one 81 °C, heptan-4-one 75 °C.

1. Orange precipitate: K is an aldehyde or a ketone.
2. No silver mirror: K is not an aldehyde, so heptanal is ruled out before you look at the melting point.
3. The sharp melting point of 74–75 °C matches 75 °C.
4. K is **heptan-4-one**, CH₃CH₂CH₂COCH₂CH₂CH₃.

## 6.1.2(e): Tollens' reagent

### (e)(i) Detecting an aldehyde

Tollens' reagent is ammoniacal silver nitrate: aqueous ammonia is added to silver nitrate solution until the brown precipitate that forms first just redissolves. Add the unknown and warm in a water bath (not a flame, because many organic compounds are flammable). An aldehyde gives a **silver mirror** on the tube, or a grey-black precipitate of silver.

### (e)(ii) Telling aldehydes from ketones

- **Aldehyde:** silver mirror forms. The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid, and the silver ions are reduced to silver metal.
- **Ketone:** no change. A ketone cannot be oxidised this way, so the Ag⁺ ions are not reduced.

The equations, with [O] accepted for the organic change:

RCHO + [O] → RCOOH  (oxidation)

Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag  (reduction)

The ratio follows from the electrons: 1 mol of –CHO groups gives up 2 mol of electrons, enough to reduce 2 mol of Ag⁺.

### Worked example 5: mass of silver

1.34 g of 3-phenylpropanal, C₆H₅CH₂CH₂CHO, reacts completely with excess Tollens' reagent. Find the mass of silver deposited. (Mᵣ C₉H₁₀O 134.0; Aᵣ Ag 107.9)

1. n(aldehyde) = 1.34 ÷ 134.0 = 0.0100 mol
2. n(Ag) = 2 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol
3. Mass of Ag = 0.0200 × 107.9 = **2.16 g**

## The three tests side by side

| Reagent | Aldehyde | Ketone | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,4-DNP | Orange precipitate | Orange precipitate | C=O of an aldehyde or ketone present |
| Tollens' reagent, warm | Silver mirror | No change | Aldehyde, not ketone |
| Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺, warm | Orange to green | Stays orange | Aldehyde, not ketone (primary and secondary alcohols also turn it green) |

Use 2,4-DNP first, then Tollens' reagent. The full scheme for unknowns is in 6.3.1(c).

## Common errors

- Starting the cyanide arrow at the nitrogen. The bond forms from carbon, so the arrow starts at the carbon lone pair.
- Missing the arrow from the C=O bond to oxygen, or putting the negative charge on carbon in the intermediate.
- Writing H₂ instead of 2[H] in NaBH₄ equations.
- Describing the 2,4-DNP result as a colour change. The positive result is a precipitate.
- Naming hydroxynitriles from the wrong end. The nitrile carbon is carbon 1.
- Using the melting point of an unrecrystallised derivative.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can nucleophiles attack the C=O bond?**
The C=O bond is polar, so the carbon is δ+, and the double bond contains a π bond that can break while the σ bond stays. A nucleophile can therefore add to the carbonyl carbon.

**What does NaBH₄ reduce aldehydes and ketones to?**
NaBH₄ reduces an aldehyde to a primary alcohol and a ketone to a secondary alcohol. Write the reducing agent as 2[H] in the equations.

**What colour change shows that an aldehyde has been oxidised by acidified dichromate?**
The mixture turns from orange to green as the dichromate ions are reduced. The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid under reflux.

**How is a 2,4-DNP derivative used to identify a carbonyl compound?**
The orange precipitate is a derivative with its own melting point. Recrystallise it, measure its melting point and compare with data; this method is part of PAG7.

**Do carboxylic acids and esters give a 2,4-DNP precipitate?**
No. They contain C=O groups, but only aldehydes and ketones give the orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP.
