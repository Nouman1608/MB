OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) is a linear, untiered qualification with no coursework.
Paper 1 (How Markets Work) and Paper 2 (How Economies Work) each run 1 hour 45 minutes, carry 80 marks
and are worth 50%, and each **combines multiple-choice questions with essay-style questions in the
same paper**. This is a recent specification: first teaching September 2023, first examined May/June
2025. These notes complement the site's guides to
[How Markets Work](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-economics-how-markets-work/) and
[Government Objectives](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-economics-government-objectives/).

## One paper, two very different kinds of writing

Moving between multiple choice and extended prose within a single sitting is a skill in itself.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise the transition rather than the two formats separately.
Specifically, practise finishing the multiple-choice section briskly and switching into extended
writing without a long warm-up -- the essay-style questions carry the larger share of the marks and
should not inherit a depleted clock.

## The paper titles are the revision structure

Paper 1 is microeconomics -- how individual markets work. Paper 2 is macroeconomics -- how whole
economies work. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise them as two bodies of technique rather than one
undifferentiated subject. Diagrams, vocabulary and typical chains of reasoning all differ, and
confusing an aggregate relationship with an individual-market one is a recurring and expensive error.

## Eighty marks in 105 minutes is 1.3 minutes per mark

**Exam-preparation priority**: hold multiple-choice questions to roughly a minute each. There is no
partial credit available for persistence on a multiple-choice item, so a question that has not
resolved in ninety seconds should be answered with the best available guess and left. That guess is
free; the time is not.

## Diagrams earn marks quickly when they are labelled

A demand and supply diagram, or its macroeconomic equivalent, can carry several marks and takes less
time than the equivalent paragraph. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise drawing them fast and
correctly -- axes labelled, curves labelled, shift or movement shown with an arrow, new equilibrium
marked -- and practise referring to them in the prose rather than leaving them to stand alone.

## The shift-versus-movement rule protects marks across both papers

Confusing a shift of a curve with a movement along it is the most common analytical error at this
level, and it appears in both micro and macro contexts. **Exam-preparation priority**: drill it until
automatic. Only a change in the good's own price produces a movement along the curve; a change in
income, costs, related goods or expectations shifts it.

## Essay-style answers need a chain and then a judgement

The extended questions reward reasoning that moves in linked steps and finishes with a supported view.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise both halves. Presenting two effects and stopping is the most
common way to lose the final marks on an extended question, and it is a structural failure rather than
a knowledge one.

## Worked routine: an essay-style answer

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - define the key term precisely in one sentence:
Definitions are creditable and they discipline the rest of the
answer.

Step 2 - state the position you will argue:
Up front, so everything after functions as support.

Step 3 - build one chain of reasoning, link by link:
Cause -> mechanism -> effect, with each link stated rather than
assumed. Insert the diagram where it does work.

Step 4 - give the strongest consideration on the other side:
Taken seriously. Evaluation marks depend on this being a real
alternative, not a token.

Step 5 - say what the answer DEPENDS on, then conclude:
Magnitude, time period, elasticity, or whether other things stay
equal -- then confirm or qualify the position from step 2.
```

Step 5 is the clearest marker of evaluative thinking at this level: identifying what the conclusion
turns on shows understanding of the economics rather than recall of it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise switching between multiple choice and extended writing in one
  sitting; revise micro and macro as separate bodies of technique; drill the shift-versus-movement
  distinction; practise drawing labelled diagrams quickly.
- **During either paper**: cap multiple-choice questions at about a minute; never leave one blank,
  since a wrong answer costs nothing.
- **In every diagram**: label axes and curves, show the shift or movement, mark the new equilibrium,
  and refer to the diagram in the prose.
- **In every extended answer**: define, take a position, build the chain link by link, give the
  strongest counter-consideration, and finish with what it depends on.

## Self-test

1. What is unusual about the internal composition of each paper?
2. How do the two papers differ in content?
3. How long should a multiple-choice question take, and why not longer?
4. What is typically missing from an incomplete extended answer?

**Answers:** 1. Each paper combines multiple-choice questions and essay-style questions in the same
sitting, so a candidate has to switch between two very different kinds of writing under one clock.
2. Paper 1 is microeconomics -- how individual markets work -- and Paper 2 is macroeconomics -- how whole
economies work; their diagrams, vocabulary and chains of reasoning differ. 3. About a minute; longer
is unprofitable because there is no partial credit for persistence, and the time belongs to the
extended questions. 4. The evaluative close: what the conclusion depends on, and a judgement -- not
just two effects presented and left.
