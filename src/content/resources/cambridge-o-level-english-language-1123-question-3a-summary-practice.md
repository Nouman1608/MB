> where candidates "write a summary as continuous writing of no more than 150 words" on Text B. The
> article is original and close to the real Text B length (about 550–600 words). Question 8 is the full
> task at the syllabus tariff of 20 marks (10 reading, 10 writing); the syllabus does not publish a
> points list, so the marking below is a Marlbridge practice convention. Other marks are practice
> values.

Read the article (original, written for this practice set).

> **The Repair Café Movement**
>
> (1) On the first Saturday of every month, the hall behind a small library fills with broken things.
> There are toasters that no longer toast, lamps with frayed cables, jeans with split seams and bicycles
> with gears that slip. Their owners have brought them to a repair café, where volunteers with
> screwdrivers, sewing machines and soldering irons try to bring them back to life, free of charge.
>
> (2) The idea is simple, and it is spreading. Organisers say the most obvious benefit is to people's
> pockets. A replacement kettle may not cost a fortune, but a family that repairs rather than replaces a
> dozen items a year can save a considerable sum. For those on low incomes, a repaired washing machine
> can be the difference between managing and not managing.
>
> (3) There is an environmental argument too. Every repaired item is one that does not end up in
> landfill, and one fewer new product that has to be manufactured, packaged and shipped across the
> world. Volunteers keep a record of what they fix, and at one café the items saved from the rubbish bin
> in a single year would have filled a delivery van.
>
> (4) Yet regular visitors often say that what they value most is not the money or even the
> environment. It is the knowledge. Volunteers are encouraged to repair things with the owner rather
> than for them, explaining each step. "I came in with a lamp and went home knowing how to change a
> plug," said one young visitor. "My grandfather could do these things. My parents never learned. Now I
> can." Skills that were once passed down in families are, in this way, being rediscovered.
>
> (5) The cafés are also, quite simply, sociable. People queue with cups of tea, compare their broken
> treasures and swap advice. For older residents who live alone, a morning at the repair café may be the
> only time in the week they spend talking to strangers, and some volunteers are themselves retired
> engineers or tailors who enjoy feeling useful again. Friendships form across generations that would
> rarely meet otherwise.
>
> (6) Not everything can be saved. Some modern devices are glued shut or need parts that manufacturers
> will not supply, and volunteers admit that their success rate is far from perfect. Critics point out,
> too, that a monthly event in a library hall can only make a small dent in a throwaway culture.
> Organisers accept this. But they argue that every visitor who leaves with a working toaster also
> leaves with a different attitude: the next time something breaks, their first thought may be "Can I
> fix it?" rather than "Where can I buy a new one?" That change of habit, they believe, is the café's
> most lasting achievement.

## Section A

**1.** Paragraph 1 lists toasters, lamps, jeans and bicycles. Are these points or examples for a
summary of benefits? Explain briefly. **[1]**

**2.** Generalise in two or three words: "screwdrivers, sewing machines and soldering irons". **[1]**

**3.** Compress into eight words or fewer: "For those on low incomes, a repaired washing machine can be
the difference between managing and not managing." **[1]**

**4.** Which parts of paragraph 6 should be left out of a summary of the benefits, and why? **[1]**

**5.** Rewrite in your own words: "Skills that were once passed down in families are, in this way,
being rediscovered." **[2]**

**6.** Rewrite as one grouped and linked sentence: "Firstly, it saves money. Secondly, it helps poor
people. Thirdly, it reduces landfill. Fourthly, fewer new things are made." **[2]**

**7.** Put these notes into two groups and name each group: saves money; less landfill; fewer new
products made; helps low-income families. **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** Summarise the benefits of repair cafés, as described in the article. Write your summary as
continuous writing of no more than 150 words. **[20]**

**9.** Why should the young visitor's words not be quoted in the summary? **[1]**

**10.** A candidate's summary is 172 words long. Explain two consequences. **[2]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Examples: they illustrate the kinds of item brought for repair and are not benefits [1].

**2.** "tools" / "repair tools" [1].

**3.** For example: "It is vital for families with little money." [1]

**4.** The failures (devices that cannot be fixed, the imperfect success rate) and the critics' view are
drawbacks, not benefits [1]. The final point about changing habits **is** a benefit and should be
kept.

**5.** For example [2]: "Abilities that families used to teach their children are now being learned
again." One mark for re-expressing "passed down in families", one for "rediscovered".

**6.** For example [2]: "Repair cafés save money, which matters most to poorer households, and they
reduce waste by keeping goods out of landfill and cutting the need for new products." One mark for
grouping money and environment, one for linking words that show relationships.

**7.** Financial: saves money; helps low-income families [1]. Environmental: less landfill; fewer new
products made [1].

**8.** Reading (10, Marlbridge practice convention: one mark per point, up to 10):

1. saves money (by fixing instead of replacing)
2. especially important for those on low incomes
3. less waste sent to landfill
4. fewer new products to manufacture, package and transport
5. visitors learn practical repair skills
6. revives skills once passed down in families
7. sociable: people meet and share advice
8. reduces isolation for older people who live alone
9. retired volunteers feel useful again
10. creates friendships across generations
11. changes attitudes: people consider repairing before buying

Not credited: the free service on its own without the saving; examples of items; the failures and
criticisms in paragraph 6; the delivery-van comparison (an illustration).

Writing (10, Marlbridge practice convention): high marks for points grouped (money, environment,
skills, social life, attitudes) and linked with relationship words; concise, accurate own words;
varied sentence structures; no lifted sentences; within the limit. Middle marks for clear prose that
follows the order of the text with some lifting. Low marks for a list, heavy copying or a response
far over the limit.

Model summary (120 words):

> Repair cafés bring clear financial benefits: fixing items instead of replacing them saves families
> money, which can be vital for those on low incomes. They also help the environment, because repaired
> goods stay out of landfill and fewer new products need to be made, packaged and transported. Perhaps
> more importantly, visitors learn practical skills by repairing items alongside volunteers, reviving
> knowledge that families once handed down. The cafés are sociable places where people meet and
> exchange advice; they ease loneliness for older people living alone, give retired volunteers a
> renewed sense of purpose and create friendships between generations. Finally, they change people's
> habits, encouraging them to consider repairing something before replacing it, which organisers see
> as their most lasting achievement.

**9.** Quoting copies the text's words and adds detail; the summary needs only the point (people learn
skills their families once had), in the candidate's own words [1].

**10.** Two points [2]: points made after the 150-word limit is reached are not credited, so reading
marks may be lost [1]; the excess words suggest detail or repetition, weakening the concise
organisation the writing marks reward [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 8**: copying the opening list of broken items, or including the critics.
- **Question 8**: missing the final benefit (changed habits), which appears in a paragraph about
  drawbacks.
- **Question 8**: following the text paragraph by paragraph with "Firstly... Secondly...", which
  reads as a list.
- **Question 4**: deleting the whole of paragraph 6 and losing the last benefit.
