This guide covers section 3.1 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Economic growth, the first economic objective in topic 3 and part of Component 02, National and International Economics. It builds on the wider [guide to economic growth, GDP and unemployment](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-economic-growth-gdp-and-unemployment/) with new examples and more on real values, data reading and sustainability. All countries and figures are invented unless a real event is named.

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by economic growth | Define it as a rise in the output of goods and services over time |
| Calculate and explain how growth is measured with reference to GDP and GDP per capita | Work out growth rates and output per person, and say what each shows |
| Analyse recent and historical GDP data | Describe trends, compare periods and explain turning points |
| Analyse the determinants of growth | Investment, changes in technology, size of workforce, education and training, natural resources, government policies |
| Evaluate the costs and benefits of growth | Including economic, social and environmental sustainability |

## Growth, GDP and why "real" matters

Gross Domestic Product measures the value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period. Economic growth is the percentage increase in that output.

GDP can rise for two reasons: more is produced, or the same output is sold at higher prices. Only the first is growth. That is why economists use **real GDP**, adjusted for inflation, rather than **nominal GDP**, measured at current prices.

Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100

### Worked example 1: nominal and real growth

Country K has nominal GDP of £500bn in Year 1, when the price index is 100. In Year 2 nominal GDP is £540bn and the price index is 105.

- Nominal growth = (540 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **8.0%**.
- Real GDP in Year 2 = 540 ÷ 105 × 100 = **£514.3bn** at Year 1 prices.
- Real growth = (514.3 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **2.9%**.

Most of the 8% rise was higher prices. Output grew by under 3%.

### Worked example 2: GDP per capita

Country K's population rises from 25 million to 25.5 million.

- Year 1: £500bn ÷ 25 million = **£20,000** per person.
- Year 2: £514.3bn ÷ 25.5 million = **£20,168** per person, a rise of **0.8%**.

Total real output grew 2.9%, but output per person grew less than 1% because the population also grew 2%.

Now compare Country L: GDP £90bn, population 3 million, so GDP per capita is **£30,000**. K's economy is over five times larger, but the average person in L has nearly 50% more output (£30,000 against £20,168 in Year 2). Total GDP shows the size of an economy; GDP per capita is the better guide to average living standards, though it says nothing about how output is shared.

## Analysing GDP data

Growth figures are often published for each quarter (three months). Here is an invented series for Country K.

| Quarter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth on previous quarter (%) | 0.6 | 0.4 | 0.1 | −0.2 | −0.4 | 0.0 | 0.3 | 0.5 |

What to say:

- **Slowing is not shrinking.** In quarters 2 and 3 growth fell, but output still rose.
- **Output fell** in quarters 4 and 5. Two quarters of negative growth in a row is a common definition of a recession.
- **Quarter 6** shows zero growth: output stopped falling but did not recover.
- **Recovery** began in quarter 7.
- **Size matters.** Growth of 0.5% in one quarter is roughly 2% over a year if it continues.

Over longer periods, growth compounds. At 2.5% a year an economy is about 28% larger after ten years; at 1% it is only about 10% larger. Small differences in the growth rate become large differences in living standards.

For historical context, UK output fell in the recession of 2008-09 that followed the global financial crisis, and fell very sharply in 2020 when the pandemic closed much of the economy, before recovering. When you are given real data in the exam, quote the figures and years from the source rather than from memory.

## The determinants of growth

Growth comes from having **more** resources or using them **better**. Sort the six determinants named in the specification this way and your chains of reasoning become clearer.

| Determinant | More resources or better use? | Chain of reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Investment | More capital | Firms buy machines and buildings, so each worker has more equipment, so output per worker rises |
| Changes in technology | Better use | New methods produce more from the same inputs, cutting costs and raising output |
| Size of workforce | More labour | Migration, a rising population or more people seeking work increase the labour available, if jobs exist |
| Education and training | Better use | Skilled workers produce more per hour and adapt to new technology |
| Natural resources | More land | Finding oil, gas or minerals adds inputs, but non-renewable resources run down |
| Government policies | Both | Spending on infrastructure, stable laws and support for research help firms produce more |

**Analysis point.** A bigger workforce raises total GDP but not necessarily GDP per capita. Investment, technology and education raise output **per worker**, so they lift living standards most.

## Costs and benefits, and sustainability

The specification asks you to judge growth against three kinds of sustainability.

**Economic sustainability: can the growth continue?** Growth built on rising productivity can last. Growth driven by heavy borrowing, a short-lived boom in house prices, or by using up a resource that will run out may not.

**Social sustainability: is it shared?** If gains go mostly to high earners or to one region, inequality widens and communities left behind may suffer. If gains fund schools and health care, growth improves lives widely.

**Environmental sustainability: does it damage the future?** More output can mean more pollution, waste and use of finite resources. But richer economies can also afford cleaner technology, and growth in services or renewable energy may do little damage.

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Higher incomes and more jobs | Pollution and use of finite resources |
| More tax revenue without raising tax rates | Rising inflation if demand outruns supply |
| Better public services can be afforded | Gains may be unequally shared |
| Firms earn more profit to invest | Stress, congestion and longer working hours |

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether economic growth is always good for a country.*

"Growth raises incomes, so households can afford more goods and services and living standards rise. **[benefit with a chain]** It also increases tax revenue without higher tax rates, so the government can spend more on hospitals and schools. **[second benefit, different group]** However, if growth comes from factories burning more fossil fuels, pollution rises and health suffers, so growth may not be environmentally sustainable. **[cost linked to sustainability]** If most of the gains go to the richest households, poorer groups may see little improvement, which is not socially sustainable. **[second cost]** Growth is therefore not always good: it depends on how it is produced and who receives it. Growth from higher productivity and cleaner technology, with the gains widely shared, is likely to benefit the country; growth that runs down resources is not." **[judgement that names the deciding factors]**

## Common mistakes

- Using nominal GDP as evidence of growth when prices have risen.
- Saying the economy "shrank" when growth only slowed.
- Treating total GDP as a measure of living standards.
- Listing determinants without a chain to higher output.
- Calling growth "bad for the environment" with no reference to how output is produced.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, keep units (£bn, per person), and round only at the end.
- **Analyse** GDP data: quote at least two figures, describe the trend, identify any recession, and explain one cause.
- **Evaluate**: always weigh benefits against costs and finish with what the outcome depends on. In Part B, use the case study's own country, sector or data.
