This guide covers subtopic **2.1 Kinetic particle model of matter** from Topic 2, Thermal physics,
in Cambridge O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series): 2.1.1 States of matter and 2.1.2 Particle
model. The [thermal physics topic guide](/resources/o-level-physics-thermal-physics/) gives an
overview of all of Topic 2. This page goes deeper into how the particle model explains each
property, how to build full-mark explanations of gas behaviour, and how to use p1V1 = p2V2 with
graphs and data.

## Distinguishing properties of the three states

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of the container | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Fills the container; changes easily |
| Compressibility | Almost incompressible | Almost incompressible | Easily compressed |
| Ability to flow | Does not flow | Flows | Flows |
| Density | Usually high | Usually high (similar to its solid) | Very low |

## Names of the changes of state

| Change | Name |
|---|---|
| Solid → liquid | Melting |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing (solidification) |
| Liquid → gas | Boiling or evaporation |
| Gas → liquid | Condensation |

Solid to gas and gas to solid are not required in 5054.

## Explaining properties with the particle model

Each property comes from three features: **how close** the particles are, **how strong** the
forces between them are, and **how they move**.

**Solids:** particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement, held by strong forces, and
**vibrate about fixed positions**. Fixed positions explain a fixed shape; close packing explains
why solids are hard to compress.

**Liquids:** particles are still close together, so liquids are almost incompressible and have a
fixed volume. The forces are weaker than in a solid and the arrangement is irregular, so particles
**move around each other**. This is why a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container.

**Gases:** particles are **far apart** compared with their size, with negligible forces between
them except during collisions. They move **randomly at high speed** in straight lines between
collisions. Large spaces explain compressibility and low density; fast, random motion explains
why a gas spreads to fill any container.

The particles may be atoms, molecules or ions; in metals, free electrons also move through the
structure (important in subtopic 2.3).

## Temperature and absolute zero

The temperature of a substance is linked to the **average kinetic energy** of its particles. When
a gas is heated, its particles move faster on average; when it is cooled, they slow down.

Cooling cannot continue for ever. There is a lowest possible temperature, **−273 °C**, called
**absolute zero**, at which the particles have the **least possible kinetic energy**. Nothing can
be colder. The kelvin scale (subtopic 2.2) starts at absolute zero, so −273 °C = 0 K.

## Gas pressure: a full-mark explanation

A strong explanation contains four links:

1. Gas particles move randomly and **collide with the walls** of the container.
2. Each collision changes the particle's momentum, so the wall exerts a force on the particle and
   the particle exerts an equal and opposite **force on the wall**.
3. Many collisions every second produce a steady total force on the wall.
4. Pressure is this **force per unit area** of wall.

## The three relationships

**Pressure and temperature at constant volume.** Heating makes particles move faster. They hit
the walls **more often** and **harder** (with greater change in momentum), so the force per unit
area increases: pressure rises with temperature.

**Volume and temperature at constant pressure.** Heating makes particles move faster and hit the
walls harder and more often. If the container can expand (for example a balloon or a piston that
moves freely), the gas expands until the collision rate per unit area falls enough for the
pressure to return to its original value: volume increases with temperature.

**Pressure and volume at constant temperature.** Squashing a gas into a smaller volume means the
same number of particles, at the same average speed, hit a smaller area of wall. Collisions
per unit area happen **more often**, so pressure increases. Note that the particles do **not**
move faster: the temperature is constant.

## Using p1V1 = p2V2

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure × volume stays constant:

```
p1 V1 = p2 V2
```

Any units can be used, provided both pressures use the same unit and both volumes use the same
unit.

**Worked example 1: a rising bubble.** A diver releases an air bubble of volume 2.0 cm³ where the
pressure is 300 kPa. It rises to just below the surface, where the pressure is 100 kPa. Assume
constant temperature.

```
V2 = p1 V1 / p2 = 300 × 2.0 / 100 = 6.0 cm³
```

Pressure falls to one third, so the volume triples.

**Worked example 2: a gas cylinder.** A cylinder holds 0.050 m³ of gas at 2400 kPa. What volume
would the gas occupy at 100 kPa, at the same temperature?

```
V2 = 2400 × 0.050 / 100 = 1.2 m³
```

## The pressure–volume graph

**Data check.** The table shows readings for a trapped gas at constant temperature.

| p / kPa | 80 | 100 | 160 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / cm³ | 50 | 40 | 25 | 20 |
| pV / kPa cm³ | 4000 | 4000 | 4000 | 4000 |

Because pV is the same for every reading, p is **inversely proportional** to V.

- A graph of **p against V** is a smooth **curve** that falls steeply at small volumes and levels
  off at large volumes; it never touches either axis.
- A graph of **p against 1/V** is a **straight line through the origin**, which is a quick way to
  show inverse proportion.

When doubling the pressure halves the volume, the relationship is inverse proportion; a straight
line sloping downwards is a wrong sketch.

## Common mistakes

- Saying gas particles "expand" when heated. The particles do not get bigger; the spaces between
  them increase.
- Saying particles move faster when a gas is compressed at constant temperature.
- Omitting "per unit area" from a pressure explanation.
- Mixing kPa and Pa, or cm³ and m³, between the two sides of p1V1 = p2V2.
- Using p1V1 = p2V2 when the temperature changes.
- Describing solid particles as "not moving". They vibrate about fixed positions.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Describe** (particle structure): arrangement, separation, forces and motion for each state.
- **Explain** (gas behaviour): collisions → force on walls → force per unit area, and say what
  changes: speed, frequency of collisions or area.
- **Calculate**: rearrange p1V1 = p2V2, substitute with units, answer with unit.
- **Sketch** (graph): correct curve shape with labelled axes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is absolute zero −273 °C and not lower?** At −273 °C the particles have the least kinetic
energy possible; they cannot have less, so there is no lower temperature.

**Do I need to explain why gases expand at constant pressure in exams?** Yes: the syllabus asks
for all three relationships in terms of particles.

**Is boiling the only way a liquid becomes a gas?** No. Evaporation also turns liquid into gas,
from the surface and below the boiling point (subtopic 2.2).
