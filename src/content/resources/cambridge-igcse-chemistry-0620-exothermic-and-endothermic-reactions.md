This guide covers subtopic **5.1 Exothermic and endothermic reactions** in Topic 5, Chemical
energetics, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The broader guide
[Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions](/resources/exothermic-and-endothermic-reactions/)
introduces the ideas with a single-bond example; this page concentrates on **reading and
drawing** pathway diagrams accurately and on **bond energy calculations with multiple and
double bonds**, where most marks are dropped.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- State that an **exothermic** reaction transfers thermal energy **to** the surroundings,
  leading to an **increase** in the temperature of the surroundings.
- State that an **endothermic** reaction takes in thermal energy **from** the surroundings,
  leading to a **decrease** in the temperature of the surroundings.
- **Interpret** reaction pathway diagrams showing exothermic and endothermic reactions.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- State that the transfer of thermal energy during a reaction is the **enthalpy change,
  ΔH**; ΔH is **negative** for exothermic and **positive** for endothermic reactions.
- Define **activation energy, Ea**, as the **minimum energy that colliding particles must
  have to react**.
- **Draw and label** reaction pathway diagrams, including reactants, products, ΔH and Ea.
- State that **bond breaking is endothermic** and **bond making is exothermic**, and explain
  ΔH in these terms.
- **Calculate** ΔH using bond energies.

## The surroundings: where the temperature change happens

In a reaction in a test tube, the **system** is the reacting chemicals and the
**surroundings** are everything else — the water they are dissolved in, the tube, the
thermometer, the air. A thermometer measures the surroundings.

- **Exothermic:** energy leaves the chemicals → surroundings **warm up** → thermometer
  reading **rises**. Examples: combustion, neutralisation, metal displacement reactions,
  respiration.
- **Endothermic:** energy is taken in by the chemicals → surroundings **cool down** →
  thermometer reading **falls**. Examples: thermal decomposition (which needs continuous
  heating), photosynthesis, the reaction of citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate.

**Worked example 1 (Core).** *A student adds zinc powder to copper(II) sulfate solution.
The temperature rises from 21 °C to 35 °C. Classify the reaction.*
The temperature of the surroundings increased by 14 °C, so thermal energy was transferred
**to** the surroundings: the reaction is **exothermic**.

Do not write "the reaction gets hotter, so it takes in heat". The rising temperature is the
evidence that the reaction **gives out** heat.

## Reading a reaction pathway diagram (Core)

The vertical axis is **energy**; the horizontal axis is the **progress of the reaction**.
Read three things:

1. **Where the products sit compared with the reactants.**
   Products **lower** → energy has been released → **exothermic**.
   Products **higher** → energy has been taken in → **endothermic**.
2. **The hump.** Every reaction has one: the reactants must gain energy before they can
   react. The height of the hump above the reactants is the activation energy.
3. **The size of the gap** between reactants and products: the larger the gap, the more
   energy is transferred.

## Drawing a pathway diagram (Supplement)

1. Draw and label the axes: **energy** (vertical), **progress of reaction** (horizontal).
2. Draw a short horizontal line on the left labelled **reactants**, with formulae if given.
3. Draw the products line on the right — **below** for exothermic, **above** for
   endothermic.
4. Join them with a **smooth curve with a single hump** higher than both lines.
5. Draw **Ea** as a vertical arrow from the **reactants line up to the top of the hump**.
6. Draw **ΔH** as a vertical arrow from the **reactants line to the products line** —
   pointing **down** for exothermic, **up** for endothermic — and label it with its value
   and sign if given.

The most common errors are an Ea arrow drawn from the products, a ΔH arrow drawn to the top
of the hump, and arrows with no heads.

## Activation energy (Supplement)

**Ea is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.** Learn the wording:
"minimum", "colliding particles" and "to react" are each likely marking points. Ea is always
positive, for both exothermic and endothermic reactions, and it does not tell you the sign
of ΔH.

## Bonds and ΔH (Supplement)

- **Breaking bonds needs energy:** endothermic.
- **Making bonds releases energy:** exothermic.

```
ΔH = total energy to break bonds in reactants − total energy released making bonds in products
```

If making the new bonds releases **more** energy than breaking the old ones needed, ΔH is
**negative** (exothermic); if less, ΔH is **positive** (endothermic).

## Bond energy calculations (Supplement)

**Method:** draw or picture every bond in every molecule, multiply by the coefficients in
the equation, total each side, subtract.

**Worked example 2: combustion of methane** — using these bond energies (kJ/mol):
C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464.

```
CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O

bonds broken:  4 × C–H = 4 × 413 = 1652
               2 × O=O = 2 × 498 =  996
               total              = 2648 kJ/mol

bonds made:    2 × C=O = 2 × 805 = 1610   (one CO2 has two C=O)
               4 × O–H = 4 × 464 = 1856   (two H2O, each with two O–H)
               total              = 3466 kJ/mol

ΔH = 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ/mol   (exothermic)
```

**Worked example 3: decomposition of water** — using H–H 436, O=O 498, O–H 464.

```
2H2O → 2H2 + O2

bonds broken:  4 × O–H = 1856 kJ/mol
bonds made:    2 × H–H + 1 × O=O = 872 + 498 = 1370 kJ/mol
ΔH = 1856 − 1370 = +486 kJ/mol   (endothermic)
```

Notice that the values are the reverse of what would be found for hydrogen burning to form
water: reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔH.

**Checks before you finish:**

- Did you count **both** C=O bonds in each CO₂?
- Did you multiply by the **coefficients**?
- Is the sign consistent with what you know? Combustion is exothermic, so ΔH must be
  negative.
- Units: **kJ/mol**.

## Common mistakes

- **"Exothermic reactions take in heat because they get hot."**
- **Ea measured from the products**, or from the axis.
- **Bonds made − bonds broken**: this flips the sign.
- **Counting one bond in O₂ but treating it as single**, or using the C–O value for C=O.
- **Forgetting coefficients**, especially the 2 in 2H₂O.
- **Missing sign on ΔH**: always write + or −.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** (Core): "transfers thermal energy to the surroundings; temperature of
  surroundings increases" — both halves.
- **Interpret / deduce** from a diagram: name the feature you used ("the products are at a
  lower energy than the reactants").
- **Draw and label**: axes, both levels, one hump, two correctly placed arrows, labels.
- **Explain** ΔH: compare the energy needed to break bonds with the energy released making
  bonds, and say which is larger.
- **Calculate**: show the bonds broken and made separately, then the subtraction with the
  sign and units.
