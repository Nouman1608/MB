This guide goes deeper into section **3.4.2 Homeostasis** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and any part of the
specification may be assessed on either paper.

The existing [Homeostasis, temperature control and blood glucose study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-homeostasis-temperature-control-and-blood-glucose/)
covers 3.4.2, 3.4.4 and 3.4.5 together. This page concentrates on the general idea of homeostasis: the parts of any
control system, where receptors are found, which structures act as coordination centres and which conditions are
controlled. The detail of temperature control is in the separate
[Temperature control study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-temperature-control/). Afterwards, use the
[Homeostasis revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-homeostasis-revision-notes/) and the
[Homeostasis practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-homeostasis-practice/).

## What 3.4.2 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Automatic control systems in the body keep conditions inside the body relatively constant |
| b | A control system has receptors, coordination centres and effectors |
| c | Where receptors are found and what they are sensitive to |
| d | Coordination centres include the brain, the spinal cord and the pancreas |
| e | Conditions that are controlled: temperature, water content, ion content and blood glucose |

The specification states that knowledge and understanding of the structure and functions of sense organs such as the
eye and the ear is **not required**. You need to know that they contain receptors and what the receptors detect, but not
how the eye or ear works inside.

## What homeostasis means

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment. The specification's wording is that
**automatic control systems** in the body keep conditions inside the body **relatively constant**. The word
"relatively" matters: conditions vary a little around a normal value, they do not stay perfectly fixed.

It is **automatic** because it happens without conscious control. You do not decide to sweat or to release a hormone.

Keeping conditions steady is important because the chemical reactions in cells, controlled by enzymes, work best in a
narrow range of conditions. If body temperature, water content, ion content or blood glucose moved far from normal,
cells could not work properly.

## The parts of a control system

The specification names three parts.

| Part | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Receptors** (cells that detect stimuli) | Detect a change in the environment (a stimulus) | Temperature-sensitive receptors in the skin |
| **Coordination centres** | Receive and process the information from receptors | Brain, spinal cord, pancreas |
| **Effectors** | Bring about responses | Muscles and glands |

The chain is therefore:

```
stimulus → receptor → coordination centre → effector → response
```

The chain is useful for any situation, including those you have not met before.

## Where receptors are found

The specification lists receptors in many organs. Learn the organ and what it detects as a pair.

| Organ | Receptors are sensitive to |
|---|---|
| Eyes | Light |
| Ears | Sound, and changes in position (so we can keep our balance) |
| Tongue and nose | Chemicals (taste and smell) |
| Skin | Touch, pressure, pain and changes in temperature |
| Brain | Blood temperature and the concentration of water in the blood |
| Pancreas | The concentration of glucose in the blood |

Two points that are often tested.

1. Receptors in the **brain** monitor the **blood**. This is not the same as receptors in the skin, which detect the
   temperature at the surface of the body.
2. The **pancreas** contains receptors for blood glucose concentration and is also a coordination centre (see below).

## Coordination centres

Coordination centres include **the brain and spinal cord** and **the pancreas**.

- The **brain** and **spinal cord** form the central nervous system, which coordinates nervous responses.
- The **pancreas** receives information about the concentration of glucose in the blood and acts on it (its hormones
  are covered in 3.4.5).

## Conditions that are controlled

| Condition | Why it matters (examples) |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Enzymes work best at a certain temperature |
| Water content of the body | Too little or too much water affects cells |
| Ion content of the body | Ions are needed for many cell processes |
| Blood glucose level | Glucose supplies cells with a source of energy for respiration |

Temperature control is the subject of 3.4.4, the control of water and ion content is in 3.4.3 and blood glucose is in
3.4.5. In this section, you only need to know that these four conditions are controlled by automatic systems.

### Worked example 1: building the chain for a situation

Invented stimulus. "On a hot day a runner's blood becomes warmer. She begins to sweat."

```
stimulus:             rise in the temperature of the blood
receptor:             cells in the brain sensitive to blood temperature
                      (and receptors in the skin sensitive to skin temperature)
coordination centre:  the brain
effector:             sweat glands
response:             sweat is released
```

The same chain applies to a drop in the concentration of water in the blood: the receptors are in the brain, the
brain is the coordination centre, and the response involves the kidneys (see 3.4.3).

### Worked example 2: blood glucose

Invented stimulus. "After a meal, the concentration of glucose in the blood rises."

- Stimulus: higher blood glucose concentration.
- Receptor: cells in the pancreas, sensitive to blood glucose.
- Coordination centre: the pancreas.
- Response: the pancreas releases a hormone (insulin) that changes how glucose is handled (see 3.4.5).

Notice that in this example the same organ is both the receptor and the coordination centre. That is why the
specification lists the pancreas in both places.

## Reading data on a control system

Control systems keep a condition close to a normal value, so data show small variation around it.

### Worked example 3: body temperature readings

Invented data. A person's core temperature was measured six times at 30-minute intervals during a rest.

| Time (min) | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core temperature (°C) | 36.8 | 37.2 | 37.0 | 36.9 | 37.3 | 37.1 |

```
mean = (36.8 + 37.2 + 37.0 + 36.9 + 37.3 + 37.1) ÷ 6 = 222.3 ÷ 6 = 37.05 °C
range = 37.3 − 36.8 = 0.5 °C
```

The readings vary by only 0.5 °C around a mean of 37.05 °C. That small range is evidence of a control system keeping
the temperature **relatively constant**.

### Worked example 4: a rise during exercise

Invented data. Core temperature during 40 minutes of running.

| Time (min) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core temperature (°C) | 37.0 | 37.6 | 38.1 | 38.3 | 38.3 |

```
rise in first 20 minutes = 38.1 − 37.0 = 1.1 °C   → 1.1 ÷ 20 = 0.055 °C per minute
rise from 20 to 30 min   = 38.3 − 38.1 = 0.2 °C   → 0.2 ÷ 10 = 0.02 °C per minute
total rise = 38.3 − 37.0 = 1.3 °C
```

The temperature rises quickly at first, because the muscles release energy faster than it is lost. Then it levels off
at 38.3 °C, because the cooling responses (such as sweating) increase until heat loss equals heat gain. A control
system limits changes but does not remove them entirely, which is why homeostasis is "relatively" constant.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that homeostasis keeps conditions "exactly the same". They stay relatively constant.
- Saying that receptors "respond" or "cause the response". Receptors **detect** stimuli; effectors bring about
  responses.
- Confusing coordination centres with effectors. A muscle or gland is an effector.
- Saying that the brain detects light. The eyes contain the receptors; the brain processes the information.
- Forgetting that the pancreas is both a receptor site and a coordination centre.
- Writing about the structure of the eye or ear. This is not required.
- Listing only temperature and glucose; there are four controlled conditions.

## Exam technique

- **Name** the parts of a control system in the order: receptor, coordination centre, effector.
- For any situation, write the stimulus, receptor, coordination centre, effector and response.
- **Explain** why homeostasis matters by linking conditions to the working of enzymes and cells.
- When given data, calculate the mean and range and say what they show about control.
