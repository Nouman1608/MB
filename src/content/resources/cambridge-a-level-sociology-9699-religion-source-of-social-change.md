Section 11.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Religion as a source of social
change*, completes heading 11 of Paper 4. The syllabus lists four points: Weber's theory of the role
of religion in the rise of capitalism; alternative views about the relationship between religion and
the origins of capitalism; liberation theology as an example of religion acting as a vehicle for
social change; and the influence of religious movements on political debates and struggles,
examined through case studies such as the Evangelical movement in US politics or the influence of
the Ayatollahs in the Iranian revolution. Heading 11 links this to *Social change and development*.

The [religion, social order and social change guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-religion-and-social-order/)
summarises Weber, Tawney, liberation theology and the two case studies. This page explains Weber's
argument in more detail, adds further evidence on each side and develops the case studies. Use it
with the [religion and social change revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-religion-source-of-social-change-revision-notes/)
and the [religion and social change practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-religion-source-of-social-change-practice/).

## Weber's argument in detail

Max Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) explains change through
meaning: how believers understood their situation shaped how they acted.

1. **Predestination.** Calvinists believed God had already decided who would be saved (the elect),
   and nothing a person did could change it.
2. **Salvation anxiety.** Believers could not know whether they were among the elect, which created
   intense psychological anxiety.
3. **The calling.** Work in a worldly occupation was a duty to God. Success in that calling came to
   be seen as a possible sign of being chosen.
4. **Asceticism.** Calvinists rejected luxury and pleasure, so profits were not spent but
   reinvested.
5. **The spirit of capitalism.** The result was a disciplined, rational, profit-seeking way of life.
   Weber illustrated this "spirit" with Benjamin Franklin's maxims such as "time is money".
6. **Elective affinity.** Weber did not claim that Calvinism alone caused capitalism. He described
   an *elective affinity*: a fit between the Calvinist ethic and the spirit of capitalism, which
   together with economic conditions helped modern capitalism emerge.

**Comparative evidence.** In studies of China and India, Weber argued that these societies had
the economic resources for capitalism but lacked a religious ethic that encouraged rational,
worldly activity, which helped explain why modern capitalism first developed in Western Europe.

**Methodological significance.** Weber's study combines structure (economic conditions) and action
(believers' meanings), and uses *verstehen* (interpretive understanding). It is therefore a good
example of the interactionist side of the structure/agency contrast that heading 11 asks for.

**Rationalisation and the iron cage.** Weber also warned that once capitalism was established it no
longer needed its religious roots: modern people were trapped in an "iron cage" of rational economic
order. Religion caused change, then lost its role in the system it helped create, which links to
secularisation (12.1).

## Alternative views on religion and the origins of capitalism

- **Economic change first:** R.H. Tawney (1926) argued that technological and economic change came
  first and Protestant ideas adapted to justify it.
- **Marxist view:** Karl Kautsky argued that capitalism predated Calvinism, and that early
  capitalists were attracted to Calvinism because it suited their interests. Religion followed the
  economy, not the reverse.
- **Counter-examples:** capitalism developed early in some Catholic regions, while some Calvinist
  societies developed slowly.
- **Gordon Marshall's study of Scotland** (*Presbyteries and Profits*, 1980) found evidence of a
  Calvinist work ethic but argued that factors such as shortages of capital and skilled labour slowed
  Scottish capitalist development. Religion may be a necessary condition, but it is not sufficient.
- **A modern parallel:** David Martin's *Tongues of Fire* (1990) argued that Pentecostalism in
  Latin America encourages discipline, sobriety and saving among its members, comparable to the
  ethic Weber described, and may help some converts improve their economic position.

## Liberation theology

### Origins

- The **Second Vatican Council** (1962–65) encouraged the Catholic Church to engage with the modern
  world.
- At the **Medellín conference** of Latin American bishops (1968), the Church addressed poverty and
  injustice, and the idea of a **preferential option for the poor** became central to the movement.
- **Base ecclesial communities** were small groups of poor Catholics who read the Bible together and
  related it to their own conditions, often under military dictatorships.
- Gustavo Gutiérrez's *A Theology of Liberation* (1971) gave the movement its name.

### Why it matters sociologically

- It shows religion opposing an unjust social order, which contradicts the Marxist claim that
  religion is always conservative.
- Michael Löwy (*The War of Gods*, 1996) argued that liberation theology was a social movement that
  combined Christian and Marxist ideas, and that religion can challenge the existing order.
- It shows the **relative autonomy** of religion (Maduro): clergy were able to side with the poor.

### Limits

- From the 1980s the Vatican criticised the Marxist elements of liberation theology, and the
  appointment of more conservative bishops weakened it.
- With the return of democracy in many Latin American countries, part of its political role passed
  to political parties and other movements.
- The growth of Pentecostalism offered a different religious response to poverty, focused on
  individual change.

## Religious movements and political struggles

### The Evangelical movement in US politics

- The **Moral Majority** (1979) and later the **Christian Coalition**, founded by Pat Robertson in
  1989, mobilised conservative Protestants on issues such as abortion, school prayer and family
  values.
- Long campaigns against *Roe v. Wade* (1973) culminated in the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in
  *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, which overturned it. This shows a religious
  movement's influence working over decades through elections, judicial appointments and law.
- **Evaluation:** Steve Bruce (*Politics and Religion*, 2003) argued that the movement gained
  attention but its wider policy successes were limited because it had to compete in a pluralist
  political system. The *Dobbs* decision can be used to question how far that judgement still holds.

### The Ayatollahs and the Iranian revolution

- The Shah's programme of rapid modernisation and Westernisation from the 1960s, including the
  "White Revolution" launched in 1963, created discontent among clergy, merchants and the poor.
- Ayatollah Khomeini, in exile, opposed the Shah. His idea of **guardianship of the Islamic jurist**
  (velayat-e faqih) argued that a senior religious scholar should hold political authority.
- Mass protests in 1978 led to the Shah leaving Iran in January 1979; Khomeini returned in February,
  and a referendum in 1979 established the Islamic Republic.
- **Sociological points:** religion provided leadership, a network of mosques, a shared language of
  justice, and an alternative to Western-style modernisation. It is often analysed as a response to
  rapid change, linking to cultural defence and to fundamentalism (12.3).

### Other examples you can use

- **The US civil rights movement:** churches and clergy, including Martin Luther King Jr. and the
  Southern Christian Leadership Conference (founded 1957), provided leadership, meeting places and
  moral language for the struggle against segregation.
- **Poland:** the Catholic Church supported the Solidarity movement in the 1980s, providing an
  institution outside state control.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that religion can be a cause of social change.*

> Weber argued that Calvinist predestination produced salvation anxiety, which believers eased
> through disciplined work and reinvestment, creating the spirit of capitalism. **[AO1]**
> However, Kautsky argued that capitalism came first and Calvinism appealed to those already
> engaged in it, so religion followed economic change. **[AO3: alternative view]** The Iranian
> revolution of 1979 provides stronger evidence that religion can drive change, since clergy led
> a mass movement that replaced a monarchy with an Islamic Republic. **[AO2: application]**
> Religion can cause change, but usually in combination with economic and political conditions
> that make people receptive to it. **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Saying Weber thought Calvinism *caused* capitalism on its own; he described an elective affinity.
- Forgetting the "alternative views" bullet: Tawney, Kautsky and Marshall are needed for balance.
- Describing liberation theology without explaining why it challenges Marxism.
- Narrating the case studies as history without drawing out what they show about religion and
  change.
- Mixing up dates in the Iranian case; learn the sequence 1963, 1978, 1979.

## Exam technique

- For **explain** questions, give the mechanism (salvation anxiety leads to methodical work) and
  apply it.
- For **evaluate** questions, set religion as a conservative force (11.2) against religion as a
  source of change (11.3), using at least one syllabus case study.
- Use the case studies flexibly: the Evangelical movement shows influence within a democracy; Iran
  shows revolutionary change.
