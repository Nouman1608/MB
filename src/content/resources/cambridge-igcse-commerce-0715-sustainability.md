**Sustainability** means meeting today's needs without using up resources or damaging the environment in a way that makes life harder for future generations. In commerce, businesses are expected to think about the effect of their activities on the environment as well as on their profit. Section 6.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has two parts: the **purpose of sustainability in the supply chain** (6.1.1) and **actions to improve sustainability** (6.1.2). This guide covers both, with two worked examples and an annotated answer.

## What is the purpose of sustainability in the supply chain? (6.1.1)

The **supply chain** is the whole set of steps that takes a product from raw materials to the final customer: sourcing, making, storing, transporting, selling and disposing. The syllabus states the purpose of sustainability in this chain as **making commercial processes eco-friendly and environmentally responsible for future generations**.

This means that at each step a business asks how to:

- use fewer natural resources such as water, energy, metals and wood;
- cause less pollution and less waste;
- protect nature and the climate;
- keep resources available for people in the future.

There are also reasons that help the business itself, which you can use as supporting points in answers:

- **Lower costs:** less energy, packaging and waste often saves money.
- **Better image:** many customers prefer eco-friendly brands.
- **Meeting rules:** governments may set limits on waste and pollution.
- **Secure supply:** careful use of materials helps ensure they are still available.

The key phrase for the exam is **for future generations**. A good answer links an action to the idea that resources and a healthy environment should remain for those who come later.

## What actions improve sustainability? (6.1.2)

The syllabus lists nine actions. For each, learn **what it is**, **how it helps** and **a cost or limit**.

| Action | What it means | Benefit | Possible limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Using sustainable materials in production** | Choosing renewable, recycled or low-impact inputs such as bamboo, recycled plastic or sustainably grown cotton | Protects forests and reduces resource use | Can cost more; supply may be limited |
| **Implementing sustainable logistics** | Using cleaner vehicles, fuller loads, shorter routes, rail instead of road, and efficient warehouses | Lower fuel use and pollution; saves money | New vehicles and systems cost money |
| **Reducing packaging** | Using less, lighter or reusable packaging | Less waste, lower material and transport costs | Goods may be more easily damaged |
| **Selling eco-friendly products** | Offering goods made to cause less harm, such as energy-saving appliances or refillable items | Meets customer demand; improves image | Prices may be higher; claims must be honest |
| **Recycling and reducing waste** | Collecting and reprocessing used materials, and designing to waste less | Saves materials and cuts disposal costs | Needs systems, space and staff effort |
| **Reducing the need for returns and exchanges** | Giving clear size guides, good descriptions and quality checks so fewer goods are sent back | Fewer extra deliveries and less wasted stock | Needs investment in information and quality control |
| **Offering digital resale marketplaces** | Letting customers sell used goods through the firm's own website or app | Items stay in use longer; less new production | Quality checking and running costs |
| **Offering free repair services** | Repairing faulty or damaged products instead of replacing them | Longer product life; less waste; builds loyalty | Costs staff time and parts |
| **Water stewardship** | Using water carefully, cleaning and reusing it, and protecting local water sources | Protects a scarce resource; avoids pollution | Needs equipment and monitoring |

### Linking actions to parts of the supply chain

| Stage | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sourcing and production | Sustainable materials; water stewardship; waste reduction |
| Storage and transport | Sustainable logistics; efficient warehouses |
| Selling and packing | Reduced packaging; eco-friendly products |
| After sale | Fewer returns; digital resale; free repairs; recycling |

In an exam, show that you understand **where** in the supply chain the action takes place.

## Who gains from sustainable actions?

A sustainable action rarely helps one group only. Link your answer to the right party.

| Party | How it gains |
|---|---|
| The business | Lower costs from using less energy, packaging and materials; a stronger brand; loyal customers |
| Customers | Products that last longer, can be repaired and are safer for the environment |
| Employees | A cleaner, safer place to work and pride in the firm's reputation |
| The community | Cleaner air, water and land; less waste going to landfill |
| Future generations | Resources and a healthy environment remain available |

Sustainable actions can also have a **cost in the short term**, such as buying new equipment or paying more for recycled materials. Many pay back in the **long term** through lower running costs and loyal customers. A strong evaluation answer says this: the cost comes first, the benefit comes later, and the business must be able to afford the first stage.

### Short-term and long-term effects of one action

Take **recycling and reducing waste** in a factory.

- **Short term:** the firm buys sorting bins and a machine to crush scrap, and trains staff. Profit falls a little.
- **Long term:** less material is thrown away, scrap is sold or reused, and disposal fees fall. Profit rises, and the firm needs fewer new raw materials.

Compare this with **using sustainable materials**. The materials may cost more per unit, but the firm may attract customers who value its choice. The business must decide whether the higher price will be accepted.

## How should a business decide which actions to take?

A business cannot do everything at once. It should compare:

- **Cost** of the action against savings and benefits.
- **Size of the environmental gain.** Cutting a large source of waste matters more than a small one.
- **Customer expectations.** Some customers will pay more for eco-friendly products; others will not.
- **Type of business.** A clothing seller may focus on returns and sustainable materials; a bakery may focus on packaging and waste.
- **Honesty.** Claims must be true. Exaggerated green claims can be misleading advertising (see the ethics section of the syllabus).

## Worked example 1: reducing packaging (invented figures)

*A sports company sells 50,000 water bottles a year. Each is packed in 120 g of cardboard. A redesign uses 90 g. Cardboard costs $2.00 per kilogram.*

1. Saving per bottle: 120 - 90 = 30 g.
2. Total saving: 30 x 50,000 = 1,500,000 g = **1,500 kg**.
3. Money saved: 1,500 x 2.00 = **$3,000** a year.

**Judgement:** the company uses 1,500 kg less cardboard and saves $3,000. It also cuts the weight in each delivery. It should test that the thinner box still protects the bottle, because damaged goods would cause returns.

## Worked example 2: fewer returns (invented figures)

*An online clothes shop sends 2,500 orders a month. 8% are returned. Each return costs the shop $6 in extra delivery and handling. Better size guides cut the return rate to 5%.*

1. Returns before: 2,500 x 8 / 100 = 200. Cost: 200 x 6 = **$1,200**.
2. Returns after: 2,500 x 5 / 100 = 125. Cost: 125 x 6 = **$750**.
3. Saving per month: 1,200 - 750 = **$450**.

**Judgement:** the shop saves $450 a month and 75 fewer parcels travel back and forth, which reduces fuel use and packaging. This links to sustainability and to profit.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain two ways in which an online clothes retailer could improve the sustainability of its supply chain.* **[4]**

> It could **reduce packaging** [1] by using smaller, recycled boxes, which lowers waste and the weight of each delivery, so fewer resources are used and less fuel is burned [1]. It could also **reduce returns** [1] by giving accurate size guides, so fewer parcels travel back and forth, which cuts emissions and wasted packaging [1].

Why it scores: each action is named, then linked to a specific environmental benefit for this retailer.

## What are the common mistakes in 6.1?

- Defining sustainability as "making a profit for a long time". The syllabus focus is on the **environment** and **future generations**.
- Naming an action without saying **how** it helps the environment.
- Giving general statements such as "it is good for the planet" with no link to the business.
- Confusing recycling (reprocessing materials) with **reducing** waste (producing less in the first place).
- Treating free repair services as only a customer service benefit, with no mention of reduced waste.
- Forgetting that eco-friendly claims must be true.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **identify**: name the action.
- **Describe**: say what the business does.
- **Explain**: add how it reduces harm or saves resources, and link it to the business in the case.
- **Assess** or **evaluate**: weigh environmental gain against cost and customer reaction, then decide.
- In calculations, show quantities and units step by step.
