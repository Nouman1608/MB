## Questions

**1.** Define the term *biome*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between weather and climate. **[2]**

**3.** Climate data for Station N:

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temp (°C) | 3 | 4 | 7 | 10 | 14 | 17 | 19 | 19 | 16 | 12 | 7 | 4 |
| Precip (mm) | 70 | 55 | 60 | 55 | 60 | 65 | 60 | 70 | 70 | 85 | 85 | 85 |

**(a)** Calculate the total annual precipitation. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the annual temperature range. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify the most likely biome and justify your answer with data. **[2]**
**(d)** State which hemisphere Station N is in, with a reason. **[1]**

**4.** Explain how temperature and precipitation together limit the productivity of a biome. **[3]**

**5.** Suggest how **two** features of hot-desert plants adapt them to their biome. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why tundra soils store large amounts of organic carbon. **[2]**

**7.** Compare the productivity of the open ocean with that of a coral reef. **[3]**

**8.** A valley floor at 400 m has a mean summer temperature of 18 °C. Assume a lapse rate of 6.5 °C
per 1,000 m.

**(a)** Estimate the mean summer temperature at 2,600 m. **[2]**
**(b)** Trees on this mountain need a mean summer temperature of at least 6 °C. Estimate the height
of the tree line. **[2]**
**(c)** Estimate the new tree line height after 1.0 °C of warming. **[1]**

**9.** Explain why tropical rainforest has a much higher NPP than boreal forest. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how the seasonal movement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone produces the wet and
dry seasons of tropical grassland. **[3]**

**11.** Suggest why plant and animal communities may change in composition, not just location, as
biomes shift with climate change. **[2]**

**12.** To what extent can the distribution of the world's biomes be explained by latitude? **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** A collection of ecosystems sharing similar climatic conditions [1] and therefore similar
communities, which can occur on different continents [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place over hours or days [1]; climate is the
average pattern of weather over a long period, usually 30 years or more [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** **820 mm** [1]
**(b)** 19 − 3 = **16 °C** [1]
**(c)** Temperate deciduous forest [1]; mild summers (19 °C) and cool but not severe winters (3 °C)
with precipitation spread through the year (55–85 mm every month) [1].
**(d)** Northern hemisphere, because the warmest months are July and August [1]. **[5]**

**4.** Photosynthesis needs warmth and water, and enzymes work slowly in the cold [1]; where either
is scarce, growth is slow or seasonal, so NPP is low (cold in tundra, water in desert) [1]; where both
are plentiful year-round, photosynthesis is continuous and NPP is high [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two, each feature [1] with its link to the limiting factor [1]: deep roots reach
groundwater [1] [1]; wide, shallow roots absorb brief rainfall quickly [1] [1]; succulent stems store
water for dry periods [1] [1]; small or spiny leaves reduce surface area and water loss [1] [1]; short
life cycles complete growth and seeding after rare rain [1] [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Low temperatures and waterlogged or frozen ground slow decomposition [1], so dead organic
matter accumulates faster than it is broken down, building a large store in the permafrost [1].
**[2]**

**7.** Open ocean productivity per area is low because nutrients are scarce in the sunlit surface
layer [1]. Coral reefs are much more productive per area [1] because shallow, clear, warm water gives
plenty of light and nutrients are recycled tightly within the reef community [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Height difference = 2.2 km; temperature fall = 2.2 × 6.5 = 14.3 °C [1]; 18 − 14.3 =
**3.7 °C** [1]
**(b)** (18 − 6) / 6.5 × 1000 = 1846 m above the valley [1]; tree line ≈ 400 + 1846 = **2,250 m**
(to 3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** 1.0 / 6.5 × 1000 = 154 m higher, so about **2,400 m** [1] **[5]**

**9.** Rainforest has high temperatures and rainfall all year, so photosynthesis continues
year-round [1]; boreal forest has long, cold winters and a short growing season [1]; boreal soils are
cold and acidic with slow decomposition, so nutrient supply is also limited [1]. **[3]**

**10.** The ITCZ follows the overhead Sun, moving north and south through the year [1]. When it is
overhead, rising air brings convectional rain (wet season) [1]; when it moves away, the area comes
under sinking, dry air from the subtropical high (dry season) [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Species have different dispersal rates and tolerance ranges [1], so some move quickly, some
slowly and some not at all, producing new combinations of species and breaking existing
interactions such as pollination [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Award up to 7: arguments for latitude, up to 3; other factors, up to 3; a judgement [1].
- *For latitude:* insolation falls from equator to poles, controlling temperature [1]; the
  tricellular model places rising, wet air near the equator and 60° and sinking, dry air near 30° and
  the poles [1]; so biomes form broad latitudinal bands, such as rainforest near the equator and hot
  deserts near 30° [1].
- *Other factors:* altitude creates cooler biomes at low latitudes, such as tundra-like zones on
  tropical mountains [1]; ocean currents warm or cool coasts, as with coastal deserts beside cold
  currents [1]; distance from the sea makes continental interiors drier with larger temperature
  ranges [1]; mountains create rain shadows [1]; human land use has replaced many biomes with farmland
  [1].
- *Judgement:* for example, latitude explains the broad global pattern, but altitude, ocean currents
  and continentality explain many regional exceptions [1]. **[7]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing climate data without quoting values.
- Giving adaptations without linking them to water, temperature or light.
- Forgetting to add the valley height in tree-line calculations.
- Evaluating latitude without naming any other factor.
