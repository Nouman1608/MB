Condensed notes for the section 3.2 row **The rules of criminal law**: "rules and principles concerning general elements of criminal liability and liability for offences against the person, property offences and attempt". These notes cover the framework for using those rules; the elements of each offence are in their own notes. For explanation and a worked example, use the [rules of criminal law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-the-rules-of-criminal-law/).

## Sources at a glance

**Common law:** murder; unlawful act manslaughter; gross negligence manslaughter; assault; battery; insanity; automatism; intoxication; duress; duress of circumstances; self-defence (alongside statute).

**Statute:**
- Homicide Act 1957 s2 (diminished responsibility, as amended by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009).
- Coroners and Justice Act 2009 ss54–55 (loss of control).
- Offences Against the Person Act 1861 ss18, 20, 47.
- Criminal Justice Act 1988 s39 (charge and penalty for assault and battery).
- Theft Act 1968 ss1–8.
- Criminal Attempts Act 1981 s1.
- Criminal Law Act 1967 s3 and Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 s76 (force in self-defence and prevention of crime).

**Judicial development of statutes:** *Ireland; Burstow* (1997) on "inflict"; *Gomez* (1993) on "appropriation".

**No new common law offences:** *Knuller v DPP* (1973). **No code:** the Draft Criminal Code (1989) was never enacted.

## Burden and standard of proof

```
Prosecution ---- every element of the offence ---- beyond reasonable doubt
                 (Woolmington v DPP, 1935: the "golden thread")

Most defences:   D raises evidence  ->  prosecution disproves beyond reasonable doubt
                 (self-defence, duress, automatism, loss of control)

Reverse burden:  D proves on the balance of probabilities
                 (insanity; diminished responsibility, s2(2) HA 1957)
```

## Judge or jury?

| Question | Decided by |
|---|---|
| What the offence means; how to direct the jury | Judge |
| Whether there is enough evidence for a defence to be left to the jury | Judge |
| Whether evidence could show an act more than merely preparatory | Judge (s4(3) CAA 1981) |
| Whether the act was more than merely preparatory | Jury |
| Whether D was dishonest (*Ivey*) | Jury |
| Whether to find intention from foresight of virtual certainty (*Woollin*) | Jury |

## Specific and basic intent

| Specific intent (intention only) | Basic intent (recklessness enough) |
|---|---|
| Murder | Manslaughter |
| s18 | s20 |
| Theft | s47 |
| Robbery | Assault |
| Attempt | Battery |

Source: *DPP v Majewski* (1977). Main use: voluntary intoxication can negate specific intent but is no defence to basic intent.

## The ladders

**Offences against the person:**
```
Murder (life, mandatory)  ->  Voluntary manslaughter (partial defence)  ->  Involuntary manslaughter
s18 (life)  ->  s20 (5 years)  ->  s47 (5 years)  ->  battery / assault (6 months)
```

**Property:** robbery (life) -> theft (7 years).

**Attempt:** same maximum as the full offence (s4(1) CAA 1981).

## Problem-answer method

1. Identify the most serious offence and the victim.
2. State the rule and its source.
3. Give an authority for each disputed element.
4. Apply to the facts.
5. Move down the ladder if an element fails.
6. Consider defences and who must prove what.
7. Conclude.

## Stating a rule well

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Murder is killing someone on purpose." | "Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace with intention to kill or cause GBH (*Vickers*, 1957)." |
| "Theft is stealing." | "Theft is dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another with intention to permanently deprive (s1 Theft Act 1968)." |
| "He can say it was self-defence." | "If there is evidence of self-defence, the prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt." |

A strong statement names the source, gives every element and, where relevant, says who must prove it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Common law offence | An offence defined by judges in case law, not by statute |
| Legal burden | The duty to prove a matter to the required standard |
| Evidential burden | The duty to raise enough evidence for an issue to be left to the jury |
| Reverse burden | A legal burden placed on the defendant |
| Beyond reasonable doubt | The criminal standard, so that the jury is sure |
| Balance of probabilities | More likely than not; used where the defendant bears a legal burden |
| Specific intent | An offence that can be committed only intentionally |
| Basic intent | An offence that can be committed recklessly |
| Lesser included offence | An offence whose elements are contained within the offence charged |

## Exam traps

- Calling murder a statutory offence.
- Writing "the defendant must prove self-defence".
- Forgetting the balance-of-probabilities burden for insanity and diminished responsibility.
- Calling s20 a specific intent offence.
- Picking the offence by instinct instead of testing from the top down.
- Concluding without naming the offence.

## Self-test

1. Name two common law offences and two statutory offences in the specification.
2. What did *Woolmington v DPP* decide?
3. What is an evidential burden?
4. Name two defences where the defendant bears the legal burden.
5. To what standard must the defendant prove those defences?
6. Who decides whether an act was more than merely preparatory?
7. Which case created the specific and basic intent distinction?
8. Is theft an offence of specific or basic intent?
9. What is the maximum sentence for s20?
10. What is the maximum sentence for attempted theft?
11. Which case held that "inflict" does not need direct force?
12. Which case stops the courts creating new offences?

**Answers:** 1. Common law: murder, battery; statutory: theft, s18. 2. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt: the "golden thread". 3. The duty to raise enough evidence for an issue to be left to the jury. 4. Insanity and diminished responsibility. 5. On the balance of probabilities. 6. The jury, once the judge decides there is evidence on which it could be so found (s4(3) CAA 1981). 7. *DPP v Majewski* (1977). 8. Specific intent. 9. Five years. 10. Seven years, the same as theft. 11. *R v Burstow* (1997). 12. *Knuller v DPP* (1973).
