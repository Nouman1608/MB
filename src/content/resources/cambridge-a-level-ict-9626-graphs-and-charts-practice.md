## Section A

**1.** Name the most suitable type of chart for each task.

**(a)** Showing how the temperature in a greenhouse changes during a day. **[1]**

**(b)** Showing what fraction of a budget is spent on each of four items. **[1]**

**(c)** Comparing the number of students in each of six classes. **[1]**

**2.** State the purpose of a legend on a chart. **[1]**

**3.** Describe how to select two separate blocks of data in a spreadsheet to plot in one chart. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the value axis of a bar chart should start at zero. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Marlowe Garage spends 9000 pounds in one month: rent 1200, wages 3000, stock 4500 and other costs 300.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the total spent on each item. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the angle of the sector for wages in a pie chart. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe **three** formatting features that would make a pie chart of this data clear. **[3]**

**(d)** The garage wants to draw attention to stock. State how this can be done in the pie chart. **[1]**

**6.** The table shows sales in thousands of pounds for two shops.

| Season | Shop A | Shop B |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | 20 | 30 |
| Summer | 35 | 25 |
| Autumn | 50 | 40 |
| Winter | 45 | 60 |

**(a)** Name a chart that compares both shops for each season and give a reason. **[2]**

**(b)** Name a chart that shows how the sales of both shops change through the year and give a reason. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the total sales for each shop over the year. **[2]**

**7.** A chart shows rainfall in millimetres as columns and average temperature in degrees Celsius as a line on one chart. The rainfall values are up to 60 and the temperatures up to 19.

**(a)** Name this type of chart. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the temperature series should use a secondary axis. **[2]**

**(c)** State suitable minimum, maximum and interval values for the rainfall axis. **[3]**

**8.** A student creates a line graph for the number of pupils in each of five different classes. Explain why this is not the best chart type, and suggest a better one. **[3]**

**9.** A pie chart has the legend showing the sector names, and every sector is also labelled with its name and percentage. Explain why this is poor design and how to improve it. **[3]**

**10.** Describe **four** items that should be checked before a chart is finished and printed. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which a chart can mislead the reader. **[4]**

**12.** A manager asks for one chart that shows both the monthly sales (in pounds) and the number of staff on duty (a small number) for a year. Evaluate the use of a combination chart for this. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1. (a)** Line graph [1].

**(b)** Pie chart [1].

**(c)** Bar (column) chart [1]. **[3]**

**2.** It shows which colour, pattern or line represents which data series [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Select the first range [1], then hold the Ctrl key and select the second range [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The heights of the bars should be in proportion to the values [1]. If the axis starts above zero, small differences look much bigger than they are, which misleads the reader [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Rent: 1200 / 9000 = 13.33% [1]. Wages: 3000 / 9000 = 33.33% [1]. Stock: 4500 / 9000 = 50% [1]. Other: 300 / 9000 = 3.33% [1].

**(b)** 3000 / 9000 × 360 = 120 degrees [2] (one mark for the method, one for the answer).

**(c)** Any three from: a title saying what the chart shows [1]; segment labels showing the names [1]; percentages or values on the segments [1]; distinct colours [1]; a legend if names are not on the segments [1].

**(d)** Extract (pull out) the stock sector [1]. **[10]**

**6. (a)** A comparative bar chart [1], because it puts the bars for the two shops side by side for each season [1].

**(b)** A comparative line graph [1], because sales over time are shown as trends, and the two lines can be compared [1].

**(c)** Shop A: 20 + 35 + 50 + 45 = 150 [1]; Shop B: 30 + 25 + 40 + 60 = 155 [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Combination chart [1].

**(b)** The two series have different units and very different sizes [1], so on one axis the temperature line would be squashed at the bottom and its trend would not be visible [1].

**(c)** Minimum 0 [1], maximum 70 (or 60 or above the highest value) [1], interval 10 (any regular interval giving 5 to 10 steps) [1]. **[6]**

**8.** A line graph suggests a trend or change over time, but the classes have no natural order [1], so the line between the points has no meaning [1]. A bar chart is better because it compares separate categories [1]. **[3]**

**9.** The names appear twice [1], which wastes space and clutters the chart [1]. Remove either the legend or the names on the segments, keeping the percentages on the segments [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Any four from: the title is present and correct [1]; the axes have titles with units [1]; the legend is present if there is more than one series [1]; the scale minimum, maximum and interval are correct [1]; the right data is plotted and headings are not plotted as data [1]; labels do not overlap and are readable when printed [1]; the chart type matches the purpose [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Any two ways, each explained: a bar chart axis that does not start at zero [1] makes small differences look large [1]; a 3-D effect [1] distorts the sizes of bars or sectors [1]; a pie chart with too many sectors [1] is hard to read and compare [1]; no units on the axis [1], so the reader does not know what the values mean [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: one chart shows both series together so the manager can see whether sales rise when more staff are on duty [1]; a secondary axis allows two different units to be read on the same chart [1]; columns for one series and a line for the other make them easy to tell apart [1]. Against: it can look cluttered or confusing if not labelled well [1]; the secondary axis can make a link between the series look stronger than it is, depending on the scale chosen [1]; the reader must check which axis belongs to which series [1]. Conclusion: it is suitable if both axes are clearly labelled and the scales are chosen fairly [1]. Any six marks. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a line graph for categories that have no order.
- Leaving out axis titles or units.
- Plotting the heading cells as data.
- Putting two series with different units on one axis.
- Giving the angle or percentage without showing the calculation.
