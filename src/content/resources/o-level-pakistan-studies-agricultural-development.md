**Agricultural development is Section 4 of Paper 2 in Cambridge O Level Pakistan Studies 2059,
and the section with the most named crops, places and methods to learn.** It covers farming as a
system, where Pakistan's crops and livestock are found, the natural and human factors behind
production, the main irrigation methods, waterlogging and salinity, and what government has done
to raise output. Strong answers tie each crop to a district, a season and a reason.

## Why does agriculture matter so much in Pakistan?

Farming still produced roughly a quarter of Pakistan's GDP and employed well over a third of its
workers in the early 2020s, according to the Pakistan Economic Survey and the Labour Force Survey.
It supplies raw materials for textiles and sugar (Section 5) and major exports such as rice and
cotton goods (Section 6). It is also the largest user of the water studied in Section 2, so almost
any Paper 2 question can bring agriculture in.

## How does a farm work as a system?

Think of every farm type as a flow diagram.

| System | Typical inputs | Processes | Outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subsistence (e.g. a 3-acre plot in a Sindh rice village) | Family labour, oxen, saved seed, canal water | Ploughing, transplanting, weeding, hand harvesting | Rice and vegetables eaten by the family; a small surplus sold |
| Cash crop (e.g. a cotton farm near Vehari) | Hired labour, tractor, certified seed, fertiliser, pesticide, tubewell water, bank loan | Machine sowing, spraying, picking | Seed cotton sold to a ginning factory; profit reinvested |
| Livestock (e.g. a buffalo dairy near Lahore) | Fodder (berseem, maize), concentrates, vet services | Feeding, milking, breeding | Milk, calves, dung for fuel and fertiliser |

A good answer shows **feedback**: profit or failure in one season changes the inputs of the next.

**Other crops to locate, with one reason and one use:** apples in Quetta, Kalat and Swat (cold
winters for dormancy; eaten fresh); apricots in Hunza and Skardu (sunny, dry summers; dried for

Swat and the Peshawar valley (monsoon rain or irrigation; food and poultry feed); mangoes in
Multan, Rahim Yar Khan and Mirpur Khas (hot summers; fresh fruit and export); millet in the sandy
Thal and Thar margins (drought-tolerant; grain and fodder); oilseeds such as sunflower, rapeseed
and mustard on the plains (cooking oil); oranges (kinnow) around Sargodha and Toba Tek Singh (mild
winters, irrigation; fresh fruit and export); pulses such as gram in the Thal (little water
needed; protein food); tobacco around Mardan and Swabi (fertile, well-drained soils; cigarettes);
vegetables close to big cities (perishable, so grown near markets).

## How do kharif and rabi seasons shape production?

**Kharif** crops are sown in spring or early summer and harvested in autumn: cotton, rice, sugar
cane, maize and millet. They need heat and plenty of water from canals and the monsoon.
**Rabi** crops are sown in October to December and harvested from March to May: wheat, gram,
oilseeds. They grow in mild winter temperatures and rely on canal water, tubewells or winter rain
from western depressions. Cotton picking and wheat sowing overlap in November, which is why
cotton-wheat farmers face a labour squeeze.

## How do natural and human factors affect the three named subsistence systems?

**Traditional rice on small, fragmented holdings.** Seedlings raised in a nursery are transplanted
by hand into puddled, flooded fields, then harvested with sickles and threshed by beating or by
animals. Clay soils hold water well, but plots split by inheritance are too small and scattered for
machines, family labour is cheap but slow, and lack of capital limits fertiliser and pest control.

**Barani wheat on the Potwar Plateau.** Wheat depends on winter rain, which varies year to year.
Gullied land and thin soils reduce yields, and farmers rarely risk buying fertiliser. Small dams
and water-harvesting ponds help.

**Dates and vegetables under karez irrigation.** In oases such as those of Balochistan, a karez
carries groundwater from the foot of the hills through an underground tunnel by gravity, losing
little to evaporation. Dates tolerate heat and some salinity, while vegetables are grown in their
shade. Maintenance is labour-intensive, and nearby tubewells lower the water table so many karez
have dried up.

**Livestock.** On small farms, buffalo and cattle are stall-fed on fodder and crop waste. In
Balochistan and the northern mountains, sheep and goats are herded by nomads or by
**transhumance**: herders move flocks up to high summer pastures and back down to valleys or the
plains in winter. In Cholistan, herders move between rain-fed ponds (tobas) and canal areas as
water dries up.

## Which irrigation methods are used, and what are their trade-offs?

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inundation canal | Fills only when river is in flood | Cheap; no barrage needed | Summer only; unreliable |
| Perennial canal | Fed all year from a barrage | Year-round water for two crops | Seepage, waterlogging, costly upkeep |
| Karez | Gravity tunnel from hill aquifer | No fuel; little evaporation | Labour to clean; dries if water table falls |
| Persian wheel | Animal turns chain of buckets from a well | Cheap, simple | Small area; ties up animals |
| Tubewell | Electric, diesel or solar pump | Water on demand; lowers water table | Fuel cost; over-pumping; brackish water in places |

**The system from river to field:** dams store water (Tarbela, Mangla); barrages raise the river
level and divert water into canals (for example Sukkur, Guddu, Taunsa); link canals move water from
western rivers to eastern ones after the Indus Waters Treaty; distribution canals and watercourses
carry it on; field channels and bunds (earth ridges) hold it on each plot.

## What causes waterlogging and salinity, and what can be done?

Seepage from unlined canals and watercourses, over-irrigation and poor drainage raise the water
table. In hot weather capillary action draws water up and evaporation leaves salt crusts. Yields
fall and land can be abandoned. **Restoring land:** Salinity Control and Reclamation Projects
(SCARPs), begun in the 1960s, sank tubewells to pump down the water table; surface and tile drains
such as the Left Bank Outfall Drain in Sindh carry saline water to the sea; fields are flushed and
gypsum is added to sodic soil. **Prevention:** lining watercourses, laser land levelling, measured
water allocation, and sprinkler or drip irrigation.

## How has government tried to raise production?

Land reforms in 1959, 1972 and 1977 lowered the ceiling on how much land one owner could hold and
aimed to give tenants security, though much land stayed with large owners. Government has also
spread high-yielding seeds (the Green Revolution wheat of the 1960s), research at institutes such as
the Ayub Agricultural Research Institute in Faisalabad, extension training, credit through the
Zarai Taraqiati Bank, and subsidies for fertiliser, tractors and solar tubewells. **Evaluate:**
yields rose, but benefits went mainly to larger farmers, chemical use raised costs and polluted
water, and tubewell expansion has drawn groundwater down.

## Is Pakistan's farming sustainable?

**Possibilities:** high-value fruit and vegetables for export, better water use, livestock and
dairy growth, crop insurance. **Problems:** water shortage, heat waves and floods such as those of
2022, small holdings, debt, and falling groundwater. A sustainable answer weighs output now against
soil and water for the future.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a kharif and a rabi crop?**
Kharif crops such as cotton and rice are sown in summer and harvested in autumn. Rabi crops such
as wheat and gram are sown in winter and harvested in spring.

**What is a barani area?**
Land that depends on rainfall rather than irrigation, such as much of the Potwar Plateau, where
wheat yields rise and fall with winter rain.

**Why have many karez dried up?**
Tubewells pumping nearby have lowered the water table below the karez tunnel, and fewer people are
available to clean the channels.

**What is transhumance?**
The seasonal movement of herders and livestock between high summer pastures and lower winter
grazing, as practised in the mountains of northern Pakistan and Balochistan.
