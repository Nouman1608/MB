These notes condense quantitative skill **QS8** from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification: make calculations of elasticity and interpret the result. For full worked examples read the [QS8 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs8-quantitative-skills/), then test yourself with the [QS8 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs8-quantitative-skills-practice/). The [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) shows where elasticity appears elsewhere in the course. All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Price elasticity of demand (PED) | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price | %change in Qd / %change in price |
| Income elasticity of demand (YED) | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in income | %change in Qd / %change in income |
| Cross elasticity of demand (XED) | Responsiveness of demand for good A to a change in the price of good B | %change in Qd of A / %change in price of B |
| Price elasticity of supply (PES) | Responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price | %change in Qs / %change in price |
| Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger percentage than the cause (value above 1, ignoring sign) | |
| Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage (value between 0 and 1, ignoring sign) | |
| Unit elastic | Equal percentage changes (value of 1) | |
| Perfectly inelastic | No change in quantity (value of 0) | |
| Normal good / inferior good | Positive YED / negative YED | |
| Substitutes / complements | Positive XED / negative XED | |

## Rearrangements

```
%change in quantity = elasticity x %change in cause
%change in cause    = %change in quantity / elasticity
new quantity        = old quantity x (1 + %change in quantity / 100)
revenue factor      = (1 + %change in price) x (1 + %change in quantity)
```

## Condensed recall

- Work out each percentage change from the starting value, unless the question tells you to use another method.
- Keep signs: PED is negative, PES positive, YED and XED either.
- Size tells you how strong the response is; sign tells you its direction or the type of relationship.
- PED and revenue: inelastic demand, price and revenue move together; elastic demand, they move in opposite directions; unit elastic, revenue unchanged.
- A small YED is still a normal good if it is positive. Only a negative YED is inferior.
- The larger the XED (ignoring sign), the closer the substitutes or complements.
- PES rises with time, spare capacity and stocks; state the time period.
- An elasticity is an estimate from past data, measured holding other things equal. Real data often break that assumption.
- Forecasts using elasticity are more reliable for small changes than large ones.

## Worked reminders

- PED -1.4, fare cut 15%: passengers +21%; revenue factor 0.85 x 1.21 = 1.0285, so revenue +2.85%.
- PED -0.4, target fall in use 12%: price rise needed = -12 / -0.4 = +30%.
- YED +2.2, incomes +4%: demand +8.8%.
- XED +1.6, rival cuts price 10%: own sales -16%.
- PES 0.2 short run and 1.6 long run, price +25%: supply +5% then +40%.
- Price +10%, sales +5%, but incomes +6% with YED +1.5: income alone would give about +9%, so the price effect is roughly -4% and PED roughly -0.4 (an approximation).

## Interpretation checklist

1. Sign: what does it show (direction, normal or inferior, substitutes or complements)?
2. Size: elastic or inelastic, and how strongly?
3. So what: what does it mean for the decision in the question (price, tax, capacity, stock)?
4. Reliability: estimate period, other things changing, size of the change, time horizon.

## Exam traps

- Dividing the wrong way round (price change over quantity change).
- Losing a minus sign in a rearranged formula.
- Calling a positive but small YED inferior.
- Saying "elastic" for a PED of -0.8 because the number looks large.
- Assuming a rise in revenue means a rise in profit.
- Using the new value as the base of a percentage change.
- Forgetting to convert a percentage forecast back into a quantity when asked for the new sales figure.
- Interpreting PES without stating short run or long run.

## Self-test

1. Price rises by 8% and PED is -0.5. Find the change in quantity demanded.
2. A 5% price cut raises sales by 12%. Find PED and classify it.
3. A firm wants sales to rise by 10%. PED is -2.5. What price change is needed?
4. Incomes rise by 5% and YED is -0.8. Find the change in demand and name the type of good.
5. XED is +0.9 and the rival's price rises by 20%. Find the change in demand and state the relationship.
6. XED is -1.2 and the price of the related good rises by 10%. Find the change in demand.
7. PES is 0.5 and price rises by 12%. Find the change in quantity supplied.
8. A firm sells 3,000 units at 5.00. It raises its price by 10% and PED is -0.3. Find new sales and new revenue.
9. Price rises by 6% and quantity supplied rises by 9%. Find PES.
10. Give one reason why a PED estimated from two years' data may be unreliable.
11. Which YED values show an income-elastic normal good?
12. PED is exactly -1 and price rises by 5%. What happens to total revenue, approximately?

### Answers

1. -0.5 x 8 = -4%.
2. 12 / -5 = -2.4; elastic.
3. 10 / -2.5 = -4%, a price cut of 4%.
4. -0.8 x 5 = -4%; inferior good.
5. 0.9 x 20 = +18%; substitutes.
6. -1.2 x 10 = -12%; complements.
7. 0.5 x 12 = +6%.
8. Sales fall 3% to 2,910; revenue = 5.50 x 2,910 = 16,005, up from 15,000.
9. 9 / 6 = 1.5; elastic.
10. Other things, such as income or rivals' prices, may also have changed between the two years.
11. YED greater than +1.
12. It stays roughly the same, because the percentage changes in price and quantity offset each other.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I write PED with a minus sign?**
It is safest to keep the sign, since it shows the direction of the response. If you quote PED without it, say that you are ignoring the sign.

**Why are elasticity forecasts less reliable for big changes?**
Elasticity usually differs at different points on a demand or supply curve, so a value measured near one price may not hold far from it.
