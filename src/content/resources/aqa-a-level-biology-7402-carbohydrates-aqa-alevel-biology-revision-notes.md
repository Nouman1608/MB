## Sugars to recall

| Sugar | Type | Made of | Reducing? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Monosaccharide | | Yes |
| Galactose | Monosaccharide | | Yes |
| Fructose | Monosaccharide | | Yes |
| Maltose | Disaccharide | Glucose + glucose | Yes |
| Lactose | Disaccharide | Glucose + galactose | Yes |
| Sucrose | Disaccharide | Glucose + fructose | **No** |

Monosaccharides are C₆H₁₂O₆; disaccharides are C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (two monosaccharides minus one water).

## Glucose isomers

- Ring of five carbons and one oxygen; carbon 6 is the CH₂OH group outside the ring, on carbon 5.
- **α-glucose:** OH on carbon 1 **below** the ring.
- **β-glucose:** OH on carbon 1 **above** the ring.
- Carbons 2 to 6 are identical in both.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Isomers | Same molecular formula, different arrangement of atoms |
| Glycosidic bond | C–O–C bond formed by condensation between two monosaccharides |
| α-1,4 bond | Carbon 1 to carbon 4: forms chains in starch and glycogen |
| α-1,6 bond | Carbon 1 to carbon 6: forms branches |
| Microfibril | Bundle of parallel cellulose chains held by hydrogen bonds |
| Reducing sugar | A sugar that reduces copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution, giving a brick-red precipitate |
| Non-reducing sugar | A sugar that gives no Benedict's result until hydrolysed |

## Polysaccharides

| | Starch (amylose) | Starch (amylopectin) | Glycogen | Cellulose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monomer | α-glucose | α-glucose | α-glucose | β-glucose |
| Bonds | 1,4 | 1,4 and 1,6 | 1,4 and many 1,6 | 1,4, alternate units inverted |
| Shape | Helix | Branched | Highly branched | Straight chains in microfibrils |
| Where | Plant cells | Plant cells | Animal cells | Plant cell walls |
| Function | Energy store | Energy store | Energy store | Strength |

## Structure-to-function chain

Feature → property → consequence → function. Examples:

- Helix → compact → much glucose in a small space → storage.
- Insoluble → no effect on water potential → no osmotic water entry; does not diffuse out → storage.
- Many branches → many ends → many enzymes act at once → rapid glucose release for active animals.
- Straight β-chains → hydrogen bonds between chains → microfibrils → tensile strength, so cell walls resist bursting.

## Tests

| Test | Method | Positive |
|---|---|---|
| Reducing sugar | Add Benedict's solution, heat in water bath | Blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate |
| Non-reducing sugar | First test negative; boil fresh sample with dilute HCl, neutralise (e.g. sodium hydrogencarbonate), add Benedict's, heat | Brick-red precipitate |
| Starch | Add iodine in potassium iodide, no heat | Yellow-brown to blue-black |

Semi-quantitative Benedict's: time to first colour change, or filter, dry and weigh the precipitate. Control sample volume, Benedict's volume (in excess), water-bath temperature and heating time.

Mini reminder with new numbers: standards give 12 mg precipitate at 1 g dm⁻³ and 36 mg at 3 g dm⁻³, rising evenly. An unknown giving 30 mg lies 18 ÷ 24 of the way from 1 to 3, so 1 + 0.75 × 2 = 2.5 g dm⁻³.

## Exam traps

- CH₂OH drawn on carbon 1 instead of carbon 5.
- "Branching stores more energy." It speeds release.
- "Insoluble, so a good store" with no water potential link.
- Using Benedict's to "prove" maltose has been hydrolysed: both maltose and glucose are reducing.
- Forgetting to neutralise after acid hydrolysis.
- Calling the bonds between cellulose chains glycosidic: they are hydrogen bonds.
- Saying cellulose is indigestible "because it is strong" instead of enzyme specificity for α-bonds.
- Writing "turns red" for Benedict's without "precipitate", or "turns black" for iodine instead of blue-black.

## Self-test

1. Name the two monosaccharides in lactose.
2. Which disaccharide is non-reducing?
3. Describe the difference between α-glucose and β-glucose.
4. Which carbons are joined at a branch point in glycogen?
5. Explain why glycogen releases glucose faster than amylose.
6. Explain why storing glucose as starch does not affect the water potential of a cell.
7. Give two features of cellulose that give plant cell walls strength.
8. Why must the acid be neutralised in the non-reducing sugar test?
9. A sample gives a positive Benedict's result, and more precipitate after acid hydrolysis. What does it contain?
10. Give two variables to control when comparing sugar concentrations with Benedict's solution.
11. Why can't amylase hydrolyse cellulose?
12. State the colour of a positive iodine test.

### Answers

1. Glucose and galactose.
2. Sucrose.
3. In α-glucose the OH on carbon 1 is below the ring; in β-glucose it is above.
4. Carbon 1 of one glucose and carbon 6 of another (α-1,6).
5. Glycogen is highly branched, so it has many free ends where enzymes can hydrolyse glucose off at the same time; amylose is unbranched with only two ends.
6. Starch is large and insoluble, so it adds almost no solute particles and does not lower the water potential.
7. Straight, unbranched chains; many hydrogen bonds between parallel chains; chains grouped into microfibrils (any two).
8. Benedict's solution works only in alkaline conditions, so acid would stop a positive result.
9. A reducing sugar and a non-reducing sugar.
10. Any two: volume of sample, volume of Benedict's (in excess), water-bath temperature, heating time.
11. Amylase is specific to α-glycosidic bonds; cellulose has β-glucose with alternate units inverted, so its bonds do not fit the active site.
12. Blue-black.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sugars are reducing sugars in AQA A-level Biology?**
All monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose. Sucrose is non-reducing.

**What are the three polysaccharides in 3.1.2?**
Glycogen and starch, both made of α-glucose, and cellulose, made of β-glucose.

**How do you explain structure and function for full marks?**
Link a feature to a property, then to a consequence, then to the function, for example helix, compact, much glucose stored, energy store.
