This guide covers **International trade** in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The specification asks you to explain "international trade" and "patterns of international trade over time", and to evaluate the "advantages and disadvantages of international trade to developed, emerging and developing countries". Worked examples use invented countries and figures; the account of real trends is broad-brush, so check exact figures against current sources such as the Office for National Statistics or the World Trade Organization.

## 1. What international trade is

**International trade** is the exchange of goods and services across national borders. **Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries; **imports** are those bought from other countries. Trade in goods (cars, oil, food) is sometimes called visible trade, and trade in services (banking, insurance, tourism, education) invisible trade.

A useful measure of how much a country trades is **trade openness**:

Trade openness = (exports + imports) / GDP x 100

**Worked example 1.** Country A has exports of 300 billion, imports of 340 billion and GDP of 1,000 billion. Openness = 640 / 1,000 = **64%**. Small economies usually have higher openness ratios than large ones, because they cannot produce the full range of goods they use.

## 2. Why countries trade

- **Different resources.** Countries have different natural resources, climates and amounts of labour and capital. A country without oil must import it; a tropical country can grow crops a temperate one cannot.
- **Specialisation.** If each country concentrates on what it produces at lowest opportunity cost and trades for the rest, total world output can rise. This extends the specialisation and division of labour studied in Component 01 from people and firms to whole countries.
- **Economies of scale.** Selling to world markets lets firms produce on a larger scale, lowering average costs.
- **Choice and quality.** Consumers gain access to a wider range of goods and services.
- **Competition and technology.** Foreign competition pushes domestic firms to cut costs and innovate, and trade spreads new technology.

**Worked example 2: gains from specialisation.** With the same resources, Country B can produce either 100 tonnes of grain or 50 machines, and Country C either 30 tonnes of grain or 60 machines. With each splitting resources equally and not trading, B produces 50 grain and 25 machines, C produces 15 grain and 30 machines: a total of **65 grain and 55 machines**. If B makes only grain and C only machines, the world has **100 grain and 60 machines**, more of both. Trade lets each country consume a share of the larger total.

## 3. Patterns of international trade over time

The specification asks you to explain how trade patterns have changed. These are the main long-run trends.

**World trade has grown faster than world output.** For most of the period since the Second World War, the volume of world trade has grown faster than world GDP, so trade has become a larger share of economic activity. Falling transport and communication costs, the container ship, and successive rounds of tariff cuts all contributed. The trend has been interrupted by sharp falls during the global financial crisis of 2008 to 2009 and the pandemic of 2020.

**Emerging economies have taken a much larger share.** The most striking change is the rise of China, which joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and became the world's largest exporter of goods. Other Asian economies have also become major exporters of manufactures. The share of world trade accounted for by the older industrial economies of Western Europe and North America has fallen, although their trade has continued to grow in absolute terms.

**Global supply chains.** Production of many goods is now split across countries: components are made in one place, assembled in another and sold in a third. A growing share of trade is in parts and intermediate goods rather than finished products.

**Trade in services has grown in importance.** Advances in communications have made it easier to sell services such as finance, software and consultancy across borders.

**Regional trade blocs.** Much trade takes place within groupings such as the European Union's single market, where barriers between members are low.

**Developing economies and commodities.** Many low-income countries still depend on exports of a few primary products such as minerals, oil or crops, although some have diversified into manufacturing and services.

**The UK in brief.** The UK has shifted from being mainly an exporter of manufactured goods to having a large share of its exports in services, especially financial and business services. The EU, taken as a bloc, has remained the UK's largest trading partner, and the United States is the largest single-country partner. The UK left the EU in January 2020, with the transition period ending at the end of that year, which changed the terms of its trade with EU members.

**Worked example 3: describing a pattern from data.** Country D's exports by destination (invented):

| Destination | Share, year 1 (%) | Share, year 20 (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Europe | 58 | 41 |
| North America | 20 | 18 |
| Asia | 15 | 32 |
| Rest of world | 7 | 9 |

Asia's share rose by 17 percentage points, more than doubling, while Europe's fell by 17 points. Europe is still the largest market, so "Europe has become less important" is true only in relative terms: total exports may have grown so much that sales to Europe rose in value.

## 4. Advantages and disadvantages by type of economy

### Developed economies

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cheaper imports raise real incomes, especially of lower-income households | Import competition can close manufacturing plants, causing structural unemployment in particular regions |
| Large export markets for high-value goods and services | Dependence on long supply chains can cause shortages when they are disrupted |
| Economies of scale and competition raise productivity | Persistent current account deficits if imports outgrow exports |

### Emerging economies

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Export-led growth creates jobs in manufacturing and raises incomes rapidly | Exposure to falls in world demand, as in 2009 |
| Inward investment by foreign firms brings technology and skills | Rapid industrial growth can cause serious pollution |
| Larger markets allow economies of scale | Gains may be concentrated in some regions or groups, widening inequality |

### Developing economies

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Export earnings pay for imports of capital goods, medicines and technology | Dependence on a few primary commodities exposes export earnings to large price swings |
| Access to bigger markets than the domestic economy offers | New domestic industries may struggle to compete with established foreign firms |
| Cheaper imported consumer goods | Low-wage jobs and weak regulation can mean poor working conditions |

**Worked example 4: commodity dependence.** Copper makes up 70% of Country E's exports. If the world copper price falls by 30% and volumes do not change, export earnings fall by 0.70 x 30% = **21%**. A diversified economy with copper at 10% of exports would lose only 3%. This is why diversification is a key aim for many developing economies.

## 5. Evaluating the gains from trade

When asked to evaluate, go beyond the lists above.

- **Gains are large in total but unevenly spread.** Consumers as a whole gain from lower prices, while workers in industries facing import competition can lose heavily. Whether a country gains overall depends on support for those who lose: retraining, regional policy and benefits.
- **The type of economy matters.** Emerging economies with growing manufacturing have often gained most; developing economies dependent on one commodity face the greatest risks.
- **What is traded matters.** Exporting high-value services and manufactures brings more stable earnings than exporting a single raw material.
- **Time scale.** Adjustment costs come quickly; productivity gains from competition and scale take longer.
- **Non-economic costs.** Environmental damage and working conditions affect whether trade is consistent with sustainable development.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a country "loses" from trade because it has a trade deficit.
- Describing patterns without data or dates, or treating a falling share as a fall in value.
- Writing the same advantages for every type of economy instead of tailoring them.
- Ignoring the distribution of gains and losses within a country.
- Confusing services with goods in UK trade.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** trade patterns as cause and effect: lower transport costs led to more trade; China's growth shifted the pattern of world exports.
- In data questions, distinguish **percentage points** from per cent and **shares** from **values**.
- **Evaluate** by naming the type of economy, weighing who gains and who loses, and concluding on whether trade benefits the economy overall in the context given.
