## Why a business keeps ledgers

A transaction is first written in a book of original entry, such as a day book or the cash book (section 2.2). Ledger accounts then sort the same information by account. One page shows everything about rent, another everything about one customer. You can then see a total or a balance at any date without searching through every document. In this guide you record straight from a description of each transaction. Posting from a book of original entry uses the same debit and credit rules.

## The three ledgers (2.3a)

| Ledger | What it holds | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal ledger | The accounts for assets, liabilities, capital, drawings, income and expenses: bank, cash, shop fittings, loans, capital, sales, purchases, returns, rent, wages and so on | Shows the total of each type of item and the balance on each account. These are the figures later used for the trial balance and the financial statements |
| Receivables ledger | One personal account for each customer who buys on credit | Shows how much each customer owes and which invoices, returns and payments make up that balance. A business uses it to send statements of account, chase late payers and decide credit limits |
| Payables ledger | One personal account for each supplier who sells to the business on credit | Shows how much the business owes each supplier and when it is due. A business uses it to plan payments and to check supplier statements |

A cash sale has no customer account, because nobody owes the business anything. It is recorded only in the nominal ledger. The total of all the individual balances in the receivables ledger is what a trade receivables control account checks (Topic 3, section 2). The same applies to payables.

## Double entry principles (2.3b)

Every transaction has two effects. You **debit** one account (left-hand side) and **credit** another (right-hand side) with the same amount. The side depends on the type of account and on whether it is increasing or decreasing.

| Type of account | To increase it | To decrease it |
|---|---|---|
| Assets: bank, cash, shop fittings, customers (receivables) | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses: rent, wages, purchases, sales returns | Debit | Credit |
| Drawings | Debit | Not decreased by ordinary transactions |
| Liabilities: suppliers (payables), loans | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income: sales, purchases returns | Credit | Debit |

Use two questions for every transaction.

1. Which two accounts are affected?
2. Is each one increasing or decreasing, so which side does it go?

Bank and cash are assets. Money coming in is a debit and money going out is a credit. A customer owes you, so the customer account is an asset: a credit sale debits the customer and a payment from the customer credits it. A supplier is owed money, so the supplier account is a liability: a credit purchase credits the supplier and a payment to the supplier debits it.

## Layout of a ledger account

Each account has a debit side on the left and a credit side on the right. Each side has a date, a details column and an amount. The details column names the **other** account in the double entry, never the account you are writing in. An opening balance carried over from the last period is written as Balance b/d (brought down).

## Worked example 1: Kiran Kitchenware, May

Kiran starts a kitchenware shop on 1 May. Her transactions, with the two accounts for each, are below.

| Date | Transaction | Debit | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| 1 May | Kiran pays 40,000 of her own money into the business bank account | Bank | Capital | 40,000 |
| 2 May | Shop fittings bought, paid by bank | Shop fittings | Bank | 9,000 |
| 3 May | Inventory bought on credit from Bashir Metals | Purchases | Bashir Metals | 6,500 |
| 5 May | Rent paid by bank | Rent | Bank | 1,200 |
| 8 May | Goods sold on credit to Hina Caterers | Hina Caterers | Sales | 4,800 |
| 10 May | Faulty goods returned to Bashir Metals | Bashir Metals | Purchases returns | 500 |
| 12 May | Cash sales | Cash | Sales | 2,300 |
| 14 May | Hina Caterers returns goods | Sales returns | Hina Caterers | 400 |
| 16 May | Cash withdrawn from the bank for the till | Cash | Bank | 1,000 |
| 20 May | Bashir Metals paid by bank | Bashir Metals | Bank | 3,000 |
| 24 May | Hina Caterers pays by bank | Bank | Hina Caterers | 2,400 |
| 28 May | Kiran takes cash for private use | Drawings | Cash | 800 |
| 30 May | Wages paid in cash | Wages | Cash | 600 |

Look at 3 May. Purchases is an expense, which increases on the debit side. Bashir Metals is a supplier, a liability, which increases on the credit side. On 14 May Hina Caterers is an asset that decreases, so it is credited, and Sales returns is an expense that increases, so it is debited.

Bank and Cash are nominal ledger accounts. Bashir Metals is in the payables ledger and Hina Caterers is in the receivables ledger. The closing balances say that Kiran has 28,200 in the bank and 1,900 in cash, she owes Bashir Metals 3,000, and Hina Caterers owes her 2,000.

The remaining nominal accounts each need no balancing, because they have entries on one side only or one entry each. Capital is a credit of 40,000. Shop fittings is a debit of 9,000. Purchases is a debit of 6,500. Rent is a debit of 1,200. Sales is two credits, 4,800 and 2,300, giving 7,100. Purchases returns is a credit of 500. Sales returns is a debit of 400. Drawings is a debit of 800. Wages is a debit of 600.

Check your work: every amount in the analysis was entered once as a debit and once as a credit, so the debit entries and the credit entries both total 72,500.

## Balancing an account

1. Add up each side separately.
2. Write the larger total in the total boxes on both sides, level with each other.
3. The difference goes on the smaller side as Balance c/d (carried down) on the last day of the period, so that both sides add to the same total.
4. Bring the balance down on the opposite side, dated the next day, as Balance b/d.

A debit balance on Bank or Cash means money held. A debit balance on a customer account means the customer owes the business. A credit balance on a supplier account means the business owes the supplier. A credit balance on Bank means the account is overdrawn.

## Worked example 2: the two-question method

For each transaction, name the account debited and the account credited.

| Transaction | Debit | Credit | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| A customer settles an old invoice in cash | Cash | Customer | Cash asset up, receivable down |
| A loan is paid into the business bank account | Bank | Loan | Asset up, liability up |
| Insurance is paid by bank | Insurance | Bank | Expense up, asset down |
| The owner pays a further sum into the bank | Bank | Capital | Asset up, capital up |
| A customer returns goods bought on credit | Sales returns | Customer | Expense up, receivable down |
| Goods are bought for cash | Purchases | Cash | Expense up, asset down |

## Common errors

- Writing the name of the account you are in, instead of the other account, in the details column.
- Reversing the customer and supplier rules. A customer is debited when it buys on credit. A supplier is credited when the business buys on credit.
- Treating Bank like a liability. Money in is a debit.
- Posting a return to the Sales or Purchases account instead of Sales returns or Purchases returns.
- Making only one entry. Every transaction needs a debit and a credit, including a cash sale.
- Balancing with the totals not level, or bringing the balance down on the same side as the Balance c/d.
- Treating drawings as an expense of the business. They have their own account, debited when the owner takes money or goods.
