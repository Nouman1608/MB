## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by an enabling Act. **[2]**

**2.** Identify who makes statutory instruments and who makes bylaws. **[2]**

**3.** Give **one** use of Orders in Council. **[1]**

**4.** State what "ultra vires" means. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain **three** reasons why Parliament delegates law-making power. **[6]**

**6.** Explain the difference between the negative and affirmative resolution procedures, and **one** weakness of each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the role of scrutiny committees in controlling delegated legislation, and the limits of that role. **[4]**

**8.** Explain substantive ultra vires, using **one** case. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how the courts control bylaws on the ground of unreasonableness, using **two** cases. **[4]**

**10.** The (fictional) Clean Rivers Act 2026 gives the Environment Secretary power to make regulations "setting maximum permitted discharge levels for factories, after consulting organisations representing manufacturers". The Secretary makes regulations, without consulting anyone, that also impose fines on householders for garden bonfires. Advise a manufacturers' organisation and a householder on whether the regulations can be challenged. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why delegated legislation is described as undemocratic, and **one** argument against that criticism. **[4]**

**12.** "The controls on delegated legislation are inadequate." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** An Act of Parliament that gives a person or body power to make delegated legislation [1] and sets the limits of that power, including any procedure to be followed [1].

**2.** Statutory instruments are made by government ministers and their departments [1]; bylaws are made by local authorities and public bodies [1].

**3.** Any one: making emergency regulations, for example under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004; transferring functions between government departments; bringing Acts into force [1].

**4.** Beyond the powers granted by the enabling Act [1].

**5.** Any three developed reasons, two marks each: lack of parliamentary time, because Parliament cannot debate every detailed rule, so it passes a framework Act and leaves detail to others [1] [1]; expertise, because technical rules (for example on safety standards) need specialist knowledge available in government departments [1] [1]; speed, because a statutory instrument can be made in days, as with the 2020 coronavirus regulations [1] [1]. (Also accept local knowledge, flexibility, consultation.)

**6.** Under the negative procedure an instrument becomes or remains law unless either House annuls it within a set period, usually 40 days [1]; weakness: few such instruments are ever debated, so many pass unnoticed [1]. Under the affirmative procedure the instrument must be approved by Parliament [1]; weakness: Parliament can only accept or reject the whole instrument and cannot amend it [1].

**7.** The Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments examines instruments on technical grounds, such as unusual or unexpected use of a power or defective drafting, and reports to Parliament [1]; the Lords Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee draws attention to instruments raising policy concerns [1]. Limits: committees cannot amend or annul an instrument [1], and their reports are not binding, so they depend on Parliament acting on them [1].

**8.** Substantive ultra vires occurs where the body makes delegated legislation that goes beyond the powers granted by the enabling Act [1]. In *Customs and Excise Commissioners v Cure & Deeley Ltd* (1962) the commissioners made a regulation giving themselves power to decide the amount of tax due [1], which went beyond their power and was declared void (also accept *R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor*, 2016) [1].

**9.** The courts may declare delegated legislation void if it is so unreasonable that no reasonable body could have made it [1]. In *Strickland v Hayes Borough Council* (1896) a bylaw banning the singing or reciting of obscene songs in any public or private place was held unreasonable because it was too wide [1]. The courts are reluctant to interfere, however [1]: in *Kruse v Johnson* (1898) a bylaw against playing music near a house after being asked to stop was upheld as reasonable [1].

**10.** Manufacturers' organisation: the Act required consultation with organisations representing manufacturers, and none took place [1]. This is procedural ultra vires [1]; following *Aylesbury Mushrooms* (1972), the regulations may be held not to bind those who should have been consulted [1]. Householder: the Act gives power only to set discharge levels for factories, so fines on householders for bonfires go beyond the power granted [1]. This is substantive ultra vires, as in *Cure & Deeley* (1962) [1]. Both could seek judicial review in the High Court, and the court could declare the relevant parts of the regulations void [1].

**11.** It is made by ministers, civil servants or local bodies rather than by elected MPs debating and voting in Parliament [1], and most instruments under the negative procedure are never debated [1], so people are subject to laws their representatives did not consider [1]. Counter-argument: Parliament has democratically chosen to delegate the power and sets its limits in the enabling Act, and bylaws are made by elected local councillors [1].

**12.** Arguments that controls are inadequate: the negative procedure means most instruments are never debated [1]; Parliament cannot amend instruments, only accept or reject them [1]; scrutiny committees can only report [1]; judicial review depends on someone bringing a challenge, after the instrument is in force [1]; wide enabling Acts and Henry VIII powers leave little for the courts to find ultra vires [1]. Arguments that controls are adequate: the enabling Act sets clear limits [1]; the affirmative procedure forces approval of significant instruments [1]; courts have struck down instruments, as in *Public Law Project* (2016) [1]. Conclusion: the controls exist at every stage, but in practice they are weak in relation to the volume and importance of delegated legislation [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a control without explaining how it works.
- In scenarios, identifying "ultra vires" without saying whether it is procedural or substantive.
- Giving a case for one judicial ground and none for the other.
- In essays, listing advantages and disadvantages without judging the controls.
