This guide covers **4.8.2 Identification of common gases** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification,
for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.8, Chemical analysis, and is assessed on Paper 2 at both
Foundation and Higher Tier. It is not chemistry only, and there is no Higher tier only statement. The
specification gives four tests: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide and chlorine. You must know the **apparatus**,
the **reagent**, and the **result** for each.

Use the [identification of common gases revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-identification-of-common-gases-revision-notes/)
for quick recall and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-identification-of-common-gases-practice/)
to test yourself. The [chemical analysis study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-analysis/)
covers the whole topic; this page goes deeper on the gas tests.

## Why gas tests matter

Many reactions in the course give off a gas: metals with acids, carbonates with acids, hydrogen peroxide with a
catalyst, and electrolysis. To say what has been made, you test the gas. Each test uses a property that is
**distinctive**: it only gives that result for one of the four gases. A good answer states the **test** and the
**positive result** together, in the form "a lit splint gives a squeaky pop, so the gas is hydrogen".

## 4.8.2.1 Test for hydrogen

- **Test:** hold a **burning (lit) splint** at the **open end** of a test tube of the gas.
- **Positive result:** hydrogen **burns rapidly with a pop sound** (a "squeaky pop").

### Why it pops

Hydrogen burns in air with oxygen to form water. In a test tube it burns very quickly, and the rapid release of
energy makes the pop.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The small mass of hydrogen gas means the tube should be held **upside down** (or kept stoppered until the test),
because hydrogen is **less dense than air** and escapes upwards. Small amounts give a squeaky pop; a larger
amount gives a louder bang.

**Where you meet it:** metals with dilute acids (for example magnesium with hydrochloric acid), the cathode in
the electrolysis of aqueous solutions of reactive metal compounds, and sodium with water or alcohol.

## 4.8.2.2 Test for oxygen

- **Test:** insert a **glowing splint** (a splint that has been lit and blown out so it glows) into a test tube
  of the gas.
- **Positive result:** the splint **relights**.

### Why it relights

Oxygen supports burning. The splint is already hot and glowing, and in air it only smoulders. In a higher
proportion of oxygen, it burns faster and **re-ignites**. The result is **relights**, not "lights" and not
"burns brightly".

**Where you meet it:** the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide with manganese(IV) oxide as catalyst, the anode in
the electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid or sodium sulfate solution, and heating some metal nitrates.

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

## 4.8.2.3 Test for carbon dioxide

- **Test:** use an **aqueous solution of calcium hydroxide (limewater)**. Bubble the gas through it, or shake the
  gas with a little limewater in a stoppered container.
- **Positive result:** the limewater **turns milky (cloudy)**.

### Why it turns milky

Carbon dioxide reacts with the calcium hydroxide to form **insoluble calcium carbonate**, which is a fine white
solid that makes the liquid cloudy.

Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

Say **milky** or **cloudy**, never "goes white clear" or "goes colourless". Limewater starts colourless, and the
change is that it becomes milky.

**Where you meet it:** carbonates with dilute acids (for example calcium carbonate with hydrochloric acid),
fermentation, combustion of hydrocarbons, and the reaction of carboxylic acids with carbonates. The test for
carbonate ions in section 4.8.3 uses this test.

## 4.8.2.4 Test for chlorine

- **Test:** put **damp litmus paper** into the gas.
- **Positive result:** the litmus paper is **bleached and turns white**.

### Why it bleaches

Chlorine reacts with the water on the paper to form acidic, bleaching compounds. The paper is bleached, so it
loses its colour completely. The specification result is "bleached and turns white". Some practical guides
mention a brief red colour first; the key mark is **bleached white**.

**Where you meet it:** the anode in the electrolysis of molten chlorides and of aqueous solutions containing
chloride ions (for example sodium chloride solution).

**Safety:** chlorine is **toxic** and causes breathing problems. Experiments are done in a fume cupboard or with
small quantities, and the gas is never smelt directly.

## Summary table

| Gas | Test | Positive result | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen, H₂ | Burning splint at open end of the tube | Burns rapidly with a pop | Burns in oxygen to make water |
| Oxygen, O₂ | Glowing splint inserted into tube | Splint relights | Oxygen supports burning |
| Carbon dioxide, CO₂ | Bubble through limewater | Limewater turns milky | Forms insoluble calcium carbonate |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | Damp litmus paper | Bleached, turns white | Chlorine reacts with water on paper |

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: unknown gas from a reaction

*A student adds dilute hydrochloric acid to a grey metal. Bubbles form. A lit splint is held at the mouth of the
tube and there is a squeaky pop. Name the gas.*

The test is a burning splint at the open end, and the result is a pop, so the gas is **hydrogen**. The metal must
be reactive enough to react with dilute acid.

### Worked example 2: choosing between two gases

*A colourless gas is collected from a reaction. A glowing splint does not relight. The gas turns limewater milky.
Which gas is it?*

The glowing splint test rules out oxygen. The limewater test matches **carbon dioxide**.

### Worked example 3: electrolysis of sodium chloride solution

*A student electrolyses sodium chloride solution. State a test for each gas and the expected result.*

- Cathode: hydrogen. Test: burning splint, squeaky pop.
- Anode: chlorine. Test: damp litmus paper, bleached white.

### Worked example 4: write a full answer

*Describe how you would show that a gas produced when a solid carbonate is heated is carbon dioxide.*

1. Heat the carbonate in a boiling tube fitted with a delivery tube.
2. Lead the gas into a second tube containing **limewater**.
3. If the limewater turns **milky**, the gas is carbon dioxide.

## Practical tips

- **Collect the gas in a test tube** and keep a bung in until you are ready to test.
- **Don't confuse** the lit splint (hydrogen) with the **glowing** splint (oxygen).
- **Damp** litmus for chlorine: dry litmus is much less affected.
- **Fresh limewater** gives a clear result. Very long bubbling can make milky limewater clear again, so stop when
  it first goes milky.

## Exam technique

- Give the **test and the result** in the same sentence.
- Use the exact words: **lit splint**, **squeaky pop**, **glowing splint**, **relights**, **limewater**,
  **milky**, **damp litmus**, **bleached white**.
- When two gases are possible, state the test that tells them apart.
- Mention **safety** with chlorine when asked about hazards.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the splints: using a glowing splint for hydrogen or a lit splint for oxygen.
- Writing "turns cloudy white" with no limewater named, or "clear" instead of milky.
- Using dry litmus paper for chlorine.
- Saying the splint "lights" in oxygen, instead of relights.
- Saying hydrogen "explodes". The expected description is a squeaky pop.

## Putting it together: a model extended answer

*A student reacts calcium carbonate with dilute hydrochloric acid. Describe the observations and how to identify
the gas.*

> The calcium carbonate fizzes and the solid gets smaller as it reacts and dissolves. The gas given off can be
> identified by leading it through a delivery tube into limewater. The limewater turns milky because the
> carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide to make insoluble calcium carbonate. The equation for the reaction
> is CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂.
