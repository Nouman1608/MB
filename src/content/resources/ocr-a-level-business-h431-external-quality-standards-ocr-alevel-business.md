## What an external standard is

An **external quality standard** is a set of requirements for a product, service or process, written by an independent organisation outside the business. It is a published description of what good practice looks like, and a business can show that it meets it.

- **British Standards (BS)** are standards for the UK, published by the national standards body.
- **European Standards (EN)** are standards agreed across European countries. The specification notes that businesses designing products need to meet the standards of the region where they sell, so that the product is marketable there.
- **International Standards (ISO)** are agreed by an international body so that products and processes are compatible and safe across many countries.

Some standards cover **products** (for example, how strong a child's safety seat must be). Others cover **processes or management systems** (for example, how a business runs its quality system or its environmental management). A business may follow a standard voluntarily, or may be required to meet it by law, by a customer or by a trade association.

**Certification.** To show that it meets a standard, a business usually asks an independent body to audit it. If it passes, it receives a certificate and may use a mark or logo. The audit is repeated regularly. A business can also claim to follow a standard without certification, but customers have less reason to trust the claim.

## Why external standards matter

- **Customer trust.** A recognised mark tells customers that an independent body has checked quality or safety. This is valuable where the customer cannot judge quality alone, such as electrical goods or food.
- **Access to markets and contracts.** Many large buyers, public bodies and overseas markets require suppliers to be certified, so a business without the standard may be shut out.
- **Reduced risk.** Following a safety standard reduces the chance of accidents, claims and recalls.
- **Consistent systems.** The process of meeting a standard makes a business write down and improve its procedures.
- **Legal support.** In a dispute, evidence of following the standard may show that the business took reasonable care.
- **Supply chain confidence.** A business can rely on certified suppliers without testing every delivery.

## Costs and limits of external standards

- **Direct costs.** Fees for the audit and certificate, annual surveillance audits and any testing.
- **Internal costs.** Staff time for writing procedures, training and keeping records, and managers' time during audits.
- **Changes.** The business may need to change materials, equipment or layout to comply.
- **Bureaucracy.** Heavy paperwork can slow work and reduce flexibility.
- **Minimum, not best.** A standard sets a basic level. A certified business can still produce poor products if it follows procedures without commitment.
- **Small firms.** The costs are a larger share of revenue for a small business.

## Worked example 1: is certification worth it?

Tamsin Components (invented) makes fixings for the building industry. A large builder will give Tamsin a contract worth £120,000 a year in sales only if it holds a recognised quality-management certificate. Tamsin earns a 12% profit margin on sales of this kind. Certification costs £14,000 to set up (consultancy, training and the first audit) and £4,000 a year in audit fees.

Annual profit from the contract = 12% × £120,000 = **£14,400**.

Net annual benefit after audit fees = £14,400 − £4,000 = **£10,400**.

Payback on set-up cost = £14,000 ÷ £10,400 = **1.3 years**.

**Evaluation.**

- On the figures the investment is worthwhile if the contract lasts at least two years.
- The benefit depends on winning and keeping the contract. If the builder switches to another supplier, Tamsin holds a certificate with limited use.
- Other benefits are not included in the numbers: other buyers may also ask for certification, and the written procedures may cut errors and waste.
- Other costs are also missing: staff time spent preparing for audits and any changes to machines.
- The decision is stronger if the business already has good quality procedures, because setup will be cheaper.

**Judgement.** Tamsin should seek certification because the contract pays for it quickly and the standard opens other doors. It would be a weaker decision if demand from builders were falling or if the contract had a short term.

## Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Access to contracts; fewer claims; reputation | Cost of certification; paperwork |
| Employees | Clear procedures; training; safer working | Extra checks and record keeping |
| Customers | Confidence in quality and safety | Higher prices if costs are passed on |
| Suppliers | Clear specification; may win contracts if certified | Must meet the standard or lose business |
| Government and community | Safer products; fewer accidents | None directly |

## Benchmarking

**Benchmarking** is comparing a business's performance with that of another organisation, called the benchmark, usually the best in the industry, to find where it can improve. The business identifies the measures that matter, finds out how the benchmark performs, studies how it achieves its results and sets targets to close the gap.

Types of benchmark:

- **Internal:** comparing branches, shifts or teams in the same business.
- **Competitor:** comparing with a direct rival, although data may be hard to find.
- **Best practice (in any industry):** comparing a process with the best in any sector, such as a manufacturer studying how a supermarket manages deliveries.

Why businesses use it:

- to discover how far they are from the best;
- to set realistic, evidence-based targets;
- to learn proven methods rather than inventing them;
- to improve continuously and keep up with rivals;
- to support claims of quality to customers.

### Worked example 2: using a benchmark

Tamsin Components compares its figures with the best firm in its industry (the benchmark).

| Measure | Tamsin | Benchmark | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per unit | £14.20 | £12.80 | £1.40 |
| Defect rate | 3.2% | 1.1% | 2.1 percentage points |
| Orders delivered on time | 88% | 97% | 9 percentage points |

Tamsin's cost per unit is £1.40 above the benchmark. As a percentage of the benchmark: 1.40 ÷ 12.80 × 100 = **10.9%** higher.

Tamsin sets a target of cutting cost to £13.50 a unit. On 50,000 units a year, the saving is (£14.20 − £13.50) × 50,000 = 0.70 × 50,000 = **£35,000**.

The next step is to find **why** the gap exists. If the benchmark firm has a lower defect rate because it checks quality at each stage, Tamsin can copy that practice. Without that step, benchmarking is only a list of gaps.

**Evaluation.**

- Benchmarking gives clear, measurable targets and ideas that have worked elsewhere.
- Data from rivals may be unavailable or out of date, and different accounting methods can make comparisons unreliable.
- The benchmark business may have a different scale, location or product, so its results may not be reachable. A firm with large economies of scale will have lower unit costs which Tamsin cannot match.
- Copying the best may only bring the business level with rivals, not ahead of them.
- It needs management time and staff co-operation. It can be demoralising if staff feel judged.

**Judgement.** Benchmarking is most useful when a business has no clear idea of how good its performance is. It should be combined with its own ideas and with customer feedback.

## Evaluating the impact and importance

A strong answer:

1. states the standard or benchmark and what it covers;
2. gives the direct benefit (contracts, trust, lower costs) with figures if available;
3. gives the cost and the time to recover it;
4. considers stakeholders, not just the owners;
5. notes the limits (minimum level only, paperwork, scale of firm);
6. concludes with a judgement that depends on the business's customers, size and objectives.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to name or describe individual standards by number. The specification does not require it.
- Saying that a standard guarantees good quality. It shows that a system is in place and has been audited.
- Mixing up internal and external standards.
- Treating benchmarking as copying. It is learning from the best and adapting.
- Forgetting the cost of audits, paperwork and changes.
- Concluding that every business should seek every standard.

## Exam technique

For "explain" questions, define the term, say what it does and give an example in the business's context. For "evaluate", balance benefit and cost and give a judgement based on the case. Where figures are given, calculate payback or the gap to the benchmark and say what the result means.
