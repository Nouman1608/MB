This guide goes deeper into **3.3.10.2 Electrophilic substitution**, the second subtopic of section 3.3.10 Aromatic chemistry in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. Despite its title, the subtopic also includes free-radical attack of chlorine on benzene and methylbenzene, so this guide treats both kinds of reaction and, above all, how to tell which one a question wants.

Condense the ideas with the [electrophilic substitution revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-10-2-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [electrophilic substitution practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-10-2-practice/). The [aromatic chemistry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-aromatic-chemistry/) gives the full equations for each reaction; the bonding behind them is in the [bonding in benzene study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-10-1/).

## What the specification asks for

- Electrophilic attack on benzene rings gives **substitution, limited to monosubstitution**.
- Nitration matters in making **explosives** and **amines**; sulfonation in making **surfactants** and **sulfonamides**; Friedel–Crafts acylation is also an important step in synthesis.
- Outline the mechanisms of **nitration** and **sulfonation** on benzene and methylbenzene, and identify the products.
- Outline the mechanisms of **acylation** and **alkylation** using AlCl₃, and identify the products.
- Outline **free-radical attack of chlorine** on benzene (ring addition) and methylbenzene (side-chain substitution), and identify the products.
- Explain the **relative reactivities** of chlorine in the ring and in the side chain.

## One mechanism, four electrophiles

Every electrophilic substitution in this subtopic follows the same three stages. Only the electrophile and the group it attaches by change.

1. **Generate the electrophile** with a strong acid or with AlCl₃.
2. **Attack**: a curly arrow from the delocalised ring (start it on the circle) to the electrophile's attacking atom. The intermediate has the new group and an H on the same carbon, with a horseshoe-shaped partial ring and a + sign across the other five carbons.
3. **Restore the ring**: a curly arrow from the C–H bond back into the ring. H⁺ is lost and the full circle returns.

| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Electrophile and how it forms | Group added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitration | Conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄, about 50 °C | NO₂⁺: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ | –NO₂ |
| Sulfonation | Fuming sulfuric acid, warm | SO₃ (δ+ sulfur), present in fuming acid | –SO₃H |
| Acylation | RCOCl + anhydrous AlCl₃, warm | RCO⁺: RCOCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ | –COR |
| Alkylation | RCl + anhydrous AlCl₃ | R⁺: RCl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ | –R |

In the AlCl₃ reactions the catalyst is regenerated: H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl. In nitration, H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → H₂SO₄. Saying how the catalyst is re-formed is often a separate mark.

Methylbenzene follows the same mechanisms. Its methyl group releases electron density into the ring, so it reacts faster than benzene (nitration works at a lower temperature) and substitution happens mainly at positions 2 and 4.

### Worked example 1: predicting products

Name the main organic product or products and the electrophile for each reaction.

| Starting compound and reagents | Electrophile | Product(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Benzene, propanoyl chloride, AlCl₃ | CH₃CH₂CO⁺ | 1-phenylpropan-1-one, C₆H₅COCH₂CH₃ |
| Benzene, chloroethane, AlCl₃ | CH₃CH₂⁺ | ethylbenzene, C₆H₅CH₂CH₃ |
| Methylbenzene, conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄ | NO₂⁺ | 2-nitromethylbenzene and 4-nitromethylbenzene |
| Methylbenzene, fuming H₂SO₄ | SO₃ | mainly 4-methylbenzenesulfonic acid |

The acylation product is a ketone, because the C=O carbon is joined to the ring on one side and to an alkyl group on the other. Name it from the longest chain containing the C=O and treat the ring as a phenyl substituent.

## Using these reactions in synthesis

The specification emphasises that these substitutions are steps in longer routes, so expect them in multistep questions with yields.

### Worked example 2: benzene to phenylamine

Phenylamine is made from benzene in two steps. Step 1 has a yield of 75.0% and step 2 a yield of 80.0%. Give the reagents for each step and calculate the mass of phenylamine (Mr 93.0) from 15.6 g of benzene (Mr 78.0).

1. Step 1, nitration: concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid, about 50 °C. Product: nitrobenzene.
2. Step 2, reduction: tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, heated under reflux, then sodium hydroxide to release the free amine. Equation: C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O.
3. n(benzene) = 15.6 ÷ 78.0 = 0.200 mol.
4. Overall yield = 0.750 × 0.800 = 0.600, so n(phenylamine) = 0.200 × 0.600 = 0.120 mol.
5. Mass = 0.120 × 93.0 = **11.2 g**.

Multiply the step yields as decimals; adding or averaging them is a common error. The reduction step belongs to section 3.3.11: see the [amine preparation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-11-1/).

### Worked example 3: acylation then reduction

Suggest a two-step synthesis of 1-phenylethanol, C₆H₅CH(OH)CH₃, from benzene.

1. Step 1, Friedel–Crafts acylation: ethanoyl chloride with anhydrous AlCl₃. Electrophile CH₃CO⁺. Product: **phenylethanone**, C₆H₅COCH₃.
2. Step 2, reduction of the ketone with NaBH₄ (from section 3.3.8): C₆H₅COCH₃ + 2[H] → C₆H₅CH(OH)CH₃.

Acylation is the step that joins a new carbon chain to the ring, which is why the specification calls it important in synthesis. Alkylation also adds a chain, but the alkylbenzene formed is more reactive than benzene and can be substituted again, so a large excess of benzene is used to favour the monosubstituted product.

## Free-radical attack by chlorine

With chlorine and **ultraviolet light** and **no** AlCl₃, the reaction is a radical one, not electrophilic. The outcome depends on the compound:

- **Benzene: ring addition.** Chlorine radicals add to the ring; once the delocalisation is broken, addition continues until **1,2,3,4,5,6-hexachlorocyclohexane**, C₆H₆Cl₆, forms.
- **Methylbenzene: side-chain substitution.** A chlorine radical removes an H from the CH₃ group, so the ring stays intact. Products in turn: (chloromethyl)benzene, (dichloromethyl)benzene and (trichloromethyl)benzene.

### Worked example 4: which mechanism?

For each set of conditions, state the type of mechanism and the main organic product from methylbenzene.

- Chlorine, UV light: **free-radical substitution** in the side chain; **(chloromethyl)benzene**, C₆H₅CH₂Cl, Mr 126.5.
- Excess chlorine, UV light: further radical substitution; the product can be identified from Mr. Each H replaced by Cl adds 34.5, so Mr 161.0 is **(dichloromethyl)benzene**, C₆H₅CHCl₂.
- Concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄: **electrophilic substitution** on the ring; 2- and 4-nitromethylbenzene.

Read the conditions first: UV light means radicals and, for methylbenzene, the side chain; an acid mixture or AlCl₃ means an electrophile and the ring.

## Ring chlorine versus side-chain chlorine

The specification asks you to explain why chlorine in the side chain is more reactive than chlorine on the ring.

- **Side chain**, as in (chloromethyl)benzene: the C–Cl bond is an ordinary polar bond to a carbon outside the ring. Nucleophiles such as OH⁻ attack the δ+ carbon and substitute the chlorine, as for any halogenoalkane.
- **Ring**, as in chlorobenzene: a lone pair on chlorine overlaps with the delocalised ring electrons. The C–Cl bond becomes stronger and shorter, and the electron-rich ring repels approaching nucleophiles. Substitution does not happen under normal laboratory conditions.

A practical test: warm each compound with aqueous sodium hydroxide, acidify with dilute nitric acid, and add silver nitrate. Only the side-chain compound gives a white precipitate of silver chloride, because only it has released Cl⁻ ions.

## Common mistakes

- Starting the attack arrow on a ring carbon instead of the delocalised ring.
- Drawing the intermediate's horseshoe over all six carbons, or leaving out the + sign.
- Forgetting to show how the catalyst (H₂SO₄ or AlCl₃) is regenerated.
- Writing 3-nitromethylbenzene as the main product from methylbenzene.
- Using UV light when the question wants ring substitution, or AlCl₃ when it wants side-chain chlorination.
- Giving chlorobenzene as the product of benzene with chlorine in UV light; it is the addition product C₆H₆Cl₆.
- Adding step yields instead of multiplying them.

## Exam technique

- **Outline the mechanism**: equation for the electrophile, two curly arrows, the intermediate with its + sign, the products, and catalyst regeneration.
- **Identify the product**: give a name and a structural formula; for methylbenzene give both 2- and 4-isomers where both form.
- **Explain** the reactivity of chlorine: compare the two C–Cl bonds directly, mentioning lone-pair overlap with the ring and bond strength.
- **Suggest** a synthesis: give each step's reagents and conditions, and name each intermediate.
