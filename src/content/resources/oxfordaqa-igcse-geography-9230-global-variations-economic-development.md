This guide covers section 3.2.2.1, Global variations in levels of economic development, in Section B (The changing economic world) of Unit 2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The key ideas are: "There are global variations in economic development and quality of life" and "Various strategies exist for reducing the global development gap." Unit 2 is examined in Paper 2, which lasts 1 hour 30 minutes and carries 80 marks; Section B is worth 20 marks. This page goes deeper on this theme than the unit-level material. The next theme, [challenges of rapid economic development](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-challenges-rapid-economic-development/), builds on it with a case study.

## What the specification lists

- Different ways of **classifying** parts of the world by level of economic development and quality of life.
- Economic and social **measures of development**: gross national income (GNI) per head, birth and death rates, infant mortality, life expectancy, people per doctor, literacy rates, access to safe water and the Human Development Index (HDI).
- **Limitations** of these measures.
- The link between the stages of the **Demographic Transition Model** (DTM) and the level of development.
- **Causes** of uneven development (physical, economic, historical) and **consequences** (disparities in wealth and health, international migration).
- **Strategies** to reduce the development gap: investment, industrial development and tourism, aid, intermediate technology, Fairtrade, debt relief, microfinance loans.
- **An example** of how the growth of tourism in an LIC or NEE helps to reduce the gap.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Development | Improvement in the economic well-being and quality of life of a country's people |
| HIC | Higher income country |
| LIC | Lower income country |
| NEE | Newly emerging economy: a country with rapid industrial growth, now between LIC and HIC |
| GNI per head | A country's total income from all sources, divided by its population |
| HDI | A score from 0 to 1 combining life expectancy, education and income |
| Infant mortality | Deaths of babies under one year old per 1 000 live births |
| Development gap | The difference in wealth and quality of life between richer and poorer countries |
| Intermediate technology | Simple tools and methods that suit local skills and resources |
| Microfinance | Very small loans to people who cannot get loans from banks |

## Classifying countries

There is no single correct classification. Common ones are:

- **HIC, NEE, LIC.** This is the most widely used in the specification. HICs have high incomes and mature economies. LICs have low incomes and rely mainly on farming and raw materials. NEEs are in between, with fast growth based on manufacturing and services.
- **Income groups** used by the World Bank: low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income, based on GNI per head.
- **HDI groups:** low, medium, high and very high human development. At the time of writing, the United Nations has used cut-offs of about 0.550, 0.700 and 0.800 on the 0 to 1 scale.
- **Global North and South,** with a dividing line sometimes known as the Brandt line. This is a broad generalisation: Australia is in the south but is a high-income country.
- **"Developed" and "developing."** These words are widely used but are criticised because they suggest every country follows one path.

When answering, say which classification you are using and name examples.

## Measures of development

| Measure | What it shows | Typical pattern |
|---|---|---|
| GNI per head | Average income per person | High in HICs, low in LICs |
| Birth rate | Births per 1 000 people a year | High in LICs, low in HICs |
| Death rate | Deaths per 1 000 people a year | Can be low in NEEs and some LICs because of young populations, so it is not a clear measure |
| Infant mortality | Deaths under age 1 per 1 000 births | High in LICs |
| Life expectancy | Average years a person is expected to live | Low in LICs, high in HICs |
| People per doctor | Healthcare access | Many thousands per doctor in LICs; a few hundred in HICs |
| Literacy rate | Percentage who can read and write | Lower in LICs, especially for women |
| Access to safe water | Percentage with clean water | Lower in LICs |
| HDI | Combines life expectancy, education and income | Low in LICs, high in HICs |

Economic measures (such as GNI) show wealth. Social measures (such as life expectancy and literacy) show quality of life. A good answer uses both.

### Limitations of the measures

- **Averages hide inequality.** A high GNI per head can hide extreme poverty if wealth is held by a few people.
- **Different units and costs.** Incomes in US dollars do not show what money can buy locally; prices differ between countries.
- **Informal economy.** Work in unregistered businesses and subsistence farming is not counted.
- **Poor data quality.** Some countries lack reliable surveys or have disputes about census data.
- **One measure is not enough.** GNI says nothing about freedom, safety, the environment or equality.
- **HDI limits.** It leaves out inequality, the environment and human rights, and can hide differences within a country.
- **Death rate can mislead.** A young population has a low death rate even if healthcare is poor.

## Link to the Demographic Transition Model

The DTM describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Population growth | Typical development |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | High | Low | Pre-industrial (few countries now) |
| 2 | High | Falling fast | Rapid | Many LICs |
| 3 | Falling | Low | Still growing | NEEs |
| 4 | Low | Low | Slow or stable | Many HICs |
| 5 | Very low | Slightly higher | Falling | Some ageing HICs |

Death rates fall first because of better food, water, sanitation and medicine. Birth rates fall later as incomes rise, more women get education, more people move to cities and contraception becomes available.

## Causes of uneven development

**Physical.** Climate extremes such as drought, hazards such as earthquakes and cyclones, a lack of natural resources, tropical diseases such as malaria, poor soils, and being landlocked (no sea port) can all hold back development.

**Economic.** Dependence on exporting raw materials, whose prices fluctuate and are low compared with manufactured goods; high levels of debt; a lack of investment; and unfair trade rules.

**Historical.** Colonial rule shaped many countries' economies to supply raw materials to colonial powers, with little industry developed locally. Slavery removed millions of people. Borders drawn by colonial powers split ethnic groups and contributed to conflict. Wars and unstable governments discourage investment.

Causes often combine and reinforce each other, creating a cycle of poverty.

## Consequences of uneven development

- **Disparities in wealth:** a gap in income, housing and services.
- **Disparities in health:** shorter life expectancy, higher infant mortality, lack of clean water and medical care, and more disease.
- **International migration:** people move from poorer to richer countries to find work, safety or better services. This can bring remittances (money sent home) but may lead to a "brain drain" of skilled workers. Receiving countries gain workers but may feel strain on services.

## Strategies to reduce the development gap

| Strategy | How it helps | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Investment | Money from foreign companies or governments builds factories, roads and power, creating jobs | Profits may leave the country; investors may leave |
| Industrial development and tourism | Provide jobs and exports | Jobs can be low-paid; tourism is seasonal and vulnerable |
| Aid | Money, food, expertise or equipment from other countries or charities; saves lives and builds schools and clinics | May create dependency, be tied to conditions or be lost to corruption |
| Intermediate technology | Simple, cheap tools such as hand pumps or solar lamps that local people can make and repair | Limited scale |
| Fairtrade | Farmers receive a guaranteed minimum price and a premium for community projects | Covers a small share of trade |
| Debt relief | Cancelling or reducing debt frees government money for health and education | Conditions may be attached; may not stop future borrowing |
| Microfinance loans | Small loans let people start small businesses, particularly women | Interest can be high; some borrowers cannot repay |

## Example: tourism in an LIC or NEE

Your class may use a different country, such as the Gambia, Nepal, Jamaica or Costa Rica. This example uses Kenya in general terms. Check it against your own notes.

- **Growth:** tourism is one of Kenya's major sources of foreign income. Visitors come for safaris in parks such as the Maasai Mara, for coastal beaches and for wildlife.
- **Benefits:** jobs in hotels, lodges, transport and as guides; foreign currency that pays for imports; money spent locally supports farmers, craft sellers and shops (the multiplier effect); entrance fees help to pay for conservation; roads, airports and services built for tourists also help local people.
- **Problems:** some profits leave the country if lodges and tour companies are foreign-owned (leakage); work can be seasonal and low-paid; pressure on water and wildlife; and tourism falls sharply after events such as security scares or global health crises.
- **How it helps close the gap:** by providing jobs and foreign income, and by spreading benefits to rural areas, tourism helps raise living standards. It works best when local people own businesses and conservation is protected.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain why measures such as GNI per head may not give a full picture of a country's level of development. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "GNI per head is an average, so it does not show how wealth is shared. A country could have a high average because of a few very rich people while most remain poor. It also leaves out the informal economy and subsistence farming, so the true level of income may be higher or lower. Finally it only measures income. It says nothing about health, education or safety, so a social measure such as life expectancy or the HDI is also needed."

The answer gives three distinct limitations, each explained.

## Common mistakes

- **Using "developed" and "developing" without examples.** Name countries and use HIC, NEE and LIC.
- **Treating one measure as enough.** Combine economic and social measures.
- **Saying a low death rate always means a rich country.** Young populations have low death rates.
- **Listing strategies without limits.** Each strategy has strengths and weaknesses.
- **Saying aid always solves problems.** Aid can create dependency or be misused.
- **Describing the example in general terms.** Include named places, jobs and evidence.
