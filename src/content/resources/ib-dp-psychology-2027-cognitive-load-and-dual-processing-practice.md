> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on cognitive load theory and dual processing theory in the cognitive approach (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. Question 10 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [cognitive load and dual processing study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-cognitive-load-and-dual-processing/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-cognitive-load-and-dual-processing-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain cognitive load theory, and give one example. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish between intrinsic load and extraneous load. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the dual processing model of thinking. **[4]**

**4.** Explain one cognitive bias, and say how the dual processing model accounts for it. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> A teacher gives students a page with a diagram of the heart in the centre and numbered facts in a list at the bottom of the page. She also reads each fact aloud while students read it silently. Students complain that they keep losing their place.

**(a)** Identify two features of the lesson that could add extraneous load. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest one change, and explain why it would reduce cognitive load. **[3]**

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> A shop has a sign: "Was 80, now 60: a quarter off". A shopper who is rushing, carrying heavy bags and checking her phone buys two items. Later she works out that a similar item at another shop cost 55 without any discount.

**(a)** Use dual processing to explain her decision. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way the shop could use the theory to encourage careful thinking, or one way the shopper could avoid the error. **[3]**

**7.** A researcher wants to test the split-attention effect. Describe how an experiment could be designed. **[4]**

**8.** Explain one limitation of measuring cognitive load. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate dual processing theory as an explanation of decision-making. **[6]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the contribution of cognitive models, including cognitive load theory and dual processing theory, to our understanding of human cognition. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Cognitive load theory proposes that learning is limited by the capacity of working memory **[1]**. Tasks place demands on working memory: intrinsic load from the complexity of the material, extraneous load from poor presentation and germane load from building schemas **[1]**. If total load exceeds capacity, learning suffers **[1]**. Example, any relevant one, such as a diagram and its labels on separate pages forcing students to hold and link them, so extraneous load rises **[1]**.

**2.** Intrinsic load is the demand set by the complexity of the material and the learner's prior knowledge **[1]**, for example a multi-step equation is harder for a novice **[1]**. Extraneous load is the demand caused by how the material is presented, which does not contribute to learning **[1]**, for example confusing layout.

**3.** The model proposes two modes of thinking **[1]**. System 1 is fast, automatic and intuitive and uses heuristics **[1]**. System 2 is slow, effortful and analytical **[1]**. System 1 is efficient but can produce systematic errors, which System 2 can correct if it is engaged **[1]**.

**4.** Any one bias. For example anchoring bias: judgements stay close to an initial reference value even if it is irrelevant **[1]**; Tversky and Kahneman (1974) found estimates shifted towards a number from a rigged wheel **[1]**. Dual processing: System 1 uses the anchor as a quick starting point and System 2 does not adjust enough **[1]**, particularly when attention is limited **[1]**. Equally creditable: confirmation bias, availability.

**5.** (a) Two features, up to **[2]**: the diagram and facts are separated, so students must keep searching to link them (split attention) **[1]**; spoken facts repeat the written facts, which is redundancy **[1]**. Credit also a long list shown all at once (high intrinsic load).

(b) Any one change **[1]**: put the numbered facts as labels on the diagram **[1]**; or remove the spoken repetition **[1]**; or reveal parts of the heart one stage at a time **[1]**. Explanation that it frees working memory capacity for building schemas **[1]**, and links to the scenario **[1]**. Maximum **[3]**.

**6.** (a) The shopper relied on System 1 and the heuristic "a discount means a bargain" **[1]**. Rushing, carrying bags and checking her phone loaded her working memory, so System 2 did not check the price against alternatives **[1]**. This led to a decision that looked good but cost more than the alternative **[1]**.

(b) Either: the shop could use clear unit pricing or a comparison with the usual price, so that System 2 needs less effort **[1]**, reducing the load of the calculation **[1]**, and encouraging a check **[1]**. Or the shopper could slow down, reduce distractions, and compare prices before buying **[1]**, giving System 2 capacity **[1]**, and avoiding reliance on the heuristic **[1]**.

**7.** Independent variable: layout, with an integrated version (text within the diagram) and a split version (text separate), allocated at random to two groups **[1]**. Dependent variable: score on a test of understanding, and possibly time taken or rated mental effort **[1]**. Controls: same content, same time, same prior knowledge test **[1]**. Prediction: the integrated group will do better because less working memory is used to link the sources **[1]**.

**8.** Cognitive load cannot be observed directly, so it is measured indirectly **[1]**. Self-rated effort is subjective and affected by how people use the scale **[1]**; secondary-task methods may interfere with the main task, and physiological measures like pupil dilation are affected by other factors such as light or emotion **[1]**. Award up to **[3]**.

**9.** Description (up to 2): two modes of thinking, fast and slow, with heuristics **[1]**; the model explains biases **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4): supported by evidence such as the bat and ball problem and the load and food choice study **[1]**; widely applicable in health and economic decisions **[1]**; the systems are descriptive labels, not separate brain systems **[1]**; hard to falsify because any result can be explained afterwards **[1]**; lab tasks and student samples limit generalisation **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**10.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Cognitive models make internal processing testable and predictive; cognitive load theory explains design effects on learning; dual processing explains bias **[2]**.
- Evidence: controlled experiments support the effects, but lab tasks and short-term measures limit ecological validity **[2]**.
- Measurement: constructs like load and "system used" are inferred indirectly, so the models are hard to falsify **[2]**.
- Bias: cognitive biases in participants and in research; student and Western samples **[2]**.
- Causality: experiments support cause and effect for specific design changes, but real classrooms and decisions involve more variables **[2]**.
- Perspective: cognitive models describe processes but may neglect biology and culture, so other approaches add explanation **[2]**.
- Change: applications such as redesigning lessons, defaults and nudges show practical value, with the question of whether they last **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the models, for example that they are useful, testable descriptions whose power increases when combined with other approaches **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** defining working memory without explaining overload.
- **Question 5(b):** suggesting a change without explaining the effect on load.
- **Question 6(a):** naming System 1 without using details in the scenario.
- **Question 10:** describing models and not evaluating them, or only one concept.
