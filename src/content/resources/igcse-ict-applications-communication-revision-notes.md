## Two groups of communication media

**Static/print-style** — newsletters, posters. No device or internet needed to view; cannot be updated once distributed; no interactivity.

**Digital/interactive** — websites, multimedia presentations, audio, video, media streaming, ePublications. Can combine text/image/sound/video; usually updatable after publication; often allow some interaction; require a suitable device (streaming also requires a working internet connection).

## Streaming vs ePublication — the one fact that answers most questions

**Media streaming:** plays audio/video continuously as data arrives, without downloading the full file first — needs an *ongoing* connection, no local storage for the full file.

**ePublication:** downloaded as a complete file, then accessed offline — needs storage space, but no ongoing connection once downloaded.

## Worked comparison: newsletter vs website vs multimedia presentation

A school communicating termly achievements to parents:

- **Newsletter** — reaches parents with no device/internet needed; fixed once distributed, cannot be corrected; ongoing print/distribution cost.
- **Website** — updatable any time, more content, can link to further detail; only reaches parents with a device and internet; audience must actively visit it.
- **Multimedia presentation** — rich, engaging, suits an in-person event (parents' evening); far less accessible independently afterward than a website.

No option is unconditionally best — the right choice depends on audience tech access, whether content needs later updates, and whether delivery is passive (arrives to the audience) or active (audience must seek it out).

## Mobile communication

SMS (short text over the mobile network), phone calls (mobile network's voice channel), **VoIP**, video calls, internet access.

**VoIP vs a standard call — the examinable distinction:** VoIP carries voice as **data over the internet**, not over a dedicated voice channel — needs a working internet/wi-fi connection, can be much cheaper (especially internationally), but depends on connection quality in a way a standard mobile voice call does not. Video calls extend the same internet-based approach with a live video feed.

## How 6.1 connects to earlier topics

Video calls and VoIP need the camera, microphone and network connection from Topics 2 and 4; media streaming depends on the same working internet connection that cloud computing (4.1) relies on. Treat 6.1 as an *application* of that earlier infrastructure, not a separate block of new facts — the same requirements (suitable device, sufficient bandwidth, working connection) recur across both topics.


## Worked example: choosing between VoIP and a standard call

A small business wants to hold a weekly call with an overseas supplier. Using VoIP over the office's existing internet connection avoids the much higher per-minute cost of an international call over the standard mobile/landline voice network, and the business already has the internet connection needed to support it. The trade-off it accepts is that call quality now depends on the internet connection's reliability and bandwidth at the time of the call -- if the connection is poor or congested, the VoIP call may suffer from delay, dropped audio or reduced quality in a way a standard voice call over a dedicated channel would not, since a standard call does not compete with other internet traffic for the same bandwidth.

## Recognising media streaming and ePublications in a scenario

Cambridge often describes a scenario without naming "streaming" or "ePublication" directly, expecting you to recognise which is which from what happens to the data. A service that lets a user "start watching immediately while the rest of the video keeps loading in the background" is describing streaming -- data is being played as it arrives. A service that requires a user to "download the full book before it can be opened, after which no internet connection is needed to read it" is describing an ePublication -- the complete file is stored locally before use. Anchoring an answer to this data-handling distinction, rather than to surface features like "video" versus "text," is what correctly identifies which term the scenario is testing.

## Exam traps

- Declaring one medium simply "better" instead of naming the specific trade-off (a poster's no-device-needed advantage is exactly why it can't be updated).
- Confusing streaming with downloading — streaming does not store the whole file; an ePublication does.
- Naming VoIP without explaining *why* it differs from a standard call (data over the internet, not a dedicated voice channel) — exam questions test the distinction, not just the name.
- Assuming VoIP is always cheaper without conditions — it depends on connection quality and availability, unlike a standard mobile voice channel.
- Treating video calls as unrelated to VoIP, when they extend the same internet-based, data-carried approach with an added video feed.

## Self-test

1. Name one advantage and one disadvantage of a poster as a communication medium.
2. State the key difference between media streaming and an ePublication, in terms of what happens to the data.
3. Why might a school prefer a website over a newsletter for termly updates, and what does the school lose by choosing a website instead?
4. What specifically distinguishes VoIP from a traditional phone call?
5. Name one syllabus topic that video calls and VoIP both depend on.
6. Why is a multimedia presentation less useful than a website for parents wanting to review the content again later?

**Answers:** 1. Advantage: no device or internet connection needed to view it; disadvantage: cannot be updated once distributed. 2. Streaming plays the content as data arrives without storing the full file; an ePublication is downloaded as a complete file before/as it is used. 3. A website can be updated at any time and hold more content, with links to further detail; the school loses reach to parents without a suitable device or internet access, and updates require parents to actively visit rather than receiving it directly. 4. VoIP carries voice as data over the internet, not over a dedicated voice channel. 5. Topics 2 (hardware: camera, microphone) and 4 (networks/connection). 6. A multimedia presentation is generally delivered live at an event and is far less practical for parents to revisit independently afterward compared with an always-available website.
