## Questions

**1.** State two reasons why producers compete with each other in a market economy. **[2]**

**2.** A small firm sells 300 pots of hand cream a week at £4.50 each. Its fixed cost is £450 a week and its variable cost is £2.00 per pot. Calculate its total cost, average cost, total revenue and profit. **[4]**

**3.** The table shows the market for flowers in Town X (invented) before and after new stalls open.

| Price (£) | Demand | Old supply | New supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 48 | 24 | 36 |
| 8 | 42 | 30 | 42 |
| 9 | 36 | 36 | 48 |
| 10 | 30 | 42 | 54 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium price and quantity before and after the new stalls open. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the equilibrium price. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse how the opening of new stalls changes the price. **[3]**

**4.** A tool firm made 5,200 tools with 130 workers in year 1 and 6,000 tools with 125 workers in year 2.

**(a)** Work out how many tools each worker made in year 1 and in year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the percentage change in productivity between the two years. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how higher productivity could help the firm compete. **[2]**

**5.** In Country C (invented) four bus firms have annual sales of £42m (Firm A), £36m (B), £24m (C) and £18m (D).

**(a)** Calculate Firm A's market share. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the share held by A and B together. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this market might be described as an oligopoly and one way it differs from a competitive market. **[3]**

**6.** A pet-grooming business has fixed cost of £900 a week and variable cost of £3.50 per visit. It charges £8 a visit.

**(a)** For 400 visits, calculate total cost, average cost, total revenue and profit. **[4]**
**(b)** For 100 visits, calculate the profit or loss. **[2]**

**7.** A packaging firm doubles output from 10,000 to 20,000 boxes. Total cost rises from £80,000 to £132,000.

**(a)** Calculate average cost at each output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in average cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one source of economies of scale. **[2]**

**8.** Explain one way the government acts as a producer and one way it can influence production by private firms. **[4]**

**9.** The hand cream firm in question 2 finds its variable cost rises to £2.40 per pot. It still sells 300 pots at £4.50.

**(a)** Calculate its new total cost and profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the likely effect on the supply of hand cream. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate the impact of competition on producers and consumers. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the importance of productivity for an economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: to earn profit [1]; to win market share or customers [1]; to survive; to grow. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two separate reasons. Repeating one reason in different words, or giving a method such as cutting price, adds little.

**2.** Variable cost = 2.00 × 300 = £600; TC = 450 + 600 = **£1,050** [1]. AC = 1,050 ÷ 300 = **£3.50** [1]. TR = 4.50 × 300 = **£1,350** [1]. Profit = 1,350 − 1,050 = **£300** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each formula and keep the units. Writing every step makes your working easy to follow and easy for you to check.

**3. (a)** Before: £9 and 36 [1]. After: £8 and 42 [1].
**(b)** (8 − 9) ÷ 9 × 100 = **−11.1%** [1].
**(c)** New stalls increase supply [1], so the supply curve shifts right and there is a surplus at £9 (48 against 36) [1], which pushes sellers to cut price until the market clears at £8 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Analyse" asks for a chain, so link the event, the shift, the surplus and the new price in order. Quoting figures from the table strengthens the chain.

**4. (a)** Year 1: 5,200 ÷ 130 = 40 tools [1]. Year 2: 6,000 ÷ 125 = 48 tools [1].
**(b)** (48 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **20%** [1].
**(c)** Each worker makes more, so labour cost per tool falls [1], letting the firm charge lower prices or earn more profit than rivals [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by workers, not by years. In the explanation, say what happens to cost per unit rather than only repeating that output rose.

**5. (a)** Total = 120; 42 ÷ 120 × 100 = **35%** [1].
**(b)** (42 + 36) ÷ 120 × 100 = **65%** [1].
**(c)** A few large firms hold most of the sales [1], so the market is dominated by a small number of firms, which is an oligopoly [1]. In a competitive market there are many firms and none can influence price alone, whereas here each firm's decisions affect rivals [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the table to support the label. A difference stated as "more firms" without saying what that means for price control is thin.

**6. (a)** Variable cost = 3.50 × 400 = £1,400; TC = 900 + 1,400 = **£2,300** [1]. AC = 2,300 ÷ 400 = **£5.75** [1]. TR = 8 × 400 = **£3,200** [1]. Profit = 3,200 − 2,300 = **£900** [1].
**(b)** TC = 900 + 350 = £1,250 [1]. TR = 800, so loss = 800 − 1,250 = **£450 loss** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Say "loss" and give the amount when revenue is below cost. A negative sign alone is fine only if the question's context makes the meaning clear.

**7. (a)** 80,000 ÷ 10,000 = **£8.00** [1]; 132,000 ÷ 20,000 = **£6.60** [1].
**(b)** (6.60 − 8.00) ÷ 8.00 × 100 = **−17.5%** [1].
**(c)** For example, purchasing: larger orders earn discounts, so material cost per box falls [1] and average cost falls as output rises [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Total cost rose but average cost fell, and the explanation should say exactly that. Naming a type of economy without saying why cost per unit falls earns little.

**8.** Producer: the government provides services such as state education [1], funded from taxation [1]. Influence: it can give a subsidy that lowers costs and raises supply [1], or set rules and taxes that change firms' costs and output [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give one of each and develop it. Two examples of the same role cannot cover both parts.

**9. (a)** New VC = 2.40 × 300 = £720; TC = 450 + 720 = **£1,170**; profit = 1,350 − 1,170 = **£180** [2].
**(b)** (180 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **−40%** [1].
**(c)** Higher costs cut profit at every price [1], so firms are willing to supply less [1] and the supply curve shifts left [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate a cost change from a price change. A cost rise shifts the whole curve, while a price rise is a movement along it.

**10.** Consumers gain: competition tends to lower price because rival cuts and new entrants raise supply [1], and firms may improve choice and quality [1]. Producers: margins fall [1], weaker firms may leave and workers lose jobs [1], but efficient firms cut costs or innovate and may win share [1]. Against consumers: if rivals collapse, fewer firms remain and prices may later rise [1]. Judgement: competition is usually good for consumers and mixed for producers [1], depending on how easily customers can switch and whether firms can cut cost without cutting quality [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided list rarely shows evaluation. Weigh producers against consumers and finish with a judgement that follows from your reasons.

**11.** Productivity is output per worker or other input [1]. Higher productivity lowers cost per unit [1], so firms can compete on price at home and abroad [1]. It raises output, incomes and tax revenue, supporting growth [1], and wages can rise without higher prices [1]. However, machines may replace workers, and some may lose jobs [1]. More output can harm the environment [1]. Judgement: productivity is important for long-run living standards, provided gains are shared and damage is limited [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not treat more production as the same as more productivity. A judgement works best when it says which effect matters more and why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out units, or the word "loss" when revenue is below cost.
- Using total cost in place of average cost, or the reverse.
- Mixing up price change (movement along) with cost change (shift).
- Calling a market of a few firms a monopoly.
- Describing benefits of competition for consumers only.
- Treating more output as higher productivity.
- Giving a list in an "evaluate" question with no supported judgement.
- Quoting percentages without dividing by the original value.
