OxfordAQA International A-Level Psychology (9685) is a modular qualification of four papers, each 1.5
hours and 90 marks. AS Paper 1 (Introductory Topics: Memory, Social Psychology, Psychopathology) and AS
Paper 2 (Biopsychology, Development and Research Methods 1) are each 20% of the full A-level. A-level
Paper 1 (Advanced Topics and Research Methods 2: Psychology of Sleep, Schizophrenia) and A-level Paper 2
(Approaches and Application) are each 30%. Units are resittable any number of times, with the best
result counting -- a distinctive feature of this specification. These notes complement the site's guides
to [Introductory Topics](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-psychology-introductory-topics/) and
[Biopsychology, Development and Research Methods 1](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-psychology-biopsychology-development-research-methods/).

## Unlimited resits change what "exam strategy" means here

Because any number of resits are permitted and only the best result counts, a weak first sitting is a
floor to build on rather than a fixed final result. **Exam-preparation priority**: where your school's
timetable allows it, sit papers as soon as content is genuinely secure rather than waiting to feel fully
ready -- an early attempt establishes a result that can only improve on resit, never count against you.

## A2 papers are worth more, so resit priority should follow the weighting

A-level Paper 1 and A-level Paper 2 are each 30% against the AS papers' 20% each.
**Exam-preparation priority**: if resit capacity is limited, prioritise improving A2 results over AS
results first, since each A2 mark is worth proportionally more of the final grade.

## Research Methods is examined across two different papers

Research Methods 1 sits within AS Paper 2 alongside biopsychology and development content; Research
Methods 2 sits within A-level Paper 1 alongside sleep and schizophrenia content. **Exam-preparation
priority**: treat research methods as a cumulative thread running through both papers rather than
content confined to a single sitting -- AS-level methods understanding is the foundation A-level Research
Methods 2 builds on.

## Evaluation of studies is examined throughout, not in one dedicated section

Across all four papers, extended answers reward evaluating named studies -- their methodology,
generalisability, ethics and validity -- rather than simply describing findings. **Exam-preparation
priority**: build a habit of pairing every study you learn with at least one methodological strength and
one limitation, so evaluation material is ready regardless of which paper's question calls for it.

## Worked routine: evaluating a study in a structured paragraph

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - state the evaluation point in one sentence:
E.g. "This study used a highly controlled laboratory setting."

Step 2 - explain the immediate implication:
What does that method choice actually affect -- validity, control,
generalisability?

Step 3 - give the specific consequence for this study:
Not evaluation in the abstract -- how it affects confidence in
this study's own findings.

Step 4 - weigh it against a counterpoint:
Every methodological choice trades one strength for a weakness
elsewhere; name the trade-off explicitly.

Step 5 - conclude what the study's finding can and cannot support:
Ties the evaluation back to the claim the study is being used to
support in the essay.
```

Step 5 is what separates evaluation from a detached methodology comment: it reconnects the critique to
the specific argument the study was cited to support.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: sit papers as soon as content is secure, using the unlimited-resits policy to
  your advantage; prioritise A2 resits over AS resits if capacity is limited; build evaluation
  points alongside every study learned, not as a separate revision task.
- **During any paper**: pair every study reference with at least one stated strength and one limitation.
- **In every evaluative paragraph**: state the point, explain the implication, give the consequence for
  this specific study, weigh a counterpoint, and conclude what the finding can and cannot support.
