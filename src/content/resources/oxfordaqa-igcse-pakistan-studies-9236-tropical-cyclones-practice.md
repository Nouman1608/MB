## Section A — short-answer questions

**1.** Above roughly what sea surface temperature do tropical cyclones usually form? **[1]**

A: 16.5 °C &nbsp; B: 20.5 °C &nbsp; C: 26.5 °C &nbsp; D: 36.5 °C

**2.** What is the calm centre of a tropical cyclone called? **[1]**

A: The eye &nbsp; B: The eye wall &nbsp; C: The front &nbsp; D: The trough

**3.** Which cyclone crossed the Makran coast of Balochistan in June 2007? **[1]**

A: Biparjoy &nbsp; B: Yemyin &nbsp; C: Phailin &nbsp; D: Katrina

**4.** In which direction do tropical cyclones rotate in the northern hemisphere? **[1]**

A: Clockwise &nbsp; B: Anticlockwise &nbsp; C: They do not rotate &nbsp; D: It changes with the season

**5.** State **two** conditions needed for a tropical cyclone to form. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** hazards brought by a tropical cyclone. **[2]**

**7.** State **two** ways in which people on the Sindh coast can be warned or protected before a
cyclone arrives. **[2]**

## Section B — extended questions

## Answers

Multiple-choice questions score 1 mark. "State" questions score 1 mark per valid point. Four-mark
questions score 1 mark per valid, linked point. The 6- and 9-mark questions are marked by levels; the
level descriptions follow the answers. Credit other accurate points.

**1.** **C: 26.5 °C** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **A: The eye** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** **B: Yemyin** [1]. Biparjoy came in 2023; Phailin struck eastern India; Katrina was an Atlantic
hurricane. **[1]**

**4.** **B: Anticlockwise** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Two conditions, 1 mark each: warm sea water above about 26.5 °C [1]; low vertical wind shear
[1]. Also credit: at least about 5 degrees from the equator so the Coriolis effect operates; moist
mid-level air; an existing disturbance. **[2]**

**6.** Two hazards, 1 mark each: strong winds [1]; storm surge [1]. Also credit: heavy rain and flash
flooding. **[2]**

**7.** Two ways, 1 mark each: forecasts and warnings from the Pakistan Meteorological Department [1];
evacuation organised by disaster management authorities [1]. Also credit: fishing bans and port
closures; shelters; embankments; mangrove belts. **[2]**

**8.** Four linked points, 1 mark each: warm sea water heats the air and causes rapid evaporation [1];
warm, moist air rises, creating low pressure at the surface [1]; condensation forms clouds and
releases latent heat, so the air rises faster and pressure falls further [1]; inflowing air is turned
by the Coriolis effect so it spirals around a calm eye [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Four linked points, 1 mark each: over land the cyclone is cut off from warm sea water [1], its
supply of heat and moisture [1]; less evaporation means less condensation and latent heat to drive
rising air [1]; friction with the land surface slows the winds [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- *Loss of life and homes:* storm surges flood low-lying villages in the Indus delta; mud and thatch
  houses are destroyed, as in the May 1999 cyclone around Thatta and Badin.
- *Livelihoods:* fishing communities lose boats and nets and cannot work during bans; farmers lose
  crops and livestock, and salty land may not be farmed for years.
- *Health:* contaminated water spreads diarrhoea and other diseases in relief camps.
- *Displacement:* evacuation, as before Biparjoy in 2023, saves lives but disrupts work and schooling.
  **[6]**

**11.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- *Salinisation:* seawater carried inland by surges makes soil salty, reducing fertility.
- *Coastal change:* waves and surges erode beaches and creeks in the delta.
- *Mangroves:* forests can be damaged, which reduces their ability to protect the coast and to provide
  breeding grounds for fish.
- *Inland flooding:* heavy rain causes flash floods and soil erosion in Balochistan, as during Yemyin
  in 2007; but rain can also recharge reservoirs and groundwater. **[6]**

**12.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* surges flood low-lying coasts such as the Indus delta, cause many deaths, and leave
  farmland salty for years; the 1999 cyclone devastated coastal villages.
- *Disagree:* heavy rain can be more damaging inland, as flash floods from Yemyin in Balochistan
  showed; strong winds destroy homes and power lines; the most dangerous hazard depends on where the
  cyclone makes landfall and how well people are prepared.
- *Judgement:* for example, the surge is most dangerous on the low Sindh coast, but rain-fed flooding
  can affect a far larger area. **[9]**

**13.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* satellite tracking and Pakistan Meteorological Department forecasts give several days'
  warning; NDMA evacuations, as before Biparjoy in 2023, kept deaths low; mangrove restoration
  strengthens natural defences.
- *Disagree:* cyclones cannot be prevented, only prepared for; poor coastal communities have weak
  housing and few resources; damage to boats, crops and land still occurs; a warming Arabian Sea may
  bring stronger storms.
- *Judgement:* for example, Pakistan has reduced loss of life, but it cannot prevent economic and
  environmental harm, so the statement goes too far. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing formation without the role of latent heat or the Coriolis effect.
- Impacts with no named place or event.
- Treating "prevent" and "prepare for" as the same thing.
- In 9-mark answers, judging without weighing both sides.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know cyclone wind-speed categories?**
No. The specification asks for causes and impacts; explain formation and give named examples.

**Why do so many Arabian Sea cyclones miss Pakistan?**
Steering winds often carry them towards Oman, Iran or Gujarat, and some weaken over cooler water
before reaching the coast.
