## Question 1 (compulsory) — Research Methods

**(a)** Explain the difference between primary data and secondary data, and between quantitative and qualitative data. **[4]**

**(b)** Define validity, reliability and representativeness. **[6]**

**(c)** Explain **two** advantages and **two** disadvantages of using questionnaires in sociological research. **[8]**

**(d)** Explain the difference between participant and non-participant observation, and between covert and overt research, giving one ethical problem with covert research. **[6]**

**(e)** Describe **two** sampling methods a sociologist could use to select a sample, and explain what is meant by a *sampling frame*. **[6]**

**(f)** Explain what is meant by a *pilot study*, and state **two** ethical principles a sociologist should follow when conducting research. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a) Primary data** is **collected first-hand by the researcher** for the purpose of the study; **secondary data** already exists, having been collected by someone else [1] [1]. **Quantitative data** is **numerical and can be counted and analysed statistically**; **qualitative data** is **descriptive, expressed in words, capturing meanings and feelings** [1] [1].

**(b) Validity** — whether the data gives a **true and authentic picture of what it claims to measure** [2]. **Reliability** — whether the method would **produce the same results if repeated** by another researcher [2]. **Representativeness** — whether the sample **accurately reflects the wider population**, so findings can be generalised [2].

**(c) Advantages:** they can be distributed to a **large, geographically spread sample cheaply and quickly**, so results are more likely to be representative and generalisable [1] [1]. They are **standardised, so they are reliable and easy to replicate**, and closed questions produce quantitative data that is quick to analyse and compare [1] [1].
**Disadvantages:** **response rates are often very low**, and those who reply may differ systematically from those who do not, undermining representativeness [1] [1]. They **lack depth and validity** — respondents cannot explain their answers, may misunderstand a question with nobody to clarify it, and may give socially desirable answers rather than truthful ones [1] [1].

**(d)** In **participant observation** the researcher **joins in the group's activities**; in **non-participant observation** they **watch without taking part** [1] [1]. **Overt** research means the group **knows they are being studied**; **covert** means the researcher's identity and purpose are **concealed** [1] [1].
Ethical problem with covert research: participants **cannot give informed consent** [1], and the researcher must deceive them, which may also require observing or even participating in illegal or harmful behaviour without intervening [1].

**(e)** Any two methods, 2 marks each: **random sampling** — every member of the population has an **equal chance of selection** [1] [1]; **stratified sampling** — the population is divided into groups (strata), and a **proportionate sample is taken from each group** [1] [1]; **systematic sampling** — every *n*th name is selected from a list [1] [1]; **snowball sampling** — existing participants recruit further participants, used for **hidden or hard-to-reach groups** [1] [1]. **Sampling frame** — the **list from which the sample is drawn**, and without one the researcher cannot guarantee every member of the population has a known chance of selection [1] [1].

**(f)** A **pilot study** is a **small-scale trial run** of the research method before the main study, used to identify problems with wording, timing or procedure [2]. Any two: **informed consent**, **confidentiality**, **protection from harm**, **no deception**, **right to withdraw** [1] [1].

**2. (a) Primary socialisation** takes place in **early childhood**, where the child learns basic norms, values and language; the main agency is the **family** [2]. **Secondary socialisation** takes place **later, beyond the family**, teaching the norms of wider society; the main agency is the **education system** [2].
Three agencies of secondary socialisation: the **education system**, through both the formal curriculum and the hidden curriculum of punctuality and obedience [1]; **peer groups**, which exert pressure to conform and confer status [1]; the **media**, which presents role models and shapes expectations [1].

**(b) Ascribed status** is **fixed at birth**, e.g. **sex** [2]. **Achieved status** is **earned through a person's own actions**, e.g. becoming a doctor [2].

**(c) Subculture** — a group with **distinct norms and values within a wider society**, e.g. a youth subculture [2]. **Counter-culture** — a subculture **actively opposed to** the mainstream norms and values of society [2].

**3.** Any two, 4 marks each: **Functionalist (Davis and Moore)** — inequality is **functional and inevitable**: the most important roles require the greatest talent and longest training, so **higher rewards are needed as an incentive** to attract the most able [1] [1] [1] [1].
**Marxist** — inequality arises from the **ownership of the means of production**; the bourgeoisie **exploit the proletariat by extracting surplus value** from their labour [1] [1]; ideology transmitted through education, religion and the media produces **false consciousness**, so the working class accept a system that disadvantages them [1] [1].
*(Also accept: Weberian — market situation, status and party as separate dimensions of stratification; Feminist — patriarchy and class combined, the "dual burden" — marked on the same pattern.)*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Giving questionnaire advantages without linking them to representativeness or reliability.
- Naming the family as an agency of secondary socialisation.
- Describing theories without explaining the mechanism of inequality.
- Describing a sampling method without explaining what makes it more or less representative.
- Confusing a subculture (distinct but not opposed) with a counter-culture (actively opposed to the mainstream).
- Attempting both Question 2 and Question 3 instead of choosing one.
