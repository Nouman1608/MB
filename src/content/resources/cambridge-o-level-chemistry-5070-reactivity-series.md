This guide covers subtopic **9.4 Reactivity series** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). The overview in [Metal Properties and Reactivity](/resources/metal-properties-and-reactivity/) introduces the series and a few displacement examples; this guide works through the complete displacement grid for the five metals the syllabus names, writes the ionic and electron-loss equations, and shows how to deduce an order from unfamiliar results.

## What the syllabus asks

1. **State** the order: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, **carbon**, zinc, iron, **hydrogen**, copper, silver, gold.
2. **Describe** relative reactivities in terms of the **tendency to form positive ions**, by displacement reactions, if any, with the aqueous ions of **magnesium, zinc, iron, copper and silver**.
3. **Describe** the reactions, if any, of potassium, sodium and calcium with **cold water**; magnesium with **steam**; and magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver and gold with **dilute hydrochloric acid**, and **explain** these reactions in terms of position in the series.
4. **Explain** the apparent unreactivity of aluminium in terms of its **oxide layer**.
5. **Deduce** an order of reactivity from a given set of experimental results.

## The order, and the two non-metals in it

```
most reactive   K  Na  Ca  Mg  Al  [C]  Zn  Fe  [H]  Cu  Ag  Au   least reactive
```

Carbon and hydrogen are non-metals included as **reference points**:

- **Hydrogen:** metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids to release hydrogen; metals below it (copper, silver, gold) do not.
- **Carbon:** metals below carbon can be extracted from their oxides by heating with carbon; metals above it cannot (subtopic 9.6).

A common memory aid is "**P**lease **S**end **C**harlie's **M**onkeys **A**nd **C**ute **Z**ebras **I**nto **H**ot **C**ountries **S**igned **G**ordon" for K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, C, Zn, Fe, H, Cu, Ag, Au. Use any version that works for you, but check it against the list above.

## What "more reactive" means

A metal reacts by **losing electrons** to form a **positive ion**. The more reactive the metal, the greater its tendency to do this:

```
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻     (strong tendency)
Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻     (weak tendency)
```

Every reaction in this subtopic is explained by this one idea. Potassium reacts violently with water because it forms K⁺ very readily; gold is found uncombined because it has almost no tendency to form ions.

## Displacement reactions

A **more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its ions**. The more reactive metal loses electrons to the ions of the less reactive metal.

### The full grid

✓ = displacement occurs; ✗ = no reaction.

| Metal added ↓ / solution → | Mg²⁺(aq) | Zn²⁺(aq) | Fe²⁺(aq) | Cu²⁺(aq) | Ag⁺(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | — | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Zinc | ✗ | — | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Iron | ✗ | ✗ | — | ✓ | ✓ |
| Copper | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | — | ✓ |
| Silver | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | — |

The pattern is a staircase: each metal displaces every metal to its right. Counting the ticks in each row gives the order directly (magnesium 4, zinc 3, iron 2, copper 1, silver 0). This is the method for outcome 5.

### Observations and equations

**Zinc in copper(II) sulfate solution**

- Observations: a pink-brown solid (copper) forms on the zinc; the blue colour of the solution fades; the mixture warms up.
- Ionic equation: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

**Copper in silver nitrate solution**

- Observations: grey or silvery crystals (silver) form on the copper; the solution turns blue as copper(II) ions form.
- Ionic equation: Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)

Notice the 2 in front of Ag⁺: a copper atom loses two electrons, and each silver ion gains only one.

### Explaining a displacement in terms of ions

*Explain why zinc displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate solution but copper does not displace zinc from zinc sulfate solution. [3]*

Model answer: "Zinc is more reactive than copper, so it has a greater tendency to form positive ions [1]. Zinc atoms lose electrons to form Zn²⁺, and copper(II) ions gain those electrons to form copper atoms [1]. Copper has a smaller tendency to form ions than zinc, so it cannot give electrons to zinc ions [1]."

The sulfate ions are spectator ions and are left out of the ionic equation.

## Reactions with water, steam and acid, explained by position

| Reaction | What happens | Explanation from position |
|---|---|---|
| K, Na, Ca + cold water | Hydroxide + hydrogen; potassium most violent, calcium steady | Top three metals: strongest tendency to form ions |
| Mg + steam | Magnesium oxide + hydrogen; bright glow | Less reactive than calcium: needs the higher temperature of steam |
| Mg, Zn, Fe + dilute HCl | Chloride + hydrogen; fizzing fastest for Mg, slowest for Fe | Above hydrogen; rate falls down the series |
| Cu, Ag, Au + dilute HCl | No reaction | Below hydrogen in the series |

Equations: 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂; Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂; Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂; Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂.

## Aluminium's apparent unreactivity

Aluminium is above zinc and iron in the series, yet a piece of aluminium placed in dilute hydrochloric acid may show no reaction at first, and aluminium does not corrode in air. The reason is a thin, tightly bonded **layer of aluminium oxide** on its surface. This layer is unreactive and stops water, air and (at first) acid reaching the metal. Once the layer is removed, for example by scratching the surface or leaving the metal in acid until the oxide dissolves, aluminium reacts as vigorously as its position predicts.

"Apparent" is the key word: aluminium is **not** unreactive; it only appears to be.

## Deducing an order from results

### Worked example

*Three metals, R, S and T, are placed in solutions of each other's nitrates. Results: R displaces S and T. T displaces nothing. S displaces T. Place the metals in order of reactivity, and suggest which is copper if the others are magnesium and zinc.*

- Count displacements: R = 2, S = 1, T = 0.
- Order: **R > S > T**.
- From the series, magnesium > zinc > copper, so **T is copper** (R is magnesium, S is zinc).

When results come from rates instead (for example, how fast bubbles appear with acid), rank by vigour, and be cautious: surface area, temperature and acid concentration must be the same for the comparison to be fair.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting aluminium below zinc** because it seems unreactive.
- **"Less reactive metal displaces the more reactive one."** It is always the more reactive metal that displaces.
- **Writing ionic equations with charges unbalanced**, especially Cu + 2Ag⁺.
- **Saying copper reacts slowly with dilute acid.** It does not react.
- **Describing displacement without observations**: give the colour change and the solid formed.
- **Omitting carbon and hydrogen** from a stated order when asked for the full series.

## Exam technique

- **State** the order exactly as the syllabus gives it, including carbon and hydrogen.
- **Describe** a displacement: what forms, what colour changes.
- **Explain**: compare the two metals' tendencies to form positive ions.
- **Deduce**: count displacements or rank vigour, then state the order with the most reactive first.
