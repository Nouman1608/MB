This guide covers section 3.2, Households, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. The section has one content statement, 3.2.1: "the influences on spending, saving and borrowing", which the syllabus says must include "income, the rate of interest and confidence – between different households and over time". All households and figures below are invented.

The combined [money, banking and household spending guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-money-banking-and-household-spending-saving-and/) sets out the basic effects of each influence. This page goes further: it shows why the same change affects different households in opposite ways, separates the effects on new and existing borrowers, and builds the evaluation that "discuss" questions need. Recall the content with the [households revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households-revision-notes/) and practise with the [households practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-households-practice/).

## The three decisions

A household's **disposable income** (income after direct taxes, plus any benefits received) has two uses:

```
Disposable income = spending + saving
```

If spending is greater than disposable income, saving is negative: the household must **borrow** or use past savings (dissaving). So the three decisions are linked. A household that decides to spend more must save less or borrow more.

## Influence 1: income

### Level of income

Higher-income households spend more in total, but spend a smaller **proportion** of their income, because their basic needs are already covered. Lower-income households spend almost all their income on necessities such as food, rent and transport, and have little left to save.

### Change in income

**Worked example 1 (invented).** Household F has disposable income of 3,200 a month. It spends 2,880 and saves 320. A pay rise lifts disposable income to 3,700, and it now spends 3,180.

```
Saving before = 3,200 - 2,880 = 320, which is 10% of income
Saving after  = 3,700 - 3,180 = 520, which is 14.1% of income
Of the extra 500: 300 spent (60%) and 200 saved (40%)
```

The household saves a larger share of the extra income than of its original income. This is typical: the first part of income goes on necessities, so more of each extra unit can be saved.

### Expected income

Households also look ahead. A student expecting a well-paid job may borrow now; a worker who fears redundancy may save more today even though current income has not changed.

## Influence 2: the rate of interest

The rate of interest is both the **reward for saving** and the **cost of borrowing**. A rise in interest rates has three separate effects, and you should name the ones that fit the question.

| Group | Effect of a higher interest rate | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| New borrowers | Loans cost more | Fewer loans taken; less spending on cars, furniture and houses |
| Existing borrowers on variable rates | Repayments on current debts rise | Less income left to spend |
| Savers | Saving earns more | More saving; but some savers, such as retired people living on interest, have more income to spend |

**Worked example 2 (invented).** The interest rate rises from 4% to 6% a year.

Household G has a variable-rate home loan of 150,000, paying interest only.

```
Interest at 4% = 150,000 x 0.04 = 6,000 a year = 500 a month
Interest at 6% = 150,000 x 0.06 = 9,000 a year = 750 a month
Extra cost = 250 a month, which must come out of other spending
```

Household H is a retired couple with savings of 90,000 in the bank, living partly on the interest.

```
Interest at 4% = 3,600 a year = 300 a month
Interest at 6% = 5,400 a year = 450 a month
Extra income = 150 a month, which they may spend
```

The same change cuts G's spending and may raise H's. Overall, spending in the economy usually falls when rates rise, because borrowers tend to spend most of any change in their income, but this example shows why an answer should not claim every household spends less.

## Influence 3: confidence

**Consumer confidence** is how optimistic households feel about their future jobs, incomes and the economy.

- **High confidence:** households spend more, borrow for large items such as cars and holidays, and save less.
- **Low confidence:** households build up savings as a cushion against losing their job, delay buying expensive durable goods and avoid new debt.

Confidence can change quickly with news of job losses, factory closures, rising prices or a recession, even before incomes change. That makes it a useful explanation when data shows spending falling while incomes are still rising.

## Other influences worth knowing

The word "including" in the syllabus means income, interest and confidence must be covered, but other influences can earn credit:

- **Wealth:** households whose homes or savings rise in value feel richer and may spend more.
- **Availability of credit:** if banks lend more easily, borrowing and spending rise.
- **Age and size of household:** see the section on differences between households below.
- **Taxation:** higher income tax reduces disposable income.
- **Attitudes and habits:** some households and cultures value saving highly.

## Between different households

The syllabus asks you to compare households. Use features such as income, age and debt.

| Household | Likely pattern | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young adult starting work | High spending, borrowing | Low current income, expects income to rise; setting up a home |
| Family with young children | High spending on necessities, low saving | Many dependants; may have a home loan |
| Middle-aged, children grown up | Higher saving | Income near its peak, saving for retirement |
| Retired | Spending from savings (dissaving) | Income from work has stopped |
| Low income, any age | Spends nearly all income; may borrow for emergencies | Necessities take most of income |
| High income | Saves a larger proportion | Necessities a small share of income |

## Over time

Patterns change over a person's life (the life stages above) and as the economy changes.

- **Economic boom:** incomes and confidence rise, households spend and borrow more.
- **Recession:** job losses reduce incomes and confidence; households cut spending and save more if they can.
- **Interest rate changes:** households respond as loans become cheaper or dearer.

**Worked example 3 (invented).** In Country N, average monthly disposable income and spending were:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disposable income | 2,500 | 2,600 | 2,550 |
| Spending | 2,250 | 2,210 | 2,295 |
| Saving | 250 | 390 | 255 |
| Saving as % of income | 10% | 15% | 10% |

In Year 2 income rose by 4% but spending fell, so saving jumped. Something other than income changed: a fall in confidence or a rise in interest rates would explain it. In Year 3 income fell 1.9% yet spending rose 3.8%, which suggests confidence recovered or rates fell.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Discuss whether a rise in the rate of interest will reduce household spending. [6]

> A higher interest rate makes borrowing more expensive, so fewer households take out loans to buy cars or houses, which reduces spending [effect on new borrowers]. Households with variable-rate loans must pay more each month, leaving less to spend on other goods [effect on existing borrowers]. Saving also becomes more rewarding, so some households save rather than spend [saving effect].
> However, households that live on interest from savings, such as retired people, receive more income and may spend more [counter-argument]. If confidence is high and incomes are rising, households may keep spending despite dearer loans [second counter-argument].
> Overall, spending is likely to fall, especially in economies where many households have large variable-rate debts, but the size of the fall depends on how much households owe and on their confidence [judgement with condition].

## Common mistakes

- Confusing saving (income not spent) with savings (the total stock built up over time).
- Saying higher interest rates make every household spend less.
- Explaining confidence as "people feel happy". Link it to expected jobs and income.
- Comparing totals when the question asks about proportions of income.
- Forgetting that borrowing allows spending above income.
