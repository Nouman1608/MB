Recall notes for section 3.3 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. The [fair distribution of income study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fair-distribution-of-income-ocr-gcse-economics/) explains each idea in full; the [fair distribution of income practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fair-distribution-of-income-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/) let you apply them. All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain the **distribution of income**, including **types of income** and the difference between **income and wealth**.
- **Calculate** income and wealth.
- **Evaluate** the **causes** of differences in income and wealth and the **consequences** for an economy.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Distribution of income | How total income is shared among households or people |
| Income | A flow of money received over a period |
| Wealth | A stock: the value of assets owned at a point in time |
| Net wealth | Assets minus liabilities |
| Asset | Something of value that is owned: property, savings, shares, pension funds |
| Liability | A debt owed: mortgage, loan, credit card balance |
| Negative net wealth | Owing more than you own |
| Dividend | Share of a company's profit paid to shareholders |
| Transfer payment | Benefit or pension paid by government with nothing produced in return |
| Value judgement | A view based on opinion about what ought to be, such as what is fair |

## Three meanings of "fair"

1. **Equal outcomes**: big gaps are unfair in themselves.
2. **Equal opportunity**: gaps are acceptable if everyone has a real chance.
3. **Reward for contribution**: effort, skill and risk deserve more pay.

State which meaning you are using when you judge fairness.

## Types of income

| From work | From owning wealth | From government |
|---|---|---|
| Wages, salaries, profit | Rent, interest, dividends | Benefits, state pensions |

**The feedback loop:** wealth → rent, interest, dividends → more saving → more wealth. Debt does the reverse: interest payments drain income. So wealth is usually shared **more unequally** than income.

## Methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Total income | Add every source for the same period |
| Share from one source | Source ÷ total × 100 |
| Net wealth | Total assets − total liabilities |
| Income from wealth | Value of asset × rate of return |
| Ratio between groups of equal size | Richer group's share ÷ poorer group's share |
| Per household income | Group's share × total income ÷ households in the group |

## Worked reminders

**Net wealth.** Flat £180,000, savings £5,000, pension £22,000; mortgage £135,000, car loan £4,000. Net wealth = 207,000 − 139,000 = **£68,000**.

**Income from wealth.** £40,000 of shares paying a 3.5% dividend gives **£1,400** a year; £15,000 of savings at 4% gives **£600**.

**Ratio.** The richest fifth receive 45% of income and the poorest fifth 5%: 45 ÷ 5 = **9 times** as much per household.

## Causes of differences

- Skills, qualifications and the demand for them
- Ownership of assets and inheritance
- Age and stage of life
- Region: wages and house prices differ
- Hours worked, insecure work, unemployment
- Discrimination
- Government taxes and benefits

**Evaluate causes** by asking: do they reflect life stage and choice, or barriers to opportunity?

## Consequences for an economy

| Negative | Positive or limiting |
|---|---|
| Weaker consumer spending, as richer people save more of their income | Savings can fund investment |
| Lost potential: poorer children may miss education and training | State education and training reduce this |
| More benefit spending, less tax revenue | Taxes on high incomes can fund support |
| Social tension and lower trust | Some inequality rewards effort and enterprise |

## Exam traps

- Income is a **flow**, wealth a **stock**: never use the words interchangeably.
- A mortgage is a **liability**, the house an **asset**.
- Net wealth can be **negative**.
- "Unequal" is a fact; "unfair" is a judgement.
- Use only households in the group when finding income per household.
- Evaluate with a reason for your judgement, not "it depends" alone.

## Self-test

1. Define the distribution of income.
2. Why is saying a distribution is "unfair" a value judgement?
3. Name two types of income that come from owning wealth.
4. A person earns a salary of £31,000, interest of £450 and rent of £3,900 in a year. Calculate total income.
5. A household owns a house worth £210,000, savings of £7,000 and shares worth £4,000, and owes £150,000 on a mortgage and £6,500 on a loan. Calculate net wealth.
6. A graduate has £1,200 in savings and a £9,000 loan. Calculate net wealth.
7. The richest fifth of households receive 42% of income and the poorest fifth 7%. How many times as much does a household in the richest fifth receive?
8. Shares worth £50,000 pay a 4% dividend. How much income do they produce in a year?
9. Explain why wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
10. Explain how age can cause differences in income.
11. State one consequence for the economy of a large gap between rich and poor.
12. Give one argument that some inequality can benefit the economy.

### Answers

1. How a country's **total income is shared** between households or individuals.
2. It depends on **opinions** about what ought to be, such as whether fairness means equal outcomes or reward for effort, and cannot be proved true or false.
3. Any two: **rent, interest, dividends**.
4. 31,000 + 450 + 3,900 = **£35,350**.
5. Assets 221,000 − liabilities 156,500 = **£64,500**.
6. 1,200 − 9,000 = **−£7,800** (negative net wealth).
7. 42 ÷ 7 = **6 times**.
8. 50,000 × 0.04 = **£2,000**.
9. Wealth produces **rent, interest and dividends** that can be saved, and wealth can be **inherited**, so gaps build up over time.
10. Earnings usually **rise with experience** and peak in middle age; young workers and many retired people have lower incomes.
11. Any one: **lower consumer spending**, wasted potential, higher benefit spending, social tension.
12. Higher rewards give **incentives** to train, work hard and start businesses, which can raise output.
