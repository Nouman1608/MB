Condensed notes for **1.5 Stakeholders in a business** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Full explanations and a worked automation example are in the [Stakeholders study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-stakeholders-in-a-business-cambridge-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [Stakeholders practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-stakeholders-in-a-business-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 1.5 asks you to handle

- **1.5.1**: who stakeholders are; internal and external; roles, rights and responsibilities.
- **1.5.2**: impact of decisions and reactions; impact of stakeholder aims on decisions; accountability; conflict between aims; effect of changing objectives.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder | Individual or group with an interest in a business's activities |
| Shareholder | Owner of shares in a company; one type of stakeholder |
| Internal stakeholder | Inside the business: owners, managers, employees |
| External stakeholder | Outside it: customers, suppliers, lenders, government, community, pressure groups |
| Influence | The power of a group to affect a business decision |
| Accountability | Having to explain and justify actions to those affected |
| Stakeholder conflict | When meeting one group's aims means not meeting another's |
| Shareholder view | Run the business mainly for its owners |
| Stakeholder view | Take account of all groups affected, even at some cost to short-term profit |

## Typical aims

- **Shareholders**: dividends, rising share value.
- **Managers**: pay, status, growth of their area.
- **Employees**: pay, job security, safe conditions, development.
- **Customers**: value, quality, reliability, honest information.
- **Suppliers**: regular orders, fair prices, prompt payment.
- **Lenders**: repayment with interest, low risk.
- **Government**: tax, employment, legal compliance.
- **Community**: jobs, low pollution, safe roads.

## Sources of influence: what each controls

Shareholders → capital and votes. Employees → labour and skills. Customers → revenue. Suppliers → inputs and credit. Lenders → finance. Government → laws, licences, taxes. Community and pressure groups → publicity and planning objections.

## Five things that raise a group's influence

1. **Dependence**: how much the business relies on the group.
2. **Replaceability**: how easily it can be replaced.
3. **Organisation**: unions and coordinated campaigns.
4. **Legal rights**: votes, regulatory powers.
5. **Publicity**: media and social media reach.

## Power–interest grid

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely |
| **Low power** | Monitor | Keep informed |

Positions **change with each decision**. Re-map for every case.

## Analysis chain for any decision

Decision → impact on the group → likely reaction → effect back on the business → which reaction matters most here and why.

## Stakeholder aims shaping decisions

- Large customer → delays a price rise.
- Bank's lending conditions → limit expansion.
- Government emissions targets → cleaner equipment.
- Shareholders' dividend demands → less profit kept for investment.

## Accountability mechanisms

- Annual reports and published accounts.
- Annual general meetings and shareholder votes.
- Employee consultation before major change.
- Reports to regulators.
- Complaint procedures and public statements.
- Public sector: reporting to government and the public on use of tax money.

**Why**: lost trust is costly: higher borrowing costs, lost customers, closer regulation.

## Handling conflict

| Approach | Example | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Prioritise | Shareholders' profit first | Opposition from losers |
| Compromise | Smaller pay rise and smaller dividend rise | No group fully satisfied |
| Compensate | Redundancy pay, community fund | Costs money |
| Communicate and consult | Explain reasons early | Takes time; may not change minds |

## Changing objectives

A switch from steady service to profit maximisation: shareholders gain; customers on unprofitable lines lose; staff face tighter targets but possibly safer jobs; local government may face pressure to step in.

## Exam traps

- **Stakeholder ≠ shareholder.**
- Not all stakeholders are equally powerful; say who matters most **in this case**.
- Give the **reason** for a reaction, not just the reaction.
- "Satisfy everyone" is not a judgement; say how the conflict is handled and who loses.
- Shareholder and stakeholder views often converge in the long run; mention time horizon in evaluation.
- Shareholders of a large plc may be classed as external to day-to-day operations; state your classification.

## Self-test

1. Define a stakeholder.
2. Give two internal and two external stakeholders.
3. What is the difference between the shareholder view and the stakeholder view?
4. Give two factors that increase a stakeholder group's influence.
5. Where on a power–interest grid would a major lender considering a new loan sit, and how should it be managed?
6. Give one way a supplier can influence a business.
7. Give one right and one responsibility of a lender.
8. Name two ways a company is accountable to its shareholders.
9. Explain why a business's decision to cut its prices might create conflict between two stakeholder groups.
10. Give one way to reduce employee opposition to a factory closure.
11. Why might shareholder and stakeholder interests converge in the long run?
12. How might a public-sector body be held accountable?

**Answers**

1. An individual or group with an interest in the activities of a business.
2. Internal: for example, employees and managers. External: for example, customers and suppliers.
3. The shareholder view says managers should run the business mainly for its owners; the stakeholder view says they should take account of all groups affected, even at some cost to short-term profit.
4. Any two: dependence of the business on the group, how hard it is to replace, how organised it is, legal rights, publicity.
5. High power, high interest: manage closely and consult early.
6. For example, raising prices, shortening credit terms or refusing to supply.
7. Right: repayment of the loan with agreed interest. Responsibility: to lend on the terms agreed.
8. Any two: annual report and accounts, annual general meeting, shareholder votes.
9. Customers gain lower prices, but shareholders may lose profit margin, so meeting one group's aim reduces the other's.
10. For example, consultation, redundancy pay, retraining or redeployment to other sites.
11. Treating employees and customers well raises productivity and loyalty, which supports future profit and so shareholder returns.
12. For example, by reporting to government and publishing information on how tax money is used and on service standards.
