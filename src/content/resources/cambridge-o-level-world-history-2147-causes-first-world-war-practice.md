These questions cover key question 1.6 of Cambridge O Level History 2147, "What caused the First World War?"

## Section A

**1.** Describe the effects of the 1902 Anglo-Japanese Alliance on Britain and Russia. **[4]**

**2.** Identify **two** members of the Triple Entente. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the Second Moroccan Crisis of 1911. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the Bosnian Crisis of 1908–09. **[4]**

**5.** Identify **two** members of the Balkan League that fought the Ottoman Empire in 1912. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the July Crisis of 1914 from the blank cheque to Austria-Hungary's declaration of war on Serbia. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Why did Britain and Russia settle their differences in 1907? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**8.** Why did the naval race increase tension between Britain and Germany? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**9.** "Colonial rivalry was the main cause of tension between the Great Powers before 1914." How far do you agree?
Explain your answer. **[10]**

**10.** "The alliance system made war less likely, not more likely." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
**[10]**

**11.** Study Source A, which is invented for practice.

*Source A (invented)*: from an imagined article in a Vienna newspaper, July 1914. "For years Belgrade has fed the
dreams of every Serb in Bosnia. Now those dreams have murdered the heir to our throne. If the Monarchy does not act
now, it will be told by every Slav in the Empire that it no longer has the strength to act at all."

Why do you think this article was published in July 1914? Use the source and your knowledge to explain your answer.
**[7]**

**12.** Study Source B, which is invented for practice.

*Source B (invented)*: from an imagined letter by a Russian minister to a colleague, late July 1914. "We gave way over
Bosnia in 1909 because we were weak and Germany threatened us. If we abandon Serbia now, no Slav will ever trust Russia
again, and Austria will take the whole peninsula piece by piece."

How far does Source B help to explain why the July crisis became a war between the Great Powers? Use the source and
your knowledge to explain your answer. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** Any four, 1 mark each: Britain was no longer isolated (1); Japan would protect British interests in East Asia
against Russia (1); Britain could bring warships home to face the German navy (1); Japan felt secure enough to fight
Russia in 1904–05 (1), knowing Britain would keep other powers out (1); Russia was defeated (1); Russia turned its
ambitions back towards the Balkans (1); a weakened Russia seemed less of a threat to Britain, which helped the 1907
entente (1). **[4]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: Britain (1); France (1); Russia (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any four, 1 mark each: France sent troops to Fez during a rising in Morocco (1); Germany sent the gunboat
*Panther* to Agadir (1); it claimed to protect German interests (1); Britain feared Germany wanted a naval base on the
Atlantic (1); Lloyd George's Mansion House speech warned Germany (1); Germany backed down and accepted land in the
French Congo (1); France gained control over Morocco (1); Britain and France held naval talks afterwards (1). **[4]**

**4.** Any four, 1 mark each: Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina in October 1908 (1); it had occupied the
provinces since 1878 (1); Serbia was furious because many Serbs lived there (1); Russia protested and wanted a
conference (1); Germany supported Austria-Hungary firmly (1); Russia, weakened after 1905, had to accept the annexation
(1); Russia resolved not to be humiliated again and rebuilt its army (1). **[4]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: Serbia (1); Bulgaria (1); Greece (1); Montenegro (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any four, 1 mark each: on 5–6 July Germany promised Austria-Hungary unconditional support, the "blank cheque" (1);
Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia for the assassination (1); on 23 July it sent Serbia an ultimatum (1) with a 48-hour
deadline (1); the demands were designed to be rejected, including Austrian involvement in the investigation inside
Serbia (1); Serbia accepted almost all the terms (1) but not every one (1); Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28
July (1). **[4]**

**7.** Levels. Level 4 (6): explains two reasons. Level 3 (4–5): explains one reason; 5 for a developed explanation.
Level 2 (2–3): identifies or describes reasons. Level 1 (1): general comment. Indicative content: **Russia's defeat by
Japan** (1904–05) and the 1905 revolution made Russia weaker and less threatening to British India and East Asia;
**fear of Germany**: both saw the growing German navy and Weltpolitik as a greater danger than each other; **France
linked them**: France was allied to Russia and had settled with Britain in 1904, so it encouraged agreement; **the
1907 convention settled old disputes** over Persia, Afghanistan and Tibet, removing the reasons for rivalry. **[6]**

**8.** Levels as Question 7. Indicative content: **Britain's security depended on its navy**, so any rival fleet in the
North Sea was seen as a direct threat to the island and its trade; **the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900** suggested Germany
aimed at Britain; **the Dreadnought** reset the race and led to public pressure for more ships in 1909; **cost**:
spending on ships fed suspicion and nationalist press campaigns on both sides; it pushed Britain closer to France,
including naval arrangements after 1912. **[6]**

**9.** Levels. Level 5 (9–10): explained arguments on both sides and a supported judgement. Level 4 (7–8): explains
both sides. Level 3 (4–6): explains one side. Level 2 (2–3): describes. Level 1 (1): general points. **Agree:** Fashoda
brought Britain and France close to war; the Kruger telegram and the Boer War damaged Anglo-German relations; the two
Moroccan crises were deliberate German challenges that hardened the Entente. **Disagree:** each colonial crisis was
resolved peacefully, and colonial agreements in 1904 and 1907 actually reduced tension between old rivals; the naval
race was a bigger cause of Anglo-German hostility; the Balkans caused the most dangerous rivalry, between
Austria-Hungary and Russia, and that is where war began; alliances and military plans spread it. **Judgement:** for
example, colonial rivalry created suspicion but was manageable; Balkan rivalry in Europe was more dangerous because it
touched the survival of Austria-Hungary. **[10]**

**10.** Levels as Question 9. **Agree:** alliances were defensive and deterred attack for long periods; Bismarck's
system kept peace for two decades; the powers settled crises at Algeciras and London; Germany restrained Austria-Hungary
in 1912–13. **Disagree:** two rival blocs bred fear of encirclement; alliance commitments, such as the blank cheque,
encouraged risk-taking in July 1914; military plans tied to alliances, especially Schlieffen, spread a Balkan war to
France and Belgium within days. **Judgement:** alliances made war less likely in individual crises but more destructive
and harder to stop once it began. **[10]**

**11.** Levels. Level 4 (6–7): explains a purpose using the source's content and the context of July 1914. Level 3
(4–5): explains the context or the message. Level 2 (2–3): describes the source's view. Level 1 (1): undeveloped
comment. Indicative content: **context**: the assassination of Franz Ferdinand on 28 June; Austrian belief that Serbia
was behind it; German support from 5–6 July; **purpose**: to build public support for strong action, even war, against
Serbia; to argue that failing to act would encourage Slav nationalism inside the empire and threaten its survival.
Top answers connect the article's fear of looking weak to Austria-Hungary's decision to send an ultimatum designed to
be rejected. **[7]**

**12.** Levels. Level 4 (6–7): explains how far the source helps, using its content, its limits and knowledge. Level
3 (4–5): explains how it helps or its limits. Level 2 (2–3): describes the source. Level 1 (1): general comment.
**Helps:** it shows why Russia would not back down over Serbia in 1914: memory of the Bosnian humiliation, its claim to
protect Slavs, and fear of Austrian expansion in the Balkans; Russian mobilisation on 30 July is what brought Germany
in. **Limits:** it explains only Russia's decision; it says nothing about the blank cheque, the Schlieffen Plan, the
invasion of Belgium or Britain's entry; it is one private view. **Judgement:** it helps to explain how an
Austro-Serbian war became a Russian one, but other factors explain the step to a general European war. **[7]**
