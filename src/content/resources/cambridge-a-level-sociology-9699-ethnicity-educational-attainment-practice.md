## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by "institutional racism" in education? **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by "ethnic capital"? **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between voluntary and involuntary minorities in Ogbu's theory. **[4]**

**4.** Describe what is meant by a "pro-education, anti-school" subculture, using one study. **[4]**

**5.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> In the fictional city of Port Arden, pupils reaching a benchmark at 16 were recorded by ethnic group and free school meal (FSM) eligibility.
>
> | Group | FSM pupils reaching benchmark | Non-FSM pupils reaching benchmark |
> |---|---|---|
> | Group P | 36 of 80 | 140 of 200 |
> | Group R | 54 of 90 | 120 of 150 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reaching the benchmark for FSM and non-FSM pupils in each group. **[4]**
**(b)** Using your answers, explain what the data suggest about the relationship between ethnicity and class. **[3]**

**6.** Explain two ways in which teachers' expectations may disadvantage some minority ethnic pupils. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two criticisms of cultural explanations of ethnic differences in attainment. **[6]**

**8.** Read the item.

> At Calder Park School, a fictional school, a group of girls from a minority ethnic background are often in trouble for "attitude" and rarely volunteer answers. They meet after school to revise together and all plan to go to university.

Using the item and sociological material, explain two ways in which the girls' response to school could be interpreted. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Ethnic differences in attainment are caused by racism in schools."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Minority ethnic pupils who form subcultures are rejecting education."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that cultural factors are more important than school processes in explaining ethnic differences in educational attainment. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that ethnicity cannot be understood separately from social class and gender in explaining educational attainment. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** Discrimination built into an organisation's routines and procedures [1], such as setting, exclusions or exam tiering, that disadvantages some ethnic groups whatever individual teachers intend [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Aspirations, values and community or family networks within an ethnic group [1] that help children succeed in education even when their family's class position is modest (Modood, 2004) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Voluntary minorities migrated by choice [1], so they tend to see education as a route to improving their position [1]. Involuntary minorities were incorporated through slavery, conquest or colonisation [1], so they may distrust institutions such as schools that have historically excluded them [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Pupils value qualifications and work hard [1] but reject the school's authority and do not seek teachers' approval [1]. Fuller (1984) found Black girls in a London comprehensive worked hard while keeping friendships with less studious peers [1], using success to reject the negative labels they faced [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Group P: FSM 36 ÷ 80 × 100 = 45% [1]; non-FSM 140 ÷ 200 × 100 = 70% [1]. Group R: FSM 54 ÷ 90 × 100 = 60% [1]; non-FSM 120 ÷ 150 × 100 = 80% [1].
**(b)** In both groups FSM pupils do worse than non-FSM pupils, so class affects attainment within each ethnic group [1]. But Group R does better than Group P at both FSM and non-FSM level [1], so an ethnic difference remains when this measure of class is held constant, suggesting ethnicity and class both matter [1]. **[7]**

**6.** Way 1: stereotyped judgements of ability [1]. Wright (1992) found primary teachers assumed Asian pupils had poor English and left them out of discussions [1], so these pupils received less challenge and participation [1].
Way 2: expectations of bad behaviour [1]. Wright found Black Caribbean boys were expected to misbehave and disciplined more often and harshly [1]; such treatment can lead to lower sets, exclusion or resistance that lowers attainment [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Criticism 1: they can blame the victim [1]. Deficit accounts such as Bereiter and Engelmann's description of Black children's language as deficient ignore Labov's evidence that non-standard dialects support complex reasoning [1], and shift attention from school processes to families [1].
Criticism 2: they treat groups as uniform [1]. Ogbu's voluntary and involuntary model and similar accounts assume one culture per group [1], ignoring differences of class, gender and individual response within groups [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Interpretation 1: a pro-education, anti-school stance [1]. Like Fuller's Black girls or Mac an Ghaill's Black Sisters, the girls value qualifications and plan for university while rejecting the school's authority [1], so "attitude" may reflect resistance to labels rather than lack of commitment [1].
Interpretation 2: a response to teacher labelling [1]. Teachers' judgement of "attitude" may reflect stereotyped expectations of minority pupils, as Wright found [1]; the girls may avoid volunteering answers to limit contact with teachers they see as unfair, while working independently to succeed [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: the view cannot explain variation between groups [1]. Chinese and Indian pupils in England have long achieved above average in the same system accused of racism [1]. Modood's ethnic capital suggests aspirations and networks outside school help explain this [1], so causes outside school matter [1].
Argument 2: the evidence for teacher racism is contested [1]. Foster (1990) found little evidence of racism in one multi-ethnic comprehensive [1] and argued earlier ethnographies over-interpreted classroom interaction [1]. It is unsafe to treat racism as the single cause, although institutional processes may still contribute [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: many subcultures are pro-education [1]. Fuller (1984) found Black girls rejected school authority but worked hard for qualifications [1]. Mac an Ghaill's Black Sisters took a similar stance [1], so rejecting school is not the same as rejecting education [1].
Argument 2: subcultures can be a response to how pupils are treated [1]. Sewell's rebels and Mac an Ghaill's Rasta Heads resisted a school they saw as unfair [1]; Wright's evidence of harsher discipline for Black Caribbean boys helps explain why [1]. The rejection is of the school's treatment, not necessarily of learning [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** cultural factors — deficit accounts and their critics; ethnic capital (Modood); Ogbu's voluntary and involuntary minorities; Fordham and Ogbu; Swann Report; school processes — Wright, Coard, Gillborn, Gillborn and Youdell, institutional racism.
- **Application (AO2):** variation between groups in a named country; setting and exclusion practices.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** Foster's sceptical evidence versus ethnographies that find racism; subcultures (Fuller, Mac an Ghaill, Sewell) as responses to school processes, showing interaction; class profiles of groups; risk of homogenising cultures.
- **Judgement:** for example, cultural factors help explain differences between groups, but school processes shape how those cultures play out in school.

**Level guidance:** top answers weigh both sides with accurate evidence and address variation between groups; middle answers describe cultural and school factors in turn; low answers generalise about "minorities".

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view:** intersectionality; class profiles differ between groups; Fuller and Mac an Ghaill show gendered responses within one ethnic group; studies of middle-class Black families show class resources do not fully protect against racism; white working-class boys' low attainment.
- **Against the view:** ethnic gaps can persist when class is held constant; racism and ethnic capital are specific to ethnicity; some ethnicity effects appear for both sexes.
- **Evaluation:** consider how class is measured; distinguish adding factors together from showing how they change one another; use evidence from more than one society.
- **Judgement:** for example, ethnicity has distinct effects, but they take different forms depending on class and gender.

**Level guidance:** top answers show how factors interact, not just that they coexist; middle answers discuss each factor separately; low answers assert that "everything matters".
