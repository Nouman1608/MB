specification, for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.7, Organic chemistry, and is assessed on
Paper 2. All of it is assessed at both Foundation and Higher Tier, and none of it is chemistry only (the
combined science course covers it too). There is no Higher tier only statement in this section.

## 4.7.1.1 Crude oil, hydrocarbons and alkanes

**Crude oil** is a **finite resource** found in rocks. It is the remains of an ancient **biomass**, mainly
**plankton buried in mud**, which changed over millions of years. It is a **mixture of a very large number of
compounds**, which are not chemically joined to each other. Most are **hydrocarbons**, molecules made of
**hydrogen and carbon atoms only**.

Most of the hydrocarbons in crude oil are **alkanes**, forming a **homologous series** with the general
formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**. Members of a series have similar chemical properties and a regular change in physical
properties. The first four are:

| Alkane | Formula | Carbon atoms |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | 1 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | 2 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | 3 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | 4 |

You do not need the names of other alkanes, but you must work out a formula from n. For n = 5, C₅H₁₂. For
n = 20, C₂₀H₄₂ (2 × 20 + 2 = 42).

Alkanes can be shown as a molecular formula (C₂H₆), a **displayed formula** (every atom and bond drawn), or
a condensed formula (CH₃CH₃). To **recognise an alkane**: it contains only carbon and hydrogen, has only single
bonds, and the hydrogen count is **twice the carbon count plus two**. C₆H₁₂ is not an alkane, because 2 × 6
+ 2 = 14, not 12.

## 4.7.1.2 Fractional distillation and petrochemicals

Crude oil is separated into **fractions** by **fractional distillation**. Each fraction contains molecules
with a **similar number of carbon atoms**, so it has a similar boiling range.

**How the process works, in terms of evaporation and condensation:**

1. The crude oil is **heated** until most of it **evaporates** (turns to vapour).
2. The vapour enters the bottom of a tall **fractionating column**. The column is **hot at the bottom and
   cooler at the top**.
3. The vapours **rise**. As each hydrocarbon reaches a part of the column that is **below its boiling point**,
   it **condenses** to a liquid and is collected.
4. **Large molecules** with high boiling points condense **near the bottom**. **Small molecules** with low
   boiling points travel **higher** and condense near the top, or leave as gases.

| Position in column | Molecule size | Boiling point | Examples of use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | Small | Low | Liquefied petroleum gases (cooking and heating) |
| Upper | Short | Fairly low | Petrol for cars |
| Middle | Medium | Medium | Kerosene for aircraft |
| Lower | Longer | Higher | Diesel oil for lorries, buses, trains |
| Bottom | Very long | Very high | Heavy fuel oil for ships and power stations |

The fractions are used as **fuels** and as **feedstock** for the **petrochemical industry**, which makes
**solvents, lubricants, polymers and detergents**. The enormous range of natural and synthetic carbon compounds
exists because carbon atoms form **families of similar compounds** by joining in chains and rings.

## 4.7.1.3 Properties of hydrocarbons

Some properties depend on the **size of the molecules**. As the number of carbon atoms in a hydrocarbon **increases**:

| Property | Trend with longer chains | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling point | **Increases** | Stronger forces between larger molecules need more energy to overcome |
| Viscosity | **Increases** (thicker, flows less easily) | More tangling and stronger forces between longer chains |
| Flammability | **Decreases** (harder to ignite) | Less volatile, so fewer vapour molecules mix with air |

These trends explain uses. Small molecules (LPG, petrol) ignite easily, so they suit fuels for engines.
Long, viscous molecules are better as lubricants or for tarring roads, and they do not ignite as easily.

### Combustion

The **combustion** of hydrocarbon fuels **releases energy**. In **complete combustion** (plenty of oxygen), the
carbon and the hydrogen are both **oxidised**, giving **carbon dioxide and water**:

hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

You must be able to write balanced equations for given formulae. Method: balance **carbon first**, then
**hydrogen**, then **oxygen** last.

### Worked example 1: propane

C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

1. Carbon: 3 on the left, so 3CO₂.
2. Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so 4H₂O.
3. Oxygen on the right: 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 atoms, so 5O₂.

**C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O**

### Worked example 2: butane (an odd number of oxygen atoms)

C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O. Oxygen on the right: 8 + 5 = 13 atoms, which is 6.5 molecules. Double
everything to avoid a fraction:

**2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O**

### Worked example 3: octane

C₈H₁₈: carbon gives 8CO₂, hydrogen gives 9H₂O. Oxygen needed: 16 + 9 = 25 atoms, so 12.5 O₂. Double:

**2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O**

## 4.7.1.4 Cracking and alkenes

**Cracking** breaks down large hydrocarbons into **smaller, more useful molecules**. The supply of long-chain
hydrocarbons from crude oil is greater than the demand for them, and there is a **high demand for fuels with
small molecules** such as petrol.

### Conditions

- **Catalytic cracking:** the vaporised hydrocarbon is passed over a hot **catalyst** (for example a
  zeolite or aluminium oxide catalyst) at a high temperature.
- **Steam cracking:** the hydrocarbon is mixed with **steam** and heated to a very high temperature.

Cracking is a **thermal decomposition** reaction. The products include **alkanes** (shorter, used as fuels) and
**alkenes**, another type of hydrocarbon. Alkenes are used to make **polymers** and as starting materials for
many other chemicals.

### Worked example 4: balancing a cracking equation

*Decane, C₁₀H₂₂, is cracked to produce octane and one other product. Identify the other product.*

1. Carbon: 10 on the left, 8 in octane (C₈H₁₈), so 2 carbons remain.
2. Hydrogen: 22 on the left, 18 in octane, so 4 remain.
3. The remaining molecule is C₂H₄, which is **ethene**, an alkene.

**C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄**

Check by counting: C 10 = 8 + 2, and H 22 = 18 + 4.

A cracking equation can have more than two products. For example C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₄H₈: carbon is 8 + 8 = 16
and hydrogen is 18 + 16 = 34.

### Testing for alkenes

**Alkenes** are more reactive than alkanes. They react with **bromine water**, which is used as a **test for
alkenes**. Shake the sample with bromine water:

- **Alkene:** bromine water changes from **orange (brown) to colourless**.
- **Alkane:** it stays orange.

### Why cracking and hydrocarbons matter

Examples to quote: cracking makes **petrol** and other fuels from heavy fractions; it makes **ethene and propene**
for poly(ethene) and poly(propene); and modern life depends on hydrocarbons for transport fuels, heating,
plastics, lubricants and detergents.

## Exam technique

- For fractional distillation, use the words **evaporates**, **condenses** and **boiling point** and say
  where the large and small molecules end up.
- For trends, always say "**as the chain length increases**" and name the direction of the change.
- For equations, balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen, and count atoms on each side at the end.
- For the alkene test, state **both** colours: orange to colourless.

## Common mistakes

- Saying crude oil is a compound or that it is "separated by boiling in a flask". It is a mixture, separated by
  fractional distillation.
- Saying large molecules have a low boiling point.
- Writing that the bromine water "turns clear". Say **colourless**; clear is not a colour.
- Forgetting that complete combustion needs plenty of oxygen.
- Writing C₂H₄ for an alkane. Alkenes have 2n hydrogens, alkanes 2n + 2.
- Giving a cracking equation with unequal numbers of atoms on each side.
