This guide covers section 4.1 **The role of government** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus has one content point, the role of government, with the guidance "locally, nationally and internationally".

The combined [role of government and macroeconomic aims guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-the-role-of-government-and-macroeconomic-aims/) gives a table of the three levels and the reasons governments are needed. This page goes further: it sets out the separate roles a government plays in an economy (provider, producer, employer, buyer, regulator, redistributor and manager of the economy), shows how each role looks at each level, explains why some problems need action at a particular level, and works through a problem that crosses all three. Companion pages: [role of government revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-government-revision-notes/) and [role of government practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-role-of-government-practice/). Places and figures are invented unless a real organisation is named.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Content point | Guidance | You must be able to |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1.1 The role of government | Locally, nationally and internationally | Describe and explain what government does at each level, and why the level matters |

This section sets the scene for the rest of Topic 4. The tools a government uses (fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy) come in 4.3 to 4.5; here you need to know what governments are responsible for and where.

## Why an economy needs a government

The [market economic system guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-economic-system/) shows that markets leave gaps: public goods are not provided, merit goods are under-consumed, external costs are ignored, monopolies can exploit consumers, incomes can be very unequal and output and jobs can be unstable. Even a market economy needs a government to enforce property rights and contracts. The role of government follows from these gaps.

## Seven roles of government

| Role | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Provider | Supplies goods and services that markets under-provide | Street lighting, schools, flood defences |
| Producer | Owns and runs firms (state-owned enterprises) | A state railway or electricity company |
| Employer | Employs workers directly | Teachers, nurses, police, civil servants |
| Buyer | Purchases goods and services from private firms | Buying medicines, building roads through contractors |
| Regulator | Makes and enforces rules for firms and consumers | Food safety standards, pollution limits, competition law |
| Redistributor | Moves income from richer to poorer people | Progressive taxes, pensions, unemployment benefits |
| Manager of the economy | Pursues the macroeconomic aims | Using taxes, spending and interest rates to support growth and stable prices |

Each role can be carried out at more than one level, but some roles naturally belong to one level.

## The role locally

**Local government** (councils, municipalities or city authorities) serves one town, city or district. Typical roles:

- **Provider**: waste collection, street cleaning, local roads, parks, libraries, markets and, in some countries, primary schools.
- **Regulator**: planning permission for buildings, licences for market stalls and taxis, local health inspections of restaurants.
- **Employer**: local government is often one of the largest employers in a town.

It is funded by local taxes (such as property taxes), charges for services and grants from the national government.

**Why act locally?** Local officials know local conditions: which roads are most congested, where a new clinic is needed. Decisions can be made quickly and residents can hold their council to account. The limits are small budgets, unequal resources between rich and poor areas, and problems that spill over into neighbouring districts.

## The role nationally

**National (central) government** acts for the whole country. Typical roles:

- **Law and defence**: courts, police systems, armed forces and border control.
- **Provider and producer**: national health and education systems, national roads and railways, state-owned firms in some industries.
- **Regulator**: competition law to control monopolies, consumer protection, health and safety at work, minimum wages, environmental standards.
- **Redistributor**: income tax, pensions, unemployment and disability benefits.
- **Manager of the economy**: setting the national budget, deciding taxes and spending, and (usually through the central bank) influencing interest rates, to pursue growth, low unemployment, stable prices, balance of payments stability and redistribution.

**Why act nationally?** Some goods serve everyone in the country at once, such as defence. Some problems need a common rule everywhere, such as food safety, so firms cannot simply move to the area with the weakest rules. And only the national government can borrow and tax on the scale needed to manage the whole economy.

## The role internationally

Governments also act together with other countries. Typical roles:

- **Trade agreements**: cutting or removing barriers to trade with other countries, or agreeing common rules.
- **Membership of international organisations**: for example the World Trade Organization, which sets rules for international trade and settles trade disputes between members; the International Monetary Fund, which can lend to countries with balance of payments problems; and the World Bank, which lends for development projects.
- **Shared problems**: agreements on climate change, cross-border pollution, shared rivers and fish stocks, and the spread of disease.
- **Aid**: giving or receiving help for development or after disasters.

**Why act internationally?** When a problem crosses borders, no single government can solve it alone. If one country cuts its fishing but others do not, the fish stock still collapses and the careful country simply loses income. Agreement means every country carries part of the cost and shares the benefit.

## Worked example: one problem, three levels

A factory in invented Town R, in Country S, pollutes a river that flows on into neighbouring Country T.

| Level | Role | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Regulator | Town R's council inspects the site, can refuse permission to expand, and warns residents not to use river water |
| National | Regulator and provider | Country S sets a legal pollution limit for all factories, fines firms that break it, and funds a water treatment plant |
| International | Shared problem | Countries S and T agree joint limits and share the cost of monitoring the river where it crosses the border |

Each level does what the others cannot. The council can act fastest, but has no power over pollution standards across the country. The national government can set and enforce a law for its own factories, but part of the damage falls in Country T, which has no say in Country S's law. An international agreement gives both countries a say and covers the whole river.

## Can government do too much?

The syllabus question is what government does, but strong answers recognise limits. Government action costs money raised through taxes; officials may lack information or respond slowly; and state-run services may face little pressure to cut costs. The right size of the government's role depends on how serious the market's failure is and how well the government can correct it, a judgement developed in the [mixed economic system guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-the-mixed-economic-system-and-government-intervention/).

## Common mistakes

- Describing only the national level when the question asks about local or international roles.
- Listing services without explaining why the government, rather than the market, provides them.
- Confusing the role of government (4.1) with the policy tools used to carry it out (4.3 to 4.5).
- Saying international organisations "control" countries. They act through agreements that member governments accept.
- Writing that local government can solve problems that spill across its borders.

## Exam technique for 4.1

- **Identify** or **State** a role: name it and give an example.
- **Describe** the role at a level: give two or three things that level of government does.
- **Explain** why a problem needs action at a particular level: link the scale of the problem to the power of that level.
- **Discuss** whether a problem is best dealt with locally, nationally or internationally: weigh local knowledge and speed against national power and international reach, and reach a judgement for the problem given.
