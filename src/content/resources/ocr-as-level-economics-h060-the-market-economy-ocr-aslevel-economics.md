## What makes an economy a market economy

A **market economy** (free market economy) is one in which most resources are privately owned and most decisions about production and consumption are made by households and firms, coordinated by prices. Its key features are:

- **Private property:** individuals and firms own land and capital and can buy, sell and use them.
- **The price mechanism:** prices rise and fall with demand and supply and carry information to everyone in the market.
- **The profit motive:** firms produce what earns a profit, so they follow consumer spending.
- **Consumer sovereignty:** consumers' spending decisions, in principle, steer what is produced.
- **Competition:** many sellers compete for customers, which pushes prices toward costs.

### How it answers the three questions

| Question | Answer in a market economy |
|---|---|
| What? | Whatever consumers are willing and able to pay for, at a price that covers costs |
| How? | The methods that minimise cost, because lower costs raise profit |
| For whom? | Those with the income or wealth to pay, which depends on what factors they own and what they earn |

**Example (invented).** Demand for electric bicycles rises in a city. Shops sell out and raise prices. Higher prices make cycle production more profitable, so firms hire workers and buy parts, and new firms enter. Nobody instructs them: the price change does the coordinating. Meanwhile demand for petrol scooters falls, prices are cut, and resources move out of that industry.

### Planned and mixed economies

In a **planned economy**, the state owns most resources and a central authority decides what is produced, how and for whom, often setting prices and output targets. A **mixed economy** combines both: markets allocate most goods, while the government provides some (such as defence and much of health care and education), taxes, regulates and redistributes. Every real economy is mixed; they differ in the share of decisions taken by the state. The UK, for example, has a large private sector alongside publicly funded health care and schooling.

## Strengths of a market economy

Each point needs a chain of reasoning, not just a label.

- **Information:** prices gather information from millions of buyers and sellers. A planner could not know every household's wants; a price summarises them.
- **Incentives:** profit rewards firms that cut costs or introduce products people want, so resources tend to move to their most valued uses.
- **Choice:** competition gives consumers a wide range of goods and the freedom to switch sellers.
- **Responsiveness:** when tastes or technology change, prices adjust and resources follow without waiting for a central decision.

## Weaknesses of a market economy

- **Missing goods:** goods that people cannot be excluded from, such as street lighting or flood defences, may not be supplied at all, because firms cannot charge for them.
- **External costs and benefits:** a factory's pollution harms people who are not part of the sale, so the market produces too much; vaccination benefits others, so the market produces too little.
- **Information problems:** consumers may undervalue goods that are good for them, such as education, or overvalue harmful ones.
- **Inequality:** those with little income have little say in what is produced; the market answers "for whom" by ability to pay, not by need.
- **Market power:** a dominant firm can restrict output and raise prices, weakening consumer sovereignty.
- **Instability:** booms and recessions can leave resources, especially labour, unused.

These weaknesses are developed later in the course under market failure and government intervention.

## The role of government within a market economy

Even a largely free market needs government to:

- enforce property rights and contracts through the law, without which markets cannot operate;
- provide goods the market will not supply, such as defence;
- correct external costs and benefits through taxes, subsidies and regulation;
- protect consumers and promote competition;
- redistribute income through taxes and benefits;
- try to keep the economy stable.

The debate is about how much intervention is worthwhile, because intervention has costs too: it uses resources and can create new problems.

## Decisions at the margin

The **margin** is the point of deciding about one more, or one less, unit of something. Economists assume agents compare the extra benefit of a unit with its extra cost.

**Worked example (invented).** A café closes at 6pm and is considering staying open later.

| Extra hour | Extra revenue | Extra cost (staff, energy, food) |
|---|---|---|
| 6pm to 7pm | £85 | £60 |
| 7pm to 8pm | £50 | £60 |

The first extra hour adds £25 to profit, so it is worth opening. The second adds £50 of revenue but £60 of cost, reducing profit by £10. The café should stay open until 7pm. Notice that the decision does not depend on the café's total profit for the day, only on the extra revenue and extra cost of each hour.

**Rationality** is the assumption that agents act consistently to achieve their aims, given the information they have: consumers to maximise satisfaction, firms to maximise profit, and government to maximise the welfare of society. It makes models predictable. Its limits are that people often have incomplete information, act out of habit, or are swayed by how choices are presented, so real decisions can depart from the model.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain one weakness of a market economy. [3]*

> In a market economy goods are produced for those who can pay. Households on low incomes have little spending power, so their needs, such as housing, may not be met even when resources exist to meet them. The market answers "for whom" according to income rather than need, which many people regard as unfair.

*Why it works:* it names the weakness, explains the mechanism through the "for whom" question, and adds an example. The final sentence signals a normative judgement, which is clearly labelled as such.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a market economy as one with "no government at all".
- Saying planned economies have no prices.
- Listing strengths with no explanation of how prices or profit cause them.
- Treating inequality as a positive fact rather than a normative concern.
- Using total revenue and total cost, instead of extra revenue and extra cost, for a marginal decision.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items may describe an economy and ask you to identify its type. Look for who owns resources and who makes decisions.
- **Section B:** in data questions, link a strength or weakness to the specific market in the data.
- **Section C:** an essay on the market economy needs both sides and a judgement that depends on the kind of good: markets work well for many consumer goods but poorly for public goods and goods with large external effects.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is any country a pure market economy?**
No. All economies are mixed; they differ in how much the state owns, spends and regulates.

**Does consumer sovereignty always hold?**
Not fully. Advertising, market power and lack of information can mean firms shape what consumers buy as much as consumers shape what firms make.
