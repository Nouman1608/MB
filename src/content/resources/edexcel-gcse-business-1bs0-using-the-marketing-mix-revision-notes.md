These notes condense content item 2.2.5 Using the marketing mix to make business decisions, from Topic 2.2 Making marketing decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples are in the [using the marketing mix study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-using-the-marketing-mix/) and exam-style questions in the [using the marketing mix practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-using-the-marketing-mix-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

1. How each element of the marketing mix can influence other elements.
2. Using the marketing mix to build competitive advantage.
3. How an integrated marketing mix can influence competitive advantage.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Marketing mix | The four elements a business controls to market a product: product, price, promotion, place |
| Competitive advantage | Something that makes customers choose a business over rivals and is hard to copy (standard term) |
| Integrated marketing mix | A mix in which all four elements are consistent and aimed at the same target market |
| Positioning | How a business wants customers to see its product compared with rivals, such as premium or budget (standard term) |
| Low-cost advantage | Winning customers through lower prices made possible by lower costs |
| Differentiation advantage | Winning customers through a product they see as different or better |

## The knock-on chain

Always trace at least two steps.

- **Product up-market** → higher unit cost → higher price → promotion must justify the price → place must match (upmarket outlets).
- **Price cut** → lower profit per unit → costs must fall → simpler product or packaging → promotion stresses value → place in high-volume outlets.
- **Heavy promotion** → demand rises → place must have stock and coverage → product must match the claims → price must cover the cost of the campaign.
- **Move online only** → no shop costs but delivery costs → price may change → promotion must move online → packaging must protect the product in transit.

## Building competitive advantage with each element

| Element | Source of advantage | How rivals might respond |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Unique design, quality, features | Copy the feature |
| Price | Lower prices from lower costs | Cut their own prices |
| Promotion | A trusted, recognisable brand | Spend more on their own brand, but this is slow |
| Place | Convenience: location, opening hours, delivery speed | Open nearby or speed up delivery |

An advantage built on one element is easier to copy than one built on all four working together.

## Integrated or not? A checklist

Ask of any case:

- Who is the target market?
- Does the product suit them?
- Does the price fit both the product and the target market's incomes?
- Does the promotion reach them and send the same message as the product and price?
- Is the product sold where they shop?

If any answer is "no", the mix is not integrated, and that element weakens the advantage.

**Quick example.** A luxury chocolate brand at £12 a box, promoted in glossy magazines, but sold in a discount shop on "three for two": the place and the special offer contradict the premium product, price and promotion.

## Why integration helps

1. Clear message: customers understand what is offered and for whom.
2. Credibility: price is believed when everything else matches it.
3. Hard to copy: rivals must copy a whole system, not one element.
4. No wasted spending on elements that work against each other.

## Quick calculation

Bracken Candles adds gift boxes (+£1.20 unit cost) and raises its price from £14 to £18. Unit cost was £5. Sales fall from 600 to 520 a month. Contribution: before £9 × 600 = £5,400; after (£18 − £6.20) × 520 = £11.80 × 520 = **£6,136**. The product change works because price and packaging are integrated around gifting.

## Exam traps

- Listing the four Ps without linking them.
- Saying "the mix gives a competitive advantage" without naming which element and why it is hard to copy.
- Mixing premium and budget elements in a recommendation.
- Defining integrated as "all elements are good".
- Ignoring the cost of changing an element.

## Self-test

1. Name the four elements of the marketing mix.
2. Define competitive advantage.
3. What is an integrated marketing mix?
4. Give one way a product improvement can affect price.
5. Give one way a move to online-only selling affects promotion.
6. Why is an advantage based only on price easy to copy?
7. Give one reason why an integrated mix makes competitive advantage stronger.
8. A premium handbag is sold in a pound shop. Which element is inconsistent?
9. A business cuts its price from £20 to £16 and sales rise from 1,000 to 1,150. Unit variable cost is £8. Calculate the change in contribution.
10. Give an example of place creating a competitive advantage.
11. State the two broad ways a business can build competitive advantage.
12. Why might heavy promotion fail if place is not adjusted?

### Answers

1. Product, price, promotion, place.
2. Something that makes customers choose a business over its rivals and that rivals find hard to copy.
3. A mix in which all four elements are consistent with each other and aimed at the same target market.
4. Higher quality raises unit costs and value, so the price is usually raised.
5. Promotion must move online, such as targeted adverts, to bring visitors to the website.
6. A rival can cut its own price quickly, unless the business has lower costs that rivals cannot match.
7. Any one: clearer message; a more believable price; harder to copy as a whole; less wasted spending.
8. Place.
9. Before: £12 × 1,000 = £12,000. After: £8 × 1,150 = £9,200. Contribution falls by **£2,800**.
10. Faster delivery, late opening hours or a location closer to customers than rivals.
11. Lower cost (lower prices) or differentiation (a product seen as different or better).
12. Demand rises but customers cannot find the product in stock nearby, so they buy a rival's product instead.
