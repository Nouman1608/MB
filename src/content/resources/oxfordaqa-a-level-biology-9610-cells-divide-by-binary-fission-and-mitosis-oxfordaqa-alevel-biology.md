## Tracking chromosomes, chromatids and DNA

Most cell cycle calculations rest on one idea: in S phase every chromosome is copied, but the two copies stay joined as sister chromatids until anaphase. The number of chromosomes is counted by centromeres, so it does not double until the centromeres divide.

**Worked example 1.** A body cell of an animal has 2n = 12 chromosomes, and a G1 nucleus contains 6.0 arbitrary units of DNA.

| Stage | Chromosomes in the cell | Chromatids per chromosome | DNA (arbitrary units) |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 12 | 1 | 6.0 |
| End of S and G2 | 12 | 2 | 12.0 |
| Prophase, metaphase | 12 | 2 | 12.0 |
| Anaphase (whole cell) | 24 separated chromatids, 12 moving to each pole | 1 | 12.0 |
| Each daughter cell after division | 12 | 1 | 6.0 |

Three things to notice:

- DNA doubles during S phase, rising gradually, not in a single step.
- DNA in the cell halves only when the cytoplasm divides at the end, after telophase.
- Each daughter cell has the same chromosome number and the same DNA content as the parent cell in G1. That is what "identical DNA" means in the specification.

On a graph of DNA mass per cell against time, this gives a flat line in G1, a steady climb through S, a higher flat line through G2 and mitosis, and a sudden drop as the cell divides.

## Why each phase of interphase matters

- **G1:** the cell has just divided and is small. It makes proteins and organelles such as mitochondria and ribosomes, so that it can grow and carry out its functions. A cell that will never divide again stays in a non-dividing state; only cells that retain the ability to divide go on through the cycle.
- **S phase:** DNA is replicated, so that each daughter cell can receive a complete copy.
- **G2:** rapid growth and protein synthesis continue, including the proteins needed for division, such as those that make up the spindle.

A common misconception is that interphase is a resting stage. It is when the cell grows, makes its proteins and copies its DNA; most of the cycle is spent there.

## Mitosis: what the chromosomes do

| Stage | Chromosome behaviour | Spindle |
|---|---|---|
| Prophase | Chromosomes condense, becoming short and thick, and are visible as two chromatids joined at a centromere; the nuclear envelope breaks down | Spindle fibres begin to form |
| Metaphase | Chromosomes line up across the equator of the cell | Spindle fibres attach to the centromere of each chromosome |
| Anaphase | Centromeres divide; sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles, centromere first | Spindle fibres shorten, pulling the chromatids apart |
| Telophase | Chromatids (now chromosomes) arrive at the poles and become less distinct | Spindle breaks down; nuclear envelopes reform |

After telophase, the cytoplasm divides and two genetically identical daughter cells form.

**Why the spindle matters.** Each sister chromatid is attached by its centromere to a fibre from the opposite pole. When the fibres shorten, one chromatid of every chromosome goes to each pole. Without spindle fibres, the chromatids would not separate in an organised way, and the daughter cells would not each receive one copy of every chromosome.

## Recognising stages from photographs

Use visible features, in this order:

1. Is there a nucleus with a boundary and no visible chromosomes? → **Interphase**.
2. Are chromosomes visible but scattered, with no clear arrangement? → **Prophase**.
3. Are chromosomes in a single line across the middle? → **Metaphase**.
4. Are there two groups moving apart, often V-shaped with the centromere leading? → **Anaphase**.
5. Are there two groups at opposite ends, perhaps with a new cell wall or a furrow between them? → **Telophase**.

In a root tip photograph, most cells are in interphase, because interphase takes up most of the cycle.

## Timing the stages of mitosis

If cells are dividing at random, the proportion found in a stage is proportional to the time that stage takes.

**Worked example 2 (illustrative counts).** In a root tip squash, 80 cells were seen in mitosis: 48 in prophase, 12 in metaphase, 8 in anaphase and 12 in telophase. Mitosis takes 90 minutes in this tissue.

- Prophase: 48 ÷ 80 × 90 = 54 minutes.
- Metaphase: 12 ÷ 80 × 90 = 13.5 minutes.
- Anaphase: 8 ÷ 80 × 90 = 9 minutes.
- Telophase: 12 ÷ 80 × 90 = 13.5 minutes.
- Check: 54 + 13.5 + 9 + 13.5 = 90 minutes.

Prophase is the longest stage here, and anaphase the shortest. The same method gives the mitotic index used in required practical 4.

## Binary fission compared with mitosis

Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and their DNA is a circular molecule, not linear chromosomes. They divide by binary fission:

1. The circular DNA replicates.
2. Any plasmids replicate.
3. The cytoplasm divides, producing two daughter cells.

| Feature | Mitosis (eukaryotes) | Binary fission (prokaryotes) |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic material | Linear chromosomes in a nucleus | Circular DNA in the cytoplasm, plus plasmids |
| Stages | Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase | No such stages |
| Spindle | Yes | No |
| Nuclear envelope | Breaks down and reforms | None present |
| Daughter cells | Identical DNA | One copy of the circular DNA each; number of plasmids may vary |

Because plasmids are not shared out precisely, two daughter cells can carry different numbers of plasmids, and so different numbers of copies of any plasmid gene.

## Conjugation and horizontal gene transmission

During conjugation, a bacterium passes DNA, typically a copy of a plasmid, to another bacterium, which can be of a different species. This is **horizontal** gene transmission: genes move between existing cells. Binary fission is **vertical** transmission: genes pass from a parent cell to its daughter cells.

The significance is speed and range. A gene for antibiotic resistance on a plasmid can spread through a mixed population of bacteria, including other species, far faster than by mutation and division alone.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Describe the behaviour of the chromosomes during mitosis. [5 marks]

**Answer:** In prophase the chromosomes condense and become visible, each made of two chromatids joined at a centromere (1). In metaphase the chromosomes line up at the equator, attached to spindle fibres by their centromeres (1). In anaphase the centromeres divide (1) and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids to opposite poles (1). In telophase the chromatids reach the poles, become less distinct and nuclear envelopes form around them (1).

*Why it scores:* every stage is named with what the chromosomes do, and the spindle's role is linked to centromeres, as the specification states. Naming the stages without describing chromosome behaviour scores very little.

## Common mistakes

- Saying DNA replicates during prophase. It replicates in S phase.
- Counting chromatids as chromosomes in G2 and metaphase.
- Saying chromosomes separate in metaphase. They separate in anaphase.
- Calling binary fission "bacterial mitosis".
- Saying each daughter bacterium receives the same number of plasmids.
- Describing conjugation as reproduction. It transfers DNA; it does not make new cells.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** mitosis stage by stage, naming chromosome and spindle behaviour.
- **Explain** why daughter cells are identical: DNA replicated in S phase, sister chromatids separated so each cell receives one copy of each chromosome.
- **Identify** stages: state the visible feature that justifies your choice.
- **Calculate** stage durations or chromosome numbers, showing the proportion used.
- **Compare** binary fission and mitosis with paired statements.

## Frequently asked questions

**When does the number of chromosomes in a cell double?**
At anaphase, when the centromeres divide and the sister chromatids separate. DNA doubles earlier, during S phase.

**Why can daughter bacteria have different numbers of plasmids?**
In binary fission the circular DNA is shared precisely, one copy to each cell, but plasmids are distributed without a spindle, so their numbers can differ.
