## The covalent bond

A covalent bond is a **shared pair of electrons** between two atoms. Each nucleus attracts the shared pair, and that attraction between the positive nuclei and the negative electron pair holds the atoms together. The atoms reach a stable outer shell by sharing, not by transfer.

- A **single bond** is one shared pair (H–H, shown as a line).
- A **double bond** is two shared pairs (O=O).
- A **triple bond** is three shared pairs (N≡N).

Electrons not used in bonding stay on the atom as **lone pairs**. A lone pair is a pair of outer electrons that belongs to one atom and is not involved in bonding.

### Counting method for dot-and-cross diagrams

1. Count the outer electrons of each atom (the group number for main-group elements).
2. Add one electron for each negative charge, or subtract one for each positive charge.
3. Share pairs until each atom has a full outer shell, normally eight electrons (two for hydrogen).
4. Check that the total number of electrons drawn matches step 1 and 2.

### Worked example 1: nitrogen, N₂

Each nitrogen has 5 outer electrons, so 10 in total. A triple bond uses 6; the remaining 4 form one lone pair on each atom. Each nitrogen then has 6 + 2 = 8 electrons. Displayed formula: N≡N with a lone pair on each atom.

### Worked example 2: carbon dioxide, CO₂

Carbon has 4 outer electrons and each oxygen has 6, so 16 in total. Two double bonds use 8 electrons. Each oxygen keeps two lone pairs, using the remaining 8. Displayed formula: O=C=O. Carbon has 8 electrons from the four shared pairs, and each oxygen has 4 shared plus 4 non-bonding.

### Worked example 3: hydrogen cyanide, HCN

H has 1, C has 4 and N has 5: total 10. H–C uses 2 electrons. C≡N uses 6. A lone pair on N uses the last 2. Displayed formula: H–C≡N, with a lone pair on nitrogen. Carbon has 4 pairs shared (8 electrons); hydrogen has the shared pair (2 electrons).

## The dative covalent (co-ordinate) bond

A **dative covalent bond** is a shared pair of electrons in which **both electrons come from one atom**. The atom that supplies the pair (the donor) must have a lone pair. The atom that receives it (the acceptor) must have an empty orbital in its outer shell, or be a species with a vacancy such as H⁺.

Once formed, a dative bond is **identical** to a normal covalent bond with the same atoms. This is why all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are the same length and strength. The arrow in the diagram records only where the electrons came from.

### Worked example 4: the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺

NH₃ has a lone pair on nitrogen. A hydrogen ion, H⁺, has no electrons and an empty 1s orbital.

```
NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
```

The nitrogen lone pair is donated to H⁺ to form a fourth N–H bond. In a displayed formula, three lines and one arrow point from N to H. The ion has four bonding pairs and no lone pair on nitrogen, and carries a charge of 1+.

### Worked example 5: the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺

A water molecule has two lone pairs on oxygen. One is donated to H⁺ to form H₃O⁺, which has three bonding pairs and one lone pair on oxygen. Acids in water produce this ion.

### Worked example 6: an adduct, NH₃BF₃

Boron in BF₃ has only 6 electrons in its outer shell (three bonding pairs), so it has an empty orbital. Nitrogen in NH₃ donates its lone pair to boron:

```
H₃N → BF₃
```

Boron now has 8 electrons around it. Some textbooks show formal charges of N⁺ and B⁻ on this adduct. An arrow from N to B is enough to show the dative bond.

### Worked example 7: aluminium chloride dimer, Al₂Cl₆

In AlCl₃ aluminium has 6 outer electrons in its bonds. Two molecules join: a chlorine atom in each molecule donates a lone pair to the aluminium of the other. Al₂Cl₆ contains two dative bonds shown by arrows from Cl to Al. Each aluminium then has 8 electrons around it.

### Worked example 8: carbon monoxide, CO

Carbon has 4 outer electrons and oxygen has 6, giving 10 in total. A triple bond between C and O has 6 electrons shared. Two of the three pairs come one electron from each atom, and one pair comes from oxygen alone, so one of the three bonds is dative. Displayed formula: C≡O, with the arrow for the dative component pointing from O to C, and a lone pair on each atom.

## Bond order, length and strength

When two atoms share more pairs, the nuclei are held closer together and the bond is stronger.

| Bond | Pairs shared | Approximate length / pm | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C | 1 | 154 | 347 |
| C=C | 2 | 134 | 612 |
| C≡C | 3 | 120 | 838 |

The pattern is that a bond with more shared pairs is shorter and has a larger bond enthalpy. The double bond is stronger than a single bond but less than twice as strong, which is one reason that C=C bonds are more reactive than C–C bonds in addition reactions: the second bond of the double bond is weaker than a single bond.

## Common representation errors

- Drawing a covalent bond with an arrow, or a dative bond with a plain line. The line is for a normal covalent bond and the arrow shows a dative bond.
- Pointing the arrow the wrong way. It goes from the atom that donates the pair to the atom that accepts it.
- Missing lone pairs, so atoms appear to have fewer than eight electrons.
- Showing a charge on a molecule that has none, or leaving off the charge on an ion such as NH₄⁺.
- Saying a dative bond is "different" or "weaker" than a covalent bond once formed.
- Counting the electrons of a negative ion without adding the extra electrons.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** a dative covalent bond: "shared pair of electrons" and "both electrons from one atom" are both needed.
- **Draw**: show all lone pairs on the atoms involved, brackets and charge for an ion, and an arrow for each dative bond.
- **Explain** how NH₃ forms a bond with H⁺: state that nitrogen has a lone pair, hydrogen ion has an empty orbital, and the lone pair is donated.
- **Suggest** why a molecule such as BF₃ accepts a lone pair: boron has only six electrons, so it has an electron-deficient outer shell.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a dative bond a different type of bond?**
No. It differs only in how it was formed. After formation it behaves as an ordinary covalent bond.

**Can any atom with a lone pair form a dative bond?**
Only when there is an acceptor with a vacancy. Water and ammonia both have lone pairs and both can donate to H⁺.

**Do all covalent molecules obey the octet rule?**
Not all. BF₃ has six electrons around boron, and species such as SF₆ have twelve around sulfur. The shapes subtopic uses these.
