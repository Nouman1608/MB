All places, firms and data are fictional unless a question asks for your own example. Revise first with the [11.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry/) and the [11.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "industrial agglomeration". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between localisation economies and urbanisation economies. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows the localised materials used by two fictional plants to make 1 tonne of product. Water is available everywhere.

| Plant | Product | Localised materials used (t) |
|---|---|---|
| Ardel | Steel | Iron ore 3.0, coking coal 1.5, limestone 0.5 |
| Bruna | Soft drinks | Concentrate 0.2, sugar 0.2 |

**(a)** Calculate the material index for each plant. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest the most likely location for each plant, giving a reason. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain one limitation of using the material index to predict location. **[2]**

**4.** Halden, a fictional region, has 400 000 jobs, of which 32 000 are in electronics. Nationally, 500 000 of 20 000 000 jobs are in electronics.

**(a)** Calculate the location quotient for electronics in Halden. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** reasons why electronics firms may have concentrated in Halden. **[4]**

**5.** Outline **two** ways in which changes in technology have affected the location of manufacturing. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how diseconomies of scale can lead firms to leave a large industrial region. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Read the description of a fictional motorcycle plant.

> The Corvan motorcycle plant assembles 300 000 machines a year. Engines are made by a separate firm in the same industrial park; seats, lights and wiring come from eleven small firms within 5 km. Finished motorcycles go by road to 400 dealers and by rail to a port for export. A local firm services the plant's robots, and a bank in the nearby city finances dealers' stock.

**(a)** Using the description, give one example each of a backward, forward and service linkage. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the advantages to the plant of having suppliers within 5 km. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why export processing zones often have weak linkages with the economy of the host country. **[5]**

**9.** A survey of 200 informal workers in Tamvo, a fictional city, found: street vending 38%, transport 15%, repairs 12%, home-based manufacturing 20%, waste collection and recycling 15%. Most street vendors worked within 300 m of the city's two bus terminals.

**(a)** Calculate the number of workers in home-based manufacturing. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why most street vendors locate near the bus terminals. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain **two** ways in which informal manufacturing may be linked to formal firms. **[4]**

**10.** "Agglomeration brings more advantages than disadvantages to manufacturing firms." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The clustering of firms in one area [1], so that they gain shared benefits such as a labour pool, suppliers and infrastructure [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Localisation economies come from firms in the same industry locating close together [1]; urbanisation economies come from locating in a large city, with its general infrastructure, services and market [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Ardel: (3.0 + 1.5 + 0.5) ÷ 1 = 5.0 [1]; Bruna: (0.2 + 0.2) ÷ 1 = 0.4 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Ardel is likely near its materials, such as an ore field, coalfield or a port receiving imported ore [1], because its MI above 1 shows the process loses weight, so moving the finished steel is cheaper than moving the inputs [1]. Bruna is likely near its market, in or close to a large city [1], because its MI below 1 shows the product gains weight from ubiquitous water, so it is cheaper to carry the light concentrate than the heavy drinks [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** It assumes transport costs depend only on weight and distance [1], whereas costs vary by mode, fall per tonne-km over long distances, and break-of-bulk points or government policy may give a cheaper location [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Halden: 32 000 ÷ 400 000 = 8%; nation: 500 000 ÷ 20 000 000 = 2.5% [1]; LQ = 8 ÷ 2.5 = 3.2 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** A government zone or tax incentives may have attracted the first firms [1], and once they arrived, suppliers and skilled workers followed, creating agglomeration economies [1]. Halden may have an airport or port giving fast links to export markets [1], which suits light, high-value electronics [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit universities supplying skilled workers or lower land costs, linked to the needs of electronics firms.

**5.** Containerisation and larger ships cut the cost of moving goods [1], so factories can be far from their markets, for example in coastal zones of LICs and MICs [1]. Digital communications allow design and management to be separated from production [1], so research may stay in HICs while assembly moves to lower-cost locations [1]. **[4]**

**6.** In a large industrial region, demand for land drives up rents and land prices [1]. Congestion raises transport times and costs [1]. Competition for workers raises wages and causes labour shortages [1]. When these costs outweigh agglomeration benefits, firms relocate to smaller towns, the urban edge or other regions [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Backward: engines from the separate firm, or seats, lights and wiring from the small firms [1]. Forward: motorcycles sent to dealers or to the port for export [1]. Service: robot servicing by the local firm, or dealer finance from the bank [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Short distances cut transport costs for parts [1]. Parts can be delivered just in time, so the plant holds little stock and saves storage costs [1]. Problems with quality can be solved quickly face to face [1]. Delivery is more reliable, with less risk of production stopping for lack of parts [1]. **[4]**

**8.** EPZ firms can import components duty-free [1], so it is often cheaper to import than to buy from local firms [1]. Local suppliers may not meet the quality, volume or delivery standards of foreign firms [1]. Most output is exported, so forward linkages to the host economy are few [1]. Profits are often sent back to the parent company abroad rather than reinvested locally [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** 20% of 200 = 40 workers [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Terminals have large flows of passengers who are potential customers [1]. Travellers want quick purchases such as food, drinks or phone credit [1]. Vendors who arrive by bus themselves can carry goods in and out easily, without paying for a fixed shop [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Formal firms subcontract work such as sewing or assembly to home-based workers [1], because this cuts their costs for premises and labour protection [1]. Informal workers buy materials or offcuts from formal firms [1], or recycle formal-sector waste into goods for sale [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define agglomeration [1] and distinguish localisation and urbanisation economies [1].

*Advantages:* a pool of skilled labour lowers recruitment and training costs [1]. Specialist suppliers nearby cut transport and stock costs [1]. Shared infrastructure and services, such as ports, power and logistics firms, are cheaper per firm [1]. Ideas spread between firms, encouraging innovation [1]. A cluster gains a reputation that attracts customers and investment [1]. Cumulative causation can make a region's growth self-sustaining [1].

*Disadvantages:* competition for land raises rents [1] and competition for workers raises wages [1]. Congestion and pollution raise costs and lower quality of life [1]. A cluster dependent on one industry is vulnerable to a fall in demand [1]. Inertia can hold firms in declining locations [1].

*Analysis:* the balance changes over time, as advantages dominate in growth and diseconomies later [1]. It varies by firm size: small firms gain most from shared suppliers [1], while large firms can provide their own services and move more easily [1]. It differs by industry: footloose firms need clusters less [1].

*Use of examples:* a named cluster where advantages dominate [1]; a named region where diseconomies or decline have pushed firms out [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion on "how far", such as advantages outweighing disadvantages in the growth stage, but not indefinitely [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* strong answers explain why the balance shifts, rather than presenting two separate lists.

## Where marks are usually lost

- MI calculations that include water or use the wrong denominator.
- LQ answers that divide job numbers instead of percentages.
- Linkage examples not taken from the extract.
- Treating EPZs only as a benefit or only as exploitation.
- Essays without a named cluster.
