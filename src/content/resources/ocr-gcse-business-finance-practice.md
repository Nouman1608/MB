## Questions

**1.** State **two** purposes of the finance function in a business. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **three** reasons why a business may need finance. **[3]**

**3.** Briarwood Gifts opened last month. The owner has £4,000 of savings but needs £9,000 for stock and a shop fit-out.

**(a)** State **two** sources of finance, other than retained profit, that Briarwood Gifts could use. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why retained profit is a more likely source of finance for an established business than for Briarwood Gifts. **[2]**

**4.** Nethergate Invitations sold 2,400 sets of wedding invitations in a year at £18.50 each. Variable cost is £6.25 per set and fixed costs for the year are £14,000.

**(a)** Calculate its revenue. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its total costs. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate its profit. **[1]**
**(d)** Define the term fixed cost and give one example. **[2]**

**5.** Briarwood Tea Rooms recorded these results.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £240,000 | £264,000 |
| Cost of sales | £150,000 | £171,600 |
| Other expenses | £57,600 | £62,040 |

**(a)** Calculate gross profit and the gross profit margin for each year. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate net profit and the net profit margin for each year. **[3]**
**(c)** Analyse the change in the business's performance between the two years. **[3]**

**6.** Briarwood Tea Rooms plans to buy an oven for £44,000. Forecast profit from it over four years is £4,000, £6,000, £7,000 and £5,000.

**(a)** Calculate the average rate of return. **[3]**
**(b)** A second project has an average rate of return of 14%. Explain which project looks better on this measure alone, and one limit of relying on it. **[2]**

**7.** Wintergate Pottery Studio charges £45 for a class. The variable cost is £18 per class and fixed costs are £8,370 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even quantity. **[3]**
**(b)** The studio considers raising the price to £50. Calculate the new break-even quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** way break-even helps the studio make decisions. **[2]**

**8.** Coldwell Valeting starts April with £1,500. Cash in and cash out are forecast as follows: April £6,200 in and £7,000 out; May £5,400 in and £6,300 out; June £8,800 in and £6,100 out.

**(a)** Complete a cash flow forecast for the three months, showing net cash flow and the opening and closing balance each month. **[4]**
**(b)** Recommend **one** action the owner could take about the month with the problem. **[2]**

**9.** In June, Coldwell Valeting finishes a £12,000 contract and pays £7,500 of costs for it that month. The customer has 60 days to pay. Explain why the business has made a profit but may have a cash problem. **[4]**

**10.** Rushbrook Farm Shop has traded for eleven years and made a net profit of £31,000 last year. It needs £24,000 for a new cold store and has £9,000 of retained profit. Option 1 is to use the retained profit and borrow £15,000 at 8% interest a year. Option 2 is to bring in a new partner who will put in £24,000 for a share of future profits. Evaluate the two options and justify which you would recommend. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: to provide financial information; to support business planning; to support decision making. One mark for each [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two separate purposes. Splitting one idea into two wordings does not usually earn a second point.

**2.** Any three of: establishing a new business; funding expansion; to run the business; recruitment; marketing. One mark for each [1] [1] [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Three clearly different reasons are needed. Naming something like "buying a shop" is a use of the money, so tie it to a reason.

**3. (a)** Any two of: owners' capital, a loan, crowdfunding, a new partner, an overdraft, trade credit. One mark for each [1] [1]. In general a share issue is a route for companies, not a new sole trader. **[2]**
**(b)** Retained profit is profit kept in the business [1]. An established business has traded long enough to have built it up, while a new business has little or none yet [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A source named in (a) should fit the case. A bare list can gain credit, but a link to the new business is stronger.

**4. (a)** 2,400 × £18.50 = **£44,400** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Variable costs = 2,400 × £6.25 = £15,000 [1]; total costs = £14,000 + £15,000 = **£29,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Profit = £44,400 − £29,000 = **£15,400** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** A fixed cost is a cost that does not change as output changes in the short term [1]; for example rent, or insurance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each step and state the formula. Showing the method in each part makes your reasoning clear to the reader.

**5. (a)** Year 1: £240,000 − £150,000 = £90,000, margin 90,000 ÷ 240,000 × 100 = 37.5% [1]. Year 2: £264,000 − £171,600 = £92,400, margin = 35% [1]. Both gross profits and margins correct [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Year 1: £90,000 − £57,600 = £32,400, margin = 13.5% [1]. Year 2: £92,400 − £62,040 = £30,360, margin = 11.5% [1]. Both correct [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Revenue rose by 10% [1], but gross margin fell from 37.5% to 35%, so cost of sales rose faster than revenue [1]. Net profit fell by £2,040 and net margin by 2 percentage points, so the business keeps less from each pound of sales despite higher revenue [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis answers do best when they quote figures and link cause to effect. Saying only "profit went down" gives little.

**6. (a)** Total profit = £4,000 + £6,000 + £7,000 + £5,000 = £22,000 [1]; average = £22,000 ÷ 4 = £5,500 [1]; ARR = 5,500 ÷ 44,000 × 100 = **12.5%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The second project looks better, because 14% is higher than 12.5% [1]. ARR ignores risk, and the profit forecasts may be wrong [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the formula you use, since the specification does not print one. A comparison needs a reason, not just a pick.

**7. (a)** Contribution = £45 − £18 = £27 [1]; break-even = 8,370 ÷ 27 [1] = **310 classes** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Contribution = £50 − £18 = £32 [1]; 8,370 ÷ 32 = 261.56, so **262 classes** (rounded up) [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Break-even shows how many classes must be sold to cover costs [1], so the studio can judge whether a price or sales target is realistic before committing [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Rounding a break-even quantity down understates what is needed. Linking (c) to a decision, not just a definition, is what separates the answers.

**8. (a)** April: opening £1,500, net −£800, closing £700 [1]. May: opening £700, net −£900, closing −£200 [1]. June: opening −£200, net +£2,700, closing £2,500 [1]. Opening balances carried forward correctly [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** May ends £200 overdrawn [1]. Any suitable action with a reason, such as arranging an overdraft to cover the gap, or asking suppliers for longer trade credit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A closing balance that is not copied to the next opening balance is a common slip. Name the month, and make the remedy fit a short gap.

**9.** Profit is revenue minus costs: £12,000 − £7,500 = £4,500 [1]. The sale is counted in the revenue for June [1]. Cash flow is money actually received and paid, and the customer has 60 days to pay, so £7,500 leaves in June but nothing comes in [1]. Without enough cash for wages and suppliers, the business may be unable to pay short-term debts, despite the profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures in the case. A general definition of cash and profit gains less than applying both to this contract.

**10.** Option 1 keeps ownership with the current owner [1] and interest of £1,200 in the first year is small against net profit of £31,000 [1]. It also uses profit the shop has already built [1]. Repayments must still be met even if sales fall, which is a risk [1]. Option 2 needs no repayments and could bring skills [1], but a share of future profit is given up permanently and decisions are shared [1]. Judgement: Option 1 is better, because the shop is established and profitable [1], so it can afford the loan and keeps control; a partner suits better if the owner wanted a shared business or could not afford repayments [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation that weighs both sides and uses the figures is stronger than a list. A judgement with no reason attached usually gains little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing sources of finance without linking them to the business in the question.
- Giving a margin without the percentage sign, or the wrong denominator.
- Using net profit when the question asks for gross profit.
- Averaging profit over the wrong number of years in ARR.
- Rounding break-even down instead of up.
- Forgetting to carry the closing balance into the next opening balance.
- Answering a cash question with a profit explanation.
- Giving one side of an evaluation, or no judgement at the end.
- Leaving out working, so one slip costs the whole answer.
