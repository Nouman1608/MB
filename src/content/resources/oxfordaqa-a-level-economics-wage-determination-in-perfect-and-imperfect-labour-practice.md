## Questions

**1.** Define monopsony power in a labour market and state two conditions that make it stronger. **[3]**

**2.** A sign-painting firm sells each finished job for 3 cu. The extra jobs completed per day by workers 1 to 5 are 30, 26, 20, 14 and 8.

**(a)** Work out the third worker's marginal revenue product. **[1]**
**(b)** The daily wage is 65 cu. How many workers does the firm hire? Show your comparison. **[2]**
**(c)** The daily wage falls to 45 cu. How many does it now hire? **[1]**

**3.** In Country B, the market for kitchen assistants has demand Qd = 900 − 30W and supply Qs = 100 + 50W, where Q is workers and W is the wage.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** Demand rises to Qd = 1060 − 30W. Find the new equilibrium. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the wage. **[1]**

**4.** A lone mill employs all workers in an isolated town. L is hundreds of workers. Supply of labour is W = 5 + L and marginal revenue product is MRP = 35 − L.

**(a)** Derive the marginal cost of labour. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the wage and employment the mill chooses. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the wage and employment in a perfectly competitive labour market. **[2]**

**5.** Describe how a diagram shows the wage, employment and monopsonistic exploitation for a profit-maximising monopsonist. **[4]**

**6.** Use the original market in question 3 (before demand rose). A union negotiates a wage of 14 cu.

**(a)** Calculate employment at 14 cu. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the excess supply of labour. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the elasticity of demand for labour over the change from the original wage. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage change in the total wage bill. **[1]**

**7.** In the mill of question 4, a union sets a wage for all workers.

**(a)** The union sets 20 cu. Find employment. **[2]**
**(b)** What is the highest union wage that keeps employment at or above the level in 4(b)? **[2]**
**(c)** State why this result differs from question 6. **[1]**

**8.** Explain two factors that affect a trade union's ability to raise wages. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how imperfect information can lead two similar workers to be paid different wages. **[3]**

**10.** A union wins a nominal pay rise of 4.5% while the price index moves from 200 to 207.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in real pay. **[2]**
**(b)** Occupation X pays 15 cu rising to 18.9 cu, and occupation Y pays 14 cu rising to 14.7 cu. Calculate each new wage as an index (old wage = 100). **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that a trade union will always raise its members' wages at the cost of fewer jobs. **[8]**

**12.** Assess how far the model of a perfectly competitive labour market explains differences in pay between occupations. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Monopsony power is the ability of a single or dominant buyer of labour to influence the wage it pays [1]. Conditions: workers have skills specific to the employer [1]; workers are immobile or have few alternative employers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the term before listing conditions, and make each condition a separate point rather than one blended phrase.

**2. (a)** MRP = 20 × 3 = **60** [1]
**(b)** MRP of the second worker is 26 × 3 = 78, which is above 65 [1]. The third gives 60, which is below 65, so the firm hires **2** [1]
**(c)** MRPs are 90, 78, 60, 42: the third (60) is above 45 and the fourth (42) is below, so **3** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each MRP beside the wage. A bare number of workers gives you less to be credited for if it is off by one.

**3. (a)** 900 − 30W = 100 + 50W, so 800 = 80W [1]. **W = 10, Q = 600** [1]
**(b)** 1060 − 30W = 100 + 50W, so 960 = 80W [1]. **W = 12, Q = 700** [1]
**(c)** (12 − 10) ÷ 10 = **+20%** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Substitute back into both equations to confirm they give the same quantity. It catches slips cheaply.

**4. (a)** Total cost = L(5 + L), so **MCL = 5 + 2L** [1]
**(b)** 35 − L = 5 + 2L [1], so **L = 10** (1,000 workers) [1]. Wage from supply: 5 + 10 = **15** [1]
**(c)** 35 − L = 5 + L [1], so **L = 15, W = 20** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is reading the wage from MCL (25) or MRP. State that the wage comes from the supply curve.

**5.** Axes: wage up the side, number of workers along the bottom [1]. A rising supply (average cost of labour) curve, a steeper MCL curve above it and a falling MRP curve [1]. Employment is where MCL cuts MRP; the wage is read from the supply curve at that employment [1]. The vertical gap between MRP and the wage at that employment is the monopsonistic exploitation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name every curve and the point where you read each variable. A description that skips the wage-reading step is incomplete.

**6. (a)** Qd = 900 − 30 × 14 = **480** [1]
**(b)** Qs = 100 + 50 × 14 = 800, so excess supply = 800 − 480 = **320** [1]
**(c)** Employment: (480 − 600) ÷ 600 = −20% [1]. Wage: (14 − 10) ÷ 10 = +40%, so elasticity = −20 ÷ 40 = **−0.5** [1]
**(d)** Before: 600 × 10 = 6,000. After: 480 × 14 = 6,720. Change = **+12%** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the demand figure (480), not the supply figure, for employment. Quote the sign and say it is inelastic.

**7. (a)** At 20, supply gives 20 − 5 = 15 and demand gives 35 − 20 = 15 [1]. Employment = **15** (hundred) [1]
**(b)** Employment stays at 10 when MRP = wage at L = 10, so the wage is 35 − 10 = **25** [1]. Above this, demand falls below 10, so check that 25 is within supply (supply at 25 is 20, which is enough) [1]
**(c)** The mill pays below MRP, so a wage rise toward MRP can raise employment; in a competitive market it cannot. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Employment equals the smaller of supply and demand at a fixed wage. Say so, rather than assuming one curve.

**8.** Any two, each with explanation: high union membership [1] makes strike threats credible because the employer cannot easily replace many workers [1]; inelastic demand for labour [1] means a wage rise costs few jobs because labour is a small share of cost or there are few substitutes [1]; also profitability, scarce skills, low unemployment or protective laws. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A factor plus no explanation earns less. Link each factor to the employer's costs or the union's threat.

**9.** Workers may not know higher wages are available elsewhere [1], so they accept a lower offer [1]. Employers cannot observe productivity, so they may pay on weak signals; searching is costly, so wages do not equalise [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say why the difference persists. "Information is poor" alone does not explain the wage gap.

**10. (a)** 1.045 ÷ 1.035 = 1.0097 [1]. Real pay rises by about **1.0%** [1]
**(b)** X: 18.9 ÷ 15 × 100 = **126** [1]. Y: 14.7 ÷ 14 × 100 = **105** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing growth factors is the exact method. Subtracting 3.5 from 4.5 happens to give the same 1.0 here, so show the division.

**11.** Competitive market: a wage set above equilibrium cuts employment along the demand curve and creates excess supply [1]. The effect is small when demand for labour is inelastic, so the wage bill can rise [1]. Monopsony: workers are paid below MRP, so a union wage can raise both wages and employment up to a ceiling [1], beyond which jobs fall [1]. The outcome depends on bargaining strength (bilateral monopoly) [1] and on union strength factors such as membership and ability to pay [1]. Other effects include insiders gaining at the expense of outsiders [1], and possible productivity gains from lower turnover. Judgement: "always" is wrong, because the outcome depends on market structure and where the wage is set [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers contrast the two market structures and reach a conclusion about the word "always". A list of union effects with no judgement does less well.

**12.** In the model, wages are set by demand and supply, so differences reflect productivity (MRP), skills and the supply of workers [1]. Higher pay in scarce skills signals entry [1], and training costs raise the wage needed to attract workers [1]. Strengths: it explains why differentials arise and narrow when workers move [1]. Limits: monopsony pays below MRP [1], unions raise wages for members [1], imperfect information and immobility let differences persist [1]. Judgement: it explains the direction of many differences but is incomplete, because every real labour market departs from it to some degree [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the model to pay differences rather than reciting its assumptions. Choose one limit and develop it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading the monopsony wage from the MCL or MRP curve.
- Forgetting the 2 in MCL = a + 2bL when supply is linear.
- Stating employment from supply when a fixed wage is above equilibrium; it is the lower of supply and demand.
- Omitting excess supply or unemployment after a union raises the wage in a competitive market.
- Using percentage points where a percentage change is needed.
- Giving a factor, such as membership, with no link to the employer's reaction.
- Describing a diagram but leaving the axes, curves or equilibrium points unnamed.
- Writing about unions in general without stating which market structure applies.
