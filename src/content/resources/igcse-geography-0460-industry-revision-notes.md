These notes condense the [Industry study guide](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-industry/); read that first for full explanations and worked answers.

They cover section 3.3 Industry of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). This section is part of Theme 3: Economic development. It is assessed in Paper 1 Geographical Themes, where you answer three 25-mark questions, one from each section. Paper 2 Geographical Skills can also set knowledge questions based on the three themes. Practise with the [Industry practice questions](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-industry-practice/), and track progress on the [Cambridge IGCSE Geography hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/geography/) and [checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/geography/).

## The syllabus in one box

- Understand an **industrial system**: inputs, processes, outputs (**products and waste**).
- Know four **industry types**: manufacturing, processing, assembly, high technology.
- Describe and explain the **distribution and location** of factories and industrial zones.
- Factors: **land, labour, raw materials, fuel and power, transport, markets, political factors**. Because the guidance says "including", learn all of them.
- Show how factors **combine** to shape location, scale of production, methods of organisation and products.
- Discuss **locational and siting** factors for zones or factories.
- Case study: **an industrial zone or factory**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Industrial system | A way of showing industry as inputs, processes and outputs |
| Input | Anything that goes into production: raw materials, energy, labour, capital, land |
| Process | What is done to inputs: cutting, smelting, mixing, welding, assembling, packing |
| Output | Products for sale, plus waste (solid, liquid, gas, heat, noise) |
| Feedback | Profit reinvested as a new input |
| Processing industry | Turns raw materials into more useful products, often losing weight |
| Manufacturing industry | Makes finished goods from materials |
| Assembly industry | Puts together components made elsewhere |
| High-technology industry | Uses research, skilled staff and advanced equipment to make high-value products |
| Footloose industry | Not tied to raw materials or power, so it can locate in many places |
| Location | The general area or region where a factory is found |
| Site | The actual plot of land a factory stands on |
| Greenfield site | Land not built on before |
| Brownfield site | Land used before and now cleared or reused |
| Industrial zone | A planned area for many firms with shared roads and services |
| Science park | An industrial zone for research and high-tech firms, usually linked to a university |
| Free trade zone | An area where goods can be imported and re-exported without customs duty |
| Just-in-time | Parts delivered only when needed, so little stock is kept |
| Industrial inertia | Industry staying in a place after the original reason has gone |

## The industrial system at a glance

Draw this as three boxes joined by arrows, with a feedback arrow from outputs back to inputs.

| Inputs | Processes | Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Physical: raw materials, water, land, energy | Cutting, smelting, mixing, pressing | Products for sale |
| Human: labour, capital, skills, machinery, research, government help | Welding, painting, assembling, testing, packing | Waste: solid, liquid, gas, heat, noise |

**Quick example.** A fictional furniture factory takes in timber, glue, electricity and 40 workers. It saws, sands, joins and varnishes the wood. It sends out tables and chairs, plus sawdust, offcuts and fumes. Sawdust sold to a chipboard maker becomes a by-product.

## Command words you will meet

The syllabus defines these command words. Match your answer to the word.

- **Describe:** state the points of a topic, or give characteristics and main features. Say what the data or map shows; no reasons needed.
- **Explain:** set out purposes or reasons, and support with relevant evidence. Every point needs a "because" or "so".
- **Suggest:** apply knowledge to a situation where there are several valid responses. Use the source's details.
- **Compare:** identify or comment on similarities and/or differences. Use "whereas" or "while" to link the two things.
- **Calculate:** work out from given facts, figures or information. Show working and units.
- **Justify:** support a case with evidence or argument. Use details from every option you are given.

## Location factors at a glance

| Factor | Pull towards | Most important for |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Flat, cheap, large plots with room to expand | Large plants and zones |
| Labour | Skilled or low-cost workers | High-tech (skills); assembly (cost and numbers) |
| Raw materials | Source of bulky, weight-losing inputs | Processing industry |
| Fuel and power | Cheap, reliable energy | Energy-hungry industries such as smelting |
| Transport | Ports, motorways, rail, airports | Importers, exporters, just-in-time firms |
| Markets | Customers | Fragile, perishable or weight-gaining products |
| Political | Grants, tax breaks, free zones, trade agreements | Firms choosing between countries |

## Case studies at a glance

| Case study | Type | Key facts | Main location factors | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jebel Ali Free Zone, Dubai, UAE | Industrial zone | Began 1985 with 19 companies; about 57 km²; operator says over 11,000 businesses; run by DP World | Beside Jebel Ali Port (opened 1979), the world's largest man-made harbour and the Middle East's busiest port; about 24 km from Al Maktoum airport; free-zone rules with no foreign ownership restrictions | Political and transport factors outweigh raw materials, but much activity is trade and storage, not making goods |
| Toyota, Burnaston, Derbyshire, England | Factory (car assembly) | First car built 16 December 1992; engines from Deeside, North Wales | Flat site of former Derby Airport; beside A38, near A50 and M1; UK then in the European Community, so no import duties on cars sold to other members; engineering skills in Derby | Shows how trade rules can change and add costs to a plant built for one market |
| Cambridge Science Park, England | High-tech zone (short example) | Founded by Trinity College in 1970; about 3 km north of the city | University links, skilled graduates, A14 road and Cambridge North station | Footloose firms still cluster where knowledge and skills are found |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Location vs site.** Location is the region ("near a port"); site is the plot ("flat, 50 hectares, next to a road junction").
- **Processing vs assembly.** Processing changes raw materials; assembly joins ready-made parts.
- **Products vs waste.** Both are outputs. A by-product is waste that can be sold or reused.
- **Physical vs human inputs.** Raw materials, land and water are physical; labour, capital and skills are human.
- **Weight-losing vs weight-gaining.** Weight-losing industries locate near raw materials; weight-gaining ones locate near markets.
- **Greenfield vs brownfield.** New land is cheaper to build on but may face objections; reused land may need clearing but is close to workers.

## Mini essay plan

*Question type:* "To what extent is transport the most important factor in the location of industry today? Refer to examples."

1. **Introduction:** define location factors; say that factors combine.
2. **For transport:** Jebel Ali sits beside a giant port; just-in-time car plants such as Burnaston need fast roads (A38, A50, M1).
3. **Against, political:** free-zone rules and avoiding import duties often decide which country a firm chooses.
4. **Against, labour:** high-tech firms in Cambridge Science Park follow skilled graduates more than transport.
5. **Against, raw materials:** processing industries such as sugar refining still locate near bulky raw materials.
6. **Judgement:** transport is often necessary but rarely enough; political and labour factors usually decide between several well-connected places.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the two kinds of output in an industrial system.
2. Give one physical input and one human input.
3. What is an assembly industry?
4. Why is high-tech industry described as footloose?
5. What is the difference between location and site?
6. Name three political factors that can attract industry.
7. Why do weight-losing industries locate near raw materials?
8. What does just-in-time mean, and which factor does it depend on?
9. A fictional zone's jobs rose from 30,000 to 42,000. Calculate the percentage increase.
10. In which year did Toyota build its first car at Burnaston, and what was the site before?
11. Give two reasons why Jebel Ali Free Zone attracts foreign firms.
12. State one advantage and one disadvantage of a brownfield site.

### Answers

1. Products and waste.
2. Physical: raw materials, water, land or energy. Human: labour, capital, skills or machinery.
3. An industry that puts together components made elsewhere, such as cars or phones.
4. Its products are light and valuable, so transport and raw materials matter little; it can locate in many places.
5. Location is the general area; site is the actual plot of land.
6. Grants, tax breaks, free trade zones, cheap land, trade agreements, planning permission.
7. The raw material is bulkier than the product, so moving the product is cheaper.
8. Parts arrive only when needed; it depends on reliable transport and nearby suppliers.
9. (42,000 − 30,000) ÷ 30,000 × 100 = 40%.
10. 1992; the former Derby Airport.
11. It is beside Jebel Ali Port, and free-zone rules allow full foreign ownership and duty-free import and re-export.
12. Advantage: close to workers and existing roads. Disadvantage: old buildings may need clearing or the land may be polluted.

## Case study answer checklist

Before you finish a case study answer, check that it has:

- the **name and country** of the zone or factory;
- at least two **real facts**, such as a date, a road, a port or the size of the site;
- a clear split between **site** points and **location** points when the question asks for both;
- a **link** from each factor to what the firm needs ("the port matters because most inputs arrive by sea");
- a short **judgement** on which factor mattered most, if the command word asks for one.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up location and site in "siting" questions.
- Leaving out waste when describing outputs.
- Naming a factor ("transport") without saying which link and why the firm needs it.
- Giving a case study with no place names, dates or facts.
- Calling every firm "manufacturing" when processing or assembly fits better.
- Not using figures from a resource when the question says "Use the data".
- Forgetting units or the percentage sign in calculations.
- Ending a "to what extent" answer with no judgement.
