This guide covers subtopic **22.1, Infrared spectroscopy**, from Topic 22, Analytical techniques, of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The
[Analytical Techniques: IR and Mass Spectrometry study guide](/resources/as-analytical-techniques-ir-and-mass-spectrometry/)
lists the absorption table; this page teaches a reliable routine for analysing a spectrum, shows how to
separate compounds whose absorptions overlap, and applies IR to following reactions and to structure
problems.

## What the syllabus asks for

There is one learning outcome: **analyse an infrared spectrum of a simple molecule to identify functional
groups**, using the Data section supplied in the examination for the functional groups required. You do not
need to memorise exact wavenumbers, but you must be quick at using the table.

## What an IR spectrum shows

Covalent bonds vibrate (stretch and bend). A bond absorbs infrared radiation at frequencies that match its
vibrations, and these depend on the masses of the atoms and the strength of the bond. An IR spectrum plots
**transmittance** (the percentage of radiation passing through the sample) against **wavenumber** in cm⁻¹.
Each absorption shows as a downward dip, or trough. Wavenumbers run from about 4000 cm⁻¹ on the left to
about 500 cm⁻¹ on the right.

## The Data section, arranged by region

| Region / cm⁻¹ | Bond | Functional groups (Data section) | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3200–3600 | O–H | hydroxy | strong and broad |
| 3300–3500 | N–H | amine, amide | medium, narrower than alcohol O–H |
| 2850–2950 | C–H | alkane | present in almost every organic molecule |
| 2500–3000 | O–H | carboxyl | very broad, overlapping the C–H region |
| 2200–2250 | C≡N | nitrile | sharp, in an otherwise empty region |
| 1710–1750 | C=O | ester | strong, sharp |
| 1670–1740 | C=O | carbonyl (aldehyde, ketone), carboxyl | strong, sharp |
| 1640–1690 | C=O | amide | strong |
| 1500–1680 | C=C | alkene, aromatic | weaker than C=O |
| 1040–1300 | C–O | hydroxy, ester | strong |

O–H absorptions are broad because O–H groups are hydrogen bonded to each other, and each hydrogen bond
shifts the absorption slightly, so a range of frequencies is absorbed together.

## A routine for any spectrum

1. **Above 3000 cm⁻¹:** a broad absorption at 3200–3600 means an alcohol O–H; a narrower one at 3300–3500
   can mean N–H.
2. **2500–3000 cm⁻¹:** a very broad absorption that spreads under the C–H peaks means a carboxylic acid O–H.
3. **2200–2250 cm⁻¹:** a sharp absorption means C≡N.
4. **1640–1750 cm⁻¹:** a strong, sharp absorption means C=O. Decide which kind from what else is present.
5. **1500–1680 cm⁻¹:** a weaker absorption with no C=O can mean C=C.
6. **1040–1300 cm⁻¹:** C–O; use it to support an alcohol or ester identification.
7. **Combine** the evidence with the molecular formula. State each bond, its wavenumber and the group it
   indicates.

## Telling similar compounds apart

The C=O ranges overlap, so a C=O peak alone rarely settles the answer. Use the other absorptions:

| Compound class | C=O 1640–1750 | O–H 3200–3600 | O–H 2500–3000 | C–O 1040–1300 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| alcohol | no | yes | no | yes |
| aldehyde or ketone | yes | no | no | no |
| carboxylic acid | yes | no | yes (very broad) | yes |
| ester | yes (often 1710–1750) | no | no | yes |

Aldehydes and ketones give very similar IR spectra; use a chemical test (17.1) to separate them.

## Worked example 1: identifying a compound from its absorptions

**Question.** Compound R, C₃H₆O₂, gives strong absorptions at 1745 cm⁻¹ and 1200 cm⁻¹ and C–H absorptions
near 2900 cm⁻¹. There is no absorption between 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹ and no broad absorption between 2500 and
3000 cm⁻¹. Identify R.

**Answer.**

- 1745 cm⁻¹: C=O, within the ester range 1710–1750.
- 1200 cm⁻¹: C–O, consistent with an ester.
- No O–H in either range: R is not an alcohol or a carboxylic acid.

C₃H₆O₂ is CₙH₂ₙO₂, which fits a carboxylic acid or an ester; the missing O–H rules out the acid. R is an
ester: **methyl ethanoate** (CH₃COOCH₃) or **ethyl methanoate** (HCOOCH₂CH₃). IR alone cannot tell these
two apart; hydrolysis products or a mass spectrum would.

## Worked example 2: following an oxidation

**Question.** Propan-1-ol is oxidised by acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ in two experiments: (A) with distillation and
(B) under reflux. Predict the key features of the IR spectrum of each product.

**Answer.**

- **Propan-1-ol** (starting material): broad O–H at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹, C–O at 1040–1300 cm⁻¹, no C=O.
- **(A) Propanal:** the broad 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ O–H absorption has **disappeared**; a strong C=O absorption
  appears at 1670–1740 cm⁻¹.
- **(B) Propanoic acid:** a strong C=O at 1670–1740 cm⁻¹ **and** a very broad O–H at 2500–3000 cm⁻¹.

Seeing a 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ absorption in the product of (A) would show that some alcohol remained unreacted.

## Worked example 3: when ranges overlap

**Question.** Compound W, C₃H₆O, shows a broad absorption at 3300 cm⁻¹ and a medium absorption at
1645 cm⁻¹. There is no strong absorption between 1670 and 1750 cm⁻¹. Suggest a structure for W.

**Answer.** The broad 3300 cm⁻¹ absorption is O–H (hydroxy). 1645 cm⁻¹ lies in both the C=C range
(1500–1680) and the amide C=O range (1640–1690), but W contains no nitrogen, so it cannot be an amide: the
absorption is C=C. The absence of a strong 1670–1750 cm⁻¹ absorption rules out an aldehyde or ketone. W is
an unsaturated alcohol, such as **prop-2-en-1-ol, CH₂=CHCH₂OH**. Always use the molecular formula to
decide between overlapping ranges.

## Using IR for other reactions in the syllabus

| Reaction | Absorption that disappears | Absorption that appears |
|---|---|---|
| halogenoalkane + KCN → nitrile | — | C≡N, 2200–2250 |
| alkene + H₂O(g) → alcohol | C=C, 1500–1680 | O–H, 3200–3600 |
| alcohol dehydration → alkene | O–H, 3200–3600 | C=C, 1500–1680 |
| acid + alcohol → ester | O–H of both reactants | ester C=O, 1710–1750 |
| halogenoalkane + NH₃ → amine | — | N–H, 3300–3500 |

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the two O–H ranges.** 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ is hydroxy (alcohol); 2500–3000 cm⁻¹, very broad, is
  carboxyl.
- **Calling every peak near 3000 cm⁻¹ an O–H.** C–H absorbs at 2850–2950 cm⁻¹ in almost all organic
  molecules.
- **Identifying an ester from C=O alone.** Show that O–H is absent and a C–O absorption is present.
- **Quoting a single wavenumber with no bond.** Write "absorption at 1715 cm⁻¹ due to C=O".
- **Expecting IR to give the whole structure.** It identifies functional groups; positional isomers often
  give very similar spectra.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Analyse** or **Identify**: name each bond, quote the wavenumber or range from the spectrum, and give the
  functional group it shows.
- **Explain** how IR distinguishes two compounds: give an absorption one has and the other lacks.
- **Deduce** a structure: combine IR evidence with the molecular formula and any chemical tests.
- **Suggest** what a spectrum would show: list the absorptions present and absent.
