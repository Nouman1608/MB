## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by the activity of a radioactive source, and give its unit. **[2]**

**2.** Describe what a beta particle is and where it comes from. **[2]**

**3.** Name the emission that:

**(a)** is the same as a helium nucleus **[1]**
**(b)** is electromagnetic radiation **[1]**
**(c)** has the greatest range in air **[1]**

**4.** Uranium-234 (atomic number 92) decays by alpha emission. State the mass number and atomic number of the nucleus formed. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a gamma ray being emitted does not change the mass number or atomic number of a nucleus. **[1]**

**6.** A source has a half-life of 10 minutes and an initial count-rate of 400 counts per second. Calculate the count-rate after 30 minutes. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Complete and balance these nuclear equations.

**(a)** ²¹⁰₈₂Pb → ²¹⁰₈₃Bi + ? **[2]**
**(b)** ²²²₈₆Rn → ? Po + ⁴₂He, giving the mass and atomic numbers of the polonium nucleus. **[2]**

**8.** A company makes aluminium foil and wants to monitor its thickness with a radioactive source and a detector on opposite sides of the foil. Evaluate whether an alpha, beta or gamma source would be most suitable. **[4]**

**9.** A student records the count-rate from a sample over time.

| Time (min) | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count-rate (counts/s) | 480 | 340 | 240 | 170 | 120 |

**(a)** Use the data to determine the half-life of the sample. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the half-life of a large sample can be predicted even though radioactive decay is random. **[2]**

**10.** (Higher tier only) A radioactive source has an initial activity of 6400 Bq and a half-life of 3 days.

**(a)** Calculate the activity after 18 days. **[2]**
**(b)** Express the net decline in activity over 18 days as a ratio. **[1]**

**11.** A hospital worker uses an irradiation source, and a separate laboratory has a spill of a liquid containing an alpha-emitting isotope.

**(a)** Explain the difference between irradiation and contamination. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why contamination with an alpha emitter is especially hazardous if the material is swallowed. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe **two** precautions the hospital worker should take when using the irradiation source. **[2]**

**12.** Scientists studying the effects of radiation on people publish their results in scientific journals. Explain why this is important. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays [1]; becquerel (Bq) [1]

**2.** A high-speed electron [1]; ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton [1]

**3. (a)** Alpha [1]
**(b)** Gamma [1]
**(c)** Gamma [1]

**4.** Mass number 234 − 4 = **230** [1]; atomic number 92 − 2 = **90** [1]

**5.** A gamma ray is electromagnetic radiation with no mass and no charge, so the numbers of protons and neutrons in the nucleus are unchanged [1]

**6.** 30 minutes is 3 half-lives: 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 [1]; **50 counts per second** [1]

**7. (a)** ⁰₋₁e (a beta particle) [1]; check: 210 = 210 + 0 and 82 = 83 + (−1) [1]
**(b)** Mass number 222 − 4 = **218** [1]; atomic number 86 − 2 = **84** [1]

**8.** Alpha would be completely stopped by the foil, so the count-rate would not change with thickness [1]; gamma would pass through almost unaffected, so small changes in thickness would hardly change the count-rate [1]; beta is partly absorbed by thin aluminium, so the count-rate falls noticeably as the foil gets thicker [1]; so beta is most suitable [1]

**9. (a)** The count-rate halves from 480 to 240 in 8 minutes (and from 240 to 120 in another 8 minutes) [1]; half-life = **8 minutes** [1]
**(b)** Each nucleus decays at random, so the decay of one nucleus cannot be predicted [1]; but in a very large number of nuclei the same fraction decays in each equal time interval, so the time for half to decay is constant [1]

**10. (a)** 18 ÷ 3 = 6 half-lives; 6400 ÷ 2⁶ = 6400 ÷ 64 [1]; activity = **100 Bq** [1]
**(b)** 100 : 6400 = **1 : 64** [1]

**11. (a)** Irradiation is exposing an object to radiation, and the object does not become radioactive [1]; contamination is the unwanted presence of radioactive atoms on or in other materials, which go on emitting radiation as they decay [1]
**(b)** Once inside the body, the radioactive atoms keep decaying close to living cells [1]; alpha particles are strongly ionising, so they cause a lot of damage to the nearby cells [1]
**(c)** Any two, for example: use shielding (such as lead screens) [1]; keep as far from the source as practical, or handle it with tongs [1]; limit the time of exposure [1]

**12.** Other scientists can check the findings by peer review [1]; so errors are found and the conclusions about safety can be trusted and acted on [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a beta particle comes from the electrons around the nucleus.
- Balancing only the mass numbers in a nuclear equation.
- Choosing a source without explaining why the other two are unsuitable.
- Reading half-life from a single pair of values that do not halve exactly; choose a pair that does.
- Saying irradiated objects become radioactive.
