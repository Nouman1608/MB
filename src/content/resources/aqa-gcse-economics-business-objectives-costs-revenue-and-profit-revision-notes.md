## Objectives in one table

| Objective | Meaning | How it is measured |
|---|---|---|
| Profit | Revenue above costs | Total revenue - total costs |
| Sales growth | More units or more sales value over time | % change in sales |
| Increasing market share | A bigger portion of the whole market | Firm's sales / market sales x 100 |

- Firms often have more than one objective, and they can conflict. Winning share by cutting price lowers profit per unit.
- A new firm often values growth more than profit at first. An established firm may focus on profit.

## Cost formulas

| Term | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost (TC) | TFC + TVC |
| Total variable cost (TVC) | AVC x output |
| Average fixed cost (AFC) | TFC / output |
| Average variable cost (AVC) | TVC / output |
| Average cost (AC) | TC / output, or AFC + AVC |

- **Fixed**: does not change with output (rent, insurance, salaries, lease).
- **Variable**: changes with output (materials, packaging, pay per item).
- AFC always falls as output rises, because the fixed bill is shared out.
- Decide fixed or variable from how the cost behaves, not from how often it is paid.

## Revenue and profit formulas

| Term | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total revenue (TR) | Price x quantity sold |
| Average revenue (AR) | TR / quantity sold (equals price if one price is charged) |
| Profit | TR - TC (a negative result is a loss) |
| Profit per unit | AR - AC |

## Method in steps

1. Write down what you are given and what you need.
2. Find TFC, then TVC (AVC x output), then TC.
3. Find TR (price x quantity). If prices differ, add each group's revenue first.
4. Subtract: profit = TR - TC.
5. Check: profit per unit x units sold should equal total profit.
6. State units (£, per unit) and show working.

## Worked reminder 1: costs and profit

Oakleaf Stools (illustrative) has fixed costs of £1,500 a month and variable cost of £22 per stool. It makes and sells 250 stools at £40.

```
TVC = 22 x 250 = £5,500
TC  = 1,500 + 5,500 = £7,000
AC  = 7,000 / 250 = £28
AFC = 1,500 / 250 = £6
TR  = 40 x 250 = £10,000
Profit = 10,000 - 7,000 = £3,000   (check: (40 - 28) x 250 = 3,000)
```

## Worked reminder 2: three routes to higher profit

Starting from the £3,000 profit above:

| Route | Working | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Lower average cost: AVC falls to £20 | TC = 1,500 + 20 x 250 = 6,500; 10,000 - 6,500 | £3,500 |
| Price falls to £38, same sales | TR = 38 x 250 = 9,500; 9,500 - 7,000 | £2,500 |
| Sell 100 stools only, at £30 | TR = 3,000; TC = 1,500 + 2,200 = 3,700 | -£700 (loss) |

## Ways to raise profit: a checklist

- **Reduce average costs**: find cheaper suppliers, cut waste, negotiate a lower rent, or spread fixed costs over more units. Name the cost that falls and say whether it is fixed or variable.
- **Increase revenue**: sell more units (for example through advertising), or charge a higher price. Say what might happen to sales if the price rises.
- **Do both**: the biggest gain usually comes when costs fall and revenue rises together.
- **Check the net effect**: extra advertising is a cost, so revenue must rise by more than the new cost for profit to increase.

## Higher prices and the incentive to expand

Chain: **price rises, so profit per unit rises (if costs are unchanged), so supplying more is more attractive, so firms expand and new firms may enter.** This explains the upward-sloping supply curve. A higher price does not always mean higher total profit, because quantity sold may fall.

## Moral and ethical considerations

The specification says producers' motives may conflict with ethical and moral interests.

- **Conflicts**: low wages, unsafe conditions, pollution, misleading claims, harmful or unsustainable materials.
- **Why a firm may do it**: lower average costs or higher revenue, so more profit.
- **Who loses**: workers, local residents, customers, the environment.
- **Evaluation**: reputation, fines and lost customers can cancel the gain; the answer depends on laws, customer awareness and firm size. Conclude with a clear judgement.

## Worked reminder 3: share, growth and mixed prices

A small juice stall (illustrative) sells £30,000 of juice a year in a town market worth £250,000. Next year its sales rise to £33,000 and the market grows to £330,000.

```
Share now  = 30,000 / 250,000 x 100 = 12%
Sales growth = (33,000 - 30,000) / 30,000 x 100 = 10%
Share next year = 33,000 / 330,000 x 100 = 10%
```

Sales grew by 10% but market share fell from 12% to 10%, because the whole market grew faster than the stall did. This is why sales growth and market share are separate objectives.

Mixed prices: the stall sells 400 cups at £3 and 100 cups at £2 in a closing-down sale.

```
TR = (400 x 3) + (100 x 2) = 1,200 + 200 = £1,400
AR = 1,400 / 500 = £2.80
```

## Evaluation frame for ethics questions

Use this order and keep each step to a sentence or two:

1. **Point**: the firm can raise profit by a named action, such as cutting waste-disposal costs.
2. **Evidence**: use any figures given, such as average cost falling from £14 to £11.50.
3. **Harm**: name who loses and how, such as local residents or workers.
4. **Counter-argument**: fines, a damaged reputation and lost customers may cost more than the saving.
5. **Judgement**: say which side is stronger for this firm, and why.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Revenue is not profit.** Revenue is money in; profit is revenue minus costs.
- **Total versus average.** Average figures are per unit; totals are for all units.
- **AVC versus AC.** AC includes fixed costs as well.
- **Sales growth versus market share.** A firm's sales can grow while its market share falls, if the whole market grows faster.
- **Higher price versus higher profit.** A price rise lifts profit per unit but may cut sales.

## Quick self-test

1. Oakleaf's sales are £18,000 in a market worth £120,000. Calculate its market share.
2. Is insurance a fixed or a variable cost? Is wood for stools?
3. Fixed costs are £1,500 and AVC is £22. Calculate total cost at 250 stools.
4. Calculate average cost and average fixed cost at 250 stools.
5. At a price of £40, calculate total revenue and profit for 250 stools (use your answer to question 3).
6. A theatre earns £9,120 from 240 tickets sold at mixed prices. Calculate average revenue.
7. The stool price falls to £38 and sales stay at 250. Calculate the new profit.
8. AVC falls to £20 at 250 stools and the price is £40. Calculate the new profit.
9. Output rises to 500 stools. Calculate average fixed cost.
10. Explain why a higher price gives producers an incentive to expand.
11. Sales of 25,000 units rise by 8%. Calculate the new sales level.
12. State one way a producer's motive for profit could conflict with ethical interests.

### Answers

1. 18,000 / 120,000 x 100 = **15%**.
2. Insurance is **fixed**. Wood is **variable**.
3. TVC = 22 x 250 = 5,500; TC = 1,500 + 5,500 = **£7,000**.
4. AC = 7,000 / 250 = **£28**; AFC = 1,500 / 250 = **£6**.
5. TR = 40 x 250 = £10,000; profit = 10,000 - 7,000 = **£3,000**.
6. 9,120 / 240 = **£38**.
7. TR = 38 x 250 = 9,500; profit = 9,500 - 7,000 = **£2,500**.
8. TC = 1,500 + 20 x 250 = 6,500; profit = 10,000 - 6,500 = **£3,500**.
9. 1,500 / 500 = **£3**.
10. A higher price widens the gap between price and average cost, so profit per unit rises. Firms therefore want to produce and sell more.
11. 25,000 x 1.08 = **27,000 units**.
12. For example, cutting costs by paying very low wages or by polluting, which harms workers or local people.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a cost fixed or variable without checking whether it changes with output.
- Dividing by the wrong quantity, such as using TVC instead of TC to find AC.
- Leaving fixed costs out of total cost before working out profit.
- Giving a final figure with no working, so a slip cannot be traced.
- Writing "profit" when the answer is revenue, or the reverse.
- Claiming a price rise always raises profit.
- Describing an ethical issue without saying who is affected and why the firm gains.
- Listing points in an evaluation without a final judgement.
- Missing the £ sign or the "per unit" label on average figures.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is revenue the same as profit?**
No. Revenue is the money coming in from sales; profit is total revenue minus total costs.

**What is the difference between AVC and AC?**
Average variable cost is total variable cost divided by output. Average cost also includes fixed costs: total cost divided by output.

**Can a firm's sales grow while its market share falls?**
Yes. If the whole market grows faster than the firm's sales, its share of the market falls.

**How do I tell if a cost is fixed or variable?**
Check whether it changes with output. A monthly salary is fixed unless it changes with the amount produced.
