These questions cover sections 1.1.5 (The mixed economy) and 1.1.6 (Externalities) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

**1.** State two characteristics of a public good. **[2]**

**2.** Define privatisation. **[2]**

**3.** Country D has government spending of 84 billion units and national output of 280 billion units. Country E has government spending of 168 billion units and national output of 300 billion units.

**(a)** Calculate government spending as a percentage of national output for each country. **[4]**
**(b)** State which country has the larger public sector and explain one reason why a country might choose a larger public sector. **[2]**

**4.** Explain two differences between the public sector and the private sector. **[4]**

**5.** A coastal town of 400 households is considering a flood barrier. It costs 1,500 units a year. Each household gains 5 units a year from the protection.

**(a)** Calculate the total benefit and the net benefit of the barrier. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why charging households would not lead a private firm to build the barrier. **[4]**
**(c)** The council funds it by an equal charge on every household. Calculate the charge. **[1]**

**6.** An electricity firm makes 2,500 units a week. Its private cost is 62 per unit and its pollution creates an external cost of 17 per unit. It sells each unit for 70.

**(a)** Find the social cost per unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the total external cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the firm's weekly profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the net gain or loss to society, and state which it is. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the firm may continue to produce although society is worse off. **[3]**

**7.** A clinic gives 12,000 vaccinations a year. Each gives a private benefit of 22 units to the patient and an external benefit of 38 units to other people.

**(a)** Calculate the social benefit per vaccination. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total external benefit. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the external benefit as a percentage of the social benefit, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a government might subsidise vaccinations. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how traffic congestion creates an external cost, and identify who bears it. **[3]**

**9.** A water company in Country G is privatised. Before the sale it employed 900 workers and charged 1.20 units per cubic metre. Afterwards it employs 720 workers and charges 1.38 units.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the workforce. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the price. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one benefit of the sale to the government. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how the problem of for whom to produce is solved in a mixed economy. **[4]**

**11.** Country H is deciding whether to privatise its state-owned rail company, which has few rivals. Evaluate whether it should do so. **[9]**

**12.** Country A has 12 million workers, of whom 1.8 million are employed by the government.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of workers employed in the public sector. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why governments, and not private firms, provide education and national defence. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Non-excludable: nobody can be stopped from using it once it exists [1]. Non-rival: use by one person leaves the same amount for everyone else [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the feature and add a short explanation; a bare word such as "shared" does not show the feature.

**2.** The transfer of ownership of an asset or business [1] from the public sector (government) to the private sector [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The definition needs both the direction of transfer and the idea of ownership; "selling things" alone is too vague.

**3. (a)** Country D: 84 ÷ 280 × 100 [1] = **30%** [1]. Country E: 168 ÷ 300 × 100 [1] = **56%** [1].
**(b)** Country E has the larger public sector [1]. A reason: it can fund services such as healthcare and education so that access does not depend on income [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division so the method is visible; state the answer with the percentage sign, and tie the reason to the sector rather than just naming a service.

**4.** Ownership: the public sector is owned by the government, while the private sector is owned by individuals or shareholders [1]; this means public sector profits or losses fall on the taxpayer, while private owners keep the profit [1]. Aims: the public sector usually aims to meet needs and provide access [1], while the private sector usually aims to make profit [1]. (Accept control or funding as an alternative.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each difference needs a contrast and a short development; naming a heading such as "aims" without saying what the aim is for each sector earns little.

**5. (a)** Total benefit = 400 × 5 = **2,000 units** [1]. Net benefit = 2,000 − 1,500 = **500 units** [1].
**(b)** The barrier protects everyone in the town whether or not they pay, so it is non-excludable [1]. One household's protection does not reduce another's, so it is non-rival [1]. Households can free ride, so few would pay voluntarily [1]. The firm's revenue would not cover its cost, so no private firm supplies it [1].
**(c)** 1,500 ÷ 400 = **3.75 units** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links both features to the free rider problem and ends on why revenue is too low; stating the features without the consequence leaves the chain incomplete.

**6. (a)** 62 + 17 = **79** per unit [1].
**(b)** 17 × 2,500 = **42,500** [1].
**(c)** Profit per unit = 70 − 62 = 8 [1]; × 2,500 = **20,000** [1].
**(d)** Social cost = 79 × 2,500 = 197,500 and revenue is 175,000, so (70 − 79) × 2,500 [1] = **a loss of 22,500** to society [1].
**(e)** The firm counts only its private cost of 62 [1], so it sees a profit of 8 per unit [1]. The external cost falls on other people, so the firm does not pay it [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In the calculation parts give units and say gain or loss; in the explanation part, say whose cost the firm ignores, as the profit figure alone does not answer "why".

**7. (a)** 22 + 38 = **60** [1].
**(b)** 38 × 12,000 = **456,000** [1].
**(c)** 38 ÷ 60 × 100 [1] = **63.3%** [1].
**(d)** Patients consider only their private benefit of 22 [1]. Others also gain because the disease spreads less [1]. So fewer people are vaccinated than is best for society [1]. A subsidy lowers the price and raises take-up towards the socially best level [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Round as asked and show the fraction before rounding; for "explain why a government might", a chain of cause and result scores better than a list of benefits.

**8.** Each extra driver slows other road users [1]. The extra time and fuel used by those drivers is a cost to them that the driver did not pay [1]. It falls on other drivers, bus passengers and businesses, which are third parties [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the third party and say what the cost is for them; "roads get busy" alone does not show an external cost.

**9. (a)** (720 − 900) ÷ 900 × 100 [1] = **−20%** [1].
**(b)** (1.38 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 [1] = **+15%** [1].
**(c)** The government receives sale proceeds [1] and no longer needs to fund losses or subsidise the firm [1]. (Accept: less management time.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign on a percentage change; a fall written without a minus or the word fall can be misread.

**10.** In the market, output goes to buyers with the income to pay for it [1], so people with higher incomes get more [1]. The government changes this by taxing higher incomes more heavily or paying benefits [1], and by providing services such as education or healthcare free or cheaply [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both halves of the mixed economy; an answer that only describes the market does not answer for a mixed economy.

**11.** Points for privatisation (up to 4): sale proceeds for the government [1]; a profit motive may cut costs and improve efficiency [1]; the government saves any subsidy [1]; investment may rise as private owners can raise funds [1]. Points against (up to 4): with few rivals, the firm may raise fares [1]; unprofitable routes may be cut [1]; workers may lose jobs [1]; the government loses control over a key service [1]. Judgement (1): a supported conclusion, for example that with few rivals the case for privatisation is weaker unless a regulator or new competitors limit fares [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the gainers against the losers and make the conclusion depend on the stated lack of competition; a list of effects with no decision is only part of an evaluation.

**12. (a)** 1.8 ÷ 12 × 100 [1] = **15%** [1].
**(b)** Education: has large external benefits [1], so a market would provide too little [1]. National defence: it is a public good, non-excludable and non-rival [1], so free riding means private firms could not charge enough [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Two different reasons are needed, and each should name the market problem behind it, such as external benefits or free riding.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "shared" or "used by everyone" instead of the terms non-excludable and non-rival.
- Treating a government-run service such as schooling as a public good.
- Defining an externality without naming a third party.
- Adding the external cost to the private benefit, or mixing up the two formulae.
- Stopping a percentage calculation at the division, or dropping the sign on a fall.
- Forgetting to multiply a per-unit figure by the quantity for a total.
- Describing the free rider problem as people refusing to use a good.
- Listing effects of privatisation on all four groups with no judgement.
