Theme 1). For the full explanation, read the

## Key terms

- **Population density** -- the average number of people per square kilometre.
- **Population distribution** -- how people are spread across an area; where it is dense or sparse.
- **Densely populated** -- many people per km2. **Sparsely populated** -- few people per km2.
- **Even / uneven distribution** -- people spread fairly evenly, or concentrated in some places and
  absent from others.
- **Choropleth map** -- a map that shades areas by density, darker for higher values.
- **Dot map** -- each dot stands for a fixed number of people, showing distribution directly.

```
population density = total population / area in km2
```

Worked example (illustrative): 6 000 000 people in 20 000 km2 gives 300 people per km2.
An average hides variation: a country can have a crowded capital and an empty interior.

**Second worked example (illustrative):** Region A has 450 000 people in 1 500 km2; Region B has
450 000 people in 15 000 km2. Region A = 300 people per km2; Region B = 30 people per km2. The totals
are the same, but A is ten times more densely populated. This is why density, not total population,
is used to compare places of different sizes.

## The world pattern

Most of the world's people live in a few dense clusters: **East Asia** (eastern China, Japan,
Korea), **South Asia** (the Ganges valley and India), **Europe** and **north-eastern North America**.
Large areas are almost empty: hot deserts such as the Sahara, cold regions such as northern Canada,
Siberia and Antarctica, high mountains such as the Himalayas and dense rainforest such as the
Amazon basin. People also cluster near coasts and along major rivers.

## Physical factors

| Dense population | Sparse population |
|---|---|
| Flat or gently sloping land | Mountains and steep slopes |
| Fertile soil | Poor, thin or infertile soil |
| Reliable water supply | Lack of fresh water (deserts) |
| Mild climate | Very hot, very cold or very wet climate |
| Mineral resources | Dense rainforest, hard to clear and settle |

Explain each link: flat land is easier to build on, farm and cross with roads and railways; fertile
soil and water support farming and food supply.

**More explained links:**

- **Climate:** a moderate temperature and reliable rainfall allow crops to grow for much of the year;
  extreme cold gives a very short growing season and frozen ground.
- **Coasts and rivers:** provide water, fishing, fertile floodplains and routes for trade, so many
  large cities grew at river mouths and natural harbours.
- **Rainforest:** dense vegetation is hard to clear, soils lose their fertility quickly once the
  forest is removed, and disease and poor access discourage settlement.
- **Natural hazards** such as frequent flooding or volcanic eruptions can discourage settlement --
  though fertile volcanic or floodplain soils can also attract dense farming populations.

## Human and economic factors

- **Jobs and industry** attract workers and their families.
- **Good transport links** make trade and commuting possible.
- **Political stability** and **government policy** (for example investment in a region) attract people;
  conflict drives them away.
- **Services** such as schools and hospitals pull people in.

Physical factors often matter most where people depend on farming; human and economic factors
matter more in industrial and urban areas. Technology can overcome physical limits, for example
irrigation in dry areas.

**Further examples of human influence:** ports and capital cities grow because government and trade
are concentrated there; mining towns appear in harsh environments because of valuable minerals;
tourism can draw people to coasts and mountains; and governments can build a new capital to spread
people inland.

## Describing a distribution on a map

1. **Overall pattern:** where is it densest and sparsest?
2. **Link to features:** coast, river, city, mountains, desert.
3. **Anomalies:** a dense area where you would not expect it, or the reverse.
4. **Evidence:** quote densities or place names from the map.

**Model description (illustrative):** "The population is unevenly distributed. The highest densities,
over 500 people per km2, are along the coast and in the river valley in the south-east. Density falls
inland, and the mountains in the west have fewer than 10 people per km2. One exception is a mining
town in the north, where density is higher than the surrounding area."

## Case study: the population distribution of a country

The syllabus asks for a case study of the population distribution of a country. Two well-documented
choices:

**Egypt.** The overwhelming majority of Egyptians -- often quoted as about 95 per cent -- live on
only a small fraction of the land, in the **Nile valley and delta**. The river provides water for
drinking and irrigation and, historically, fertile silt; the flat floodplain is easy to farm and
build on; and the main cities, **Cairo** and **Alexandria**, grew there as centres of government,
trade and industry. Away from the river, the **Western Desert** and **Eastern Desert** are almost
empty because rainfall is extremely low and there is no reliable surface water. Small clusters exist
at **oases**, along the **Suez Canal** and at tourist resorts on the Red Sea coast, where water is
piped or desalinated. The government has built new towns in the desert to relieve crowding in the
valley.

**Brazil.** Most people live in the south-east and along the Atlantic coast, in cities such as
**São Paulo** and **Rio de Janeiro**, where the first colonial settlements, ports, industry and
farmland developed. The **Amazon basin** in the north and west is sparsely populated: dense
rainforest, poor access, heavy rainfall and few jobs discourage settlement. The government built a
new capital, **Brasília**, in the interior (opened in 1960) and roads into the Amazon to encourage
people to move inland, with partial success.

For your chosen country, learn: where people are concentrated, where they are sparse, two physical
and two human reasons for each, and any government attempt to change the pattern.

## Exam traps

- "Population density" is **per km2**, not a total. A big total is not a high density.
- Naming a factor with no explanation (for example just "flat land").
- Giving only physical factors when human and economic factors are asked for, or the reverse.
- Describing without any figures or place names from the map.
- Treating the average density of a country as true of every part of it.
- Saying a hazard always repels people -- fertile soils can attract them despite the risk.

## Self-test

1. Define population density and state the units.
2. Give three physical factors that give a dense population.
3. Why do mountains tend to have sparse populations?
4. Name two human or economic factors that attract people to an area.
5. Why can a country's average density be misleading?
6. Calculate the density of an area with 2 400 000 people in 8 000 km2.
7. Name two of the world's most densely populated regions.
8. Why are rainforests sparsely populated?
9. Why do most Egyptians live in the Nile valley and delta?
10. Give one example of technology allowing people to live in a harsh environment.
11. Why did Brazil build Brasília in the interior?

**Answers:** 1. The average number of people per square kilometre. 2. For example flat land, fertile
soil and a reliable water supply. 3. Steep slopes make building, farming and transport hard, and
the climate is often harsh. 4. For example jobs and good transport links. 5. It hides large
differences between crowded and empty parts of the country. 6. 300 people per km2. 7. For example
East Asia and South Asia (or Europe). 8. Dense vegetation is hard to clear, soils soon lose fertility
once cleared, and access is poor. 9. The Nile provides water and fertile, flat land in an otherwise
desert country, and the main cities and jobs are there. 10. For example irrigation or desalination
in dry areas. 11. To draw people and development away from the crowded coast into the sparsely
populated interior.
