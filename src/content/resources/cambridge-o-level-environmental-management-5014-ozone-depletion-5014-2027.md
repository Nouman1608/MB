Sub-topic 4.4 Ozone depletion is the shortest sub-topic in Topic 4 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029, with four statements: the cause (4.4.1), the impacts (4.4.2), the strategies (4.4.3) and a discussion of their benefits and limitations (4.4.4). It is also the clearest example in the course of an environmental problem that international action has largely brought under control, which makes it a useful comparison when you discuss climate change.

## Where the ozone layer is and what it does

From sub-topic 4.1 you know that the **ozone layer** is in the **lower stratosphere**. Ozone there absorbs much of the ultraviolet radiation from the Sun. When the layer becomes thinner, more of that radiation reaches the Earth's surface. "Ozone depletion" means this thinning of the ozone layer, and the most severe thinning, seen over Antarctica in spring, is often called the "ozone hole".

## The cause: CFCs

Statement 4.4.1 asks you to **state** that **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** in **aerosols** and **refrigerants** are the cause of ozone depletion in the ozone layer. A short statement is enough for the marks, but understanding why helps with later questions.

- CFCs were widely used because they are unreactive, non-flammable and non-toxic at ground level. They were used as propellants in aerosol cans and as the cooling fluid in fridges, freezers and air conditioners.
- Because they are so unreactive, CFCs are not broken down in the lower atmosphere. They drift slowly up into the stratosphere.
- There, strong ultraviolet radiation breaks the CFC molecules apart and releases chlorine, which destroys ozone. One chlorine atom can destroy many ozone molecules.
- CFCs stay in the atmosphere for many decades, so ozone destruction continues long after their use stops.

Do not confuse this with climate change. CFCs are the cause of ozone depletion in this syllabus; carbon dioxide and methane are not.

## The three impacts

| Impact | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Higher levels of solar radiation reaching the Earth's surface | A thinner ozone layer absorbs less ultraviolet radiation, so more passes through |
| Increased rates of cancer and cataracts | Ultraviolet radiation damages skin cells, increasing the risk of skin cancer, and damages the lens of the eye, causing cataracts, which cloud vision |
| Damage to vegetation resulting in reduced crop yields | Ultraviolet radiation damages plant tissues, so plants grow less and harvests fall |

The impacts form a chain: CFCs → thinner ozone layer → more ultraviolet radiation at the surface → harm to people and plants. In an "explain" answer, show each link rather than jumping straight from CFCs to cancer.

## The three strategies

### International agreement and policies

The **Montreal Protocol**, agreed in 1987, is the international agreement under which countries committed to phase out the production and use of CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances. Its targets have been tightened over the years, and almost every country has joined. Countries then put the agreement into practice through their own policies and laws.

### CFC ban and alternatives to CFCs

Countries **banned** the production and use of CFCs, and manufacturers switched to **alternatives**: other propellants or pump sprays in aerosols, and different cooling fluids in fridges and air conditioners. A ban only works if alternatives exist, which is one reason this strategy succeeded: substitutes were available at a reasonable cost.

### Safe disposal of CFCs

Fridges, freezers and air conditioners made before the ban still contain CFCs in their cooling systems and insulating foam. **Safe disposal** means removing and collecting the CFCs before the appliance is scrapped, so they are not released into the air.

## Benefits and limitations

| Strategy | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| International agreement and policies | A single global framework; almost all countries joined; production of CFCs fell sharply | Relies on every country meeting its commitments; monitoring is needed to detect illegal production |
| CFC ban and alternatives | Stops new CFCs entering the atmosphere; alternatives allow industries to keep making the same products | Some early alternatives still damaged ozone a little, and some later ones are powerful greenhouse gases; a ban does nothing about CFCs already released |
| Safe disposal | Prevents release from the large number of old appliances still in use | Needs collection systems and trained workers; appliances dumped or broken up informally still release CFCs |

The most important limitation applies to all three: **CFCs already in the atmosphere last for decades**, so the ozone layer recovers slowly even when the strategies work. A good judgement weighs this slow recovery against the success of the global response.

## Worked example: interpreting the response to CFCs

*Figures invented for practice.* The table shows global CFC production and an index of chlorine in the stratosphere.

| Year | CFC production (thousand tonnes) | Stratospheric chlorine index |
|---|---|---|
| 1985 | 1,000 | 80 |
| 1995 | 200 | 100 |
| 2005 | 20 | 95 |
| 2015 | 5 | 88 |

1. Fall in production from 1985 to 2015 = 1,000 − 5 = 995 thousand tonnes.
2. Percentage fall = 995 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 99.5%.
3. Chlorine index from 1985 to 1995 rose from 80 to 100, an increase of 25%, even though production fell by 80% over the same decade.

Interpretation: production fell steeply after the international agreement, but chlorine in the stratosphere kept rising for some years and has fallen only slowly since. This is because CFCs released earlier take years to reach the stratosphere and then remain there for decades. The data support two conclusions at once: the strategies worked, and recovery takes a long time.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Discuss the benefits and limitations of strategies to manage the impacts of ozone depletion.* **[6]**

> The Montreal Protocol was an international agreement that almost every country joined, so action was taken worldwide, which matters because CFCs spread through the whole atmosphere. ✔ *(benefit)*
> Banning CFCs worked because alternatives were available for aerosols and refrigerants, so industries could adapt without great cost. ✔ *(benefit)*
> Safe disposal of old fridges stops CFCs in existing appliances from escaping. ✔ *(benefit)*
> However, CFCs remain in the atmosphere for decades, so the ozone layer will take a long time to recover. ✔ *(limitation)*
> Some alternatives are greenhouse gases, so solving one problem has added to another. ✔ *(limitation)*
> Overall the strategies have been largely successful because they stopped new CFCs, but the full benefit will not be seen for many years. ✔ *(judgement)*

## Common mistakes

- Naming carbon dioxide or methane as the cause of ozone depletion.
- Saying ozone depletion causes global warming. They are separate problems.
- Writing "more sunlight" instead of "more ultraviolet (solar) radiation".
- Giving only "skin cancer" when asked for impacts; cataracts and crop damage are also named.
- Describing the ban without mentioning alternatives, which were essential to its success.

## Exam technique

**State** questions on the cause need CFCs plus their uses in aerosols and refrigerants. **Describe** questions on impacts need the three named effects, ideally with the ultraviolet link. **Describe** questions on strategies need what each strategy is and how it reduces CFC release. **Discuss** questions need benefits, limitations (especially slow recovery) and a judgement; comparing with climate change can strengthen it, as long as you keep the two processes separate.
