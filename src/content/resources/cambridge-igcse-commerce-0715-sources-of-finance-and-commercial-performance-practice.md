## Section A - short questions

**1.** Which is a source of long-term finance? **[1]**
A overdraft   B debenture   C trade credit   D debt factoring

**2.** Working capital is calculated as: **[1]**
A revenue - cost of sales   B current assets - current liabilities   C gross profit - expenses   D assets + liabilities

**3.** A firm sells an asset and rents it back from the buyer. This is: **[1]**
A leasing   B hire purchase   C sale and leaseback   D crowdfunding

**4.** State **two** sources of short-term finance. **[2]**

**5.** Define retained profit. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** methods a business could use to improve its profit. **[2]**

## Section B - structured questions

**7.** *Nadia has designed a new board game. She has no savings, no assets and no sales record.*
(a) Identify **one** source of finance that would suit Nadia. **[1]**
(b) Explain **one** reason why it suits her. **[2]**
(c) Explain **one** reason why a bank loan might not suit her. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** advantages and **one** disadvantage of debt factoring. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the difference between ordinary shares and preference shares. **[4]**

**10.** *A shop sold 600 cases at $14 each. Cost of sales was $5,040 and expenses were $1,860. Current assets were $9,800 and current liabilities were $6,300.*
(a) Calculate revenue. **[1]**
(b) Calculate gross profit. **[1]**
(c) Calculate the gross profit margin. **[2]**
(d) Calculate net profit. **[1]**
(e) Calculate working capital. **[1]**
(f) Calculate the mark-up percentage, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**11.** *A wholesaler buys an item at $48 and adds a mark-up of 25%.*
(a) Calculate the selling price. **[2]**
(b) Explain **one** way in which the wholesaler could raise its mark-up. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether a growing company should raise money by issuing shares or by taking a long-term loan. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Debentures are long-term loans to a company. **[1]**

**2.** B. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. **[1]**

**3.** C. The asset is sold and then rented back. **[1]**

**4.** Any two: trade credit [1]; debt factoring [1]; hire purchase [1]; leasing [1]; sale and leaseback [1]; overdraft [1]; selling assets [1]; owner's savings [1]; retained profit [1]. **[max 2]**

**5.** Profit that is kept in the business [1] instead of being paid out to the owners as drawings or dividends [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two: reduce expenses [1]; increase mark-up [1]; change prices [1]; improve advertising [1]; introduce new products [1]. **[max 2]**

**7.** (a) Crowdfunding (or venture capital) [1]. (1 mark)
(b) Many people give small sums, so Nadia does not need assets or a trading record [1], and the response shows whether people want the game [1]. (2 marks)
(c) Banks usually want security and a record of earnings [1], which Nadia does not have, so she would probably be refused or charged high interest [1]. (2 marks) **[5]**

**8.** Any two advantages, 2 marks each: the business receives cash at once instead of waiting for customers to pay [2]; the business spends less time chasing debts [2]; it improves cash flow and working capital [2]. One disadvantage, 2 marks: the factor charges a fee [1], so the business receives less than the full value of the invoices [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Ordinary shareholders own the company and usually have voting rights [1]; their dividend varies with profit and may be zero in a poor year [1]. Preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend [1] that is paid before ordinary shareholders, and they usually have no vote [1]. **[4]**

**10.** (a) 600 x 14 = **$8,400** [1]. (1 mark)
(b) 8,400 - 5,040 = **$3,360** [1]. (1 mark)
(c) 3,360 / 8,400 [1] x 100 = **40%** [1]. (2 marks)
(d) 3,360 - 1,860 = **$1,500** [1]. (1 mark)
(e) 9,800 - 6,300 = **$3,500** [1]. (1 mark)
(f) Unit cost: 5,040 / 600 = $8.40 [1]. Mark-up: (14 - 8.40) / 8.40 x 100 [1] = **66.7%** [1]. (3 marks) **[9]**

**11.** (a) Mark-up in dollars: 48 x 25 / 100 = $12 [1]. Selling price: 48 + 12 = **$60** [1]. (2 marks)
(b) Any one, 2 marks: buy from a cheaper supplier or negotiate a bigger trade discount [1], which lowers the unit cost, so the same selling price gives a larger mark-up [1]; or raise the selling price [1] while keeping the same unit cost, though customers may buy less [1]. (2 marks) **[4]**

**12.** Up to 2 for shares, up to 2 for loan, up to 2 for a justified conclusion.
Shares: no repayment or fixed interest [1]; no pressure on cash flow in a poor year [1]; but new shareholders share control and profit [1].
Loan: owners keep control [1]; interest is a known cost and may be set against profit [1]; but interest and repayments must be paid even when profits are low, and security may be needed [1].
Conclusion: for example, a company whose profits are steady and wants to keep control may choose a loan, while one with uncertain profits may prefer shares [2]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up gross profit and net profit.
- Calculating gross profit margin on cost of sales instead of revenue.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 for a percentage.
- Naming a source of finance without saying why it suits the business.
- Writing that working capital is "cash".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is mark-up the same as margin?** No. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price; mark-up is profit as a percentage of the cost.

**What are the six commercial formulae in 0715?** Revenue, gross profit, gross profit margin, net profit, working capital and mark-up percentage.
