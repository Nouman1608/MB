These notes condense the six concepts of the IB Psychology course for first assessment 2027: bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective and responsibility. They apply at SL and HL. Read the fuller [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts/) first, then test yourself with the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts-practice/).

## The big picture

- Concepts are broad ideas that connect content and contexts. They are not taught as a separate unit.
- Choose **three to four** relevant concepts for each context.
- Paper 1 Section C (15 marks, one of two questions) and Paper 2 Section B (15 marks, unseen study) test concepts directly.
- The related terms in the guide are suggestions. Any other term is accepted if the link is relevant, explained and clearly justified.
- Markbands reward: explained knowledge, critical analysis, **links between concepts**, a reasoned conclusion consistent with the argument, and accurate terminology.

## One question per concept

The guide gives a model question for each concept. Memorise them, because they work as prompts for any study.

| Concept | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Bias | In what way may our knowledge of human behaviour be biased? |
| Causality | How can we know the cause(s) of observed behaviour? |
| Change | How can we know whether a behaviour is a result of natural maturation or purposeful intervention? |
| Measurement | How can complex constructs be expressed as observable, measurable behaviour? |
| Perspective | What are the strengths and limitations of a reductionist approach? |
| Responsibility | Can the potential benefits of research justify partly relaxing ethical standards? |

## Bias

- **Definition:** a limitation in objective thinking, a tendency to view information through a filter of experience and preference. It can be implicit or explicit.
- **Research biases:** researcher, participant, sampling, confirmation, publication.
- **Group biases:** gender bias and cultural bias (affecting diagnosis, prevalence figures and treatment).
- **Perspective bias:** determinism and reductionism.
- **Thinking biases:** cognitive biases such as anchoring, representativeness and availability.
- **Controls:** random sampling, double-blind designs, standardised procedures, reflexivity, inter-rater reliability, triangulation.
- **Do not stop at naming.** Say how the bias changes what was collected or how it was read.

## Causality

- Psychologists aim for cause and effect, but behaviour is usually several variables **interacting**.
- **Influence** is one factor pushing behaviour; **interaction** is factors acting together.
- Only a well-controlled **true experiment** supports a strong causal claim.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Watch for a third variable and for **bidirectional ambiguity**.
- Controls: extraneous variables, placebos, double-blind, wait-listing.
- **Internal validity** = did the independent variable cause the change? **External validity** = does it generalise? **Mundane realism** = realism of the task.
- **Significance** is about chance, not importance.

## Change

- Change can be gradual (development) or sudden (mood), planned or unplanned, deliberate or not.
- Free will versus determinism is the underlying debate.
- Researchers study **interventions, prevention and promotion strategies** and their **effectiveness**, and **barriers to change**.
- Designs: **longitudinal** (same people over time), **repeated measures**, cross-sectional (different people at one time).
- Maturation versus intervention: use a control or wait-list group to separate them.

## Measurement

- **Operationalisation** turns a construct into something measurable. Always give an example.
- **Triangulation** (more than one method) raises credibility.
- Evidence can be direct or indirect; anecdotal, empirical or self-reported; quantitative, qualitative or mixed.
- Techniques named in the guide: brain imaging, twin studies, virtual reality simulations, questionnaires.
- Statistical significance and **type I error** (false positive) and **type II error** (false negative).
- Prospective versus retrospective; longitudinal versus cross-sectional.

## Perspective

- The three approaches are biological, cognitive and sociocultural. No single one is enough.
- Every approach has **assumptions** about behaviour and beliefs about research.
- **Etic**: outsider view, compares cultures with the same tools. **Emic**: insider view, studies a group in its own terms.
- **Deductive** research starts from theory and tests it; **inductive** research builds theory from data.
- **Reductionism** explains behaviour by its simplest parts; **holism** looks at the whole.
- Include the perspectives of Indigenous people and alternative explanations.

## Responsibility

- Standards: informed consent, deception, anonymity, debriefing, right to withdraw, protection from harm, cost-benefit analysis.
- Animals: **reduce, refine, replace**.
- Children, public observation and socially sensitive topics need special care.
- Ethics can **change the data**: consent adds participant variables; the right to withdraw can bias the sample; anonymity can raise honesty and validity.
- After the study: protect marginalised groups from stigma, explain uncertainty to the public, advocate for policy that follows evidence.

## Linking concepts

A top-band answer links concepts and explains each link. Good pairs:

- **Bias and measurement:** poor operationalisation lets bias in.
- **Bias and causality:** a third variable is a hidden bias in the explanation.
- **Causality and responsibility:** a strong causal design (for example withholding a treatment) may cost participants, so ethics limits the design.
- **Change and measurement:** judging change needs a repeated or longitudinal measure.
- **Perspective and bias:** reductionism favours one explanation and can hide others.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a concept without applying it.
- Writing about one concept only.
- Claiming causation from correlational data.
- Listing ethical principles without saying which one is at stake and why.
- Ending without a conclusion, or with one that does not follow from the argument.

## Self-test

1. Give one example each of a research bias, a group bias and a cognitive bias.
2. Why can a correlational study not show causality? Name two reasons.
3. What is operationalisation? Operationalise "anxiety" in two ways.
4. What does the 3Rs principle stand for?
5. Explain how the right to withdraw can lead to sampling bias.
6. Which design best shows change within the same individuals over time?
7. State the difference between etic and emic.
8. Name two concepts that link to reductionism.

**Answers:** 1. For example, researcher bias; gender bias in diagnosis; anchoring. 2. A third variable may explain both; the direction of cause is unclear (bidirectional ambiguity); variables were not manipulated. 3. Turning a construct into measurable terms; for example score on an anxiety questionnaire and heart rate. 4. Reduce, refine, replace. 5. If participants who find the task unpleasant leave, those remaining may differ from the intended sample. 6. A longitudinal design (or repeated measures). 7. Etic is an outside view comparing cultures with the same measure; emic studies a group from the inside. 8. Bias and causality (also perspective).

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the concepts examined separately from the content?**
No. Paper 1 Section C asks concept-based questions from one context, and Paper 2 Section B asks you to evaluate an unseen study using the named concepts.

**Do I need to learn every related term?**
No. The guide says there is no requirement to teach all of them, and other terms are accepted if the link is relevant, explained and justified.

**How many concepts should I use in an answer?**
Use what the question names. In your own study of each context, the guide recommends choosing three to four relevant concepts.
