Each question uses one of the specification's command words at its published International A Level tariff: **define** (2), **calculate** (4), **explain** (4), **analyse** (6), **discuss** (8) and **assess** (12). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Sources

> **Source 1: Pennine Bakeries**
>
> Pennine has four bakeries and 480 employees. It has a strong role culture:
> jobs are tightly defined and most staff have worked there for over ten
> years. The board plans to install automated production lines. Option A
> is to install them in all four bakeries over one weekend; the managing
> director expects four days of disruption at $30,000 lost sales a day.
> Option B is to install one bakery per quarter over twelve months, with
> extra project management costing $8,000 a month. A trade union says 60
> jobs are at risk, and staff have said they were not told why the change
> was needed. The managing director wants to lead the change by setting out
> a clear vision for the business.
>
> **Source 2: Quill Online Retail**
>
> Quill sells books online. It has assessed three risks. An IT failure has
> an 8% yearly chance and would cost $300,000. Loss of the warehouse manager
> has a 25% yearly chance and would cost $80,000. A flood at the warehouse
> has a 1% yearly chance and would cost $1,000,000. A cloud backup service
> costing $10,000 a year would cut the cost of an IT failure to $60,000.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "resistance to change". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the cost of Option A and the cost of Option B. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate the percentage of Pennine's employees whose jobs the union says are at risk. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which Pennine's culture could affect the change. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** advantage of the phased approach for Pennine. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse why staff at Pennine may resist the change. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "business continuity planning". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the expected yearly loss from each of the three risks and the total. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the net yearly benefit of the cloud backup service. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **one** way in which Quill could prepare for the loss of its warehouse manager. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss whether Pennine's managing director should adopt a transformative style of leadership to manage the change. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Quill should invest in contingency planning for all three risks. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Opposition by employees (or other stakeholders) [1] to a planned change in the way a business operates [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Option A = 4 days x $30,000 [1] = **$120,000** [1]. Option B = 12 months x $8,000 [1] = **$96,000** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Jobs at risk = 60 out of 480 [1]. 60 / 480 x 100 [1] = **12.5%** [2]. **[4]**

**4.** Pennine has a strong role culture with tightly defined jobs [1], so employees are used to clear rules and stability and may see the change as a threat to their roles [1]. Most staff have worked there for over ten years, so habits are deeply embedded [1], which will make them slow to accept new automated ways of working [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Phasing the installation over four quarters allows Pennine to learn from the first bakery and avoid repeating mistakes in the others [1]. It also lets staff in later bakeries see that the change works and gives more time to retrain them [1], which reduces the risk of a shutdown affecting all four bakeries at once [1]. Phasing is also $24,000 cheaper than Option A (96,000 compared with 120,000), although benefits arrive later [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Staff fear job losses, since the union says 60 jobs are at risk [1]. Employees who have been in post for ten years or more may lack the skills for automated lines and worry about their status [1]. They were not told why the change was needed [1], so they may mistrust management and imagine the worst [1]. A role culture values stability, so a rapid change breaks established routines [1]. Resistance could appear as absence, lower productivity or resignations, which would slow or even defeat the change [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Planning that allows a business to keep operating, or to restart quickly [1], after a serious disruption such as a flood or an IT failure [1]. **[2]**

**8.** IT failure = 8% x 300,000 = **$24,000** [1]. Warehouse manager = 25% x 80,000 = **$20,000** [1]. Flood = 1% x 1,000,000 = **$10,000** [1]. Total = **$54,000** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Expected loss with backup = 8% x 60,000 = $4,800 [1]. Saving = 24,000 - 4,800 = $19,200 [1]. Net benefit = 19,200 - 10,000 [1] = **$9,200 a year** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Quill could start succession planning by training a deputy manager [1], so that someone already knows the warehouse systems and can take over at once [1]. This reduces the cost of disruption and lost orders if the manager leaves, the 25% yearly chance of which is the second largest expected loss at $20,000 [1]. Documenting procedures and offering the manager a career path would also help to reduce the chance of leaving [1]. **[4]**

**11.** **For:** a transformative leader would explain why automation is needed and set out a clear vision, which tackles the lack of communication staff complained about [1]; inspiring staff could turn resistance into commitment [1]; major change in a long-established role culture needs a change in attitudes, not just obedience [1].
**Against:** it takes time to build trust, and the 60 job risks may make the vision hard for staff to accept [1]; the managing director's vision may not be shared by long-serving staff, who might see it as a threat [1]; it depends heavily on one leader [1].
**Judgement:** the managing director should adopt a transformative style but support it with practical steps: consultation with the union, training and clear information on jobs [1]. A vision alone will not win over staff who fear for their livelihoods, and the approach would be less suitable if the business were in crisis and needed instant decisions [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **For investing in all three:** the combined expected loss is $54,000 a year, so the risks together are material [1]; a flood is rare but a $1,000,000 loss would be far greater than Quill could easily absorb [1]; loss of key staff is quite likely (25%), and succession planning is cheap compared with a $80,000 cost [1]; the IT backup saves a net $9,200 a year, so it pays for itself [1].
**Against investing in all three:** the flood has an expected loss of only $10,000, so an expensive solution such as a second warehouse may cost more than it saves [1]; insurance may be a cheaper way to deal with a rare event [1]; each plan needs time, management attention and regular testing, which small firms may not have [1]; plans that are never tested may not work [1].
**Judgement:** Quill should plan for all three, but in proportion: act on the IT backup straight away because it has a clear net benefit, start succession planning because it is cheap and the risk is likely, and cover the flood by insurance rather than a costly physical solution [1]. The priority depends on how Quill's cash flow and insurance premiums compare with the potential $1,000,000 loss [1]. The plans should be tested and updated yearly [1], and the probabilities and costs reviewed, because a change in either would alter which risks deserve spending [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating Option A as one day's loss rather than four days.
- Working out expected loss with the percentage and cost the wrong way round.
- Forgetting to subtract the $10,000 cost of the backup from the saving.
- Describing resistance in general terms rather than using the union's 60 jobs and the lack of communication.
- Describing transformative leadership as forcing change through.
