## Section A

**1.** Name the three experimental designs, and state which design
counterbalancing is used to address. **[3]**

**2.** State the difference between validity and reliability. **[2]**

**3.** Name the three sampling techniques on this syllabus. **[3]**

**4.** State two things a Paper 2 planning answer is NOT required to
include, unlike a Paper 4 plan. **[2]**

**5.** Name the four descriptive pairs used to classify observations. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** A researcher conducts a memory experiment in a quiet laboratory
using a standardised word list, and finds very consistent results
across repeated trials.

**(a)** Explain why this study can be described as having high
reliability. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this same study might still lack ecological
validity. **[2]**

**7.** A researcher wants to observe children's behaviour in a
playground without the children knowing they are being watched, in
order to reduce demand characteristics.

**(a)** Name two ethical issues specifically relevant to using human
participants in this scenario. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the checklist of ethical issues would be different
if the study instead involved animals. **[2]**

**8.** A candidate is asked to plan a field experiment as part of the
Section B planning question.

**(a)** Name three general features that must be included in any
Paper 2 plan, regardless of method. **[3]**
**(b)** Name two features specific to a field experiment that should
also be included. **[2]**

**9.** A candidate includes aims, hypotheses and an evaluation of the
plan's ethics in their Paper 2 planning answer, believing this will
gain extra credit.

**(a)** Explain why this is a mistake. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest what the candidate should have focused on instead. **[1]**

**10.** Explain why the "describe, evaluate, apply" pattern is
described as useful for revising all six research methods, using
longitudinal studies as your example. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Independent measures, matched pairs and repeated measures [2].
Counterbalancing is used to address order effects, which occur
specifically in repeated measures designs [1].

**2.** Validity concerns whether a study measures what it claims to
measure; reliability concerns the consistency of results if the study
were repeated [2].

**3.** Opportunity sampling, random sampling, and volunteer
(self-selecting) sampling [3].

**4.** Any two of: aims or hypotheses in the plan; evaluation of
issues relating to sample or generalisability of the plan; evaluation
of ethics of the plan [2].

**5.** Overt/covert; participant/non-participant;
structured/unstructured; naturalistic/controlled [4].

**6. (a)** The study has high reliability because repeating the
procedure under the same standardised conditions produces similar
results each time, showing the findings are consistent [2].
**(b)** The study might lack ecological validity because real-world
memory rarely involves memorising isolated word lists in a quiet
laboratory, so the findings may not accurately reflect how memory
actually functions in everyday situations, even though the results are
highly consistent [2].

**7. (a)** Any two of: informed consent (children cannot meaningfully
consent themselves, so parental or guardian consent becomes relevant);
privacy (covert observation in a semi-public space raises different
considerations than in a clearly private space); deception (the
children are not told they are being observed); debriefing [2].
**(b)** Because the ethical framework for animals covers different
concerns — such as replacement (whether a non-animal method could
answer the same question), the specific species used, and procedural
issues like pain, suffering or distress — rather than consent-based
issues, which do not apply to animal participants [2].

**8. (a)** Any three of: the procedure of the study; how the plan
makes the study valid; how the plan makes the study reliable; the
types of data to be collected [3].
**(b)** Any two of: the independent variable; the dependent variable;
the controls used; the choice of experimental design; the location of
the study [2].

**9. (a)** This is a mistake because Paper 2 explicitly does not
require aims, hypotheses, or an evaluation of the plan's ethics; time
spent including these earns no credit and takes time away from the
features that are actually assessed [2].
**(b)** The candidate should have focused on the general features the
syllabus does require (procedure, validity, reliability, types of
data) plus the method-specific features relevant to the study being
planned [1].

**10.** The pattern works for longitudinal studies the same way it
works for every other method: first describe the method's main
features (studying the same participants over an extended period of
time), then evaluate it (for example, in terms of validity gained
through tracking real change over time, weighed against practical
issues such as participant attrition), then apply it to a novel
research scenario given in the question [3]. Because the underlying
structure of what is being asked never changes across methods, a
candidate confident with this pattern can approach an unfamiliar
scenario question about longitudinal studies with the same confidence
as a well-rehearsed question about experiments.

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Keep the six research methods and the block of methodological concepts
as two connected but distinct layers when revising: the methods
describe what a study is, while concepts like validity, reliability
and ethics describe what any study can be judged by, regardless of
method. When revising Paper 1's core studies alongside this paper,
always note which research method each one used, since Section A's
scenario questions frequently link a familiar study to the method that
produced it. For the planning question specifically, work through the
syllabus's own checklist of general features before adding
method-specific detail, and resist the temptation to add Paper 4-level
content such as aims, hypotheses or ethical evaluation, since these
earn no credit at Paper 2 and cost valuable time.
