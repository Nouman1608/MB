## Key equations

| Equation | Symbols | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ω = v/r = 2πf | ω angular speed (rad s⁻¹) | Circular motion, SHM |
| a = v²/r = ω²r | a centripetal acceleration | Circular motion |
| F = mv²/r = mω²r | F resultant force to centre | Circular motion |
| a = −ω²x | x displacement from equilibrium | Defines SHM |
| x = A cos ωt | A amplitude | SHM, timed from x = A |
| v = ±ω√(A² − x²) | v velocity at displacement x | SHM |
| v_max = ωA, a_max = ω²A | | SHM |
| T = 2π√(m/k) | k spring constant | Mass-spring |
| T = 2π√(l/g) | l length to centre of bob | Pendulum (small angles) |
| Q = mcΔθ | c specific heat capacity | Temperature change |
| Q = ml | l specific latent heat | Change of state |
| pV = nRT = NkT | n moles, N molecules | Ideal gas, T in kelvin |
| W = pΔV | ΔV change in volume | Work at constant pressure |
| pV = ⅓Nm(c_rms)² | c_rms root mean square speed | Kinetic theory |
| ½m(c_rms)² = (3/2)kT = (3/2)RT/N_A | | Mean molecular kinetic energy |

## 3.6.1.1 Circular motion

- 2π rad = 360°. θ (rad) = arc length/radius.
- Constant speed in a circle means changing velocity, so there is an acceleration towards the centre and a resultant (centripetal) force towards the centre.
- The centripetal force is supplied by real forces. Never add it as an extra arrow.
- Estimates: pick sensible r and f (or v), then a = ω²r and F = mω²r.

## 3.6.1.2 Simple harmonic motion

**Condition**: acceleration proportional to displacement and directed towards the equilibrium position.

**Method: SHM calculation**
1. Find ω = 2πf = 2π/T.
2. Maximum speed ωA at equilibrium; maximum acceleration ω²A at the extremes.
3. Speed at x: ω√(A² − x²). Acceleration at x: −ω²x.
4. Position at time t: x = A cos ωt (radians mode), only if timing starts at x = +A.

**Graph links**

| From | To | How |
|---|---|---|
| x–t | v–t | Gradient |
| v–t | a–t | Gradient |
| x–t (cosine) | a–t | Same shape, inverted, scaled by ω² |

## 3.6.1.3 Simple harmonic systems

- Periods do not depend on amplitude. Mass-spring T ∝ √m; pendulum T ∝ √l, and independent of the mass of the bob.
- Pendulum SHM relies on the small-angle approximation sin θ ≈ θ.
- Energy: E_k + E_p = constant (undamped). E_k maximum at x = 0; E_p maximum at x = ±A. Against time, each energy has **half** the period of the oscillation.
- Damping: light (amplitude decays, period about the same); critical (back to equilibrium fastest, no oscillation); heavy (slow return, no oscillation).
- **Required practical 7**: time many oscillations, repeat, time from the equilibrium position; plot T² against m or l and use the gradient (4π²/k or 4π²/g).

## 3.6.1.4 Forced vibrations and resonance

**Must-know distinctions: free vs forced**

| Free vibration | Forced vibration |
|---|---|
| No driving force after release | Periodic driving force |
| Oscillates at the natural frequency | Oscillates at the driving frequency |

- Resonance: driving frequency = natural frequency, giving maximum amplitude.
- Increasing damping: peak lower, broader, slightly lower in frequency.
- Examples: swings, car suspension, strings and air columns resonating at their stationary-wave frequencies.

## 3.6.2.1 Thermal energy transfer

- Internal energy = sum of randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the particles.
- First law (qualitative): internal energy rises when the body is heated or work is done on it.
- During a change of state: potential energies change, kinetic energies don't, so temperature is constant.
- Continuous flow: power = (mass per second) × c × Δθ.

**Method: mixtures**
1. Write energy lost by the hot part = energy gained by the cold part(s).
2. Include latent heat for any melting or boiling.
3. Use the same final temperature T in every term and solve.

**Must-know distinction**: temperature measures mean kinetic energy per particle; internal energy also includes potential energy and depends on how many particles there are.

## 3.6.2.2 Ideal gases

| Law (fixed mass) | Constant | Straight-line graph |
|---|---|---|
| Boyle | T | p against 1/V |
| Charles | p | V against T (through 0 K) |
| Pressure law | V | p against T (through 0 K) |

- Absolute zero: extrapolated intercept at about −273 °C. Add 273 to convert °C to K.
- k = R/N_A; N = nN_A; n = mass/molar mass; molecular mass = molar mass/N_A.
- Work done by a gas expanding at constant pressure = pΔV (area under a p–V graph).
- **Required practical 8**: Boyle (constant temperature, change pressure slowly) and Charles (constant pressure, water bath).

## 3.6.2.3 Kinetic theory

- Brownian motion: random motion of visible particles, evidence for molecules.
- Gas laws are empirical; kinetic theory is derived from assumptions.
- Assumptions: many identical molecules, random motion, elastic collisions, negligible molecular volume, no intermolecular forces except in collisions, negligible collision time.
- Derivation chain: Δp = 2mu → time between hits 2L/u → force mu²/L → sum over N → mean u² = ⅓(c_rms)² → p = F/L².
- c_rms = √(mean of c²), which is not the same as mean speed.
- Ideal gas internal energy = total kinetic energy = (3/2)NkT.

## Quick self-test

1. A turntable rotates at 45 revolutions per minute. Find its angular speed.
2. A cyclist rides round a circular track of radius 20 m at 8.0 m s⁻¹. Find the centripetal acceleration.
3. A 0.20 kg ball on a string moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.50 m at ω = 6.0 rad s⁻¹. Find the centripetal force.
4. An oscillator has A = 0.10 m and T = 2.0 s. Find v_max and a_max.
5. Find the period of a simple pendulum of length 1.00 m (g = 9.81 m s⁻²).
6. The mass on a spring is made four times larger. What happens to the period?
7. How much energy heats 0.50 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C? (c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
8. How much energy boils away 0.20 kg of water at 100 °C? (l = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹)
9. A gas occupies 0.0249 m³ at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and 300 K. How many moles are there? (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
10. Three molecules have speeds 200, 400 and 600 m s⁻¹. Find their mean speed and c_rms.
11. Find the mean kinetic energy of a gas molecule at 400 K. (k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹)
12. A gas expands from 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³ to 3.0 × 10⁻³ m³ at a constant 1.2 × 10⁵ Pa. Find the work done.

### Answers

1. ω = 2π × 45/60 = **4.7 rad s⁻¹**
2. a = 8.0²/20 = **3.2 m s⁻²**
3. F = mω²r = 0.20 × 36 × 0.50 = **3.6 N**
4. ω = π rad s⁻¹; v_max = **0.31 m s⁻¹**; a_max = **0.99 m s⁻²**
5. T = 2π√(1.00/9.81) = **2.0 s**
6. T ∝ √m, so the period **doubles**.
7. Q = 0.50 × 4200 × 80 = **1.7 × 10⁵ J**
8. Q = 0.20 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = **4.5 × 10⁵ J**
9. n = pV/RT = 2490/2493 = **1.0 mol**
10. Mean = **400 m s⁻¹**; c_rms = √[(200² + 400² + 600²)/3] = **432 m s⁻¹**
11. (3/2)kT = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 400 = **8.3 × 10⁻²¹ J**
12. W = pΔV = 1.2 × 10⁵ × 1.0 × 10⁻³ = **120 J**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not converting revolutions per minute to hertz before using ω = 2πf.
- Using x = A cos ωt with the calculator in degrees.
- Defining SHM without both parts: proportional to displacement **and** directed towards equilibrium.
- Drawing energy-time graphs with the same period as the motion instead of half.
- Describing resonance as "the system vibrates a lot" without saying driving frequency equals natural frequency.
- Forgetting the meltwater warming term in an ice-mixture calculation.
- Using °C in pV = nRT, or grams where kilograms are needed.
- Treating mean speed and c_rms as the same quantity.
- Skipping the "mean u² = ⅓(c_rms)²" step, or not explaining it, in the kinetic theory derivation.
