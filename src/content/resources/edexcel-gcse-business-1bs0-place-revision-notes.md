These notes condense content item 2.2.4 Place from Topic 2.2 Making marketing decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples are in the [place study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-place/) and exam-style questions in the [place practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-place-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one line

Place: **methods of distribution: retailers and e-tailers (e-commerce)**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Place | How and where a product reaches customers |
| Distribution | The process of getting products from the producer to the customer |
| Retailer | A business that sells goods to the final consumer, usually in shops |
| E-tailer | A business that sells to consumers online |
| E-commerce | Buying and selling over the internet |
| Online marketplace | A large website where many sellers list products, usually paying a fee |
| Multi-channel ("clicks and bricks") | Selling through both shops and online (standard terms) |
| Footfall | The number of people passing or entering a shop (standard term) |

## Retailers: for the producer

**Benefits:** the retailer's customers and footfall; customers can see and try products; the retailer runs premises, staff and payments; a known shop builds trust.

**Drawbacks:** the retailer's mark-up reduces what the producer receives; less control over price, display and service; large retailers can push prices down and pay slowly; limited shelf space.

## E-tailers: for the producer

**Own website:** keeps the full price, controls presentation, gathers customer data; but pays for the site, advertising, packing, delivery, payment fees and returns.

**Online marketplace:** huge reach and trusted payment systems; but fees or a share of each sale, intense competition on the same page and less control.

## For customers

| Shops | Online |
|---|---|
| See, touch and try before buying | Shop at any time from anywhere |
| Take the product home straight away | Wide choice and easy price comparison |
| Advice from staff | Delivery wait and possible returns |

## Choosing a method: five factors

1. **Product type:** does the customer need to see or try it?
2. **Target market:** where do these customers prefer to buy?
3. **Reach:** local, national or overseas?
4. **Control:** how much does the producer want over price and presentation?
5. **Cost:** retailer's share versus delivery, fees and returns.

## Calculations

- Contribution per unit through a route = amount received − unit cost − route costs per unit.
- Total contribution = contribution per unit × forecast volume.
- Marketplace fee = fee % × selling price.

**Quick calculation.** Thistle Socks makes a pack for £3. A shop pays £6 for it. Online, Thistle sells it for £10 but pays £1.80 postage and a 15% marketplace fee (£1.50). Contribution: shop £3.00; online £10 − £1.50 − £1.80 − £3 = **£3.70**. Online earns 70p more per pack, but only if enough people find it.

## Which route suits which business?

| Business | Likely route | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Local bakery selling fresh bread | Own shop or local retailers | Short shelf life; customers buy daily and nearby |
| Maker of phone accessories | Online marketplace and own website | Standard, light items bought on description; national reach |
| Bed maker | Shops plus a website with free returns | Customers want to test comfort before a large purchase |
| Craft jeweller | Own website and gift shops | Shops reach browsers; the website keeps more of a premium price |

## Linking place to the rest of the mix

- A premium price needs a place that matches: an upmarket shop or a well-designed website.
- Selling through a supermarket usually requires a lower price and large volumes.
- Online selling depends on online promotion, such as targeted adverts, to bring visitors.
- Products that must be tried, such as mattresses or perfume, often need shops or generous free returns.

## Exam traps

- Saying online selling is free because there is no shop.
- Comparing contribution per unit and ignoring volume.
- Forgetting that the retailer adds its own mark-up.
- Treating "retailer" as any business rather than one selling to the final consumer.
- Recommending online selling for a product customers need to try, without addressing returns.

## Self-test

1. Define the term e-tailer.
2. What is the difference between a retailer and an e-tailer?
3. Give two benefits to a producer of selling through retailers.
4. Give two costs of selling through a producer's own website.
5. A marketplace charges a 12% fee on a £25 product. Calculate the fee.
6. A producer makes an item for £8. A shop pays £14 for it. Calculate the contribution per item.
7. Why might a furniture maker prefer to sell through shops?
8. Give one benefit to customers of buying online.
9. A route earns £1.50 per unit on 4,000 units; another earns £2.40 per unit on 2,000 units. Which earns more in total, and by how much?
10. What is meant by multi-channel distribution?
11. Give one reason a producer might lose control when selling through a large retailer.
12. Why are returns a particular cost for online clothing sellers?

### Answers

1. A business that sells goods to consumers online.
2. A retailer sells to final consumers mainly through shops; an e-tailer sells to them online.
3. Any two: access to the retailer's customers; customers can see and try products; the retailer runs premises and payments; trust from being stocked by a known shop.
4. Any two: website upkeep; online advertising; packing and delivery; payment fees; returns.
5. 12% × £25 = **£3**.
6. £14 − £8 = **£6**.
7. Customers want to see, sit on and judge the quality of furniture before paying a large sum.
8. Convenience, wider choice, easy price comparison or shopping at any time.
9. First: £6,000; second: £4,800. The first earns **£1,200 more**.
10. Selling through more than one channel, such as both shops and online.
11. The retailer decides the shelf position, display and final price, and may push for lower prices.
12. Customers often order several sizes and send back the ones that do not fit, so the seller pays delivery and return costs on items that are not sold.
