## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** weaknesses of the Peace of Augsburg that contributed to the Thirty Years' War. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the events in Bohemia in 1618-1620. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why the Edict of Restitution (1629) widened the war. **[3]**

**4.** Identify **three** features of the *trace italienne* or of siege warfare in this period. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **two** reasons why Hideyoshi invaded Korea in 1592. **[4]**

**6.** Identify **two** ways in which Korea resisted the Japanese invasions. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why the will of Charles II of Spain led to war in 1701. **[3]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why the Japanese failed to conquer Korea. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the contribution of Gustavus Adolphus to Swedish success in the Thirty Years' War. **[4]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the effects of **two** Early Modern wars, each chosen from a different region, on the states that fought them. **[6]**

**11.** "Changes in military technology and tactics were the main reason for the outcomes of Early Modern wars." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view, using examples from at least **two** wars. **[8]**

**12.** To what extent was religion the main cause of the Thirty Years' War? **[15]**

**13.** Examine the reasons for the outcome of **one** Early Modern war. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: it recognised only Lutheranism and Catholicism, excluding Calvinism, which spread afterwards (1); it left disputes over church lands secularised by Protestants unresolved (1); the "ecclesiastical reservation" on bishops who converted was disputed (1); it gave no means of enforcing its terms when princes disagreed (1). **[2]**

**2.** Bohemian Protestants, fearing the Catholic Ferdinand, threw royal officials from a window of Prague Castle in May 1618 (1). They deposed Ferdinand and elected the Calvinist Frederick V of the Palatinate as king (1619) (1). Imperial and Catholic League forces defeated the Bohemians at White Mountain in 1620, and Frederick fled (1). **[3]**

**3.** It ordered the return of church lands taken by Protestants since 1552, threatening many Protestant princes, including Lutherans who had stayed loyal to the emperor (1). It showed how powerful Ferdinand II had become after his victories, alarming Protestant and foreign powers alike (1). It encouraged Sweden's intervention in 1630, which turned the war into a wider European conflict (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: low, thick walls that absorbed cannon fire (1); angled bastions allowing defenders to cover every approach with crossfire (1); outworks and ditches that kept attackers at a distance (1); sieges that took months and required trenches and large armies (1); the high cost of building and besieging such fortresses (1). **[3]**

**5.** 2 marks for each developed reason: Hideyoshi aimed to conquer Ming China, and Korea was the route; when Korea refused passage, he invaded (2); after unifying Japan in 1590 he had a large warrior class to employ and reward, and foreign conquest promised new lands (2); he sought personal glory and recognition as the dominant ruler in East Asia (2). **[4]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: Admiral Yi Sun-sin's naval victories (1); armoured "turtle ships" (1); volunteer "righteous armies" harassing Japanese forces (1); appeal to the Ming for help as a tributary state (1). **[2]**

**7.** Charles II died childless in 1700 and left his whole empire to Philip of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV (1). Other powers feared that France and Spain would be united under one family and dominate Europe and colonial trade (1). Louis's actions, including recognising the exiled Stuart claimant as king of England and moving troops into the Spanish Netherlands, convinced Britain and the Dutch to join Austria against him (1). **[3]**

**8.** 2 marks for each developed reason: Korean naval victories under Yi Sun-sin cut Japanese supply lines by sea, so armies inland ran short of food and reinforcements (2); Ming China intervened from 1593, recapturing Pyongyang and forcing Japanese forces back to the south (2); Korean righteous armies and difficult terrain made it hard to hold the countryside (2); Hideyoshi's death in 1598 ended the second invasion (2). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks: he trained a disciplined, partly conscripted army with smaller, flexible infantry units (1); he used lighter, mobile field artillery and aggressive cavalry charges (1); his victory at Breitenfeld (1631) saved the Protestant cause and carried Swedish armies deep into Germany (1); he secured French subsidies and allies, though his death at Lützen (1632) limited Sweden's later success (1). **[4]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example the Thirty Years' War (Europe) and the Imjin War (Asia and Oceania). Similarities (up to 2): both caused great devastation to the lands where they were fought (1); both strained the finances of intervening powers, the Habsburgs and the Ming (1). Differences (up to 4): Westphalia produced a lasting written settlement, while the Imjin War ended with Japanese withdrawal and no peace treaty at the time (1); the Thirty Years' War strengthened France and Sweden, while the Imjin War weakened the Toyotomi and opened the way for the Tokugawa (1); Westphalia confirmed the independence of the Dutch and Swiss and the rights of German princes, whereas Korea's borders were unchanged (1); the European war reshaped the religious settlement, which had no parallel in Korea (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: Gustavus Adolphus's tactics and artillery won Breitenfeld (1631) and transformed the Protestant position (2); Yi Sun-sin's turtle ships and cannon-armed fleet defeated the Japanese at sea (2). Against: the Imjin War turned on supply lines, Ming intervention and Hideyoshi's death more than on technology (2); in the War of the Spanish Succession, coalitions, finance and leadership, such as Marlborough and Eugene's at Blenheim, mattered more than any new weapon (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. Case for religion: Augsburg's exclusion of Calvinism; confessional alliances (1608, 1609); the Bohemian revolt against a Catholic ruler; the Edict of Restitution; Swedish claims to defend Protestantism. Case against: Habsburg dynastic ambition; the constitutional rights of princes against the emperor; Spanish-Dutch conflict; French intervention on the Protestant side from 1635 to weaken the Habsburgs; Swedish interest in the Baltic. A strong answer distinguishes causes of the outbreak from causes of the war's length and widening, and may argue that religion was central in 1618 but gave way to power politics. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, the War of the Spanish Succession.* Allied strengths: a broad coalition (Britain, the Dutch Republic, Austria); Marlborough and Eugene's victories (Blenheim 1704, Ramillies 1706); British naval power and credit. French strengths: Philip V's popularity in Castile; French resilience after Malplaquet (1709). Political change: the new British government's peace policy from 1710; the death of Emperor Joseph I (1711), which made his brother Charles heir to Austria and reduced allied support for his claim to Spain. Outcome: Philip V kept Spain but renounced France. A strong answer explains why the result was a compromise, ranking military and political factors. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the English Civil War a good example for this topic?**
Yes, as a civil war. Compare its causes or effects with a war from another region if a question requires two regions.

**Do I need the military revolution debate?**
It is not compulsory, but it strengthens answers on practices and effects if you apply it accurately to your examples.
