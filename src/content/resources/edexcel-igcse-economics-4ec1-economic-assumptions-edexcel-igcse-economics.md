This guide covers section 1.1.2, Economic assumptions, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. It is examined in Paper 1, Microeconomics and Business Economics, and there is a single tier of entry. Every firm, person and figure below is invented.

## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | The underlying assumptions that consumers aim to maximise their benefit and businesses aim to maximise their profit |
| b | Reasons why consumers may not maximise their benefit: they are not always good at calculating their benefits; they have habits that are hard to give up; they sometimes copy others' behaviour |
| c | Reasons why producers may not maximise their profit: managers may revenue maximise or sales maximise; they may prioritise caring for customers; they may complete charitable work |

There are exactly three reasons on each side. Learn them in the specification's words, because data-response questions often describe a situation and ask you to identify which one it shows.

## Why economists make assumptions (point a)

Economics builds simplified models of how people behave. A model needs a starting rule about what people are trying to do, so economists assume:

- **Consumers aim to maximise their benefit.** With limited income, they choose the goods and services that give them the most satisfaction for the money they spend.
- **Businesses aim to maximise their profit.** Profit is total revenue minus total costs, and firms choose the output and price that make this gap as large as possible.

These assumptions make predictions possible. If consumers maximise benefit, they buy more of a good when its price falls, because each unit of money now buys more benefit; that is why the demand curve slopes downwards. If firms maximise profit, they offer more for sale when the price rises, because each unit earns more; that is why the supply curve slopes upwards. Without the assumptions, the demand and supply model in section 1.1.3 would have no basis.

An assumption is not a claim that everyone always behaves this way. It is a simplification that is usually close enough to be useful. Section 1.1.2 asks you to know when it is not.

## Why consumers may not maximise their benefit (point b)

### 1. Consumers are not always good at calculating their benefits

Some choices involve comparisons that are hard to make quickly: special offers, different pack sizes, contracts that run for months, or costs that arrive later.

*Worked example (invented prices).* A phone company offers two plans for 12 months:

```
Plan X: 18 a month, no upfront charge   → 12 × 18 = 216
Plan Y: 12 a month + 80 upfront charge  → 80 + 12 × 12 = 224
```

A consumer who sees only the lower monthly price chooses Plan Y and pays 8 more over the year for the same service. They have not maximised their benefit, because they did not calculate the total cost correctly.

Other examples: buying a "3 for 2" offer on food that goes off before it is eaten, or paying for a gym membership that is rarely used.

### 2. Consumers have habits that are hard to give up

People often repeat the same purchase without re-checking whether it is still the best choice. A shopper keeps buying the same brand of cereal after a rival brand becomes cheaper and just as good; someone keeps a subscription they no longer use. Habits save time and effort, but the consumer may no longer be getting the most benefit for their money. Some habits, such as smoking, are especially hard to give up even when the person knows they would benefit from stopping.

### 3. Consumers sometimes copy others' behaviour

Consumers may buy what friends, celebrities or people online are buying, rather than what gives them the most benefit. A teenager buys an expensive pair of trainers because classmates have them, even though a cheaper pair would meet their needs. Copying can also lead to sudden rushes to buy a product, such as stockpiling a good because others are seen doing so.

**A point for evaluation:** copying is not always irrational. Following others can save the time and cost of researching every choice, and some goods give extra benefit because others use them, such as a messaging app that friends already use.

## Why producers may not maximise their profit (point c)

### 1. Managers may revenue maximise or sales maximise

In many companies, the owners (shareholders) are not the people running the business day to day. Managers may have their own aims: pay or bonuses linked to sales, the prestige of running a larger firm, or a bigger market share. So they may aim for:

- **revenue maximisation**: the output where total revenue is highest, or
- **sales maximisation**: the highest volume of sales, often as long as the firm does not make a loss.

*Worked example (invented figures).* A café chain's daily figures for one product:

| Output (units) | Price | Total revenue | Total cost | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 10.50 | 1 050 | 900 | 150 |
| 200 | 9.00 | 1 800 | 1 200 | 600 |
| 300 | 7.50 | 2 250 | 1 500 | 750 |
| 400 | 6.00 | 2 400 | 1 800 | 600 |
| 500 | 4.50 | 2 250 | 2 100 | 150 |
| 600 | 3.00 | 1 800 | 2 400 | −600 |

- **Profit maximising** output is **300** units, with profit of 750.
- **Revenue maximising** output is **400** units: revenue is highest at 2 400, but profit falls to 600, so 150 of profit is given up.
- **Sales maximising** without a loss is **500** units: profit is only 150, so 600 of profit is given up compared with the profit-maximising choice.

Each aim means a lower price and higher output than profit maximisation, which benefits consumers but not the owners.

### 2. Producers may prioritise caring for customers

A firm may choose actions that cost money and reduce profit, at least in the short run, because it wants to look after its customers: free delivery for elderly customers, keeping prices low during a crisis, a generous returns policy or extra staff to cut queues. These choices may build loyalty and reputation, so they can help profits in the long run, which is a strong evaluation point.

### 3. Producers may complete charitable work

Businesses sometimes give money, products or staff time to good causes: donating surplus food, sponsoring a local school, or paying staff to volunteer. This lowers profit directly. *Invented example:* a printing firm gives 20 hours of paid staff time a month to a charity at a wage cost of 15 an hour. Its monthly costs rise by 20 × 15 = 300, so profit falls by 300 unless the publicity brings in extra business.

## Evaluating the assumptions

- **When they hold well:** routine, simple purchases where prices are easy to compare; firms in strongly competitive markets, where failing to maximise profit can mean closure.
- **When they hold less well:** complex or one-off purchases; habit-forming goods; strong social pressure; large companies where managers are not the owners; firms with social aims.
- **Long run versus short run:** caring for customers and charitable work may reduce profit now but increase it later through loyalty and reputation, so a firm may still be aiming at long-run profit.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** *Explain one reason why a business might not aim to maximise profit.*

> The managers of a large company may aim to maximise sales instead ✔ (reason). Their bonuses may be linked to sales or market share, rather than profit ✔ (development 1). So they may cut prices and expand output beyond the profit-maximising level, giving up some profit to sell more ✔ (development 2).

*Commentary:* a reason from the specification, followed by two linked developments, matches the 4EC1 meaning of "explain": a statement of fact with two expansion points.

## Common mistakes

- Giving reasons outside the specification list (such as "the business wants to survive") when the question asks for the specification's reasons.
- Confusing revenue maximisation with profit maximisation: highest revenue is not highest profit.
- Saying consumers "do not want" the most benefit. They usually want it but fail to achieve it.
- Treating every deviation as permanent loss: customer care may raise long-run profit.
- Forgetting that profit = total revenue − total cost when reading a table.
