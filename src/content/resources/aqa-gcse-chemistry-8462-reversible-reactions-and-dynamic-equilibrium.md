## 4.6.2.1 Reversible reactions

In some reactions the **products can react to form the original reactants**. These are **reversible reactions**.
They are shown with the symbol **⇌** (two half arrows):

**A + B ⇌ C + D**

The **direction** can be changed by changing the **conditions**.

### Two examples to know

**Hydrated copper(II) sulfate** (blue crystals) loses water when heated to give anhydrous copper(II) sulfate
(white powder). Adding water to the white powder reverses the change, giving blue again.

CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O
blue ⇌ white + water

- **Heating** drives the forward reaction (water is driven off).
- **Adding water** drives the reverse reaction. The white powder turns blue and gets **hot**.

**Ammonium chloride** decomposes on heating into ammonia and hydrogen chloride gases. These recombine on
cooling at the cooler top of the tube to form ammonium chloride again.

NH₄Cl ⇌ NH₃ + HCl

Another reversible reaction is the **Haber process** for ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. The industrial conditions
for it are covered in the section on the Haber process.

## 4.6.2.2 Energy changes and reversible reactions

If a reversible reaction is **exothermic in one direction**, it is **endothermic in the opposite direction**.
The **same amount of energy** is transferred in each case.

For hydrated copper sulfate, heating the blue crystals is endothermic (energy is taken in to remove the water),
so adding water to the white powder is exothermic, and the same amount of energy is released.

| Direction | Energy | Temperature of surroundings |
|---|---|---|
| Forward (heating blue crystals) | Endothermic, +x kJ | Falls |
| Reverse (adding water to white powder) | Exothermic, −x kJ | Rises |

If the forward reaction in N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ releases 92 kJ per mole of equation, the reverse reaction takes in
92 kJ.

## 4.6.2.3 Equilibrium

When a reversible reaction takes place in **apparatus that prevents the escape of reactants and products**
(a **closed system**), **equilibrium** is reached when the **forward and reverse reactions occur at exactly
the same rate**.

What this means:

1. At the start, there are only reactants, so the **forward rate is fast** and the reverse rate is zero.
2. As products build up, the **reverse rate rises** and the forward rate falls as the reactants are used.
3. Eventually the **two rates are equal**. This is **dynamic equilibrium**.
4. Both reactions are **still happening**, but the **amounts of reactants and products stay constant**.

A good exam sentence: "At equilibrium, the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction,
so the concentrations of reactants and products do not change, even though both reactions are still
occurring."

Equilibrium is only reached in a **closed system**. If gas can escape, such as carbon dioxide leaving an open
flask, the reverse reaction cannot occur, and the reaction goes to completion instead.

An equilibrium may lie far to the **right** (mostly products) or far to the **left** (mostly reactants), and
the point depends on the conditions.

## 4.6.2.4 Le Chatelier's Principle (Higher tier only)

The amounts of all the reactants and products at equilibrium depend on the **conditions**. **Le Chatelier's
Principle** says that if a system at equilibrium is subject to a change, the system responds to
**counteract the change**. Use it to make qualitative predictions.

### 4.6.2.5 Concentration (Higher tier only)

If the concentration of a substance changes, the system is no longer at equilibrium. The concentrations then
change until a new equilibrium is reached.

- **Increase a reactant's concentration:** more products form. The position shifts to the **right**.
- **Decrease a product's concentration** (remove it): more reactants react to replace it. The position shifts
  to the **right**.
- Increase a product's concentration, or decrease a reactant's: the position shifts to the **left**.

This is why industry **removes products** as they form: it keeps the system shifting forward and increases the
yield.

### 4.6.2.6 Temperature (Higher tier only)

| Change | Endothermic forward reaction | Exothermic forward reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature **increased** | Relative amount of products **increases** | Relative amount of products **decreases** |
| Temperature **decreased** | Relative amount of products **decreases** | Relative amount of products **increases** |

The system moves in the direction that **opposes** the change. Increasing the temperature favours the
**endothermic** direction, which absorbs the extra heat. Decreasing the temperature favours the
**exothermic** direction, which releases heat.

### 4.6.2.7 Pressure (for gases) (Higher tier only)

- **Increased pressure:** the position shifts towards the side with the **smaller number of gas molecules**.
- **Decreased pressure:** the position shifts towards the side with the **larger number of gas molecules**.

Count the molecules in the **balanced symbol equation**. If both sides have the same number, pressure has no
effect on the position.

### Worked example 1: ammonia

*N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), forward reaction exothermic. Predict the effect on the amount of ammonia of (a)
increasing the pressure, (b) increasing the temperature.*

(a) Left side: 1 + 3 = **4** molecules. Right side: **2** molecules. Higher pressure shifts to the side with
fewer molecules, the right, so **more ammonia**.

(b) The forward reaction is exothermic. A higher temperature favours the endothermic (reverse) reaction, so
**less ammonia** at equilibrium.

### Worked example 2: nitrogen dioxide

*2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), where NO₂ is brown and N₂O₄ is colourless. A sealed syringe of the mixture is
compressed. Predict the change in colour.*

Left: 2 molecules. Right: 1 molecule. Higher pressure shifts to the right, towards the colourless N₂O₄, so the
brown colour **fades** after the initial darkening. (When compressed, the gas is briefly darker because the
same molecules are in a smaller volume.)

### Worked example 3: interpreting data

| Temperature / °C | 200 | 300 | 400 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Percentage of ammonia at equilibrium (same pressure) | 63 | 31 | 14 |

As the temperature increases, the yield of ammonia **falls**. This shows the forward reaction is
**exothermic**: the system opposes the increase in temperature by favouring the endothermic reverse reaction.

### Worked example 4: concentration

*In the equilibrium CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O, what happens if water vapour is removed?* The system shifts to the
right to replace the water, so more anhydrous copper sulfate forms.

## Exam technique

- For equilibrium definitions, quote **equal rates** and **constant concentrations**, and mention a **closed
  system**.
- For prediction questions, **say which way the position shifts** and then the effect on the product amount.
- For pressure, count gas molecules on both sides and say so.
- For temperature, find out whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic first.
- Never say the reaction "stops" at equilibrium.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not necessarily equal.
- Saying that at equilibrium nothing happens.
- Forgetting that equilibrium needs a closed system.
- Mixing up the effect of temperature: the exothermic direction is favoured by **lowering** the temperature.
- Using the number of moles of all substances when the question is about pressure, rather than only the gases.
- Believing a catalyst changes the position of equilibrium. It speeds up both directions equally and only helps
  equilibrium to be reached sooner.

## Putting it together: a model extended answer

*Sulfur trioxide is made by 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), exothermic in the forward direction. Explain the effect of
temperature and pressure on the yield, and why a compromise is used (Higher tier only).*

> A lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction, so the yield of sulfur trioxide is higher, but the
> rate would be too slow. A higher temperature is faster but lowers the yield, so a moderate temperature is used.
> There are 3 gas molecules on the left and 2 on the right, so a higher pressure shifts the position to the
> right and increases the yield and the rate. However, high pressure is costly and needs strong equipment, so a
> moderate pressure is used.
