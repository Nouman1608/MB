Section A has short-answer and calculation questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Every business and figure is invented. ## Section A

Questions 2 to 6 use the following data for Hearth Kitchens, a chain of restaurants, for one year: employees at the start of the year 240; employees at the end of the year 260; employees who left during the year 45; meals served 600,000; revenue $12,000,000; employee costs $3,360,000; profit $900,000.

**1.** Define the term labour turnover. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Hearth Kitchens' labour turnover. **[3]**

**3.** Calculate its labour productivity in meals per employee. **[2]**

**4.** Calculate its employee costs as a percentage of turnover. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate its labour cost per meal. **[2]**

**6.** Calculate:

**(a)** sales per employee **[2]**

**(b)** profit per employee. **[2]**

**7.** Hearth Kitchens recruited 60 kitchen staff last year. A year after joining, 39 were still employed. Calculate the percentage of recruits still employed one year after recruitment. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why labour productivity figures may not be a fair way to compare a bakery that hand-makes cakes with a factory producing sliced bread. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Carrow Hotels owns two hotels.

| | Harbour hotel | Castle hotel |
|---|---|---|
| Employees at start of year | 90 | 64 |
| Employees at end of year | 110 | 56 |
| Employees who left | 32 | 9 |

**(a)** Calculate labour turnover for each hotel. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **two** possible reasons why labour turnover is higher at the Harbour hotel. **[4]**

**10.** Whitlow Joinery employed an average of 40 staff last year, who produced 48,000 units with total labour costs of $960,000. This year it gave a 5% pay rise, so labour costs rose to $1,008,000. With the same number of staff, output rose by 10% to 52,800 units.

**(a)** Calculate labour cost per unit for each year. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain what your answers show about the pay rise. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **two** limitations of using sales per employee to compare the performance of different businesses. **[4]**

**12.** Redbrook Care runs residential homes for older people. Its labour turnover is 38%, and only 48% of new care workers are still employed a year after joining. Employee costs are 62% of its turnover. In exit interviews, the most common reasons for leaving were unpredictable shift patterns and lack of training. A rival care group nearby pays 6% more per hour. The managing director proposes a 6% pay rise for all care workers to reduce labour turnover. Evaluate whether a pay rise is the best way for Redbrook to reduce labour turnover. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of employees leaving the business in a year [1] as a percentage of the average number of employees [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Average employees = (240 + 260) ÷ 2 = 250 [1]. Labour turnover = 45 ÷ 250 × 100 [1] = **18%** [1]. **[3]**

**3.** 600,000 ÷ 250 [1] = **2,400 meals per employee** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 3,360,000 ÷ 12,000,000 × 100 [1] = **28%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 3,360,000 ÷ 600,000 [1] = **$5.60 per meal** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 12,000,000 ÷ 250 [1] = **$48,000** [1].

**(b)** 900,000 ÷ 250 [1] = **$3,600** [1]. **[4]**

**7.** 39 ÷ 60 × 100 [1] = **65%** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Hand-making cakes is labour-intensive, so each worker produces relatively few items [1], while the bread factory is capital-intensive, with machines doing most of the work [1]. Lower productivity at the bakery reflects its production method and product, not lower effort, so only similar businesses should be compared [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Harbour: average = (90 + 110) ÷ 2 = 100 [1]; turnover = 32 ÷ 100 × 100 = **32%** [1]. Castle: average = (64 + 56) ÷ 2 = 60 [1]; turnover = 9 ÷ 60 × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** The Harbour hotel is growing fast (from 90 to 110 staff), so many employees are new; if induction is rushed, new staff may leave in their first months [1], pushing up turnover [1]. The Harbour hotel may have poorer management or working conditions, such as long shifts in a busy location [1], so staff move to other hotels nearby that offer better conditions [1]. **[8]**

**10. (a)** Last year: 960,000 ÷ 48,000 [1] = **$20.00** [1]. This year: 1,008,000 ÷ 52,800 [1] = **$19.09** [1].

**(b)** Labour cost per unit fell even though pay rose [1], because productivity rose by 10%, more than the 5% pay rise, so the rise more than paid for itself if the extra output was caused by better motivation [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Sales per employee depends on the type of business: a retailer reselling expensive goods has high sales per employee even if staff do little processing [1], so it cannot fairly be compared with a labour-intensive service business [1]. It measures revenue, not profit, so a business with high sales per employee may still have high costs and low profit [1]; profit per employee or labour cost per unit should be checked as well [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a judgement, for example:

- 38% turnover means more than a third of staff leave each year, bringing heavy recruitment and training costs [1].
- Only 48% of new care workers stay a year, so the problem is concentrated among new staff [1].
- A rival pays 6% more, so a pay rise would remove one reason to move [1].
- In Herzberg's terms pay is a hygiene factor: raising it may reduce dissatisfaction [1] but may not motivate staff to stay if other problems remain [1].
- Exit interviews point to unpredictable shifts and lack of training, not pay, as the main reasons for leaving [1].
- Fixing shift patterns, for example by publishing rotas weeks in advance, could cost little [1].
- Better induction and training would tackle the early leaving shown by the 48% one-year figure [1].
- Employee costs are already 62% of turnover, so a 6% rise for all care workers would squeeze profit [1], unless lower turnover saves enough in recruitment and agency costs [1].
- A smaller, targeted rise linked to training and length of service could reward staff who stay [1].
- Judgement: a pay rise alone is not the best way; Redbrook should first tackle shift patterns and training, which the exit interviews identify, and consider a targeted pay increase if turnover remains high [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing leavers by the end headcount instead of the average.
- Giving productivity without units, such as "2,400" rather than "2,400 meals per employee".
- Rounding labour cost per unit to a whole number when cents matter.
- Comparing productivity across very different types of business without comment.
- Recommending a pay rise without using the evidence on why staff actually leave.
