This guide covers section 1.3, Classification of businesses, in Topic 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. It has one point, 1.3.1, on primary, secondary and tertiary activities, and the specification defines each one:

- **primary sector**: "extracting raw materials from the earth"
- **secondary sector**: "converting raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods"
- **tertiary sector**: "provision of a wide variety of services".

The content is short, but questions use it in three ways: classify a business or an activity, follow a product through the sectors, and explain why the sector affects a business's decisions. Both papers can use it, with a small business in Paper 1 and a large one in Paper 2. The first overview, alongside location decisions, is in the [classification of businesses and location decisions guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-classification-of-businesses-and-location-decisions/). Companion pages: the [classification of businesses revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-classification-of-businesses-revision-notes/) and the [classification of businesses practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-classification-of-businesses-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## 1. Classifying an activity

Ask one question: what does the business do to create value?

- Takes something from land or sea: **primary**. Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, drilling for oil.
- Turns materials into goods or builds something: **secondary**. Manufacturing, food processing, construction, refining, assembling.
- Provides a service to people or businesses: **tertiary**. Retail, transport, banking, insurance, tourism, education, health care, repairs.

Classify the **activity**, not the product. A shop that sells bread is tertiary; the bakery that makes it is secondary; the farm that grows the wheat is primary.

### Cases that cause mistakes

| Business | Sector | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fish farm | Primary | Raises and harvests fish; still extracting a natural resource |
| Construction company | Secondary | Builds, converting materials into a finished structure |
| Oil refinery | Secondary | Converts crude oil into fuels |
| Car repair garage | Tertiary | A service; nothing new is made |
| Restaurant | Secondary and tertiary | It prepares food (converting ingredients) and serves customers (service); the service usually dominates |
| Online clothing retailer | Tertiary | Sells and delivers; it does not make the clothes |
| Water company | Primary and tertiary | Extracts water and delivers a supply service |

When a business does more than one thing, name each activity and its sector, then say which is the main one, using revenue or what the case says it does most.

## 2. The chain of production

The sectors link. Goods usually pass from primary to secondary to tertiary before reaching the customer, and each stage depends on the others.

### Worked example 1: a wooden chair

1. **Primary**: a forestry business fells trees and sells logs.
2. **Secondary**: a sawmill cuts logs into planks (a semi-finished good); a furniture maker turns planks into chairs (finished goods).
3. **Tertiary**: a haulier transports the chairs; a furniture shop sells them; a bank lends the shop money to buy stock.

Tertiary businesses also serve every stage: the forestry firm needs insurance, the sawmill needs transport, the furniture maker needs advertising.

**Why the chain matters.** A problem at one stage spreads. If storms close the forests for a month, the sawmill runs short of logs, the furniture maker delays orders and the shop has fewer chairs to sell. In an "analyse" answer, follow the effect along the chain to the business in the question.

## 3. Why the sector affects decisions

The sector tells you what a business needs most, and so what its main risks and costs are.

| Sector | Needs most | Typical risks | Links to other topics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Land or access to a natural resource; equipment | Weather, disease, the resource running out, world price changes | Location is often fixed by the resource (1.4) |
| Secondary | Machinery, materials, factory space, skilled and semi-skilled labour | Rising material and energy costs, breakdowns, competition from cheaper producers | Economies of scale and production (section 5) |
| Tertiary | Customers, skilled people, convenient access or a good website | Changes in tastes, competition, reputation | Communication, recruitment and the marketing mix |

### Worked example 2: using the sector in an answer

Glenmoor Dairy keeps 300 cows and sells milk to a processor. A question asks why Glenmoor's profit may vary more than a supermarket's.

*Glenmoor is in the primary sector, so its output depends on natural conditions. A dry summer reduces grass, so the farm must buy more feed and its costs rise. It also sells a raw material, so it has little control over the price the processor pays. A supermarket, in the tertiary sector, sells many products, so a problem with one is less likely to hit its total profit.*

That answer classifies, applies the risk to the business and compares.

## 4. Large businesses in several sectors

Many Paper 2 businesses operate in more than one sector. An oil company may drill (primary), refine (secondary) and run fuel stations (tertiary). A chocolate maker may own cocoa farms (primary), factories (secondary) and shops (tertiary).

### Worked example 3: share of revenue by sector

Kestrel Energy has these revenues (in millions): drilling 420, refining 1,050, fuel stations 630.

- Total = 420 + 1,050 + 630 = 2,100.
- Primary: 420 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = **20%**.
- Secondary: 1,050 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = **50%**.
- Tertiary: 630 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = **30%**.

Kestrel depends most on refining, so a fall in demand for refined fuels, or a rise in refining costs, would hit it hardest. Operating in all three sectors gives it control over its own supplies and outlets.

## 5. Reading sector data

The specification's command words include "identify" (select the correct answer from a graph or table) and "complete the table". Sector data suits both.

### Worked example 4: employment in a region

| Sector | Year 1 (jobs) | Year 5 (jobs) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Primary | 24,000 | 18,000 |
| Secondary | 56,000 | 52,000 |
| Tertiary | 120,000 | 150,000 |
| Total | 200,000 | 220,000 |

- Identify the sector that grew: tertiary.
- Percentage change in primary jobs: (18,000 − 24,000) ÷ 24,000 × 100 = **−25%**.
- Tertiary share in Year 5: 150,000 ÷ 220,000 × 100 = **68.2%**.

A business can use such data: a training company might see the growing tertiary sector as a market, while a tractor dealer might see a shrinking customer base.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying by the product rather than the activity (a bread shop is not secondary).
- Calling construction tertiary; it is secondary.
- Forgetting that semi-finished goods, such as planks or steel sheet, are secondary output.
- Listing the sectors without applying them to the business in the case.
- Giving a sector share without showing the total it was calculated from.

## Exam technique

"Define" needs the specification's own meaning of each sector. "Outline" needs two linked points in the case: the sector, then what it means for this business. "Analyse" needs a chain, often along the chain of production. In calculations, show the total and the division, and give percentages to the stated decimal places.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a restaurant secondary or tertiary?**
It does both: preparing food converts ingredients, and serving customers is a service. Most answers treat it as mainly tertiary, but naming both activities earns credit.

**Can one business be in all three sectors?**
Yes. A large oil or food company may extract raw materials, process them and sell to customers.
