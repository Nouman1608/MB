This guide covers section 3.7 Environmental risks of economic development of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). Section 3.7 is the last part of Theme 3: Economic development. Theme content is assessed in Paper 1 Geographical Themes (1 hour 45 minutes, 75 marks), where you answer three 25-mark questions, one from each section. Paper 2 Geographical Skills can also set knowledge questions based on the three themes, so pollution or land-use data may appear there too.

## Economic activities as threats: local and global

Every part of Theme 3 can harm the environment. This section asks you to pull those links together.

| Economic activity | Example threats |
|---|---|
| Farming | Soil erosion, desertification, water pollution from fertiliser, methane from cattle and rice paddies |
| Industry | Air and water pollution, noise, carbon dioxide from burning fuel |
| Mining and quarrying | Visual pollution, dust, noise, polluted run-off, loss of habitat |
| Energy | Carbon dioxide from fossil fuels, oil spills, flooding of land behind dams |
| Tourism | Litter, sewage, noise, footpath erosion, traffic fumes |
| Transport | Exhaust fumes, aircraft and road noise, greenhouse gases |

**Local threats** affect the area near the activity: a river polluted by a factory, or dust from a quarry. **Global threats** affect the whole planet: greenhouse gases from millions of sources add to enhanced global warming. Some threats cross borders, such as acid rain or a polluted river flowing into another country.

The syllabus also says threats to **people**. Always say who is affected: farmers who lose soil, fishing families who lose catches, or residents who breathe polluted air.

## Soil erosion

**Soil erosion** is the removal of topsoil by water or wind faster than new soil can form. Topsoil holds most of the nutrients, so its loss lowers crop yields.

**Causes linked to economic activity**
- **Deforestation:** trees and roots no longer bind the soil or intercept rain.
- **Overgrazing:** too many animals remove the grass cover.
- **Ploughing up and down slopes:** furrows act as channels for running water.
- **Removing hedges** to make larger fields, which exposes soil to wind.

**Effects:** lower yields, gullies that cut up fields, silt that fills rivers and reservoirs and can increase flooding downstream.

**Reducing soil erosion:** terracing on steep slopes, contour ploughing (along the slope, not down it), planting shelter belts of trees, keeping crop cover on the land, and controlling the number of grazing animals.

## Desertification

**Desertification** is the degradation of land in dry (arid and semi-arid) areas, so that it becomes more like a desert. It is not deserts "moving"; it is good land being damaged.

**Human causes:** overgrazing, overcultivation (growing crops every year with no fallow period), cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, and poorly managed irrigation that leaves salt in the soil. These pressures grow when population rises.

**Natural causes:** long droughts and unreliable rainfall. Climate change may make droughts more frequent in some dry regions.

**Example: the Sahel.** The Sahel is a semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara in Africa. The **Great Green Wall** is an African-led response. The African Union endorsed it in 2007. It stretches roughly 8,000 km across the continent, brings together more than 20 countries, and aims to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030.

**Evaluation:** the scheme combines tree planting with better farming and new jobs, so it tackles causes as well as symptoms. But progress has been slower than planned, and seedlings planted in very dry land often die unless local people protect and water them. Success depends on local communities, not only on government targets.

## Enhanced global warming

The **natural greenhouse effect** keeps the Earth warm. Gases such as carbon dioxide and methane trap some of the heat given off by the Earth's surface. Without them the planet would be too cold for most life.

**Enhanced global warming** is the extra warming caused when human activities add more greenhouse gases to the atmosphere.

**Sources:** burning coal, oil and gas in power stations, factories and vehicles; deforestation (fewer trees take in carbon dioxide, and burning releases it); methane from cattle, rice paddies and landfill; flaring of gas at oil wells.

**Effects:** rising sea levels from melting ice and warmer oceans, threatening low-lying coasts; more intense heatwaves, droughts and storms; changing crop yields; loss of coral reefs and other habitats.

This is the clearest **global** threat. Emissions in one country affect people everywhere. Some of the people most at risk, such as farmers in dry regions or residents of low-lying islands, produce few emissions themselves. Under the Paris Agreement (2015), countries agreed to try to limit warming to well below 2 °C.

## Pollution: water, air, noise and visual

The syllabus names four types. Learn a source and an effect for each.

| Type | Sources from economic activity | Effects on environment and people |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | Factory waste, untreated sewage, fertiliser run-off, oil spills, mine waste | Fish die when oxygen falls; algae blooms (eutrophication); unsafe drinking water; lost fishing income |
| **Air** | Factory chimneys, vehicle exhausts, power stations, burning forests | Breathing illnesses; smog; acid rain damaging trees and lakes; greenhouse gases |
| **Noise** | Airports, motorways, factories, quarry blasting, construction | Stress and poor sleep; wildlife disturbed; lower house prices |
| **Visual** | Quarries, spoil heaps, power lines, large factories, wind turbines, litter | Spoils landscapes; can reduce tourism; lowers quality of life |

**Eutrophication** happens when nitrates and phosphates from fertiliser or sewage enter water. Algae grow quickly, then die. Bacteria break down the dead algae and use up the oxygen, so fish suffocate.

Visual and noise pollution are local. Air and water pollution can be local or travel long distances in winds and rivers.

## The need for sustainable development and management

**Sustainable development** was defined by the Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future* (1987), as development "that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".

Sustainable development balances three things:
1. **Economic:** jobs and incomes.
2. **Social:** health, fairness and quality of life.
3. **Environmental:** clean air, water and soil, and healthy ecosystems.

**Why it is needed:** many resources are finite; damaged land and water cost money to repair; pollution harms health; and climate change threatens future development everywhere.

**Sustainable management methods**
- **Laws and standards:** limits on emissions and waste; fines for polluters.
- **Environmental impact assessments** before new projects are approved.
- **Protected areas** such as national parks and marine reserves.
- **Cleaner technology:** filters on chimneys, treatment of factory waste water, renewable energy.
- **International agreements**, such as the Paris Agreement, for global threats.
- **Community involvement**, so local people benefit from protecting the land.

**Evaluation:** laws only work if they are enforced. Poorer countries may lack money for inspectors, and some firms may move to places with weaker rules. Global agreements depend on every country keeping its promises.

## The importance of resource conservation

**Resource conservation** means using natural resources carefully so that they last longer and are not wasted.

- **Non-renewable resources** (coal, oil, gas, metal ores) are finite. Once used, they are gone.
- **Renewable resources** (forests, fish, soil, fresh water) can recover, but only if they are used no faster than they renew. Overfishing or pumping groundwater too quickly turns a renewable resource into a disappearing one.

**Why conservation matters:** it keeps resources for future generations; reduces pollution and waste; cuts greenhouse gas emissions; and saves money.

**Methods:** the "three Rs" (reduce, reuse, recycle); energy efficiency, such as insulation and efficient engines; switching to renewable energy; fishing quotas; replanting after logging; and water metering.

## Case study: oil in the Niger Delta, Nigeria

The Niger Delta in southern Nigeria is one of the largest wetlands in Africa. It has mangrove forests, creeks and farmland, and millions of people depend on fishing and farming. Shell found oil at Oloibiri in 1956. Oil and gas have since provided most of Nigeria's export earnings and a large share of government revenue. This is economic development that has put the environment at risk.

**Threats to the environment and people**
- **Water and land pollution:** thousands of oil spills from pipelines, wells and tankers. Oil coats mangroves and creeks, kills fish and makes farmland infertile.
- **Air pollution and enhanced global warming:** gas that comes up with oil is often burned off (**gas flaring**). This releases carbon dioxide, soot and other pollutants. People living near flares report breathing problems.
- **Threats to people:** loss of fishing and farming income and polluted drinking water.

**Ogoniland and Bodo.** Ogoniland is an area of about 1,000 km² in the delta. In 2011 the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) published an assessment of Ogoniland based on more than 4,000 samples from over 200 sites. It found oil contamination in soil and groundwater at many sites. In one community, drinking water contained benzene, a cancer-causing chemical, at about 900 times the World Health Organization guideline. UNEP estimated that full recovery could take up to 30 years, and that the first five years would need about US$1 billion.

Two spills from Shell Nigeria's Trans-Niger pipeline, which began in 2008, badly polluted Bodo, a fishing community in Ogoniland. After a case in a London court, Shell agreed in 2015 to pay £55 million: £35 million to individuals and £20 million to the community.

**Causes of spills, and different views:** spills come from corroded pipelines, equipment failure, and also from sabotage, oil theft and illegal refining. Oil companies tend to stress theft and sabotage. Local communities and campaigners tend to stress poor maintenance and slow clean-up. The Nigerian government both earns oil revenue and regulates the industry. Critics say its laws on spills and flaring have been weakly enforced; the government points to its clean-up agency and its plans to cut flaring. Estimates of how much oil has been spilled vary widely.

**Management**
- The Nigerian government set up a clean-up agency, the Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project (HYPREP), to act on UNEP's report. It was first set up in the early 2010s; the Ogoniland clean-up was launched in 2016, and its work has included restoring mangroves.
- Compensation payments, such as the Bodo settlement.
- Efforts to reduce gas flaring by capturing gas for use instead of burning it.

**Evaluation:** clean-up has been slow and new spills continue, so the area shows how hard it is to manage damage after it happens. The case supports the need for sustainable management from the start: maintained pipelines, strict laws that are enforced, and local people sharing in the benefits. It also shows a global link: flaring adds to enhanced global warming far beyond Nigeria.

## Worked example: using the case study

*For a named area, explain how economic development has put the environment and people at risk. (7 marks)*

> In the Niger Delta, Nigeria, oil extraction since the oil discovery at Oloibiri in 1956 has caused many oil spills (knowledge). At Bodo in Ogoniland, two pipeline spills that began in 2008 polluted creeks and mangroves, so fish died and families who depended on fishing lost their income (application). UNEP's 2011 assessment found oil in soil and groundwater, and drinking water with benzene about 900 times the WHO guideline, which shows that pollution threatens health as well as jobs (analysis). Gas flaring adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, so the risk is global as well as local. The greatest risk is long-term, because UNEP estimated recovery could take up to 30 years (evaluation).

The answer names a place, uses real details, links each threat to people, and covers local and global scales.

## Worked example: a sustainability judgement

*Explain why sustainable management is needed in areas of rapid economic development. (4 marks)*

> Rapid development often uses resources faster than they can recover, such as overgrazing in dry areas, which can lead to desertification (point and example). Repairing damage later is slow and costly; UNEP estimated about US$1 billion for the first five years of clean-up in Ogoniland (development). Sustainable management, such as enforced pollution laws, keeps jobs while protecting land and water, so future generations can still use them (link to the definition).

## Common errors

- Writing "global warming" instead of **enhanced** global warming, and not saying it is caused by extra human emissions.
- Confusing global warming with the ozone hole. They are different problems.
- Describing desertification as deserts spreading on their own, with no human cause.
- Naming a type of pollution without a source or an effect on people.
- Forgetting noise and visual pollution, which the syllabus names.
- Defining sustainable development only as "protecting the environment", leaving out future generations and the economy.
- Treating renewable resources as unlimited. Fish, soil and water can be used up.
- Giving a case study with no place names, dates or figures.
