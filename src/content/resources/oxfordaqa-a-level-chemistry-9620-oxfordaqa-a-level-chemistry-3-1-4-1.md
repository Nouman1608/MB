This guide covers **3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. The specification states that reactions can be **endothermic or exothermic**, that **enthalpy change (ΔH) is the heat energy change measured under conditions of constant pressure**, and that **standard enthalpy changes refer to standard conditions, ie 100 kPa and a stated temperature** (for example ΔH°₂₉₈). You must be able to define the **standard enthalpy of combustion (ΔcH°)** and the **standard enthalpy of formation (ΔfH°)**.

The [energetics overview](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-energetics/) introduces the whole of section 3.1.4. This page concentrates on the language of enthalpy: signs, conditions, standard states, the two definitions and the equations that go with them. These ideas underpin [calorimetry](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-2/), [Hess's law](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-3/) and [bond enthalpies](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-4/). Afterwards use the [enthalpy change revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-1-revision-notes/) and the [enthalpy change practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-1-practice/).

## Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Think of the reacting chemicals as the **system** and everything else (the solvent, the container, the air) as the **surroundings**.

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Heat energy | Transferred from the system to the surroundings | Transferred from the surroundings to the system |
| Sign of ΔH | Negative | Positive |
| Temperature of surroundings | Rises | Falls |
| Products compared with reactants | Lower enthalpy | Higher enthalpy |
| Examples | Combustion of fuels, neutralisation | Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate, dissolving ammonium nitrate |

On an **enthalpy level diagram**, enthalpy is on the vertical axis. For an exothermic reaction the products' line is drawn **below** the reactants' line, with a downward arrow labelled ΔH; for an endothermic reaction the products are **above**.

## What ΔH means

Enthalpy, H, is the heat content of a system. It cannot be measured directly, but the **change**, ΔH, can. The specification defines ΔH as the heat energy change measured **at constant pressure**, which is what happens in an open beaker or a calorimeter open to the air.

ΔH is quoted in **kJ mol⁻¹**. "Per mole" refers to the equation as written: the amounts in moles shown by the balancing numbers. So the value always belongs to a specific equation:

```
CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)          ΔH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹
2CH₄(g) + 4O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)        ΔH = −1780 kJ mol⁻¹
CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) → CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g)          ΔH = +890 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Doubling the equation doubles ΔH; reversing it changes the sign.

## Standard conditions and standard states

The amount of heat transferred depends on the conditions, so values are compared under **standard conditions**:

- a pressure of **100 kPa**;
- a **stated temperature**, usually 298 K, shown as a subscript: ΔH°₂₉₈.

The **standard state** of a substance is its normal physical state under standard conditions. At 298 K and 100 kPa:

| Substance | Standard state |
|---|---|
| Oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, chlorine | Gas: O₂(g), H₂(g), N₂(g), Cl₂(g) |
| Bromine | Liquid: Br₂(l) |
| Iodine | Solid: I₂(s) |
| Carbon | Solid graphite: C(s) |
| Water | Liquid: H₂O(l) |
| Sodium, magnesium, sulfur | Solid |

State symbols are part of every standard enthalpy equation. Water as H₂O(g) in a combustion equation is not in its standard state.

## The two definitions

**Standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH°**: the enthalpy change when **one mole** of a substance is **completely burned in oxygen**, under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard states.

**Standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH°**: the enthalpy change when **one mole** of a compound is formed **from its elements**, under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard states.

Two consequences follow:

- The enthalpy of formation of an **element in its standard state is zero**, because no change takes place.
- Some reactions fit both definitions. C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) is the combustion of carbon and the formation of carbon dioxide, so ΔcH°(C) = ΔfH°(CO₂) = −394 kJ mol⁻¹. In the same way ΔcH°(H₂) = ΔfH°(H₂O(l)) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Worked example 1: writing formation equations

Write equations for the standard enthalpy of formation of ethanol, sodium chloride, ammonia and calcium carbonate.

```
2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)
Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s)
½N₂(g) + 1½H₂(g) → NH₃(g)
Ca(s) + C(s) + 1½O₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)
```

The product has a balancing number of **one**, so fractions are needed for diatomic elements. Each element appears in its standard state.

## Worked example 2: which equation fits which definition?

| Equation | ΔcH°? | ΔfH°? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) | No | No | Two moles burned and two formed; ΔH = 2 × ΔfH°(H₂O) |
| CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) | Yes, of CO | No | CO is a compound, not an element |
| C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) | Yes, of C | Yes, of CO₂ | One mole burned and one mole formed from elements |
| C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g) | No | No | Water is not in its standard state |

## Worked example 3: using ΔH with amounts

ΔcH° for methane is −890 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the heat released when 4.00 g of methane is completely burned.

```
n(CH₄) = 4.00 ÷ 16.0 = 0.250 mol
heat released = 0.250 × 890 = 223 kJ
```

The answer is a quantity of heat, so it is given as **223 kJ released**; the negative sign belongs to ΔH, not to the heat.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out "one mole", "completely" or "in oxygen" from the combustion definition.
- Writing "from its elements" without "in their standard states", or forgetting standard conditions.
- Giving standard pressure as 1 atm or 101 kPa: this specification uses **100 kPa**.
- Writing formation equations that produce two moles to avoid fractions.
- Missing or wrong state symbols, especially H₂O(g) instead of H₂O(l).
- Giving ΔH a positive sign for an exothermic reaction because "heat is given out".

## Exam technique

- **Define**: include every element: one mole, the process (burned completely in oxygen, or formed from its elements), standard conditions and standard states.
- **Write an equation**: one mole of the substance in question, correct formulae, state symbols, fractions where needed.
- **State** whether exothermic or endothermic: give the sign of ΔH and what happens to the temperature of the surroundings if asked.
- **Draw** an enthalpy level diagram: label the axis, both levels with formulae, and the ΔH arrow pointing from reactants to products.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are standard conditions in OxfordAQA 9620 Chemistry?**
Standard conditions are a pressure of 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K. Standard enthalpy changes also need every substance in its standard state.

**Why is the standard enthalpy of formation of oxygen zero?**
Oxygen gas is already an element in its standard state, so forming it from its elements involves no change and no enthalpy change.

**Is the enthalpy of combustion of carbon the same as the enthalpy of formation of carbon dioxide?**
Yes. The equation C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) burns one mole of carbon completely and forms one mole of carbon dioxide from its elements, so both values are −394 kJ mol⁻¹.
