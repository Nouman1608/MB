These notes condense **section 12.1 Digital currencies** (Topic 12 IT in society) of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. A Level content, examined in Paper 3 Advanced Theory. Full explanations are in the [digital currencies study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-digital-currencies/); test yourself with the [digital currencies practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-digital-currencies-practice/).

## The types in one table

| Type | What it is | Issued or controlled by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital/electronic currency | A national currency held and moved electronically | Banks, within the national currency | Card payments, bank transfers |
| Virtual currency | Money used inside one online community | The company running the platform | Gold or gems in a game |
| Cryptocurrency | Digital money secured by cryptography, usually on a blockchain | No central owner | Bitcoin, Litecoin |
| Central bank digital base money | Digital form of legal tender | The central bank | A government-issued digital currency |
| Stored value card | Prepaid card with a fixed value | The card issuer | Gift card, travel card, prepaid card |

## Centralised and decentralised

| | Centralised | Decentralised |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Bank or card company | Network of users and software rules |
| Examples | Debit cards, credit cards, EPOS | Bitcoin, Litecoin, peer-to-peer systems, mobile electronic wallets |
| Approval | The organisation approves each payment | The network checks the transaction |
| Reversal | Possible (refunds, chargebacks) | Normally impossible |
| Privacy | The provider sees all spending | Often pseudonymous, but transactions are public on the ledger |

**EPOS:** terminal reads the card, sends details to the card company, receives approval, records the sale.

**Mobile wallet:** app stores payment details or keys, so a phone pays by contactless tap or code.

## Blockchain: recall

- **Ledger:** record of all transactions. A **distributed ledger** is copied on many computers.
- **Block:** a group of validated transactions, with a time stamp, its own **hash** and the **hash of the previous block**.
- **Hash:** fixed-length code worked out from the contents. A small change gives a very different hash.
- **Chain:** each block holds the hash of the one before, so changing an old block breaks every later block.
- **Consensus:** the network agrees which block is added next.
- **Miners or verifying nodes:** computers that check transactions and add blocks, usually rewarded with new coins or fees.
- **Digital signature:** a payment is signed with a private key and checked with the public key.

**Why it is trusted:** copies are held by many nodes, so an altered copy is spotted and rejected.

**Why it is not perfect:** wallets, exchanges and passwords can still be attacked; lost private keys mean lost coins; mining can use large amounts of electricity.

## Impact: recall

| Group | Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Individuals | Quick payments; access without a bank account; control of own money | Volatile prices; scams and theft; lost keys; no refunds |
| Businesses | Lower fees; international sales; new customers | Price swings; irreversible payments; unclear rules; set-up costs |
| Governments | Tracking, tax collection and faster benefits with digital money; lower cash costs | Reduced control of money supply; crime, laundering and tax evasion; hard to regulate |
| Global economy | Cheap international payments; inclusion | Price crashes spreading losses; energy use; instability |

## Advantages and disadvantages by type

| Type | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Cards | Widely accepted; fraud protection | Fees; provider sees all spending |
| Stored value cards | No bank account; helps budgeting | Lost card means lost value; limited use |
| Central bank digital money | Stable; government backed | Privacy concerns |
| Cryptocurrency | Fast; international; no central control | Volatile; irreversible; energy use; crime |
| Virtual currency | Convenient inside the platform | Limited use; company can change the rules |
| Mobile wallet | Quick; no cards to carry | Depends on phone and battery; theft if the phone is unprotected |

## Quick example: volatility

0.1 of a coin bought at £30,000 costs £3,000. If the price drops by 40% to £18,000, it is worth 0.1 × 18,000 = £1,800. The loss is **£1,200**. A bank account in pounds does not change in value in this way.

## Exam traps

- Treating "digital currency" as only meaning cryptocurrency.
- Saying blockchain cannot be hacked.
- Forgetting that crypto payments cannot be reversed.
- Writing about "the economy" in general without naming individuals, businesses, governments or the global economy.
- Giving an advantage that applies to every type, such as "it is convenient".
- Explaining how a block is added but not how the hashes link the blocks.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish virtual currency from cryptocurrency.
2. What is central bank digital base money?
3. Give two examples of centralised payment systems.
4. Give two examples of decentralised systems.
5. What is a distributed ledger?
6. What does each block in a blockchain contain, apart from transactions?
7. Why is it hard to change an old block?
8. Give one reason why decentralised transactions are risky for a buyer.
9. State one benefit of digital currencies for a business.
10. State one risk of cryptocurrencies for a government.
11. State one environmental concern about cryptocurrencies.
12. A coin bought at £500 falls by 20%. What is its new value?

### Answers

1. Virtual currency is controlled by a company and used within its platform; cryptocurrency is secured by cryptography and is usually decentralised on a blockchain.
2. A digital form of a country's legal tender, issued by the central bank.
3. Any two: debit card, credit card, electronic point of sale.
4. Any two: Bitcoin, Litecoin, peer-to-peer system, mobile electronic wallet.
5. A record of transactions copied on many computers in a network, with no single owner.
6. Its own hash and the hash of the previous block (and usually a time stamp).
7. Changing a block changes its hash, which breaks the link in the next block and every later block, and the other copies would still hold the original.
8. The payment cannot be reversed, and there is no bank to refund it.
9. Any one: lower fees, fast international payments, new customers.
10. Any one: money laundering and tax evasion, loss of control of money supply, difficulty regulating.
11. Mining uses large amounts of electricity.
12. 500 × 0.8 = £400.
