These notes condense section 12.3, Chromatography, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). All four outcomes are required for every candidate. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [chromatography study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-chromatography/); then try the [chromatography practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-chromatography-practice/).

## The four outcomes in one line each

1. **Describe** paper chromatography for separating mixtures of soluble substances with a suitable solvent.
2. **Describe** the use of a locating agent for colourless substances (no named agent needed).
3. **Interpret** chromatograms: identify unknowns by comparison with knowns; tell pure from impure.
4. **State and use** Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent.

## Method checklist

- Pencil baseline, about 1–2 cm from the bottom.
- Small, concentrated spots; samples spaced out along the line.
- Solvent depth **below** the baseline.
- Lid on the beaker.
- Remove before the solvent reaches the top; mark the solvent front straight away.
- Dry; apply a locating agent if the substances are colourless.

## Why each step matters

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Pencil, not pen | graphite does not dissolve; ink would run |
| Baseline above solvent | otherwise samples dissolve into the solvent pool |
| Small spots | sharp, separate spots that do not merge |
| Lid | solvent does not evaporate from the paper |
| Mark solvent front quickly | it disappears as the paper dries |
| Locating agent | turns invisible spots into coloured ones |

## How separation happens

The solvent moves up the paper and carries dissolved substances. A substance that is more soluble in the solvent and less strongly held by the paper travels further. Different substances travel different distances, so they separate. A substance insoluble in the chosen solvent stays on the baseline: choose a different solvent.

## Reading a chromatogram

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Sample gives one spot | pure (in that solvent) |
| Sample gives two or more spots | impure / a mixture |
| Spot level with a known substance on the same paper | sample contains that substance |
| Spot matches no known substance | sample contains a substance not among the references |
| Spot remains on baseline | that substance is insoluble in the solvent |

## Rf values

```
Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent
     (both measured from the baseline)
```

- No units. Always between 0 and 1.
- Measure to the **centre** of the spot.
- Same substance + same solvent → same Rf.
- Rearranged: distance of substance = Rf × distance of solvent.

Quick check: solvent front 9.0 cm, spot 3.6 cm → Rf = 0.40.

## Quick interpretation drill

A sample, U, and three references are run in the same solvent. The solvent front is 5.0 cm from the baseline.

| Sample | Spots (cm from baseline) | Rf |
|---|---|---|
| U | 1.5 and 4.0 | 0.30 and 0.80 |
| V | 1.5 | 0.30 |
| W | 2.5 | 0.50 |
| X | 4.0 | 0.80 |

U is impure (two spots) and contains V and X but not W. Work across the row: count spots, match heights, then calculate Rf if asked.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chromatography | a method of separating dissolved substances by how far a solvent carries them over paper |
| Chromatogram | the dried paper showing the separated spots |
| Baseline | the pencil line on which samples are spotted |
| Solvent front | the furthest point reached by the solvent |
| Locating agent | a substance applied to a chromatogram to make colourless spots visible |
| Rf value | distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent |
| Suitable solvent | one in which the substances being separated dissolve |

## Exam traps

- Ink baseline: always wrong.
- Solvent above the baseline: always wrong.
- Rf is not "solvent ÷ substance". If you get more than 1, you have it upside down.
- Distances are from the **baseline**, not the bottom of the paper.
- Compare unknown and known **on the same paper**; colours alone do not identify a substance.
- You do not need to name a locating agent; you do need to say it makes colourless spots visible.
- One spot means pure only if every component moved; check the baseline for an undissolved spot.

## Self-test

1. Why is the baseline drawn in pencil?
2. Why must the solvent level be below the baseline?
3. What is a locating agent used for?
4. State the equation for Rf.
5. A spot travels 4.2 cm and the solvent front 7.0 cm. Calculate the Rf value.
6. Why can an Rf value never be greater than 1?
7. A sample gives three spots. What does this show?
8. An unknown gives a spot at the same height as known substance P on the same chromatogram. What can you conclude?
9. A spot stays on the baseline. Suggest why, and what the student should change.
10. Dye Z has Rf 0.60. The solvent front travels 5.5 cm. How far does Z travel?
11. Why is a lid placed on the beaker?
12. Why must the solvent front be marked immediately?

## Answers

1. Pencil (graphite) does not dissolve in the solvent, so it does not move or add spots; ink would.
2. Otherwise the samples dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of being carried up the paper.
3. To make the spots of colourless substances visible, so they can be seen and measured.
4. Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent.
5. 4.2 ÷ 7.0 = 0.60.
6. A substance cannot travel further than the solvent that carries it.
7. The sample is impure: it is a mixture of at least three substances.
8. The unknown contains substance P.
9. The substance is insoluble in that solvent; use a different solvent in which it dissolves.
10. 0.60 × 5.5 = 3.3 cm.
11. To reduce evaporation of the solvent from the paper.
12. The solvent evaporates as the paper dries and the front can no longer be seen; it is needed to calculate Rf.
