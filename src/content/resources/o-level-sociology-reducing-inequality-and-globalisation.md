Inequality is not inevitable: governments and organisations try to reduce it, with mixed results,
and globalisation changes how unequal societies are compared with one another. Key questions 3.2
and 3.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) ask you to explain legislation, welfare states and the
work of non-governmental organisations (NGOs), to assess sociological views of their success, and to
explain how migration, ecological problems and exploitation affect inequalities between societies.

Key question 3.1 is covered in the
[O Level social stratification and inequality guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-sociology-social-stratification-and-inequality/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251 (2025–2027):

- **3.2 What attempts have been made to reduce social inequalities?** — 3.2.1 legislation, e.g.
  progressive taxation, redistribution of wealth through benefits, a minimum wage, equal
  opportunities laws, other government measures; 3.2.2 welfare states and life chances (free and/or
  universal education, unemployment benefits, pensions, low-cost housing, free healthcare, universal
  basic income); 3.2.3 NGOs (reducing poverty, providing medical assistance, promoting business,
  improving education); 3.2.4 sociological views of success (New Right criticisms of the welfare
  state/underclass, the culture of poverty and dependency culture, Marxist criticisms of the welfare
  state, criticisms of NGOs, feminist criticisms of equality legislation).
- **3.3 How has globalisation affected inequalities between societies?** — 3.3.1 migration
  (immigration/emigration, push and pull factors, cultural diversity, economic consequences,
  negative perceptions of migrants); 3.3.2 global ecological issues (transnational companies,
  climate change, pollution, urbanisation); 3.3.3 explanations (Marxist views of capitalist
  exploitation, colonialism, feminist ideas of patriarchy).

## How do governments use laws to reduce inequality?

- **Progressive taxation** takes a larger share of higher incomes, funding services for everyone.
- **Redistribution through benefits** transfers money to poorer households. Pakistan's Benazir
  Income Support Programme, launched in 2008, makes cash payments to low-income families, paid to
  women in the household.
- **A minimum wage** sets a legal floor under pay.
- **Equal opportunities laws** outlaw discrimination; Britain's Equal Pay Act (1970) required equal
  pay for equal work by men and women.
- **Other measures** include quotas for disadvantaged groups in education or government jobs and
  subsidies on basic foods.

## What does a welfare state provide?

| Provision | How it improves life chances | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Free/universal education | Opens routes to better jobs and social mobility | Sri Lanka introduced free education from kindergarten to university in the 1940s |
| Unemployment benefits | Prevents destitution between jobs | Common in Europe; limited in many developing countries |
| Pensions | Income in old age | State and employer pension schemes |
| Low-cost housing | Secure, affordable homes | Public housing programmes |
| Free healthcare | Treatment regardless of income | Britain's NHS (1948) |
| Universal basic income | Unconditional income for all | Debated and trialled in several countries |

## What do NGOs do?

- **Reducing poverty:** the Edhi Foundation, founded by Abdul Sattar Edhi in Pakistan in 1951, runs
  ambulances, shelters and orphanages for the poor.
- **Providing medical assistance:** international medical charities run clinics and vaccination
  campaigns in crisis areas.
- **Promoting business:** Akhuwat, founded in Pakistan in 2001, gives interest-free small loans so
  poor families can start businesses.
- **Improving education:** NGOs run low-cost schools, teacher training and literacy programmes,
  especially for girls.

## Have these attempts worked? Sociological views

- **New Right:** welfare is too generous and creates an **underclass** that depends on benefits and
  lacks work discipline (Charles Murray, 1984 and 1990). They favour lower taxes and less state
  help.
- **Culture of poverty / dependency culture:** Oscar Lewis argued the poor develop fatalistic values
  passed to their children; the related idea of **dependency culture** claims benefits discourage
  self-reliance. Critics say poverty is caused by lack of opportunity, not values.
- **Marxist:** the welfare state is a "safety valve" that prevents unrest and keeps workers fit for
  work while leaving the ownership of wealth unchanged.
- **Criticisms of NGOs:** they may depend on foreign donors, follow donors' priorities, lack
  accountability to local people, duplicate state services, and cannot tackle structural causes.
- **Feminist:** equality laws exist, but women still earn less, do most unpaid work and are
  under-represented in power; laws do not change patriarchal attitudes.

## How does migration affect inequality between societies?

- **Push factors:** unemployment, low wages, conflict, natural disasters. **Pull factors:** jobs,
  higher wages, education, family already abroad.
- A major example is labour migration from South Asia to the Gulf states for construction and
  service work.
- **Economic consequences:** sending countries receive **remittances**, which support families and
  national income, but may lose skilled workers; receiving countries gain labour.
- **Cultural diversity** grows in receiving societies.
- **Negative perceptions** of migrants include stereotyping and blame for unemployment or crime;
  migrant workers may face poor conditions and limited rights.

## How do global ecological issues affect inequality?

- **Transnational companies** may move factories to countries with lower wages and weaker safety
  rules; the collapse of the Rana Plaza garment building in Bangladesh in 2013 killed more than
  1,100 workers.
- **Climate change:** poorer countries suffer most despite producing fewer emissions; the 2022
  floods in Pakistan affected millions of people.
- **Pollution:** industrial waste and air pollution are often worst in poorer communities.
- **Urbanisation:** rapid migration to cities such as Karachi and Dhaka has created large informal
  settlements with poor sanitation.

## Why are some societies richer than others?

- **Marxist views:** global capitalism allows rich countries and companies to profit from cheap labour
  and raw materials in poorer ones.
- **Colonialism:** colonial rule reshaped economies to export raw materials and drained wealth; its
  effects continue in trade patterns and institutions.
- **Feminist ideas of patriarchy:** women's unequal access to land, education and paid work holds
  back whole societies, and globalisation often relies on women's low-paid labour.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do New Right and Marxist views both criticise welfare?** The New Right says it does too much
and encourages dependency; Marxists say it does too little and protects capitalism.

**Is migration good or bad for sending countries?** Both: remittances raise incomes, while emigration
of skilled workers can weaken services. Answers should weigh the two.

**Which local examples can I use?** Any you can explain accurately — national cash-transfer schemes,
local NGOs or recent climate disasters are all relevant.
