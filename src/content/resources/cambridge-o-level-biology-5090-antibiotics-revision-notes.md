These notes condense subtopic **12.2 Antibiotics** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series: the meaning of a drug, the use of antibiotics to treat bacterial infections, why antibiotics do not affect viruses, and how the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria such as MRSA can be minimised. The full explanations and worked examples are in the [12.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-antibiotics/). Then try the [12.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-antibiotics-practice/).

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning in exam wording |
|---|---|
| Drug | Any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body |
| Antibiotic | A drug that kills bacteria; used to treat bacterial infections |
| Resistant | Not killed by an antibiotic that normally kills the bacteria |
| MRSA | A strain of bacteria resistant to many antibiotics, a problem in hospitals |

## Antibiotics

- Used to treat **bacterial** infections.
- Kill bacteria (or stop their growth).
- **Do not affect viruses**: viruses are not cells and replicate inside host cells.
- Do not work for colds, influenza or HIV.

| | Bacteria | Viruses |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Living cell | Protein coat and genetic material |
| Antibiotics work? | Yes | No |

## How resistance develops

1. A **mutation** occurs by chance, making a bacterium resistant.
2. The antibiotic **kills the non-resistant** bacteria.
3. The resistant bacterium **survives and reproduces**, with little competition.
4. Its offspring inherit the resistance gene.
5. The resistant population grows, and the antibiotic no longer works.

Data example: 10 resistant bacteria dividing every 20 minutes for 4 hours (12 divisions) become 10 x 2 to the power 12 = 10 x 4096 = 40 960.

## Minimising resistance

| Action | Reason |
|---|---|
| Use antibiotics only when essential | Fewer chances for resistant bacteria to be selected |
| Never use them for viral infections | No effect on viruses; only promotes resistance |
| Complete the prescribed course | Kills all the bacteria, so survivors do not multiply |
| Limit use in farming | Reduces exposure of bacteria to antibiotics |
| Hospital hygiene | Stops MRSA spreading |

## Inhibition zone practical

- Spread bacteria on agar; place discs soaked in antibiotics.
- After incubation a **clear zone** forms where bacteria cannot grow.
- A larger zone means a more effective antibiotic; no zone means resistance.
- Area of zone = pi x radius squared. Diameter 24 mm gives 3.14 x 12 x 12 = 452 mm squared; diameter 12 mm gives 113 mm squared, which is four times smaller.
- Safety: sterile technique; plate taped, not sealed; incubate at no more than about 25 to 30 degrees Celsius.

## Links to other topics

- Bacteria are prokaryotes (topic 2); viruses are not cells.
- Resistance comes from **mutation** followed by **selection**.
- Vaccination (12.3) prevents disease, so fewer antibiotics are needed.
- Cholera and tuberculosis are bacterial; HIV and influenza are viral; malaria is caused by a protoctist.

## Why resistance matters

- Infections become harder and costlier to treat.
- Routine operations carry more risk.
- New antibiotics take years to develop, and bacteria can become resistant to them too.

## Plan for a "discuss" answer

1. Say what antibiotics do and what they do not do.
2. Explain how resistance develops (mutation, selection, reproduction).
3. Give two actions to slow it.
4. State that these actions slow but do not stop the process.

## Short model answers

**Define a drug.** Any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.

**Explain why antibiotics do not cure influenza.** Influenza is caused by a virus. Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not affect viruses.

**Explain how antibiotic-resistant bacteria develop.** A mutation makes some bacteria resistant. When the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed but the resistant ones survive and reproduce, passing on the resistance to their offspring.

**Explain why antibiotics should only be used when essential.** The more they are used, the more chances resistant bacteria have to survive and multiply, so antibiotics become less useful.

## Exam traps

- Antibiotics kill **bacteria**, not viruses.
- **Bacteria** become resistant, not people.
- Resistance results from **chance mutation and selection**, not from "getting used to" the antibiotic.
- MRSA is a **bacterium**.
- A drug is any substance that modifies chemical reactions, not only a medicine.
- The key syllabus point is **using antibiotics only when essential**; completing a prescribed course is also good practice.

### Answers

1. Any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
2. Bacterial infections.
3. Viruses are not cells and replicate inside host cells, so antibiotics do not affect them.
4. MRSA is a bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics.
5. A chance mutation.
6. The mutation protects them, so the antibiotic does not kill them.
7. They survive, reproduce and pass on the resistance to offspring.
8. Any two: use antibiotics only when essential; do not use them for viral infections; complete the course; limit use in farming; good hospital hygiene.
9. A cold is caused by a virus, so antibiotics do not work, and the use encourages resistance.
10. The antibiotic is effective against the bacteria.
11. 3 hours = 6 divisions; 2 to the power 6 = 64 bacteria.
12. The bacteria are resistant to that antibiotic (or the antibiotic is not effective).
