This guide covers **sub-topic 1.4 Population Density and Distribution** of Cambridge O Level
Geography (2217), 2026 (Version 3). The
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/)
places it within Theme 1; this page concentrates on the factors, the map-reading skills and the
case study this sub-topic needs.

## What 1.4 asks you to do

You need to be able to:

- describe the **density** and **distribution** of population, at **global** scale and within a
  **country or region**;
- explain the **physical** and **human** factors that make some areas densely populated and others
  sparsely populated;
- use a named case study of a country or region with both high-density and low-density areas.

This sub-topic is also a common setting for data and map questions, so you should be comfortable
describing a pattern from a map or table before you explain it.

## Key idea 1: density and distribution are not the same

- **Population density** is a number: the average number of people per square kilometre.
- **Population distribution** is a pattern: how people are spread across an area, where they are
  clustered and where they are scarce.

```
population density = total population / land area (km²)
```

A country's average density hides huge variation. Two countries with the same average can have
very different distributions: one with people spread fairly evenly, another with almost everyone
squeezed into a river valley. That is why exam questions ask you to describe **distribution** from
a map rather than simply quote a density figure.

Words for describing distribution: **uneven**, **clustered** (concentrated), **linear** (along a
river, coast or road), **dispersed** (scattered), **sparse**, **dense**. Always use compass
directions and named features from the map.

## Key idea 2: the world pattern

Large parts of the world's land are very thinly settled, while a few regions hold a large share
of the world's people.

| Densely populated | Sparsely populated |
|---|---|
| South Asia (Ganges and Indus plains) | Hot deserts: Sahara, Arabian Peninsula, central Australia |
| East Asia (eastern China, Japan) | Cold lands: Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland, Antarctica |
| Western Europe | High mountains and plateaus: Himalaya, Tibet, Andes |
| North-eastern USA | Dense rainforest: much of the Amazon Basin |
| Nile valley and delta; Java | |

Notice the link to physical geography: most dense areas are **lowland, well-watered, fertile** and
have a moderate climate, while most sparse areas are too **dry, cold, high or densely forested**.
Many dense areas are also old centres of farming civilisation or modern industry, which is a human
factor.

## Key idea 3: factors, organised

Examiners reward answers that separate physical from human factors and explain **how** each one
affects where people live.

| Factor | Encourages dense population | Encourages sparse population |
|---|---|---|
| Relief | Flat or gently sloping lowland, easy to farm and build on | Steep, high land; thin soils; difficult access |
| Climate | Moderate temperatures, reliable rainfall | Extreme heat or cold; very low or very high rainfall |
| Water | Rivers, lakes, groundwater for drinking and irrigation | Lack of fresh water |
| Soils | Fertile alluvial or volcanic soils | Thin, leached or saline soils |
| Vegetation | Grassland or cleared land | Dense forest that is hard to clear |
| Resources | Minerals, fuels, fishing grounds | Few resources |
| Economic | Jobs in industry and services; ports and trade | Few jobs; remoteness from markets |
| Transport | Good road, rail and port links | Isolation, poor access |
| Political | Capital cities, planned development, stability | Conflict, insecurity, closed border areas |
| Social and historical | Long-established cities and farming regions | Recent or no permanent settlement |

Two refinements earn extra credit. First, **technology** changes the balance: irrigation, air
conditioning and desalination allow cities to grow in deserts, as in the Gulf states. Second, a
resource can create **pockets** of dense population in an otherwise sparse region, such as mining
towns.

## Case study material

**Pakistan.** The distribution is very uneven.
- *Dense*: the **Punjab plains** and the **Indus valley**. Flat land, fertile alluvial soils and
  a large irrigation network fed by the Indus and its tributaries support intensive farming and
  large cities such as Lahore and Faisalabad. **Punjab** is the most populous province, with about
  half of the country's people. **Karachi**, on the coast, is the largest city, growing as the
  main port and industrial centre.
- *Sparse*: **Balochistan**, the largest province by area, has well under a tenth of the
  population. It is mainly dry, rugged plateau and mountains, with limited water, poor soils and
  long distances to markets. The **northern mountains** (Karakoram and Himalaya) are also sparsely
  populated because of steep slopes, cold winters and short growing seasons; people live mainly in
  the valleys.

**Egypt** is a clear contrast for a global-scale answer: the great majority of Egyptians live in the
narrow **Nile valley and delta**, which covers only a few per cent of the country's land. Away from
the river, the desert is almost empty, because without the Nile there is too little water for
farming.

**Brazil** shows a human factor at work: most people live near the Atlantic coast, especially in the
south-east, while the Amazon interior is sparsely populated. The government built a new capital,
**Brasília**, inaugurated in 1960, partly to draw people and development inland.

## Worked example: density and description

Illustrative data for three districts of one region:

| District | Population | Area (km²) | Density (people per km²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Riverside | 1,560,000 | 1,300 | ? |
| Hill Country | 210,000 | 7,000 | ? |
| Dry Plateau | 48,000 | 16,000 | ? |

- Riverside: 1,560,000 ÷ 1,300 = **1,200** people per km².
- Hill Country: 210,000 ÷ 7,000 = **30** people per km².
- Dry Plateau: 48,000 ÷ 16,000 = **3** people per km².
- Whole region: 1,818,000 ÷ 24,300 = about **75** people per km².

The regional average of 75 describes none of the districts well, which is exactly why
distribution matters. Riverside has 1,560,000 of 1,818,000 people, about **86%**, on only about
**5%** of the land. A good description would say that population is highly concentrated in the
river district and very sparse on the plateau, with figures, before explaining why.

## Common mistakes

- Defining density as "how crowded a place is" with no unit.
- Explaining when the question says **describe**. Describe the pattern first; explain only when
  asked.
- Writing "the climate is good". Say what about it: reliable rainfall for crops, moderate
  temperatures.
- Giving physical factors only, or human factors only.
- Treating a sparse area as empty: deserts and mountains often have dense pockets around oases,
  valleys or mines.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a distribution from a map: general pattern, where it is dense, where it is sparse,
  with named places, directions and any figures from the key.
- **Explain**: factor, then how it attracts or discourages settlement, with the link to people's
  needs (water, food, jobs).
- **Suggest**: apply the factors to an unfamiliar map or photograph, such as a valley with a river
  and steep slopes.
- **Calculate**: show the division and give the unit, people per km².
- For the case study, name **specific areas** of both high and low density within the country and
  give a reason for each.
