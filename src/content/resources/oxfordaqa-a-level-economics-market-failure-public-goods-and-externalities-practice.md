## Questions

**1.** Explain what a misallocation of resources means. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a flood barrier protecting a town is likely to be a pure public good. **[4]**

**3.** A town has 12 households. Each values a clock on the main square at 5 currency units. Installing it costs 42.

**(a)** Calculate the net benefit to the town of installing the clock. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a private firm may still not supply it. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a road may be a quasi-public good rather than a pure public good. **[4]**

**5.** Using broadcasting as an example, explain how technological change can allow a public good to be supplied privately. **[3]**

**6.** A lake is open to all. With B boats, the total catch is 30B − B² tonnes, valued at 1 currency unit per tonne. Each boat costs 12 to run. (Illustrative.)

**(a)** Boats keep joining while the catch per boat, 30 − B, exceeds the cost per boat. Calculate the number of boats on the lake. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net value (total catch less total cost) with 9 boats. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the open lake ends up with too many boats. **[2]**

**7.** A dyeing works faces demand P = 60 − 2Q and private supply P = 12 + 2Q, where Q is thousands of metres of cloth. Its waste costs a downstream town 8 per thousand metres.

**(a)** Calculate the market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the price and quantity when the external cost is included. **[2]**
**(c)** State the overproduction and calculate the welfare loss. **[2]**

**8.** Evening swimming lessons have private demand P = 48 − Q and supply P = 8 + Q, where Q is hundreds of places. Each place gives the wider community an external benefit of 6.

**(a)** Calculate the market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the quantity at which the full benefit to society is counted. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the underconsumption as a percentage of the quantity found in (b), to 1 decimal place. **[1]**

**9.** Explain how the absence of property rights can lead to a negative externality, using a river as an example. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that the tragedy of the commons is unavoidable. **[8]**

**11.** A sandpit sells sand. The table shows each extra unit (hundreds of tonnes). Burying the nearby road in dust costs the town 3 for each unit. (Illustrative.)

| Unit | Value to buyers | Cost to the sandpit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 22 | 10 |
| 2 | 20 | 12 |
| 3 | 18 | 14 |
| 4 | 16 | 16 |
| 5 | 14 | 18 |
| 6 | 12 | 20 |

**(a)** State the number of units the sandpit will supply, with a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the number of units that is best for society, showing your comparison. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the gain to society from moving to your answer in (b). **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the sandpit's output is certainly too high. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Resources are used in a way that does not give society the greatest possible benefit [1]. Example: too much or too little of a good is produced compared with the socially best amount, or none at all [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention society, not just the firm or consumer. A reply that only says "the market does not work" does not show the idea.

**2.** It is non-rival, because one more person protected does not reduce protection for others [1]. It is non-excludable, because nobody in the town can be left out of the protection [1]. A firm therefore cannot charge individuals [1]. With no revenue, private firms are unlikely to supply it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each feature needs its own explanation in the context given. Naming "non-rival and non-excludable" with no link to the barrier is a list, not an explanation.

**3. (a)** Total benefit = 12 × 5 = 60 [1]. Net benefit = 60 − 42 = **18** [1].
**(b)** Each household can gain from the clock without paying, so each hopes others will pay (free-rider problem) [1]. With no one paying, the firm earns no revenue and the market is missing, although benefit exceeds cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), use the figures: benefit of 60 against a cost of 42 shows that the failure is not about the clock being unworthy.

**4.** When uncongested, extra users do not reduce others' use, so it is non-rival [1]. When congested, each extra vehicle slows others, so it becomes rival [1]. Tolls allow drivers to be excluded, so it is excludable [1]. It therefore has some, not all, public-good features [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A good explanation shows the feature changing with conditions. Writing "it is used by many people" makes no point about either feature.

**5.** Broadcast signals once reached every receiver, so viewers could not be excluded [1]. Encryption and a paid decoder or subscription allow non-payers to be excluded [1]. A firm can now charge and earn revenue, so private supply becomes possible [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the before-and-after change in excludability. Simply saying "technology improved" does not answer the question.

**6. (a)** Set 30 − B = 12 [1], so B = **18** [1].
**(b)** Catch = 30 × 9 − 81 = 189 [1]. Cost = 9 × 12 = 108, so net value = **81** [1].
**(c)** Each boat gains its own catch but ignores that it lowers the catch of every other boat [1]. Boats join until net value is 0 (216 − 216), so effort is wasted compared with 81 at 9 boats [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Support (c) with the figures from (a) and (b). Comparing 0 with 81 makes the overuse concrete.

**7. (a)** 60 − 2Q = 12 + 2Q [1], so Q = 12 and **P = 36** [1].
**(b)** Supply including external cost: P = 20 + 2Q. Then 60 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q [1], so **Q = 10 and P = 40** [1].
**(c)** Overproduction = 12 − 10 = **2** thousand metres [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 8 × 2 = **8** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State which curve moves and by how much. Replacing the 12 with 20 in the supply equation is the step to show.

**8. (a)** 48 − Q = 8 + Q [1], so Q = 20 and **P = 28** [1].
**(b)** Demand showing full benefit: P = 54 − Q. Then 54 − Q = 8 + Q [1], so **Q = 23** [1].
**(c)** Underconsumption = 3. 3 ÷ 23 × 100 = **13.0%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the social quantity (23) as the base, as the question asks. Using 20 as the base would give a different value.

**9.** Nobody owns the river, so no one can exclude a firm or charge it for dumping waste [1]. The firm counts only its own costs [1]. Private cost is therefore below the full cost to society, which includes harm to fishers and towns downstream [1]. The firm produces more than the socially best amount, a market failure [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the missing right to the firm's cost decision. Saying "the river gets polluted" without the chain scores little.

**10.** Each user gains in full from extra use but shares the cost, so overuse follows [1]. Without limits, the resource may be depleted [1]. Against "unavoidable": users can communicate and agree rules, so the outcome is not automatic [1]. Open access differs from shared ownership; where a group controls entry, use can be limited [1]. People are not purely self-interested, as norms matter [1]. Property rights or regulation can limit use, though enforcement can be costly [1]. How much harm occurs depends on how quickly the resource renews [1]. Judgement: the tragedy is a risk under open access, not a certainty, and it depends on the rules in place [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with a verdict and name the condition it rests on. Criticisms piled up without a conclusion leave the question unanswered.

**11. (a)** The sandpit supplies up to 4 units [1], because unit 4 costs it 16, equal to what buyers pay, and unit 5 costs 18 against 14 [1].
**(b)** Cost to society = sandpit cost + 3: 13, 15, 17, 19 for units 1 to 4 [1]. Unit 3: value 18 is above 17, so it adds to welfare [1]. Unit 4: value 16 is below 19, so it reduces welfare. Best = **3 units** [1].
**(c)** Unit 4 is worth 16 and costs society 19, a net loss of 3 [1]. So stopping at 3 gives a gain of **3** [1].
**(d)** The external cost of 3 may be hard to measure, so the best output may differ [1]. Dust damage might vary with how many units, or with weather, so a flat 3 is an assumption [1]. Even so, the sandpit's own margin at unit 4 is zero, so any positive external cost makes unit 4 a net loss: output of 4 is too high unless the dust causes no harm at all [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* For an evaluation of a number, say how sure you are of the input. Testing how far the cost could change before the answer flips is a strong approach.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a public good is one the state provides, which describes a policy and not the good.
- Listing "non-rival and non-excludable" without explaining either in the context.
- Skipping the free-rider link when asked why a firm will not supply.
- Moving the wrong curve, or moving it the wrong way.
- Dividing by the wrong quantity in a percentage.
- Not stating the units or what the numbers measure.
- Describing a problem with no consequence for output or welfare.
- Evaluation that lists points but ends with no judgement.
