## Culture and socialisation

**Culture** — the whole way of life of a group: norms, values, roles, beliefs, language.

**Primary socialisation** in the family; **secondary** through education, peers, media, religion and workplace.

**Nature versus nurture:** the sociological evidence is **cross-cultural variation** in norms and gender roles, and **feral children** cases where individuals raised without human contact fail to develop language or normal social behaviour. Both show that behaviour assumed natural is learnt.

**Ascribed status** is fixed at birth; **achieved status** is earned. **Subculture** — distinct norms within a wider society; **counter-culture** — actively opposed to the mainstream.

## Structure and action

The organising debate of the whole subject.

| | Structuralist (macro) | Social action (micro) |
|---|---|---|
| View | Society shapes individuals | Individuals create society |
| Perspectives | Functionalism, Marxism, feminism | Interactionism |

- **Functionalism** — society as interdependent parts held by **value consensus**.
- **Marxism** — **class conflict**; the superstructure serves the economic base; **false class consciousness** conceals exploitation.
- **Feminism** — patriarchy. Liberal (reform), Marxist (linked to capitalism), radical (patriarchy as fundamental).
- **Interactionism** — meanings, labelling, the self-fulfilling prophecy.
- **Postmodernism** — grand narratives no longer explain society; identity is fluid and chosen.

**Giddens's structuration is the synthesis:** structure and action shape each other continuously, since people act within structures that their actions then reproduce or change. Top-band answers resolve the opposition rather than describing it.

## Research methods

**Positivism** wants objective, quantitative, reliable data and causal patterns. **Interpretivism** wants *verstehen* — understanding meanings — and prefers qualitative, valid data.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Repeating gives the same result |
| **Validity** | Gives a true picture |
| **Representativeness** | Sample reflects the population |

**Quantitative methods are high in reliability and low in validity; qualitative the reverse.** That trade-off answers most methods questions.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Cheap, large, reliable | Low response, low validity |
| Structured interview | Comparable | Interviewer effect |
| Unstructured interview | Valid, detailed | Slow, unreliable |
| Participant observation | Very valid | Hawthorne effect, going native, ethical issues |
| Official statistics | Cheap, large-scale | Socially constructed by the state |

**Evaluate with PET** — Practical, Ethical, Theoretical — and within the theoretical, **RVR**: reliability, validity, representativeness. **Always evaluate for the specific research topic**, not in the abstract.

**Triangulation** combines methods so each offsets the other's weaknesses.

## Social inequality

**Class** predicts life chances more strongly than any other single factor. **Intergenerational** mobility compares a person with their parents; **intragenerational** compares stages of their own career.

**Absolute poverty** is lacking necessities for survival; **relative poverty** is falling below the standard considered acceptable in that society — which is why relative poverty exists in wealthy countries.

**The cycle of deprivation:** poor housing and diet → poor health and education → low-paid work → poverty reproduced.

**Gender** — the glass ceiling, horizontal and vertical segregation, the dual burden and triple shift.

**Ethnicity** — discrimination, institutional racism, media representation. **Ethnic and class disadvantage overlap heavily**, and much of the apparent ethnic effect narrows once class is controlled for.

## Exam traps

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Describing a perspective without applying it.
- Treating structure and action as a simple either/or.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract.
- Confusing absolute with relative poverty.
- One-sided answers — both sides plus a judgement are required.

## Self-test

1. What two kinds of evidence support the nurture side of the debate?
2. Distinguish structuralist from social action perspectives, and name the synthesis.
3. Distinguish reliability from validity, and say which methods favour each.
4. What does PET stand for, and what are the three theoretical criteria?
5. Why is relative poverty found in wealthy countries?

**Answers:** 1. Cross-cultural variation in norms and gender roles, and feral children cases where individuals raised without human contact fail to develop language or normal social behaviour. 2. Structuralist approaches see society as shaping individuals from above; social action approaches see individuals as creating society through interaction; Giddens's structuration synthesises the two. 3. Reliability is getting the same result on repetition, favoured by quantitative methods; validity is giving a true picture, favoured by qualitative methods. 4. Practical, Ethical, Theoretical; the theoretical criteria are reliability, validity and representativeness. 5. Because it is defined relative to the standard of living considered acceptable in that particular society, so it measures inequality rather than absolute deprivation.
