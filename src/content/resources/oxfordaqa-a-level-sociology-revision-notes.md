Unit 1 is assessed in two sections: **Section A Families** (40 of the unit's 60 marks) and **Section B Research methods** (20 marks). Section A carries two thirds of the unit — prioritise it accordingly.

## Section A: Families (3.1.1)

**Family types and marriage (3.1.1.1).** **Extended families** (vertically across generations, or horizontally across one generation), **nuclear families**, **single-parent families**; non-family arrangements — **communes**, **living alone**, **foster care/children's homes**. **Therborn's typology** compares family patterns across world regions. **Patriarchal** vs **matriarchal** structures; **arranged marriage**, **love marriage**, **monogamy**.

**Perspectives on families (3.1.1.2).**

| Perspective | Key claim |
|---|---|
| Functionalist | The nuclear family performs functions that "fit" modern industrial society |
| Marxist (**Engels**) | The family is an agent of social control serving capitalism |
| Feminist (**Oakley**) | Families shape different, unequal positions for men and women |

**Social change and changing families (3.1.1.3).** Demographic drivers: family size, birth/death rates, infant/child mortality, migration, an ageing population — plus industrialisation and urbanisation. Newer diversity types: **pivot/sandwich-generation**, **reconstituted/blended**, **living-apart-together**, **child-free couples**, **beanpole families**. **Individualisation** describes more freely chosen arrangements, while **Smart's** concept of **personal life** stresses that these choices stay embedded in relationships, alongside **diasporas/transnational families**, **friends as family**, and companion animals treated as family.

**Gender, age and family life (3.1.1.4).** Male "breadwinner"/instrumental role vs female expressive role, including the **triple shift** (paid work, domestic work, emotional work). Division of domestic labour; gendered power and decision-making; violence against women and children; child status and "child centredness"; **"boomerang" families**; roles of older people.

**Named sociologists for this unit:** Engels, Oakley, Parsons, Smart, Stacey, Therborn — never discuss a perspective on the family without naming one.

## Section B: Research methods and theory (3.1.3)

**Positivism** wants objective, quantitative, reliable data and causal patterns. **Interpretivism** wants *verstehen* — understanding meanings — and prefers qualitative, valid data.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Repeating gives the same result |
| **Validity** | Gives a true picture |
| **Representativeness** | Sample reflects the population |

**Quantitative methods are high in reliability and low in validity; qualitative the reverse.** That trade-off answers most methods questions.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Cheap, large, reliable | Low response, low validity |
| Structured interview | Comparable | Interviewer effect |
| Unstructured interview | Valid, detailed | Slow, unreliable |
| Participant observation | Very valid | Hawthorne effect, going native, ethical issues |
| Official statistics | Cheap, large-scale | Socially constructed by the state |

**Evaluate with PET** — Practical, Ethical, Theoretical — and within the theoretical, **RVR**: reliability, validity, representativeness. **Always evaluate for the specific research topic**, not in the abstract.

**Triangulation** combines methods so each offsets the other's weaknesses.

**Other methods named in 3.1.3.2.** **Experiments**: laboratory (high control, but artificial and rarely ethical for social topics) and field (natural setting, less control). **Longitudinal studies** follow the same sample over time, showing change but losing members (attrition). **Secondary sources**: qualitative (diaries, letters, personal documents: rich meaning, but possibly unrepresentative or not written for research) and quantitative (official statistics: large-scale and comparable, but shaped by how the state defines and counts).

**Issues in research (3.1.3.3).**

- **Operationalisation**: turning a concept such as "poverty" into something measurable.
- **Sampling**: the **population** and **sampling frame**; random, systematic, quota, multistage, stratified random and snowball samples.
- **Bias**: researcher or interviewer bias, the **Hawthorne effect** (people change behaviour when observed) and **social desirability** (answering to look good).
- **Ethics**: informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity and participant wellbeing.
- **Causation and correlation**: two variables moving together does not prove one causes the other.
- **Representativeness and generalisability**, and practical issues of time and funding.

**Interpreting data (3.1.3.4).** Section B includes a structured question based on data about an unseen issue of global significance. For tables, charts and graphs, quote exact figures with units, describe the trend, and note what the data cannot show (who collected it, how terms were defined). For field-note or interview extracts, identify the meanings participants express and comment on how typical the extract might be.

**Worked example.** Assess the usefulness of participant observation for studying a criminal gang.

```
STRENGTHS   high validity - behaviour observed in its natural setting
            access to a group that would refuse a questionnaire
            reveals meanings members attach to their own actions

WEAKNESSES  not reliable - cannot be replicated
            small, unrepresentative sample
            researcher may 'go native', losing objectivity
            covert work raises consent and safety issues
```

The conclusion should turn on **purpose**: for understanding meaning it is the strongest method available; for measuring prevalence it is the wrong tool.

Remember 3.1.3 resurfaces in Unit 2 (Exploring Sociology) as a single extended-response question, not new content — a worked Families example from Section A above doubles as evidence for a Unit 2 methods answer.

## Exam traps

- Naming no sociologists when discussing a family perspective — Engels, Oakley, Parsons, Smart, Stacey and Therborn are all directly assessable.
- Treating family diversity as purely modern, rather than explaining it through demographic and social change.
- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract rather than against a specific research aim.
- One-sided answers — both sides plus a judgement are required.
- Under-revising Section A relative to Section B — Families is twice the marks.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is the Unit 1 exam?**
1 hour 45 minutes for 60 marks: Section A Families (40 marks) and Section B Research methods (20 marks). It is 50% of the International AS and 20% of the International A-level.

**Do I have to use examples from my own society?**
Yes, for selected extended-response questions the specification requires examples from your own society or one you know about, so prepare family examples from at least one named country.

**Will polygamy or civil partnerships be examined?**
No. The specification says they may be taught where appropriate but will not be directly assessed; they are credited only if used relevantly.

## Self-test

1. Name the three family perspectives covered in 3.1.1.2 and their named sociologists.
2. Give three demographic factors behind family change, and two newer family diversity types.
3. What is the triple shift, and which sociologist developed the concept of personal life?
4. Distinguish reliability from validity, and say which methods favour each.
5. Give one strength and one weakness of participant observation for studying a criminal gang.

**Answers:** 1. Functionalist (fit with modern industrial society), Marxist (Engels — family as an agent of social control serving capitalism), Feminist (Oakley — families shape unequal positions for men and women). 2. Any three of: family size, birth/death rates, infant/child mortality, migration, an ageing population, industrialisation, urbanisation; diversity types include pivot/sandwich-generation, reconstituted/blended, living-apart-together, child-free couples, beanpole families. 3. Paid work, domestic work and emotional work combined; Smart (personal life). 4. Reliability is getting the same result on repetition, favoured by quantitative methods; validity is giving a true picture, favoured by qualitative methods. 5. Strength: high validity, since behaviour is observed in its natural setting and the method gives access to a group that would refuse a questionnaire. Weakness: it is not reliable, since it cannot be replicated, and the researcher may 'go native', losing objectivity.
