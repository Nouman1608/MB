This guide covers content item 1.4.4, Business plans, in Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. A business plan pulls together almost everything in Theme 1, so this item is a natural place for questions that draw on several topics at once. The broader [guide to the marketing mix and the business plan](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-the-marketing-mix-and-the-business-plan/) lists the contents of a plan and works through a forecast. This page looks at how a reader judges a plan, how a plan reduces risk in practice, and where its value runs out. All businesses and figures are invented.

Companion pages: the [business plans revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-plans-revision-notes/) and the [business plans practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-plans-practice/).

## What 1.4.4 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| The role and importance of a business plan | To identify: the business idea; business aims and objectives; target market (market research); forecast revenue, cost and profit; cash-flow forecast; sources of finance; location; marketing mix |
| The purpose of planning business activity | The role and importance of a business plan in minimising risk and obtaining finance |

A business plan is a written document that sets out what a business will do, how it will do it, and the money involved. Notice that the specification's list mirrors the rest of Theme 1: aims (1.3.1), revenue and costs (1.3.2), cash flow (1.3.3), finance (1.3.4), location (1.4.2) and the marketing mix (1.4.3).

## The sections work as a chain

A good plan is not eight separate boxes. Each section depends on the ones before it, and a weakness early in the chain spreads through the rest.

1. **Business idea** → what need is being met?
2. **Target market (market research)** → who has that need, how many of them, and what will they pay?
3. **Marketing mix** → a price, product, promotion and place that suit that market.
4. **Location** → where those customers can be reached at an affordable cost.
5. **Forecast revenue, cost and profit** → revenue depends on the price and the sales estimated from research; costs depend on location and promotion.
6. **Cash-flow forecast** → when the money actually arrives and leaves.
7. **Sources of finance** → how any gap shown by the cash-flow forecast will be filled.
8. **Aims and objectives** → targets that the forecasts suggest are realistic.

If the market research is weak, the sales estimate is a guess, so the revenue forecast, the profit forecast and the cash-flow forecast are all guesses too. This is a powerful point in an Evaluate answer: a plan is only as reliable as the research behind it.

## How a plan minimises risk

Risk is the chance that the business fails or loses money. Planning reduces it in four practical ways.

- **Testing the idea before spending.** Research may show there is too little demand, which is far cheaper to discover on paper than after signing a lease.
- **Spotting cash shortages early.** A cash-flow forecast shows the months when outflows exceed inflows, so the owner can arrange an overdraft in advance.
- **Asking "what if?".** The owner can recalculate the forecasts with lower sales or higher costs to see how much room for error there is.
- **Setting targets to check progress.** Once trading, actual figures can be compared with the plan, so problems are noticed quickly.

### Worked example: testing a forecast

Ollie plans Fresh Fold, a laundry collection service. His plan forecasts 600 orders a month at £15 each. Variable costs are £6 an order and fixed costs are £3,600 a month.

1. Forecast revenue = 600 × £15 = £9,000
2. Total costs = £3,600 + (600 × £6) = £3,600 + £3,600 = £7,200
3. Forecast profit = £9,000 − £7,200 = £1,800 a month

What if orders are 25% lower than forecast?

4. Orders = 600 × 0.75 = 450
5. Revenue = 450 × £15 = £6,750; total costs = £3,600 + (450 × £6) = £6,300
6. Profit = £6,750 − £6,300 = £450 a month

Break even = £3,600 ÷ (£15 − £6) = 400 orders, so the margin of safety at the forecast is 600 − 400 = 200 orders. A lender reading this sees that Ollie would still make a small profit if sales were a quarter below forecast. That is the kind of evidence that reduces risk for both the owner and the lender.

## How a plan helps obtain finance

Lenders and investors want to know two things: will the business survive, and will they get their money back (or a return on it)? Each section of the plan answers part of that.

| Section | What a lender or investor looks for |
|---|---|
| Business idea and target market | Evidence of real demand, not just the owner's enthusiasm |
| Forecast revenue, cost and profit | Realistic figures that show the business can make a profit |
| Cash-flow forecast | Enough cash each month to make repayments |
| Sources of finance | How much the owner is putting in; an owner risking their own money is more committed |
| Location and marketing mix | Sensible choices that fit the target market |

A clear plan also shows the owner understands their business, which builds confidence. Without one, a bank has little to judge the request on and is more likely to refuse it or to set tougher terms.

## The limits of a business plan

The specification asks for the role and importance of a plan, and judging importance means knowing its limits.

- **Forecasts are estimates.** Customer numbers, prices and costs may turn out differently, especially for a brand-new business with no past figures.
- **Conditions change.** A new competitor, a rise in costs or a change in customer tastes can make a plan out of date quickly.
- **Optimism.** Owners may overestimate sales. A plan built on hope rather than research can give false confidence.
- **Time and cost.** Researching and writing a plan takes time a busy owner might prefer to spend setting up the business.

None of these make planning pointless. They mean a plan should be based on research, tested with "what if?" figures, and updated as the business trades.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse style):** Analyse the importance of a business plan to Hana, who needs a £20,000 bank loan to open a bike café. [6]

> A bank will want evidence that Hana's bike café can repay £20,000 *(context)*. Her cash-flow forecast can show that there will be enough cash each month to meet the repayments *(section of the plan linked to finance)*, which makes the bank more likely to lend *(consequence)*. The plan also makes Hana research whether enough cyclists will visit *(target market)*, so she can find out before borrowing whether the idea will work *(risk reduced)*. However, the forecasts are only estimates for a new café, so if fewer customers come than planned she could still struggle to repay *(limitation, linked back)*.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the contents of a plan when the question asks why it is important.
- Saying a plan "guarantees" finance or success. It improves the chance of each.
- Forgetting that forecasts depend on market research.
- Treating the plan as a one-off document instead of something to update.

## Exam technique

- **Give** or **State:** name a section of the plan, or one reason for planning.
- **Explain:** one reason (for example, reducing risk) and two linked expansions.
- **Analyse:** connect specific sections of the plan to the business's situation in the source.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the benefits against the limits, then decide how important the plan is for this business, and why.
