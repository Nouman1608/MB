Before sociologists collect any evidence they must decide how to approach society and what kind of
data to use. Key questions 1.1 and 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) cover exactly this: the
positivist and interpretivist approaches, ways of combining them, the concepts used to judge
research, and the main types of primary and secondary data, including how to read tables and graphs
in the exam. These ideas are tested in the compulsory Research methods question of Paper 1.

For research design, sampling and methods (key question 1.3), see the
[O Level research methods, identity and inequality guide](/resources/o-level-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/).

The 2251 syllabus content is the same as Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495, so these concepts are
identical; the examples on this page are chosen for O Level learners.

## What is the positivist approach?

Positivists believe sociology should copy the natural sciences. They take a **macro view** of
society as a structure of **social facts** — such as the class system or religious institutions —
that exist outside individuals and shape behaviour. They use the **scientific method**: form a
hypothesis, collect **quantitative data**, look for **patterns and trends**, and test for
**correlation and causation**. Good research is **objective** (free from the researcher's values)
and **reliable** (repeatable).

*Example:* Peter Townsend's *Poverty in the United Kingdom* (1979) used a large social survey of
households to measure how many people lacked items most of society regarded as normal. Its
standardised questions allowed comparison between groups — a positivist strength.

## What is the interpretivist approach?

Interpretivists take a **micro view**: society is created through people's interactions and the
**meanings and motivations** behind them. They use **qualitative, in-depth** methods to achieve
**verstehen** — Max Weber's term for empathetic understanding. They accept that research involves
some **subjectivity**, but argue it produces greater **validity**.

*Example:* Erving Goffman's *Asylums* (1961) was based on fieldwork in a large US psychiatric
hospital, where he observed how patients adapted to institutional life. The rich detail about
patients' experiences could not have come from a questionnaire.

## How can approaches be combined?

- **Triangulation** — using several methods to cross-check. Eileen Barker's *The Making of a Moonie*
  (1984) combined participant observation, in-depth interviews and questionnaires to study why
  people joined the Unification Church.
- **Longitudinal studies** — following the same people over time. The international *Young Lives*
  study, begun in 2002, has followed around 12,000 children in Ethiopia, India, Peru and Vietnam to
  understand how childhood poverty affects later life. Strengths: shows change and sequence.
  Limitations: costly, and some participants drop out.

## How do sociologists judge research choices?

| Concept | Question to ask | O Level example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bias** | Have the researcher's values affected the questions or conclusions? | A survey on women's employment funded by a group with a fixed view |
| **Interviewer effect** | Does who asks the question change the answer? | A male interviewer asking women about domestic violence |
| **Hawthorne / observer effect** | Are people acting differently because they are watched? | Teachers teaching differently when an observer sits in the classroom |
| **Validity** | Is this a true picture? | Students saying they study three hours a night when they do not |
| **Reliability** | Would the same results come again? | Two researchers using the same questionnaire get similar results |
| **Representativeness** | Is the sample typical? | Only urban schools surveyed in a mainly rural country |
| **Generalisability** | Can findings apply more widely? | One village studied in depth |

## What is the difference between primary and secondary, quantitative and qualitative data?

| | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary data** (collected first-hand) | Designed for the research aims; up to date | Time-consuming and costly; access may be difficult |
| **Secondary data** (already exists) | Cheap; may cover large populations or the past | Not designed for the research; definitions may differ |
| **Quantitative data** (numbers) | Easy to compare, show trends, reliable | Lacks depth; may miss meanings |
| **Qualitative data** (words, images) | Rich, valid, shows meanings | Hard to compare; small samples; interpretation may be subjective |

## How useful are different kinds of secondary evidence?

- **Official statistics** — for example national census data, such as Pakistan's 2023 census, which
  was the country's first carried out digitally. Strengths: national coverage and regular
  collection. Limitations: undercounting of some groups, and categories decided by government.
- **Historical documents** — parish records, colonial reports, old newspapers. They allow study of
  the past but may be incomplete or written from the viewpoint of those in power.
- **Personal documents** — diaries, letters, autobiographies. They reveal meaning but may be
  exaggerated or unrepresentative.
- **Digital sources** — online forums, social media posts, blogs. Large and current, but hard to
  verify and not everyone is online.
- **Media content** — newspapers, television, advertisements. Useful for studying representation,
  but shaped by the media's own priorities.

## How should you analyse tables, charts and graphs?

1. Read the title, source and date first.
2. Identify exactly what is being measured (percentages or numbers?).
3. Quote figures accurately with units.
4. Describe the overall trend before giving detail.
5. Explain a limitation — sample size, date, missing groups, how terms were defined, or whether a
   correlation proves causation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do positivists value reliability?** Because if results can be repeated by another researcher,
they can be checked and treated as scientific evidence.

**Are official statistics positivist?** They are a favourite positivist source because they are
quantitative and large-scale, but interpretivists argue they are socially constructed by the people
who collect them.

**What does the exam expect in data questions?** Accurate identification of information from the
source, explanation of an aspect of the research, and explanation of problems with the evidence.
