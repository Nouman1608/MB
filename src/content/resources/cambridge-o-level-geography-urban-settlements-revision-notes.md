Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.6 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.
**Case study required:** an urban area or urban areas (you may use one city for land use and
another for problems).

## The two learning outcomes

1. Describe and give reasons for the characteristics of, and changes in, **land use** in urban
   areas (CBD, residential, industrial, rural–urban fringe; countries at different levels of
   development; urban sprawl).
2. Explain the **problems** of urban areas, their causes and possible solutions (air, noise,
   water and visual pollution; inequality; housing; traffic congestion; conflicts over land use
   change).

## Land use: the core logic

- City centre = where routes meet = **most accessible** → **highest land values**.
- Land values fall with distance from the centre → density and building height fall too.
- High-profit users (shops, offices) take the centre; space-hungry users (large factories,
  retail parks, airports) go to the edge.

| Zone | Remember it by |
|---|---|
| CBD | Tall buildings, shops and offices, few residents, highest land value |
| Inner city (HIC) | Old terraced housing and old industry beside railways, rivers or docks |
| Suburbs | Lower density, newer housing with gardens, reached by public transport and car |
| Industrial | Old: central, on water and rail. New: edge of city, beside ring roads, cheap large sites |
| Rural–urban fringe | Retail parks, business parks, airports, sewage works, golf courses, farmland |

**Lower-income cities:** CBD may mix offices with markets; wealthy housing near the CBD or along
a main road; **squatter settlements on the edge** and on hazardous land; industry along main
routes. The poorest often live furthest from the centre, the reverse of many HIC cities.

## Changes in land use

- **Deindustrialisation** → derelict **brownfield** land → redevelopment (e.g. London Docklands,
  Canary Wharf, from the 1980s).
- **Gentrification**: renovation of inner-city housing by wealthier newcomers; prices rise.
- **Decentralisation**: shops and offices move to out-of-town sites.
- **Urban sprawl**: outward spread onto **greenfield** land.

**Effects of sprawl:** loss of farmland and habitats; more runoff and flood risk; longer
commutes and more car use; villages become commuter settlements with higher house prices and
fewer local services; costly new infrastructure.
**Responses:** green belts, brownfield-first policies, higher-density housing, new towns (e.g.
Navi Mumbai).

## Urban problems: cause → solution

- **Air pollution**: traffic, industry, burning waste → public transport, emission controls.
- **Noise**: traffic, aircraft, construction → barriers, zoning, flight-path rules.
- **Water pollution**: untreated sewage, industrial waste → treatment works, fines.
- **Visual pollution**: litter, dereliction, hoardings → redevelopment, planning controls.
- **Inequality**: uneven wealth and services → targeted investment, affordable housing.
- **Housing**: demand outstrips supply → social housing, upgrading, new towns.
- **Traffic congestion**: car ownership, old streets, commuting → mass transit, park-and-ride,
  congestion charging (central London, 2003).
- **Land use conflict**: housing vs green space, offices vs homes → consultation, zoning.

Always add a **limitation**: cost, time, who loses out, or whether it moves the problem elsewhere.

## Second case-study outline: Lahore, Pakistan

- **Land use**: the old **Walled City** is a dense historic core of bazaars and workshops; newer
  commercial areas grew along main roads such as Mall Road and in Gulberg; planned housing schemes
  for wealthier residents spread outwards on the edge of the city; **katchi abadis** are found
  beside railway lines, drains and on vacant land; industry lines the main roads out of the city.
- **The model needs adjusting**: unlike the simple LIC pattern, much high-income housing is in
  planned suburbs on the edge, so describe the real city rather than forcing it into a model.
- **Urban sprawl**: rapid growth has spread the built-up area onto surrounding farmland, raising
  journey lengths and car and motorbike use.
- **Problems**: winter smog from traffic, industry and crop burning; congestion; pressure on
  groundwater as the water table falls.
- **Responses**: the Metrobus (2013) and the Orange Line metro train (2020) for mass transit; smog
  emergency measures; plans to restore parts of the Walled City.
- **Evaluation**: mass transit carries large numbers along its routes but is costly and covers
  only some corridors; sprawl continues because planning controls are hard to enforce.

## Model paragraph: explaining a land use

*Explain why the CBD has tall buildings.* "The CBD is the most accessible part of the city, where
main roads and public transport routes meet. Many shops and offices compete for space there
because they need large numbers of customers and workers, so land values are very high. To make
the most of expensive land, developers build upwards, giving tall office and apartment blocks."

The chain is **accessibility → competition → land value → building form**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| CBD | Central Business District: the commercial core of a city |
| Accessibility | How easily a place can be reached by people and transport |
| Land value | The price or rent of land, highest where demand and accessibility are greatest |
| Brownfield site | Previously developed land, often derelict, available for reuse |
| Greenfield site | Land not previously built on, usually farmland or open countryside |
| Urban sprawl | The outward, often unplanned, spread of the built-up area |
| Rural–urban fringe | The transition zone where urban and rural land uses meet |
| Green belt | A protected zone of open land around a city where building is restricted |
| Gentrification | Wealthier people moving into and renovating a poorer area |
| Squatter settlement | Housing built without legal right to the land, usually lacking services |
| Decentralisation | Movement of shops, offices or industry from the city centre to the edge |
| Mass transit | Public transport that moves large numbers of people, such as metro or bus rapid transit |

## Exam traps

- Describing zones with no **reasons** (accessibility, land value, age of buildings).
- Applying the HIC model unchanged to a city in a lower-income country.
- Solutions with no limitation or judgement.
- Naming a problem but not its cause; Cambridge asks for causes **and** solutions.
- A case study with no named districts, schemes or dates.

## Self-test

1. Why are land values highest in the CBD?
2. Give two reasons why modern industrial estates are found on the edge of cities.
3. State one way the location of high-income housing differs between many HIC and LIC cities.
4. Give two effects of urban sprawl on nearby rural settlements.
5. Distinguish between a brownfield and a greenfield site.
6. Name one cause and one solution for traffic congestion.
7. Why is the Walled City of Lahore densely built up?
8. Give one way Lahore's land use differs from the simple model for lower-income cities.
9. Give one advantage and one limitation of a metro train line as a solution to congestion.
10. Explain why developers build upwards in the CBD.

**Answers:** 1. It is the most accessible point, where routes converge, and many users compete
for a small area of land. 2. Any two of: cheaper land; large plots with room to expand; access to
ring roads and motorways; away from housing. 3. In many HIC cities high-income housing is in
the outer suburbs; in many LIC cities it is close to the CBD or along a main road, with the
poorest on the edge. 4. Any two of: rising house prices; villages become commuter
settlements; local services close; loss of surrounding farmland. 5. Brownfield has been built
on before and can be reused; greenfield has never been built on. 6. Cause: rising car
ownership, narrow old streets or commuting into the CBD. Solution: mass transit, park-and-ride,
bus lanes or congestion charging. 7. It is the oldest part of the city, built before motor
vehicles, with narrow streets and buildings packed close together around its bazaars. 8. Much
high-income housing is in planned schemes on the edge of the city rather than near the CBD.
9. Advantage: carries many people quickly without using road space; limitation: very expensive
and serves only the corridors it runs along. 10. Land values are very high, so building upwards
fits more floor space on each costly plot.
