**What do these questions practise?** Section 1 of Paper 1, Key Questions 1 to 5. The syllabus
says each Section B question has a 4-mark knowledge part (one short paragraph), a 7-mark part
needing at least two paragraphs, and a 14-mark essay of about two pages. Here the three types are
grouped so you can drill each skill. Allow about 5, 9 and 16 minutes respectively.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the Hindi–Urdu controversy of 1867. **[4]**

**2.** Describe the part played by Bahadur Shah Zafar in the War of Independence of 1857–58. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739. **[4]**

**4.** Describe Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858. **[4]**

**5.** Describe how the British annexed the Punjab between 1845 and 1849. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why the Mughal Empire declined after the death of Aurangzeb in 1707. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why Sir Syed Ahmad Khan opposed Muslim participation in the Indian National
Congress. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why Urdu was chosen as the national language of Pakistan. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why the War of Independence of 1857–58 did not achieve independence. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**10.** "The policies of Aurangzeb were the main reason for the decline of the Mughal Empire." How far
do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**11.** "Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's educational work was his most important contribution to the Muslims of
the subcontinent." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "The promotion of regional languages since 1947 has been successful." How far do you agree?
Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge parts: 1 mark per relevant point, with a further mark for supporting detail. Explanation
and evaluation parts are marked by levels (see the tables after the answers); indicative content
is a guide, and other accurate points should be credited.

**1.** In 1867 Hindus in Benares (Varanasi) demanded that Urdu be replaced by Hindi as an official
language [1], written in the Devanagari script instead of the Perso-Arabic script [1]. Muslims saw
Urdu as part of their cultural heritage, so the demand caused bitter disagreement [1]. Sir Syed Ahmad
Khan concluded from it that Hindus and Muslims could not work together as one nation [1]. Also
credit: the campaign spread to other provinces; Urdu defence associations were formed. **[4]**

**2.** He was the last Mughal emperor, elderly and with little real power [1]. When sepoys from
Meerut reached Delhi in May 1857 they proclaimed him their leader [1]. He was a reluctant figurehead
and could not unite or direct the rebels [1]. When the British retook Delhi in September 1857 he was
captured, tried and exiled to Rangoon [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Nadir Shah, ruler of Persia, invaded the Mughal Empire in 1738–39 [1]. He defeated Emperor
Muhammad Shah's army at the Battle of Karnal in 1739 [1]. He occupied Delhi, where many inhabitants
were killed after fighting broke out [1], and carried away vast treasure, including the Peacock
Throne [1]. **[4]**

**4.** It was issued in November 1858, after the Crown took over from the East India Company [1]. It
promised that the British would not interfere in the religious beliefs of Indians [1]. It promised to
respect the rights and territory of the princes, ending the policy of annexation [1], and said that
Indians would be eligible for government posts regardless of race or religion [1]. Also credit: it
offered a pardon to most rebels not involved in killing British subjects. **[4]**

**5.** After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839 the Sikh state was weakened by power struggles [1]. In the
First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46) the British won and took territory under the Treaty of Lahore [1].
A revolt at Multan in 1848 led to the Second Anglo-Sikh War [1], which ended with the British victory
at Gujrat in February 1849, after which Dalhousie annexed the Punjab, including Peshawar [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- *Weak successors:* wars of succession and short reigns meant no emperor could restore authority;
  nobles such as the Sayyid brothers made and unmade emperors.
- *Finance:* the Deccan wars had emptied the treasury, so the army and officials could not be paid
  properly.
- *Rival powers:* the Marathas and Sikhs took territory and revenue.
- *Foreign invasion:* Nadir Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Abdali exposed Mughal weakness.
- *The East India Company:* after Plassey (1757) it controlled Bengal's wealth. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Congress wanted representative government with elections; Hindus were the majority, so Muslims
  would always be outvoted.
- He believed Muslims were not yet educated enough to compete with Hindus for government posts by
  open examination.
- His experience of the Hindi–Urdu controversy convinced him Hindus and Muslims were separate
  nations with different interests.
- He wanted to keep British goodwill, which Congress agitation might lose. **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- It was understood across most of the provinces, so it could act as a link language.
- It was written in the Perso-Arabic script and drew on Arabic and Persian, linking it to Islamic
  culture.
- It had been tied to the Pakistan Movement since the Hindi–Urdu controversy and Sir Syed's defence
  of it.
- It had a rich literature, including the poetry of Ghalib and Allama Iqbal.
- Choosing a regional language could have favoured one province over the others. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- No united leadership or common aim: risings in different places were not co-ordinated.
- The Punjab and the North-West stayed under British control, and troops raised there helped to
  retake Delhi.
- Many rulers, especially in the south, did not join the uprising.
- The British had better weapons, the telegraph and reinforcements from overseas. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* Aurangzeb's decades of Deccan campaigns drained the treasury; the reimposition of the
  jizya (1679) and other religious policies alienated Rajputs, Sikhs and Marathas; his long absence
  from the north weakened control there; he left no clear system of succession.
- *Disagree:* his successors were weak and divided; Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded; the
  Marathas and Sikhs built their own empires; the East India Company took Bengal after Plassey and
  Buxar and expanded until the 1850s.
- *Judgement:* for example, Aurangzeb's policies weakened the empire, but it was the failure of later
  rulers to deal with invasions and the Company that turned weakness into collapse. **[14]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Educational work:* the Scientific Society, *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*, the Aligarh school of 1875 and the
  Muhammadan Educational Conference produced an educated Muslim class, many of whom later led the
  Muslim League.
- *Other contributions:* his writings, such as *The Causes of the Indian Revolt*, helped to restore
  British trust in Muslims; his Two-Nation Theory gave the later Pakistan Movement its basis; his
  religious writing argued Islam and science were compatible; his opposition to Congress kept
  Muslims politically separate.
- *Judgement:* for example, education was the most important because it created the leaders who
  carried his Two-Nation idea forward. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Successful:* regional literatures, such as Sindhi poetry of Shah Abdul Latif Bhitai and Pushto
  poetry of Rahman Baba and Khushal Khan Khattak, are celebrated; academies such as the Pashto Academy
  in Peshawar promote research and publishing; regional languages are used on radio and television
  and taught in schools in their provinces.
- *Not successful:* Urdu and English dominate government, higher education and business, so many
  parents see less value in regional languages; there were tensions, such as the Bengali language
  movement before 1971 and protests over Sindhi in 1972; some smaller languages have little official
  support.
- *Judgement:* for example, regional languages have flourished culturally but remain less important
  than Urdu and English in public life. **[14]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a 4-mark answer as an essay: four precise facts are enough.
- Describing the course of the War of Independence when the question asks why it failed.
- Arguing only one side of a 14-mark question, which caps the answer at Level 3.
- Mixing up Syed Ahmad Barelvi and Sir Syed Ahmad Khan.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do the 14-mark questions always use "How far do you agree"?**
They test judgement, so expect "how far", "how successful" or "how important"; each needs both sides
and a conclusion.
