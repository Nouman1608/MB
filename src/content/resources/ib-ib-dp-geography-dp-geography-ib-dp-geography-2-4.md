## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Evaluate the sustainable use of ocean resources such as fish, energy and minerals.
- Evaluate how oceans are governed, nationally and internationally, and the tensions involved.
- Discuss possible futures for the oceans and how management could change them.

## Why stocks collapse: a simple model

Fisheries scientists use the idea that a fish population grows fastest at an intermediate size. When the stock is small, there are few breeders; when it is near the maximum the environment can support (its **carrying capacity**), competition slows growth. A common simple model puts the fastest growth at **half** the carrying capacity.

**Worked example (invented stock).** Carrying capacity K = 200,000 tonnes. Growth rate r = 0.4 per year. Annual growth of the stock is r x N x (1 - N / K), where N is the stock size.

```
At N = 100,000 (half of K):
growth = 0.4 x 100,000 x (1 - 0.5) = 20,000 tonnes a year
This is the maximum sustainable yield (MSY).

At N = 60,000 (stock already reduced):
growth = 0.4 x 60,000 x (1 - 0.3) = 16,800 tonnes a year
```

If the fleet keeps catching 20,000 tonnes when the stock is 60,000, the catch exceeds growth by 3,200 tonnes, so the stock shrinks; next year growth is lower still, and the gap widens. This **downward spiral** explains why stocks can collapse suddenly after years of apparently stable catches, as happened with cod on the Grand Banks.

Limits of the model, which belong in an evaluation: real growth rates vary with water temperature and food supply; species interact; stock estimates are uncertain; and climate change is shifting where fish live, moving stocks across national boundaries.

## Controlling catches

| Tool | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total allowable catch | A cap on the total tonnage taken from a stock each year | Directly limits pressure on the stock | Can trigger a race to fish before the cap is reached |
| Individual transferable quotas (ITQs) | Each owner holds a share of the total catch that can be bought and sold | Removes the race; owners gain from a healthy stock | Quotas can concentrate in a few large companies, squeezing out small fishers |
| Gear and season rules | Mesh sizes, closed seasons, no-trawl zones | Protect young fish and spawning periods | Hard to enforce at sea |
| Marine protected areas | No-take or restricted zones | Let stocks and habitats recover; spill-over to nearby waters | Fishing effort may simply move elsewhere |
| Removing harmful subsidies | Ends payments that keep excess fleets at sea | Tackles a cause of overcapacity | Politically difficult; affects jobs |

New Zealand introduced a national system of tradeable quotas in 1986, and Iceland also manages its fisheries largely through transferable quotas; both are often cited as examples of the approach. At the global scale, the World Trade Organization adopted an Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies in 2022, aimed at subsidies that support illegal fishing and the fishing of overfished stocks; it came into force in 2025.

## Aquaculture: the future of seafood?

Fish and shellfish farming has grown rapidly and now supplies around half of the fish and seafood eaten worldwide.

**Arguments that it can meet future demand:** it reduces pressure on wild stocks; it can be located near markets; some forms, such as farming shellfish and seaweed, need no feed and can even filter water.

**Arguments against relying on it:** farming carnivorous fish such as salmon uses fishmeal and fish oil made from wild-caught fish; coastal shrimp ponds have replaced large areas of mangrove; waste and chemicals pollute nearby water; disease and parasites can spread to wild fish; escaped farmed fish can interbreed with wild populations.

A balanced conclusion: aquaculture is likely to supply most future growth in seafood, but whether it is sustainable depends on the species farmed, the feed used and how sites are regulated.

## New global agreements and their limits

- **High seas biodiversity.** The Agreement on Marine Biological Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction, often called the High Seas Treaty, was adopted in 2023. It provides a legal route to create marine protected areas on the high seas and requires environmental assessments for some activities there. It reached the 60 ratifications needed to take effect in 2025 and entered into force in January 2026. Its weakness is the same as other treaties: it binds only the states that join, and enforcement far from any coast is difficult.
- **Plastic pollution.** In 2022 the UN Environment Assembly agreed to negotiate a legally binding agreement on plastic pollution. Negotiating sessions in 2024 and 2025 ended without an agreed text, mainly because states disagreed over whether to limit plastic production or focus on waste management. This shows how economic interests shape ocean futures.

## Offshore energy

The future ocean is also an energy landscape.

- **Offshore wind** is expanding fast in shallow seas such as the North Sea, where winds are strong and steady. It reduces emissions but competes with fishing grounds and shipping lanes, and construction disturbs the seabed.
- **Tidal and wave energy** are predictable but still small-scale and costly.
- **Offshore oil and gas** continue, with spill risks, and their future depends on climate policy.

Marine spatial planning, which allocates sea areas to different uses much as land-use planning does on land, is one way to reduce conflicts between energy, fishing, shipping and conservation.

## Annotated model answer

*To what extent can marine protected areas secure the future of ocean fisheries?* **(sample conclusion)**

"Marine protected areas allow stocks to rebuild because fish inside them grow larger and produce more young, which can spill over into fished waters nearby **[mechanism]**. However, they cover only part of the ocean and fishing effort may simply move elsewhere, so they work best alongside catch limits such as transferable quotas **[limitation and complement]**. On the high seas, protection has depended on new agreements that bind only the states that join **[governance limit]**. Climate change is also moving stocks out of protected areas, so fixed boundaries may need to change **[future uncertainty]**. Marine protected areas are therefore a necessary part of securing fisheries, but only partly effective on their own **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating maximum sustainable yield as a safe target even when the stock has already fallen below half its carrying capacity.
- Describing quotas without explaining the race to fish or quota concentration.
- Presenting aquaculture as free of environmental cost.
- Assuming a treaty works as soon as it is signed.
- Discussing the future without any scenario or time scale.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** management tools with criteria: effect on the stock, enforceability, fairness to small fishers and developing states, and adaptability to climate change.
- **Discuss** futures by setting out at least two scenarios and the decisions that would lead to each.
- **To what extent** answers need a position early and evidence from more than one ocean or fishery.
- Use the model's numbers carefully: say what happens to the stock, not just the catch.
