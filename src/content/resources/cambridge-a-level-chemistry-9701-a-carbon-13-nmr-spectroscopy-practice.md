## Section A

**1.** State what the number of peaks in a carbon-13 NMR spectrum tells you about a molecule. **[1]**

**2.** Predict the number of peaks in the carbon-13 NMR spectrum of:

**(a)** butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃ **[1]**

**(b)** pentan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃ **[1]**

**(c)** 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH **[1]**

**3.** A carbon-13 spectrum contains a peak at 198 ppm. State the type of functional group that this carbon
could belong to, and explain how it differs from a carbon giving a peak at 175 ppm. **[2]**

**4.** Predict the number of peaks in the carbon-13 NMR spectrum of methylbenzene, C₆H₅CH₃, and identify the
environments. **[2]**

**5.** State which of the following gives exactly **three** peaks in its carbon-13 NMR spectrum: propan-2-ol,
propanal, propanone, 2-chloropropane. Explain your choice. **[2]**

**6.** Cyclohexane gives one carbon-13 peak. Cyclohexanol gives four. Explain the difference. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The three isomers of dichlorobenzene, C₆H₄Cl₂, are the 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-isomers.

**(a)** Predict the number of carbon-13 peaks for each isomer. **[3]**

**(b)** A sample of one isomer gives four peaks, all in the 110–160 ppm region. Identify the isomer and explain
why it gives four peaks. **[2]**

**8.** Compound **C** has the molecular formula C₃H₆O₂. Its carbon-13 spectrum has three peaks: one in the
160–185 ppm region, one in the 50–70 ppm region and one below 50 ppm.

**(a)** Explain what the peak in the 160–185 ppm region shows. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why compound C cannot be propanoic acid. **[2]**

**(c)** Give the structures of **two** esters that fit the data. **[2]**

**9.** Compound **D**, C₄H₈O, is a carbonyl compound. Its carbon-13 spectrum shows **three** peaks, one of them
in the 190–220 ppm region.

**(a)** Butanal and butanone both give four peaks. Show this by identifying the environments in each. **[2]**

**(b)** Deduce the structure of D and explain your reasoning. **[2]**

**10.** Two isomers of C₄H₁₀O each give a carbon-13 spectrum with two peaks: one in the 50–70 ppm region and
one below 50 ppm.

**(a)** Identify the two isomers. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain, for each isomer, which carbons give the peak in the 50–70 ppm region. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one other technique that would distinguish the two isomers, and state what it would show.
**[2]**

**11.** Propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN, and propenal, CH₂=CHCHO, both have three carbon environments.

**(a)** Use the carbon-13 shift table to give the region expected for each carbon in propenal. **[3]**

**(b)** State which carbon in propanenitrile is expected in the 100–125 ppm region, and why its peak could be
confused with an alkene carbon. **[2]**

**12.** Benzoic acid, C₆H₅COOH, and methyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOCH₃, are analysed by carbon-13 NMR.

**(a)** Predict the number of peaks for each compound. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the region below 70 ppm differs between the two spectra. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of **different carbon environments** in the molecule [1]. (Not the total number of carbon
atoms.)

**2. (a)** **4** — all four carbons are in different environments [1].

**(b)** **3** — the two CH₂ groups are equivalent and the two CH₃ groups are equivalent, plus the C=O
carbon [1].

**(c)** **2** — the three CH₃ carbons are equivalent; the central carbon bonded to O is the second
environment [1].

**3.** An **aldehyde or ketone** carbonyl carbon (190–220 ppm) [1]. A peak at 175 ppm is in the 160–185 region,
so that carbon would be in a **carboxylic acid or ester** group instead [1].

**4.** **5 peaks** [1]: the CH₃ carbon; the ring carbon bonded to CH₃; the two ring carbons next to it; the
two carbons beyond those; the carbon opposite the CH₃ group [1].

**5.** **Propanal**, CH₃CH₂CHO [1]: it has no symmetry, so all three carbons are different. Propan-2-ol,
propanone and 2-chloropropane each have two equivalent CH₃ groups and give only two peaks [1].

**6.** In cyclohexane all six carbons are **equivalent by symmetry**, so there is one environment [1]. In
cyclohexanol the OH group breaks that symmetry [1]: a mirror plane passes through the C–OH carbon and the
carbon opposite, giving C–OH, two equivalent carbons next to it, two equivalent carbons beyond, and the
opposite carbon — four environments [1].

**7. (a)** 1,2-isomer: **3** [1]; 1,3-isomer: **4** [1]; 1,4-isomer: **2** [1].

**(b)** **1,3-dichlorobenzene** [1]. The mirror plane passes through carbon 2 and carbon 5, so the two C–Cl
carbons are equivalent and carbons 4 and 6 are equivalent, while carbons 2 and 5 are each unique — four
environments [1].

**8. (a)** A **carboxyl or ester carbon**, C=O in R–COOH or R–COOR (not an aldehyde or ketone) [1].

**(b)** Propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH, has **no sp³ carbon bonded to oxygen** [1]; its two other carbons are
alkyl carbons, so it would not give a peak in the 50–70 ppm region [1].

**(c)** **Methyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₃** [1] and **ethyl methanoate, HCOOCH₂CH₃** [1].

**9. (a)** Butanal, CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO: CHO, CH₂ next to CHO, central CH₂, CH₃ — four different carbons [1].
Butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃: CH₃ next to C=O, C=O, CH₂, CH₃ — four different carbons [1].

**(b)** Three peaks for four carbons means two carbons are equivalent [1]. The structure is
**2-methylpropanal, (CH₃)₂CHCHO**: the two CH₃ groups are equivalent, giving CH₃, CH and CHO, with the CHO
carbon in the 190–220 region [1].

**10. (a)** **2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH** [1] and **ethoxyethane, CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃** [1].

**(b)** In 2-methylpropan-2-ol, the **single central carbon bonded to OH** [1]. In ethoxyethane, the **two
equivalent CH₂ carbons bonded to the ether oxygen** [1].

**(c)** One mark for a suitable technique and one for the matching observation [2]. Accept any one of:
**IR** — only 2-methylpropan-2-ol shows a broad O–H absorption in the 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ range; **proton NMR with
D₂O** — only the alcohol has an O–H peak that disappears on shaking with D₂O; **proton NMR splitting** —
ethoxyethane shows a quartet and a triplet, while the alcohol shows a large singlet for its nine equivalent
CH₃ protons.

**11. (a)** CH₂= carbon: **110–160** (alkene) [1]; =CH– carbon: **110–160** (alkene) [1]; CHO carbon:
**190–220** (aldehyde) [1].

**(b)** The **C≡N carbon** [1]. The nitrile range, 100–125 ppm, overlaps the lower end of the alkene/arene
range, 110–160 ppm, so a peak between 110 and 125 ppm could be either [1].

**12. (a)** Benzoic acid: **5** (COOH, C1, two ortho, two meta, one para) [1]. Methyl benzoate: **6** (the
same five plus the O–CH₃ carbon) [1].

**(b)** Benzoic acid has **no peaks below 70 ppm** [1]; methyl benzoate has one, in the **50–70 ppm** region,
from the CH₃ carbon bonded to oxygen [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the number of carbon atoms instead of the number of environments.
- Treating every disubstituted benzene as if it gave the same number of peaks.
- Assigning ester or acid C=O carbons to the 190–220 region.
- Missing the O–CH₃ carbon of an ester, or the C≡N carbon of a nitrile, as a separate environment.
- Naming one structure when the data allow two, or not saying what extra evidence would decide.
- Stating a number of peaks with no reason when the question says "explain".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do the three dichlorobenzenes give different numbers of carbon-13 peaks?**
The position of the two chlorine atoms changes the symmetry of the ring. The 1,4-isomer is the most
symmetrical and gives two peaks, the 1,2-isomer gives three and the 1,3-isomer gives four.

**How do I tell an ester carbon from a ketone carbon in carbon-13 NMR?**
Use the shift table: carboxylic acid and ester carbons fall in the 160–185 ppm region, while aldehyde and
ketone carbons fall at 190–220 ppm.

**What should I write if two structures fit a carbon-13 spectrum?**
Give both. The syllabus asks you to deduce possible structures, so a correct list of candidates, with the
evidence that would separate them, is the complete answer.
