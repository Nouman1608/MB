These revision notes cover the key and related concepts of IB MYP Language Acquisition. They are aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Language acquisition*, from 2020 (first eAssessment May 2023/November 2023), and suit MYP years 4 and 5 at any proficiency level. There is no SL/HL split in the MYP. MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units -- and these notes cover the concepts the IB's brief names for language acquisition.

The notes are in English. Examples come from French, Spanish and German; each method works in any target language.

## What the brief actually says

- Key concepts such as **communication, connections, creativity and culture** broadly frame the MYP curriculum.
- Related concepts promote deeper learning grounded in the subject. The brief's examples for language acquisition are **word choice, conventions and idiom**.
- You explore key and related concepts through the **six global contexts**: identities and relationships; orientation in space and time; personal and cultural expression; scientific and technical innovation; globalization and sustainability; fairness and development.
- The curriculum explores **factual, conceptual and debatable** questions.
- Schools choose the content. Anything beyond this -- for example, other related concepts from the full guide -- your teacher will share.

## Definitions at a glance

| Term | Type | Plain meaning | One-line classroom example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication | Key concept | Exchanging meaning, matched to audience, purpose and mode | Same news told to a friend by text and to a head teacher by email |
| Connections | Key concept | Links between languages, subjects, people and places | Cognate hunt between English and your target language |
| Creativity | Key concept | Using known language in new combinations for a purpose | Inventing and performing a radio advert |
| Culture | Key concept | Shared beliefs, values, habits and products of a group | Choosing the right form of address for an older stranger |
| Word choice | Related concept (example) | Picking words for register, connotation and precision | Replacing "stuff" with the exact noun |
| Conventions | Related concept (example) | The expected rules of a text or exchange | Correct opening and closing of a formal email |
| Idiom | Related concept (example) | A fixed phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words | Explaining *tomar el pelo* as "to tease" |
| Statement of inquiry | Unit planning | One general claim joining a key concept, related concept(s) and a global context | "Cultural expectations shape the conventions of polite requests." |

## The statement-of-inquiry formula

```
key concept  +  related concept(s)  +  global context
      →  one general, transferable claim (a sentence, not a question)
```

Checklist -- a strong statement of inquiry is:

1. a statement, not a question or an instruction;
2. general (no named app, film or textbook chapter);
3. built on a key concept you could name;
4. linked to at least one related concept;
5. pointed at one of the six global contexts;
6. still true in another language or unit.

**Weak:** "In this unit we will learn about holidays in Spain." (A task description; no concept.)
**Strong:** "Where and how people celebrate reflects the culture and history of a community." (Culture; conventions; orientation in space and time.)

## Method in steps

### Choosing the right words (word choice)

1. Who is the audience? Stranger, friend, official, child?
2. What is the purpose? Inform, persuade, complain, entertain?
3. Set the register: formal or informal -- including the correct form of "you".
4. Swap vague words for precise ones ("nice" → "welcoming", "spacious", "tasty").
5. Check connotation: does each word carry the feeling you want?
6. Check for false friends.

### Following conventions

1. Name the text type or exchange (formal letter, blog, interview, voicemail).
2. List its expected parts (opening, body, closing; greeting, question, reply).
3. Check layout details: date format, sign-off, paragraphing.
4. Check language-specific rules: ¿ ¡ in Spanish; capital letters for all German nouns; formal sign-off formulas.
5. In speaking, follow turn-taking: answer, add a detail, ask something back.

### Handling an idiom you meet

1. Notice that the literal meaning makes no sense in context.
2. Use surrounding clues to infer the real meaning.
3. Record it as: idiom → meaning → your own example sentence.
4. In translation, match the meaning, not the words.

### Using an idiom in your own work

1. Use only idioms you have checked with a teacher or a reliable dictionary.
2. Fit the register: many idioms are informal.
3. One well-placed idiom beats three forced ones.

## Idioms worth knowing (checked meanings)

| Idiom | Literal | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| French *tomber dans les pommes* | to fall into the apples | to faint |
| French *poser un lapin à quelqu'un* | to put a rabbit on someone | to stand someone up |
| Spanish *tomar el pelo* | to take the hair | to tease, to pull someone's leg |
| Spanish *costar un ojo de la cara* | to cost an eye of the face | to be very expensive |
| German *Ich verstehe nur Bahnhof* | I only understand "station" | I don't understand a thing |

## False friends worth knowing

| Word | Looks like | Actually means |
|---|---|---|
| French *actuellement* | actually | currently |
| French *librairie* | library | bookshop |
| Spanish *embarazada* | embarrassed | pregnant |
| Spanish *éxito* | exit | success |
| German *Gift* | gift | poison |
| German *bekommen* | become | to get, to receive |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Key concept vs related concept.** Key concepts (communication, connections, creativity, culture) are broad and frame the whole MYP. Related concepts (word choice, conventions, idiom) are specific to language acquisition.
- **Concept vs topic.** "Sport" is a topic; "communication" is a concept that works across topics.
- **Statement of inquiry vs inquiry question.** A statement is a claim; an inquiry question is factual, conceptual or debatable.
- **Factual vs conceptual vs debatable.** Factual has one checkable answer. Conceptual asks how or why, and transfers. Debatable has reasonable answers on more than one side.
- **Word choice vs conventions.** Word choice is about individual words. Conventions are about the rules of the whole text or exchange.
- **Idiom vs slang.** An idiom is a fixed phrase with a non-literal meaning. Slang is informal vocabulary, often new or group-specific. Some idioms are formal enough for any text; most slang is not.
- **Culture as facts vs culture as perspective.** "Siesta exists" is a fact. "Why daily timetables differ between communities" is a perspective.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four key concepts the brief lists for language acquisition.
2. Name the three related-concept examples the brief gives.
3. How many global contexts are there, and which one fits a unit on festivals and art?
4. Classify: "Should schools ban mobile phones?" -- factual, conceptual or debatable?
5. What is wrong with "What makes a good holiday postcard?" as a statement of inquiry?
6. Give the meaning of German *Ich verstehe nur Bahnhof*.
7. Which related concept is broken when a formal job application ends "See ya!"? Give the best answer and one other.
8. What does Spanish *éxito* mean, and why is it a trap?
9. Which key concept is most at work when you choose *usted* rather than *tú* for an older stranger?
10. Rewrite "The food was nice" with more precise word choice, for a restaurant review.

### Answers

1. Communication, connections, creativity, culture.
2. Word choice, conventions, idiom.
3. Six. Personal and cultural expression.
4. Debatable -- reasonable people answer both ways.
5. It is a question, not a statement, and it names a specific text type instead of a transferable idea. Example fix: "Writers adapt conventions to suit the space and the reader they have."
6. "I don't understand a thing" (literally, "I only understand 'station'").
7. Conventions (a formal letter needs a formal close). Word choice is also broken (the register is wrong).
8. "Success". It looks like English "exit" (which is *salida*), so learners misread it.
9. Culture -- the form of address reflects expected respect. Communication and word choice are also involved.
10. Any precise version, for example: "The paella was fresh, full of flavour and generously served."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing the key concepts but not explaining how one shapes a specific task. Naming is recall; applying is understanding.
- Writing a statement of inquiry that is a question, a task instruction or tied to one textbook page.
- Choosing a related concept that does not fit the unit (for example, "idiom" for a unit that never uses fixed expressions).
- Translating idioms word for word, which produces nonsense in the target language.
- Using an informal idiom or slang in a formal written task, breaking register.
- Getting the form of address wrong partway through a role-play (switching from formal to informal "you").
- Missing language-specific conventions: Spanish opening ¿ ¡, German noun capitals, formal sign-off formulas.
- Treating culture as a list of facts, with no comparison of perspectives between your own community and another.
- Vague word choice ("nice", "good", "thing") at a level where precise vocabulary is expected.
