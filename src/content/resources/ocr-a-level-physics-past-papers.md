This page is a guide to OCR A Level Physics A (H556) past papers from June 2024 to June 2026. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from OCR's grade-boundary documents, and for June 2025 and June 2024 a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from OCR's examiner reports. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on OCR's own site or its teacher platform.

OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder, and grade boundaries on the page above. Examiner reports for each series are listed on the assessment page for the qualification, alongside a separate moderators' report on the practical endorsement. The full question papers and mark schemes that the reports refer to are held on Teach Cambridge, OCR's teacher site, so ask your teacher or exams officer if you cannot open them.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it tells you why answers that looked right still lost marks. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. H556 is a linear qualification first assessed in 2017 and still current. There are three written papers. Paper 1 (H556/01, Modelling physics) is 2 hours 15 minutes, 100 marks and 37% of the qualification. Paper 2 (H556/02, Exploring physics) is also 2 hours 15 minutes, 100 marks and 37%. Paper 3 (H556/03, Unified physics) is synoptic, 1 hour 30 minutes, 70 marks and 26%. The three papers total 270 marks and decide the A* to E grade. The practical endorsement (H556/04) is assessed in school over at least 12 practical activities and is reported separately as a pass or not classified. It carries no marks towards the 270, so it has no threshold in the tables below. The 2024 reports describe Paper 1 as covering modules 1, 2, 3 and 5, Paper 2 as covering modules 1, 2, 4 and 6, and Paper 3 as covering all modules.

## June 2025

The June 2025 reports point to the same strengths and weaknesses across the papers. Candidates who set out calculations step by step, used the data and relationships booklet carefully and described practical work through a graph did well. Candidates who misread the question, left out parts of the extended answers, or gave a rehearsed answer to an unfamiliar context did not. On Paper 3 the examiners say mathematical questions were done more confidently than descriptive ones.

**Where marks were lost – H556/01 (Modelling physics)**

- Reading and answering: some candidates misread the question, did not address every requirement of an extended-response question, or reused an answer from an earlier mark scheme without adapting it.
- Practical and data: planning questions were strongest when candidates named the instruments, said what they would do with the readings and described a graph and the meaning of its gradient or intercept. Weaker answers left out the graph.
- Mathematics: trigonometric functions were a recurring source of error, and in gravitational potential questions candidates missed that a difference in potential, multiplied by the mass, is what gives an energy change. A missed square on a distance and unit conversions between astronomical units and metres caused slips.
- Mechanics: a thrust and drag question needed the resultant force from the previous part, and some candidates did not use their earlier answer and instead found an average force over the wrong time. Definitions that were remembered accurately earned credit.
- Astrophysics and uncertainty: reading the width of a line on a scale diagram in the wrong unit, and carrying that into a percentage uncertainty, lost marks on one of the harder items.

**Where marks were lost – H556/02 (Exploring physics)**

- Multiple choice: not all questions were attempted, and this did not appear to be a timing problem. Estimating a physical quantity, choosing the best experimental procedure and a question mixing potential and field strength were the hardest items.
- Working: lengthy calculations without working could not earn method marks. Rounding to one significant figure, or ignoring significant figures in a final answer, also cost marks.
- Extended-response questions: answers gave little detail, for example describing only one of two models when both were asked for.
- Practical skills: lines of best fit were drawn through a false origin, a gradient was found over a small range or from just two points, and precision, resolution and accuracy were confused.
- Content: a path difference and geometry problem needed two equations to be combined, and a capacitor question was done best by finding the capacitance first. Candidates were comfortable with logarithms and exponential equations, and a common error in a graph question was a curve drawn where a straight line through the origin was expected.

**Where marks were lost – H556/03 (Unified physics)**

- Working and rearranging: formulae were rearranged incorrectly, working was set out haphazardly, data was copied wrongly from the question or a graph, and common SI prefixes were given incorrect values.
- Applying knowledge: answers to descriptive questions were often generic and did not use the context. In one question about a device that turns movement into electricity, many answers were vague and gained nothing.
- Electricity and fields: candidates tried to use Fleming's rules where they did not apply and held misconceptions about the direction of current. A capacitor question was often marked down for a misread value from the graph.
- Data analysis: comments on how close values were were too vague, with no reference to the percentage difference or range.
- Radiation and the atmosphere: some responses linked the properties of alpha radiation to the context only vaguely, and answers on lightning rarely mentioned atmospheric effects.

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports ask for the same habits: clear working, accurate language, and practical ideas that candidates can apply in unfamiliar settings. Paper 3 raised a further point about presentation, and the report warns that illegible answers will lose marks.

**Where marks were lost – H556/01 (Modelling physics)**

- Practical work: candidates forgot how investigative work is performed and reported, including the near-universal need for a relevant graph, and some forgot parts of GCSE physics such as resultant force, Hooke's law experiments and conservation of momentum.
- "Show that" questions: marks were limited to one where the rearrangement step was left out.
- Moments: a beam question was harder because the weight of the beam was not drawn on the diagram and had to be included.
- Thermal physics: a missed square in a mean square speed calculation was the most common error, and answers on temperature drifted into the gas laws instead of the physics asked for.
- Astrophysics: a multi-step inverse-square question was hard, with some candidates relying only on the formula given, and a question about a range of luminosities that correspond to a single temperature was very challenging.
- Extended response: the final one was difficult to get started on, and a purely qualitative evaluation was limited to the lowest level.

**Where marks were lost – H556/02 (Exploring physics)**

- Multiple choice: some candidates did not complete the questions. The combination of cells treated as resistors and the internal resistance of a cell were weaker, and most were secure on capacitance and oscillation calculations.
- Working: not enough working in longer calculations meant method marks could not be given when the final answer was wrong, and final answers were cut to one significant figure.
- Extended response: answers lacked detail, with a poor use of the diagram in one and no safety precautions named in another.
- Logarithms: some candidates were not able to use logarithms or logarithmic graphs in the questions that needed them.
- Nuclear physics: the idea that an increase in binding energy per nucleon means energy is released was poorly known. Neutrino and antineutrino were mixed up in decay equations, and quarks were counted wrongly.
- Drawing: lines drawn with uneven spacing on a diagram lost marks.

**Where marks were lost – H556/03 (Unified physics)**

- Presentation: steps were missed out, answers were rushed and lacked structure, handwriting and digits were illegible, and a ruler too short was used for a line of best fit.
- Extended response: not every part of the two extended-response questions was answered.
- Powers of ten: errors with powers of ten cost marks that candidates could have kept.
- Data and precision: some candidates believed that data given to three significant figures is more precise than data given to two. Better answers described precision in terms of a narrow spread.
- Graphs: scales were usually sensible, but units were sometimes missing from an axis.

## What examiners keep saying

Across the two series with reports, the same points come up on the three papers.

- Show every step. Method marks are lost when the working is missing, and "show that" questions need the rearrangement as well as the answer.
- Answer all of an extended-response question. Read the instruction, cover every part and use the diagram or context you are given.
- Describe practical work through a graph: say what you would plot, and what the gradient or intercept tells you. Know the difference between precision, resolution and accuracy.
- Use the data and relationships booklet carefully, check units, powers of ten and significant figures, and do not round to one figure.
- Apply ideas to the context in the question. A generic description earns little, and rehearsed answers from earlier papers do not fit new situations.
- Write legibly, use a sharp pencil and a long enough ruler for graphs, and do not leave multiple-choice questions blank.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OCR A Level Physics A (H556) past papers?**
OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder. Grade boundaries are on OCR's grade boundaries page, and examiner reports are listed on the assessment page for the qualification.

**Which H556 papers should I sit?**
All three written papers. Paper 1 (Modelling physics) and Paper 2 (Exploring physics) are 100 marks each, and Paper 3 (Unified physics) is 70 marks. The practical endorsement is assessed separately in school.

**How are the grade thresholds used?**
The overall grade comes from adding the three paper marks, out of 270, and comparing the total with that series' boundaries. The paper rows show how each paper performed on its own.

**What mistakes do H556 examiners report most often?**
Missing working or rearrangement steps, generic answers that ignore the context, incomplete extended-response answers, confusion between precision, resolution and accuracy, and slips with units and powers of ten.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
