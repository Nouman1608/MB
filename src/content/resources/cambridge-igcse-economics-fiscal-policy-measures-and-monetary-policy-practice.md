## Questions

**1.** Define fiscal policy. **[2]**

**2.** Country C (invented) plans the following, in units of currency. Revenue: income tax 55, sales tax 38, corporation tax 22, other 10. Spending: health 60, education 41, defence 19, infrastructure 9, interest on debt 8. National output is 600.

**(a)** Calculate total revenue and total spending. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the budget balance and say whether it is a deficit or a surplus. **[2]**
**(c)** Express the balance as a percentage of national output. **[1]**

**3.** Country D (invented) has revenue of 96 and spending of 88.

**(a)** Calculate the budget balance. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain two ways the government might use it. **[2]**

**4.** Country D's sales tax rate falls from 15% to 12% on a taxable base of 400.

**(a)** Calculate the change in sales tax revenue if the base does not change. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the actual change might be smaller. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how higher government spending on road building could reduce unemployment. **[4]**

**6.** Define money supply and monetary policy. **[3]**

**7.** A household in Country D borrows 150,000 at an interest rate of 3%. The rate rises to 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the extra interest per year and per month. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the rise could reduce spending by households and firms. **[3]**

**8.** In Country C, 1 C-unit = 2.5 D-units. The rate falls to 1 C-unit = 2.0 D-units.

**(a)** A C-made good costs 50 C-units. Calculate its price in D-units before and after, and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(b)** A D-made good costs 100 D-units. Calculate its price in C-units before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one effect of the change on Country C's balance of payments. **[2]**

**9.** In Country E (invented) the first 10 units of income are tax free. The rest is taxed at 20%.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid, and the tax as a percentage of income, for a worker earning 20 and a worker earning 50. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this tax is progressive. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how the government could use the revenue to redistribute income. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how a cut in the interest rate could raise economic growth but also risk higher inflation. **[5]**

**11.** Country A (invented) has inflation of 9%, unemployment of 3% and a current account deficit. The government is considering a rise in the interest rate or a rise in income tax. Evaluate which is the better choice. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The use of government spending [1] and taxation [1] to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that names only one lever, tax or spending, is incomplete; name both.

**2. (a)** Revenue: 55 + 38 + 22 + 10 = **125** [1]. Spending: 60 + 41 + 19 + 9 + 8 = **137** [1].
**(b)** 125 − 137 = −12 [1], so a **deficit of 12** [1].
**(c)** 12 ÷ 600 × 100 = **2.0%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the sums that give each total; a correct balance with no working is hard to credit if one total is wrong. Name the result as deficit or surplus.

**3. (a)** 96 − 88 = **+8 (a surplus)** [1].
**(b)** Two of: repay national debt, which lowers future interest payments [1]; pay for extra spending or a tax cut in a later year [1]. Keeping a reserve against a future fall in revenue is another valid use. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two uses with a short reason each earn more than two bare labels; avoid giving the same idea twice in different words.

**4. (a)** Old revenue: 0.15 × 400 = 60 [1]. New revenue: 0.12 × 400 = 48, so revenue **falls by 12** [1].
**(b)** Lower tax reduces prices, so buyers may buy more [1]; the taxable base grows, so the fall in revenue is less than 12 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Answer (b) with a cause and its effect on the base. Saying "people spend more" without linking it to revenue stops short.

**5.** The government pays construction firms [1], which hire workers and buy materials [1]. Workers and suppliers spend their extra income in shops and firms [1], which sell more and hire more staff, so unemployment falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain in which each step leads to the next gains credit; a list of words such as "jobs, spending, growth" does not show reasoning.

**6.** Money supply: the total amount of money in an economy [1]. Monetary policy: use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate [1] to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name all three levers in the definition. Do not describe monetary policy as the same thing as taxation.

**7. (a)** 150,000 × (0.05 − 0.03) = **3,000 a year** [1]; 3,000 ÷ 12 = **250 a month** [1].
**(b)** Loans become dearer, so households have less income left to spend or borrow less [1]. Firms find fewer projects earn more than the cost of borrowing [1], so they cut investment [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both households and firms when the question names both. State the direction of change, for example "spend less".

**8. (a)** Before: 50 × 2.5 = **125** D-units [1]. After: 50 × 2.0 = **100** D-units [1]. Change: (100 ÷ 125 − 1) × 100 = **−20%** [1].
**(b)** Before: 100 ÷ 2.5 = **40** C-units [1]. After: 100 ÷ 2.0 = **50** C-units [1].
**(c)** C-made exports are cheaper abroad, so foreign buyers may buy more [1]; imports cost more, so C buyers may buy fewer [1]. The balance of payments may improve if demand is price elastic enough. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide to convert an import into home currency and multiply to convert an export; check your direction using the size of the rate. State a percentage with its sign or the word "fall".

**9. (a)** Worker on 20: taxable 10, tax 2, 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = 10% [1]. Worker on 50: taxable 40, tax 8 [1], 8 ÷ 50 × 100 = 16% [1].
**(b)** The share of income paid as tax rises as income rises [1].
**(c)** Spend revenue on health, education or welfare payments aimed at low-income households [1], so their living standards rise relative to higher earners [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Define progressive by the rising share, not just by the higher amount. Link the use of revenue to who benefits.

**10.** A lower rate makes borrowing cheaper [1]. Households spend more and firms invest more [1]. Firms receive more orders and produce more, so output grows [1]. If firms cannot raise output as fast as spending grows, they raise prices [1]. A lower interest rate may also lower the exchange rate, making imports dearer and raising costs [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Give both sides, growth and inflation, and link them by the same cause. A one-sided chain is unlikely to earn full credit.

**11.** A strong answer draws on points such as these, and ends with a reasoned judgement.
- A higher interest rate raises the cost of borrowing, so households and firms spend less, easing price rises [1].
- It may attract foreign savers and raise the exchange rate, making imports cheaper and reducing import costs [1], but making exports dearer and so worsening the current account [1].
- Time lags and banks not passing on the full rise may delay the effect [1].
- A rise in income tax cuts take-home pay, reducing spending including on imports, which eases prices and the deficit [1].
- Unemployment is already low at 3%, so some rise is affordable; but the tax hits all workers and may be unpopular, and firms may cut jobs [1].
- Judgement, such as: the rate rise is better if much inflation is from import costs, because a stronger currency helps; the tax rise is better if a quick, direct cut in spending is needed; combining both may be best. The answer must state which is preferred and why. [2] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation gains most when it picks one option and says what the choice depends on. Do not stop at a list of advantages and disadvantages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing spending − revenue and calling the result the balance.
- Leaving off the words deficit or surplus after the figure.
- Treating a tax cut as always reducing revenue by the full amount.
- Giving a measure without a step-by-step chain to the aim.
- Multiplying when a conversion needs dividing, or the reverse.
- Mixing up appreciation and depreciation.
- Confusing a change in the interest rate with a change in the money supply.
- Listing evaluation points with no judgement or condition.
- Using aggregate demand when the answer can be given through spending and jobs.
