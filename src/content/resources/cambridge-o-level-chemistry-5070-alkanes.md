Section 11.4 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has four learning outcomes. You must be able to state that the bonding in alkanes is single covalent and that alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons; describe alkanes as "generally unreactive, except in terms of combustion and substitution by chlorine"; state what a substitution reaction is; and describe the reaction with chlorine as photochemical, "with ultraviolet light providing the activation energy, Ea", drawing the products "limited to monosubstitution". 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Petroleum, Alkanes and Alkenes guide](/resources/petroleum-alkanes-and-alkenes/) introduces alkane structure and the chlorine reaction. This guide adds the skills that cost marks: balancing combustion equations for the first four alkanes, predicting every monosubstituted product (including the two from propane and from butane), and explaining the role of ultraviolet light in terms of activation energy.

## Bonding: single covalent, saturated

In an alkane every carbon atom forms **four single covalent bonds**, to hydrogen atoms or to other carbon atoms. There are no double bonds. That makes alkanes:

- **hydrocarbons**: hydrogen and carbon only
- **saturated**: all carbon–carbon bonds are single bonds

The first four alkanes, all with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, are methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈ and butane C₄H₁₀.

## Why alkanes are generally unreactive

Alkanes contain only C–C and C–H single bonds and have no functional group to react. So they do not react with acids, alkalis or most other common reagents. In particular, an alkane does **not** decolourise aqueous bromine under normal laboratory conditions, which is why the bromine test in section 11.5 can tell an alkane from an alkene.

There are two exceptions the syllabus names: **combustion** and **substitution by chlorine**.

## Combustion

Alkanes burn in air, releasing a lot of energy (an exothermic reaction), which is why they are used as fuels. With plenty of oxygen, combustion is complete and the products are **carbon dioxide and water**.

| Alkane | Balanced equation for complete combustion |
|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O |
| Ethane | 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O |
| Propane | C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O |
| Butane | 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O |

### A reliable balancing method

**Worked example 1.** Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of butane, C₄H₁₀.

1. **Carbon**: 4 C atoms, so 4CO₂.
2. **Hydrogen**: 10 H atoms, so 5H₂O.
3. **Oxygen on the right**: (4 × 2) + (5 × 1) = 13 O atoms, so 6½O₂ on the left.
4. **Remove the half** by doubling everything:

```
C4H10 + 6½O2 → 4CO2 + 5H2O
2C4H10 + 13O2 → 8CO2 + 10H2O
```

Check: left has 8 C, 20 H, 26 O; right has 8 C, 20 H, 16 + 10 = 26 O.

Alkanes with an even number of carbons (ethane, butane) always need this doubling step; methane and propane do not.

**Worked example 2.** 20 cm³ of propane is burned completely in excess oxygen. Calculate the volume of oxygen used and the volume of carbon dioxide formed, all volumes measured at room temperature and pressure.

*Answer.* From C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O, the mole ratio propane : oxygen : carbon dioxide is 1 : 5 : 3 [1]. Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of moles, so the volume ratio is the same. Oxygen used = 5 × 20 = **100 cm³** [1]; carbon dioxide formed = 3 × 20 = **60 cm³** [1]. Water is a liquid at room temperature, so it has no gas volume here.

The balanced equation is the whole calculation. An unbalanced equation gives the wrong ratio and loses every mark that follows.

With too little oxygen, combustion is **incomplete** and carbon monoxide or carbon particulates form instead of carbon dioxide (see [air quality and climate](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-air-quality-and-climate/)).

## Substitution by chlorine

A **substitution reaction** is one in which one atom or group of atoms is replaced by another atom or group of atoms. In an alkane, a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom. The hydrogen that is removed joins the other chlorine atom to form **hydrogen chloride**.

```
methane + chlorine → chloromethane + hydrogen chloride
CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl
```

### Why ultraviolet light is needed

The reaction is **photochemical**: it needs light. At room temperature in the dark, methane and chlorine do not react, because too few colliding particles have the **activation energy, Ea**, the minimum energy colliding particles must have to react. **Ultraviolet light provides the activation energy.** Exam answers should say "ultraviolet (UV) light", not just "light" or "heat".

### Drawing the products (monosubstitution)

"Monosubstitution" means only **one** hydrogen atom is replaced. The product has the same carbon chain with one Cl in place of one H, and the other product is always HCl.

For methane and ethane, every hydrogen is in an equivalent position, so there is only one monosubstituted product:

| Alkane | Product | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | chloromethane | CH₃Cl |
| Ethane | chloroethane | CH₃CH₂Cl |

For propane and butane, the hydrogen replaced can be on an end carbon or on an inner carbon, so **two** monosubstituted products are possible.

**Worked example 3.** Propane reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light. Draw the displayed formulae of the two possible monosubstituted organic products.

*Answer.*

```
1-chloropropane, CH3CH2CH2Cl      2-chloropropane, CH3CHClCH3

    H   H   H                         H   Cl  H
    |   |   |                         |   |   |
H - C - C - C - Cl                H - C - C - C - H
    |   |   |                         |   |   |
    H   H   H                         H   H   H
```

Each product has formula C₃H₇Cl. They are structural isomers of each other. Moving the chlorine to the other end carbon gives 1-chloropropane again, not a third product.

Butane similarly gives **1-chlorobutane**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl, and **2-chlorobutane**, CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃.

### Substitution compared with addition

| | Substitution (alkanes) | Addition (alkenes, section 11.5) |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | an atom replaces another atom | atoms add across the C=C bond |
| Number of products | two (organic product + HCl) | one |
| Example | CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl | C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ |

Counting the products is the quickest way to recognise the reaction type in an equation.

## Common mistakes

- Writing H₂ as the second product of substitution. It is **HCl**.
- Drawing a product with two chlorine atoms when the question asks for monosubstitution.
- Giving "sunlight", "heat" or "a catalyst" as the condition. Use **ultraviolet light**, and say it provides the activation energy.
- Claiming alkanes decolourise aqueous bromine.
- Unbalanced oxygen in combustion equations, or leaving a fraction in the final equation.
- Drawing chlorine with two bonds, or a carbon with five bonds, in a displayed formula.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **State**: "single covalent bonds"; "saturated hydrocarbons"; "ultraviolet light".
- **Describe** (substitution): name the reagent and condition, say one H atom is replaced by one Cl atom, and name both products.
- **Explain** (UV light): it provides the activation energy needed for the reaction to occur.
- **Draw**: every atom and bond; one Cl only; all carbons with four bonds.
