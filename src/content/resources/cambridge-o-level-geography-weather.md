Level Geography (2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The [Natural
Environment study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-natural-environment/)
gives the theme overview; this page concentrates on the practical,

have worked through it, test yourself with the [weather revision

## What 2.4 covers

Subtopic 2.4 is about **collecting and using weather data**. It is
not a unit on air masses or depressions. You need to be able to:

- describe and explain the characteristics, siting and use of a
  Stevenson Screen;
- use, describe and explain the function of the named instruments:
  rain gauge, maximum-minimum thermometer, wet-and-dry bulb
  thermometer (hygrometer), sunshine recorder, barometer, anemometer,
  wind vane and simple digital instruments;
- observe and describe the types and amount of cloud;
- make calculations using readings from these instruments;
- use and interpret graphs and other diagrams of weather and climate
  data.

Questions on 2.4 are usually short and precise: a photograph or
diagram of an instrument, a table of readings, or a graph. Marks go
to exact vocabulary and correct arithmetic, so this is one of the
most "learnable" parts of the syllabus.

## The Stevenson Screen in detail

The screen exists for one reason: to measure the **temperature and
humidity of the air in the shade**, so that readings from different
places and days can be fairly compared. Every feature serves that
purpose, and examiners want the feature *and* its reason.

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Painted white | Reflects the Sun's radiation, so the box itself does not heat up |
| Louvred (slatted) sides | Air flows freely through, but direct sunlight and rain are kept off the instruments |
| Raised on legs, about 1.2 m above the ground | Readings are not affected by heat radiated from the ground surface |
| Double or insulated roof | Reduces heating from the Sun overhead |
| Door opens away from the Sun (facing north in the northern hemisphere) | Sunlight does not fall on the thermometers when readings are taken |
| Sited on grass in an open space | Avoids heat stored and released by tarmac or concrete |
| Away from buildings and trees | Avoids shelter, shade and heat from walls, so readings are representative |

Inside the screen you would expect the maximum-minimum thermometer and
the wet-and-dry bulb thermometer. Instruments that must be exposed to
the weather, such as the rain gauge, anemometer, wind vane and
sunshine recorder, are kept outside it.

## The instruments and how each works

**Rain gauge.** A funnel collects rain into a container below. The
gauge is sunk into the ground with its rim standing above the surface
(often about 30 cm) so that rain does not splash in from the ground,
and it is placed in the open away from buildings and trees. The water
is poured into a measuring cylinder and read in millimetres, at the
same time every day.

**Maximum-minimum thermometer (Six's thermometer).** A U-shaped tube
in which the liquid pushes small metal markers (indexes). The markers
stay at the highest and lowest temperatures reached since the
thermometer was last reset; the reading is taken from the end of each
marker nearest the liquid. The markers are then reset, usually with a
magnet, ready for the next 24 hours.

**Wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (hygrometer).** Two thermometers side
by side. The bulb of one is wrapped in muslin dipped in water.
Evaporation from the muslin cools the wet bulb, so it normally reads
lower than the dry bulb. The *difference* between the two readings is
used, with a table, to find the **relative humidity**. A large
difference means dry air (much evaporation); no difference means the
air is saturated (100 per cent relative humidity).

**Sunshine recorder.** A glass sphere focuses the Sun's rays onto a
strip of card behind it. When the Sun shines, the focused rays burn a
line along the card; the total length of the burnt trace gives the
hours of sunshine.

**Barometer.** Measures air pressure in millibars (mb), also written as
hectopascals (hPa). An aneroid barometer contains a sealed metal box
with most of the air removed, which is squeezed by high pressure and
expands as pressure falls; the movement is shown by a needle. A
barograph records pressure continuously on a rotating drum. A
barometer can be kept indoors because air pressure is the same inside
a building as outside it.

**Anemometer.** Three or four cups on arms catch the wind and spin;
the rate of spin gives the wind speed, in kilometres per hour or
knots. It is mounted high on a mast, clear of obstacles that would
slow the wind.

**Wind vane.** An arrow on a pivot, with fixed compass points beneath.
The arrow points *into* the wind, that is, towards the direction the
wind is blowing **from**. Winds are always named by where they come
from, so a south-westerly blows from the south-west.

**Simple digital instruments.** Automatic weather stations and data
loggers record temperature, rainfall, pressure, humidity and wind
electronically at set intervals. They save labour and can record
through the night, but they need power, can fail without anyone
noticing, and still have to be sited correctly.

## Observing cloud

Cloud **amount** is estimated in **oktas**: the sky is imagined in
eighths, so 0 oktas is a clear sky and 8 oktas is a completely
overcast one. Cloud **type** is identified by shape and height:
cirrus (high, thin and wispy), cumulus (heaped, white, flat-based,
often in fair weather), cumulonimbus (towering, dark-based, bringing
heavy rain and thunderstorms) and stratus (a low, grey, even layer).

## Worked example 1: calculations from readings

A school weather station records the following over one week
(illustrative data).

| Day | Max (°C) | Min (°C) | Rainfall (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | 31 | 22 | 0 |
| Tue | 33 | 23 | 12 |
| Wed | 29 | 21 | 25 |
| Thu | 27 | 19 | 3 |
| Fri | 30 | 20 | 0 |
| Sat | 32 | 22 | 0 |
| Sun | 34 | 24 | 6 |

- **Daily temperature range** = maximum minus minimum. Tuesday:
  33 − 23 = **10 °C**.
- **Mean daily temperature** = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2. Wednesday:
  (29 + 21) ÷ 2 = **25 °C**.
- **Total rainfall** for the week = 0 + 12 + 25 + 3 + 0 + 0 + 6 =
  **46 mm**.
- **Mean daily rainfall** = 46 ÷ 7 = **6.6 mm** (to one decimal
  place). Note that this mean hides the fact that three days were
  completely dry.
- **Range for the whole week** = highest maximum minus lowest minimum
  = 34 − 19 = **15 °C**.

Always show the working and give the unit. A correct number without
°C or mm can lose the mark.

## Worked example 2: interpreting a reading

The dry bulb reads 28 °C and the wet bulb reads 28 °C. What does
this tell you? There is no difference, so no evaporation is taking
place from the muslin. The air must already be holding as much water
vapour as it can: relative humidity is 100 per cent. Fog, mist or
rain would be likely. If the next day the readings were 28 °C and
20 °C, the 8 °C difference would show much drier air.

## Reading graphs and diagrams

Expect line graphs of temperature, bar graphs of rainfall, combined
climate graphs and occasionally a **wind rose**, which shows how often
the wind blew from each direction (the longest arm is the most
frequent, or *prevailing*, wind). When describing any graph:

1. Give the overall pattern first (for example, "temperature rises
   through the week").
2. Quote the highest and lowest values with units and when they
   occurred.
3. Use data for any change ("rainfall fell from 25 mm on Wednesday to
   0 mm on Friday").
4. Point out any anomaly, such as a single very wet day.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a wind vane points to where the wind is going. It shows where
  the wind comes **from**.
- Writing "white so it does not get hot" without the idea of
  **reflecting** the Sun's radiation.
- Putting the rain gauge under a tree "for protection", or putting it
  inside the Stevenson Screen.
- Confusing the barometer (pressure) with the anemometer (wind speed).
- Calculating mean daily temperature as maximum minus minimum (that is
  the range).
- Describing a graph with no figures, or with figures but no units.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State / Name**: one precise word or phrase ("anemometer", "oktas").
- **Describe**: what the instrument looks like or what the data show,
  with values.
- **Explain**: give a reason, usually with "so that" or "because".
  For a two-mark siting question, make two separate points, each with
  its reason.
- **Calculate**: show the method, the answer and the unit.
- **Suggest**: apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation, such
  as why a school's readings might be unreliable (a gauge near a
  wall, a screen on tarmac, readings at different times each day).
