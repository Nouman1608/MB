## Section A

**1.** Define the term buffer stock. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term lead time. **[2]**

**3.** Kelso Stationers sells 180 boxes of printer paper a week. It holds a buffer stock of 360 boxes, its maximum inventory level is 1,800 boxes, and the supplier's lead time is 2 weeks. Calculate:

**(a)** the re-order level **[2]**

**(b)** the re-order quantity, assuming each delivery arrives as inventory reaches the buffer. **[2]**

**4.** Dunmore Tiles starts week 1 with 1,200 boxes of tiles (its maximum level). It sells 200 boxes a week. Its buffer stock is 200 boxes, its re-order level is 600 boxes, its re-order quantity is 1,000 boxes and the lead time is 2 weeks.

**(a)** Calculate Dunmore's inventory at the end of each of weeks 1 to 4. **[2]**

**(b)** State the week at the end of which an order is placed, and the inventory level immediately after that order arrives. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** influences on the amount of inventory a business holds. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** advantage of just in time inventory management for a florist. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why a chocolate maker might require its cocoa suppliers to hold third party accreditation. **[3]**

## Section B

**8.** Dunmore Tiles (Question 4) learns that its supplier's lead time will increase from 2 weeks to 3 weeks. Sales stay at 200 boxes a week and the re-order level stays at 600 boxes.

**(a)** Calculate Dunmore's inventory at the end of week 6, just before the order placed at the end of week 3 arrives. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the re-order level Dunmore would need so that inventory does not fall below its buffer stock. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** other way Dunmore could respond to the longer lead time. **[2]**

**9.** Fairlight Cases designs phone cases and buys them from manufacturers. It currently uses a single manufacturer. Analyse whether Fairlight should use several manufacturers instead. **[6]**

**10.** Marlowe Garments makes shirts from fabric imported by sea. A strike at the port delays fabric deliveries by four weeks. Explain **two** effects this supply chain issue could have on Marlowe. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** reasons why effective inventory control is valuable to a supermarket. **[4]**

**12.** Hartwell Engineering makes steel parts for agricultural machinery. It holds about six weeks of steel in inventory, which costs about $60,000 a year to store and insure. Its main steel supplier is 400 km away, and about one delivery in ten arrives late. Hartwell's customers, machinery manufacturers, fine Hartwell for late deliveries. A consultant recommends switching to just in time, with daily deliveries of steel. Evaluate whether Hartwell should adopt just in time. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum level of inventory a business keeps [1] as a safety margin against late deliveries or unexpected demand [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The time between placing an order with a supplier [1] and receiving the goods [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** 360 + (180 × 2) [1] = **720 boxes** [1].

**(b)** 1,800 − 360 [1] = **1,440 boxes** [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** Week 1: 1,000; week 2: 800; week 3: 600; week 4: 400. All four correct [2]; two or three correct [1].

**(b)** The order is placed at the end of **week 3**, when inventory reaches the re-order level of 600 [1]. It arrives at the end of week 5, when inventory has fallen to 200, so inventory rises to 200 + 1,000 = **1,200 boxes** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Any two of: how predictable demand is [1]; supplier lead time and reliability [1]; storage space and cost; whether goods perish or date; cash available; bulk discounts; whether the firm uses JIT or JIC. **[2]**

**6.** Flowers perish within days [1], so holding large stocks means many would wilt before being sold [1]. With JIT, fresh flowers arrive as orders come in, reducing waste and the cash tied up in stock [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Cocoa farming has been linked to concerns about child labour and deforestation, which customers may care about [1]. Independent accreditation gives evidence that suppliers meet labour and environmental standards [1], which supports the chocolate maker's ethical marketing claims and reduces the risk of a damaging scandal [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Weeks 4, 5 and 6 each use 200 boxes: 600 − (3 × 200) [1] = **0 boxes** [1].

**(b)** 200 + (200 × 3) [1] = **800 boxes** [1].

**(c)** Dunmore could increase its buffer stock [1], so that a late delivery or a busy week does not cause a stock-out, although this raises storage costs [1]. Alternatively, it could look for a supplier with a shorter lead time. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In part (a) the buffer has been used completely; any extra sales in those weeks would mean a stock-out. Saying so shows interpretation as well as calculation.

**9.** With one manufacturer, Fairlight is likely to get bulk discounts and a close relationship, which helps when launching new designs quickly [1]. But if that manufacturer has a breakdown or quality problem, Fairlight has no other source of cases [1], so it could miss a key season such as a new phone launch and lose sales [1]. Several manufacturers would spread this risk [1] and create competition that keeps prices down [1]. However, quality and colour may vary between manufacturers, and Fairlight would need more staff time to manage orders; a main manufacturer plus a back-up could balance cost and risk [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Production may stop once existing fabric runs out, leaving machinists idle while wages are still paid [1], which raises unit costs and lowers profit [1]. Marlowe may miss delivery dates for retailers' new-season orders [1], damaging its reputation and risking lost future orders [1]. **[4]**

**11.** It prevents stock-outs of everyday products [1], so shoppers do not switch to rival supermarkets when items are missing [1]. It avoids holding too much perishable food [1], cutting waste and the cash tied up in inventory [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit chains of reasoning and a judgement, for example:

- JIT would cut most of the $60,000 a year spent storing and insuring steel [1].
- It would free cash now tied up in six weeks of inventory, which could fund other investment [1].
- Less inventory means less space needed and less risk of steel being damaged in storage [1].
- However, JIT needs reliable, nearby suppliers, and Hartwell's supplier is 400 km away [1].
- One delivery in ten already arrives late; with daily deliveries and almost no inventory, a late delivery would stop production that day [1].
- Stopped production would make Hartwell late to its customers, triggering fines that could exceed the storage savings [1], and could cost it future contracts [1].
- Daily deliveries over 400 km raise transport costs and may lose bulk-buying discounts [1].
- Agricultural machinery demand is seasonal, so steady daily orders may not match Hartwell's needs [1].
- Hartwell could adopt JIT partly, cutting inventory from six weeks to one or two while keeping a buffer [1], or find a closer supplier first [1].
- Judgement: Hartwell should not switch to full JIT yet, because its supplier is too distant and unreliable and late deliveries are penalised; it should reduce inventory gradually while improving supplier reliability [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring lead time from the delivery back to the buffer rather than from the order.
- Forgetting that a delivery adds the re-order quantity to whatever inventory remains.
- Recommending more inventory without mentioning its cost.
- Analysing single sourcing without linking the risk to the business's own products or seasons.
- Evaluating JIT without using the supplier's distance and reliability given in the case.
