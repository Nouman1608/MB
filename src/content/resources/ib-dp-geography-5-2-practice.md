## Section A

**1.** Define the term externality, with reference to a sports stadium. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by the tourist-historic city. **[2]**

**3.** State two problems caused by visitor pressure at a rural honeypot site. **[2]**

**4.** Suggest two reasons why new stadiums are often built on the edge of cities. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one way in which second-home ownership affects a rural village. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows bed-nights at an invented mountain resort.

| Season | Bed-nights (thousands) |
|---|---|
| Winter (ski season) | 180 |
| Spring | 25 |
| Summer | 70 |
| Autumn | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the winter share of total bed-nights. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the peak-to-trough ratio. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest two strategies the resort could use to reduce its seasonality. **[2]**

**7.** A survey of visitors to an invented national park gives the following.

| Origin zone | Population | Visitors per year | Visitors per 1,000 residents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 30 km | 150,000 | 60,000 | |
| 30-100 km | 900,000 | 135,000 | |
| Over 100 km | 4,000,000 | 200,000 | |

**(a)** Complete the final column. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the data support distance decay even though the largest number of visitors comes from the furthest zone. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why the costs and benefits of tourism are unevenly distributed within a city. **[4]**

**9.** Suggest how a national government could use tourism to support a peripheral region, and outline one risk of this strategy. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why spreading visitors to alternative rural sites may not solve the problems of honeypot pressure. **[3]**

## Section C

**11.** Examine the factors that influence the location of sports facilities at the local and national scale. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that the benefits of tourism to a host region outweigh its costs. Refer to one or more named places. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** An externality is an effect of an activity on people who are not directly involved in it [1], such as traffic, noise and litter for residents near a stadium, or extra trade for nearby cafes on match days [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A model of European cities in which the historic core and the modern commercial centre overlap [1] to form a compact zone of monuments, museums, hotels and restaurants used mainly by visitors [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two, for example: footpath erosion [1]; traffic congestion and parking on narrow roads [1]; litter [1]; disturbance of livestock or wildlife [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, for example: land is cheaper and large sites are available [1]; good road access near motorway junctions widens the catchment [1]; fewer residents nearby to object to noise and traffic [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**5.** Second-home buyers raise house prices beyond what local people can afford [1]; homes stand empty out of season, so shops, schools and bus services lose users and may close [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Total = 180 + 25 + 70 + 15 = 290 thousand [1]. Winter share = 180 / 290 x 100 = **62.1%** [1].

**(b)** 180 / 15 = **12** [1].

**(c)** Any two, for example: develop summer activities such as mountain biking and hiking [1]; host festivals or conferences in spring and autumn [1]; market off-season breaks to retired visitors [1]. Maximum [2]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Under 30 km: 60,000 / 150,000 x 1,000 = **400**; 30-100 km: 135,000 / 900,000 x 1,000 = **150**; over 100 km: 200,000 / 4,000,000 x 1,000 = **50**. All three correct [2]; two correct [1].

**(b)** The furthest zone contains by far the largest population, so its absolute visitor numbers are high [1], but the visit rate falls from 400 to 50 per 1,000 residents with distance, which is distance decay [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Tourism clusters in a few zones, such as the historic core and waterfront, so pressure on housing, prices and public space is concentrated there [1]. Residents of these zones bear crowding and rising rents, and may be displaced by short-term rentals [1]. Benefits go to hotel owners, restaurant owners and property owners, some of whom live elsewhere [1]. Residents a short distance away may see little effect either way [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Strategy, for example: invest in transport access, protected-area status and accommodation to attract visitors to an area of scenery or heritage [1], creating jobs in a region with few other industries and spreading income away from the capital [1] [1]. Risk, for example: dependence on seasonal, low-wage work that is exposed to shocks [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Spreading demand reduces pressure at the main site [1], but it brings visitors, traffic and erosion to quieter sites that may be less able to cope [1], and it can reduce the solitude that made those sites attractive, so the total impact may be displaced rather than reduced [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Threshold and hierarchy:** higher-order facilities need larger populations, so they locate in or near large cities; local facilities are spread widely [1] [1].
- **Land cost and site size:** large modern stadiums need extensive sites, often on the urban edge or former industrial land [1].
- **Accessibility:** motorway junctions and rail stations widen the catchment [1].
- **Planning and public funding:** venues are often part of regeneration schemes, as in Stratford for London 2012 [1].
- **Tradition and identity:** clubs often stay close to their historic home; Arsenal moved only a short distance from Highbury to the Emirates Stadium in 2006 [1].
- **Physical geography:** snow, mountains and coast determine where skiing, climbing or water sports facilities are possible [1].
- **National scale:** national stadiums and training centres are usually in capitals or the largest cities for access and prestige [1].
- **Externalities:** local opposition to noise and traffic can block sites [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, threshold and accessibility explain most locations, but politics and identity often decide the final site [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Economic benefits:** jobs, income, multiplier effects and foreign exchange [1]; investment in transport and services [1].
- **Economic costs:** leakage, seasonal low-wage work and price inflation [1] [1].
- **Social effects:** cultural exchange and pride, against crowding, commodification and displacement, for example resident protests in crowded historic cities [1].
- **Environmental effects:** conservation funding, against erosion, habitat pressure and emissions [1].
- **Distribution:** benefits often go to owners and investors, costs to residents of busy zones [1].
- **Change over time:** Doxey's index suggests attitudes harden as numbers grow; Butler suggests benefits fall at stagnation [1].
- **Use of named places:** accurate detail from one or more located examples [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, benefits outweigh costs where leakage is low and growth is managed within carrying capacity, but not where tourism is concentrated and unmanaged [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* for both essays, examiners reward answers that sort effects by stakeholder and by scale and reach a conditional judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using visitor counts rather than rates to claim distance decay.
- Treating "the city" or "the community" as one group.
- Forgetting that local strategies can displace rather than reduce pressure.
- Giving an evaluation with no named place or no final judgement.
