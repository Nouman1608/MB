These questions cover section **4.5.6 Forces and motion** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463) specification,
examined on Paper 2 at both tiers. Parts 8(b), 13(b) and 13(c) test content marked (HT only) and are labelled
**Higher tier only**. Take g = 9.8 N/kg. A calculator is allowed throughout.

Learn the content first in the
[forces and motion study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-forces-and-motion-aqa-gcse/) and the
[forces and motion revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-forces-and-motion-aqa-gcse-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between distance and displacement. **[2]**

**2.** Give a typical value for the speed of a person cycling and for the speed of sound in air. **[2]**

**3.** A walker covers 3.6 km in 40 minutes. Calculate her average speed in m/s. **[3]**

**4.** A sprinter accelerates from rest to 9.0 m/s in 3.0 s. Calculate her acceleration. **[2]**

**5.** State Newton's first law of motion. **[2]**

**6.** A trolley of mass 2.5 kg accelerates at 1.2 m/s². Calculate the resultant force on it. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A dog walker records the distance she has walked from home at different times.

| Time (s) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance (m) | 0 | 40 | 80 | 80 | 140 |

**(a)** Calculate her speed during the first 40 s. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe her motion between 40 s and 60 s. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate her speed between 60 s and 80 s. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate her average speed for the whole 80 s. **[2]**

**8.** A lift starts from rest and accelerates uniformly to 3.0 m/s in 2.0 s. It then moves at 3.0 m/s for
8.0 s, and finally decelerates uniformly to rest in 3.0 s.

**(a)** Calculate the acceleration of the lift in the first 2.0 s. **[2]**
**(b)** (Higher tier only) Use the area under the velocity–time graph to calculate the total distance
travelled by the lift. **[3]**

**9.** A car accelerates uniformly from 8.0 m/s to 22 m/s over a distance of 150 m.

**(a)** Calculate the acceleration of the car. **[3]**
**(b)** The car's mass is 1100 kg and the resistive forces on it total 800 N. Calculate the driving force.
**[3]**

**10.** A small steel ball is dropped into a tall tube of oil. Its velocity–time graph rises steeply at first,
then curves and levels off at 0.25 m/s.

**(a)** Describe and explain the shape of the graph in terms of the forces on the ball. **[4]**
**(b)** State the resultant force on the ball when it moves at 0.25 m/s. **[1]**

**11.** The table shows estimates for a car. The driver's reaction time is 0.8 s and the braking deceleration
is 6.5 m/s².

| Speed (m/s) | Thinking distance (m) | Braking distance (m) | Stopping distance (m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | 10.4 | 13.0 | 23.4 |
| 22 | ? | ? | ? |
| 31 | 24.8 | 73.9 | 98.7 |

**(a)** Calculate the thinking distance at 22 m/s. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the braking distance at 22 m/s. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why braking distance increases much more than thinking distance as speed increases. **[2]**
**(d)** State how a wet road would change the values in the table. **[1]**

**12.** Students measure reaction times by catching a dropped ruler.

**(a)** One student catches the ruler after it has fallen 0.15 m from rest. Calculate the student's reaction
time. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how the students could test whether a distraction, such as a phone conversation, affects
reaction time. **[2]**

**13.** A loaded lorry of mass 32 000 kg brakes uniformly from 20 m/s to rest in 80 m.

**(a)** Calculate the deceleration of the lorry. **[2]**
**(b)** (Higher tier only) Estimate the braking force. **[2]**
**(c)** (Higher tier only) Use the idea of inertial mass to explain why the lorry needs a much larger braking force than a car to
stop in the same distance from the same speed. **[2]**

**14.** A swimmer pushes the water backwards with her hands and moves forwards. Explain this using Newton's
third and second laws. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Distance is how far an object moves and has no direction (a scalar) [1]; displacement is the
straight-line distance from start to finish together with its direction (a vector) [1]

**2.** Cycling: about 6 m/s [1]; sound in air: about 330 m/s [1]

**3.** 3.6 km = 3600 m and 40 min = 2400 s [1]; speed = 3600 / 2400 [1]; = **1.5 m/s** [1]

**4.** a = Δv / t = 9.0 / 3.0 [1]; = **3.0 m/s²** [1]

**5.** If the resultant force on an object is zero, a stationary object stays stationary [1]; and a moving
object continues to move at the same speed in the same direction (the same velocity) [1]

**6.** F = m a = 2.5 × 1.2 [1]; = **3.0 N** [1]

**7. (a)** speed = 80 / 40 [1]; = **2.0 m/s** [1]
**(b)** She is stationary (the distance does not change) [1]
**(c)** (140 − 80) / 20 = **3.0 m/s** [1]
**(d)** 140 / 80 [1]; = **1.75 m/s** (1.8 m/s) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Average speed uses the total time, including the time spent standing still.

**8. (a)** a = 3.0 / 2.0 [1]; = **1.5 m/s²** [1]
**(b)** ½ × 2.0 × 3.0 = 3.0 m and 8.0 × 3.0 = 24 m [1]; ½ × 3.0 × 3.0 = 4.5 m [1]; total = **31.5 m** [1]

**9. (a)** 22² − 8.0² = 2 × a × 150 [1]; 484 − 64 = 420 = 300 a [1]; a = **1.4 m/s²** [1]
**(b)** Resultant force = 1100 × 1.4 = 1540 N [1]; driving force − 800 = 1540 [1]; driving force =
**2340 N** [1]
*Examiner insight:* F = ma gives the resultant force; the driving force must also overcome the resistive
forces.

**10. (a)** At first the weight is much bigger than the drag from the oil, so there is a large resultant
force and the ball accelerates (steep start) [1]; as the speed increases, the drag increases [1]; the
resultant force decreases, so the acceleration decreases and the gradient of the graph falls [1]; when the
drag equals the weight, the resultant force is zero and the ball moves at its terminal velocity, so the
graph is horizontal [1]
**(b)** Zero [1]

**11. (a)** 0.8 × 22 = **17.6 m** [1]
**(b)** 22² ÷ (2 × 6.5) = 484 ÷ 13 [1]; = **37 m** (37.2 m) [1]
**(c)** Thinking distance is proportional to speed [1]; braking distance is proportional to speed squared,
for the same braking force, so it grows much faster [1]
**(d)** The braking distances, and so the stopping distances, would be larger (thinking distances would not
change) [1]

**12. (a)** v² = 2 × 9.8 × 0.15 = 2.94, so v = 1.71 m/s [1]; t = Δv / a = 1.71 / 9.8 [1]; = **0.17 s** [1]
**(b)** Measure each student's reaction time many times without the distraction and calculate a mean [1];
repeat with the distraction, keeping other factors the same (same dropper, same hand), and compare the
means [1]

**13. (a)** 0 − 20² = 2 × a × 80 [1]; a = −400 / 160 = −2.5 m/s², so the deceleration is **2.5 m/s²** [1]
**(b)** F = m a = 32 000 × 2.5 [1]; ≈ **80 000 N** [1]
**(c)** The lorry has a much larger inertial mass, so it is much more difficult to change its velocity [1];
the same deceleration needs a force in proportion to the mass, F = m a, so the braking force must be much
larger [1]

**14.** The swimmer's hands push backwards on the water; by Newton's third law the water pushes forwards on
the swimmer with an equal and opposite force [1]; this forward force is larger than the drag on her, so
there is a resultant force forwards [1]; by Newton's second law the resultant force makes her accelerate
forwards (or keeps her moving against the drag) [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving km and minutes unconverted.
- Using the driving force in F = ma when resistive forces are given.
- Forgetting to take the square root after v² − u² = 2as.
- Describing a terminal velocity graph without explaining the forces.
- Saying a wet road increases thinking distance.
- Third-law answers that put both forces on the swimmer.
