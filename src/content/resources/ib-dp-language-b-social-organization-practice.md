## Questions

**1.** State the two possible questions the subject brief gives for Social organization, and name
the optional recommended topic that best fits a text about a new law on cycling helmets. **[3]**

### Passage A — notice (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Hollins Row Community Garden: rules for plot holders**
>
> The garden is run by its members for its members. These rules were agreed at the annual meeting in
> March and apply to all 40 plots.
>
> 1. Plot holders must attend at least two of the four shared work days each year.
> 2. Children are welcome but must be supervised by an adult at all times.
> 3. Bonfires are not permitted under any circumstances.
> 4. Members may share surplus produce on the stall by the gate. Selling it is not allowed.
> 5. You do not have to grow vegetables: flowers and herbs are equally welcome.
> 6. A plot left untended for eight weeks will be offered to the next person on the waiting list,
>    unless the holder has informed the committee of illness or absence.
>
> Every member has one vote at the annual meeting.

**2.** Answer briefly, using Passage A.

**(a)** When were the rules agreed? **[1]**
**(b)** How many shared work days must a plot holder attend each year? **[1]**
**(c)** Who decides the rules of the garden? **[1]**

**3.** For each rule, state whether it expresses obligation, absence of obligation, permission or
prohibition.

**(a)** Rule 3 **[1]**
**(b)** The first sentence of rule 4 **[1]**
**(c)** Rule 5 **[1]**

**4.** Decide whether each statement is true or false, and justify your answer with a short
quotation from Passage A.

**(a)** Members can sell vegetables they do not need at the gate. **[2]**
**(b)** A plot holder who tells the committee she is ill will not lose her plot after eight weeks. **[2]**

**5.** Identify two features of formal or impersonal language in Passage A and explain the effect of
each. **[4]**

### Passage B — school magazine opinion piece (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Volunteering by order? Yes, please**
>
> From next year, every student at our school will have to complete twenty hours of community service
> before the end of Year 12. Some of my classmates are furious. "Helping is only real if you choose
> it," one told me. I understand the argument, but I disagree.
>
> Last spring I spent my Saturday mornings sorting tins at the food bank on Mill Street. I went because my brother needed a lift there, not because I was
> a saint. Yet by June I knew the names of half the volunteers and had changed my mind about who uses
> a food bank.
>
> A reason that had nothing to do with generosity got me through the door. What kept me there was the
> people. A rule could do the same for others. Schools already require us to learn algebra, whether we
> like it or not; learning how our own town works seems at least as important.
>
> Of course, the hours must be flexible. Students who care for a relative or work at weekends should
> be able to count that time. A rule that ignores their lives would teach the opposite of what it
> intends.

**6.** Answer briefly, using Passage B.

**(a)** How many hours of community service will each student have to complete? **[1]**
**(b)** Why did the writer first go to the food bank? **[1]**

**7.** State the writer's position on compulsory community service and explain how the writer
supports it. Use quotations. **[4]**

### Passage C — radio transcript (original text written for Marlbridge)

Have someone read this aloud once; answer without looking.

> PRESENTER: Today we're talking to Tomás, 19, who works at Green Door, a café owned by the people
> who work in it.
>
> TOMÁS: That's right. There are eleven of us, and each of us owns an equal share. There's no
> manager. Every Monday we meet for an hour before opening and decide the week's rota together.
>
> PRESENTER: Doesn't that take forever?
>
> TOMÁS: At first, yes. My first meeting lasted almost three hours because we couldn't agree about
> the new menu. Now we vote if we haven't agreed after twenty minutes. The hardest part for me was
> learning to say what I thought. In my last job I just did what I was told.
>
> PRESENTER: And would you go back to that?
>
> TOMÁS: Honestly? Some days I miss having someone else decide. But here, when something goes right,
> the credit is ours.

**8.** Answer briefly, using Passage C.

**(a)** How many people own the café? **[1]**
**(b)** When is the week's rota decided? **[1]**
**(c)** What happens if the group has not agreed after twenty minutes? **[1]**

**9.** Explain how Green Door shows people who "organize themselves" rather than being organized,
and identify one difficulty Tomás mentions. **[3]**

- A formal letter to your head teacher about how the new community-service rule should work.
- An article for a local newspaper on whether young people should have more say in local decisions.
- A speech to younger students introducing the community-service programme.

**11.** Individual oral preparation (SL). Your stimulus is a photograph of a public meeting in a town
hall: a teenager speaks at a microphone while adults sit in rows, and a banner above the stage reads
"Have your say: new bus routes" in the target language. Plan the presentation you would give, linked

## Answers

**1.** "What is the individual's role in the community?" [1] "What role do rules and regulations
play in the formation of a society?" [1] The topic is **law and order**. [1]
*Examiner insight:* A close paraphrase of a possible question is enough; an optional topic in its
place earns nothing.

**2. (a)** **At the annual meeting in March.** [1]
**(b)** **At least two** (of the four). [1]
**(c)** **The members**, who agree them at the annual meeting (one vote each). [1]
*Examiner insight:* For (b), "two" without "at least" turns a minimum into an exact number.

**3. (a)** **Prohibition.** [1]
**(b)** **Permission.** [1]
**(c)** **Absence of obligation.** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Rule 5 is the trap: "do not have to" is not a ban.

**4. (a)** **False** [1] — "Selling it is not allowed." [1]
**(b)** **True** [1] — "unless the holder has informed the committee of illness or absence". [1]
*Examiner insight:* The justification must prove the answer; for (b), quoting the rule without its
"unless" clause proves the opposite.

**5.** Model answer (two features, each identified and explained):
- **Passive** "were agreed at the annual meeting" [1]: presents the rules as a shared decision of
  the group, not one person's orders. [1]
- **Absolute phrase** "under any circumstances" [1]: leaves no room for exceptions, which shows how
  seriously the group treats the ban on bonfires. [1]

Also creditworthy: third-person subjects ("Plot holders must attend"), which apply each rule equally
to every member.
*Examiner insight:* The explanation mark needs the effect in this text; "the passive is formal" is
only a label.

**6. (a)** **Twenty.** [1]
**(b)** Because **the writer's brother needed a lift there** (not out of generosity). [1]
*Examiner insight:* "To help people" is wrong for (b): answer from the text, not from what seems
likely.

**7.** Position: the writer **supports the rule, with conditions**: "I understand the argument, but I
disagree." [1] Personal experience: going for a practical reason still led to real involvement,
"What kept me there was the people." [1] Comparison with other school requirements: "Schools already
require us to learn algebra, whether we like it or not." [1] Concession: "the hours must be
flexible", so the rule does not ignore students who care for a relative or work. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name each strategy (experience, comparison, concession) before
quoting; weak answers string quotations together.

**8. (a)** **Eleven.** [1]
**(b)** **Every Monday**, in an hour before opening. [1]
**(c)** **They vote.** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Three hours" is a distractor: it was his first meeting, not the current
system.

**9.** The workers **own equal shares and there is no manager**: "There's no manager." [1] They
**make decisions together**, meeting weekly and voting if they cannot agree. [1] Difficulty: **long
meetings at first**, **learning to say what he thought**, or **sometimes missing having someone else
decide** (any one). [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs the link to the guiding principle; facts about the café alone
earn only part of the marks.

**10.** The real Paper 1 is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point. These
points are **indicative**; any valid equivalent earns the point.

Model plan for the formal letter:

```
Formal "you" throughout; set opening and closing formulas
1. Who I am; why I am writing (the new 20-hour rule)
2. Support for the aim: what volunteering can teach
3. Concern: students with caring duties or weekend jobs
4. Proposal: count caring and paid work; spread hours over two years
5. Hypothetical: if the rule were flexible, more students would…
6. Polite request for a meeting with the student council; closing formula
```

Model opening: *"Dear Mrs Okafor, I am writing on behalf of the student council about the new
community-service requirement. We support its aim, but would like to suggest how it could work for
every student."*

Indicative points: text-type conventions (formal opening and closing) [1]; formal "you" kept
throughout [1]; purpose stated in the first paragraph [1]; clear audience awareness [1]; logical
paragraphing [1]; the other side acknowledged [1]; a concrete proposal [1]; polite request forms
[1]; accurate rule language (must, may, don't have to) [1]; links to a possible question on the
individual's role or on rules [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong responses keep the formal register to the last line; a friendly sign-off
on a formal letter loses the sense of audience.

**11.** Indicative points (the real oral is assessed holistically against criteria your teacher will
share): a **brief description**: the meeting, the speaker, the banner [1]; a **link to Social
organization**, e.g. community or social engagement [1]; the **direction**: a council organizing a
consultation, and a citizen choosing to take part [1]; the **individual's role**: why a young person's
view on bus routes matters [1]; the **role of rules**: who makes the final decision and how [1]; a
specific **cultural comparison** from the target-language culture [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strong orals describe briefly and interpret at length; describing every person in
the photograph is not discussion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating "don't have to" as a ban, or "must not" as optional.
- Dropping a limiting word (*at least*, *only*, *unless*) from a short answer.
- Justifying true/false with a quotation that does not prove the point.
- Labelling a feature ("passive", "formal") without explaining its effect in the text.
- Crediting the writer with a view that belongs to someone quoted.
- Mixing formal and informal register in a letter to an institution.
