## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aridity | Long-term water deficit: potential evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation |
| Aridity index (UNEP) | P/PET. Hyper-arid below 0.05; arid 0.05-0.20; semi-arid 0.20-0.50; dry sub-humid 0.50-0.65 |
| Subtropical high | Belt of sinking, warming, drying air near 20-30 degrees latitude (descending limb of the Hadley cell) |
| Rain shadow | Dry area on the lee side of mountains where descending air warms and dries |
| Temperature inversion | Warm air above cool air; blocks convection (caused over cold currents) |
| Diurnal range | Difference between day maximum and night minimum temperature |
| Thermal fracture | Rock breakdown from repeated heating and cooling stress |
| Exfoliation | Peeling of outer rock shells; moisture usually needed |
| Salt weathering | Crystal growth and hydration of salts in pores prise grains apart |
| Deflation | Wind removal of loose fine particles |
| Abrasion (corrasion) | Wind-blown sand wearing rock, strongest near the ground |
| Saltation | Bouncing movement of sand grains |
| Traction (surface creep) | Rolling and pushing of larger grains along the surface |
| Suspension | Fine dust carried within the air |
| Ephemeral stream | Flows only after rain |
| Episodic rainfall | Rare, irregular, intense storms |
| Sheet flood | Unconfined water spreading across a gentle surface |
| Flash flood | Sudden, short, high-energy flow in a channel |
| Bahada | Merged alluvial fans along a mountain front |
| Playa | Flat floor of an enclosed basin, briefly flooded, then dries to salt or clay |
| Pluvial | Wetter period in the past, especially in the Pleistocene |
| Physiological drought | Water is present but plants cannot absorb it, e.g. in salty soil |
| Salinisation | Build-up of salts at the surface after capillary rise and evaporation |
| Desertification | Land degradation in drylands from climatic variations and human activities (UNCCD) |

## Processes and landforms at a glance

| Landform | Main process | Key point to explain |
|---|---|---|
| Barchan dune | Aeolian deposition | One wind direction, limited sand; horns point downwind |
| Seif (linear) dune | Aeolian deposition | Two alternating wind directions |
| Yardang | Abrasion and deflation | Long ridge parallel to wind in soft rock or sediment |
| Zeugen | Abrasion undercutting | Hard cap rock over softer rock; jointing helps |
| Wadi / arroyo | Flash flood erosion | Steep sides, flat floor; may be inherited from pluvials |
| Alluvial fan | Fluvial deposition | Channel loses gradient and confinement at mountain front |
| Pediment | Sheet flood erosion and slope retreat | Gentle bedrock slope; piedmont angle |
| Playa / salt lake | Deposition and evaporation | Lowest point of enclosed basin |
| Inselberg | Deep weathering and stripping, or scarp retreat | Isolated steep hill on a plain, e.g. Uluru |

## Studies and examples at a glance

| Who or what | What | Key detail | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atacama and Namib | Cold-current deserts | Humboldt and Benguela currents create inversions and fog | Shows several causes acting together, not ocean currents alone |
| Griggs (1936) | Laboratory test of insolation weathering | Dry heating and cooling did not break granite; adding water did | Lab samples were small and time was compressed, so results are indicative only |
| Lake Bonneville, Utah | Pleistocene pluvial lake | About 30,000 to 13,000 years ago; over 300 m deep; shorelines remain | Clear proof of past wetter climate in a now dry basin |
| Mega-Chad | Holocene humid phase in the Sahara | Lake Chad was once far larger; shorelines visible from satellites | Shows wetter phases occurred after the Pleistocene too |
| Charney (1975) | Albedo hypothesis | Loss of vegetation raises albedo, which may reduce rainfall | Later research linked Sahel rainfall mainly to ocean temperatures, so vegetation is now seen as an amplifier, not the main cause |
| FMNR, Niger | Natural regeneration from stumps | Began in Maradi in 1983; about five million hectares by 2004 | Depends on secure rights to trees |
| Zaï pits, Burkina Faso | Water harvesting pits with manure | Yacouba Sawadogo, Yatenga, from the 1980s | Heavy labour; manure in short supply |
| Great Green Wall | African Union initiative, 2007 | Target 100 million hectares by 2030 | 2020 UN report: about 4% of target reached |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Arid versus semi-arid.** Arid: P/PET 0.05-0.20, very sparse plants. Semi-arid: 0.20-0.50, grass and scrub, a short wet season, and rain-fed farming possible in good years.
- **Aridity versus drought.** Aridity is permanent; drought is a temporary shortfall.
- **Thermal fracture versus exfoliation.** Thermal fracture breaks rock into grains or blocks; exfoliation peels off shells.
- **Saltation versus traction.** Saltation bounces; traction rolls or creeps.
- **Sheet flood versus flash flood.** Sheet flood is unconfined; flash flood is in a channel.
- **Alluvial fan versus bahada.** A bahada is many fans joined together.
- **Pediment versus bahada.** A pediment is an erosional bedrock surface; a bahada is a depositional surface of sediment.
- **Physical versus physiological drought.** No water versus unusable water.
- **Natural versus human causes of desertification.** Drought starts degradation; land use decides whether it becomes lasting.

## Vegetation and soils in brief

- **Low biomass and productivity.** Water, not heat or light, is the limiting factor. Biodiversity is lower than in humid biomes.
- **Limited nutrient cycling.** Little litter falls, decomposers work slowly in dry soil, and nutrient stores are small.
- **Fragility.** Damaged plant cover and soil crusts recover slowly, sometimes over decades.
- **Adaptations.** Succulents store water and open stomata at night; phreatophytes send roots deep to groundwater; ephemerals survive as dormant seeds; xerophytes have small, waxy or spiny leaves; halophytes tolerate salt. Pale, hairy surfaces and upright leaves limit heating.
- **Salinisation.** Capillary rise plus evaporation leaves salts at the surface; over-irrigation without drainage raises the water table and speeds this up.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Assess the extent to which landforms in hot arid environments are the result of past rather than present processes."

1. **Introduction.** Define aeolian and fluvial processes and pluvials. State your line: many large landforms are inherited, but present processes still reshape them.
2. **Evidence for past processes.** Lake Bonneville shorelines and salt flats; Mega-Chad shorelines; oversized wadi networks; vegetated, inactive dunes.
3. **Present fluvial processes.** Episodic rain, flash floods deepening wadis, sheet floods on pediments, fans still building.
4. **Present aeolian processes.** Active barchans, yardangs, abrasion near the ground, deflation hollows.
5. **Weathering.** Salt weathering and thermal fracture supply sediment today.
6. **Evaluation.** The balance varies by landform: playas and fan size reflect past wetness, while dunes may be active now. Dating evidence is often uncertain.
7. **Conclusion.** Weigh the evidence and answer "to what extent" directly.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the P/PET range for a semi-arid area.
2. Why does sinking air near 30 degrees latitude produce few clouds?
3. Name the cold current off the Namib Desert.
4. What does salt weathering need to be most active?
5. Which wind transport process moves grains by bouncing?
6. Why is abrasion strongest near the ground?
7. What is the difference between a pediment and a bahada?
8. Give one piece of evidence for Pleistocene pluvials.
9. Define physiological drought.
10. Explain how capillary action leads to salinisation.
11. Give one strength and one limitation of farmer-managed natural regeneration.
12. Why does the Great Green Wall now favour a "mosaic" approach?

### Answers

1. 0.20 to 0.50.
2. Air warms as it sinks, so its relative humidity falls and condensation cannot happen.
3. The Benguela Current.
4. A supply of salty water (groundwater, dew or spray) and high evaporation.
5. Saltation.
6. Saltating sand travels mostly within a metre or two of the surface.
7. A pediment is a gently sloping bedrock surface formed by erosion; a bahada is a depositional slope of merged alluvial fans.
8. Old lake shorelines around the Bonneville basin in Utah (or the Bonneville Salt Flats).
9. Water is present but plants cannot take it up, because salty soil water reverses osmosis.
10. Evaporation pulls water up through soil pores; when it evaporates, dissolved salts are left at the surface.
11. Strength: cheap and uses existing roots. Limitation: needs secure rights to trees and community rules.
12. Desert margins shift with rainfall, so a fixed tree belt made little sense; the initiative now promotes water harvesting, protecting existing vegetation and improved local land use.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing causes of aridity without linking each one to a named desert.
- Saying deserts have "no rain" and ignoring flash floods and episodic rainfall.
- Explaining exfoliation by heat alone without noting moisture.
- Giving landform descriptions with no process sequence (for example, not saying why the fan's channel loses energy).
- Drawing unlabelled diagrams; label the wind direction, slip face and horns on a barchan sketch.
- Confusing pediments with bahadas, or playas with oases.
- Treating desertification as only overgrazing, with no natural factor or interaction.
- Describing the case study but not judging whether solutions are sustainable.
- Writing a one-sided essay when the question asks "to what extent" or "assess".
