## Section A

**1.** Define the term *rural–urban fringe*. **[1]**

**2.** State two characteristics of the buildings in a CBD. **[2]**

**3.** Give two reasons why inner-city areas in many high-income countries
contain old factories and high-density housing. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between a *brownfield site* and a *greenfield site*. **[2]**

**5.** Name **two** types of pollution found in urban areas, and give one
cause of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** A transect is taken from the centre of a city to its edge. The
information below is illustrative.

| Distance from centre (km) | Average building height (storeys) | Main land use |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 18 | Offices and shops |
| 2 | 4 | Terraced housing and workshops |
| 5 | 2 | Semi-detached housing |
| 9 | 1 | Retail park and farmland |

**(a)** Describe how building height changes with distance from the centre.
Use data in your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why building height changes in this way. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why a retail park is located 9 km from the centre. **[2]**

**7.** In many cities in low-income countries, high-income housing is close to
the CBD while squatter settlements are found on the city edge.

**(a)** Suggest two reasons why high-income groups live close to the CBD.
**[2]**
**(b)** Explain why squatter settlements often grow on steep slopes, flood
plains or land beside railway lines. **[3]**

**8.** A city is growing outwards onto surrounding farmland.

**(a)** Explain **three** effects of this urban sprawl. **[6]**
**(b)** Describe **one** planning strategy that could reduce urban sprawl and
state one limitation of it. **[3]**

**9.** Two groups disagree about the use of an area of open parkland on the
edge of a city. A developer wants to build 400 new homes; local residents want
it to remain as green space.

**(a)** Give **one** argument each group could use. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how planners could reduce this conflict. **[2]**

**10.** For a named urban area you have studied, explain the causes of **one**
urban problem and evaluate attempts to solve it. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The zone at the edge of a built-up area where urban and rural land
uses meet and mix [1].

**2.** Any two of: tall or high-rise buildings [1]; high density, closely
packed [1]; mainly used for shops and offices rather than housing [1]; often
modern office blocks alongside older buildings [1].

**3.** Any two of: the area was built during industrialisation, when factories
needed to be close to rivers, canals or railways near the old centre [1];
workers had to live within walking distance of the factories [1]; housing was
built cheaply and densely to fit many workers on limited land [1].

**4.** A brownfield site has been built on before (often derelict, such as an
old factory) and can be redeveloped [1]; a greenfield site has never been
built on, usually farmland or countryside [1].

**5.** Two of the following, 1 mark for the type and 1 for a matching cause:
air pollution from vehicle exhausts or factory emissions [2]; noise pollution
from traffic, aircraft or construction [2]; water pollution from untreated
sewage or industrial waste [2]; visual pollution from litter, derelict
buildings or advertising hoardings [2].

**6. (a)** Building height decreases with distance from the centre [1], from
18 storeys at 0.5 km to 1 storey at 9 km [1].
**(b)** The centre is the most accessible point, where transport routes meet
[1], so demand for land is greatest and land values are highest there [1];
users therefore build upwards to get the most floor space from expensive land,
whereas cheaper land further out allows low, spread-out buildings [1].
**(c)** Any two of: land is cheaper so large stores and car parks can be built
[1]; access from ring roads or main roads for car-borne shoppers [1]; less
congestion than the city centre [1]; room for future expansion [1].

**7. (a)** Any two of: close to jobs in offices and services in the CBD [1];
better access to schools, hospitals and shops [1]; better security and
infrastructure such as reliable water and electricity [1]; avoids long
commutes on congested roads [1].
**(b)** Migrants cannot afford to buy or rent legal housing [1], so they
occupy land that no one else wants or that is not being developed [1]; such
land is often hazardous or unsuitable for formal building, so landowners or
authorities are less likely to evict them quickly; it may also be close to
jobs or transport [1].

**8. (a)** Three explained effects, 2 marks each (1 for the effect, 1 for
development): loss of farmland, so less local food production and lost farm
jobs [2]; loss of wildlife habitats and green space, reducing biodiversity and
recreation [2]; more impermeable surfaces, so rainwater runs off faster and
flood risk increases [2]; longer commuting distances, more car use, congestion
and air pollution [2]; villages become commuter settlements, house prices rise
and local services may close [2].
**(b)** A green belt: a ring of protected open land around the city where new
building is restricted [1], so the city cannot spread outwards [1]. Limitation:
development may leapfrog the green belt, increasing commuting distances, or
land prices inside the city may rise [1]. (Brownfield-first policies or
higher-density housing are equally creditworthy.)

**9. (a)** Developer: the city needs more housing for a growing population, or
the homes would bring investment and jobs [1]. Residents: parkland provides
recreation, wildlife habitat and cleaner air, or more homes would add traffic
and pressure on schools [1].
**(b)** Any two of: public consultation so residents' views shape the plan
[1]; build on part of the site and keep the rest as a protected park [1];
direct development to a nearby brownfield site instead [1]; require the
developer to fund new services or green space [1].

**10.** Levels-style marking:
- **Level 3 (6–7 marks):** a clearly named urban area with place-specific
  detail; causes are explained and linked to the problem; attempts are
  described and evaluated (what has worked, what has not, and for whom).
- **Level 2 (3–5 marks):** causes and attempts are described with some
  explanation, but detail about the place is limited or evaluation is weak.
- **Level 1 (1–2 marks):** simple, generic statements that could apply to any
  city.

Example of Level 3 content (London, traffic congestion): large commuting
workforce and a historic street pattern unsuited to modern traffic; the
Congestion Charge introduced in 2003 for vehicles entering the central zone;
investment in the Underground, buses and cycle lanes; evaluation that charging
reduces traffic in the zone but is criticised for its cost to small businesses
and lower-income drivers, and that public transport investment is expensive
and slow to deliver.

## A note on exam technique for this topic

The commonest way to lose marks on 1.6 is to describe *what* a zone looks like
without saying *why* it is there. Link every characteristic to accessibility or
land value. For problems, use a fixed chain of cause, solution and limitation,
and in case-study answers make sure every paragraph includes at least one
detail that only fits your chosen city.
