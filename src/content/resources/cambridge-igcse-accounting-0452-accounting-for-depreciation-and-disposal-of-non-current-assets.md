This guide covers sub-topic **4.2 Accounting for depreciation and disposal of non-current assets**. The syllabus lists: the meaning of depreciation and the need to account for it; calculating it "using the straight-line, reducing balance and revaluation methods"; "the appropriate methods of depreciation that can be applied to different types of non-current assets"; journal entries and ledger accounts to record depreciation; journal entries for the purchase and sale of non-current assets; the ledger accounts for purchase and sale ("non-current asset account, provision for depreciation account, and disposal of non-current asset account"); and calculating the profit or loss on disposal.

## 1. What depreciation is and why it is needed

**Depreciation** is the part of the cost of a non-current asset that is used up in an accounting period. Assets lose value through wear and tear, the passage of time and becoming out of date as better versions appear.

It must be accounted for because:

- the **matching** concept requires the cost of an asset to be charged against the revenue of the years it helps to earn, not all in the year of purchase;
- without it, profit would be overstated in every year after the purchase;
- the statement of financial position would show assets at more than a realistic value, which breaks **prudence**.

Depreciation is a **non-cash expense**. No money leaves the business when it is charged, and it does not set aside cash to replace the asset. The cash was paid when the asset was bought.

## 2. The three methods

### Straight-line, with a part-year charge

The same amount is charged each full year.

**Annual depreciation = (cost - residual value) / expected useful life**

Cliffside buys an oven on 1 April 2027 for 18,600. It is expected to last six years and then be sold for 2,400. Annual depreciation is (18,600 - 2,400) / 6 = 2,700. Ruth's policy is to charge depreciation for each month an asset is owned, so for 2027 the oven is charged for nine months: 2,700 x 9/12 = **2,025**.

Always read the policy. Other common policies are "a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal". A percentage may also be given instead of a life: "20% per annum on cost" is straight-line.

### Reducing balance

A fixed percentage is applied each year to the **net book value** (cost less accumulated depreciation), so the charge falls each year.

The delivery van cost 26,000 on 1 January 2026 and is depreciated at 30% per year on the reducing balance.

| Year | Net book value at start | Depreciation at 30% | Net book value at end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 | 26,000 | 7,800 | 18,200 |
| 2027 | 18,200 | 5,460 | 12,740 |
| 2028 | 12,740 | 3,822 | 8,918 |

### Revaluation

Used for groups of small items that are hard to track one by one. The items are valued at the start and end of the year.

**Depreciation = opening valuation + purchases - closing valuation**

Cliffside's baking tins and utensils were valued at 2,350 on 1 January 2027. Tins costing 780 were bought during the year, and the year-end valuation was 2,190. Depreciation for 2027 = 2,350 + 780 - 2,190 = **940**.

## 3. Choosing a method for each type of asset

| Asset | Suitable method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ovens, machinery used at a steady rate | Straight-line | Similar benefit every year |
| Fixtures and fittings | Straight-line | Wear evenly over a long life |
| Delivery vans and cars | Reducing balance | Lose most value early; repairs rise as they age, so a falling depreciation charge evens out total cost |
| Computers and similar equipment | Reducing balance | Become out of date quickly |
| Small tools, tins and utensils | Revaluation | Many low-value items, lost or broken irregularly |

Once chosen, a method is applied **consistently** from year to year so that profits can be compared.

## 4. Worked example: a sale at a profit, in the journal and the ledger

Ruth depreciates bakery equipment at 20% per year on cost, calculated monthly. On 1 January 2027 the equipment account balance was 48,000 and the provision for depreciation of equipment was 21,300. The 48,000 included a mixer bought on 1 July 2024 for 12,000; the other equipment, costing 36,000, was all bought before 2027 and is not fully depreciated.

- 30 September 2027: the mixer was sold for 6,200, received by bank transfer.
- 1 October 2027: a new mixer was bought on credit from Ovenworks for 15,000.

**Profit or loss on disposal**

| Step | Working | $ |
|---|---|---|
| Months owned, 1 July 2024 to 30 September 2027 | | 39 |
| Accumulated depreciation on the mixer sold | 12,000 x 20% x 39/12 | 7,800 |
| Net book value at disposal | 12,000 - 7,800 | 4,200 |
| Sale proceeds | | 6,200 |
| **Profit on disposal** | 6,200 - 4,200 | **2,000** |

**Depreciation charge for 2027**

| Asset | Working | $ |
|---|---|---|
| Other equipment, full year | 36,000 x 20% | 7,200 |
| Mixer sold, 9 months | 12,000 x 20% x 9/12 | 1,800 |
| New mixer, 3 months | 15,000 x 20% x 3/12 | 750 |
| **Total** | | **9,750** |

The 1,800 for the mixer sold is already included in the 7,800 transferred on disposal.

It arises because the mixer lost less value than the depreciation charged, so the profit corrects earlier charges rather than being trading income.

**Statement of financial position extract at 31 December 2027:** equipment at cost 51,000, less accumulated depreciation 23,250, net book value **27,750**.

## Common mistakes

- Applying the reducing balance percentage to cost every year.
- Ignoring the residual value in straight-line, or the months-owned policy.
- Crediting depreciation to the asset account instead of the provision for depreciation account.
- Comparing sale proceeds with cost instead of net book value.
- Forgetting the depreciation for the part-year before disposal when the policy is monthly.
- Describing depreciation as "money saved to replace the asset".

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** in labelled steps: months owned, accumulated depreciation, net book value, proceeds, profit or loss.
- **Prepare** ledger accounts with the other account named in each details line; balance the asset and provision accounts and close the disposal account to the statement of profit or loss.
- To **explain** a choice of method, link the pattern of the charge to how the asset is used or loses value.
- When a purchase is on credit, credit the supplier's account, not bank.
