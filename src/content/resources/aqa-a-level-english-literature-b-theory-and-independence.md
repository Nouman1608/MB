This guide covers **Component 3: Theory and Independence**, the
non-exam assessment (NEA) of AQA A-Level English Literature B (7717).
Where [Paper 1 Literary Genres](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-literature-b-literary-genres/)
and [Paper 2 Texts and Genres](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-literature-b-texts-and-genres/)
are timed, closed- or open-book written examinations on set texts,
Component 3 is the qualification's only coursework element -- marked
by your own centre and moderated by AQA rather than sat under exam
conditions.

## Where this fits in 7717

Component 3 is worth 20% of the A-level, exactly half the per-component
weighting of each exam paper (Paper 1 and Paper 2 are each 40%). Unlike
the exam papers, which work with texts chosen from AQA's own set-text
lists, Component 3 is deliberately independent: candidates choose their
own two texts, subject to the specification's structural rules, and
apply critical theory to them without the time pressure of an exam
hall. This independence is genuine, not nominal -- the specification's
title, "Theory and Independence," names both the critical-theory
application and the self-directed research and text selection as the
skills actually being assessed.

## What you submit

Two essays, **1,250-1,500 words each**, on **two different texts**:

- One text must be **poetry**, the other must be **prose**.
- Each text must be linked to a **different section** of the AQA
  Critical anthology.
- One response is a **conventional essay**; the second can be
  **re-creative** -- or you may submit two conventional essays instead.

There is no exam-hall time pressure, but each essay should be 1,250-1,500
words, with 1,500 the maximum, and a strict requirement that the two texts draw on genuinely
*different* critical lenses.

## The Critical anthology and how to choose texts against it

The anthology offers accessible extracts across six critical methods:
narrative theory (how the story is structured and told), feminist
theory (gender, power and voice), Marxist theory (class, economics and
social structure), eco-critical theory (nature, environment and the
non-human), post-colonial theory (empire, race and cultural power), and
literary value and the canon (why some texts are judged "great"). The
specification requires that texts chosen "must allow access to a range
of critical views and interpretations, including over time" and "must
maximise opportunities for writing with reference to the AQA Critical
anthology". It does not say whether the lens or the text comes first,
but a sound approach is to select texts with the critical lens in mind,
rather than choosing a favourite text and then searching for a critical
framework that fits it after the fact. A text that offers genuinely rich material for, say, a
post-colonial reading is a far safer choice than a text you enjoy
reading but which actively resists the critical framework you intend
to apply to it -- an essay that has to strain to make a critical lens
fit its chosen text rarely scores as well as one where the fit was
considered from the start.

## The conventional essay

A conventional response is a debate-style essay that uses the Critical
anthology to explore potential meanings in the text. The
specification's own worked example uses post-colonial ideas to
interrogate whether *A Passage to India* makes it "impossible to
sympathise with any of the English characters" -- an argumentative
task format: state a position, then use anthology-informed ideas as
evidence and counter-evidence, arriving at a considered, not
one-sided, conclusion.

## The re-creative essay

A re-creative response rewrites or extends part of the base text -- a
new narrative voice, a missing scene, journal entries from a
marginalised character -- as a way of **offering a critical reading**
of that text, not as free creative writing for its own sake. The
specification's own worked example uses journal entries from Miss
Kenton in *The Remains of the Day*, informed by feminist and/or
Marxist ideas. Two things make a re-creative piece succeed at A-level:
purposeful narrative-gap work, meaning the piece fills in an absence
or silence the base text leaves implicit (an unheard character's
perspective, an unshown scene) to expose something the original text
does not state directly; and the accompanying commentary, which is not
optional decoration but the place where the candidate explicitly
connects their creative choices to the base text and to the specific
Critical anthology section used, explaining *why* each significant
choice was made and what critical reading it produces. Marginalised or
minor characters in the base text usually make more interesting
re-creative narrators than already-central ones, precisely because
their absence from the original narrative is itself the "gap" a
re-creative piece can exploit.

## Text-selection rules

One prose text and one poetry text (a single-author poetry collection
or one longer narrative poem both count as the poetry text). If using
a poetry or short-story collection, the *whole* text must be studied,
then at least two poems or stories selected as representative examples
for detailed analysis -- unless the piece is re-creative, in which case
a single poem or story from the collection may be the sole focus. One
of the two texts may be a text in translation, provided it is a
translation recognised by academia as high quality, in which case it
is treated as the original author's own words for assessment purposes.
Exam set texts are banned outright: a text cannot be used if it
appears anywhere on the A-level exam paper set-text lists, even if the
candidate's own centre studies a different exam text and would never
be examined on the banned one specifically.

## How the five assessment objectives apply here

Component 3 is marked against the same AO1-AO5 framework as the exam
papers, but at half the per-component weighting: AO1 (informed,
accurate, well-expressed personal response) carries the largest share,
followed by AO2 (how meanings are shaped in texts) and AO3
(significance of context) roughly equally, with AO4 (connections
across texts) and AO5 (different interpretations) each carrying a
smaller share. AO4 is easy to under-serve in Component 3 specifically,
because the two essays sit on different texts and different critical
lenses by design -- but the specification still expects each essay to
gesture toward the wider network of texts and contexts the studied
work sits within, not treat its text as read in total isolation from
the wider literary and critical landscape.

## How to approach it

Choose the critical lens before the text, not after -- confirm early
which two of the six anthology sections you intend to use, and only
then select texts that genuinely reward that specific critical
reading. If choosing the re-creative option, draft the accompanying
commentary alongside the creative piece rather than as an afterthought
once the writing is finished, since the commentary is where the
critical understanding the piece is actually assessed on gets made
explicit. Confirm exact submission deadlines with your teacher before
beginning serious drafting, since these are centre-managed rather than
fixed exam-day constraints. AQA's NEA teaching guide for 7717 says
students "should make a concerted effort to work within the stipulated
word limits"; the same guide gives the count as 1,250-1,500 words "not
including quotations", and says of a re-creative piece and its
commentary, "Together the two pieces of writing should constitute
1,250-1,500 words", so both are counted in one total.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long are the 7717 NEA essays?**
Two essays of 1,250 to 1,500 words, one on a poetry text and one on a prose text.

**What is the Critical anthology for?**
Each essay links to a different section of the AQA Critical anthology, such as narrative, feminist, Marxist, eco-critical or post-colonial theory.

**Can one essay be creative?**
Yes. One essay may be re-creative, but it must be accompanied by a commentary.
