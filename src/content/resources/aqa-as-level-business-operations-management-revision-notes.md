## Managing operations

- **Five operations objectives:** efficiency, quality, volume of goods and services, flexibility, environmental impact. They can conflict.
- **Influences:** internal (finance, skills, equipment, other objectives) and external (competitors, customers, technology, suppliers, law, environmental pressure).
- **Added value** = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and services. Operations adds value through quality, design, speed or less waste.
- **Location factors:** costs, demand, access to resources and markets, infrastructure, government incentives, ethical and people considerations, break-even, return on investment, environmental.
- Operations links to finance, marketing and people, and drives competitiveness through cost, quality and flexibility.

## Operations data and KPIs

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Employee productivity | Output over a time period ÷ number of employees |
| Unit costs (average costs) | Total costs ÷ number of units of output |
| Capacity utilisation (%) | Actual output ÷ maximum possible output × 100 |
| Inventory turnover | Cost of sales ÷ average inventories held |

Method: write the formula, check the time periods match, substitute, add units, then say what the figure means.

**Interpret only:** wait times, returns, defects, complaints, speed of response, delivery times, customer service and satisfaction ratings, and environmental and sustainability measures. Use index numbers and percentage changes.

## Efficiency

Ways to improve resource utilisation and efficiency: improved capacity utilisation; **lean production** (reducing inventory, re-works, waiting times and transportation times; **kaizen**; standardised processes); reducing waste, recycling and reusing materials; effective scheduling; improving employee productivity; improving cost control. Weigh each against its cost, staff resistance and possible harm to quality.

## Quality

- **Costs of poor quality:** re-works, waste, returns, lost sales, damaged reputation, compensation.
- **Methods:** quality control (inspect to find faults), quality assurance (prevent faults at every stage), Total Quality Management (everyone responsible for quality), quality circles (staff groups solving quality problems), benchmarking (copying best practice).
- Better quality costs money first; the case is that it costs less than poor quality.

## Environmental impact

- **Value:** lower costs, compliance, reputation, access to customers who demand sustainability.
- **Ways:** waste reduction, resource efficiency, reducing emissions, cutting the carbon footprint, sustainable sourcing, circularity.
- **Challenges:** cost, supply chain limitations, disruption to change processes.

## Matching output to demand

For high, low and fluctuating (cyclical or seasonal) demand: change production scheduling; increase or decrease inventory; use part-time, temporary, zero-hours or freelance staff; outsource; invest in capital and technology; increase or decrease capacity. Not matching supply to demand means lost sales or idle, costly resources.

## Supply chain and inventory

- **Make or buy in:** control and margin against lower cost, expertise and no capital outlay.
- **Supply chain management:** supplier relationships and logistics; number and choice of suppliers; impact of supply chain issues; how profit is shared along the chain; transparency and third-party accreditations.
- **Inventory:** reasons to hold it, influences on how much, and **just in time** (low holding cost, but delays stop production) versus **just in case** (buffer covers delays and spikes, but higher storage cost and cash tied up).
- **Worked reminder:** cost of sales £300,000, opening inventory £22,000, closing £28,000. Average inventory £25,000; inventory turnover = 300,000 ÷ 25,000 = **12 times a year**.

## Innovation, projects, scale and technology

- **Innovation:** purpose, value and challenges; developed through R&D, talent acquisition and retention, cross-functional teams, intrapreneurship; protected by patents and copyrights.
- **Network analysis:** float time = latest finish time − duration − earliest start time. Activities with zero float form the **critical path**, which sets the shortest project time.
- **Economies of scale:** technical, purchasing, financial. **Diseconomies:** control, communication, coordination.
- **Technology (including AI):** in inventory management, supply chain management and automation, to improve competitiveness; costs are investment, training, system failure and job losses.

## Case-study technique

Use the figures in the case: calculate, then interpret against targets, the past and rivals. Name the operations objective the business is chasing, judge the trade-off (cost against quality, flexibility against efficiency), and finish with a recommendation and the condition it depends on.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Productivity and production | Output per employee against total output |
| Quality control and quality assurance | Finding faults after production against preventing them throughout |
| Just in time and just in case | Minimal stock delivered as needed against buffer stock held |
| Economies and diseconomies of scale | Unit costs falling with size against rising with size |
| Critical and non-critical activities | Zero float against some float |

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five operations objectives in the specification.
2. A bakery makes 5,400 pies with a capacity of 6,000. Calculate capacity utilisation.
3. A team of 15 makes 6,900 items. Calculate employee productivity.
4. Output per worker rises from 400 to 460. Calculate the percentage change.
5. A firm sells an item for £85 using £52 of bought-in materials. Calculate the added value.
6. Total costs are £31,500 for 4,500 units. Calculate unit cost.
7. Cost of sales is £180,000 and average inventory is £15,000. Calculate inventory turnover.
8. An activity has an earliest start time of 4 days, a duration of 3 days and a latest finish time of 9 days. Calculate its float.
9. State one benefit and one difficulty of just in time.
10. Give two ways a business can match supply to demand at a seasonal peak.
11. What is the difference between quality assurance and quality control?
12. Name the three economies of scale in the specification.

### Answers

1. Efficiency, quality, volume of goods and services, flexibility, environmental impact.
2. 5,400 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **90%**.
3. 6,900 ÷ 15 = **460 items per employee**.
4. (460 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = **15% increase**.
5. £85 − £52 = **£33**, which is 38.8% of the price.
6. £31,500 ÷ 4,500 = **£7.00**.
7. 180,000 ÷ 15,000 = **12 times**.
8. 9 − 3 − 4 = **2 days**.
9. Benefit: lower holding costs. Difficulty: any supplier delay stops production.
10. Any two of: changing production scheduling, building inventory beforehand, part-time, temporary, zero-hours or freelance staff, outsourcing.
11. Quality assurance prevents faults throughout the process; quality control inspects output to find faults after they have been made.
12. Technical, purchasing and financial.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a formula and no workings, or leaving out the units.
- Mixing time periods, such as monthly output with weekly capacity.
- Calculating unit cost from variable costs only.
- Using closing inventory instead of average inventory in inventory turnover.
- Describing JIT or lean production without applying it to the business in the case.
- Listing benefits of improving quality with no reference to the cost of doing so.
- Calling a rise in productivity good without asking whether the extra output can be sold.
- Evaluation that ends with "it depends" and no reasoned decision.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between quality control and quality assurance?**
Quality control inspects output, usually at the end, to find faults after they have happened. Quality assurance builds quality into every stage of the process to prevent faults in the first place.

**How do you calculate float time in network analysis?**
Float time equals the latest finish time minus the duration minus the earliest start time. Activities with zero float are on the critical path.

**What is the difference between just in time and just in case?**
Just in time keeps minimal stock, with materials delivered only as needed, which cuts holding costs. Just in case holds buffer stock, which covers delays and demand spikes but costs more to store.

**Which economies of scale are named in the AQA AS Business (7137) specification?**
Technical, purchasing and financial economies of scale. Diseconomies of scale come from problems of control, communication and coordination as a business grows.

**What is the difference between productivity and production?**
Production is total output. Productivity is output per employee over a time period, so a firm can raise production without improving productivity.
