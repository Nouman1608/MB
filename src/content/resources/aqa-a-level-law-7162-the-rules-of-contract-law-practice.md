These questions test the section 3.4 row on the rules of contract law: where the rules come from, how agreement is judged, which rules apply to which contracts, the status of flawed contracts and how to work through a problem.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **one** area of contract law that is mainly common law and **one** that is mainly governed by statute. **[2]**

**2.** State the standard of proof in a contract claim. **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the objective approach to agreement. **[2]**

**4.** State the definition of a "consumer" in s2 Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a void and a voidable contract. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the difference between a bilateral and a unilateral contract, giving an example of a unilateral contract. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the decision in *Smith v Hughes* (1871) and what it shows about how courts judge agreement. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why it is important to identify whether a contract is a consumer contract or a business contract before applying the rules on terms and exclusion clauses. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how equity supplements the common law in contract, giving **two** examples. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how rescission of a voidable contract may be lost. **[3]**

**11.** Jamal, a self-employed decorator, buys a ladder from a DIY superstore for his business. It collapses on its first use. He also buys a second-hand bicycle for his son from his neighbour, Kat, who says it "has new brakes"; the brakes are worn. Explain which body of rules governs each contract and outline the issues Jamal would need to consider. **[6]**

**12.** Explain the order in which you would work through a contract problem question and why that order is useful. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Mainly common law: any one of offer and acceptance, consideration, intention, frustration or economic duress [1]. Mainly statute: consumer implied terms under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, or exclusion clauses under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 [1].

**2.** The balance of probabilities [1].

**3.** Courts decide whether there is agreement, and on what terms, by asking what a reasonable person would understand from the parties' words and conduct [1], not by what one party privately intended [1].

**4.** An individual [1] acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside that individual's trade, business, craft or profession [1].

**5.** A void contract has no legal effect at all; in law there is no contract [1]. A voidable contract is valid until the innocent party chooses to set it aside by rescission, for example after a misrepresentation [1].

**6.** In a bilateral contract each party makes a promise in exchange for the other's promise [1]. In a unilateral contract one party promises in return for the performance of an act [1], for example the offer of £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball and caught influenza in *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* (1893) [1].

**7.** A buyer agreed to buy oats, believing they were old oats, when they were new [1]. The seller had said nothing about their age and had not caused the mistake [1]. The court held that the buyer's private mistake did not prevent a binding contract, because the question was what a reasonable person would understand the seller to be promising [1]. This shows that agreement is judged objectively, which gives certainty to commercial dealing [1].

**8.** The rules differ by type of contract [1]. In a trader-to-consumer contract, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 implies terms such as satisfactory quality (s9) [1] and prevents traders excluding them (s31) [1]. In a business-to-business contract, exclusion clauses are controlled by the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, often through a reasonableness test [1]. In a private sale, neither consumer statute applies in the same way, so the common law governs [1].

**9.** Equity provides remedies where common law damages are inadequate [1]. Specific performance orders a party to carry out the contract, for example for the sale of a unique item [1]. Rescission sets aside a voidable contract and restores the parties to their original positions [1]. Promissory estoppel can prevent a party going back on a promise to accept less, as in *Central London Property Trust v High Trees House* (1947) [1].

**10.** Rescission is lost if the innocent party affirms the contract after discovering the problem [1], if there is undue delay in seeking rescission [1], or if a third party has acquired rights in the subject matter in good faith [1].

**11.** **Ladder:** Jamal buys it for his business, so he is not acting wholly or mainly outside his trade and is not a consumer [1]. This is a business-to-business contract, so the consumer rights in the Consumer Rights Act 2015 do not apply; any exclusion clause would be tested under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 [1], and he would need to consider the implied terms that apply to business sales and the common law on breach and remedies [1]. **Bicycle:** Kat is a private seller, not a trader, so the Consumer Rights Act 2015 does not apply [1]. The main issue is whether "has new brakes" was a term or a representation [1]: if a term, Jamal can claim damages for breach; if a representation, he may claim misrepresentation and seek rescission [1].

**12.** First, formation: there must be a contract before any other issue arises [1]. Second, terms, express and implied, because a breach is a breach of a term [1]. Third, whether a term has been broken or a vitiating factor applies, since these give different remedies [1]. Fourth, any exclusion clause, which may remove liability for the breach [1]. Fifth, discharge, which explains whether the contract has ended [1]. Finally, remedies, with a conclusion for each party; the order mirrors the life of a contract and prevents remedies being discussed before liability is shown [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the Consumer Rights Act 2015 to a business purchase or a private sale.
- Calling a contract induced by misrepresentation "void".
- Giving the objective approach without an authority.
- Starting a problem answer with remedies.
