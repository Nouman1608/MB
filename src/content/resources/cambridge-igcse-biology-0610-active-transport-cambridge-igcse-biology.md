## 3.3.1 The definition, part by part

**Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration.**

- **Through a cell membrane:** active transport is always across a membrane, unlike diffusion, which can happen in air or water as well.
- **From lower to higher concentration:** the opposite direction to diffusion. "**Against** a concentration gradient" says the same thing.
- **Using energy from respiration:** this is what makes it "active". The energy is released by respiration in the cell's own mitochondria. Diffusion and osmosis use only the kinetic energy of the particles.

### Comparing the three processes

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| What moves | Any particles | Water molecules | Particles such as ions and glucose |
| Direction | Higher → lower concentration | Dilute → concentrated solution | **Lower → higher** concentration |
| Membrane needed? | Not always | Yes, partially permeable | Yes, cell membrane |
| Energy from respiration? | No | No | **Yes** |

## Evidence that active transport needs respiration

If a process uses energy from respiration, anything that slows respiration should slow it too. Experiments with roots show this:

- **Removing oxygen** (for example, bubbling nitrogen through the solution) greatly reduces ion uptake, because aerobic respiration needs oxygen.
- **Lowering the temperature** reduces uptake, because the enzymes of respiration work more slowly.
- **A respiratory poison** such as cyanide almost stops uptake.

Diffusion would not be affected by a lack of oxygen, so these results show the ions are being moved actively.

### Worked example 1: reading uptake data (illustrative)

Roots were placed in a solution containing potassium ions. One set had air bubbled through; the other had nitrogen bubbled through, removing oxygen.

| Time / hours | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uptake with air (arbitrary units) | 0 | 12 | 22 | 30 | 36 |
| Uptake with nitrogen (arbitrary units) | 0 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |

- **Describe:** with air, uptake rises steadily to 36 units after 4 hours; with nitrogen, it reaches only 7 units.
- **Compare with a calculation:** 36 ÷ 7 = about **5.1 times** more uptake with air.
- **Explain:** with air, root cells respire aerobically and release energy for active transport of potassium ions; without oxygen, little energy is released, so active transport is much slower.
- **Suggest** why some uptake still happens without oxygen: some ions may enter by diffusion at first, while the concentration outside is still higher than in some cells, or a little energy may come from anaerobic respiration.

### Worked example 2: is this active transport?

*The concentration of nitrate ions in a root hair cell is 45 units; in the soil water it is 3 units. The ions keep moving into the cell.*

- Ratio: 45 ÷ 3 = **15**, so the cell contains 15 times the concentration in the soil.
- The ions are moving from a **lower** concentration (soil) to a **higher** concentration (cell), against the gradient.
- So the movement must be **active transport**, using energy from respiration. Diffusion would move the ions out of the cell, not in.

## 3.3.2 Why active transport matters -- Extended only

Active transport lets cells take in substances they need even when the concentration outside is lower, and remove substances even when the concentration outside is higher. Without it, cells could only take in a substance until the concentrations became equal.

### Ion uptake by root hairs

Soil water usually contains mineral ions, such as nitrate and magnesium ions, at a **lower concentration** than inside the root hair cells. Plants need these ions: nitrate to make amino acids and proteins, and magnesium to make chlorophyll. Diffusion would not bring them in against this gradient, so root hair cells use **active transport**, with energy from respiration, to absorb them.

This links to other parts of the course:

- Root hair cells have a large surface area for absorption (sub-topic 2.1), and they need oxygen from air spaces in the soil for respiration, which is one reason waterlogged soil harms plants.
- Absorbing ions lowers the water potential of the root cells, which helps water enter by osmosis (sub-topic 3.2).

### Other examples

- **Absorption in the small intestine:** cells lining the villi absorb glucose actively, so glucose can still be taken up when little remains in the gut.
- Cells that carry out a lot of active transport usually contain **many mitochondria**, which release the energy needed.

## 3.3.3 Protein carriers -- Extended only

**Protein carriers move molecules or ions across a membrane during active transport.** The carriers are protein molecules that span the cell membrane.

A simple description of how they work:

1. A particle on the side of lower concentration fits into a specific carrier protein.
2. Energy from respiration is used to change the carrier's shape.
3. The particle is released on the other side of the membrane, where its concentration is higher.
4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to move another particle.

Each kind of carrier moves particular molecules or ions, so a cell can control which substances it takes up.

## Common errors

- Writing "active transport moves particles **down** a concentration gradient". It moves them **against** the gradient.
- Saying the energy comes from "the sun" or "food" without linking it to **respiration**. The syllabus wording is "energy from respiration".
- Saying active transport moves water. Water moves by osmosis.
- (Extended) Explaining ion uptake by root hairs as diffusion.
- (Extended) Forgetting the **protein carriers**.
- Describing a data trend without numbers, or without comparing the two conditions.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** (active transport): direction, membrane and energy from respiration, all three.
- **Explain** (Extended, importance): say why diffusion cannot do the job (the gradient is the wrong way) and why the substance is needed.
- **Compare:** for each feature, give both processes in one sentence.
- **Suggest:** for unusual data, give a reasoned link to respiration or to diffusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is active transport Core or Extended?** The definition is Core; its importance, including ion uptake by root hairs, and the role of protein carriers are Extended only.

**Why does a lack of oxygen reduce active transport?** Less aerobic respiration means less energy is released for moving particles against the gradient.

**Can active transport happen in dead cells?** No. Dead cells do not respire, so there is no energy for it.
