## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by water insecurity. **[1]**

**2.** Name **two** renewable sources of energy. **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by desalination? **[1]**

**4.** Give **one** reason why energy consumption is rising globally. **[1]**

**5.** State what is meant by a carbon footprint. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented data for domestic water use in two countries.

| | Country A | Country B |
|---|---|---|
| Population (millions) | 40 | 120 |
| Water used per person per day (litres) | 25 | 150 |
| People without safe water (%) | 45 | 3 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times more water each person uses per day in Country B than in Country A. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of people without safe water in Country A, in millions. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons for the differences between the two countries. **[4]**

**7.** The table shows invented data for energy use per person, in kilograms of oil equivalent, in three countries in two years.

| | 2000 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Country P | 300 | 600 |
| Country Q | 1 500 | 2 700 |
| Country R | 5 000 | 4 500 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in energy use per person in Country Q between 2000 and 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes shown for Country P and Country R. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** reason why energy use per person fell in Country R. **[1]**

**8.** Explain **two** impacts of water insecurity. **[4]**

**9.** For a named large-scale water transfer scheme, explain its advantages and disadvantages. **[6]**

**10.** For a named local scheme in an LIC or NEE, explain how it has increased the supply of water or energy sustainably. **[4]**

**11.** For a named example, explain the advantages and disadvantages of extracting a fossil fuel. **[6]**

**12.** Explain how energy supply can be affected by political factors. **[3]**

**13.** "The best way to ensure future water and energy supplies is to reduce demand rather than increase supply." To what extent do you agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Not having reliable access to enough safe water for drinking, farming and industry [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two from: wind; solar; hydroelectric; tidal; wave; geothermal; biomass [1 each]. **[2]**

**3.** Removing salt from seawater to produce fresh water [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any one from: economic development; rising population; new technology and appliances [1]. **[1]**

**5.** The amount of carbon dioxide (and other greenhouse gases) released by an activity, person or organisation [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 150 ÷ 25 = 6 times [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 45% of 40 million = 0.45 × 40 [1] = 18 million [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Country B is likely to be wealthier, so it can afford pipes, treatment works and appliances, and more people use water for washing machines, showers and flush toilets [2]. Country A may be poorer, with limited infrastructure, so many people rely on wells and rivers and collect only small amounts [2]. Other valid points include differences in climate, industry or pollution. **[4]**

**7. (a)** (2 700 - 1 500) ÷ 1 500 × 100 [1] = 80% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Country P doubled from 300 to 600 [1]. Country R fell from 5 000 to 4 500 [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Any one from: more efficient appliances, vehicles and buildings; energy conservation; a shift from industry to services; higher prices; use of renewables [1]. **[1]**

**8.** Two impacts, each with development (2 marks each). For example: waterborne disease, where people forced to drink unsafe water suffer diarrhoea, cholera or typhoid [2]; lower food production, where a shortage of water for irrigation reduces harvests and increases hunger [2]; reduced industrial output, where factories and power stations lack water, so jobs and income fall [2]; conflict, where countries or groups compete for shared rivers [2]. **[4]**

**9.** Named scheme and location [1]. Advantages with detail, such as supplies water to dry, populated regions, supports industry and farming, reduces over-use of groundwater (up to 2 marks) [2]. Disadvantages with detail, such as very high cost, people relocated, damage to the source area, pollution risk (up to 2 marks) [2]. A statement that weighs the benefits against the costs [1]. Maximum 6, and a balanced answer is needed for full marks. **[6]**

**10.** Named scheme and location [1]. How it works, for example sand dams store water in sand behind small walls, or micro-hydro uses a stream to turn a turbine [1]. Why it is sustainable, such as low cost, built and run by local people, or uses a renewable resource [1]. Benefit to people, such as water closer to home, electricity for lighting, saving time and money [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Named example [1]. Advantages with detail, such as export earnings, jobs, government revenue and fuel supply (up to 2 marks) [2]. Disadvantages with detail, such as oil spills or flaring causing pollution, damage to farming and fishing, little benefit for local people, conflict and climate change (up to 2 marks) [2]. A judgement on the balance, or on who gains and who loses [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

**12.** Governments can restrict supply, for example by cutting exports or setting quotas [1]. Conflict or sanctions can stop energy being produced or transported, for example when a pipeline is closed [1]. Political instability discourages investment in exploration and infrastructure, so supply falls [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Indicative content:
- Agree: conservation, efficiency and recycling are usually cheaper than new dams, transfers or power stations [1].
- Agree: lower demand cuts carbon emissions and protects aquifers and other resources [1].
- Agree: it can be done by individuals, communities and industries [1].
- Disagree: in poorer countries, many people use very little and need more supply [1].
- Disagree: rising population and development mean demand will keep growing, so extra supply is also needed [1].
- Disagree: some changes, such as renewable power, are needed to replace fossil fuels, not just to cut demand [1].
- Judgement: a combination is best; richer countries can reduce demand, while poorer countries need more supply; a justified conclusion is needed [1]. Maximum 6. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing only advantages or only disadvantages for a scheme.
- Naming no scheme, or giving no facts about it.
- Not showing working in percentage calculations.
- Calling nuclear power renewable.
- Confusing conservation (less demand) with increasing supply.
- Judgements that do not weigh both sides.
