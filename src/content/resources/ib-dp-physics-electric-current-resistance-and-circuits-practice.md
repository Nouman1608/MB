## Questions

**1.** A phone battery charges at a steady 3.2 mA for 5.0 hours, with a p.d. of 3.7 V across it.
**(a)** Calculate the charge that flows. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy transferred to the battery. **[2]**

**2.** Explain, in terms of charge carriers, why copper conducts while a plastic does not, and why the resistance of a copper wire rises as it warms. **[4]**

**3.** A constantan wire has length 0.60 m and diameter 1.2 mm. Its resistivity is 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m (given).
**(a)** Calculate its resistance. **[3]**
**(b)** A second wire of the same material is twice as long and has half the diameter. Determine its resistance. **[2]**

**4.** The table gives readings for a small filament lamp.

| p.d. / V | 1.0 | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| current / A | 0.10 | 0.16 | 0.24 | 0.30 |

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of the lamp at 1.0 V and at 6.0 V. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether the lamp is ohmic, and explain the change in resistance in terms of the lattice. **[4]**

**5.** Two resistors of 6.0 Ω and 12 Ω are connected across an ideal 12 V supply, first in series and then in parallel. Calculate the total power dissipated in each arrangement and state the ratio of parallel power to series power. **[5]**

**6.** An ideal 12 V cell is connected to a 10 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of 15 Ω and 30 Ω resistors. Calculate:
**(a)** the current from the cell, **[3]**
**(b)** the current in the 30 Ω resistor. **[3]**

**7.** A student measures the terminal p.d. of a cell at two currents: 1.38 V at 0.20 A and 1.14 V at 0.60 A.
**(a)** Determine the internal resistance and the emf of the cell. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the current if the cell is short-circuited. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the external resistance when the current is 0.50 A. **[2]**

**8.** A 12 V supply is connected across a 4.0 kΩ fixed resistor in series with an LDR. The LDR has resistance 1.0 kΩ in bright light and 20 kΩ in the dark.
**(a)** Calculate the p.d. across the LDR in bright light and in the dark. **[3]**
**(b)** The fixed resistor and the LDR are swapped. State and explain how the p.d. across the LDR in the dark changes. **[2]**

**9.** A 12 V cell of negligible internal resistance is connected to two 5.0 kΩ resistors in series. A voltmeter of constant resistance 5.0 kΩ is connected across one of the resistors. Calculate the voltmeter reading, and state what an ideal voltmeter would read. **[4]**

**10.** A solar panel of area 1.6 m² receives light of intensity 800 W m⁻² and is 18% efficient.
**(a)** Calculate the electrical energy it delivers in 3.0 hours. **[3]**
**(b)** Give one advantage and one disadvantage of this panel compared with a chemical cell as a source for a remote sensor. **[2]**

**11.** A heater coil is made from nichrome wire of diameter 0.30 mm and resistivity 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m (given). It is connected to a cell of emf 12 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω. The coil resistance is 5.5 Ω.
**(a)** Calculate the length of wire in the coil. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the current and the power dissipated in the coil. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the power dissipated inside the cell and the fraction of the total power that heats the coil. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Time = 5.0 × 3600 = 18 000 s [1]. q = IΔt = 3.2 × 10⁻³ × 18 000 = **58 C** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** W = Vq [1] = 3.7 × 57.6 = **2.1 × 10² J** (213 J) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Converting hours to seconds before substituting is where most slips occur; a unit missing from the final line often costs the answer line.

**2.** Copper has many free (mobile) electrons [1]; plastic has almost no mobile charge carriers, so the same p.d. gives negligible current [1]. On warming, the lattice ions vibrate with larger amplitude [1], so electrons collide with them more often, which lowers their mobility and raises the resistance [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers earn credit for naming the particles involved (free electrons, lattice ions) and the collision link; "it gets harder for current to flow" on its own is too vague.

**3. (a)** r = 0.60 mm, so A = π(0.60 × 10⁻³)² = 1.13 × 10⁻⁶ m² [1]. R = ρL/A [1] = (4.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.60) ÷ 1.13 × 10⁻⁶ = **0.26 Ω** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** R ∝ L/A, and A is a quarter, so R is 2 × 4 = 8 times larger [1]: **2.1 Ω** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the radius step explicitly; using the diameter in place of the radius in A is a common way to lose all the later credit.

**4. (a)** At 1.0 V: R = 1.0 ÷ 0.10 = **10 Ω** [1]. At 6.0 V: R = 6.0 ÷ 0.30 = **20 Ω** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Not ohmic, because R is not constant (current is not proportional to p.d.) [1]. More current heats the filament [1]. The lattice ions vibrate with larger amplitude [1], so there are more collisions with electrons and R rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from the table to support "not ohmic"; a bare statement about the curve gains less than a comparison of two resistances.

**5.** Series: R = 18 Ω [1], P = V²/R = 144 ÷ 18 = **8.0 W** [1]. Parallel: Rp = (6.0 × 12) ÷ 18 = 4.0 Ω [1], P = 144 ÷ 4.0 = **36 W** [1]. Ratio = 36 ÷ 8.0 = **4.5** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* V²/R is the quickest form because the supply p.d. is fixed; choosing a form that needs an unknown current adds steps.

**6. (a)** 1/Rp = 1/15 + 1/30 = 3/30, so Rp = 10 Ω [1]. Total R = 20 Ω [1]. I = 12 ÷ 20 = **0.60 A** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** p.d. across the pair = 0.60 × 10 = 6.0 V [1]. Current in 30 Ω = 6.0 ÷ 30 [1] = **0.20 A** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the p.d. across the parallel pair, not the supply p.d.; checking that the branch currents add to 0.60 A catches this.

**7. (a)** ε = V + Ir for both readings [1]. 1.38 + 0.20r = 1.14 + 0.60r [1], so r = 0.24 ÷ 0.40 = **0.60 Ω** [1]. ε = 1.38 + 0.20 × 0.60 = **1.50 V** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** I = ε/r = 1.50 ÷ 0.60 = **2.5 A** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** ε = I(R + r), so R + r = 1.50 ÷ 0.50 = 3.0 Ω [1]; R = 3.0 − 0.60 = **2.4 Ω** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating the relation you use before substituting makes your method clear even if an arithmetic slip follows.

**8. (a)** Bright: V = 12 × 1.0 ÷ 5.0 = **2.4 V** [1]. Dark: V = 12 × 20 ÷ 24 = **10 V** [1]. The share of the p.d. is proportional to the resistance in a series pair [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** After the swap the fixed resistor is 4.0 kΩ, the LDR is still 20 kΩ in the dark, so nothing changes in series: p.d. across the LDR stays **10 V** [1] because the order of series components does not alter the current or the individual p.d.s [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned "no change" is a complete answer; saying the p.d. changes without checking the series relationship is a common trap.

**9.** The voltmeter is in parallel with 5.0 kΩ, so the pair has 1/R = 1/5.0 + 1/5.0, R = 2.5 kΩ [1]. Total = 2.5 + 5.0 = 7.5 kΩ [1]. Reading = 12 × 2.5 ÷ 7.5 = **4.0 V** [1]. Ideal voltmeter: **6.0 V** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two readings: the difference shows why the guide's ideal-meter assumption matters.

**10. (a)** Power in = 800 × 1.6 = 1280 W [1]. Electrical power = 0.18 × 1280 = 230 W [1]. Energy = 230.4 × 10 800 = **2.5 × 10⁶ J** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Advantage: no fuel is used up, so it can recharge from sunlight (or: no emissions in use) [1]. Disadvantage: output depends on light, so it fails at night or in heavy cloud [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the pair to the remote-sensor context; generic points score less than ones that mention reliability or maintenance.

**11. (a)** A = π(0.15 × 10⁻³)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]. L = RA/ρ [1] = (5.5 × 7.07 × 10⁻⁸) ÷ 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ = **0.35 m** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** I = ε/(R + r) = 12 ÷ 6.0 = **2.0 A** [1]. P = I²R = 2.0² × 5.5 [1] = **22 W** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Power in cell = I²r = 4.0 × 0.50 = **2.0 W** [1]. Total = 22 + 2.0 = 24 W (= εI) [1]. Fraction = 22 ÷ 24 = **0.92** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Using I²R with the same current for both the coil and the cell keeps the working short; mixing in V²/R with the wrong p.d. is a frequent source of error.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not converting hours, minutes, millimetres or kilohms before substituting.
- Using the diameter rather than the radius in A = πr².
- Quoting "R rises" without explaining it using lattice vibrations and collisions.
- Using the supply p.d. across one part of a series-parallel circuit.
- Treating the terminal p.d. as the emf when current flows through a real cell.
- Forgetting to invert 1/Rp at the end.
- Giving a one-sided answer when asked to compare energy sources.
- Leaving units off final answers or over-rounding intermediate values.
