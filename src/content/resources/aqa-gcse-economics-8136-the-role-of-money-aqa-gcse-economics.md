Money is anything that is generally accepted in payment for goods and services and in settlement of debts. Section 3.2.5.1 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has two content points: the **functions of money** and the **definition of money**. You must understand money as a **medium of exchange**, a **unit of account**, a **store of value** and a **means of deferred payment**, and understand that money can be defined as **more than the banknotes and coins in circulation**.

The section opens 3.2.5 The role of money and financial markets, part of 3.2 How the economy works, which is assessed on Paper 2. There are no tiers. ## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Functions of money | Explain money as a means of deferred payment, a store of value, a unit of account and a medium of exchange, and apply each to a situation |
| Definition of money | Explain why money is more than the notes and coins in circulation |

## Starting point: life without money

The clearest way to understand each function is to see the problem it solves in a **barter** economy, where goods are swapped directly for other goods.

| Problem with barter | Function of money that solves it |
|---|---|
| You must find someone who has what you want **and** wants what you have: a double coincidence of wants | **Medium of exchange**: you sell to anyone for money and buy from anyone with money |
| Every good has to be valued in terms of every other good | **Unit of account**: every price is stated in one measure, such as pounds |
| Many goods rot, wear out or are hard to keep, so wealth is hard to save | **Store of value**: money can be kept and spent later |
| Agreeing to repay a debt "in sheep next year" is risky and hard to measure | **Means of deferred payment**: debts are fixed in money and repaid over time |

### Worked example: why a unit of account matters (illustrative)

In a barter economy with 5 goods, each pair of goods needs its own exchange rate (for example, "3 loaves for 1 fish"). The number of pairs is 5 × 4 ÷ 2 = **10** exchange rates. With 100 goods it is 100 × 99 ÷ 2 = **4,950** exchange rates. With money as a unit of account, 100 goods need only **100** prices, one for each good. Comparing value becomes quick, which is why shops, firms and governments all measure in money.

## The four functions in detail

### Medium of exchange

Money is accepted in exchange for goods and services. This is the most important function, because it lets people **specialise**: a nurse can be paid in money and spend it on food, rent and transport, without needing to find a farmer or landlord who wants nursing. Specialisation raises productivity, so a working medium of exchange supports higher output in the whole economy.

### Unit of account

Money provides a common measure of value. Prices, wages, costs, profits and GDP are all measured in money, so they can be compared and added up. A firm could not work out its profit, or a government its tax revenue, without a unit of account.

### Store of value

Money can be held and used later without losing its usefulness. Holding money lets people save for the future and smooth their spending. Money is only a good store of value if prices are stable: when inflation is high, each pound buys less over time.

### Means of deferred payment

Money lets people buy now and pay later, because debts can be written in money terms. Mortgages, car loans, buying on credit and paying bills monthly all rely on this function. It depends on the store of value function: a lender will accept repayment in the future only if money will still be worth something.

### Worked example: inflation and deferred payment (illustrative)

Sam borrows £1,000 and must repay £1,000 in one year (ignore interest). Prices rise by 5% during the year.

- The £1,000 repaid buys only 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = **£952** of goods at last year's prices.
- The lender loses about £48 of purchasing power; Sam, the borrower, gains.

This is why lenders charge higher interest rates when they expect high inflation, and why very high inflation can stop people lending at all. Inflation harms the store of value and the means of deferred payment, and if prices change very fast it also harms the unit of account, because prices quickly become out of date.

## What makes something work well as money?

Money performs its functions well only if it has certain qualities. These are not separate specification points, but they help explain the functions:

- **Generally acceptable**, so that it can be a medium of exchange;
- **Durable**, so that it lasts and can be a store of value;
- **Divisible**, so that small and large purchases can be priced;
- **Portable**, so that it can be carried or moved easily;
- **Limited in supply and hard to forge**, so that it keeps its value.

These qualities explain why perishable goods such as fish make poor money, and why modern money is mainly electronic.

## Why money is more than notes and coins

The specification asks you to understand that money can be defined as **more than the banknotes and coins in circulation**. In a modern economy most money is held as **deposits** in bank and building society accounts.

- When you pay by debit card, bank transfer or phone, no cash moves. A deposit is moved from your account to the seller's account.
- Deposits perform all four functions: they are accepted in payment, prices are stated in the same pounds, balances can be saved, and loans are paid back from them.
- When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account, so new deposits, and so new money, are created.

So a definition of money that counted only cash would leave out most of the money people actually spend and save. In the UK, notes and coins are only a small share of the total.

### What does not count as money?

- **A debit card** is not money itself; it is a way of moving deposits, which are money.
- **A credit card** is a way of borrowing. The card holder spends money lent by the card company and repays later, so it is linked to the means of deferred payment, but the card is not money.
- **Assets such as houses or shares** are stores of value but are not generally accepted in payment, so they are not money.

Questions sometimes ask whether an item is money. Test it against the functions, especially whether it is **generally accepted** as a medium of exchange.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up store of value (saving) and means of deferred payment (repaying debts later).
- Writing "money is notes and coins". The specification wants you to explain that it is more.
- Calling a credit card or debit card money. The deposits are the money.
- Listing a function without applying it to the situation in the question.
- Forgetting that inflation affects more than one function.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Identify / State:** name the function. "Unit of account" for comparing prices.
- **Define:** one precise sentence, such as "money is anything generally accepted in exchange for goods and services".
- **Explain:** name the function, say what it means and link it to the situation. "A mortgage is repaid over 25 years, so money is acting as a means of deferred payment because the debt is fixed in pounds and settled later."
- **Calculate:** for inflation questions, divide by (1 + the inflation rate as a decimal), and show the working.
- **Evaluate:** for example, judge how far inflation reduces money's usefulness, weighing a low and stable rate against a high and rising one.

### Annotated model answer

*"Explain why money is defined as more than the banknotes and coins in circulation."*

> Most payments in the UK are made by card or bank transfer, which move bank deposits rather than cash (**point**). These deposits are generally accepted in payment, so they act as a medium of exchange, and they can be saved, so they act as a store of value (**links deposits to the functions**). Therefore deposits do the same job as cash, so a definition of money must include them as well as notes and coins (**conclusion**).

## Frequently asked questions

**Which function of money is the most important?** The medium of exchange, because it removes the need for a double coincidence of wants and allows specialisation.

**Does inflation stop money being money?** Low inflation does not. Very high inflation weakens money as a store of value, a means of deferred payment and eventually a unit of account.

**Is a bank deposit money?** Yes. Deposits are generally accepted in payment through cards and transfers, so they are part of the money supply.
