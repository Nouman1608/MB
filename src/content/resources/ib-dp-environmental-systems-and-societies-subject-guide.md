Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS) is an interdisciplinary IB
Diploma Programme course, encompassing both the sciences and
individuals and societies, offered at both Standard Level (SL) and
Higher Level (HL). ESS combines methodologies, techniques and
knowledge associated with both disciplines, sitting distinctly apart
from the DP's other science and humanities subjects precisely because
it does not belong wholly to either.

## What the course covers

ESS is a complex, contemporary course engaging students with 21st
century environmental issues, requiring a diverse set of skills and
understanding from different disciplines -- from ecosystem structure
and biogeochemical cycles on the scientific side, to resource
management, environmental economics and ethical decision-making on the
societal side. Students develop a
scientific approach through explorations of environmental systems, and
acquire understandings and methods from individuals and societies
subjects while studying sustainability issues within social, cultural,
economic, political and ethical contexts. Because of its
interdisciplinary nature, students can choose to have ESS count as
either a sciences or an individuals-and-societies course, or as both
-- in the latter case, freeing up an additional subject choice from any
other subject group, which is a genuinely useful piece of flexibility
for students who want to concentrate their remaining subject choices
elsewhere, such as an additional language or a second arts subject.

## Aims

The DP describes ESS's aims as encompassing both scientific method and
societal understanding equally, which distinguishes it from a
single-discipline science course:

ESS aims to empower and equip students to:

1. develop understanding of their own environmental impact, in the
   broader context of humanity's impact on the Earth and its
   biosphere
2. develop knowledge of diverse perspectives to address issues of
   sustainability
3. engage with and evaluate the tensions around environmental issues
   using critical thinking
4. develop a systems approach providing a holistic lens for exploring
   environmental issues
5. be inspired to engage with environmental issues across local and
   global contexts, and confident participating as actively responsible
   citizens.

## How it's assessed

Environmental Systems and Societies is assessed through a combination
of external written examinations and an internally assessed individual
investigation, with the balance shifting slightly between Standard
Level and Higher Level. Two written papers make up the external
assessment -- 75% of the final grade at SL and 80% at HL -- combining
short-answer, data-based and extended-response questions that draw on
both the scientific and societal strands of the course. Because the
same core content is examined at both levels, HL's higher external
weighting reflects the more demanding style of question set, rather
than a longer or additional syllabus to prepare for.

The internal assessment is worth 25% at SL and 20% at HL. It takes the
form of an individual investigation, normally built around fieldwork,
in which students formulate their own research question, collect
primary data, apply relevant ESS techniques to analyse it, and draw
evidence-based conclusions in a written report capped at 3,000 words
and around 10 teaching hours of work. Because ESS is designed to be
studied as either a sciences or an individuals-and-societies subject,
this fieldwork-based investigation is deliberately built to draw on
methods from both disciplines rather than treating them separately --
a strong investigation typically combines a scientific data-collection
method (such as quadrat sampling or water-quality testing) with an
analysis that also considers the social, economic or political
dimensions of the issue under study, rather than treating the
fieldwork as a purely scientific exercise.

## SL and HL cover the same core content

Unlike most DP sciences, where HL adds substantial extra content beyond SL, ESS is taught to the
same core syllabus content at both levels -- there is no separate "HL-only" strand of topics the
way there is in, for example, DP Biology or Physics. What changes between SL and HL is depth of
treatment and the demand of assessment: HL students are expected to engage with greater complexity
and more sophisticated evaluation of the same material, examined through papers that ask for more
extended, synthesised responses rather than through additional syllabus content. This is worth
knowing early, since students sometimes assume (incorrectly, based on how other DP sciences work)
that there is a distinct list of HL-only topics to seek out and prioritise.

## Why ESS counts toward two subject groups

Every other DP subject belongs to exactly one of the six subject groups. ESS is the DP's one
genuinely interdisciplinary course, and a student can use it to satisfy either the Group 4
(sciences) requirement or the Group 3 (individuals and societies) requirement -- or, in some DP
combinations, both at once, which frees up an entire additional subject slot for something else in
the student's programme. Confirming with a school's DP coordinator exactly how ESS is being counted
in a given student's subject combination is essential, since this decision affects which other
subject groups still need to be filled.

## Where ESS sits in a typical DP combination

Because ESS blends scientific and social-science approaches, it is often chosen by students who
want the analytical rigour of a science course without committing to a full discrete-science
subject group requirement, or by students building a combination weighted toward the humanities who
still want a genuinely scientific methodology represented in their programme. Either way, the course
still demands the same practical, data-handling and fieldwork skills expected of any DP science --
it is not a "lighter" alternative to Biology, Chemistry or Physics, simply a differently structured
one.
