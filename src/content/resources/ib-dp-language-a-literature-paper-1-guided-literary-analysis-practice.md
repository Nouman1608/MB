## Questions

### Passage A -- poem (original text written for Marlbridge)

> *Night Shift*
>
> At four the ward goes quiet
> the way a field goes quiet under snow:
> not empty, only covered.
>
> She walks the corridor and counts
> the green lights, the slow breaths,
> the one bed where the breathing is too fast.
>
> Her hands know what to do.
> Her hands have always known.
> It is the rest of her
> that stands at the window
>
> and watches the first bus
> cross the bridge with its lights on,
> full of people going to work,
> as if the night were an ordinary thing.

**1.** Identify the simile in the first stanza and explain what it suggests about the ward. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how the list in the second stanza builds tension. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the effect of the repetition in the third stanza and of the break between the third
and fourth stanzas. **[4]**

**4.** The poem is written in free verse. Explain one way this form suits the poem. **[2]**

### Passage B -- prose fiction extract (original text written for Marlbridge)

> The letter came on a Tuesday, which Grandad said was the wrong day for anything important. He
> read it standing up, by the back door, with his glasses pushed onto his forehead, so that he was
> really reading it with his arm held out, the way he read seed packets.
>
> "Well," he said.
>
> I waited. The kettle began its long complaint on the stove, and nobody moved to stop it.
>
> "They want the allotment back," he said at last. "For houses." He folded the letter in half, then
> in half again, then once more, until it was small enough to be nothing, and put it in the pocket
> where he kept string.
>
> That afternoon he planted the beans anyway. I held the canes while he tied them, and neither of
> us said that they would not be ready until August.

**5.** What does the comparison "the way he read seed packets" suggest about Grandad? **[2]**

**6.** Explain the effect of the sentence about the kettle. **[3]**

**7.** Analyse how the writer uses the folding of the letter to show Grandad's response. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the final sentence creates meaning through what is not said. **[3]**

### Passage C -- drama extract (original text written for Marlbridge)

> [A kitchen, late evening. One chair is pushed away from the table. JUNE, in her sixties, is
> drying a plate that is already dry. Her son TOM stands in the doorway with his coat still on.]
>
> TOM: I can stay. Tonight, I mean. If you want.
> JUNE: There's no need.
> TOM: I know there's no need. I'm asking if you want.
> JUNE: [Still drying the plate.] The spare bed's not made up.
> TOM: I can make a bed, Mum.
> JUNE: [She puts the plate down. Picks it up again.] Your father always said you couldn't.
> [Pause. TOM takes off his coat.]

**9.** Explain what the opening stage directions suggest before anyone speaks. **[3]**

**10.** Analyse how dialogue and action work together from "The spare bed's not made up" to the
end of the extract. **[4]**

### Extended tasks

**11.** Guiding question for Passage A: *How does the poem explore the gap between what the woman
does and what she feels?* Write a thesis and a three-paragraph plan, then write one body paragraph
in full. **[10]**

**12.** Guiding question for Passage B: *How does the writer use everyday details to convey strong
feeling?* Write an introduction ending in a thesis, and a three-paragraph plan. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The simile is "the way a field goes quiet under snow" [1]. The next line, "not empty, only
covered", makes the quiet a covering, not an absence [1]. The calm hides pain and danger
underneath, preparing for the next stanza's tension [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use the line after the simile; "it shows the ward is quiet"
only repeats the text.

**2.** The list begins with calm items, "the green lights, the slow breaths" [1], then ends with
its longest item, "the one bed where the breathing is too fast" [1]. The broken pattern pulls the
reader's attention, like hers, onto the patient in danger: routine turns to alarm [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "There is a list of three" is a label; credit comes from explaining why the
last item breaks the pattern.

**3.** The repetition "Her hands know what to do. / Her hands have always known." [1] presents her
skill as automatic, held in her body rather than her thoughts [1]. "It is the rest of her"
separates the working hands from the feeling self, suggesting exhaustion [1]. The sentence runs
across the stanza break, "stands at the window // and watches", so the white space enacts a pause
outside her duties [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comment on a stanza break is analysis of form only when tied to meaning; "the
sentence runs on" alone earns little.

**4.** Free verse has no regular rhyme or metre [1]. The uneven lines follow the rhythm of her
attention, such as the long line for the fast breathing; a neat pattern would make the night sound
tidy [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs a link to something specific in this poem.

**5.** It shows a familiar, slightly comic habit: he reads at arm's length with his glasses on his
forehead [1]. "seed packets" places his life in his garden and hints at what the letter threatens
[1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two-mark "suggest" questions reward two different suggestions; the same idea
said twice gains one.

**6.** The kettle's "long complaint" personifies it [1], so an object voices the distress that
neither person expresses [1]. "nobody moved to stop it" shows both characters frozen by the news:
the noise makes the tense silence audible [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers see the feeling displaced onto an object; weak ones stop at
"personification makes it vivid".

**7.** He folds it "in half, then in half again, then once more" [1]: the building phrases slow the
moment into a controlled action, not an outburst [1]. Folding it until it is "small enough to be
nothing", then keeping it with "string", shrinks the threat into something ordinary: quiet denial
[1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Short embedded quotations keep analysis precise; "this shows he is upset" does
not.

**8.** "planted the beans anyway" shows defiance or stubborn hope [1]. "neither of us said that
they would not be ready until August" leaves the key point unspoken: the reader infers the
allotment may be gone before the harvest [1]. The shared silence of "neither of us" binds narrator
and Grandad in grief and loyalty, stronger than any stated feeling [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Implication questions reward the inference spelled out; "it is sad" does not
say what the reader works out.

**9.** "One chair is pushed away from the table" suggests an absence in the household [1]. Drying "a
plate that is already dry" is pointless, so it reads as grief kept busy [1]. Tom "with his coat
still on" is unsure whether he is staying [1].
**[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Stage directions are evidence in their own right; answers on dialogue alone
miss half the text.

**10.** June meets an emotional offer with a practical objection, "The spare bed's not made up",
avoiding what she wants: subtext [1]. Putting the plate down and picking it up again shows the
indecision her words hide [1]. "Your father" names the absent figure for the first time, in a
gentle joke that reveals the loss behind the scene [1]. The "Pause" and Tom taking off his coat
settle it through action: he will stay, and she has accepted [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest drama answers show words and action pulling against each other,
not just what is said.

**11.** *Model thesis:* The poem presents a woman whose skilled body works on while her feelings stand
apart, through the ward's hidden tension, the split between "hands" and "the rest of her", and the
ordinary morning outside.

```
P1  Hidden feeling in the setting: "not empty, only covered";
    the list that ends "too fast".
P2  Body and self divided: "Her hands ... Her hands";
    "the rest of her"; the stanza break at the window.
P3  Her night against the world's morning: "full of people
    going to work"; "as if the night were an ordinary thing".
```

*Model paragraph (P2):* The poem divides the woman in two. The repeated "Her hands" gives her
skill a life of its own. "The rest of her" is set apart, as if her feelings belong to a separate person who does no work. The sentence
runs over the stanza break, "stands at the window // and watches", so the white space becomes a
pause in which nothing is done. What she does and what she feels are no longer in one place.

Indicative creditworthy points: thesis on doing versus feeling [1]; plan by idea, three stages
[1]; simile as hidden feeling [1]; the list's final item [1]; repeated "Her hands" as automatic
skill [1]; "the rest of her" as a divided self [1]; stanza break as a pause [1]; the final "as
if" contrast [1]; short embedded quotations [1]; paragraph linked back to the question [1].
**[10]**
The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point.
*Examiner insight:* A response built on the question's own terms (doing, feeling) stays focused; a
line-by-line tour drifts into description.

**12.** *Model introduction:* In this prose extract, narrated by a grandchild, a letter tells Grandad his
allotment will be taken for houses. Neither says how they feel; the writer conveys shock, grief
and quiet defiance through a kettle, a folded letter and a row of beans.

```
P1  Shock: "Well," and the kettle's "long complaint" that
    "nobody moved to stop".
P2  Control and denial: the letter folded "small enough to be
    nothing", kept with "string".
P3  Defiance and grief: "planted the beans anyway"; what
    "neither of us said".
```

Indicative creditworthy points: form, narrator and situation [1]; thesis answers the question
[1]; plan by feeling, not device [1]; kettle as displaced feeling [1]; folding as denial [1]; the
unspoken final sentence [1]. **[6]**
The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point.
*Examiner insight:* Strong introductions are short and end on a thesis, not a plot summary.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the passage instead of arguing an answer to the guiding question.
- Naming a technique ("enjambment", "personification") and not saying what it does here.
- Calling the speaker of Passage A "the poet", or treating the narrator of Passage B as the author.
- Ignoring stage directions in drama, or line and stanza breaks in poetry.
- Vague effects that fit any text: "it makes the reader want to read on".
- Inventing context, such as a date or the writer's life.
- Long quotations dropped in, or no conclusion because time ran out.
