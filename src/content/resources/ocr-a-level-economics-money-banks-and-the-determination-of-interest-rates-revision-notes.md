## Money in one page

| Item | Key points |
|---|---|
| Functions | Medium of exchange; unit of account; store of value; standard of deferred payment |
| Characteristics | Acceptable, durable, portable, divisible, uniform, limited in supply |
| Money supply | Stock of money held by the public and firms at a point in time. A bank's own till cash is not counted |
| Liquidity | How quickly and cheaply an asset becomes spending power without losing value |
| Narrow money | Most liquid: notes and coins held by the public plus deposits spendable on demand |
| Broad money | Narrow money plus less liquid deposits such as savings and time deposits |

Match each characteristic to a function. Acceptable and divisible serve exchange. Durable and limited supply serve a store of value. Uniform units serve the unit of account.

**Barter arithmetic.** Rates needed = n(n − 1)/2. For 9 goods that is 36 rates. With money there are 9 prices.

**Narrow and broad.** Notes and coins 25, current accounts 95, savings 210, time deposits 70. Narrow = 25 + 95 = 120. Broad = 120 + 210 + 70 = 400. Moving 40 from current to savings: narrow 80, broad 400.

## Formulas

| Result | Formula |
|---|---|
| Bank credit multiplier (maximum) | 1 / reserve ratio |
| Maximum total deposits | Initial deposit / reserve ratio |
| Credit created | Maximum deposits − initial deposit |
| Multiplier with cash drain | 1 / (reserve ratio + cash drain ratio) |
| Real interest rate (approximate) | Nominal rate − inflation rate |
| Real interest rate (exact) | (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1 |
| Nominal rate needed | (1 + real) × (1 + expected inflation) − 1 |
| Fisher equation of exchange | MV = PT |
| Price level | P = MV / T |
| Velocity | V = PT / M |
| Growth form | %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔT |
| Bond yield | Annual payment / price × 100 |

## Method: bank credit creation

1. Write the reserve ratio as a decimal.
2. Maximum total deposits = initial deposit / ratio.
3. Credit created = total minus the initial deposit.
4. If cash is withdrawn and not redeposited, add the drain ratio to the reserve ratio before dividing.
5. State that this is a maximum.

**Reminder.** New deposit 500, reserve ratio 20%. Maximum deposits = 500 / 0.20 = 2,500. Credit created = 2,000. Multiplier = 5.

**Theory versus practice.**

| | Theory | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | A deposit arrives | A loan is agreed and a deposit is credited |
| Constraint | Reserve ratio | Demand for loans from creditworthy borrowers, bank willingness to lend, capital and liquidity requirements |
| Direction | Money supply set by reserves | Money supply responds to demand for credit |
| Repayment | Not considered | Repaying a loan removes a deposit, so money is destroyed |

## Interest rate determination

**Liquidity preference theory** (money market).
- Money demand slopes downward: a higher rate raises the opportunity cost of holding money.
- Money supply is vertical, set by the central bank.
- Axes: interest rate on the vertical axis, quantity of money on the horizontal axis.
- Increase in money supply: supply shifts right, rate falls.
- Increase in income or spending: demand shifts right, rate rises.
- Adjustment: excess money is used to buy bonds, bond prices rise and yields fall.

**Loanable funds theory** (saving and borrowing).
- Supply of funds slopes upward: it comes from saving.
- Demand for funds slopes downward: it comes from borrowers.
- Higher saving: supply shifts right, rate falls, more funds lent.
- Higher business confidence: demand shifts right, rate rises, more funds lent.

**Reminder numbers.** Liquidity preference with Md = 200 − 10r and Ms = 120: r = 8%. With Ms = 150: r = 5%. Loanable funds with Qs = 20 + 5r and Qd = 110 − 10r: 15r = 90, so r = 6% and funds are 50.

**Bond reminder.** A bond paying 6 a year at a price of 120 yields 5%. At a price of 75 it yields 8%. Price and yield move in opposite directions.

**Distinctions.**

| | Liquidity preference | Loanable funds |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Stock of money | Flow of saving and borrowing |
| Supply | Central bank | Saving |
| Demand | Money held | Borrowing |
| Emphasis | Short run | Link to saving and investment |

## Diagram checklist

Draw both interest rate diagrams with the interest rate on the vertical axis.

| Event | Liquidity preference diagram | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Central bank raises money supply | Ms shifts right | Rate falls |
| Central bank cuts money supply | Ms shifts left | Rate rises |
| Incomes and spending rise | Md shifts right | Rate rises |
| Greater caution about spending | Md shifts left | Rate falls |

| Event | Loanable funds diagram | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Households save more | Supply shifts right | Rate falls, more funds lent |
| Firms become more pessimistic | Demand shifts left | Rate falls, fewer funds lent |
| Firms become more optimistic | Demand shifts right | Rate rises, more funds lent |

Always label both axes, both curves, the original and new equilibrium and the arrow showing the direction of the shift.

## Inflation and the nominal interest rate

- **Nominal** rate: the quoted rate. **Real** rate: adjusted for inflation.
- Reminder: nominal 9%, inflation 6%. Approximate real = 3%. Exact = 1.09 / 1.06 − 1 = 2.83%.
- Savers lose in real terms when inflation exceeds the nominal rate. Borrowers gain when inflation is higher than expected when the loan was agreed.
- Lenders look at **expected** inflation. A lender wanting a 2.5% real return with 3% expected inflation needs 1.025 × 1.03 − 1 = 5.6%.
- Higher expected inflation tends to raise nominal rates. This is often called the Fisher effect.
- Evaluate: nominal rates do not always move one for one with expected inflation. Central bank policy, risk and the supply of saving also matter.

## The Fisher equation of exchange and the quantity theory

**Reminder.** M = 60, V = 5, T = 100. P = 60 × 5 / 100 = 3.00. If M rises 10% to 66, P = 3.30. The rise is 10%.

**Identity or theory?** MV = PT is true by definition. It becomes the **quantity theory of money** when V and T are assumed constant, so that P changes in proportion to M.

| Case for | Case against |
|---|---|
| Long-run link between faster money growth and higher inflation | V can change, for example when people hold more money in uncertain times |
| Clear policy implication | T can rise when there is spare capacity |
| Explains high inflation | Money may follow spending if banks lend on demand |
| | Inflation can be cost-push |

**Judgement.** Strongest over the long run and at high inflation. Weakest in the short run and when there is spare capacity. Always say which assumption fails and what happens to P as a result.

## Quick self-test

1. Among 9 goods, how many separate exchange rates would barter need, and how many prices would a money economy need?
2. M = 60, V = 5 and T = 100. Find P.
3. P = 2.5, T = 400 and M = 200. Find V.
4. A bank holds a 20% reserve ratio. A new cash deposit of 500 is made. Find the maximum credit created.
5. Nominal rate 9%, inflation 6%. Find the exact real rate.
6. A lender wants a 2.5% real return and expects 3% inflation. Find the exact nominal rate.
7. Loanable funds: Qs = 20 + 5r and Qd = 110 − 10r. Find the equilibrium rate and funds lent.
8. Liquidity preference: Md = 200 − 10r. Find the rate when Ms = 120 and when Ms = 150.
9. A bond pays 6 a year. Find the yield at a price of 120 and of 75.
10. Notes and coins 25, current accounts 95, savings 210, time deposits 70. Find narrow and broad money.

### Answers

1. 9 × 8 / 2 = 36 rates; 9 prices.
2. P = 60 × 5 / 100 = 3.
3. V = PT / M = 2.5 × 400 / 200 = 5.
4. Maximum deposits = 500 / 0.20 = 2,500, so credit created = 2,000.
5. 1.09 / 1.06 − 1 = 2.83%.
6. 1.025 × 1.03 − 1 = 5.575%, about 5.6%.
7. 20 + 5r = 110 − 10r, so r = 6%; funds = 20 + 30 = 50.
8. 200 − 10r = 120 gives 8%; 200 − 10r = 150 gives 5%.
9. 6 / 120 = 5%; 6 / 75 = 8%.
10. Narrow = 120; broad = 400.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the multiplier is 5" without saying it is a maximum.
- Including a bank's own cash in the money supply.
- Describing narrow money as "cash only" when it includes spendable deposits.
- Drawing a diagram without labelled axes, the equilibrium rate or the direction of the shift.
- Shifting the wrong curve: an increase in the money supply moves the supply curve, not demand.
- Quoting the nominal rate as if it were the real return.
- Calling the quantity theory proven. It rests on assumptions about V and T, and so test them.
- Giving a list of weaknesses without a judgement about when the theory is more or less relevant.
- Mixing up the two theories: liquidity preference is about money, loanable funds is about saving and borrowing.
