This set practises key question 1.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What types of data and
evidence do sociologists use?* Parts (a) to (e) follow the shape the syllabus describes for the
compulsory Paper 1 Question 1; parts (f) to (i) give extra practice on secondary evidence and on
reading a table.

---

## Source A (invented for practice)

*A researcher investigated how young adults in one country feel about marriage. Official statistics
showed that the number of marriages registered per 1,000 adults fell from 8.2 in 2005 to 5.1 in 2023.
She also analysed 2,000 public social media posts that used a popular wedding hashtag, and carried
out unstructured interviews with 12 newly married couples whom she contacted through one wedding
photographer. Many couples said they had delayed marriage until they could afford a large
celebration.*

## Source B (invented for practice)

*Survey of 250 households in one town, carried out by telephone: percentage of households with home
internet access.*

| Year | 2010 | 2015 | 2020 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Households with home internet | 48% | 61% | 77% | 86% |

---

## Questions

**(a)(i)** Using Source A, identify the marriage rate in 2023. **[1]**

**(a)(ii)** Using Source A, explain why the researcher's study uses both quantitative and qualitative
data. **[3]**

**(a)(iii)** Using Source A, explain **two** problems with the evidence in the study. **[4]**

**(b)** Define the terms *primary data* and *secondary data*. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **three** strengths of official statistics for sociologists. **[6]**

**(d)** Evaluate the view that personal documents are of little use to sociologists. **[14]**

**(e)** You have been asked to investigate how older people use the internet to stay in contact with
their families. Explain the primary research methods, sampling and secondary evidence you would use,
and give reasons for your choices. **[8]**

### Extra practice on secondary evidence and data analysis (1.2.1 to 1.2.3)

**(f)** Explain **two** limitations of using media content as sociological evidence. **[4]**

**(g)** Explain **two** ethical problems with using social media posts as data. **[4]**

**(h)** Using Source B, describe the trend in home internet access and explain **one** reason why the
figures may not be representative of the town. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain the difference between quantitative and qualitative data, giving an example of each.
**[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### (a)(i) [1]

5.1 marriages per 1,000 adults (1). The unit is needed: "5.1%" is wrong.

### (a)(ii) [3]

The marriage statistics are **quantitative** — numbers showing a trend over time (1). The interviews
with couples produce **qualitative** data about their reasons, such as waiting until they could
afford a celebration (1). Using both lets the researcher show *how much* marriage has changed and
*why* young adults delay it, so each type of data fills a gap left by the other (1).

### (a)(iii) [4]

Two problems, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for applying it to the source):

- **Unrepresentative interview sample** — all 12 couples came through one wedding photographer, so
  they are likely to be people who chose a paid, celebrated wedding; couples who married quietly or
  not at all are missing.
- **Small sample** — 12 couples is too few to generalise about young adults across a country.
- **Hashtag posts are self-selecting** — people who use a wedding hashtag are already enthusiastic
  about weddings, so the posts are biased towards positive views; posts may also be edited images
  rather than honest feelings.
- **Official statistics count only registered marriages** — religious or customary marriages that
  are not registered, and couples living together, are missed, so the fall may be exaggerated.
- **Ethics** — the posts were used without the users' informed consent.

### (b) [4]

- **Primary data (2):** data collected first-hand by the researcher for the current study (1), for
  example the researcher's own interviews (1).
- **Secondary data (2):** data that already exists, produced by someone else for a different purpose
  (1), for example official statistics or diaries (1).

### (c) [6]

Three strengths, 2 marks each (identify + develop):

- **Large scale** — a census or registration system covers almost the whole population, which no
  individual researcher could survey, so findings are representative.
- **Trends over time** — figures are collected regularly in similar ways, so changes such as falling
  marriage rates can be tracked across decades.
- **Cheap and accessible** — they are usually free and already collected, saving time and money.
- **Comparison** — figures for regions or countries can be compared to look for patterns and
  correlations.
- **Little researcher influence** — no interviewer is present, so there is no interviewer effect.

### (d) [14]

**Levels guidance:** 11–14 for a balanced, well-developed answer using evidence and evaluation
concepts, with a reasoned conclusion; 7–10 for a mostly one-sided or less developed answer; 4–6 for
limited points with little development; 1–3 for simple assertions.

**Arguments for the view (little use):**
- **Authenticity** — documents may be forged or wrongly dated.
- **Credibility** — writers may exaggerate, hide things or write for an audience, as in
  autobiographies written to protect a reputation.
- **Representativeness** — in the past only literate and often wealthier people kept diaries or wrote
  letters, so documents may not reflect ordinary lives.
- **Interpretation** — the researcher decides what a document means, raising subjectivity and low
  reliability.
- **Availability** — documents may be incomplete or lost.

**Arguments against the view (useful):**
- They are the only evidence for the past; Laslett's use of parish records (1965) challenged beliefs
  about pre-industrial families.
- They reveal **meanings and motivations** in people's own words, giving high validity —
  interpretivists value them for verstehen. Thomas and Znaniecki's study of Polish migrants' letters
  (1918–1920) is an example.
- They are produced without a researcher present, so there is no interviewer or Hawthorne effect.
- They can be **triangulated** with official statistics to explain trends.

**Conclusion:** usefulness depends on the research aim and on checking authenticity and credibility;
documents are weak for measuring patterns but valuable for understanding meanings and the past.
Credit any reasoned judgement.

### (e) [8]

The syllabus states that no subject knowledge of the topic is needed and that you are **not** expected
to evaluate your methodology. Credit:

- **Primary method(s) with reasons** — semi-structured interviews in participants' homes, to explore
  what online contact means to older people (validity) and to allow help with any questions they find
  confusing; possibly a short questionnaire with closed questions on how often they use video calls
  or messaging, to show patterns.
- **Sampling** — a sampling frame such as membership lists of community centres or local doctors'
  patient lists (with permission); a stratified sample by age band and gender so that people in their
  sixties and in their eighties are both represented; snowball sampling to reach older people who live
  alone.
- **Secondary evidence** — official statistics on internet use by age to set the wider pattern;
  published reports from organisations working with older people, with reasons (cheap, large scale).
- **Practical and ethical points** — informed consent, confidentiality, interviews offered face to face
  so that people without internet are not excluded.

Levels: 7–8 for a clear, justified plan covering methods, sampling and secondary evidence; 4–6 for a
plan with some reasons; 1–3 for listed methods with little justification.

### (f) [4]

Two limitations, 2 marks each:

- **Reflects producers' agenda** — owners, editors and journalists select and frame stories, so
  content shows how an issue is represented, not how it really is (1 + 1).
- **Audience interpretation** — counting images in content analysis does not show how viewers
  understand them; different audiences read the same report differently (1 + 1).
- **Researcher's categories** — deciding what counts as, for example, a "negative" story involves
  judgement, reducing reliability (1 + 1).

### (g) [4]

Two problems, 2 marks each:

- **Informed consent** — users did not agree to their posts being studied, even if the posts are
  public (1 + 1).
- **Privacy and confidentiality** — quoting a post can allow the author to be identified through a
  search, exposing them to harm (1 + 1).
- **Vulnerable users** — the researcher may not know a user's age, so children's posts could be used
  without parental consent (1 + 1).

### (h) [4]

- **Trend (2):** home internet access rose throughout the period (1), from 48% of households in 2010
  to 86% in 2025 (1).
- **Reason not representative (2):** one reason identified (1) and its effect explained (1). For
  example, the survey was carried out by telephone (1), so households without a phone line, or that
  ignore unknown callers, are missed and the sample no longer matches the town (1). Alternatively, 250
  households is a small sample (1) that may not reflect the town's mix of ages and incomes (1).

### (i) [4]

- **Quantitative data (2):** numerical data that can be counted and compared (1), for example the
  percentages in Source B (1).
- **Qualitative data (2):** descriptive data in words or images that shows meaning (1), for example the
  couples' explanations of why they delayed marriage in Source A (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name studies in source questions?** No — in part (a) the marks come from using the
source. Named studies are more useful in part (d).

**How precise must "identify" answers be?** Exactly as printed, with the unit; rounding or changing
units can lose the mark.
