## Factors affecting location

- **Closeness to customers.** Essential for retail and services that customers visit, less so for online sellers.
- **Closeness to suppliers and raw materials.** Important where materials are heavy or bulky, or perishable.
- **Labour.** The supply of workers with the right skills and the wage levels in the area.
- **Costs.** Rent or purchase price, business rates, utilities and transport costs.
- **Transport and communications.** Road, rail, port and airport links, and broadband.
- **Government help.** Grants, tax incentives and enterprise zones in some areas.
- **Competitors.** Being near rivals can attract customers (as with restaurants in a street), or increase competition.
- **Room to expand.** Space for extra capacity.
- **Legal and environmental restrictions.** Planning permission and rules on noise, pollution and waste.
- **Personal and ethical factors.** An owner's home area or commitment to a community.
- **Digital infrastructure.** Reliable internet matters to online and data-based businesses.

The weight of each factor depends on the business. Heavy manufacturing looks at transport and materials. A hairdresser looks at footfall. A software firm looks at skilled staff and broadband.

## Comparing locations

Typical choices include city centre, out-of-town, a business park, a rural site or an overseas site. City centres give footfall and a large labour pool, but have high rents and congestion. Out-of-town sites give space, parking and lower costs, but are further from customers and may rely on cars. Regional sites may attract grants but are further from large markets.

### Worked example 1: choosing a site

Tarn Interiors (invented) makes 60,000 units a year with 12 staff and must choose between two sites. It also scores each site against factors weighted by importance.

**Cost comparison.**

| Annual cost | Site A: city | Site B: out of town |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | £96,000 | £54,000 |
| Labour (12 staff) | 12 × £30,000 = £360,000 | 12 × £27,000 = £324,000 |
| Transport | £30,000 | £52,000 |
| **Total** | **£486,000** | **£430,000** |
| Cost per unit (÷ 60,000) | £8.10 | £7.17 |

Site B is £56,000 a year cheaper, or about £0.93 per unit.

**Weighted score.** Each factor is scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) and multiplied by its weight (weights add to 100).

| Factor | Weight | A score | B score | A weighted | B weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost | 40 | 2 | 4 | 80 | 160 |
| Skilled labour | 20 | 5 | 3 | 100 | 60 |
| Access to customers | 20 | 5 | 2 | 100 | 40 |
| Room to expand | 10 | 1 | 5 | 10 | 50 |
| Transport links | 10 | 4 | 4 | 40 | 40 |
| **Total** | 100 | | | **330** | **350** |

Dividing by 100 gives 3.30 for A and 3.50 for B.

**Evaluation.** B is cheaper and scores slightly higher, but the margin is small. The result is sensitive to the weights and scores, which are judgements. If access to customers were weighted at 30 and cost at 30, A would score 3.60 and B 3.30, so A would win. The scoring method gives a structure, but it gives a false sense of precision.

**Recommendation.** If cost and room to expand are the priority, as for a manufacturer that sells to trade customers, B is the better choice. If customers visit the premises, A is better. Tarn sells through retailers and online, so it should choose B, but should check that it can recruit skilled staff in the area.

## Logistics

**Logistics** is the management of the movement and storage of materials and products from suppliers to customers. Its parts are:

- **Supply chain management.** Coordinating all the businesses and activities that turn raw materials into products delivered to customers, including suppliers, makers, stores and shops. The aim is smooth flow, low cost and no shortages.
- **Distribution management.** Planning how finished goods get to customers: routes, vehicles, timetables, and the choice between road, rail, sea and air.
- **Warehousing.** Storing stock until it is needed. Warehouses hold buffer stock and allow bulk buying, but they cost rent, labour and insurance.
- **Distribution centres.** Large facilities that receive goods in bulk, sort them and dispatch them to shops or customers. They shorten delivery times and cut the number of journeys.
- **Digital logistics.** Software that tracks stock and vehicles, plans routes, links systems with suppliers and customers, and gives customers delivery updates.

**Factors affecting logistical decisions:** cost; speed; reliability; nature of the product (perishable, fragile, bulky or valuable); the number and location of customers; the environment (emissions and congestion); and the technology available.

### Worked example 2: own delivery fleet or an external provider

Tarn Interiors makes 24,000 deliveries a year. An in-house fleet needs 3 vans (lease £14,000 each a year), 3 drivers (£28,000 each), insurance of £6,000 and fuel of £18,000, which varies with the number of deliveries. An external logistics firm charges £7.20 a delivery.

In-house fixed costs = 3 × £14,000 + 3 × £28,000 + £6,000 = £42,000 + £84,000 + £6,000 = **£132,000**.

In-house total at 24,000 deliveries = £132,000 + £18,000 = £150,000, which is **£6.25** a delivery. External = 24,000 × £7.20 = £172,800. The in-house fleet is £22,800 cheaper.

If volume falls to 15,000 deliveries, fuel falls to 15,000 × £0.75 = £11,250 (£0.75 a delivery). In-house cost = £132,000 + £11,250 = £143,250, or **£9.55** a delivery. External = 15,000 × £7.20 = £108,000. Now the external provider is £35,250 cheaper.

The break-even volume is where £132,000 + £0.75x = £7.20x, so x = 132,000 ÷ 6.45 = **20,465 deliveries**. Above that, in-house is cheaper; below it, external is.

**Evaluation.**

- In-house gives control over timing and customer contact, and the vans can carry the firm's brand. It carries fixed costs and risks: unused vans, driver sickness, vehicle breakdowns.
- External provision is flexible, gives access to a larger network and expert routing, and turns fixed costs into variable ones. The business loses some control, and a poor delivery may be blamed on Tarn.
- Demand that varies suggests external provision; steady high demand suggests in-house.

**Recommendation.** If Tarn expects 24,000 deliveries a year or more, in-house is cheaper and gives control; if demand is uncertain, an external provider reduces risk. A mix, with in-house for local runs and an external firm for peaks and distant deliveries, may be best.

## Outsourcing, subcontracting, offshoring and re-shoring

- **Outsourcing:** paying another business to carry out a function or process that the business could do itself, such as payroll, IT or delivery.
- **Subcontracting:** passing part of a specific job or order to another business, often to cope with peaks in demand or to use a specialist skill.
- **Offshoring:** moving production or a business function to another country, usually to cut costs, such as labour.
- **Re-shoring:** bringing production or functions back to the home country.

### Worked example 3: should Tarn offshore production?

Tarn makes 40,000 units of a product in the UK at a cost of £14.00 each. An overseas factory could make them for £9.50. Extra costs are: shipping £1.80 a unit, import duties and paperwork at 6% of the £9.50 (£0.57) and extra holding costs of £0.35 a unit because the longer lead time requires more stock.

Offshore cost per unit = £9.50 + £1.80 + £0.57 + £0.35 = **£12.22**. Saving = £14.00 − £12.22 = £1.78 a unit, or 40,000 × £1.78 = **£71,200** a year.

One-off set-up costs (visits, tooling, redundancy) are £150,000, so payback = £150,000 ÷ £71,200 = **2.1 years**.

If an extra 4% of units are defective, each costing £9.50 in materials, extra cost = 4% × £9.50 = £0.38, so the cost becomes £12.60. The saving is then £1.40 a unit, or £56,000, and payback becomes £150,000 ÷ £56,000 = **2.7 years**.

**Evaluation.**

- The saving is real but modest compared with the risk. Exchange rates, tariff changes, shipping delays and political events can wipe it out.
- Quality control is harder at a distance, and the product takes longer to reach customers.
- UK workers could lose their jobs and some customers prefer UK-made goods; there may also be reputation risk if overseas working conditions are poor.
- Some firms have re-shored because of rising overseas wages, supply chain disruption, a wish for faster response, and automation that reduces the labour cost advantage.

**Judgement.** Offshoring is attractive only if Tarn expects high volumes for many years and has a reliable partner. Faster delivery and design changes would favour keeping production in the UK or subcontracting only the high-volume basic items.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a list of location factors without saying which matters most for this business.
- Treating a weighted score as an accurate answer rather than a way to structure a judgement.
- Confusing outsourcing, subcontracting and offshoring. Offshoring is about the country; outsourcing is about who does the work.
- Ignoring the hidden costs of offshoring such as shipping, stock and quality.
- Forgetting that logistics includes storing goods, not only moving them.

## Exam technique

When recommending a location, tie the factors to the product, customers and size of the business, and compare at least two sites. For logistics, use the case to decide between in-house and external provision, and calculate the cost if figures are given. State what the answer depends on.
