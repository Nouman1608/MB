## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene mutation | A change to the base sequence of a gene |
| Substitution | One base replaced by another |
| Insertion / deletion | Bases added to / lost from the sequence |
| Frameshift | Change in reading frame after an insertion or deletion not in a multiple of three |
| SNP | A single-base position that varies between individuals, produced by substitution |
| Degeneracy | Most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon |
| Mutagen | Chemical or radiation that increases the mutation rate |
| Germ cell | Cell that gives rise to gametes; its mutations can be inherited |
| Somatic cell | Body cell; its mutations are not inherited but can cause cancer |
| Gene knockout (HL) | Making a gene inoperative to investigate its function |
| Guide RNA (HL) | RNA whose sequence matches the target, leading Cas9 to it |
| Conserved sequence (HL) | Sequence identical or similar across a species or group of species |

## D1.3.1 to D1.3.3 Types and consequences

| Type | Frame | Usual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution | Kept | None (silent), one amino acid changed, or an early stop |
| Insertion or deletion of 1 or 2 bases | Shifted | All downstream codons changed; usually non-functional polypeptide |
| Insertion or deletion of 3 bases | Kept | One amino acid gained or lost; may still disrupt folding |
| Large insertion or deletion | Varies | Usually non-functional |

**Sickle cell anaemia (substitution).** Coding strand GAG to GTG at codon 6 of the beta-globin gene; mRNA GAG to GUG; glutamic acid to valine. Hydrophobic valine makes haemoglobin form fibres at low oxygen; red cells sickle, block capillaries and are destroyed.

**Cystic fibrosis (deletion).** Three bases lost from the CFTR gene; one phenylalanine missing; frame kept, but the chloride channel misfolds and is mostly broken down, so mucus is thick.

**Method: classify a substitution**
1. Write the old and new mRNA codons.
2. Look both up in the code.
3. Same amino acid: silent. Different amino acid: missense. Stop codon: nonsense.

## D1.3.4 Causes

| Mutagen | Damage |
|---|---|
| Ultraviolet | Thymine dimers on one strand |
| X-rays, gamma rays | Breaks in the sugar-phosphate backbone |
| Benzo[a]pyrene (tobacco smoke) | Product binds guanine, causing copying errors |
| Nitrous acid | Alters bases so they mispair |

Also: replication or repair errors that are not corrected.

## D1.3.5 Randomness

- Can occur at any base; some bases (such as methylated cytosine) change more often.
- No known natural mechanism directs a change to a chosen base to alter a trait.
- Evidence: replica plating. Antibiotic-resistant colonies appear at the same positions on every replica, so resistance existed before exposure; the antibiotic selected it.

## D1.3.6 and D1.3.7 Consequences and variation

- Germ cell mutation: can be inherited; present in every cell of the offspring.
- Somatic mutation: passed only to descendant cells by mitosis; several in cell cycle genes can cause cancer.
- Mutation is the only source of new alleles; meiosis and fertilisation only recombine them.
- Most mutations are harmful or neutral; rare beneficial ones supply variation for natural selection.

## HL only: D1.3.8 to D1.3.10

**Knockout.** Disable a gene, compare with normal organisms, infer function. Knockout libraries exist for model species such as mice, yeast and *Arabidopsis*. Caution: other genes may compensate.

**CRISPR-Cas9 steps.**
1. Design a guide RNA of about 20 bases matching the target.
2. Guide RNA and Cas9 form a complex; the guide base-pairs with the target DNA.
3. Cas9 cuts both strands.
4. Repair by rejoining often causes small insertions or deletions (knockout); repair with a supplied template makes a precise change.

**Example of use.** UK authorisation in 2023 of a treatment for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia: blood stem cells edited outside the body to restart fetal haemoglobin production, then returned.

**Ethics.** Body-cell editing in consenting patients versus heritable embryo editing; differing national rules; international efforts to agree standards.

**Conserved sequences.** Example: histone H4. Hypotheses: functional requirement (selection removes changes) or a slower mutation rate. Test: selection predicts silent changes still accumulate; a low mutation rate predicts few changes of any kind.

## Exam traps

- "Mutations always cause disease": many are silent or neutral.
- "Every deletion causes a frameshift": three-base deletions do not.
- "The antibiotic caused resistance": it selected existing mutants.
- "Somatic mutations are passed to children": they are not.
- "Meiosis makes new alleles": only mutation does.
- (HL) "Cas9 recognises the sequence": the guide RNA does.
- (HL) "No phenotype in a knockout means no function": other genes may compensate.

## Self-test

**1.** Distinguish between a substitution and an insertion in terms of the reading frame.

**2.** What is a SNP, and what kind of mutation produces one?

**3.** Why might a substitution have no effect on a polypeptide?

**4.** Give the base change, codon change and amino acid change in sickle cell anaemia.

**5.** Why does the common cystic fibrosis deletion not cause a frameshift, yet still stop the protein working?

**6.** Name one form of radiation and one chemical that are mutagens, and state how one of them damages DNA.

**7.** What did replica plating show about the origin of antibiotic resistance?

**8.** Why is a mutation in a skin cell not inherited, while one in a cell of the testis may be?

**9.** Why is mutation described as the original source of all genetic variation?

**10.** (HL) What is a knockout organism used for?

**11.** (HL) State the roles of the guide RNA and of Cas9.

**12.** (HL) Give the two hypotheses for highly conserved sequences.

## Self-test answers

**1.** A substitution keeps the reading frame; an insertion of one or two bases shifts it so all later codons change.

**2.** A single base position that differs between individuals in a population; it is produced by a base substitution.

**3.** The code is degenerate, so the new codon may code for the same amino acid.

**4.** Coding strand GAG to GTG (mRNA GAG to GUG); glutamic acid replaced by valine.

**5.** Three bases are lost, one codon's worth, so the frame is kept; but the missing phenylalanine makes the channel protein misfold and it is broken down.

**6.** Radiation: ultraviolet (forms thymine dimers) or X-rays or gamma rays (break the backbone). Chemical: benzo[a]pyrene or nitrous acid.

**7.** Resistant mutants were already present before exposure to the antibiotic; the antibiotic only selected them.

**8.** Skin cells are somatic and do not form gametes; cells in the testis are germ cells that give rise to sperm, so their mutations can reach offspring.

**9.** It is the only process that creates new alleles; other processes only recombine existing alleles.

**10.** To find the function of a gene by making it inoperative and comparing the organism with normal individuals.

**11.** The guide RNA base-pairs with the target sequence and leads Cas9 to it; Cas9 cuts both DNA strands there.

**12.** The gene product has a functional requirement so selection removes changes; or the sequence has a slower rate of mutation.
