## Questions

**1.** Explain two ways in which different world views about land led to conflict between settlers and Indigenous peoples. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract, which is *constructed for practice* and is not a real quotation.

> "At the signing, the chiefs understood that they kept authority over their lands and villages, and that the Queen's governor would keep order among the settlers. They did not believe they had given away everything." (A historian's summary, constructed for practice)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the Māori understanding of the Treaty of Waitangi. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the Crown's understanding may have differed. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the social and economic impact of the gold rushes in Australia or New Zealand. **[6]**

**4.** To what extent was the First World War the main influence on the growth of national identity in Australia or New Zealand? **[15]**

**5.** Evaluate the successes and failures of government policy towards Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, or Māori, between 1945 and 2020. **[15]**

**6.** Discuss the extent to which the Federation movement was a response to external threats. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the point, 1 for the link to conflict). For example: Aboriginal peoples saw land as Country with spiritual and legal duties, so fencing, farming and sacred-site damage by squatters broke law and kinship, leading to resistance; for Māori, land (whenua) was held by groups through ancestry, so individual sales and later the Native Land Court cut across tribal authority and led to disputes and war; settlers saw land as private property to be used, so they saw Indigenous use as wasted land and claimed it. **[4]**

**2.** **(a)** The extract suggests that the chiefs believed they kept authority over land and communities (1), and that the Crown's role was to keep order among settlers (1). This links to the Māori text, which guaranteed *tino rangatiratanga* and gave the Crown *kāwanatanga* (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Award up to 3. The English text speaks of ceding sovereignty, so officials, settlers and the Crown could see the Treaty as giving them ultimate authority (1). Hobson and the Colonial Office wanted to control settlement and prevent conflict, which meant the Crown aimed at governing and acquiring land (1). The difference between the texts, and translation of key terms, allowed each side to read the agreement in its own favour (1). **[3]**

**3.** Marking points (up to 6): large-scale migration brought population growth, new towns and trade (up to 2); wealth and spending stimulated farming, shipping and services, and funded public works (up to 1); the Eureka Stockade (1854) or similar disputes showed demands for political rights (up to 1); tension with Chinese miners shows social division (up to 1); impact on Indigenous peoples through the loss of land and displacement (up to 1). **[6]**

**4.** Marking points (up to 15):
- Thesis and a definition of national identity (up to 2).
- The war: Gallipoli and the ANZAC legend, the scale of loss, ANZAC Day, the conscription debate in Australia (up to 4).
- Earlier influences: Federation in 1901 and dominion status in 1907, the bush legend, sport, art and literature, such as the Heidelberg School and the 1905 rugby tour (up to 4).
- Who was left out: Indigenous peoples and the experience of Aboriginal and Māori soldiers (up to 2).
- Perspectives: historians who see the war as the "birth of the nation" and those who say identity already existed (up to 1).
- A judgement consistent with the evidence (up to 2). **[15]**

**5.** Marking points (up to 15):
- Thesis and criteria for "success" (up to 2).
- Australia: 1962 vote, the 1967 referendum, land rights in 1976, Mabo (1992), the Native Title Act (1993), Bringing Them Home (1997) and the apology (2008). New Zealand: the Waitangi Tribunal (1975), Māori language recognition (1987), settlements such as Ngāi Tahu (1998) (up to 5).
- Failures and gaps: continuing gaps in health, income and imprisonment; slow settlements; the Foreshore and Seabed dispute (up to 3).
- The roles of Indigenous activism, such as the Tent Embassy or the land march (up to 2).
- Perspectives: views that stress progress and views that stress limits (up to 1).
- A reasoned, consistent judgement (up to 2). **[15]**

**6.** Marking points (up to 6): external threats included fear of German, French and Russian expansion in the Pacific and concern about Asian migration (up to 2); internal reasons included a common market without customs barriers, shared railways and water, and a growing sense of national identity (up to 2); a judgement weighing the two, for example that external fear provided urgency while internal economic and political advantages explain why the colonies agreed on a constitution (up to 2). **[6]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I write about both countries in one essay?**
Yes, for the early topics, if the question allows it. For the post-1945 topics, write on the one country you chose.

**How many Indigenous examples should I use?**
Use at least two or three specific examples, such as a law, a decision and a movement, with dates.
