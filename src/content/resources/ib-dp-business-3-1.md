## The role of finance: two meanings

"Finance" is used in two ways in business, and answers are clearer when you know which you mean.

**1. Finance as money the business needs.** Every business needs funds:

* **to start up**, before any revenue arrives
* **to operate** from day to day, paying wages, suppliers and bills while waiting for customers to pay
* **to replace** worn-out or outdated assets
* **to expand**, for example into new markets or products
* **to survive shocks**, such as a sudden fall in sales or a large unexpected repair

**2. Finance as a business function.** The finance department (or, in a small business, the owner and an accountant) carries out several jobs:

| Job | What it involves | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Raising funds | Choosing and arranging sources of finance (section 3.2) | The wrong source can be costly or risky |
| Managing cash | Making sure money is available when bills fall due (section 3.7) | A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash |
| Recording | Keeping accurate records of transactions and producing final accounts (section 3.4) | Owners, lenders and tax authorities rely on them |
| Analysing and advising | Calculating ratios and appraising investments (sections 3.5 and 3.8) | Managers need evidence for decisions |
| Controlling spending | Comparing actual spending with plans | Stops costs drifting out of control |

### Applying the role of finance (AO2)

The role changes with the stage and situation of the business. A start-up needs finance mainly to buy assets and survive until sales build. An established firm planning growth needs it to fund expansion without running short of cash. A firm in decline may need finance just to meet its debts while it restructures. Always say which need matters most for the business in the case, and why.

## Capital expenditure and revenue expenditure

**Capital expenditure** is spending on non-current assets that will be used in the business for more than one year, including spending that improves or extends the life of such assets.

**Revenue expenditure** is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, whose benefit is used up within the year.

### Borderline cases

The core guide covers the repair trap. These further cases appear often in case studies.

| Item | Capital or revenue? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery and installation of a new machine | Capital | Needed to bring the asset into use, so it is part of its cost |
| Training staff to use the new machine | Revenue | Builds staff skills, not the asset itself |
| Software licence bought outright for several years | Capital | A long-term asset used over more than one year |
| Monthly cloud software subscription | Revenue | A running cost that buys access for a short period |
| Building an extension to a shop | Capital | Adds to the value and capacity of the building |
| Repainting the same shop | Revenue | Maintains the building rather than improving it |
| Replacing a whole delivery van | Capital | A new non-current asset |
| Replacing the van's tyres | Revenue | Routine maintenance |
| Fuel for the van | Revenue | Used up immediately |

The test for each is the same: does the spending create or improve an asset that will serve the business for more than a year? If yes, capital. If it keeps things running in the current year, revenue.

### Worked example: Orchardleigh Dental Practice

Orchardleigh Dental Practice is invented, and all figures are invented. Money is in dollars. In one year it spends:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| New X-ray unit | 42,000 |
| Delivery and installation of the X-ray unit | 3,000 |
| Training for staff on the X-ray unit | 1,200 |
| Rent for the year | 54,000 |
| Replacing a broken drill head | 350 |
| Building an extension to the waiting room | 25,000 |
| Repainting the existing waiting room | 2,800 |
| Five-year practice software licence bought outright | 6,000 |
| Annual online booking subscription | 1,800 |

**Capital expenditure:** 42,000 + 3,000 + 25,000 + 6,000 = **76,000**.

**Revenue expenditure:** 1,200 + 54,000 + 350 + 2,800 + 1,800 = **60,150**.

**Total:** 136,150, so capital expenditure is 76,000 ÷ 136,150 × 100 = **55.8%** of the year's spending, which is unusually high and reflects a year of investment.

## Why the classification matters

### Effect on the final accounts

* Revenue expenditure is recorded as an expense in the **statement of profit or loss** for the year, so it reduces that year's profit in full.
* Capital expenditure is recorded as a non-current asset in the **statement of financial position**. Its cost is spread over the years the asset is used, through depreciation, which is HL-only content in section 3.4.

### Effect of a mistake

Suppose Orchardleigh wrongly records the 25,000 waiting-room extension as revenue expenditure. In that year:

* expenses are overstated by 25,000, so profit is understated by 25,000 (before depreciation is considered)
* non-current assets on the statement of financial position are understated by 25,000

The opposite mistake, recording revenue expenditure as capital, overstates profit and assets. This makes the business look healthier than it is, which could mislead owners, lenders and investors. This is why accurate classification is part of the finance function's recording job.

### Effect on funding decisions

Revenue expenditure is regular and is normally paid from the cash that sales bring in. Capital expenditure is often large and irregular, so it may need a separate source of finance. A sensible principle is to match the life of the finance to the life of the asset, which leads directly into section 3.2.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why the X-ray unit is capital expenditure for Orchardleigh Dental Practice.*

> The X-ray unit is a non-current asset that the practice will use for many years to treat patients, not something used up within the year. **[definition applied]** Its 42,000 cost, plus the 3,000 to install it, will appear in the statement of financial position and be spread over its working life, rather than being charged against one year's profit. **[consequence for accounts]** Because it is large and one-off, Orchardleigh is unlikely to pay for it from a single year's trading cash and may need longer-term finance. **[consequence for funding]**

Each sentence applies the definition to the case, which is what AO2 requires.

## Common mistakes

* Treating installation costs as revenue expenditure. They are part of the asset's cost.
* Treating training on new equipment as capital. It does not add to the asset.
* Saying capital expenditure "is not an expense". Its cost is spread over the asset's life.
* Describing the role of finance in general terms without linking it to the business's stage or situation.
* Forgetting that misclassification changes reported profit.

## Exam technique

* **Define**: give the standard meaning precisely.
* **Distinguish**: state a clear difference, such as the length of time the benefit lasts.
* **Explain**: apply the definition to the item in the case and give a consequence, for accounts or for funding.
