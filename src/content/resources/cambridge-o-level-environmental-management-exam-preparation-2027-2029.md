Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 is assessed by two
written papers. Both are 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks and 50% of the
qualification, both are externally assessed, and candidates answer all
questions on both. The syllabus is not tiered and grades run A* to E.
This page builds a preparation plan for the 2027-2029 papers, alongside
the site's 2027-2029 guides to
[Rocks, Ores and Minerals](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-rocks-ores-and-minerals/),
[Energy Resources](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-energy-resources/)
and [Land](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-land/).

## The two papers

| | Paper 1 Principles of Environmental Management | Paper 2 Environmental Management in Context |
|---|---|---|
| Time and marks | 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks, 50% | 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks, 50% |
| Question types | Short-answer and structured questions with extended response questions based on source material | Short-answer, data processing and analysis, and extended response questions based on source material |
| What else | Tests AO1, AO2 and AO3 | Uses source material to illustrate issues of environmental management and fieldwork investigations; tests AO1, AO2 and AO3 |

The changes page of the syllabus adds three points that shape
preparation: Paper 1 no longer has sections, practical method-based
questions are now limited to Paper 2, and Paper 2 no longer has a
single-country context.

## Where the marks sit: assessment objectives

| Assessment objective | Paper 1 | Paper 2 | Whole qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| AO1 Knowledge with understanding | 55% | 30% | 43% |
| AO2 Handling information and problem-solving | 30% | 30% | 30% |
| AO3 Investigation skills and making judgements | 15% | 40% | 27% |

The syllabus calls these weightings approximate. **Exam-preparation
priority**: Paper 1 leans towards knowledge, so learn every "limited to"
list by the syllabus's own names. Paper 2 leans towards investigation
skills and judgement: planning fieldwork, evaluating methods and forming
conclusions from evidence carry about 40% of its marks.

## Command words

The 2027-2029 syllabus publishes a list of command words with their
meanings (p.43). The ones that decide how much to write:

| Command word | What it asks for |
|---|---|
| State, Give, Identify | A short answer: express, produce or name, with no explanation |
| Define | The precise meaning; many definitions are given word for word in the syllabus |
| Describe | The points of a topic, or its characteristics and main features |
| Explain | Purposes or reasons, making the relationships between things clear, supported with relevant evidence |
| Compare | Similarities and/or differences |
| Discuss | Write about the issue in depth in a structured way; in this syllabus it usually comes as "discuss the benefits and limitations" |
| Suggest | Apply knowledge to a situation where there is more than one valid answer |
| Calculate, Determine | Work out an answer from the figures or information given |
| Justify | Support a case with evidence or argument |
| Predict | Say what may happen, based on the information available |
| Plot, Sketch, Show (that) | Mark points on a graph; draw a simple freehand diagram of the key features; give structured evidence that leads to a given result |

**Exam-preparation priority**: before writing, underline the command
word. A "describe" answer that explains wastes time, and an "explain"
answer that only describes loses the marks for reasons.

## Fieldwork: now examined explicitly

Fieldwork has its own sub-topic, 5.4 Fieldwork investigations, and the
syllabus lists fieldwork requirements, apparatus and safety guidance
(pp.37-39). The sampling strategies are random and systematic sampling;
the sampling techniques are pitfall traps, pooters, sweep nets,
quadrats, transects, aerial photography and drones, and automated
sampling. The required apparatus list includes a hand lens, a
humidity/moisture meter, a light meter, a metre ruler, a pH meter, a
pitfall trap, a pooter, a quadrat (open frame and grid), a 30 cm ruler, a
sweep net, a transect (long tape measure or string) and a tray for
hand-sorting.

**Exam-preparation priority**: be ready to plan an investigation from a
given aim. That means stating a hypothesis, naming the independent,
dependent and control variables, choosing and justifying a sampling
technique and strategy, saying how many repeats or replicates to take,
identifying a hazard with a precaution, and saying how the results will
be recorded and processed.

## Data skills

The mathematical requirements (p.40) include percentages and percentage
change, mean and range, standard form, significant figures and decimal
places, area of a rectangle, scale, interpreting population pyramids and
a range of graphs, gradient and intercept, correlation, 8-point compass
directions, and latitude and longitude. Calculators may be used in all
parts of the exam. The presentation-of-data guidance (p.41) sets
conventions: table columns headed with the quantity and unit separated by
a solidus (for example "time / s"), units not written in the body of a
table, the independent variable on the x-axis, points plotted as crosses
or encircled dots, a single thin best-fit line that ignores clear
anomalies, pie charts in rank order starting at "noon" and going
clockwise, bar charts with separate bars, and histograms with touching
bars.

## Worked routine: a data-response question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a
reproduction of any official past paper, insert or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - read the title, axes, units and scale before the data.

Step 2 - underline the command word:
  DESCRIBE  -> the pattern, with quoted figures and units
  EXPLAIN   -> the process causing it
  CALCULATE -> show the working and give the unit
  SUGGEST   -> a plausible reason applied to this context

Step 3 - if describing: overall trend, one or two quoted values,
         and any anomaly or change in the pattern.

Step 4 - if the question asks about the method: name one limitation
         (for example too few samples, or no repeats) and one
         specific improvement.
```

For example, "percentage cover of grass falls from 80% at 0 m to 20% at
30 m from the path" quotes two values with units and a direction; "the
cover decreases" earns less.

## Before and during the exam

- **Before**: learn every "limited to" list and every syllabus
  definition; practise planning a fieldwork investigation; practise
  percentage change and reading graphs; prepare a benefit and a
  limitation for every strategy the syllabus asks you to discuss.
- **During both papers**: work through all the questions (there is no
  choice), and use the marks shown as a guide to how many points to make.
- **On extended responses**: use the source material, weigh benefits
  against limitations, and finish with a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Which paper gives more weight to investigation skills and making
   judgements, and roughly what share of its marks?
2. Which of the two papers now carries practical method-based questions?
3. Name the two sampling strategies and four of the sampling techniques
   listed in the syllabus.
4. A reading rises from 40 to 50. What is the percentage change?
5. How should the axis for time in seconds be labelled?

**Answers:** 1. Paper 2, about 40%, so about 32 of its 80 marks. 2. Paper
2 only; the syllabus says they are no longer assessed in Paper 1. 3.
Random and systematic sampling; any four of pitfall traps, pooters, sweep
nets, quadrats, transects, aerial photography and drones, and automated
sampling. 4. (50 - 40) / 40 x 100 = 25%. 5. "time / s".
