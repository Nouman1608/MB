## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define price elasticity of demand and state the formula used to calculate it. **[2]**

**2.** A weekly magazine raises its price from £4.00 to £4.40. Sales fall from 25,000 to 23,000 copies.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in the publisher's total revenue. **[2]**

**3.** Real incomes in Country K rise by 6%. Demand for good X rises by 9%, demand for good Y rises by 3% and demand for good Z falls by 1.2%. Calculate the YED of each good and classify each one. **[3]**

**4.** The price of printer ink cartridges rises by 15% and sales of printers fall by 3%. Calculate the cross elasticity of demand and state what it shows about the two goods. **[2]**

**5.** A venue raises the price of tickets for a sold-out concert by 30%. The number of tickets supplied does not change. State the price elasticity of supply and explain why it takes this value. **[2]**

**6.** A good has an income elasticity of demand of −0.4 and a price elasticity of demand of −0.3. Which statement is correct?

A: It is a luxury good with price elastic demand

B: It is an inferior good with price inelastic demand

C: It is a normal good with price inelastic demand

D: It is an inferior good with price elastic demand **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** A café's weekly demand for sandwiches is shown below (invented data).

| Price (£) | 12 | 10 | 8 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 400 | 600 | 800 | 900 |

**(a)** Calculate total revenue at each price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the PED when the price is cut from £10 to £8. **[2]**

**(c)** Using PED, explain why cutting the price from £8 to £6 reduces total revenue. **[3]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why farmers' total revenue may fall after an unusually large harvest. **[6]**

**9.** A government is considering a tax on sugary drinks that would raise their price by 15%. The estimated PED is −0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage fall in the quantity demanded. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the tax may raise substantial revenue but do little to improve health. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two factors that make the price elasticity of supply of new housing low in the short run. **[4]**

**11.** A car manufacturer estimates that the YED for its cars is +2.5. Real incomes are forecast to fall by 2% next year.

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage change in demand for its cars. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain two ways the manufacturer could use this estimate. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of price elasticity of demand to a firm deciding whether to raise its price. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** PED measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price [1]. PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "How much demand changes" is not enough; the word responsiveness and the percentage formula carry the marks.

**2. (a)** % change in price = 0.40 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = +10%; % change in quantity = −2,000 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = −8% [1]. PED = −8 ÷ 10 = **−0.8** [1].

**(b)** Old TR = 4.00 × 25,000 = £100,000; new TR = 4.40 × 23,000 = £101,200 [1]. TR rises by **£1,200** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The rise in revenue is consistent with inelastic demand; saying so shows you can link the two parts.

**3.** X: 9 ÷ 6 = **+1.5**, a normal, income elastic (luxury) good [1]. Y: 3 ÷ 6 = **+0.5**, a normal, income inelastic good (a necessity) [1]. Z: −1.2 ÷ 6 = **−0.2**, an inferior good [1]. **[3]**

**4.** XED = −3 ÷ 15 = **−0.2** [1]. The negative sign shows they are complements, and the small size shows the link is weak [1]. **[2]**

**5.** PES = **0** (perfectly inelastic) [1] because the number of seats is fixed by the size of the venue, so supply cannot respond to price [1]. **[2]**

**6.** **B** [1]. A negative YED means an inferior good, and a PED below 1 in size means inelastic demand. **[1]**

**7. (a)** TR = £4,800 and £6,000 [1]; £6,400 and £5,400 [1].

**(b)** % change in price = −2 ÷ 10 = −20%; % change in quantity = 200 ÷ 600 = +33.3% [1]. PED = 33.3 ÷ −20 = **−1.67** [1].

**(c)** From £8 to £6 price falls 25% and quantity rises 12.5% [1], so PED = −0.5, which is inelastic [1]. With inelastic demand the fall in price outweighs the rise in quantity, so TR falls from £6,400 to £5,400 [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the PED value you calculated in the explanation; a general statement about inelastic demand earns less.

**8.** Diagram with price and quantity axes, a steep (inelastic) demand curve and supply shifting right from S1 to S2 [1]; new equilibrium shows a large price fall and a small rise in quantity [1]. Demand for food is price inelastic because it is a necessity with few substitutes [1]. The bumper harvest shifts supply to the right [1]. Price must fall by a large percentage to persuade buyers to take the extra output [1]. Because the percentage fall in price exceeds the percentage rise in quantity, the revenue rectangle shrinks, so farmers' total revenue falls [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the revenue rectangles on your diagram rather than leaving the diagram to speak for itself.

**9. (a)** 0.4 × 15 = **6%** fall [1].

**(b)** Quantity falls by only 6% while price rises by 15%, so the tax base stays large [1] and the government collects revenue on most of the original sales [1]. But the same inelasticity means consumption of sugary drinks hardly falls, so the health benefit is small unless the tax is very large or combined with other measures [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Building takes a long time, from planning permission to completion [1], so the quantity supplied cannot rise quickly when prices rise [1]. Land with permission to build is scarce in many areas [1], and builders face shortages of skilled labour and materials, so they cannot easily expand output [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** 2.5 × −2 = **−5%** [1].

**(b)** The manufacturer could cut planned production by about 5% to avoid unsold stock [1], which reduces storage and financing costs [1]. It could shift marketing or new models toward cheaper cars or towards customers whose incomes are less affected [1], so that total sales are more stable over the cycle [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- PED tells the firm whether a price rise will increase or reduce total revenue [1]: if demand is inelastic, revenue rises [1].
- With inelastic demand, fewer units are sold, so total costs may fall while revenue rises, making a strong case for the rise [1].
- PED depends on substitutes, so a firm with a strong brand or few rivals is likely to face inelastic demand [1].
- Limits: PED is estimated from past data and may have changed [1]; it varies along the demand curve, so a large rise may move the firm into the elastic range [1].
- Rivals may not follow the rise, making demand more elastic than estimated [1]; demand also becomes more elastic over time as buyers find substitutes [1].
- PED ignores other aims such as market share and the reaction of regulators or the public [1].
- Judgement: PED is a useful starting point for small price changes in a stable market, but less reliable for large changes or where rivals and time matter, so the firm should test the rise and watch rivals [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation marks come from naming when PED is reliable and when it is not, not from listing more determinants.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the percentage change in price by the percentage change in quantity.
- Calculating changes in units rather than percentages.
- Leaving out the sign for YED and XED, where the sign is the classification.
- Saying "TR increases" without the figures that show it.
- Drawing diagrams with unlabelled axes or with demand and supply curves that do not cross at the starting point.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement that depends on a stated condition.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I include the minus sign in PED?**
Yes. Including it shows the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, and you then interpret the size of the number to decide whether demand is elastic or inelastic.

**Why does cutting price sometimes reduce revenue?**
When demand is price inelastic, the percentage rise in quantity is smaller than the percentage fall in price, so price × quantity falls.

**How many decimal places should elasticity answers have?**
Two decimal places is a safe choice unless the question says otherwise. Show the percentage changes first so that method marks can be awarded.
