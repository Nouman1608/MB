Study first with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why the Qing government tried to limit European trade to Canton. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract, which is *constructed for practice* and is not a real quotation.

> "The foreign merchant sells smoke and takes away silver. If we do not stop him, the treasury will be empty and the soldiers weak. Let the Commissioner act at once." (A memorial to the throne, constructed for practice)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about Chinese concerns over the opium trade. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of such a memorial for a historian studying the causes of the First Opium War. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the social impact of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. **[6]**

**4.** To what extent were the Opium Wars the main cause of the decline of the Qing dynasty? **[15]**

**5.** Evaluate the reasons for the failure of the Self-Strengthening Movement and the Hundred Days' Reform to save the Qing dynasty. **[15]**

**6.** Explain the importance of Yuan Shikai in the events of 1911 and 1912. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the reason, 1 for explanation). The court wanted to control foreign contact and keep foreigners away from the interior, so one port allowed supervision; the Co-hong merchants could be held responsible for foreign conduct and collect fees; the tributary view saw trade as a favour to outsiders, not a right; officials feared foreign influence on the population. **[4]**

**2.** **(a)** The extract suggests that officials saw opium as a threat to the economy, because silver left the country (1), and to the military, because addiction weakened soldiers (1). It shows a view of foreigners as harming China and a call for firm action (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Award up to 3. A memorial was written for the emperor by an official who wished to persuade, so it may exaggerate harm or leave out arguments for legalisation, which some officials made (1). It reflects one Chinese view and not that of the British traders, whose demands for trade rights also caused the war (1). It cannot show what the emperor decided, or how far Britain's wish for compensation and access, not opium itself, caused the conflict (1). **[3]**

**3.** Marking points (up to 6): the rebels banned opium, gambling and foot-binding, and tried to give women a public role (up to 2); the plan for shared land and common stores offered hope to poor peasants but was only partly carried out (up to 1); strict religious rule and the rejection of Confucian ideas alienated the gentry (up to 1); war brought death, famine and displacement across the Yangtze region, with estimates of at least 20 million deaths (up to 2). Credit a balanced judgement on the movement's effect. **[6]**

**4.** Marking points (up to 15):
- Thesis and criteria for "main cause" (up to 2).
- Opium Wars: the Treaties of Nanjing and Tianjin, the loss of tariff control, indemnities, loss of prestige, treaty ports (up to 4).
- Internal weaknesses: rebellions before 1839, corruption, population pressure; the Taiping Rebellion as a larger internal challenge (up to 4).
- Other external pressures: Japan in 1895, the Boxer Protocol of 1901 (up to 2).
- Perspectives: impact-response against China-centred history (up to 1).
- A judgement ranking the causes, for example that the wars exposed weakness and provided the occasion but the dynasty's internal problems explain why it could not recover (up to 2). **[15]**

**5.** Marking points (up to 15):
- Thesis addressing both reforms (up to 2).
- Self-Strengthening: arsenals, shipyards and a steamship company; the lack of national coordination, provincial funding and conservative opposition; defeat in 1895 (up to 4).
- Hundred Days: Kang Youwei and the Guangxu Emperor, radical edicts, resistance from Cixi and officials, the coup of September 1898 (up to 4).
- The role of Cixi and Prince Gong, and whether the court or the officials were the larger obstacle (up to 2).
- Evaluation of perspectives, such as views of Cixi as an obstacle or a pragmatist (up to 1).
- A judgement consistent with the evidence (up to 2). **[15]**

**6.** Marking points (up to 6): Yuan commanded the modern Beiyang Army, and the court called him back after the Wuchang uprising (up to 2); he negotiated with both the court and the revolutionaries, so the dynasty could abdicate without further war (up to 2); in exchange he became president in 1912, which took power from Sun Yixian and began a period of weak republican government (up to 2). **[6]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I describe the treaties in detail?**
Only enough to support the argument. Use two terms from each treaty that answer the question.

**How do I handle the date of the end of the Qing?**
Say the Xinhai Revolution began in October 1911 and the emperor abdicated in February 1912.
