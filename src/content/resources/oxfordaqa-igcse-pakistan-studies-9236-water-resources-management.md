Key development 3.2.1.4 of the 9236 specification, Energy and water, includes "uses of water as a
natural resource, management of water supplies and implications for the economy and people of
Pakistan", and "importance of maintaining supplies of energy and water resources to support social
and economic development". This guide covers the water half of that key development: where
Pakistan's water comes from, how it is used, how supplies are managed, and why shortages matter. It
is part of the compulsory topic examined in Paper 2 Section A. For energy resources and the rest of
the topic, see the
[Landscape and Natural Resources study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-landscape-and-natural-resources/).

## Where Pakistan's water comes from

- **Rivers of the Indus system**, fed by snow and glacier melt in the northern mountains in summer and
  by monsoon rain from July to September. Flow is therefore very uneven through the year.
- **Groundwater**, pumped by millions of tube wells, especially from the large aquifer beneath the
  Punjab plains, which is partly recharged by seepage from canals and rivers.
- **Rainfall**, which is low across most of the country except in the north and north-east.
- **Traditional systems** such as the **karez** of Balochistan, underground channels that carry
  groundwater from the foot of hills to fields by gravity, reducing evaporation.

## How water is used

| Use | Detail |
|---|---|
| Agriculture | By far the largest user; irrigation of wheat, rice, cotton, sugar cane and fodder |
| Domestic | Drinking, cooking, washing and sanitation in homes |
| Industry | Textiles, sugar mills, fertiliser and food processing |
| Hydel power | Dams at Tarbela, Mangla and elsewhere generate electricity |
| Fisheries and environment | River, lake and delta fisheries; flows needed to sustain the Indus delta and its mangroves |

## The Indus Basin Irrigation System

Pakistan has one of the largest connected irrigation systems in the world. **Barrages**, such as the
Sukkur Barrage in Sindh, raise the river level and divert water into **canals**, which carry it to
distributaries, watercourses and finally fields. **Link canals** move water between rivers. Some
canals flow all year (perennial); others flow only when rivers are high.

## The 1960 treaty and the 1991 accord

**The Indus Waters Treaty (1960)**, agreed with India with the help of the World Bank, divided the
rivers of the Indus system:

- the **eastern rivers** (Ravi, Beas and Sutlej) were allocated to India;
- the **western rivers** (Indus, Jhelum and Chenab) were allocated to Pakistan.

To replace water from the eastern rivers, Pakistan built the **Indus Basin Project**, including the
**Mangla Dam** on the Jhelum (completed 1967), the **Tarbela Dam** on the Indus (completed 1976), new
barrages and link canals.

**The Water Apportionment Accord (1991)** set out how Indus water is shared between the provinces, and
the **Indus River System Authority (IRSA)** was created to regulate the distribution.

## Problems in managing water

- **Limited storage.** Most river water arrives in a few summer months, but reservoirs can store only
  a small part of it. Tarbela and Mangla also lose capacity as silt settles behind the dams.
- **Waterlogging and salinity.** Seepage from unlined canals and over-watering raise the water table;
  when water evaporates, salts are left in the soil, reducing yields. Salinity control programmes
  from the 1960s used tube wells to lower the water table.
- **Falling groundwater.** In many areas tube wells now pump out more than is recharged, so water
  tables fall and pumping costs rise.
- **Waste.** Flood irrigation, canal leakage and theft mean much water never reaches crops.
- **Provincial disputes.** Downstream Sindh fears that upstream projects will reduce its share; the
  long dispute over a proposed dam at Kalabagh is an example.
- **Pollution.** Untreated sewage and industrial waste enter rivers and canals, and some groundwater
  is contaminated, for example by arsenic in parts of the Indus plain.
- **Urban demand.** Cities such as Karachi and Lahore face shortages as population grows. Karachi
  draws water from the Indus through Keenjhar Lake and from the Hub Dam.
- **Climate change.** Changes in glacier melt and more intense floods and droughts make supplies less
  predictable.

## Managing supplies: strategies

| Strategy | How it helps | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| New dams (Diamer-Bhasha and Mohmand, under construction) | More storage, flood control and hydel power | High cost; displacement of people; long build times |
| Lining canals and watercourses | Less seepage and waterlogging | Expensive across a huge network |
| Drip and sprinkler irrigation; laser land levelling | Water applied efficiently to crops | Equipment costs for small farmers |
| Groundwater regulation | Slows falling water tables | Difficult to monitor millions of private wells |
| Rainwater harvesting and small dams | Local storage in dry areas such as Balochistan | Small volumes |
| Wastewater treatment | Cleaner rivers; treated water reused | Few treatment plants; high cost |
| National Water Policy (2018) | A national framework for conservation, storage and efficiency | Depends on provincial action |

## Implications for the economy and people

**Economy.**

- Agriculture employs a large share of the workforce and supplies textiles, Pakistan's main export
  industry, with cotton; it depends on irrigation.
- Hydel power from dams is a cheap, renewable source of electricity; low reservoir levels reduce
  output and increase reliance on imported fuel.
- Shortages reduce crop yields and can raise food prices and import bills.

**People.**

- Safe drinking water and sanitation reduce diseases such as diarrhoea and typhoid; contaminated
  supplies cause illness, especially among children.
- In rural areas, women and girls often spend hours collecting water, time lost from school and work.
- Unequal access between upstream and downstream areas, and between rich and poor, can cause conflict.
- Water shortage and salinity can push families to migrate to cities.

## Why maintaining supplies matters for development

The specification also asks about the "importance of maintaining supplies of energy and water
resources to support social and economic development". Water and energy are closely linked in
Pakistan.

- **Water produces energy.** Hydel stations depend on reservoir levels, so a dry year reduces
  electricity output just when demand for pumping and cooling rises.
- **Energy moves water.** Tube wells, urban pumping stations and treatment plants all need power, so
  load-shedding interrupts water supply.
- **Development depends on both.** Reliable water and power allow factories to run, hospitals and
  schools to function, and farmers to plan their crops.
- **Population growth.** As the population grows, the water available per person falls unless storage
  and efficiency improve.

An answer that links water to energy, food and health shows the connections examiners reward.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain how waterlogging and salinity damage farmland in Pakistan. **[4]**

```
1  water seeps from unlined canals and from over-irrigated fields
2  the water table rises close to the surface, waterlogging the
   roots of crops
3  in the hot climate water evaporates from the surface, leaving
   salts behind
4  salt builds up in the soil, reducing yields until land may be
   abandoned
```

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up the eastern and western rivers** in the Indus Waters Treaty.
- **Saying the 1991 accord was with India.** It divides water between Pakistan's provinces.
- **Describing problems without strategies,** or strategies without their limits.
- **Ignoring groundwater,** which supplies a large share of irrigation water.
