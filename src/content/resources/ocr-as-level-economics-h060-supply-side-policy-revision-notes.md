## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply side policy | Measures to raise productive capacity and market efficiency |
| Market-based policy | Reduces the role of the state, sharpens incentives |
| Interventionist policy | Uses state spending or subsidy to correct market failures |
| Privatisation | Transfer of state-owned firms to private ownership |
| Deregulation | Removal of rules that restrict entry or raise costs |
| Producer subsidy | Payment to firms that lowers costs or rewards an activity |
| Competition policy | Rules on mergers, cartels and abuse of dominance |
| Infrastructure | Transport, energy and digital networks |
| Labour market flexibility | Ease with which wages, hours and jobs adjust |
| Structural unemployment | Mismatch between workers' skills or location and available jobs |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour |
| Unit labour cost | Wage per hour divided by output per hour |

## Measure by measure

| Measure | Mechanism | Main objective helped | Key drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Privatisation | Profit motive, shareholder pressure | Efficiency, growth | Private monopoly risk |
| Deregulation | Easier entry, more competition | Lower prices, growth | May remove protections |
| Subsidies | Lower costs, reward R&D or investment | Growth, jobs | Cost; may prop up weak firms |
| Competition policy | Stops cartels and abuse of dominance | Efficiency, lower inflation | Slow, hard to measure |
| Infrastructure | Lower transport and energy costs | Growth | Very long lags, high cost |
| Education and training | Higher skills and mobility | Growth, lower structural unemployment | Long lags; weak if no jobs |
| R&D | New technology | Long-run growth | Uncertain returns |
| Tax reform | Stronger incentives to work and invest | Growth | Revenue loss, inequality |
| Benefit reform | Makes work pay | Lower unemployment | Hardship for those unable to work |
| Labour market flexibility | Faster adjustment of jobs and wages | Lower unemployment | Insecurity, less training |
| Immigration control | Changes labour supply and skills | Capacity (looser) or protection of wages (tighter) | Skills gaps or pressure on services |

### Worked reminder: training

Output per hour rises from 40 to 42 (+5%); wages rise by 3%. Unit labour costs change by 1.03 / 1.05 - 1 = **-1.9%**. SRAS and LRAS shift right.

### Worked reminder: work incentives

Benefits 100 a week; job pays 250 with tax of 30. If all benefits are withdrawn, the net gain is 250 - 30 - 100 = **120**: 52% of earnings is lost. If only half the benefit is withdrawn, the gain is **170**: 32% is lost.

### Worked reminder: labour force

A scheme brings 50,000 extra workers into a labour force of 5 million: a **1%** rise. With unchanged output per worker, potential output rises by about 1%.

## Diagram checklist

- Axes: price level and real output.
- Draw AD, LRAS1 and LRAS2; add SRAS if costs fall too.
- New equilibrium: higher real output, lower price level than with LRAS1.
- Arrow showing the rightward shift, labelled with the measure.

## Evaluation in six words

**Lags, cost, uncertainty, equity, targeting, interaction.**

- **Lags:** education and infrastructure take years.
- **Cost:** spending and tax cuts raise borrowing.
- **Uncertainty:** responses to incentives may be small.
- **Equity:** flexibility and benefit cuts can hurt the low-paid.
- **Targeting:** helps structural, not cyclical, unemployment.
- **Interaction:** interventionist spending raises AD first; measures work best in combination.

## Exam traps

- Supply side policy is not only tax cuts.
- Spending on infrastructure raises AD **now** and LRAS **later**.
- It cannot quickly fix a recession caused by low AD.
- A privatised monopoly may still need regulation.
- Immigration: explain the labour supply effect, then evaluate.
- Draw LRAS shifting, not AD.

## Self-test

1. Define supply side policy.
2. Give two market-based and two interventionist supply side policies.
3. Explain how privatisation may increase efficiency.
4. Explain how competition policy can lower inflation in the long run.
5. Why does investment in infrastructure shift both AD and LRAS?
6. Wages rise 2% and productivity 6%. Calculate the change in unit labour costs.
7. How can benefit reform increase the labour force?
8. Give one argument for and one against greater labour market flexibility.
9. How can looser immigration controls shift LRAS?
10. Why is training of little help in a recession caused by falling AD?
11. Give two reasons why the effects of supply side policies are uncertain.
12. What combination of effects on output and the price level does a rightward LRAS shift give?

### Answers

1. Measures to increase productive capacity and the efficiency of markets, shifting LRAS right.
2. Market-based: any two of privatisation, deregulation, tax cuts, benefit reform, labour market flexibility. Interventionist: any two of infrastructure, education, training, R&D, subsidies.
3. Private owners seek profit and face shareholder pressure and takeover threats, so they cut costs and innovate.
4. More competition forces firms to cut costs and prices, and raises efficiency, shifting AS right.
5. The spending is part of G, raising AD now; the finished assets raise capacity later.
6. 1.02 / 1.06 - 1 = **-3.8%**.
7. By raising the net gain from working, more people choose to look for and take jobs.
8. For: faster adjustment and lower unemployment. Against: insecurity, irregular hours, less training.
9. More workers, and workers with skills in short supply, raise the labour force and its productivity.
10. Workers' skills are not the problem; there are too few jobs because demand is low.
11. Any two: uncertain response to incentives; long lags; government failure in choosing projects; other shocks.
12. Higher real output and a lower price level.
