Planning questions ask you to design, or to criticise the design of, an investigation set in an unfamiliar biological context. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 1.1.1 you must be able to design an experiment, including choosing suitable apparatus and techniques, identify the variables that must be controlled, and evaluate whether a method is appropriate for the expected outcome. The specification adds that you should apply scientific knowledge from the rest of the course to the practical context, so a plan that ignores the biology will not score.

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.1.1(a) to (c) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the module-wide [planning, implementing, analysing and evaluating study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-planning-implementing-analysing-and-evaluating/). Use the [planning revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-planning-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [planning practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-planning-practice/) to test yourself.

## Start with the question being asked (a)

Every good plan begins with a question that can be answered with measurements. "Does temperature affect enzyme activity?" is a topic. "How does the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide change between 20 °C and 60 °C?" is a testable question, because it names what you will change and what you will measure.

Three kinds of variable organise the plan.

| Variable | Meaning | Example (catalase and temperature) |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | The one factor you deliberately change | Temperature of the reaction mixture |
| Dependent | The factor you measure to see the effect | Volume of oxygen released per minute |
| Controlled | Every other factor that could change the result and so must be kept the same | pH, enzyme concentration, substrate concentration, volume of each solution, mass and surface area of tissue |

An answer that says "keep everything else the same" scores nothing. Name each variable and say **how** you will hold it constant.

## Choosing a range, intervals and repeats

- **Range.** Cover the values that matter biologically. For a human enzyme, 20 to 60 °C includes the optimum and the temperature at which denaturation is clear.
- **Intervals.** Use at least five values of the independent variable, evenly spaced, so a trend or a peak can be seen. Five temperatures could be 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C.
- **Repeats.** Repeat each value at least three times and calculate a mean. Repeats show how much the results vary and let you spot an anomaly.
- **Pilot study.** A short trial run checks that the range is sensible, for example that reactions are not too fast to time.

## Choosing apparatus and techniques (a)

Choose apparatus because of what it measures and how precisely, and say why.

| Need | Suitable choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Hold a steady temperature | Thermostatically controlled water bath | A beaker heated on a bench drifts in temperature |
| Measure a small gas volume | Gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder over water | Gives a numerical volume, whereas counting bubbles does not, because bubbles differ in size |
| Measure small volumes of liquid | Graduated pipette or syringe | More precise than a measuring cylinder for 1 to 10 cm³ |
| Measure mass | Balance reading to 0.01 g | Allows small changes in mass to be seen |
| Measure time | Stopwatch | Gives the resolution to 0.1 s or 1 s as needed |
| Measure colour or turbidity | Colorimeter | Gives a number rather than a judgement by eye |

Apply biology to every choice. If the enzyme and substrate are mixed before reaching the target temperature, the reaction starts at the wrong temperature. So each solution is held at the test temperature in the water bath for several minutes before mixing.

## Worked example 1: designing a dilution series

A plan to test how substrate concentration affects the rate of a catalase reaction needs five concentrations of hydrogen peroxide made from a 100 % stock. Each tube must contain 10 cm³ of solution in total.

| Concentration | Volume of stock / cm³ | Volume of distilled water / cm³ |
|---|---|---|
| 20 % | 2 | 8 |
| 40 % | 4 | 6 |
| 60 % | 6 | 4 |
| 80 % | 8 | 2 |
| 100 % | 10 | 0 |

Check: for each row the two volumes add to 10 cm³, and the stock fraction (2/10, 4/10 and so on) gives the stated percentage. The total volume is the same in every tube, so the volume of liquid is controlled as well.

## Worked example 2: a complete plan

**Question.** Plan an investigation into the effect of sodium chloride concentration on the mass of potato tissue.

**Plan.** The independent variable is the concentration of sodium chloride solution. The dependent variable is the percentage change in mass of the potato.

1. Make solutions of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol dm⁻³ by diluting a stock solution with distilled water.
2. Use a cork borer to cut cylinders of potato from the same potato and trim them to the same length, for example 30 mm. A single potato avoids differences in the natural solute concentration between potatoes.
3. Blot each cylinder dry and find its mass on a balance reading to 0.01 g.
4. Place one cylinder in 20 cm³ of each solution. Use three cylinders per concentration.
5. Leave all tubes at the same temperature for the same length of time, for example 30 minutes in a water bath at 25 °C.
6. Remove, blot, and weigh each cylinder again.
7. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each cylinder, then the mean for each concentration.

Percentage change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100. A cylinder of 2.40 g that ends at 2.64 g has changed by (2.64 − 2.40) ÷ 2.40 × 100 = **+10.0 %**.

Using a percentage change allows for cylinders of slightly different starting masses. The concentration at which the mean change is zero estimates where the solution and the potato tissue have the same water potential.

**Controlled variables:** size and shape of cylinders, source of potato, volume of solution, temperature, time, drying method.

**Hazards:** the cork borer is sharp, so cut onto a tile, away from the hand. Wipe up spills so that nobody slips.

## Variables that cannot be controlled (b)

Some variables cannot be held perfectly constant. The skill is to say how you will reduce their effect.

- Natural variation between organisms: use a large sample, or the same batch or one organism, and calculate a mean.
- Room temperature that changes over a long experiment: use a water bath or a lid, and record the temperature.
- Differences between cuts of tissue: use the same borer and the same ruler to cut each piece.

Where a variable has a known effect that cannot be removed, **record it**. A temperature log shows whether it changed enough to matter.

A **control experiment** shows that the dependent variable changes because of the independent variable and not because of something else. In an enzyme study, a tube with boiled enzyme in place of active enzyme shows that the product does not form without a functioning enzyme.

## Evaluating whether a method is appropriate (c)

To judge a method, ask five questions and answer them for the context given.

1. **Does it measure the dependent variable?** Counting bubbles of gas measures the rate roughly, but a gas syringe measures it in cm³.
2. **Is the range suitable?** Five values between 20 and 60 °C show a trend. Two values do not.
3. **Are the other variables controlled?** If the potato cylinders have different lengths, the comparison is not fair.
4. **Is the measuring apparatus precise enough?** A ruler reading to 1 mm cannot detect a shrinkage of 0.2 mm.
5. **Are there enough repeats to find anomalies and a reliable mean?**

When you criticise, give the **effect on the results** and an **improvement**. "The thermometer was not accurate" earns less than "the thermometer reads to the nearest 1 °C, so the temperature could be 0.5 °C out, which could change the rate; use a digital thermometer reading to 0.1 °C".

## Exam technique

- Write the **independent variable**, the **dependent variable** and **how it is measured** in your first sentences.
- Give **values** (volumes, temperatures, times), not "a suitable amount".
- Link each control to the biology: "same pH, because pH affects the shape of the active site".
- Use the data in the question. If a table is supplied, say what it shows before you criticise it.
- In "evaluate" questions, say **appropriate**, **partly appropriate** or **not appropriate**, and give your reason.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "keep the temperature the same" without saying how (water bath).
- Changing two variables at once, for example different volumes and different concentrations.
- Using a measuring cylinder to measure 1 cm³ when a syringe is available.
- Forgetting a control, a repeat or a mean.
- Writing a method that cannot be followed, with no volumes or times.
- Ignoring safety, such as eye protection with dilute acids, alkalis and hydrogen peroxide.
