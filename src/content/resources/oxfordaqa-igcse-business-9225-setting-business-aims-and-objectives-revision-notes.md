Condensed recall for section 3.1.3 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-setting-business-aims-and-objectives/). Figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- What **aims and objectives** are, why they are set and their role in running a business.
- The main objectives: **survival, profit maximisation, growth (domestic and international), market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives, shareholder value**.
- Why objectives **differ** (size, competition, type of business) and **change** as businesses evolve.
- **Public sector** objectives.
- Judging success in ways **other than profit**.
- The **government's role** in promoting enterprise.
- Reasons why **new businesses fail**.

## Aim or objective?

| | Aim | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| Time scale | Long term | Shorter term |
| Detail | General direction | Specific and measurable |
| Example | Be the leading bakery in the region | Increase sales by 10% in 12 months |

## Why set objectives?

- Gives **direction** and focus.
- **Motivates** staff to reach targets.
- Helps **decision making**: compare options with the objective.
- Allows **success to be measured**.
- Helps departments **work together**.
- Lenders and investors want to know the targets.

## The main objectives

| Objective | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| Survival | Stay in business, especially in the early years or in a downturn |
| Profit maximisation | Make as much profit as possible |
| Growth | Get bigger: more shops, customers or countries |
| Market share | Percentage of total market sales held by the business |
| Customer satisfaction | Meet or exceed customer expectations |
| Social and ethical | Act responsibly towards people, communities and the environment; may include religious and cultural obligations |
| Shareholder value | Raise the share price and dividends |

## Market share and growth formulae

- Market share (%) = business sales / total market sales x 100.
- Growth (%) = (new figure - old figure) / old figure x 100.

Example: sales $2.4 million in a $12 million market is 2.4 / 12 x 100 = 20%. A rise from $800,000 to $920,000 is 120,000 / 800,000 x 100 = 15% growth.

Formulae are not given in the exam, so learn both.

## Why objectives differ

- **Size:** small firms often aim to survive; large firms aim to lead the market.
- **Competition:** strong competition may push a firm towards market share or customer satisfaction.
- **Type of business:** not-for-profit organisations aim to help people, not to earn profit.

## Why objectives change

- A **start-up** aims for survival and customers.
- A **larger, established business** may aim for dominance, international expansion, higher shareholder value and ethical or environmental goals.
- Economic downturns can move a business from growth to survival.
- New laws or public concern can add environmental or ethical objectives.

## Public sector objectives

- Provide services to everyone (healthcare, education, transport).
- Stay within a budget funded by taxes.
- Meet service standards, such as shorter waiting times.
- Not driven by profit.

## Judging success

Success is not only profit. Other measures:

- market share target reached
- customer satisfaction scores
- survival for a set number of years
- staff retention
- ethical or environmental targets reached
- people helped (for a charity).

A loss-making business may still be succeeding if its objective is growth or survival.

## Government and enterprise

Governments encourage new businesses because they create **jobs**, bring in **tax**, provide **new products** and support the economy. Help includes **grants and cheap loans**, **training and advice**, **lower taxes** for small firms and **support centres**.

## Why new businesses fail

- Poor cash flow
- Not enough planning or market research
- Poor management or inexperience
- Strong competition
- Too little finance
- Changes in the economy or the law

## Exam traps

- Not every business aims to maximise profit.
- An objective must be specific; an aim is general.
- Link every objective to the type, size or stage of the business in the case.
- Objectives can conflict, such as growth against profit, or ethics against low costs.
- Use percentages for market share and growth, with the correct formula.

## Model answer outline: a 9-mark "evaluate" question

Question style: "Evaluate whether profit is the best measure of success for a new coffee shop."

- **For profit:** it shows that revenue covers costs, it is needed to survive and grow, and lenders and owners want to see it.
- **Against:** a new shop may aim first for survival or customer satisfaction; a loss in year one may be part of a plan to grow; profit does not show reputation, staff or community impact.
- **Other measures:** market share, customer satisfaction scores, number of customers, survival for three years, meeting ethical targets.
- **Judgement:** profit matters, but for a new business it is not the best single measure. Survival and customer numbers are better guides in the first year, with profit becoming more important as the business matures. Say what would change your mind, for example if the shop had large debts.

## Revision checklist

- I can distinguish an aim from an objective.
- I can list seven objectives and give an example of each.
- I can calculate market share and percentage growth.
- I can explain three reasons why objectives differ and two reasons why they change.
- I can describe public sector objectives.
- I can name ways to measure success other than profit.
- I can give two ways governments support enterprise and four reasons start-ups fail.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between an aim and an objective.
2. State two reasons why a business sets objectives.
3. Name two objectives a new business might set.
4. A firm sells $1.5 million in a market of $10 million. Calculate its market share.
5. Sales rise from $400,000 to $460,000. Calculate the percentage growth.
6. Explain one reason why a large business may have different objectives from a small one.
7. Give one example of a public sector objective.
8. State two ways to judge success other than profit.
9. Give two ways a government might help new businesses.
10. State two reasons why new businesses fail.
11. Explain why a business in a recession might change its objective from growth to survival.
12. A business has a social objective to reduce plastic. Give one possible cost and one possible benefit.

### Answers

1. An aim is a long-term goal; an objective is a specific, measurable target that helps reach it.
2. Any two: gives direction; motivates staff; helps decisions; allows success to be measured; helps coordination.
3. Any two: survival; profit; growth; customer satisfaction; market share.
4. 1.5 / 10 x 100 = **15%**.
5. (460,000 - 400,000) / 400,000 x 100 = 60,000 / 400,000 x 100 = **15%**.
6. For example, a large business may aim to dominate the market and raise shareholder value, whereas a small business may only aim to survive and earn enough for the owner.
7. For example, providing healthcare to all, reducing waiting times, or staying within the budget.
8. Any two: customer satisfaction; market share; survival; staff retention; ethical targets.
9. Any two: grants; cheap loans; training; advice; lower taxes.
10. Any two: poor cash flow; poor planning; poor management; strong competition; lack of finance.
11. Customers spend less in a recession, so sales fall and costs may exceed revenue, so keeping the business going matters more than expanding.
12. Cost: alternative packaging may be more expensive. Benefit: customers who value the environment may buy more, improving the image.
