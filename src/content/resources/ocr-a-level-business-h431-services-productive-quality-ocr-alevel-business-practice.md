## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a 'service'. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a service cannot be stored. **[2]**

**3.** Fenwick Repairs completed 2,500 jobs last year and received 175 complaints. Calculate the number of complaints per 1,000 jobs. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** limitation of using the number of complaints to measure the quality of service. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is a mystery shopper?

A: A customer who leaves a review online.

B: A trained person who poses as a customer and scores the service.

C: A manager who inspects the premises.

D: A supplier who visits to take orders.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Fenwick Repairs repairs household appliances. It loses 4% of its 2,500 customers each year because of poor service. Each customer spends £180 and the business makes a profit of 30% on that spend.

**(a)** Calculate the number of customers lost and the profit lost. **[3]**

**(b)** Fenwick plans a staff-training programme that costs £7,500. It is expected to halve the proportion of customers lost. Calculate the extra annual profit and the payback period, to one decimal place. **[4]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Fenwick Repairs should invest in the training programme. **[8]**

**7.** A customer survey of 400 Fenwick customers found that 312 were satisfied. After the training, a new survey of 400 found that 340 were satisfied.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the percentage of satisfied customers. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of surveys and mystery shoppers for monitoring the quality of service at Fenwick Repairs. **[8]**

**8.** Evaluate the importance of services to the UK economy. **[9]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which a hotel could improve the quality of its customer service. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A service is an activity carried out for a customer [1] where the benefit comes from the activity itself rather than from owning a physical product [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A service is produced and used at the same time [1], so if it is not used when it is available, such as an empty seat or an unused appointment, the income is lost and cannot be recovered later [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 175 ÷ 2,500 [1] × 1,000 = **70 per 1,000** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Many unhappy customers do not complain, but simply stop using the business [1], so a low number may give a false impression of satisfaction [1]. Accept: the number takes no account of how many customers there were. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Customers lost = 4% × 2,500 = **100** [1]. Revenue lost = 100 × £180 = £18,000 [1]. Profit lost = 30% × £18,000 = **£5,400** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Halving the loss keeps 50 customers [1]. Extra profit = 50 × £180 × 30% = **£2,700** a year [1]. Payback = £7,500 ÷ £2,700 [1] = **2.8 years** [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** The payback of 2.8 years is long for a small business, and the whole annual profit loss from poor service is only £5,400 [1]. The programme is expected to recover half of this [1]. The assumption that it will halve the loss is uncertain, and staff may leave and take their training with them [1].
Other benefits not in the figures: fewer complaints to deal with, a better reputation from word of mouth, and higher staff morale [1]. The figures may also understate the cost of poor service, because unhappy customers tell others [1].
Costs not included: time away from repair work during training [1].
Judgement: Fenwick should go ahead if it believes that the non-financial benefits are significant [1], but it could cut the risk by starting with cheaper measures such as clearer communication about repair times, then measure results before spending £7,500 [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Before: 312 ÷ 400 × 100 = 78% [1]. After: 340 ÷ 400 × 100 = 85%. The increase is **7 percentage points** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Surveys: they give comparable figures over time, as the 78% to 85% change shows [1], and can ask about specific parts of the service [1]. However, only customers who respond are counted, and 400 may be a small sample of 2,500 customers [1].
Mystery shoppers: they give detailed observation of the whole process, including how staff behave and whether procedures are followed [1], but cover only a few visits and are costly for a small business [1].
Comparison: surveys show what customers feel; mystery shoppers show what staff do [1].
Evaluation: neither method alone is enough [1]. Judgement: for a small firm, a short survey after every job is the best main method because it is cheap and continuous, with an occasional mystery shopper visit to check the process [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Services are the largest part of the UK economy, around four-fifths of output and jobs [1], so the performance of the economy depends heavily on them [1]. They employ people across many skill levels, from care workers to lawyers [1], and generate tax revenue that funds public spending [1]. The UK exports services such as finance, insurance and education, which earn income from abroad [1]. Other sectors rely on services such as transport, banking and advertising [1].
Limits: the economy depends on a few sectors, such as finance, which can be hit by a downturn [1]. Some service jobs are low paid or insecure, and services can be concentrated in some regions [1].
Judgement: services are central to the UK economy because of their size and their role in supporting other sectors, but over-reliance on a few services makes the economy vulnerable [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Training: staff are trained in listening and handling complaints [1], so guests are dealt with politely and problems are solved at the first contact [1]. Better systems: an online booking and check-in system [1] reduces waiting times and errors [1]. Accept empowerment, acting on feedback or reward systems, explained in the same way. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the number of complaints without dividing by the number of customers.
- Writing about goods instead of services, for example "the product can be stored".
- Listing measurement methods without evaluating them.
- Not linking the answer to the type of business.
- Omitting employees from the stakeholders affected by poor service.
