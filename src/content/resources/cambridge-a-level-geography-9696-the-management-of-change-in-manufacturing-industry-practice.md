These questions cover **11.4 The management of change in manufacturing industry**, the case study section of the Paper 4 option Production, location and change. Places and data in resource questions are fictional; other questions ask for your own case study of one country. Revise first with the [11.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-change-in-manufacturing-industry/) and the [11.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-change-in-manufacturing-industry-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "industrial policy". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between import substitution and export orientation as industrial strategies. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows manufacturing employment (thousands) in Larnia, a fictional middle-income country.

| Sector | 1990 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Textiles and clothing | 420 | 150 |
| Electronics | 80 | 390 |
| Vehicles and parts | 60 | 170 |
| Food processing | 140 | 160 |
| Total | 700 | 870 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Larnia's manufacturing employment in electronics in 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the character of Larnia's manufacturing changed between 1990 and 2020. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest how industrial policy may have caused these changes. **[4]**

**4.** In Corvenia, a fictional country, the capital region's share of manufacturing output rose from 52% in 1995 to 61% in 2020, while the interior's share fell from 28% to 17%. Grants for firms locating in the interior were available throughout the period.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the interior's share, in percentage points. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why regional grants failed to stop manufacturing concentrating in the capital region. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how rising wages can change the location and organisation of a country's manufacturing. **[4]**

**6.** Outline **two** ways in which a government can change the organisation of manufacturing. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** With reference to one country you have studied, describe how industrial policy changed the character of its manufacturing. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the issues that can arise when a government supports a small number of very large firms. **[6]**

**9.** Read the extract about a fictional town.

> Port Ellery depends on one shipyard, which employs 9 000 people and buys from 120 local suppliers. Orders have collapsed and the yard will close. The government is considering three responses: (A) retraining workers for jobs in a city 200 km away; (B) building a new industrial park with tax relief for incoming firms; (C) a two-year wage subsidy to keep the yard open while new orders are sought.

Assess the likely success of the three responses. **[8]**

**10.** "Industrial policy has changed the character of manufacturing more successfully than its location." With reference to one country you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Deliberate government action [1] to influence what manufacturing produces, where and by whom, using tools such as incentives, zones, protection or state firms [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Import substitution produces goods at home that were previously imported [1], protected by tariffs or quotas, to supply the domestic market [1]. Export orientation produces goods to compete in world markets, encouraged by export zones, tax relief or cheap credit [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** 390 ÷ 870 × 100 [1] = 44.8% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Textiles and clothing fell sharply from 420 000 to 150 000 jobs [1]. Electronics rose almost fivefold, from 80 000 to 390 000, becoming the largest sector [1]. Vehicles nearly tripled while food processing changed little, so manufacturing shifted from labour-intensive, lower-value goods towards higher-value products [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Export zones and tax relief may have attracted foreign electronics firms [1]. State support or protection may have built a vehicle industry [1]. Spending on technical education may have supplied the skilled workers that electronics needs [1]. Reduced protection for textiles, or rising minimum wages, may have pushed low-cost clothing production out [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** 28 − 17 = 11 percentage points fall [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** The capital region offers agglomeration economies, such as suppliers and skilled labour, worth more than the grants [1]. Ports, airports and the largest market are near the capital, lowering transport costs [1]. Grants are a one-off payment, while location costs in the interior continue every year [1]. Managers and skilled workers may prefer to live in the capital, so firms find it hard to recruit elsewhere [1]. **[4]**

**5.** As wages rise, labour-intensive assembly becomes less profitable at home [1], so firms move it to lower-wage countries or regions [1]. Firms that stay automate and specialise in higher-value products [1]. Large domestic firms become TNCs with branch plants abroad, changing the organisation of production [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Setting up or privatising state-owned enterprises [1], which changes who owns and runs major plants [1]. Directing credit, licences or contracts to chosen firms [1], which can create or strengthen large business groups [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit rules on foreign ownership or joint ventures, or support for small firms and co-operatives, each with its effect.

**7.** Credit any country. Example for South Korea: in the 1960s export promotion made labour-intensive goods such as textiles, clothing and wigs the main exports [1], supported by cheap credit and the Masan Free Export Zone [1]. The 1973 Heavy and Chemical Industry drive shifted the focus to steel, ships, machinery and chemicals [1], such as POSCO steel at Pohang and shipbuilding at Ulsan [1]. From the 1980s support for research and education moved production towards semiconductors, electronics and cars [1], raising value added per worker [1]. **[6]**

**8.** The economy becomes dependent on a few firms, so the failure of one causes large job losses [1], as when Daewoo collapsed in South Korea in 1999 [1]. Favoured firms may borrow heavily because they expect state rescue [1], increasing financial risk in a crisis [1]. Small and medium firms may struggle to obtain credit or compete [1], and large firms gain political influence that makes reform difficult [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Response A may help individual workers [1], but many will not move 200 km because of family ties and housing [1], and it does nothing for the 120 local suppliers or the town [1]. Response B keeps jobs in the town if firms come [1], but firms may not choose a declining town with no linkages, and skills may not match [1]. Response C saves jobs in the short term [1], but if world demand stays low it only delays closure at high cost [1]. On balance, B combined with retraining for local jobs has most long-term potential, though success is uncertain [1]. **[8]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define industrial policy [1], and character and location in the syllabus sense [1].

*Character changed successfully:* in South Korea, export promotion built labour-intensive industry in the 1960s [1]; the 1973 drive created steel, ships and machinery [1]; later support moved production to semiconductors and electronics [1]; this upgrading happened within a few decades [1].

*Location changed by policy:* new coastal complexes were built at Pohang, Ulsan and Changwon [1]; Gumi became an electronics centre inland [1]; these places grew because the state chose them [1].

*Location changed less successfully:* high-technology industry clustered around Seoul [1] because of research institutes and graduates [1]; the south-west lagged despite regional measures [1]; relocating public bodies to Sejong moved government more than industry [1].

*Analysis:* policy changes location most when it matches location factors such as ports [1], and least when it works against agglomeration [1]. Character depends on firms and skills that policy can build nationally [1], whereas location must overcome market forces [1].

*Comparison:* Malaysia's zones also reshaped location, concentrating industry in Penang and the Klang Valley [1].

*Judgement:* largely agree: character changed more fully than location, though policy did create new industrial places [1]. A clear final view on "how far" [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* top answers judge location success separately for new heavy industry and for later high-technology industry.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing changes in employment without figures.
- Explaining location only through policy, without location factors.
- Case-study answers with no named places.
- Assessments that discuss options but give no final judgement.
- Essays that ignore "organisation", the third syllabus word.
