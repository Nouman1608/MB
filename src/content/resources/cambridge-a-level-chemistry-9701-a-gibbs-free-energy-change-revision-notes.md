**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level**
subtopic 23.4: state and use ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵; calculate with it; decide feasibility from
the sign of ΔG; predict the effect of temperature on feasibility from ΔH⦵ and ΔS⦵.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gibbs equation | ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵ |
| ΔG⦵ | standard Gibbs free energy change, kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Feasible | ΔG is negative: the reaction can occur without a continuous external energy input |
| Not feasible | ΔG is positive under the stated conditions |
| Changeover temperature | the temperature at which ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH ÷ ΔS |
| Kinetic stability | a feasible reaction that is very slow because its activation energy is high |

## The calculation routine

1. Convert ΔS from J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (÷ 1000), or ΔH from kJ to J (× 1000).
2. Convert T to kelvin (°C + 273).
3. Substitute with brackets: ΔG = (ΔH) − (T)(ΔS).
4. State the sign and the conclusion about feasibility.
5. For a changeover temperature, set ΔG = 0: T = ΔH ÷ ΔS, both in the same energy unit.

**Example** *(data supplied)*: 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g); ΔH⦵ = −197.8 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔS⦵ = −187.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

    ΔG at 298 K = −197.8 − (298 × −0.1876) = −197.8 + 55.9 = −141.9 kJ mol⁻¹   feasible
    changeover T = 197 800 ÷ 187.6 = 1054 K   not feasible above this

**Example** *(data supplied)*: N₂O₄(g) → 2NO₂(g); ΔH⦵ = +57.2 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔS⦵ = +175.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

    ΔG at 298 K = 57.2 − (298 × 0.1759) = 57.2 − 52.4 = +4.8 kJ mol⁻¹   not feasible
    changeover T = 57 200 ÷ 175.9 = 325 K   feasible above this

The second example shows how close ΔG can be to zero: a modest rise in temperature changes the
conclusion.

## The four sign cases, with the reason

| ΔH | ΔS | −TΔS term | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| − | + | negative at all T | always feasible |
| + | − | positive at all T | never feasible |
| − | − | positive, grows with T | feasible below T = ΔH ÷ ΔS |
| + | + | negative, grows with T | feasible above T = ΔH ÷ ΔS |

**Reasoning to quote:** "As temperature increases, the magnitude of TΔS increases. Because ΔS
is positive, −TΔS becomes more negative, so ΔG becomes more negative and the reaction becomes
more feasible."

## Graph of ΔG against T

- Straight line: intercept = ΔH, gradient = −ΔS.
- ΔS positive → line slopes **down**; ΔS negative → line slopes **up**.
- Where the line crosses ΔG = 0 is the changeover temperature.

## Phase changes

At a melting or boiling point, the two states are in equilibrium, so ΔG = 0 and
ΔS = ΔH ÷ T. Example *(value supplied)*: ΔH<sub>vap</sub>(water) = +40.7 kJ mol⁻¹ at 373 K gives
ΔS = 40 700 ÷ 373 = +109 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

## Feasible versus observed

- A negative ΔG says nothing about rate.
- Magnesium in air, a hydrogen–chlorine mixture kept in the dark, and hydrogen peroxide in a
  bottle are all feasible changes that may be very slow until energy or a catalyst is supplied.
- Explanation needed: **high activation energy**, so the reaction is kinetically, not
  energetically, prevented.

## Assumption in changeover temperatures

ΔH⦵ and ΔS⦵ are measured at 298 K. Using them at other temperatures assumes they **stay
approximately constant**, so a calculated changeover temperature is an estimate.

## Exam traps

- Divide ΔS by 1000 (or multiply ΔH by 1000) before combining them.
- T in kelvin, never °C.
- When ΔS is negative, −TΔS is positive: keep brackets.
- Only the same-sign cases have a changeover temperature.
- Do not say a feasible reaction "will happen quickly" or "will be seen".
- ΔG = 0 is the boundary, not "no reaction".
- State the conclusion: "ΔG is negative, so feasible" earns the mark; a number alone may not.
- Round sensibly: three significant figures for ΔG and a whole number of kelvin is usual.

## Self-test

1. State the Gibbs equation.
2. What sign of ΔG shows that a reaction is feasible?
3. ΔH⦵ = −50.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = +40.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K.
4. For the reaction in Question 3, explain whether it is feasible at all temperatures.
5. ΔH⦵ = +120.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = +150.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate the minimum temperature for
   feasibility.
6. ΔH⦵ = −137.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = −120.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Above what temperature does the
   reaction stop being feasible?
7. Explain why a reaction with ΔH positive and ΔS negative is never feasible.
8. A reaction has ΔG⦵ = −200 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K but no change is seen when the reactants are
   mixed. Explain.
9. What assumption is made when using 298 K data to find a changeover temperature?
10. On a graph of ΔG against T, what do the intercept and gradient represent?
11. Ethanol boils at 351 K with ΔH<sub>vap</sub> = +38.6 kJ mol⁻¹ *(value supplied)*.
    Calculate ΔS<sub>vap</sub>.
12. A student substitutes ΔS = −120 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with ΔH = −80 kJ mol⁻¹ and T = 298 K, and
    gets ΔG = +35 680. What has gone wrong?

**Answers**

1. ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵.
2. Negative.
3. −50.0 − (298 × 0.0400) = −50.0 − 11.9 = **−61.9 kJ mol⁻¹**.
4. Yes: ΔH is negative and −TΔS is negative at every temperature, so ΔG is always negative.
5. T = 120 000 ÷ 150.0 = **800 K**.
6. T = 137 000 ÷ 120.6 = **1136 K**.
7. ΔH is positive and −TΔS is also positive, so ΔG is positive at every temperature.
8. The activation energy is high, so the rate is extremely slow even though the reaction is
   feasible.
9. ΔH and ΔS stay approximately constant as temperature changes.
10. Intercept = ΔH; gradient = −ΔS.
11. ΔS = 38 600 ÷ 351 = **+110 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
12. ΔS was not converted to kJ: ΔG = −80 − (298 × −0.120) = −80 + 35.8 = **−44.2 kJ mol⁻¹**.
