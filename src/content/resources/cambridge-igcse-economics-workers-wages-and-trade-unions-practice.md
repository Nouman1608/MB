## Questions

**1.** Define a trade union. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two non-wage factors that may influence a person's choice of occupation. **[4]**

**3.** Job X pays 3,000 a month. Job Y pays 2,700 a month plus a bonus of 450 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly money reward from job Y. **[1]**
**(b)** State which job has the higher money reward. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why a worker might still choose the other job. **[2]**

**4.** The table shows the market for warehouse workers in Country B (invented).

| Hourly wage | Workers demanded | Workers supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 1,600 | 1,000 |
| 9 | 1,500 | 1,200 |
| 10 | 1,400 | 1,400 |
| 11 | 1,300 | 1,600 |
| 12 | 1,200 | 1,800 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium wage and the number employed. **[2]**
**(b)** A training scheme raises supply by 300 workers at every wage. State the new equilibrium wage and employment. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the wage. **[1]**

**5.** The government of Country B sets a minimum wage of 12 in the market above.

**(a)** Calculate the number of workers who want a job but cannot find one. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the hourly wage bill compared with equilibrium. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the minimum wage helps low-paid workers. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why electricians usually earn more than cleaners. **[4]**

**7.** In a country, average pay for men is 22.5 an hour and for women 18.

**(a)** Calculate the pay gap as a percentage of men's pay. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how discrimination could cause such a gap. **[3]**
**(c)** State one government policy to reduce it. **[1]**

**8.** In Country C, average monthly pay is 2,400 in the public sector and 3,000 in the private sector.

**(a)** Calculate by what percentage public pay is below private pay. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why some workers still choose public-sector jobs. **[3]**

**9.** A tailoring workshop has eight workers. Each makes 6 jackets a week alone. After tasks are divided, the workshop makes 72 jackets. Each worker is paid 90 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in output per worker. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the labour cost per jacket before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Discuss the disadvantages of division of labour for workers and for the economy. **[4]**

**10.** A firm employs 60 workers on 350 a week. The union and firm agree a rise of 4%.

**(a)** Describe what is meant by collective bargaining. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rise in the yearly wage bill (52 weeks). **[3]**
**(c)** Inflation had been 6% with a pay rise of 4%. Explain what happened to real pay. **[2]**
**(d)** In another dispute, 150 workers on 70 a day strike for 5 days. Calculate the wages lost. **[2]**

**11.** A union has 168 members among 480 workers in an industry.

**(a)** Calculate union membership as a percentage of workers. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain two factors that would raise the union's strength. **[4]**
**(c)** Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of strong union activity for firms and the government. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An organisation of workers [1] that aims to protect and improve members' pay and working conditions [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good definition says who the members are and what the body is for; "a group that goes on strike" describes one action, not the body.

**2.** Any two, each with a reason: job security [1] because a stable income lets the worker plan and repay loans [1]; flexible hours [1] because they fit around childcare or study [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a factor and explaining its effect on the choice are separate points; a list of four factors with no link to the decision gains little.

**3. (a)** 2,700 + 450 = **3,150** [1]
**(b)** **Job Y** [1]
**(c)** Job X may have shorter hours or a shorter journey [1], which the worker values more than the extra 150 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the question when comparing; the comparison must be of the full money package, not the basic pay.

**4. (a)** Demand equals supply at 10 [1]: **wage 10, employment 1,400** [1]
**(b)** Supply is now 300 higher at each wage: at 9, supply is 1,500 and demand is 1,500 [1], so **wage 9** [1] and **employment 1,500** [1]
**(c)** (9 - 10)/10 = **-10%** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show which row you used; a bare "9" with no matching employment figure leaves a gap, and a rise in supply must lower the wage.

**5. (a)** At 12, supply is 1,800 and demand 1,200 [1]: 1,800 - 1,200 = **600** [1]
**(b)** Before: 10 x 1,400 = 14,000 [1]. After: 12 x 1,200 = 14,400, a rise of 400/14,000 = **2.9%** [1]
**(c)** Workers kept in jobs earn 20% more per hour [1]; but 200 jobs are lost and 600 are left unemployed in total [1]; firms face higher costs and may use machines [1]; judgement, such as that it helps if few jobs are lost and harms if many [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a judgement tied to something the answer depends on; saying only "it has good and bad effects" stops short of that.

**6.** Electricians need long training, so supply is small [1]; their work is valuable, so demand is high [1]; the diagram shows a high equilibrium wage [1] while cleaners are plentiful and need little training, so supply is large and the wage low [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking each reason to a supply or demand curve scores better than saying one job is harder.

**7. (a)** 22.5 - 18 = 4.5 [1]; 4.5/22.5 = **20%** [1]
**(b)** Employers treat women worse for reasons unrelated to skill [1]; demand for women in better-paid jobs falls [1]; so women earn less than equally productive men [1]
**(c)** Equal pay laws [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the base of the percentage; dividing by the lower figure gives a different answer and does not answer "as a percentage of men's pay".

**8. (a)** 3,000 - 2,400 = 600 [1]; 600/3,000 = **20%** [1]
**(b)** Public jobs may offer greater job security [1], a good pension [1], so the lower wage is offset by non-wage benefits [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A reference to non-wage benefits must name the benefit; "better conditions" alone is vague.

**9. (a)** Before: 6 per worker; after: 72/8 = 9 [1]; (9 - 6)/6 = **50%** [1]
**(b)** Before: 90/6 = **15** [1]; after: 90/9 = **10** [1]
**(c)** Workers may find tasks repetitive and lose satisfaction [1]; skills become narrow so moving jobs is hard [1]; the economy can suffer structural unemployment if a specialised industry declines [1]; judgement, for example that higher output usually outweighs these costs if retraining is available [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Both groups in the question need separate points; two points on workers alone cannot cover the economy.

**10. (a)** Union representatives negotiate for all members [1] with the employer over pay and conditions [1]
**(b)** Before: 60 x 350 x 52 = 1,092,000 [1]; after: 60 x 364 x 52 = 1,135,680 [1]; rise **43,680** [1]
**(c)** Pay rose 4% but prices rose 6% [1], so real pay fell (by about 1.9%) [1]
**(d)** 150 x 5 x 70 = **52,500** [2] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication; for real pay, compare the rise with inflation and state the direction.

**11. (a)** 168/480 = **35%** [1]
**(b)** High membership [1] because the union speaks for most workers and a strike would stop output [1]; scarce skills [1] because strikers cannot easily be replaced [1]
**(c)** Firms: higher pay raises costs [1], but a single bargaining partner and motivated staff help [1]. Government: fewer disputes and a voice for workers [1], but pressure for costly policy and price rises [1]. Judgement based on strength of the union and the state of the economy [2]. **[11]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both firms and government and finish with a reasoned conclusion; a one-sided list lacks the evaluation the command word asks for.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a trade union definition that omits pay or conditions.
- Naming a non-wage factor without explaining how it affects the choice.
- Reading the wrong row from a wage table.
- Forgetting that a minimum wage below equilibrium has no effect.
- Using the wrong base in a percentage gap.
- Treating a rise in supply as raising the wage.
- Giving only workers' views on division of labour or unions.
- Skipping a judgement in an evaluation question.
