Recall notes for **4.4.2 Atoms and nuclear radiation** (4.4.2.1 to 4.4.2.4) in AQA GCSE Physics (8463),
Paper 1. The net-decline ratio calculation is **Higher tier only**.

Worked examples on choosing sources, equations and half-life are in the [atoms and nuclear radiation study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-atoms-and-nuclear-radiation/).
Practise with the [atoms and nuclear radiation practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-atoms-and-nuclear-radiation-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Radioactive decay | Random process in which an unstable nucleus gives out radiation to become more stable |
| Activity | Rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays, in becquerel (Bq) |
| Count-rate | Number of decays recorded each second by a detector, such as a Geiger-Muller tube |
| Half-life | Time for the number of nuclei of an isotope in a sample to halve, or for the count-rate (or activity) to fall to half |
| Radioactive contamination | Unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials |
| Irradiation | Exposing an object to nuclear radiation; the object does not become radioactive |
| Peer review | Checking of published findings by other scientists |

## The emissions

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) | Neutron (n) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | 2 protons + 2 neutrons (helium nucleus) | Fast electron from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton | Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus | A neutron |
| Symbol in equations | ⁴₂He | ⁰₋₁e | γ | n |
| Stopped by | Paper | A few mm of aluminium | Thick lead or concrete (reduces it) | -- |
| Range in air | A few cm | Further than alpha | Very far | -- |
| Ionising power | Strong | Moderate | Weak | -- |

Only penetration, range in air and ionising power are required for alpha, beta and gamma.

## Changes to the nucleus

- **Alpha:** mass number −4, atomic number −2 (mass and charge both decrease).
- **Beta:** mass number unchanged, atomic number +1 (charge increases).
- **Gamma:** no change to mass or charge.
- Balance both rows: top numbers (mass) and bottom numbers (atomic).

Examples:

```
²³⁸₉₂U  → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He
¹³¹₅₃I  → ¹³¹₅₄Xe + ⁰₋₁e
²⁴¹₉₅Am → ²³⁷₉₃Np + ⁴₂He
```

## Choosing a source

| Job | Best emission | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring paper thickness | Beta | Partly absorbed by paper, so count-rate changes with thickness |
| Tracer detected outside the body | Gamma | Leaves the body; weakly ionising |
| Source must be stopped by a thin barrier | Alpha | Stopped by paper |

Answer frame for an "evaluate the best source" question:

1. State what the radiation must do (pass through the body, be partly absorbed by the material, be stopped).
2. Rule out each unsuitable emission with its property (alpha stopped by paper; gamma passes through almost
   unaffected).
3. Name the best emission and link its penetration or ionising power to the job.
4. If asked about safety, add the effect of its ionising power on living cells.

## Half-life

- Random for one nucleus; predictable for very large numbers.
- From data: count the halvings and divide the time by that number.
- From a graph: halve the y-value, read across and down; repeat and average.
- **Higher tier only:** after n half-lives, the fraction left is (1/2)ⁿ. After 3: 1/8; after 4: 1/16; after 6:
  1/64. Express the net decline as a ratio, for example 1 : 16.

Worked reminders:

- 800 Bq to 100 Bq in 12 days: three halvings, half-life **4 days**.
- Count-rate 640 counts/s, half-life 2 min: after 8 min (4 half-lives) it is **40 counts/s**.

## Contamination versus irradiation

| | Contamination | Irradiation |
|---|---|---|
| Object radioactive? | Carries radioactive atoms | No |
| When the hazard stops | When the atoms have decayed or been removed | When the source is removed or shielded |
| Main precaution | Gloves, avoid ingestion or inhalation | Distance, shielding, limited time, tongs |

Studies of radiation's effects on humans are published and shared so they can be checked by peer review.

## Exam traps

- **Beta origin:** from the nucleus, not the electron shells.
- **Balancing:** both mass and atomic numbers, including −1 for the beta particle.
- **Half-life:** not "half the time to decay completely".
- **Irradiation:** does **not** make the object radioactive.
- **Activity vs count-rate:** activity is the source's decays per second; count-rate is what the detector records.
- **Source choice:** explain why the others are unsuitable, not just why the chosen one works.

## Self-test

1. Define activity and give its unit.
2. What is a beta particle?
3. Which emission is most strongly ionising?
4. Which emission is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium?
5. What happens to the mass and atomic numbers in alpha decay?
6. Complete: ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ?
7. Polonium-210 (atomic number 84) emits an alpha particle. Give the mass and atomic numbers of the new nucleus.
8. Define half-life.
9. A sample's count-rate falls from 360 to 45 counts per minute in 24 hours. Find the half-life.
10. (Higher tier) What fraction of a sample's activity remains after 5 half-lives?
11. Does an irradiated object become radioactive?
12. Why must results on the effects of radiation be published?

### Answers

1. The rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays; becquerel (Bq).
2. A high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton.
3. Alpha.
4. Beta.
5. Mass number falls by 4; atomic number falls by 2.
6. ⁰₋₁e (a beta particle).
7. Mass number **206**, atomic number **82**.
8. The time for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve (or for the count-rate to fall to half).
9. 360 → 180 → 90 → 45 is three halvings, so 24 ÷ 3 = **8 hours**.
10. **1/32**.
11. No.
12. So other scientists can check the findings by peer review.
