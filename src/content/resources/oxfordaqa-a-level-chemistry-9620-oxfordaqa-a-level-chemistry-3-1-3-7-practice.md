These questions cover **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Boiling points are approximate. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [forces between molecules study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7/) or the [forces between molecules revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Name all the types of force that act between the particles in each substance.

**(a)** krypton, Kr **[1]**

**(b)** hydrogen bromide, HBr **[1]**

**(c)** methylamine, CH₃NH₂ **[1]**

**2.** Explain how a temporary dipole in one molecule leads to an attraction with a neighbouring molecule. **[2]**

**3.** The boiling points of the halogens are: F₂ −188 °C, Cl₂ −34 °C, Br₂ 59 °C, I₂ 184 °C. Explain this trend. **[2]**

**4.** From HCOOH, CH₃CHO, CH₃NH₂, CH₃F and (CH₃)₃N, identify the two compounds whose molecules can form hydrogen bonds with each other. **[2]**

**5.** Hexane boils at 69 °C and its isomer 2,3-dimethylbutane boils at 58 °C. Explain why. **[2]**

**6.** The boiling points of the Group 14 hydrides rise steadily from CH₄ (−162 °C) to SnH₄ (−52 °C), with no anomaly for CH₄. Explain why CH₄ shows no anomaly. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Boiling points of the Group 15 hydrides: NH₃ −33 °C, PH₃ −88 °C, AsH₃ −62 °C, SbH₃ −17 °C.

**(a)** Explain the increase in boiling point from PH₃ to SbH₃. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the boiling point of NH₃ does not fit this trend. **[2]**

**(c)** Draw a diagram to show a hydrogen bond between two ammonia molecules. Include partial charges and the relevant lone pair. **[3]**

**8.** Butane (Mr 58), propanone (Mr 58) and propan-2-ol (Mr 60) boil at about −1 °C, 56 °C and 82 °C.

**(a)** Name the forces between molecules in each liquid. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the order of boiling points. **[3]**

**9.** This question is about ice.

**(a)** Describe the arrangement of molecules in ice. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why ice floats on liquid water. **[2]**

**(c)** Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, melts at −86 °C. Explain why ice melts at a much higher temperature. **[2]**

**10.** Ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂CH₂OH (Mr 62), boils at 197 °C. Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (Mr 60), boils at 97 °C.

**(a)** Explain why ethane-1,2-diol has the much higher boiling point. **[3]**

**(b)** Draw a hydrogen bond between a molecule of ethane-1,2-diol and a water molecule, with the hydrogen atom of the hydrogen bond coming from water. **[2]**

**11.** Chlorine, Cl₂, boils at −34 °C. Hydrogen chloride, HCl, boils at −85 °C.

**(a)** Explain why chlorine has the higher boiling point even though its molecules are non-polar. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why HCl molecules do not form hydrogen bonds with each other. **[1]**

**12.** Propylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, and trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N, are isomers. Propylamine boils at 48 °C; trimethylamine boils at 3 °C.

**(a)** Explain the difference in boiling points. **[3]**

**(b)** Trimethylamine molecules can still form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Explain how. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. (a)** Van der Waals (induced dipole–dipole) forces only [1]

**(b)** Van der Waals and permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]

**(c)** Van der Waals, permanent dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Van der Waals forces are present in every case; leaving them out of (b) or (c) loses the mark.

**2.** Electrons move, so at one instant the electron distribution is uneven, giving a temporary dipole [1]; this induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule, and the two dipoles attract [1] **[2]**

**3.** Down the group the molecules have more electrons [1]; so van der Waals forces are stronger and more energy is needed to overcome them [1] **[2]**

**4.** HCOOH [1] and CH₃NH₂ [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* (CH₃)₃N has a nitrogen lone pair but no N–H bond, so its molecules cannot hydrogen bond to each other.

**5.** 2,3-Dimethylbutane is branched, so its molecules are more compact with less surface contact [1]; its van der Waals forces are weaker, needing less energy to overcome [1] **[2]**

**6.** Carbon is not electronegative enough to make C–H bonds strongly polar [1]; so CH₄ cannot form hydrogen bonds and has only van der Waals forces, which fit the trend [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** The molecules have more electrons from PH₃ to SbH₃ [1]; so the van der Waals forces get stronger and more energy is needed to separate the molecules [1]

**(b)** NH₃ forms hydrogen bonds between molecules because H is bonded to the very electronegative nitrogen, which has a lone pair [1]; hydrogen bonds are stronger than the other forces, so extra energy is needed to overcome them [1]

**(c)** Two NH₃ molecules, with δ+ on an H and δ− on N [1]; a lone pair shown on the nitrogen of the second molecule [1]; a dashed line from the δ+ H of one molecule to that lone pair [1] **[7]**

**8. (a)** Butane: van der Waals forces only [1]; propanone: van der Waals and permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]; propan-2-ol: van der Waals, permanent dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding [1]

**(b)** The three have similar numbers of electrons, so similar van der Waals forces [1]; propanone's extra permanent dipole–dipole forces raise its boiling point above butane's [1]; propan-2-ol forms hydrogen bonds, the strongest intermolecular force, so it needs the most energy to separate its molecules [1] **[6]**

**9. (a)** Each water molecule is hydrogen bonded to four others [1]; in an open, regular tetrahedral lattice with a lot of space [1]

**(b)** When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and the open lattice collapses [1]; the molecules pack more closely in the liquid, so liquid water is denser and ice floats [1]

**(c)** Water molecules form hydrogen bonds; H₂S molecules do not, because sulfur is not electronegative enough [1]; more energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds in ice [1] **[6]**

**10. (a)** Both molecules form hydrogen bonds and have similar van der Waals forces [1]; ethane-1,2-diol has two O–H groups per molecule, propan-1-ol only one [1]; so ethane-1,2-diol forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule and more energy is needed to separate its molecules [1]

**(b)** δ+ H on the water molecule and δ− O on the diol, with a lone pair on that O [1]; dashed line from the water's H to the lone pair on the diol's oxygen [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** Cl₂ molecules have more electrons than HCl molecules (34 compared with 18) [1]; so the van der Waals forces between Cl₂ molecules are stronger [1]; this outweighs the permanent dipole–dipole forces in HCl, so more energy is needed to separate Cl₂ molecules [1]

**(b)** Hydrogen is bonded to chlorine, not to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* In (a), answers that say only "HCl is polar so it should boil higher" miss the point; compare the sizes of the van der Waals forces.

**12. (a)** Propylamine has an N–H bond, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other [1]; trimethylamine has no H bonded to N, so it has only van der Waals and permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]; more energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds in propylamine [1]

**(b)** The nitrogen in trimethylamine has a lone pair [1]; a δ+ H of an O–H bond in water is attracted to that lone pair, forming a hydrogen bond [1] **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that covalent bonds are broken on boiling.
- Leaving out van der Waals forces when other forces are present.
- Claiming hydrogen bonding for H atoms bonded to carbon or chlorine.
- Drawing a hydrogen bond to an atom rather than to a lone pair, or without partial charges.
- Explaining a boiling-point difference by polarity alone when the molecules differ greatly in their numbers of electrons.
