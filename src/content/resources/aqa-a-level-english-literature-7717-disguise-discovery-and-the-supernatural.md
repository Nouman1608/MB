**This guide covers one aspect from AQA's list for Option 1B Aspects of Comedy:** "the use of
disguise, escapes and discovery, elements of the supernatural". The
[Aspects of Comedy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-1b-aspects-of-comedy-7717/)
names the main disguises in the set texts; this page explains what disguise, escape, discovery and
the supernatural *do* in a comedy, how they connect, and how to analyse them in each section of
Paper 1.

## Why these four belong together

AQA groups them in one phrase because they form a sequence. A **disguise** or concealment creates
a gap between who a character is and who others think they are. The gap produces confusion, danger
and comedy. An **escape** removes a character from a threat or from the rules of ordinary life,
often into a place where disguise is easier. **Discovery** closes the gap, usually in the final act,
and allows the comic resolution. The **supernatural** sits beside these as another source of the
unexplained: dreams, witches, magic or the appearance of something impossible, which the comedy may
take seriously, mock, or explain away.

When you analyse any one of the four, ask where it sits in this sequence and what the audience knows
that the characters do not.

## Disguise

**Disguise of gender.** In *Twelfth Night*, Viola's disguise as Cesario makes her the go-between in
Orsino's courtship of Olivia, so she must woo on behalf of the man she loves. The disguise creates
dramatic irony in almost every scene she shares with them. On the Elizabethan stage, female roles
were played by boy actors, so the audience watched a boy playing a woman playing a young man; the
layered performance adds to the play's interest in how fluid identity and desire can be.

**Disguise of rank.** In *She Stoops to Conquer*, Kate Hardcastle exploits Marlow's mistake: he takes
her for a barmaid, and she plays the part. The disguise reveals the split in Marlow's manners, shy
with women of his own class and bold with servants, and lets Kate know the real man before she
marries him. The title names the strategy.

**Disguise as multiplication.** The Bianca plot in *The Taming of the Shrew* multiplies false
identities: Lucentio becomes the tutor "Cambio", his servant Tranio becomes Lucentio, Hortensio
poses as a music tutor, and a travelling pedant is persuaded to pose as Lucentio's father. The comedy
lies in the number of disguises and in the risk that they will collide, which they do when the real
Vincentio arrives in Padua.

**Disguise as habit.** In *The Importance of Being Earnest*, Jack is "Ernest" in town and Jack in the
country, and Algernon arrives at Jack's country house pretending to be the invented brother Ernest.
Wilde makes disguise a way of life for the leisured class: both young men escape obligation by
inventing selves. The two women's discovery that each believes herself engaged to "Ernest" turns
the deception back on its inventors.

**Concealment without costume.** *Emma* contains no literal disguise, but Frank Churchill's flirtation
with Emma conceals his secret engagement to Jane Fairfax. Austen shows that social manners can work
as disguise, and that the heroine's confidence in her own reading of people is the real target.

## Escapes

Escapes in comedy can be physical or social.

- **Physical escape.** In "The Nun's Priest's Tale", Chauntecleer escapes from the fox's jaws by
  persuading it to speak; in "Tam o' Shanter", Tam escapes the witches by reaching the bridge, and
  his mare loses her tail at the last moment. Both escapes are comic because they are narrow and
  slightly undignified.
- **Social escape.** "Bunburying" in *Earnest* is an escape from duty; the elopement planned by
  Hastings and Constance in *She Stoops to Conquer* is an attempted escape from Mrs Hardcastle's
  control. Escapes often fail or are overtaken by discovery, which is part of the comedy.
- **Escape from disaster.** *Twelfth Night* begins with Viola's survival of a shipwreck, an escape
  that makes the whole comedy possible and that keeps the possibility of loss in view.

## Discovery

Discovery is the moment the gap closes. Look at **how** it is staged.

- **Recognition by evidence.** In the final scene of *Twelfth Night*, Viola and Sebastian test each
  other's memories of their father before they accept that each is alive. Orsino's line about "one
  face, one voice, one habit, and two persons" voices the audience's wonder at the twins seen
  together for the first time.
- **Recognition by object.** In *Earnest*, the handbag that Miss Prism left at a railway station
  becomes the proof of Jack's identity. Wilde makes the object absurd, so discovery parodies the
  tokens of recognition in older comedy.
- **Discovery as humiliation.** Marlow's discovery that the "barmaid" is Miss Hardcastle exposes him,
  but the play turns his embarrassment into the means of his marriage.
- **Self-discovery.** Emma's discovery that she loves Mr Knightley follows her discovery of how wrong
  she has been about others. In *Emma*, discovery is moral as well as factual.

## The supernatural

AQA's phrase "elements of the supernatural" covers a range of effects.

- **Dreams and prophecy.** In "The Nun's Priest's Tale", Chauntecleer dreams of a beast that will seize
  him, and he and Pertelote argue at length about whether dreams can be trusted. The mock-learned
  debate is comic, yet the dream comes true, so the tale plays with the supernatural rather than
  dismissing it.
- **The witches' dance.** "Tam o' Shanter" brings a drunken farmer to a church where witches dance to
  the Devil's music. Burns mixes terror and farce: Tam's shout of admiration gives him away and starts
  the chase. The supernatural becomes a comic consequence of drink and curiosity.
- **Fake possession.** In *Twelfth Night*, the tricksters treat Malvolio as possessed, and Feste visits
  him in the dark disguised as a curate, Sir Topas. Here disguise and the supernatural combine, and
  the joke is that the "madman" is the sanest person in the scene.
- **Myth and legend.** Fanthorpe's "Not My Best Side" gives voices to the figures in Uccello's painting
  of Saint George and the Dragon, so a legendary creature complains about how he has been painted.
  The supernatural is domesticated for comic effect.

## Worked example: a recognition-scene paragraph

**Question shape (original):** Explore the significance of discovery in the final scene of *Twelfth
Night*.

> Shakespeare delays the twins' recognition even after both are on stage together, and the delay is
> what makes the discovery moving as well as funny. The other characters have spent the act accusing
> Cesario of crimes Sebastian committed, so the comedy of mistaken identity reaches its peak just
> before it is resolved. When Viola and Sebastian finally meet, they do not embrace at once; they
> question each other about their father, as if the joy is too great to trust without evidence. The
> slow, formal exchange contrasts with the frantic confusion that precedes it, and it lets the
> audience enjoy what it has known all along. Yet Viola remains in her man's clothes, and Orsino
> will go on calling her Cesario until he sees her in her "woman's weeds", so the play ends with her
> disguise still in place. The discovery restores identities, but the final image keeps the
> instability that made the comedy possible.

**Why it works.** It analyses staging and pace (AO2), shows the dramatic irony of the audience's
knowledge, and finishes with an interpretation that questions how complete the discovery is (AO5).

## Common mistakes

- **Listing disguises** without explaining the dramatic irony or the gap they create.
- **Treating the supernatural as decoration.** Ask whether the text takes it seriously, mocks it or
  leaves it open.
- **Ignoring how discovery is staged.** Delay, evidence and tokens are choices to analyse.
- **Forgetting the audience.** Disguise comedy depends on what the audience knows; say so.
- **Overlooking poems.** Escapes and the supernatural are often clearest in the narrative poems.

## Exam technique

For a passage question, identify who is disguised or concealed in the passage and who knows, then
analyse how the gap creates comedy. For an essay on discovery, plan one paragraph each on the set-up
of the deception, the moment of greatest confusion and the discovery, then evaluate what remains
unresolved. In Section C, compare how two texts close the gap between appearance and identity.
