This guide teaches **Section P: Quantities and units in mechanics (P1)** of the AQA A-level Mathematics (7357) specification, version 1.3 (31 January 2018), for A-level exams from June 2018 onwards. The specification lists Section P under **Paper 2**, with vectors, kinematics, forces and moments, so this content is examined there. A calculator is required in every 7357 paper.

Section P is short, but every mechanics answer depends on it. A correct method with the wrong units, or with grams left as grams, gives a wrong number.

## What Section P covers

| Ref | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| P1 (fundamental) | Understand and use the fundamental quantities and units in the SI system: length, time, mass |
| P1 (derived) | Understand and use the derived quantities and units: velocity, acceleration, force, weight, moment |

Appendix B of the specification lists formulae you must use **without them being provided**. For mechanics it includes weight = mass × g and Newton's second law in the form F = ma. Both appear below, because they are what give force and weight their units.

## Notation the specification uses

Appendix A of the specification sets out the mechanics notation. Use it in your answers.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| kg | kilogram |
| m | metre |
| km | kilometre |
| m/s or m s⁻¹ | metres per second (velocity) |
| m/s² or m s⁻² | metres per second per second (acceleration) |
| N | newton |
| N m | newton metre (moment of a force) |
| F | force or resultant force |
| t, s, u, v, a | time, displacement, initial velocity, (final) velocity, acceleration |
| g | acceleration due to gravity |

Watch the two uses of "s" and "m". The unit s means seconds, while the variable s means displacement. The unit m means metres, while the variable m usually means mass. Write units after numbers and variables in your working so the two never clash.

## P1: fundamental quantities and units

The SI system builds every unit in this course from three **fundamental** (base) quantities:

| Quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| length | metre | m |
| time | second | s |
| mass | kilogram | kg |

The kilogram, not the gram, is the SI unit of mass. A question may give you grams, centimetres, minutes, hours or kilometres. Convert to kg, m and s **before** you substitute into any formula.

Useful conversions:

- 1 km = 1000 m; 1 m = 100 cm
- 1 kg = 1000 g
- 1 minute = 60 s; 1 hour = 3600 s
- 1 km h⁻¹ = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 1/3.6 m s⁻¹, so divide by 3.6 to go from km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹, and multiply by 3.6 to go back

**Worked example 1.** Convert each quantity to SI units: (a) 3.2 km, (b) 450 g, (c) 1 hour 20 minutes, (d) 90 km h⁻¹, (e) 360 cm s⁻².

```
(a) 3.2 km = 3.2 × 1000 = 3200 m
(b) 450 g = 450 ÷ 1000 = 0.45 kg
(c) 1 h 20 min = 80 min = 80 × 60 = 4800 s
(d) 90 km/h = 90 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 25 m s⁻¹
(e) 360 cm s⁻² = 360 ÷ 100 = 3.6 m s⁻²
```

In (e) only the length unit changes. The "per second per second" part is already SI.

To check a speed conversion, compare sizes. A metre is much shorter than a kilometre, but a second is much shorter than an hour, and the second effect wins: the same speed is always a **smaller** number in m s⁻¹ than in km h⁻¹. If you had multiplied by 3.6 you would get 324 m s⁻¹, bigger than 90, so you would know to stop and check.

## P1: derived quantities and units

A **derived** quantity is built from the fundamental ones. Its unit follows from its definition.

| Quantity | Definition | SI unit | In base units |
|---|---|---|---|
| velocity | rate of change of displacement | m s⁻¹ | m s⁻¹ |
| acceleration | rate of change of velocity | m s⁻² | m s⁻² |
| force | mass × acceleration (F = ma) | newton, N | kg m s⁻² |
| weight | the force of gravity on a mass (W = mg) | newton, N | kg m s⁻² |
| moment | force × perpendicular distance from the point | newton metre, N m | kg m² s⁻² |

### Velocity and speed

Velocity is displacement per unit time, so its unit is metres per second. Speed has the same unit. Velocity has a direction and speed does not, but that difference does not change the unit.

### Acceleration

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time: (m s⁻¹) ÷ s = m s⁻². Read it as "metres per second, every second". An acceleration of 2 m s⁻² means the velocity goes up by 2 m s⁻¹ each second.

### Force and the newton

Newton's second law, F = ma, defines the unit of force. **One newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻².** So

1 N = 1 kg × 1 m s⁻² = 1 kg m s⁻².

Large forces are often given in kilonewtons: 1 kN = 1000 N.

**Worked example 2.** A car of mass 1.2 tonnes (1 tonne = 1000 kg) speeds up from rest to 54 km h⁻¹ in 10 s with constant acceleration. Find the resultant force on the car.

```
mass:      1.2 tonnes = 1200 kg
velocity:  54 km/h = 54 ÷ 3.6 = 15 m s⁻¹
acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time
             = (15 − 0) ÷ 10 = 1.5 m s⁻²
F = ma = 1200 × 1.5 = 1800 N
```

Every quantity was converted before substitution. Using 1.2 for the mass, or 54 for the velocity, gives a force that is wrong by a factor of 1000 or 3.6.

### Weight

**Weight is a force**, so it is measured in newtons. **Mass is the amount of matter** in a body and is measured in kilograms. They are linked by

W = mg,

where g is the acceleration due to gravity. The specification says g may be taken as constant and its value is used to varying degrees of accuracy. It also says g is not a universal constant: it depends on location. A body's mass stays the same wherever it is, while its weight changes if g changes.

Use the value of g the question tells you. If it says "take g = 9.8 m s⁻²", use exactly 9.8.

**Worked example 3.** A parcel has mass 2.5 kg. Find its weight using (a) g = 9.8 m s⁻², (b) g = 9.78 m s⁻². Comment on the mass in each case.

```
(a) W = mg = 2.5 × 9.8  = 24.5 N
(b) W = mg = 2.5 × 9.78 = 24.45 N, or 24.5 N to 3 s.f.
```

The mass is 2.5 kg in both cases. Only the weight depends on g.

Never write a mass in newtons or a weight in kilograms. "The box weighs 3 kg" is everyday language, but in mechanics the box has **mass** 3 kg and **weight** 3g N.

### Moment

The **moment** of a force about a point measures its turning effect. Its size is

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.

Force is in newtons and distance in metres, so the unit is the **newton metre, N m**. The calculations with moments (balancing, equilibrium of rigid bodies) belong to Section S; here you need the quantity and its unit.

**Worked example 4.** A force of 30 N acts at right angles to a spanner, 40 cm from the centre of a nut. (a) Find the moment of the force about the centre of the nut. (b) Find the force needed at 60 cm from the centre to give the same moment.

```
(a) distance = 40 cm = 0.4 m
    moment = 30 × 0.4 = 12 N m
(b) F × 0.6 = 12
    F = 12 ÷ 0.6 = 20 N
```

Had you used 40 instead of 0.4, the answer would be 1200, which is the moment in N cm, not in N m.

## Using units to check your work

Every term in a correct equation has the same units. This gives you a fast check on a formula you have written down, and a way to find the units of a constant. Treat it as a checking habit; it is not a separate method you will be asked to name.

**Worked example 5.** A model says the air resistance on a cyclist is R = kv², where R is in newtons and v is the speed in m s⁻¹.

(a) Find the SI units of k.
(b) R = 72 N when v = 24 m s⁻¹. Find k.
(c) Use the model to find R when the speed is 90 km h⁻¹.

```
(a) k = R ÷ v²
    units: N ÷ (m s⁻¹)² = kg m s⁻² ÷ (m² s⁻²) = kg m⁻¹
(b) k = 72 ÷ 24² = 72 ÷ 576 = 0.125 kg m⁻¹
(c) 90 km/h = 25 m s⁻¹
    R = 0.125 × 25² = 78.125 = 78.1 N (3 s.f.)
```

In (c), substituting v = 90 gives R = 1012.5 N, more than ten times too large. The model only works with v in the units k was found in.

The same check catches a misremembered formula. In v² = u² + 2as, each term has units (m s⁻¹)² = m² s⁻², and 2as has units m s⁻² × m = m² s⁻². The units match. If you wrote v = u + as, the left side is in m s⁻¹ but as is in m² s⁻², so the formula cannot be right.

## Accuracy

Your answers can only be as precise as your data. If g is given to two significant figures (9.8), an answer such as 24.45 N says more than the data justify. Give answers to a sensible degree of accuracy, follow any instruction in the question, and keep full calculator values in your working until the last line so rounding errors do not build up.

## Common errors

- Substituting grams, centimetres, minutes or km h⁻¹ straight into F = ma, W = mg or a moment calculation.
- Multiplying by 3.6 when converting km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹. You divide, because m s⁻¹ values are smaller.
- Giving a weight in kg, or a mass in N.
- Writing the unit of acceleration as m s⁻¹, or the unit of moment as N or N m⁻¹.
- Treating g as a fixed universal constant, or using 9.81 when the question says 9.8.
- Rounding an intermediate value (such as a converted speed) and carrying the rounded value forward.
- Missing units on a final answer, or giving N cm when the question works in metres.
- Mixing up the variable m (mass) with the unit m (metre), or the variable s (displacement) with the unit s (second).
