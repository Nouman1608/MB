**PESTLE** sorts external factors into Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental, feeding the **opportunities and threats** of a SWOT analysis (see [Mission, objective and SWOT analysis](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-mission-objective-and-swot-analysis/)).

One change can be an opportunity for one firm and a threat for another: an ageing population gives Ivybrook Care, a care-home group, more customers, but shrinks the pool of young drivers Rushbank Couriers recruits from.

**Value of PESTLE**

- A structured scan of the outside world, so fewer changes are missed.
- Early warning, so the firm can adjust before rivals do.
- A sensible first step before entering a new country, where the factors may be very different.

**Limitations:** it is a snapshot that fast-moving factors soon date; it does not rank factors by likelihood or impact; categories overlap (an emissions law is legal and environmental); forecasting change is uncertain. Its value depends on how often it is updated and whether managers act on it.

## 2. The political and legal environment

You need to understand how law **within a country** affects business. Specific laws are not required.

| Area of law | Typical effect on the business | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Competition | Limits price-fixing, abuse of dominance, some mergers | Grow organically; compete on product |
| Consumer protection | Product safety, honest labelling and advertising, refunds | Tighter quality control; checked marketing |
| Labour market and employee rights | Minimum pay, hours, fair dismissal, non-discrimination | Raise productivity; review HR policies |
| Environmental issues | Limits on emissions, waste, land use | Cleaner processes; green positioning |
| Health and safety | Risk assessment, training, protective equipment | Training; safer workplace design |

**Government policy.** You must be able to analyse policy on:

- **Supporting businesses**: grants and subsidies (for research or energy-saving equipment) lower costs but come with conditions and can be withdrawn.
- **The ease of doing business**: how quickly a firm can register, get permits, pay tax and enforce contracts. It shapes location decisions.
- **International trade agreements**: lower tariffs and shared standards open export markets, but also let foreign rivals in.
- **Protectionism**: **tariffs** (taxes on imports), **quotas** (limits on the quantity imported) and **trade regulations** (standards, licences, paperwork) protect domestic producers but raise costs for importers of inputs and invite retaliation against exporters.
- **Infrastructure**: investment in roads, ports, power and internet affects delivery times, reliability and costs.

Specific government spending and taxation policies are not required.

**Worked example: Coldharbour Paints.** Coldharbour exports a third of its paint. A stricter solvent-emissions limit at home arrives just as its largest export market imposes a 20% tariff on imported paint. *Operations* must invest in water-based formulas; *finance* faces capital spending as export margins fall; *marketing* must raise the export price or absorb the tariff, and can promote the low-solvent range; *HR* retrains staff. The *strategic* response could be to switch export effort to countries covered by a trade agreement, or to open a small mixing plant inside the tariff wall.

## 3. The economic environment

### GDP, growth and recession

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the value of output produced in an economy. Growth usually lifts incomes and demand; a **recession** is a period of falling GDP, which hits income-elastic products hardest. Responses: cheaper ranges, cost cuts, protecting cash, delaying expansion. A recession in a major export market hurts exporters even when the home economy is growing.

### Taxation rates

- **Income tax** changes disposable income (so demand) and employees' take-home pay.
- **Sales taxes** are added to the price; a firm passes a rise on or absorbs it.
- **Corporation tax** is a tax on company profit. A rise from 25% to 30% on a profit before tax of £640,000 cuts profit after tax from £480,000 to £448,000, leaving £32,000 less for dividends or reinvestment.

**Worked example: Thistlecombe Bakehouse.** A loaf sells for £8.00 before sales tax. The rate rises from 10% to 12.5%.

```
Price with tax before = 8.00 × 1.10  = £8.80
Price with tax after  = 8.00 × 1.125 = £9.00
If the shop keeps the shelf price at £8.80:
Revenue kept per loaf = 8.80 ÷ 1.125 = £7.82 (to 2 d.p.)
Loss per loaf         = 8.00 − 7.82  = £0.18, about 2.2% of revenue
```

Passing the rise on risks lost sales if demand is price elastic; absorbing it squeezes margin.

### Exchange rates

A currency **appreciates** when it buys more foreign currency and **depreciates** when it buys less. **Volatility** means frequent, unpredictable movement, which makes costs and revenues hard to plan.

Rates in these examples are illustrative.

**Worked example 1, import costs: Gillamoor Bikes.** Gillamoor buys motors from a Japanese supplier at ¥36,000 each. The rate moves from £1 = ¥200 to £1 = ¥180, so the pound has depreciated.

```
Cost per motor before = 36,000 ÷ 200 = £180
Cost per motor after  = 36,000 ÷ 180 = £200
Increase              = 20 ÷ 180 × 100 = 11.1%
Order of 4,000 motors: £720,000 → £800,000, an extra £80,000
```

**Worked example 2, selling prices abroad: Corrowan Spices.** Corrowan sells a jar for £12 and exports to the eurozone. The rate moves from £1 = €1.15 to £1 = €1.25, so the pound has appreciated.

```
Euro price before = 12 × 1.15 = €13.80
Euro price after  = 12 × 1.25 = €15.00   (an 8.7% rise for buyers)
If Corrowan holds the euro price at €13.80:
Revenue in pounds = 13.80 ÷ 1.25 = £11.04, 8% less per jar
```

Responses: fix a rate in advance with the bank, invoice in the home currency, match the currency of inputs to the currency of sales, or switch suppliers. Whether to change price depends on price elasticity and rivals' prices.

### Inflation and deflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level; **deflation** is a sustained fall. Both are measured with a **price index**, where a base year is set at 100.

**Worked example: Quenby Laundry.** A national price index reads 106.0 in one year and 110.24 the next.

```
Change in points = 110.24 − 106.0 = 4.24
Inflation rate   = 4.24 ÷ 106.0 × 100 = 4%
```

Divide by the earlier index, not by 100. Quenby raised its prices by 3%, so in real terms they fell by about 1% (1.03 ÷ 1.04 = 0.990). An index falling from 100.0 to 98.5 shows 1.5% deflation. Inflation raises input costs and wage demands; deflation can make customers delay purchases and makes debts heavier in real terms.

### Unemployment rates

Rising unemployment cuts demand but eases recruitment and pay pressure. Low unemployment brings skills shortages, so firms raise pay, train their own staff or automate.

### Interest rates

**Worked example: Brindlecombe Haulage.** Brindlecombe has a variable-rate loan with £1,500,000 outstanding. Each year it repays £300,000 of the loan plus interest on the opening balance. The rate rises from 5% to 6.5%.

```
Interest at 5%   = 1,500,000 × 0.05  = £75,000  → total payment £375,000
Interest at 6.5% = 1,500,000 × 0.065 = £97,500  → total payment £397,500
Extra cost this year = £22,500, a 6% rise in the annual payment
```

Higher rates also cut credit-financed demand. Responses: fixed-rate borrowing, delayed projects, or growth funded by retained profit.

## 4. The social environment

- **Age structure**: an older population shifts demand towards health, leisure and care, and shrinks the young workforce.
- **Migration**: inward migration enlarges the labour pool and creates new markets; outward migration can drain skills.
- **Size and composition of the workforce**: more part-time or older workers changes recruitment and shift patterns.
- **Education and training**: a well-educated labour market supports high-skill strategies; skills gaps raise training costs.
- **Cultural issues**: tastes, religion, language and attitudes differ between and within countries, so products and promotion may need adapting.

Responses: redesign products for new customer groups, offer flexible working, train in-house.

### Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

**CSR** means a business taking responsibility for its effect on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.

- **Reasons for**: reputation and customer loyalty, easier recruitment and retention, investor support, lower long-term risk, owners' values.
- **Reasons against**: cost cuts short-term profit, benefits are hard to measure, and a gap between claims and conduct damages trust.
- **Pressures** come from customers, pressure groups, media and social media, employees, investors and government. Its **value** is greatest when it fits the brand and customers reward it.

The **shareholder concept** says a business exists mainly to maximise owners' returns; the **stakeholder concept** says it should balance the interests of all groups it affects, which makes CSR more likely.

## 5. Technological change

- **Digital technology** (online sales, data analysis, cloud systems, automation) cuts costs and opens channels, but needs investment, skills and data security.
- **Disruptive technology** is a new product or business model that displaces established ones, often starting as a cheaper, simpler offer; firms that ignore it can lose their market.
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** can answer customer queries, forecast demand, check quality and draft content: lower unit costs and faster decisions, but fewer roles in some jobs, retraining needs, and risks over errors, bias and data use.

Responses: invest early, retrain staff, partner with technology firms, or reposition towards what technology cannot easily copy.

## 6. Environmental change: climate change

Climate change brings **physical** risks (floods, heat, disrupted supply chains, higher insurance), **pressure to cut emissions** from law, customers and investors, and opportunities for low-carbon products. Responses: cut energy use, change inputs, diversify suppliers, protect sites and report progress honestly.

## Common errors

- Listing PESTLE factors without saying whether each is an opportunity or a threat **for this business**.
- Multiplying when you should divide by the exchange rate, or saying an appreciation helps exporters.
- Dividing an index change by 100 instead of by the earlier index value.
- Charging interest on the original loan instead of the outstanding balance given.
- Describing a change without tracing it to a functional area and a response.
- Quoting specific laws, which the specification says are not required.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is PESTLE analysis used for?**
PESTLE sorts external factors into political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental groups, giving a structured scan of the outside world and an early warning of change. Its limitation is that it is a snapshot that fast-moving factors soon date, and it does not rank factors by likelihood or impact.

**What is the difference between the shareholder and stakeholder concepts?**
The shareholder concept says a business exists mainly to maximise owners' returns. The stakeholder concept says it should balance the interests of all the groups it affects, which makes corporate social responsibility more likely.

**Do you need to know specific laws for OxfordAQA A-level Business 9725?**
No. The specification requires you to understand how law within a country affects business, such as competition and consumer protection law, but specific laws are not required. Specific government spending and taxation policies are not required either.

**How can a business respond to exchange rate changes?**
Responses include fixing a rate in advance with the bank, invoicing in the home currency, matching the currency of inputs to the currency of sales, or switching suppliers. Whether to change price depends on price elasticity and rivals' prices.

**What risks does climate change bring to businesses?**
Climate change brings physical risks, such as floods, heat, disrupted supply chains and higher insurance costs, and pressure to cut emissions from law, customers and investors. It also creates opportunities for low-carbon products.
