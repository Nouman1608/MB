Section 14.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026 is a case study: "candidates must study one country's regional development policy, its regional disparities, some of the difficulties faced in trying to overcome these disparities and evaluate the attempted solutions." It applies the 14.3 theory of core-periphery and cumulative causation to a real country.

The [Topic 14 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-economic-transition/) uses China's Western Development strategy. This page gives you the tools to analyse any policy, a full second case study (Italy's policy for the Mezzogiorno, its southern regions) and an evaluation framework you can reuse. Recall the essentials with the [14.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [14.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-regional-development-practice/). The theory behind it is in the [14.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-regional-development-within-countries/).

## The four demands of the case study

| Syllabus phrase | What to prepare |
|---|---|
| "one country's regional development policy" | Named policies, agencies, dates and measures |
| "its regional disparities" | The core and periphery, with indicators and figures |
| "some of the difficulties faced" | Physical, economic, political and social obstacles |
| "evaluate the attempted solutions" | Judgement on success, using clear criteria and evidence |

## The policy toolkit

Regional policies combine a few standard instruments. Knowing the type of each lets you link it to theory.

| Tool | Aim in 14.3 terms | Example of the idea |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure (roads, railways, power, water) | Reduce the periphery's access disadvantage; encourage spread | A new rail link from the core to an interior region |
| Financial incentives (grants, tax breaks, cheap loans) | Offset higher costs of peripheral locations | Grants per job created in assisted areas |
| Growth poles | Concentrate investment in one place to start cumulative causation in the periphery | A large state-owned plant in a peripheral city |
| Special economic zones or free trade zones | Attract investors with tax and customs advantages | A free trade zone in a remote city |
| Relocating government or a new capital | Move jobs and spending out of the core | Moving ministries or building a new capital inland |
| Controls on the core | Limit further concentration in the core | Permits required for new factories in the core |
| Human capital and services | Raise skills and wellbeing in the periphery | Universities and hospitals in peripheral regions |

**Growth pole theory.** The idea, developed by the French economist François Perroux in the 1950s, is that investment in a leading industry at one point creates linkages and multiplier effects that spread outwards. In regional policy it became a way of trying to create a new core in the periphery.

## Worked case study: Italy and the Mezzogiorno

### Regional disparities

Italy has one of Europe's sharpest internal divides. The North-west (the industrial triangle of Milan, Turin and Genoa) and much of the North-east are the core. The Mezzogiorno, the regions of the South and the islands of Sicily and Sardinia, is the periphery, with lower GDP per capita, higher unemployment and lower employment of women and young people. In the 1950s and 1960s millions of southerners migrated to the industrial cities of the North and abroad: a textbook backwash effect.

### Policy

- **1950: Cassa per il Mezzogiorno** (Fund for the South) set up to finance land reform, land reclamation, irrigation, roads, water supply and other infrastructure.
- **Late 1950s onwards: industrialisation through growth poles.** State-controlled companies were required to place a large share of their investment in the South. Large, capital-intensive plants were built, including a state-owned steelworks at Taranto in the 1960s and petrochemical complexes in Sicily and Sardinia.
- **Incentives** for private firms locating in the South: grants, cheap credit and tax relief.
- **1984:** the Cassa was wound up after criticism of waste and political interference; its successor agency closed in the early 1990s.
- **Since then:** the South has been the main Italian recipient of European Union regional (cohesion) funds, used for infrastructure, business support and training.

### Difficulties

- **Economic.** The big plants were capital-intensive and created relatively few jobs. They bought many inputs from the North and sold outputs to national markets, so local linkages were weak. Critics called them "cathedrals in the desert".
- **Political.** Spending was vulnerable to patronage and, in some areas, to organised crime involvement in public contracts.
- **Physical and locational.** Mountainous interiors, distance from the main European markets, and water shortages in the summer-dry climate.
- **Social.** Continued out-migration of young and educated people reduced the skilled workforce and future entrepreneurs.
- **Environmental.** Heavy industry brought pollution. In 2012 a court ordered parts of the Taranto steelworks to be seized over pollution and health concerns, showing the long-term costs of a growth-pole strategy.

### Evaluating the attempted solutions

Use explicit criteria:

| Criterion | Evidence for success | Evidence against | Judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effectiveness: did disparities narrow? | Infrastructure, irrigation and living standards improved greatly from the 1950s | The South's GDP per capita remains far below the North's | Absolute living standards rose; the relative gap has persisted |
| Efficiency: value for money | Some plants became major employers | High cost per job; waste and corruption | Weak |
| Equity: who benefited? | Workers in the new plants, farmers given land | Many jobs and profits went to northern firms and skilled incomers | Mixed |
| Sustainability: did growth continue without subsidy? | Some clusters survived | Several growth poles declined when heavy industry contracted; pollution legacies | Weak |

**Overall judgement.** Italy's policy improved infrastructure and living standards in the South but did not create self-sustaining cumulative causation. Growth poles based on heavy industry had too few local linkages, so spread effects were small while backwash through migration continued. Comparison with China's Western Development strategy suggests a common lesson: infrastructure alone can increase flows towards the core as easily as away from it, and success depends on building local firms and skills.

## An evaluation framework for any country

1. **State the aim** of the policy: closing gaps, raising peripheral living standards, or national integration.
2. **Choose measures**: relative (index or ratio) and absolute (gap), economic and social.
3. **Judge by time scale**: short-term results versus long-term change.
4. **Judge by group and place**: which parts of the periphery and which people benefited.
5. **Identify unintended effects**: environmental damage, displacement, dependency on subsidy.
6. **Explain the outcome with theory**: were spread effects created, or did backwash continue?
7. **Conclude**: a clear statement of how far the policy succeeded, and why.

## Annotated essay plan

*"With reference to one country you have studied, evaluate the success of policies to reduce regional disparities." [20]*

1. **Introduction:** locate Italy's core and periphery; state the line of argument (improved living standards, little relative convergence).
2. **Disparities:** indicators and the history of backwash migration.
3. **Infrastructure phase:** what was built; judgement of moderate success.
4. **Growth pole phase:** Taranto and petrochemicals; few linkages; judgement of limited success.
5. **Difficulties:** political, physical, social and environmental, each linked to an outcome.
6. **Criteria table in prose:** effectiveness, efficiency, equity, sustainability.
7. **Conclusion:** success depends on the measure and time scale; theory explains why spread effects stayed weak.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the policy without figures for the disparities it targeted.
- Listing difficulties without saying how each limited success.
- Judging success by money spent rather than by outcomes.
- Ignoring unintended effects such as pollution or dependency.
- Writing about regional policy in general without one named country.
- A conclusion that does not answer "how successful".
