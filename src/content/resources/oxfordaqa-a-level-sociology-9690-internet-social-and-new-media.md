## The internet: fast global access to information

Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web in 1989 while working at CERN, and it opened to the public in 1991. Manuel Castells (*The Rise of the Network Society*, 1996) argued that information networks now form the basic structure of society: economic, political and cultural power depends on position in global networks. In *Communication Power* (2009) he added **mass self-communication**: individuals can now reach mass audiences themselves.

Fast access has two sides:
- **Opportunity**: education, health information and news available to anyone connected; easier organising across borders.
- **New gatekeepers**: search engines and recommendation systems now decide what is visible, replacing newspaper editors as the main filters for many people. Information abundance also means attention, not information, is scarce.

## Changes in communication

- **Networked publics**: danah boyd (*It's Complicated*, 2014), studying American teenagers, identified four features of online communication: **persistence** (posts last), **visibility** (large potential audiences), **spreadability** (content is easily shared) and **searchability** (content can be found later).
- **Context collapse**: Alice Marwick and danah boyd (2011) described how social media merge audiences (family, friends, teachers, employers) who would be separated offline, so users must manage one self for many audiences.
- **Connection or isolation?** Sherry Turkle (*Alone Together*, 2011) argued that constant connection can replace deep conversation with shallow contact. Others reply that online communication sustains relationships across distance, especially for diasporas and for young people from minority groups who find support online.

## Social media and mental health: evaluating the evidence

The unit guide contrasts Haidt with Orben and Przybylski. Add:

- **Jean Twenge** (*iGen*, 2017) linked rises in reported depression and loneliness among US teenagers in the early 2010s to the spread of smartphones.
- **Mechanisms suggested**: social comparison with idealised images; cyberbullying that follows young people home; disrupted sleep; displacement of in-person activity.
- **Methodological issues**: most studies are correlational; many rely on self-reported screen time, which is often inaccurate; causation may run the other way (unhappy young people may use social media more); "screen time" lumps together very different activities, from schoolwork to messaging friends.

A strong answer concludes that effects probably differ by user, platform and type of use, and that the evidence for large, universal harm is contested.

## Ownership and control of new and social media

- **Platform capitalism**: Nick Srnicek (*Platform Capitalism*, 2016) argued that platforms profit by positioning themselves between users, advertisers and producers, and that **network effects** (a platform becomes more valuable as more people use it) push markets towards a few giants.
- **Surveillance capitalism**: Shoshana Zuboff (*The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019) argued that platforms turn users' behaviour into data used to predict and influence what they will do, which is sold to advertisers.
- **The culture of connectivity**: José van Dijck (2013) showed how platforms' technical design and business models shape what "sharing" and "friendship" mean online.
- **A case**: in 2018 it emerged that the political consultancy Cambridge Analytica had obtained personal data from millions of Facebook users without their consent, raising questions about who controls user data.

**Marxist and pluralist readings.** Marxists see platform owners as a new media elite with greater reach than newspaper owners. Pluralists point out that anyone can publish, and that users have abandoned once-dominant platforms before.

## Misinformation, disinformation and conspiracy theories

- **Filter bubbles**: Eli Pariser (*The Filter Bubble*, 2011) argued that personalised algorithms show users more of what they already like, narrowing their view. **Echo chambers** (Cass Sunstein, *Republic.com*, 2001) describe groups that only hear like-minded voices.
- **Speed of falsehood**: a large study by Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral (2018) of stories shared on Twitter found that false news spread farther and faster than true news, and that people, not automated bots, were mainly responsible.
- **Infodemic**: the World Health Organization used this term during the COVID-19 pandemic for an overabundance of information, including false information, that makes it hard to find reliable guidance.
- **Conspiracy theories**: QAnon, which emerged online in 2017, shows how anonymous posts can grow into a movement. Uscinski and Parent's finding that conspiracy talk did not rise over a century cautions against assuming the internet created conspiracism.

**Evaluation.** Axel Bruns (*Are Filter Bubbles Real?*, 2019) argued that evidence for strong filter bubbles is weak: most users encounter a range of sources, and polarisation has deeper political causes. Algorithms amplify, but people choose what to share.

## Audience involvement: creators, citizen journalists, influencers

- **Prosumers and produsers**: Alvin Toffler (*The Third Wave*, 1980) predicted the "prosumer", who both produces and consumes. Axel Bruns (2008) used **produsage** for collaborative creation such as wikis.
- **Citizen journalism**: during the uprisings in Tunisia and Egypt in 2010 and 2011, protesters shared videos and updates that international broadcasters then used. In 2020 a teenager's phone video of the killing of George Floyd in Minneapolis was central to public awareness; Darnella Frazier received a special Pulitzer citation in 2021.
- **Influencers and microcelebrity**: Theresa Senft (*Camgirls*, 2008) coined **microcelebrity** for ordinary people building audiences online. Crystal Abidin (*Internet Celebrity*, 2018) analysed influencers as a commercial industry built on the appearance of intimacy and authenticity.

**Evaluation.**
- Evgeny Morozov (*The Net Delusion*, 2011) criticised **cyber-utopianism**, the belief that the internet naturally empowers citizens; authoritarian states also use it for surveillance and propaganda.
- Malcolm Gladwell ("Small Change", 2010) argued that online activism relies on weak ties and often amounts to **slacktivism**: low-cost support, such as liking a post, that brings little change.
- Platforms profit from user content, and only a small share of creators earn a living, so "audience power" is unevenly spread.

## Annotated model answer

*Extract from a 12-mark answer: Evaluate the view that social media spread misinformation more than 'traditional' media.*

> Vosoughi, Roy and Aral found that false news spread farther and faster than true news on Twitter **[knowledge]**. During the pandemic, false health claims circulated widely on messaging apps in my society **[application]**. Algorithms reward engagement, and Pariser argues personalisation narrows what users see **[analysis]**. However, Bruns finds filter bubbles weaker than claimed, and traditional media have spread propaganda too, so the difference is speed and scale rather than a new problem **[evaluation and judgement]**.

## Common mistakes

- Stating that social media cause mental illness as fact.
- Treating filter bubbles as proven.
- Calling every creator an influencer or citizen journalist.
- Forgetting that platforms profit from user-generated content.
- Writing about "the internet" as one thing; distinguish platforms, uses and users.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions, give a feature of social media and its consequence (spreadability means false stories travel before corrections). For **evaluate** answers, contrast optimistic (participatory culture, citizen journalism) and critical (platform capitalism, Morozov) views, and use own-society examples of platforms, campaigns or influencers where asked.
