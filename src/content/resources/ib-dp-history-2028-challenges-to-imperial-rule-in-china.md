This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. Challenges to imperial rule in China (1736 to 1911) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It traces how the Qing dynasty, at its height under the Qianlong Emperor, faced rebellion, foreign pressure and failed reform, and how it fell in 1911.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china-practice/).

## Challenges to the Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735 to 1796)

**Society and ideas.** The Qing were Manchu rulers of a mainly Han Chinese population. They governed through a Confucian order: a scholar-official class chosen by examination, respect for hierarchy, and an emperor who held the "Mandate of Heaven". China saw foreign states as tributaries who showed respect through ceremonies such as the kowtow. This view made equal diplomacy hard.

**Political structure.** Qianlong ruled through the Grand Council, a small group of trusted advisers, and the six boards, with Manchu and Han officials paired in office. The Eight Banners provided a Manchu military core. The emperor's personal control was high, but late in his reign his favourite Heshen used office for corruption, and the army lost its edge.

**Growth of the empire.** Qianlong's "Ten Great Campaigns" added Xinjiang after the defeat of the Dzungars in the 1750s and secured Tibet. The empire became larger than any earlier Chinese state. Population rose strongly, reaching around 300 million by 1800, which put pressure on land and food in many provinces.

**Rebellions.** The White Lotus Rebellion (1794 to 1804) began in the hill regions of central China, among poor settlers and followers of a Buddhist-inspired sect, and was put down at great cost. The Miao Rebellion (1795 to 1806) in the south-west arose from land taken by Han settlers. Both showed that tax pressure, corruption and population growth were weakening the state.

## Efforts to "open up" China

**The Canton system.** From 1757 European trade was restricted to Canton, where it had to pass through a licensed guild of Chinese merchants (the Co-hong), and foreigners lived under tight rules. Britain bought large amounts of tea and silk and had little to sell that China wanted.

**British missions.** Lord Macartney (1793) asked for more ports, a resident envoy and trade changes. Qianlong refused, saying China needed nothing from outside. William Pitt Amherst (1816) was sent home after a dispute over court ceremony. Lord Napier (1834), the first British trade superintendent, tried to deal directly with the governor at Canton, was rebuffed and died soon after. The failures led British traders and officials to think that only force would change the system.

**The opium trade.** The East India Company grew opium in Bengal and licensed private "country traders" to sell it to China, paying for tea. Imports rose sharply in the 1820s and 1830s. Silver flowed out of China, and addiction spread among officials, soldiers and townspeople. The social cost included weakened government revenue, crime and corruption.

## Reasons for, and impact of, the Opium Wars

**Chinese prohibition.** Emperors had banned the opium trade (1729 and later), but the ban was poorly enforced. In 1839 Commissioner **Lin Zexu** was sent to Canton. He wrote to Queen Victoria, made foreign merchants surrender more than 20,000 chests of opium, and destroyed them.

**First Opium War (1839 to 1842).** British merchants and officials demanded compensation and trade rights, and Parliament sent a naval expedition. Modern ships and artillery defeated Qing forces. The **Treaty of Nanjing (1842)** ceded Hong Kong, opened five treaty ports (Canton, Amoy, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai), ended the Co-hong monopoly, and required a large indemnity. Later agreements added extraterritoriality (British subjects tried under British law) and most-favoured-nation status.

**Second Opium War (1856 to 1860).** It began after the seizure of the ship *Arrow* and the killing of a French missionary. Britain and France captured Canton and Tianjin. The **Treaty of Tianjin (1858)** opened more ports, allowed foreign envoys in Beijing and missionaries across the country, and legalised the opium trade under a tariff. When China delayed ratification, Anglo-French forces entered Beijing and burned the Summer Palace. The **Convention of Beijing (1860)** ceded Kowloon, and increased indemnities and the number of treaty ports.

**Impact.** Politically, China lost control of its tariffs and law in treaty ports. Economically, foreign goods and shipping entered, and treaty-port cities such as Shanghai grew. Culturally, missionaries spread Christianity and Western learning, and anger towards foreigners rose. The wars also exposed Qing military weakness and encouraged rebellion. Take care with the phrase "century of humiliation", which is a later nationalist idea and should be treated as a perspective.

## The Taiping Rebellion (1850 to 1864)

**Causes.** In Guangxi, population pressure, floods and famine, official corruption and hostility between local people and Hakka migrants created unrest. Defeat in the Opium War had damaged Qing prestige and led to unemployment among transport workers in the south.

**Hong Xiuquan.** A man who failed the civil service examinations several times, he read Christian tracts and came to believe he was the younger brother of Jesus. He founded the God Worshipping Society. The rebels proclaimed the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace, and captured Nanjing in 1853, making it their capital.

**Policies and social impact.** The Taipings banned opium, foot-binding, gambling and the sale of women, and promised equal land. Their rule was strict and, in practice, many leaders lived in luxury. Their religion offended Confucian scholars. A violent leadership quarrel in 1856 weakened them. The war killed millions, with common estimates of at least 20 million deaths, many from famine and disease.

**Defeat and regional leaders.** The dynasty's own armies were poor. **Zeng Guofan** raised the Xiang Army from local scholars and peasants, and **Li Hongzhang** the Huai Army, with help from a foreign-led force later called the Ever Victorious Army. Nanjing fell in July 1864. The result was important: power passed to regional leaders with their own armies and funds, which would matter in later decades.

## Failure of the Qing to reform

**Leadership.** In 1861 Empress Dowager **Cixi**, working with Prince Gong (**Yixin**), took control for her young son. Prince Gong ran the new foreign affairs office, the Zongli Yamen. Cixi became the dominant political figure for nearly fifty years.

**Self-Strengthening Movement (about 1861 to 1895).** Officials such as Zeng and Li founded arsenals, shipyards, mines, telegraphs and a steamship company, and sent students abroad. The slogan was "Chinese learning for the essence, Western learning for practical use". But the effort was funded by provinces without a national plan, courtiers resisted change, and it did not alter institutions.

**First Sino-Japanese War (1894 to 1895).** Japan defeated China over influence in Korea. The Treaty of Shimonoseki gave Japan Taiwan, the Pescadores and an indemnity, and recognised Korean independence. The shock of defeat by a country long seen as a pupil of China was profound.

**The Hundred Days' Reform (1898).** The Guangxu Emperor, advised by Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, issued edicts on education, the army and government. Cixi and conservative officials ended it with a coup in September 1898 and confined the emperor.

## Decline and fall of the Qing

**Boxer Rebellion (1899 to 1901).** The Boxers, a movement in northern China that blamed foreigners and missionaries for drought and hardship, attacked missions and then besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900. The court backed them. An eight-nation force relieved the legations. The **Boxer Protocol (1901)** imposed a huge indemnity (450 million taels), allowed foreign troops to protect the legations, and punished officials.

**Late Qing reforms.** After the Boxers, the court began the "New Policies": the civil service examinations ended in 1905, new schools and armies were formed, and a plan for a constitution and assemblies was announced in 1908. These changes aimed to save the dynasty but also created new groups, such as the modern army and provincial assemblies, who wanted power.

**Sun Yixian and the Three Principles.** Sun (Sun Yat-sen) set up revolutionary groups, and in 1905 helped found the Tongmenghui in Tokyo. His **Three Principles of the People** were nationalism (ending Manchu rule and foreign domination), democracy and people's livelihood.

**Xinhai Revolution (1911).** A plan to nationalise railways angered investors in Sichuan. An army uprising at Wuchang on 10 October 1911 spread to province after province. The court called back **Yuan Shikai**, the commander of the modern northern army. Yuan negotiated, and the child emperor Puyi abdicated in February 1912, with Yuan becoming president. The empire ended, but the republic began weak.

## Perspectives and historiography

- John Fairbank's "impact-response" view stressed the West as the force that disturbed China. Later historians such as Paul Cohen urged a "China-centred" approach that looks at Chinese causes.
- "New Qing History" scholars (such as Evelyn Rawski and Mark Elliott) see the Qing as a Manchu, Inner Asian empire and not simply a Chinese dynasty.
- Nationalist histories stress humiliation and foreign guilt. Others argue that internal weakness was as important.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent did the Opium Wars cause the decline of the Qing dynasty?"**

1. Thesis: the wars exposed and accelerated weakness but did not create it.
2. Show the evidence of pre-existing weakness: rebellions of the 1790s, corruption and population pressure.
3. Show the impact of the wars: treaties, indemnities, loss of prestige and the example of weakness.
4. Add the Taiping, which was the greater internal threat, and failed reform.
5. Weigh the perspectives: impact-response against China-centred views.
6. Judgement that ranks the causes.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Qing as an ethnically Chinese dynasty and ignoring its Manchu identity.
- Giving the Opium Wars as the only cause.
- Missing the importance of the regional armies after the Taiping.
- Presenting Cixi as only a reactionary; the picture is more mixed.
