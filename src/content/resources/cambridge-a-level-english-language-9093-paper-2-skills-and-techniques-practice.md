## Section A

**1.** Name the structure that the syllabus gives as an example for each of: (a) imaginative writing, (b) discursive writing, (c) review writing. **[3]**

**2.** Identify the effect that each technique is designed to produce, as the syllabus gives them: (a) withholding key information, (b) juxtaposing counterarguments, (c) evidentiary logic. **[3]**

**3.** Correct the error in each sentence and name its type. **[4]**

- **(a)** "The park is beautiful, it has a lake and a playground."
- **(b)** "He opened the door and sees a figure on the stairs."
- **(c)** "The childrens' games were loud."
- **(d)** "Each of the players have a locker."

**4.** Rewrite this weak paragraph opening so that it has a clear topic sentence and a suitable connective: *"Phones. Many people have them. Some schools ban them."* **[3]**

**5.** Give **three** features appropriate for each of the following (one mark per two correct): (a) descriptive writing, (b) argumentative writing, (c) critical writing. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** *Write the opening paragraph (about 100 words) of a short story in which a character is waiting for news. Withhold the key information, and make the structure create the effect.* **[8]**

**7.** *Write one paragraph (about 120 words) for a discursive article on the question "Should students be allowed to use phones in lessons?" using a dialectical structure.* **[8]**

**8.** Plan a review (outline only) of a local café, using the structure introductory, summary and evaluative sections. Give the purpose of each section, an example of one evaluative phrase and the verdict. **[5]**

**9.** Here is a student's commentary on her text.

> *"I used short sentences. This makes the text fast. I also used lots of describing words, which makes it interesting for the reader."*

Identify **three** weaknesses and rewrite the commentary using a named choice, a short quotation, an effect and a link to audience. Her text was a leaflet for teenagers about a new skate park, and it contained the sentence "Ride in. Rise up. Repeat." **[6]**

**10.** A candidate has 2 hours for Paper 2. She spends 20 minutes planning and writing Question 1(a), 25 minutes on Question 1(b), 25 minutes on proofreading, and has 50 minutes left for Section B. Comment on the balance of her time. **[4]**

**11.** "Careful planning of structure matters more than choosing sophisticated vocabulary." Discuss in relation to Section B. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Freytag's Pyramid [1]; (b) dialectical structure [1]; (c) introductory, summary and evaluative sections [1]. **[3]**

**2.** (a) to build tension or surprise in imaginative writing [1]; (b) to set arguments against each other so the reader feels the contrast, leading to a resolution, in discursive writing [1]; (c) to build a judgement step by step from claims and evidence in critical writing [1]. **[3]**

**3.** (a) comma splice: "The park is beautiful, and it has a lake and a playground." [1] (b) tense shift: "sees" should be "saw" [1] (c) apostrophe placement: "children's" [1] (d) subject-verb agreement: "has" [1]. **[4]**

**4.** For example: *Mobile phones divide opinion in schools. Although most students own one, many schools ban them during lessons.* Credit a topic sentence that states the point [1], a connective such as "although" or "however" [1], and joined sentences with a clear relationship [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any three, one mark per two correct features, for example: descriptive: imagery, sensory detail, precise verbs; argumentative: rhetorical questions, rule of three, direct address; critical: evaluative adjectives, comparatives, hedging. **[3]**

**6.** Credit up to 8: a clear mood and setting in the opening [2]; the key information withheld and hinted at through behaviour or detail [2]; control of sentence length and rhythm to build suspense [1]; imagery or a precise vocabulary choice [1]; accurate spelling and punctuation, about 100 words [2]. Example: *The phone sat on the kitchen table, face down. Mara washed the same cup three times. Her brother stared at the clock as if it owed him money. When it buzzed, neither of them moved. Outside, a car passed, slowly, and the whole house seemed to hold its breath.* **[8]**

**7.** Credit up to 8: a topic sentence stating the first view [1]; development with a reason or example [1]; a counter-view introduced with a contrastive connective [1]; development of the counter-view [1]; a resolution or balanced judgement [2]; modality such as "could" or "may" [1]; accurate expression, about 120 words [1]. Example: *Supporters of phones in lessons argue that they are useful tools: a student can check a fact, translate a word or photograph the board in seconds. Yet the same device that answers questions also delivers messages, games and videos, and few teenagers can resist glancing at them. The strongest case is therefore not for open use, but for controlled use: phones out when the teacher asks for them, and away when they are not needed.* **[8]**

**8.** Introductory section: introduces the café, its location and the reviewer's expectations [1]. Summary section: describes what the café offers, such as the menu, atmosphere and service [1]. Evaluative section: judges strengths and weaknesses with reasons [1]. One evaluative phrase, for example "a warm but over-priced welcome" [1]. Verdict, for example: "recommended for a quiet breakfast, less so for a quick lunch" [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Weaknesses, any three [3]: "short sentences" and "describing words" are not exemplified with a quotation [1]; the effects ("fast", "interesting") are vague [1]; there is no link to the audience or purpose [1]; "lots of describing words" does not name a feature [1]. Rewrite, up to 3 [3]: for example, *In the leaflet for teenagers I used a short, repeated imperative structure, "Ride in. Rise up. Repeat.", which creates a quick, rhythmic slogan that echoes the movement of skating, suiting an audience who respond to energy and brevity.* Credit a named choice [1], a short quotation [1], and an effect linked to audience [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Her balance is mixed. Strengths: she leaves time for proofreading [1]. Weaknesses: Section B is worth 25 marks, the same as Section A, but she gives it 50 minutes, nearly the same as Question 1 together [1]; she spends 25 minutes on a commentary worth 10 marks, more than on writing worth 15 [1]; 25 minutes of proofreading may be more than needed, and some of it could go to planning Section B [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content, up to 6: up to 2 for structure, up to 2 for vocabulary, up to 2 for a judgement.

Structure: a plan gives the piece shape, such as a dialectical sequence, a Freytag pyramid or a review structure [1]; structure helps to create effects like suspense or contrast [1].

Vocabulary: precise vocabulary strengthens imagery, evaluation and argument [1]; sophisticated vocabulary used inaccurately causes errors and unclear meaning [1].

Judgement, for example: structure matters more because it shapes the whole piece, but precise vocabulary supports it, so a candidate needs both, with accuracy more important than sophistication [2]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 3**: recognising an error but naming it wrongly.
- **Question 6**: revealing the key information instead of withholding it.
- **Question 7**: presenting both views in a list, with no resolution.
- **Question 9**: using words such as "interesting" and "effective" with no explanation.
- **Question 10**: ignoring the mark weighting of each part.
