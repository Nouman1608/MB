## Section A

**1.** State **two** differences between a civil law (codified) system and a common law system. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the difference between an adversarial and an inquisitorial system of trial. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** intrinsic aids to statutory interpretation. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by an original precedent, giving an example. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between affirmative resolution and negative resolution procedures. **[2]**

**6.** State the three exceptions to the rule that the Court of Appeal (Civil Division) is bound by its own decisions. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the stages a government proposal passes through from green paper to Royal Assent. **[8]**

**8.** A local council makes a bylaw under an enabling Act: "No person shall play any musical instrument in any park, street or private garden in the borough." The enabling Act allows bylaws "for the good rule and government of public places". The Act also requires the council to consult the local residents' association, which it did not do.

**(a)** Explain **two** grounds on which a court could declare the bylaw invalid. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain who could bring the challenge and the procedure used. **[2]**

**9.** Explain the role and composition of the Law Commission, and assess its effectiveness. **[8]**

**10.** An Act makes it an offence "to sell or offer for sale any knife, dagger, sword or other bladed article". Rafi sells a pair of garden shears. Explain how the literal rule and the *ejusdem generis* rule might be applied to decide whether Rafi is guilty. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which the Human Rights Act 1998 affects the way judges interpret statutes, and explain why parliamentary supremacy is preserved. **[6]**

**12.** Assess the extent to which judges in the Supreme Court are able to change the law through precedent. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A civil law system is based on comprehensive written codes, whereas common law relies on judicial decisions as a source of law [1]. In a civil law system judges apply the code and earlier cases are not formally binding, whereas in common law precedent binds lower courts [1].

**2.** In an adversarial system each party gathers and presents its own evidence and the judge acts as a neutral referee [1]. In an inquisitorial system the judge takes an active role, directing the investigation and questioning witnesses [1].

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: long title [1]; short title [1]; preamble [1]; headings; schedules; marginal notes; interpretation (definition) sections.

**4.** An original precedent is a decision on a point of law that has not been decided before, so the court must reason by analogy or create a new rule [1]. Example: *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932), establishing the neighbour principle in negligence [1].

**5.** Under affirmative resolution the instrument does not become law unless Parliament votes to approve it [1]. Under negative resolution it becomes law automatically unless either House annuls it within a set period, so it may receive no debate [1].

**6.** Where there are two conflicting Court of Appeal decisions, it may choose which to follow [1]. Where its decision conflicts with a later House of Lords or Supreme Court decision, it must follow the later one [1]. Where its earlier decision was made *per incuriam* [1].

**7.** A **green paper** is a consultation document setting out proposals and options for comment [1]. A **white paper** sets out the government's firm proposals [1]. The Bill is drafted by parliamentary counsel and the **First Reading** formally introduces it, with no debate [1]. **Second Reading** is the main debate on the Bill's principles, followed by a vote [1]. At **Committee Stage** a committee examines it clause by clause and may amend it [1]. At **Report Stage** amendments are reported back and debated [1]. **Third Reading** is the final vote, and the Bill then repeats the process in the other House, with the Lords able only to delay under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 [1]. **Royal Assent** turns the Bill into an Act, which comes into force on the date stated or set by commencement order [1].

**8. (a)** **Procedural ultra vires**: the enabling Act required consultation with the residents' association and this was not done [1]; as in *Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms* (1972), failure to follow a mandatory procedure makes the legislation invalid, at least against those who should have been consulted [1]. **Substantive ultra vires**: the Act allows bylaws only for public places, but the bylaw extends to private gardens, going beyond the power granted [1]. The bylaw may also be unreasonable, as in *Strickland v Hayes Borough Council* (1896), where a bylaw covering private places was struck down [1].
**(b)** The challenge is by **judicial review** in the High Court (Administrative Court) [1], brought by a person with **locus standi**, a sufficient interest, such as a resident affected or the residents' association [1].

**9.** **Role**: established by the Law Commissions Act 1965 to keep the law under review and recommend reform, including codification and repeal of obsolete law [1]. It researches, publishes consultation papers, consults widely and produces a final report, often with a draft Bill [1]. **Composition**: a full-time body chaired by a senior judge, with four other commissioners who are experienced lawyers or academics [1], supported by legal staff and researchers [1]. **Effectiveness – for**: its expertise and consultation produce well-researched reform [1], with successes such as the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 and the Fraud Act 2006 [1]. **Against**: it cannot make law, so implementation depends on government finding parliamentary time; the draft Criminal Code has never been enacted [1]. **Conclusion**: effective at producing quality proposals but limited by political will [1].

**10.** **Literal rule**: the words are given their plain, ordinary meaning [1]. Shears have blades, so on a literal reading they are a "bladed article" and Rafi would be guilty, however surprising the result [1]. ***Ejusdem generis***: where general words follow a list of specific words, the general words are limited to things of the same kind [1], as in *Powell v Kempton Park Racecourse* (1899) [1]. The listed items (knife, dagger, sword) are weapons or cutting implements that can be carried [1], so the court might hold that garden tools are of a different class and acquit Rafi [1].

**11.** **Section 3** requires judges to read and give effect to legislation in a way that is compatible with Convention rights so far as it is possible to do so [1]; in *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* (2004) "as his or her wife or husband" was read to include a same-sex partner [1]. **Section 2** requires courts to take into account decisions of the European Court of Human Rights [1], which can influence the meaning given to words [1]. Supremacy is preserved because where a compatible reading is impossible, the court can only make a **declaration of incompatibility** under s4 [1]; the Act remains valid and it is for Parliament to decide whether to amend it [1].

**12.** **Knowledge**: the Practice Statement 1966 allows the House of Lords, now the Supreme Court, to depart from its own previous decisions when it appears right to do so [1]. **Examples**: *British Railways Board v Herrington* (1972) overruled *Addie v Dumbreck* (1929) [1]; *R v Shivpuri* (1986) overruled *Anderton v Ryan* (1985) [1]. **Other routes**: the court can create original precedent [1] and can overrule decisions of lower courts [1]. **Limits**: the power is used sparingly to preserve certainty, especially in criminal law and contract [1]; judges can only change law in cases that reach them, so change is slow and depends on litigants [1]; judges are unelected, so they defer to Parliament on major policy issues [1]; any change can be reversed by Parliament under parliamentary supremacy [1]. **Conclusion**: a real but deliberately restrained power, which suits a system that values certainty and democratic law making [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a rule of interpretation without applying it to the given facts.
- Naming ultra vires without saying whether it is procedural or substantive and why.
- Listing Law Commission facts without an assessment and a conclusion.
- Forgetting that a declaration of incompatibility leaves the Act in force.
