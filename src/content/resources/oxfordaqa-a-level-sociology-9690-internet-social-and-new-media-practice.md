## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by context collapse. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two forms of audience involvement in social media named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by network effects in relation to social media platforms. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two problems with research that claims social media harm young people's mental health. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which search engines and recommendation systems act as gatekeepers. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, from a fictional survey of 600 young people aged 14 to 18 in a fictional city.

| Time on social media per day | Respondents | Respondents reporting frequent low mood |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 hour | 200 | 30 |
| 1 to 3 hours | 250 | 50 |
| Over 3 hours | 150 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reporting frequent low mood in the "under 1 hour" group and in the "over 3 hours" group. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain why these data do not prove that social media use causes low mood. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> In the fictional town of Hollin, a message claiming that a new vaccine "changes children's DNA" spread through local messaging groups in two days. It began as a post by a parent who misunderstood a news article, then was copied by an anonymous account that added a fake doctor's name. Most people who shared it were already members of groups that distrust the health service. A correction from the town's hospital reached far fewer people than the original message.

**(a)** Identify two features of social media that helped the message spread. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how social media contribute to misinformation and disinformation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which social media have changed communication between people. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three ways in which platform owners exercise control over new and social media. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse the role of citizen journalists in reporting news events. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that social media have made conspiracy theories more widespread. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent have social media harmed the mental health of young people? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** The merging on social media of audiences that are kept separate offline, such as family, friends and employers [1], so users must present one self to many audiences at once (Marwick and boyd) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: content creators [1]; citizen journalists [1]; social media influencers. **[2]**

**3.** A platform becomes more valuable to each user as more people join it [1], because there are more people to connect with and more content [1], so markets tend to concentrate in a few dominant platforms that are hard to compete with (Srnicek) [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Most studies are correlational [1], so they cannot show whether social media cause low mood or unhappy young people use social media more [1]. Many rely on self-reported screen time [1], which is often inaccurate and treats very different activities, such as schoolwork and scrolling, as the same [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Search engines rank results [1], so the sources shown first get most attention while others become almost invisible [1]. Recommendation systems choose what appears in users' feeds based on engagement [1], so they, rather than editors, decide which stories many people see [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Under 1 hour: 30 / 200 x 100 = 15% [1]. Over 3 hours: 45 / 150 x 100 = 30% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Low mood doubles from 15% to 30% between the lightest and heaviest users [1], but this shows correlation, not causation [1]. Young people with low mood may spend more time online, so the cause may run the other way [1], and other factors, such as family problems or lack of sleep, may affect both, which a survey cannot separate [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: messaging groups allowing fast sharing [1]; anonymous accounts [1]; groups of like-minded members; ease of copying and editing posts. **[2]**
**(b)** The parent who misunderstood an article spread misinformation: false content without intent to deceive [1], showing how ordinary users become sources of falsehood [1]. The anonymous account that added a fake doctor's name turned it into disinformation, deliberately misleading [1], helped by anonymity that removes accountability [1]. Sharing mainly within groups that distrust the health service fits echo chambers or filter bubbles [1], and the correction reaching fewer people supports findings that false claims spread farther and faster than accurate ones (Vosoughi, Roy and Aral) [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Communication has shifted from one-to-many to many-to-many [1], so ordinary users can reach large audiences without broadcasters (Castells's mass self-communication) [1]. Posts are persistent and searchable (boyd) [1], so young people must manage how a message may be seen by unintended audiences later [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Owners design algorithms that decide what users see [1], shaping which content and creators gain attention [1]. They set and enforce content rules, removing posts or accounts [1], which gives them power over public debate [1]. They collect and sell data on user behaviour [1], which Zuboff calls surveillance capitalism [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Citizen journalists are non-professionals who report news events, often by phone [1].
- They can record events before professional reporters arrive [1].
- Protesters' videos in Tunisia and Egypt (2010 to 2011) were used by international broadcasters [1].
- The George Floyd video (2020) shaped global awareness [1].
- They challenge the gatekeeping of traditional media [1].
- Counter: footage may lack context or be manipulated [1].
- Professional media still select and frame citizen footage [1].
- Analysis: citizen journalism widens who reports but does not remove editorial power [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Conspiracy theories claim hidden groups secretly control events [1].
- Social media let anonymous posters reach large audiences quickly [1].
- QAnon (2017) grew from online posts into a movement [1].
- Algorithms reward engagement, which favours dramatic claims [1].
- Filter bubbles and echo chambers may isolate believers from counter-evidence [1].
- Application: own-society example of an online health or political rumour [1].
- Counter: Uscinski and Parent found no long-term rise in conspiracy talk [1].
- They argue conspiracy theories are used by groups out of power, whatever the medium [1].
- Bruns: evidence for strong filter bubbles is weak [1].
- Traditional media and rumour spread conspiracy theories long before the internet [1].
- Platforms have removed some conspiracy content [1].
- Judgement: social media increase visibility and speed, not necessarily the share of people who believe [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Haidt (2024): smartphones and social media harmed adolescent mental health [1].
- Twenge (2017): rises in teen depression in the early 2010s linked to smartphones [1].
- Social comparison with idealised images may lower self-esteem [1].
- Cyberbullying follows young people home [1].
- Late-night use disrupts sleep [1].
- Own-society application: concern about young people's phone use in schools or families [1].
- Orben and Przybylski (2019): only a small negative association [1].
- Most evidence is correlational [1].
- Reverse causation: unhappy young people may use social media more [1].
- Self-reported screen time is inaccurate [1].
- Online communities support isolated or minority young people [1].
- Social media sustain friendships and family links across distance [1].
- Moral panic (Cohen): fears about new media recur with each technology [1].
- Own-society application: use of social media for support or study [1].
- Analysis: effects differ by user, platform and type of use [1].
- Judgement: some young people are harmed, but large universal harm is not established [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the evidence, not just the claims; a methodological point is often the strongest evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating correlation in survey data as proof of cause.
- Confusing misinformation with disinformation.
- Describing platforms without analysing algorithms or data.
- Assuming citizen journalism is always accurate.
- Ignoring the moral panic angle when discussing new media fears.
