## Key definitions

- **Strategic positioning**: the long-term choice of how a business will compete against rivals in its chosen market.
- **Market map**: a diagram placing competing brands or firms on two axes that matter to customers (for example, price and perceived quality).
- **Low cost strategy**: aiming to produce a similar product at lower cost than rivals, then either undercutting their price or matching it for a wider margin.
- **Differentiation strategy**: making the product distinct in ways customers value so that it can command a higher price or stronger loyalty.
- **Competitive advantage**: an edge over rivals, from lower costs or greater benefits, that lets the business earn above-average profit or win more customers.

## Market mapping in steps

1. Choose two features customers use to compare products (price, quality, convenience, style).
2. Score each competitor, ideally from customer research rather than managers' opinion.
3. Plot them; group close rivals.
4. Look for gaps and for firms that seem out of line (high price, low benefits).
5. Test any gap: is there demand, and can it be served at a profit?

**Value:** fast, visual, shows rivals and gaps, guides positioning. **Limits:** only two variables, a gap may have no demand, perceptions differ by segment and change, opinion-based maps may be biased.

**Worked reminder.** Two car washes in Pebblerow: Brightsuds charges £9, scores 5.0 for quality; a hand-wash rival charges £15 and scores 8.0. Nothing sits at around £12 with a score near 7. That is a possible gap, not proof of a market.

## Benefits and price: the positions

| Benefits vs rivals | Price vs rivals | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Similar | Lower | Low price |
| Fewer | Lower | Budget |
| More | Similar | Better value |
| More | Higher | Premium / differentiated |
| More | Lower | More for less (hard to fund) |
| Same or fewer | Higher | Overpriced (fails unless customers lack choice) |

Rule of thumb: a price above rivals needs benefits customers can see and believe.

## Low cost vs differentiation

| | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Source of edge | Scale, efficiency, bulk buying, simple range | Design, quality, brand, service, innovation |
| Advantages | Survives price wars; wide appeal; scale deters entrants; suits downturns | Higher unit margin; loyal, less price-sensitive buyers; brand hard to copy |
| Disadvantages | Thin margins need volume; can be undercut; cost cuts may hurt quality and morale | Costly to build and keep; customers may not value extras; features copied; vulnerable in recession |

**Must-know distinction:** low **cost** is about the cost base; low **price** is a choice the low cost firm may or may not make. Matching rivals' prices with lower costs gives a higher margin per unit.

**Worked reminder.** Pebblerow car washes charge £12 a wash. One has a cost of £7.50 per wash; a rival with 20% lower costs (£6.00) earns £6.00 a wash instead of £4.50 at the same price.

## Influences on the choice

- **Internal:** core strengths and skills, scale, technology, brand reputation, finance available, objectives (growth and share vs margin).
- **External:** customers' price sensitivity and incomes, rivals' positions (market map), strength of the five forces, the economy, technology, regulation.

Method for "which position should X choose?":

1. Identify what the firm is genuinely better at (costs or benefits).
2. Check what the target customers value and can afford.
3. Check where rivals already sit.
4. Test the numbers: contribution per unit, volume needed, effect of a price war.
5. Judge, naming the one or two factors that decide it for this firm.

## Functional implications

| Function | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Price-focused promotion, wide distribution, narrow range | Brand and quality message, selective channels, wider or premium range |
| Operations | Standardisation, high capacity use, lean methods, bulk purchasing | Quality systems, innovation, flexible or tailored production, research and development |
| Finance | Capital for capacity and automation; strict cost control | Funding for research, design and promotion; needs premium margins |
| Human resources | Lean staffing, multi-skilling, cost-conscious pay | Skilled staff, training, retention, possibly higher pay |

## Competitive advantage

**Benefits:** higher margins; loyal customers and steadier sales; more power over suppliers and distributors; profit to reinvest; a base for growth.

**Difficulties of maintaining it:** rivals imitate or patents expire; tastes change; technology makes a feature standard or cuts rivals' costs; entrants and substitutes; rising input costs; complacency and loss of key staff.

**Protecting it:** keep innovating, build a brand, protect ideas legally, secure supply, invest in staff skills, keep cutting costs. Each costs money, so weigh the cost against how long the advantage is likely to last.

## Quick self-test

1. State the purpose of a market map.
2. Give two limitations of market mapping.
3. Explain the difference between a low cost strategy and a low price.
4. A car wash charges £12 and has a cost of £7.50 per wash. It sells 2,400 washes a month. Calculate monthly contribution.
5. A rival also charges £12 but has a cost of £5.80 per wash. By what percentage is its contribution per wash higher than the firm in question 4?
6. Which benefits-price position is least likely to survive in a competitive market, and why?
7. A premium valet costs £15 when the market average is £12. Calculate the price premium as a percentage.
8. Give two human resources implications of a differentiation strategy.
9. Give two reasons why a competitive advantage is hard to maintain.
10. Why might a recession favour a low cost position?
11. Suggest two ways a business could protect a competitive advantage.

### Answers

1. To show where competitors are positioned on two customer-relevant features, so the business can spot close rivals and gaps and plan its own position.
2. Any two: only two variables; a gap may lack demand or profit; perceptions differ by segment and change; maps based on opinion may be biased.
3. Low cost means the firm's costs are below rivals'. It may pass this on as a lower price, or keep the price at rivals' level and earn a wider margin.
4. (12 − 7.50) × 2,400 = 4.50 × 2,400 = **£10,800**.
5. Rival contribution = 12 − 5.80 = £6.20. (6.20 − 4.50) ÷ 4.50 × 100 = **37.8%** higher.
6. Same or fewer benefits at a higher price: customers can get as much or more elsewhere for less, so they switch unless they have no choice.
7. (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = **25%**.
8. Any two: recruit skilled staff; more training; focus on retention; possibly higher pay or incentives tied to quality.
9. Any two: rivals copy; tastes change; new technology; new entrants or substitutes; rising costs; complacency.
10. Falling incomes make customers more price-sensitive, so they trade down to cheaper options and value low prices more than extras.
11. Any two: keep innovating; build a strong brand; protect ideas with patents or trademarks; secure exclusive supply; train and retain the staff whose skills create the edge.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a market map but not saying what a manager would **do** with it.
- Claiming a gap on a map is automatically profitable.
- Using "low cost" and "low price" as if they mean the same thing.
- Listing benefits of differentiation without evidence that the case customers will pay for them.
- Giving functional implications in general terms, not tied to the firm in the case.
- Calculating profit for one option only, or comparing options on revenue rather than profit.
- Naming difficulties of keeping an advantage without explaining how each would cut profit for this business.
- Concluding with "it depends" and no stated deciding factor.
