## What the specification lists

- Meaning of capacity utilisation and of capacity management.
- Calculate, interpret and analyse capacity utilisation.
- Evaluate the impact and importance of capacity utilisation and of capacity management to a business and its stakeholders.
- Recommend and justify ways to change capacity.
- Recommend and justify ways to improve capacity management.

## Core recall

### Definitions and formula

- **Capacity:** the maximum output possible in a period with existing resources.
- **Capacity utilisation (%)** = actual output ÷ maximum possible output × 100.
- **Capacity management:** planning and controlling capacity to match demand at the lowest cost without losing quality or customers.
- The specification does not print the formula; this is the standard form. State the time period.

### What the figure shows

| Utilisation | Meaning | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Low (spare capacity) | Resources idle | High fixed cost per unit, possible losses, short-time working |
| Around 85% to 90% | Efficient with some room | Can handle a rush order and planned maintenance |
| 100% or over | No spare capacity | Overtime, errors, breakdown risk, lost orders |

### Utilisation and cost

- Fixed cost per unit = fixed costs ÷ output; it falls as utilisation rises.
- Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
- Break-even utilisation = break-even output ÷ maximum output × 100.

### Why 100% is not always best

- No room for urgent orders or maintenance.
- One breakdown or absence cuts output at once.
- Fatigue causes errors and accidents.

### Ways to increase capacity

- Buy or rent more space or machines.
- Add shifts or overtime.
- Hire temporary staff.
- Subcontract.
- Use technology to raise output per hour.

### Ways to reduce capacity

- Sell or close part of a site.
- Cut shifts.
- Redundancy (costly, harms morale).
- Rent out spare space.

### Ways to improve capacity management

| Method | Benefit | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Better demand forecasting | Matches capacity to need | Forecasts can be wrong |
| Flexible staffing (part-time, annualised hours, multi-skilling) | Staff hours follow demand | Less commitment; training cost |
| Smoothing demand (off-peak discounts, booking) | Steadier use | Lower revenue per unit |
| Planned maintenance in quiet periods | Fewer peak-time breakdowns | Needs quiet periods |
| Producing for stock in quiet periods | Meets peaks from stock | Storage cost; shelf life |

### Stakeholders

- Employees: stable hours and jobs, or short-time working and redundancy.
- Customers: reliable delivery, or delays and lost orders.
- Suppliers: steady orders, or sudden changes.
- Owners: return on assets, or idle assets.

## Worked reminder

Orrin Print (invented): maximum 12,000 units a month, fixed costs £48,000, price £11, variable cost £6.

- Utilisation at 9,000 units = 9,000 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = **75%**.
- Contribution per unit = £5; break-even = 48,000 ÷ 5 = 9,600 units = **80%** utilisation.
- Profit at 9,000 units = £45,000 − £48,000 = **−£3,000**; at 10,200 units = **+£3,000**.
- If demand rises to 15,000: a night shift (capacity 16,000, extra £9,000 fixed) gives profit £75,000 − £57,000 = **£18,000**; subcontracting 3,000 units at £8.50 gives £12,000 + £7,500 = **£19,500**.
- The two options cost the same at 3,600 units above capacity; above that, the shift is cheaper.

## Choosing between options

1. Is the extra demand permanent or temporary? Temporary points to overtime, temporary staff or subcontracting.
2. How much cash is available? New premises need finance; subcontracting needs little.
3. How much control over quality is needed?
4. How quickly is capacity needed?
5. What are the objectives (growth, profit, reputation)?

## Quick case practice

A print shop (invented) can make 9,000 leaflets a month and makes 7,200. Fixed costs are £27,000 a month and each leaflet earns a contribution of £5.

- Utilisation = 7,200 ÷ 9,000 × 100 = **80%**.
- Break-even output = £27,000 ÷ £5 = 5,400 leaflets, which is 5,400 ÷ 9,000 × 100 = **60%** utilisation.
- Profit = 7,200 × £5 − £27,000 = **£9,000**.

Model response outline: the shop is 20 percentage points above break-even, so it is profitable, with room for rush orders; a rise to 100% would add 1,800 × £5 = £9,000 of profit but leave no spare capacity for repairs; recommend a target near 85% to 90% and use temporary staff or subcontracting for peaks.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capacity | Maximum output in a given period |
| Capacity utilisation | Actual output as a percentage of maximum output |
| Spare capacity | Unused capacity, shown by utilisation below 100% |
| Over capacity | Demand above normal maximum output |
| Subcontracting | Paying another business to produce part of the output |
| Capacity management | Matching capacity to demand |
| Contribution | Selling price less variable cost per unit |

## Exam traps

- The base of the percentage is **maximum** output, not demand.
- An average utilisation figure can hide seasonal problems.
- Do not recommend permanent capacity for short-term demand.
- Capacity changes alter fixed costs, so recalculate profit.
- Always say what the figure means for cost, profit or stakeholders.

## Self-test

1. Define capacity utilisation.
2. A bakery can make 4,000 loaves a day and makes 3,400. Calculate its utilisation.
3. Fixed costs are £4,000 a day and output is 3,400 loaves. Calculate fixed cost per loaf, to the nearest penny.
4. Give two disadvantages of running at 100% for a long period.
5. Give two ways to increase capacity quickly.
6. Why might subcontracting be better than a new site for a short rise in demand?
7. What is capacity management?
8. Give two ways to smooth demand.
9. Contribution per loaf is £1.40 and fixed costs are £4,000 a day. Calculate break-even output as a loaf count and as a percentage of capacity.
10. Name two stakeholders affected by low utilisation, and how.

### Answers

1. Actual output as a percentage of maximum possible output in a period.
2. 3,400 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **85%**.
3. £4,000 ÷ 3,400 = **£1.18**.
4. Any two: no capacity for urgent orders; no time for maintenance; breakdowns cut output; errors and accidents from overwork.
5. Any two: overtime; extra shift; temporary staff; subcontracting.
6. It needs little finance, adds no permanent fixed cost and can be stopped when demand falls.
7. Planning and controlling capacity so that it matches demand at the lowest cost without losing quality or customers.
8. Any two: off-peak discounts; booking systems; seasonal products.
9. £4,000 ÷ £1.40 = 2,857.1, so **2,858 loaves** (rounded up to cover costs); 2,858 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **71.5%** of capacity.
10. Employees (short-time working or redundancy) and owners (lower return on assets, possible losses).
