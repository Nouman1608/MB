## Questions

**1.** A craft stall in Country A raises the price of a ceramic mug from £2.50 to £2.75. Weekly sales fall from 400 to 340.

**(a)** Calculate PED, showing your working. **[3]**
**(b)** Classify demand as elastic or inelastic and calculate the change in the stall's revenue. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the shape of the demand curve and give the value of PED for **(a)** a perfectly inelastic good and **(b)** a good with unit elasticity. **[4]**

**3.** Weekly demand for guided walks in Country B is Q = 800 - 40P, where P is in pounds.

**(a)** Calculate PED at P = 5, P = 10 and P = 15. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why PED differs along this straight-line curve. **[2]**
**(c)** State the price that maximises total revenue and explain why. **[2]**

**4.** A firm sells 800 units a week at £10. Calculate the percentage change in total revenue when **(a)** PED = -0.4 and the firm raises its price by 5%, and **(b)** PED = -2.5 and it cuts its price by 4%. **[4]**

**5.** A pharmacy in Country C sells a branded pain-relief tablet. Evaluate the factors that determine the PED for the tablet. **[8]**

**6.** In a market in Country D, Qd = 2,000 - 250P and Qs = 250P.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** A tax of £1 per unit is imposed on sellers. Find the new price paid by buyers and the new quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the amount of tax paid by buyers and by sellers per unit, and the tax revenue. **[2]**
**(d)** Use PED and PES to explain the split in (c). **[2]**

**7.** In a market in Country E, Qd = 1,800 - 100P and Qs = 200P. The government pays a subsidy of £1.50 per unit to sellers.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium before the subsidy. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the price buyers pay and the quantity after the subsidy. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the benefit per unit to buyers and to sellers, and the cost to the government. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why output rises by only 8.3%. **[2]**

**8.** Income in Country F rises by 8%. Quantity demanded of good X rises from 200 to 224, of good Y from 500 to 510, and of good Z falls from 80 to 76.

**(a)** Calculate YED for X, Y and Z. **[3]**
**(b)** Classify each good. **[3]**
**(c)** Income then falls by 6%. Predict the change in quantity demanded of each good. **[3]**

**9.** The price of brand A rises from £3.00 to £3.36. Over the same period the quantity demanded of brand B rises from 150 to 183, of a complementary item C falls from 500 to 455, and of an unrelated item D rises from 300 to 301.5.

**(a)** Calculate XED for B, C and D. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the relationship in each case. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how the manufacturer of brand B can use the XED you found. **[2]**

**10.** Supply of a good in Country G is Qs = 30P - 60.

**(a)** Calculate PES at P = £4 and at P = £6. **[3]**
**(b)** Another good's price rises from £10 to £11 and its quantity supplied from 500 to 515. Calculate PES. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two factors that could make that supply inelastic. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of PED, YED, XED and PES to a government deciding whether to tax sugary snacks, and to a firm selling them. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Price change = 0.25 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +10% [1]. Quantity change = (340 - 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = -15% [1]. PED = -15 ÷ 10 = **-1.5** [1].
**(b)** Elastic, since the size is above 1 [1]. Revenue falls from £1,000 to £935 (2.75 × 340), a fall of £65 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show both percentage changes from the starting values. A bare final figure leaves no working to credit if it is wrong.

**2. (a)** Vertical line [1]. PED = 0 [1].
**(b)** Rectangular hyperbola [1]. PED = -1 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the shape and the value as separate statements. A value alone does not describe the curve.

**3. (a)** At P = 5, Q = 600, PED = -40 × 5 ÷ 600 = **-0.33** [1]. At P = 10, Q = 400, PED = **-1.0** [1]. At P = 15, Q = 200, PED = **-3.0** [1].
**(b)** The slope is constant, but PED = slope term × P ÷ Q [1]. As price falls and quantity rises, P ÷ Q falls, so the same absolute change is a smaller percentage [1].
**(c)** £10 [1], where PED = -1: above it a price cut raises revenue and below it a price cut lowers it [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link (c) to the elasticity value at that point, not only to revenue figures.

**4. (a)** Quantity falls 2% to 784, price £10.50, revenue = 10.50 × 784 = £8,232 [1], up from £8,000, so **+2.9%** [1].
**(b)** Quantity rises 10% to 880, price £9.60, revenue = 9.60 × 880 = £8,448 [1], so **+5.6%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Multiply the factors, 1.05 × 0.98 for example, rather than adding +5% and -2%.

**5.** Substitutes: branded tablets compete with generic equivalents [1], so demand tends to be elastic where cheaper equivalents are known [1]. Necessity: pain relief feels essential at the moment of need [1], pushing demand towards inelastic for those in pain [1]. Share of income: a low-priced item takes a small share of spending, so buyers pay little attention to a rise [1]. Time: a regular buyer can switch brands over weeks, so long-run demand is more elastic [1]. Loyalty to a trusted brand reduces responsiveness [1]. Judgement: substitutes probably dominate, because generics are easy to find, so demand is likely elastic for the brand but inelastic for the whole category [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers rank the factors and apply them to the named product. A plain list of determinants gains little.

**6. (a)** 2,000 - 250P = 250P gives 500P = 2,000 [1], so **P = £4, Q = 1,000** [1].
**(b)** Supply becomes 250(P - 1): 250P - 250 = 2,000 - 250P gives P = 4.50 [1]. **Buyers pay £4.50 and Q = 875** [1].
**(c)** Buyers pay £0.50 and sellers £0.50 per unit [1]. Revenue = 1 × 875 = **£875** [1].
**(d)** PED = -250 × 4 ÷ 1,000 = -1 and PES = 250 × 4 ÷ 1,000 = 1 [1]. Equal elasticities mean neither side can avoid the tax more easily, so the burden splits equally [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare buyers' and sellers' responsiveness explicitly. Saying the burden is "equal" with no elasticities explains nothing.

**7. (a)** 200P = 1,800 - 100P gives **P = £6, Q = 1,200** [1].
**(b)** Supply becomes 200(P + 1.50) [1]. 200P + 300 = 1,800 - 100P gives P = 5 [1]. **Buyers pay £5 and Q = 1,300** [1].
**(c)** Buyers gain £1.00 and sellers £0.50 (they receive £6.50) [1]. Cost = 1.50 × 1,300 = **£1,950** [1].
**(d)** Quantity rises by 100 on 1,200, which is 8.3% [1]. Demand is inelastic, since PED = -100 × 6 ÷ 1,200 = -0.5, so the 16.7% fall in price brings only an 8.3% rise in quantity [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote the elasticity and tie it to both percentage changes to explain why the output response is small.

**8. (a)** X: +12% ÷ 8% = **1.5** [1]. Y: +2% ÷ 8% = **0.25** [1]. Z: -5% ÷ 8% = **-0.625** [1].
**(b)** X is superior (above 1) [1]. Y is a normal good (between 0 and 1) [1]. Z is inferior (negative) [1].
**(c)** X falls 1.5 × 6 = **9%** [1]. Y falls 0.25 × 6 = **1.5%** [1]. Z rises 0.625 × 6 = **3.75%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign through the prediction. Z has a negative YED, so a fall in income raises demand.

**9. (a)** Price change +12% [1]. B: +22% ÷ 12% = **+1.83**, C: -9% ÷ 12% = **-0.75**, D: +0.5% ÷ 12% = **+0.04** [1]. Each change is from the starting value [1].
**(b)** B is a substitute [1]. C is a complement [1]. D is non-related, since the value is close to zero [1].
**(c)** XED above 1 means demand for B responds strongly to A's price [1], so B's maker gains sales if A's price rises and must watch A's pricing [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the relationship from the sign and justify "non-related" from a value near zero, not from the small quantity change alone.

**10. (a)** At P = 4, Q = 60, PES = 30 × 4 ÷ 60 = **2** [1]. At P = 6, Q = 120, PES = 30 × 6 ÷ 120 = **1.5** [1]. Elastic at both, as the curve cuts the price axis [1].
**(b)** Price +10%, quantity +3% [1]. PES = **0.3** [1].
**(c)** Spare capacity: if firms are near full output they cannot raise supply quickly [1]. Time: in the short run plant and labour cannot be added [1]. A perishable product cannot be stored to release when price rises, so quantity responds little [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a link to why quantity cannot rise quickly. Naming a factor alone is incomplete.

**11.** Government: PED shows how far a tax cuts snack sales [1]. If PED is inelastic, a tax raises revenue but cuts consumption little [1], and buyers bear most of it, which may hit low-income households [1]. If PED is elastic, consumption falls more but revenue is lower [1]. PES shows the burden split: elastic supply passes more of the tax to buyers [1]. YED shows whether rising incomes would raise snack demand and weaken the policy later [1]. XED shows whether buyers switch to healthier or equally sugary substitutes [1]. Firm: PED and total revenue guide the price response to the tax [1]. XED to rivals shows whether pushing a price rise onto customers risks losing sales [1]. YED helps forecast sales through the cycle [1]. Limits: estimates rest on past data, change over time and differ by price level [1]. Judgement: PED and PES matter most for a tax decision, with YED and XED more helpful as support. Elasticities are best used with other evidence, since estimates are uncertain [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the two agents and judge which measure matters most for each. Defining all four measures first spends words without evaluating.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring percentage changes from the final value, not the starting value.
- Dropping the negative sign on YED or XED, so goods are misclassified.
- Saying PED is constant along a straight-line demand curve.
- Stating that revenue rises on a price rise without checking the size of PED.
- Giving a tax or subsidy answer with no figures for each side's share.
- Adding percentage changes instead of multiplying the price and quantity factors for revenue.
- Listing determinants with no application to the named product and no ranking.
