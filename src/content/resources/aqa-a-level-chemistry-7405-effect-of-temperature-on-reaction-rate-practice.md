These questions cover section **3.1.5.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Effect of temperature study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-temperature-on-reaction-rate/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-temperature-on-reaction-rate-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Define the rate of a reaction and state its units when concentration is in mol dm⁻³ and time is in seconds. **[2]**

**2.** The concentration of a reactant falls by 0.045 mol dm⁻³ in 90 s. Calculate the mean rate of reaction. **[2]**

**3.** State two reasons why increasing the temperature increases the rate of a reaction. Say which is more important. **[3]**

**4.** Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain why a small increase in temperature can cause a large increase in the rate of a reaction. You may include a labelled sketch. **[4]**

**5.** A student heats a reaction mixture and says: "The activation energy decreases because the particles have more energy." Identify the error. **[2]**

**6.** The table shows the time taken for a cross to disappear when sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with hydrochloric acid at different temperatures. The concentrations and volumes are the same each time.

| Temperature / °C | Time / s |
|---|---|
| 25 | 52 |
| 35 | 26 |
| 45 | 13 |

**(a)** Explain why rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time in this experiment. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the factor by which the rate increases from 25 °C to 35 °C. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the rate relative to the rate at 25 °C when the temperature is 45 °C. **[2]**

**(d)** Describe the trend and suggest what the data show about the effect of a 10 °C rise. **[2]**

**7.** State two variables that must be controlled in the experiment in question 6. **[2]**

**8.** State a hazard in the experiment in question 6 and a precaution. **[2]**

**9.** The student judges the end point by eye. Suggest one problem this causes and one way to overcome it. **[2]**

**10.** In question 6 the time at 45 °C is measured with an uncertainty of ±0.5 s. Calculate the percentage uncertainty and state why it is more significant at higher temperatures. **[2]**

**11.** Both solutions are placed in a water bath at the required temperature before they are mixed. Explain why this is done. **[2]**

**12.** A reaction at 300 K has an activation energy of 50 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain, using the idea of the tail of the distribution, why the proportion of molecules with enough energy is small. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time [1]; mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Rate = 0.045 ÷ 90 [1] = **5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The particles move faster, so they collide more frequently [1]; a greater proportion of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]; the second is the more important reason [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The distribution moves to the right (peak lower and at higher energy) and the high-energy tail grows [1]; the activation energy is in the tail, where the area is small [1]; so a small shift gives a large proportional increase in the number of molecules with energy equal to or greater than Eₐ [1]; more collisions are successful per second, so the rate increases greatly [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Temperature does not change the activation energy [1]; the particles have more energy, so a greater proportion have energy equal to or greater than the (unchanged) activation energy [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** The same amount of sulfur has to form to hide the cross each time, so rate = fixed amount ÷ time [1]

**(b)** 52 ÷ 26 = **2.0** [1]

**(c)** 52 ÷ 13 = **4.0** [1] times the rate at 25 °C [1]

**(d)** The rate increases as the temperature increases [1]; each 10 °C rise approximately doubles the rate [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Any two of: concentration of thiosulfate, concentration of acid, volume of each solution, same flask and cross, same observer [1 each]. **[2]**

**8.** Hazard: sulfur dioxide is formed and is toxic and can trigger asthma [1]; precaution: use a fume cupboard or a well-ventilated room (and wear eye protection) [1]. **[2]**

**9.** The end point is subjective and different judgements give different times [1]; use the same observer each time, or a light sensor that records when the light transmitted falls below a set value [1]. **[2]**

**10.** 0.5 ÷ 13 × 100 = **3.8%** [1]; at higher temperatures the times are shorter, so the same absolute error in timing is a larger percentage of the time [1]. **[2]**

**11.** If they are at different temperatures, the mixture is at a temperature between them and not the temperature stated [1]; this temperature would change as the mixture settles, so the rate would not be measured at a known, constant temperature [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Most molecules have energy below Eₐ, because the activation energy lies far to the right in the tail of the distribution, where the curve is low [1]; so only a small proportion of the collisions are successful at 300 K [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving only the increase in collision frequency as the reason.
- Saying the activation energy decreases on heating.
- Not linking the explanation to the tail of the distribution.
- Writing that rate is proportional to time, instead of 1 ÷ time.
- Not controlling the concentration and volume.
