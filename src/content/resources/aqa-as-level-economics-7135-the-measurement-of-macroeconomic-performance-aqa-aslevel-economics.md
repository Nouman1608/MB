This guide covers **3.2.1 The measurement of macroeconomic performance** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, the opening sub-topic of Paper 2. At AS it has three parts: the objectives of government economic policy, macroeconomic indicators, and uses of index numbers. The A-level part on uses of national income data is not in the AS content. This guide concentrates on the data skills that Paper 2 data-response questions depend on. All countries and figures below are invented.

The shared [study guide on the measurement of macroeconomic performance](/resources/a-level-aqa-economics-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance/) introduces the objectives and indicators. | 3.2.1.2 | Macroeconomic indicators | Use real GDP, real GDP per capita, CPI and RPI, unemployment measures, productivity and the current account balance |
| 3.2.1.3 | Uses of index numbers | Calculate and interpret index numbers, including base years and weights; know the underlying features of CPI and RPI |

## 3.2.1.1 Objectives and why they conflict

The four main objectives are **economic growth, price stability, minimising unemployment and a stable balance of payments on current account**. Governments may also aim to balance the budget and achieve an equitable distribution of income.

The specification says conflict can arise "at least in the short run". The reason is that one policy affects several objectives at once. A rise in aggregate demand, from lower interest rates for example, tends to:

- raise real output and cut unemployment (helping two objectives);
- add to inflationary pressure as spare capacity is used up (hurting price stability);
- raise spending on imports (worsening the current account).

The phrase "at least in the short run" matters for evaluation: if supply-side improvements raise productive capacity, growth and low inflation can be achieved together in the long run.

**Priorities change over time.** Since 1997 the Bank of England has set interest rates to meet an inflation target set by the government, making price stability a central objective of UK monetary policy. In answers, it is enough to show awareness that the weight given to each objective depends on economic conditions and political choices.

## 3.2.1.2 Reading the indicators

| Indicator | What it measures | Reading carefully |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | Output adjusted for inflation | A fall in the growth rate (3% to 1%) is still growth |
| Real GDP per capita | Real output per person | Rises more slowly than real GDP when population grows |
| CPI | Average change in consumer prices; the measure used for the government's inflation target | Falling inflation does not mean falling prices |
| RPI | An older price index whose coverage includes some housing costs, such as mortgage interest payments, that CPI leaves out | RPI and CPI can show different inflation rates for the same year |
| Unemployment measures | Number or percentage of the labour force without work and seeking it | The rate and the level can move differently if the labour force changes |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked | The key long-run influence on living standards and costs |
| Current account balance | Trade in goods and services, plus primary and secondary income | Usually given in money terms or as a percentage of GDP |

**Percentage change versus percentage point change.** If inflation falls from 4% to 3%, it has fallen by **1 percentage point**, which is a fall of 25% in the inflation rate. Prices are still rising, only more slowly. Writing "prices fell" here is one of the most common errors in Paper 2 data answers.

## 3.2.1.3 Index numbers

An index number expresses a value relative to a **base year** set at 100:

```
Index = (value in given year / value in base year) x 100
```

### Worked example 1: a weighted price index

A simplified basket for Country R has four categories. Weights show each category's share of the average household's spending (out of 100). Price indices are for this year, with last year = 100.

| Category | Weight | Price index | Weight x index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 25 | 108 | 2,700 |
| Housing | 35 | 104 | 3,640 |
| Transport | 15 | 95 | 1,425 |
| Recreation | 25 | 102 | 2,550 |
| Total | 100 | | 10,315 |

```
Weighted index = 10,315 / 100 = 103.15  ->  average prices up 3.15%
Unweighted average = (108 + 104 + 95 + 102) / 4 = 102.25
```

The weighted figure is higher because housing, which has the largest weight, rose by 4%, while transport, which fell, has the smallest weight. Weights matter because a 10% rise in the price of something households rarely buy should matter less than a 10% rise in rent.

### Worked example 2: changes between two non-base years

A price index (base year 2020 = 100) reads 115 in 2023 and 121 in 2024.

```
Change in index points = 121 - 115 = 6
Percentage change = 6 / 115 x 100 = 5.2%   (not 6%)
```

Only a change measured from the base year equals the change in index points.

### Worked example 3: rebasing

The same series reads 108 in 2022. To rebase so that 2022 = 100, divide every value by 108 and multiply by 100: 2024 becomes 121 / 108 x 100 = 112.0. Rebasing changes the reference point, not the percentage changes between years.

### Worked example 4: from nominal to real

Country R's nominal GDP rises from 2,000 bn to 2,180 bn while the GDP price index rises from 100 to 105.

```
Real GDP this year, at last year's prices = 2,180 / 1.05 = 2,076.2 bn
Real growth = (2,076.2 - 2,000) / 2,000 = 3.8%
Nominal growth was 9.0%; the rest was inflation
```

If the population rose from 50.0 million to 50.5 million, real GDP per capita went from 40,000 to 41,113, a rise of only 2.8%. A data question that gives population figures expects this adjustment.

### Features of CPI and RPI to be aware of

You are not expected to know how CPI and RPI are technically constructed. You should know their underlying features:

- they measure the cost of a **basket of goods and services** bought by an **"average family"** or household;
- the basket and the weights are updated to reflect changing spending patterns;
- because no real household buys exactly the average basket, a household's own inflation rate can differ from the index (a household that spends a large share on rent will feel rent rises more).

## Annotated model answer

*Question style: using the data, explain whether living standards in Country R improved last year.*

> Nominal GDP rose by 9%, but after adjusting for 5% inflation real GDP grew by about 3.8%. Because the population grew by 1%, real GDP per capita rose by about 2.8%, so average output per person increased. This suggests average living standards improved, though the data do not show whether the gains were evenly shared or whether hours worked increased.

Why it works: it turns nominal data into real per capita data, quotes the figures, answers the question and adds one limitation without drifting into A-level content on national income data.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a fall in the inflation rate as a fall in prices.
- Calculating the change from 115 to 121 as 6%.
- Using an unweighted average when weights are given.
- Comparing nominal values across years without adjusting for inflation.
- Naming an indicator without linking it to the objective it measures.
- Listing the four objectives without explaining any conflict.

## Exam technique for Paper 2

- In Section A multiple-choice questions on index numbers, write the formula in the margin and check whether the change starts from the base year.
- "Using the data" means quoting at least one figure with its units and saying what it shows.
- When asked to explain a conflict, run the chain from policy to two objectives, and say whether the conflict is short-run.
