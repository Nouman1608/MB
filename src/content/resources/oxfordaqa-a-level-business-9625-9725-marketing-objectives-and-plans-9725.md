## What the specification asks for

The 9725 content for this sub-section has three headings.

| Heading | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Marketing objectives | Know sales volume, sales value, market share, customer retention and repeat sales, brand loyalty, and sales per product, employee, region, store or customer; calculate and interpret data on them |
| Marketing plans | Know the three elements (marketing objectives, marketing mix, marketing budget); explain the purpose and value of a plan and of each element; calculate return on marketing spending |
| External and internal influences | Analyse how market conditions and degrees of competition, resources (budget, skills of employees, capacity) and influences on demand (price, income, competition, marketing activities, number of customers, customer preferences) shape plans, objectives and decisions |

Unit 1 has a calculation section, so the formulas below must be quick and accurate. They are the forms printed in the specification's Appendix: Formulae and Key data.

## Marketing objectives: what each measure tells you

A marketing objective is a target for the marketing function that supports the corporate objectives. Each measure answers a different question, so the skill is choosing the right one for the situation.

| Objective | Formula (9725 appendix) | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Sales volume | number of units sold in a time period | Are we selling more items? |
| Sales value | units sold × average selling price per unit | Are we earning more revenue? |
| Market share (%) | sales of a business ÷ total market sales × 100 | Are we winning against rivals? |
| Customer retention rate (%) | (end period customers − new customers) ÷ start period customers × 100 | Are we keeping the customers we had? |
| Sales per product, employee, region, store, customer | value of total sales ÷ the relevant count | Where is the revenue coming from, and how efficiently? |

**Brand loyalty** has no appendix formula. It is the tendency of customers to keep choosing the same brand, and a business tracks it through repeat purchases, membership schemes or survey answers. Loyal customers are less sensitive to price, so brand loyalty supports a premium price.

**Volume or value?** A firm that cuts prices to sell more can raise volume while value falls. A firm that moves upmarket can raise value with flat volume. When a question gives both, compare their percentage changes: the gap between them tells you what happened to the average price.

**Why market share matters even when sales rise.** If a market grows by 10% and a firm's sales grow by 4%, the firm is losing ground. Market share shows performance relative to rivals, which sales figures alone cannot.

## Worked example 1: Kestrel Kayaks

Kestrel Kayaks sells sea kayaks through four regional showrooms.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Units sold | 3,200 | 3,400 |
| Average selling price | $450 | $480 |
| Total market sales | $18.0m | $19.2m |
| Employees (average) | 24 | 25 |

```
Sales value Y1 = 3,200 × 450 = $1,440,000
Sales value Y2 = 3,400 × 480 = $1,632,000

Volume growth  = 200 ÷ 3,200 × 100       = 6.25%
Value growth   = 192,000 ÷ 1,440,000 × 100 = 13.3% (1 d.p.)

Market share Y1 = 1.44 ÷ 18.0 × 100 = 8.0%
Market share Y2 = 1.632 ÷ 19.2 × 100 = 8.5%
Market growth   = 1.2 ÷ 18.0 × 100   = 6.7% (1 d.p.)

Sales per region Y1 = 1,440,000 ÷ 4 = $360,000
Sales per region Y2 = 1,632,000 ÷ 4 = $408,000
Sales per employee Y1 = 1,440,000 ÷ 24 = $60,000
Sales per employee Y2 = 1,632,000 ÷ 25 = $65,280
```

**Interpretation.** Value grew about twice as fast as volume, so much of the growth came from the higher price. Kestrel's value growth (13.3%) beat market growth (6.7%), so it gained share. Sales per employee rose, so the extra employee did not dilute revenue per head. A good answer then asks a question the data cannot settle: did the price rise reflect a better product, or is it a risk to future volume if rivals stay cheaper?

**Retention.** Kestrel's paddling club started the year with 1,500 members, gained 390 and ended with 1,650.

```
Retention rate = (1,650 − 390) ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 1,260 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 84%
```

So 240 of the original members (16%) left. Total membership rose by 150, which hides that loss. Keeping existing customers is usually cheaper than winning new ones, so a falling retention rate is a warning even when the total is growing.

## Marketing plans

A marketing plan sets out how the marketing function will reach its objectives. In 9725 it has three elements:

1. **Marketing objectives**: the measurable targets, such as raising market share from 8.5% to 9.5% within two years.
2. **Marketing mix**: the decisions on product, price, promotion and place that will deliver the objectives.
3. **Marketing budget**: the money allocated to marketing activities for the period.

**Purpose and value.** The plan links the target to the actions and the money. Objectives give direction and a yardstick for later review. The mix shows how the target will be reached and lets operations, finance and human resources prepare. The budget limits spending, forces priorities and allows results to be compared with cost. The plan's value is limited when the market changes faster than the plan is revised, or when the budget is set by what is affordable rather than by what the objectives need.

### Return on marketing spending

The 9725 appendix gives:

**Return on marketing spending (%) = profit from marketing activities ÷ marketing spend × 100**

It shows how much profit each dollar of marketing generates, so campaigns of different sizes can be compared. Use the profit figure the question attributes to the marketing activities; if it gives extra revenue and a profit margin, calculate the profit first.

## Worked example 2: comparing two campaigns

Kestrel ran two campaigns.

| | Campaign A: trade shows | Campaign B: online video |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing spend | $40,000 | $15,000 |
| Profit from marketing activities | $58,000 | $27,000 |

```
Campaign A = 58,000 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 145%
Campaign B = 27,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = 180%
```

Campaign B earns more profit per dollar spent, but Campaign A earns more profit in total. Whether B is "better" depends on whether it could be scaled up: doubling online spending may not double the profit, because the most interested viewers are reached first. The profit figure is also an estimate, since some of those sales might have happened anyway.

## Influences on marketing plans, objectives and decisions

**Market conditions and degrees of competition.** In a growing market, volume and share targets are realistic because new customers are arriving. In a mature or shrinking market, growth must come from rivals' customers, so retention, loyalty and value targets become more important. With many close competitors, price and promotion decisions are constrained by what rivals do; a dominant firm has more freedom over price.

**Resources.**

- **Budget**: a small budget rules out national advertising and may push the business towards cheaper online promotion.
- **Skills of employees**: a social media campaign needs staff who can create content and read the data it produces.
- **Capacity**: a promotion that generates more orders than operations can supply damages the brand. Marketing objectives must fit what operations can deliver.

**Influences on demand.** Demand depends on price, income, competition, marketing activities, number of customers and customer preferences. Only price and marketing activities are directly under the firm's control, which is why plans need targets that can be revised. A fall in consumer incomes can make a volume target unreachable, however good the mix.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Kestrel's managers want to set a market share objective for next year. Explain one reason why the degree of competition may influence this objective.

> Kestrel has several close competitors selling similar kayaks, so customers can switch easily. **[identifies the influence in context]** If Kestrel sets a high share target, it may have to cut prices to win rivals' customers, and rivals may respond with their own cuts. **[develops a chain of reasoning]** This could raise share but lower sales value and profit, so managers may set a modest share target alongside a profit target. **[consequence for the objective]**

The answer stays on one reason, uses Kestrel's situation and ends with the effect on the objective, which is what the question asked about.

## Common mistakes

- Treating sales value and sales volume as interchangeable.
- Reporting rising sales as success without comparing with market growth.
- Calculating retention as end customers ÷ start customers, which counts new customers as retained.
- Using revenue instead of profit in return on marketing spending.
- Listing the 9625 plan elements (strategy and tactics) instead of the 9725 three.
- Discussing influences on demand in general without saying how one changes the plan in the case.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, the substitution and the answer with units or a % sign. Round only at the end.
- **Explain**: one point, developed with a "because" and a "so" in the context given.
- **Analyse**: build a chain of cause and effect, for example competition → price cuts → lower margin → profit target at risk.
- **Evaluate**: weigh both sides using the case data, then give a judgement that says what it depends on.

When a calculation is followed by a written question, use your figure in the written answer: "Sales per store fell to..." earns more credit than a general statement.
