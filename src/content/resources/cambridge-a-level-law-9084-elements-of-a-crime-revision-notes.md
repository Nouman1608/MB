## The structure of liability

**Actus reus + mens rea = liability** (subject to defences). Actus reus is every element except the mental element; mens rea is the state of mind the offence definition requires, judged subjectively.

**Three parts of actus reus:** conduct (the act), result (the consequence), circumstances (surrounding facts).

## Conduct must be voluntary

- *Hill v Baxter* (1958) – driver attacked by bees would not be acting voluntarily.
- **State of affairs exceptions:** *R v Larsonneur* (1933) – "found" in the UK after being brought in against her will; *Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent* (1983) – "found drunk on a highway" after police put him there. Criticised as liability without fault.

## Omissions

General rule: no liability for failing to act. Exceptions where there is a **legal duty**:

| Duty source | Case |
|---|---|
| Statute | Road Traffic Act 1988 – breath specimen |
| Contract | *R v Pittwood* (1902) |
| Public office | *R v Dytham* (1979) |
| Voluntary assumption | *R v Stone and Dobinson* (1977) |
| Relationship | *R v Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) |
| Created danger | *R v Miller* (1983); *R v Evans* (2009) |

*Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993) – withdrawing treatment is an omission; no duty to continue treatment that is not in the patient's best interests.

Bland treats withdrawing treatment as a lawful omission." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>An omission is criminal only where the defendant was under a legal duty to act.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Causation

**Step 1 – factual:** "but for" – *R v White* (1910) (no causation; attempt only).

**Step 2 – legal:** operating and substantial cause, more than minimal, need not be sole cause – *R v Smith* (1959).

**Step 3 – any break in the chain?**

| Intervening act | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Third party | Reasonable, foreseeable reaction does not break | *R v Pagett* (1983) |
| Medical treatment | Breaks only if so independent and potent that original injury is insignificant | *R v Cheshire* (1991); contrast *R v Jordan* (1956) |
| Victim's escape | Breaks only if "so daft" as to be unforeseeable | *R v Roberts* (1971) |
| Victim's free choice | Free, deliberate, informed act breaks | *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007) |
| Victim's characteristics | Thin skull rule – take the victim as you find them | *R v Blaue* (1975) |

## Intention

- **Direct:** aim or purpose – *R v Mohan* (1975).
- **Oblique:** *Moloney* (1985) → *Hancock and Shankland* (1986) → *Nedrick* (1986) → *R v Woollin* (1998).
- **Woollin test:** jury not entitled to find intention unless the consequence was a **virtual certainty** (barring unforeseen intervention) **and** the defendant **appreciated** that.
- *R v Matthews and Alleyne* (2003) – foresight is evidence of intention, not intention itself.

## Recklessness

- **Subjective test:** *R v Cunningham* (1957) – gas meter.
- *MPC v Caldwell* (1982) – objective test, **overruled**.
- *R v G* (2003) – reckless if **aware of a risk** and it was **unreasonable to take** it in the circumstances known to the defendant.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actus reus | Physical element: conduct, result, circumstances |
| Mens rea | Mental element required by the offence |
| Omission | Failure to act; criminal only where a duty exists |
| Factual causation | "But for" the defendant's act, the result would not have happened |
| Legal causation | Act is an operating and substantial cause |
| *Novus actus interveniens* | New intervening act that breaks the chain |
| Thin skull rule | Defendant takes the victim as found |
| Direct intention | Consequence is the aim or purpose |
| Oblique intention | Consequence virtually certain and appreciated as such |
| Subjective recklessness | Defendant foresaw the risk and unreasonably took it |

## Exam traps

- Stopping at "but for" and forgetting legal causation.
- Assuming poor medical treatment always breaks the chain – *Cheshire* sets a high bar.
- Confusing *Roberts* (victim's reaction foreseeable, chain intact) with *Kennedy (No 2)* (free choice, chain broken).
- Writing that foresight of virtual certainty automatically equals intention.
- Using the *Caldwell* objective test, which no longer applies.
- Describing an omission duty without naming its source.
- Ignoring the requirement that conduct be voluntary.

## Self-test

1. What are the three possible components of an actus reus?
2. Why was Larsonneur convicted, and why is the case criticised?
3. Name the six situations in which a duty to act arises.
4. What did *R v Evans* (2009) add to the duty situations?
5. Why was White not guilty of murder?
6. State the test for legal causation from *R v Smith* (1959).
7. When does medical treatment break the chain of causation?
8. What principle does *R v Blaue* (1975) establish?
9. State the *Woollin* test for oblique intention.
10. What did *R v Matthews and Alleyne* (2003) clarify?
11. What is the definition of recklessness from *R v G* (2003)?
12. Which case did *R v G* overrule, and why?

**Answers**

1. Conduct, result and circumstances.
2. She was "found" in the UK as an alien even though she was brought in against her will; it imposes liability without voluntary conduct or fault.
3. Statute, contract, public office, voluntary assumption of care, special relationship, creating a dangerous situation.
4. A person who contributes to a dangerous situation, such as supplying drugs, may be under a duty to get help.
5. His mother died of a heart attack unrelated to the poison, so "but for" causation failed.
6. The defendant's act must be an operating and substantial cause, though not necessarily the only one.
7. Only where it is so independent of the defendant's act and so potent that the original injury becomes insignificant (*Cheshire*).
8. The thin skull rule: the defendant takes the victim as they find them, including their beliefs.
9. Intention may be found only if the consequence was a virtual certainty, barring unforeseen intervention, and the defendant appreciated this.
10. That foresight of virtual certainty is evidence from which intention may be found, not a substantive definition of intention.
11. The defendant was aware of a risk that the result would occur and it was, in the circumstances known to them, unreasonable to take it.
12. *MPC v Caldwell* (1982), because its objective test convicted people, including children, who had not seen the risk.
