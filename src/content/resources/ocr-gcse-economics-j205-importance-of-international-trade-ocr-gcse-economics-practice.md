These questions cover section 4.1 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Importance of international trade. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [international trade study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-importance-of-international-trade-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [international trade revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-importance-of-international-trade-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by an import. **[1]**

**2.** Explain one reason why a country exports goods. **[2]**

**3.** For each transaction, state whether it is a UK import or a UK export.

**(a)** A UK firm pays an American company for software services. **[1]**
**(b)** A tourist from Japan pays for a hotel stay in York. **[1]**

**4.** An imported jacket costs £80 before tax. A tariff of 12.5% is charged.

**(a)** Calculate the price of the jacket with the tariff. **[1]**
**(b)** The tariff is removed. Calculate the percentage fall in the price paid by consumers, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**5.** Which one of the following is a feature of a customs union but not of a free trade agreement? **[1]**

- A. No tariffs on trade between members
- B. A common tariff on imports from non-members
- C. Trade in goods only
- D. Quotas on trade between members

**6.** Explain one benefit of international trade for consumers. **[2]**

**7.** State what is meant by a quota. **[1]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Country P and Country Q each have 200 workers.

| Per worker | Rice (tonnes) | Computers |
|---|---|---|
| Country P | 12 | 3 |
| Country Q | 3 | 6 |

**(a)** Without trade, each country puts 100 workers on each good. Calculate total output of rice and computers across both countries. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate total output if P makes only rice and Q makes only computers. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the gain in output from specialisation. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why a country may choose not to specialise completely. **[2]**

**9.** Country R joins a free trade agreement. A domestic car maker sells 50,000 cars a year at £18,000 each. An equivalent imported car costs £16,000 before tax, and R charged a 10% tariff on it before joining.

**(a)** Calculate the price of the imported car before and after joining. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the likely effect on the domestic car maker. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one benefit of the agreement for R's exporters. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how international trade can allow producers to benefit from economies of scale. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the benefits and costs for a country of joining a free trade agreement. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether consumers or producers gain more from international trade. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A good or service bought from another country, so money flows out of the importing country [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A country's home market may be too small to buy all it can produce [1]. Selling abroad raises firms' sales and revenue and earns money to pay for imports (or: allows economies of scale) [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Import [1]. **(b)** Export [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow the money. If money leaves the UK, it is an import, whether for a good or a service.

**4. (a)** 80 × 1.125 = **£90** [1].
**(b)** (90 − 80) ÷ 90 × 100 = **11.1%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A 12.5% tariff does not give a 12.5% fall when removed, because the fall is measured from the higher price.

**5.** **B** [1]. A common external tariff is what turns a free trade area into a customs union. **[1]**

**6.** Imports from more efficient producers abroad are often cheaper than domestic goods [1]. Consumers pay less, so their income buys more and living standards rise (or: wider choice, including goods that cannot be produced at home) [1]. **[2]**

**7.** A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported [1]. **[1]**

**8. (a)** P: 1,200 rice and 300 computers; Q: 300 rice and 600 computers [1]. Total = **1,500 tonnes of rice and 900 computers** [1].
**(b)** P: 2,400 tonnes of rice; Q: 1,200 computers [1].
**(c)** **900 tonnes more rice and 300 more computers** [1].
**(d)** Depending on one product is risky: if world demand or prices fall, or supply is disrupted, the whole economy suffers [1]. Keeping some domestic production gives security of supply for essentials such as food (or: transport costs may cancel the gains) [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Before: 16,000 × 1.1 = £17,600; after: £16,000 [1].
**(b)** Before, the import was £400 cheaper than the domestic car; now it is £2,000 cheaper [1], so the domestic maker is likely to lose sales unless it cuts costs or improves quality, and may have to reduce output and jobs [1].
**(c)** Exporters can sell in member countries without tariffs, so their goods become cheaper for foreign buyers [1], raising sales and allowing economies of scale [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Trade gives producers access to a much larger market than the home market alone [1]. Higher sales allow them to produce on a larger scale, for example by buying materials in bulk or using larger, more efficient machinery [1]. Cost per unit falls, so firms can cut prices or earn higher profit, which makes them more competitive [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Benefits: consumers gain lower prices and more choice as tariffs on imports fall [1]. Exporters gain tariff-free access to members' markets, increasing sales and allowing economies of scale [1]. More competition pushes domestic firms to become more efficient [1]. Foreign firms may invest in the country to serve the whole free trade area [1]. Costs: domestic firms that cannot compete lose protection and may close [1], with job losses often concentrated in particular regions [1]. The government loses tariff revenue and some freedom to set trade policy [1]. Judgement: the agreement is likely to benefit the country overall if its industries are competitive and displaced workers can move into growing sectors, but some regions may lose out unless retraining is provided [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Consumers: lower prices, wider choice and better quality benefit almost every household [1], and the gains are widely spread [1]. Producers: exporters gain larger markets and economies of scale, and firms gain cheaper inputs [1], but producers facing import competition may lose sales or close [1]. So producers as a group have both winners and losers, while nearly all consumers gain something [1]. Judgement: consumers gain more consistently, but efficient exporting firms may gain the most per firm, so the answer depends on whether we measure total gains or how evenly they are spread [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating services as if they cannot be traded.
- Confusing tariffs with quotas.
- Calculating the fall in price from the original price rather than the tariff-inclusive price.
- Not saying who gains: consumers or producers.
- Evaluating without a final judgement.
