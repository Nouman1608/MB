"Dictatorship and conflict in the USSR, 1924–53" is Depth Study 5 on Paper 1 of Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE History (4HI1). It covers how Stalin won the struggle to succeed Lenin,
transformed the Soviet economy through industrialisation and collectivisation, ruled through
terror and propaganda, and led the USSR to victory in the Second World War before his death in
1953. As with every depth study, you answer one question in three parts: extract impression,
two effects and a 16-mark essay.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics:

1. **The leadership struggle, 1924–29** — the rivals (Stalin, Trotsky, Kamenev, Bukharin,
   Zinoviev), the strengths and weaknesses of Stalin and Trotsky, Stalin's steps to power and
   the reasons for his success.
2. **Five-year Plans and collectivisation** — Stalin's economic aims, the failings of the NEP,
   Gosplan, the first three Five-year Plans, Stakhanovites, successes and failures;
   collectivisation, kolkhozes, Motor Tractor Stations, the kulaks and the famine of 1932–33.
3. **Purges, show trials, the cult of Stalin and the revision of history** — the murder of Kirov,
   the purges, Yezhov, the NKVD and the gulags, the Moscow Show Trials (1936–38), the purge of the
   armed forces; the cult of personality, censorship, socialist realism, education and the
   Soviet interpretation of history.
4. **Life in the Soviet Union, 1924–41** — living and working conditions in town and countryside,
   social groups, family life, women, education and the persecution of ethnic minorities.
5. **The Second World War and after, 1941–53** — Soviet setbacks in 1941–42, survival and victory,
   war production, Stalingrad, post-war recovery and the Fourth Five-year Plan, Stalin's
   popularity, post-war purges and the USSR's strength in 1953.

## How did Stalin win the leadership struggle?

Lenin died in January 1924. His "Testament" warned that Stalin was too rude to be General
Secretary, but it was not published. **Trotsky** — creator of the Red Army and a brilliant
speaker — seemed the obvious successor, but he was seen as arrogant, had joined the Bolsheviks
only in 1917, and missed Lenin's funeral. **Stalin**, General Secretary since 1922, controlled
appointments and built support among party officials.

Stalin allied with **Zinoviev and Kamenev** to remove Trotsky as Commissar for War (1925). His policy
of "Socialism in One Country" appealed more than Trotsky's "Permanent Revolution". When Zinoviev
and Kamenev joined Trotsky in opposition, Stalin, now backed by **Bukharin** and the Right, had them
expelled from the party (1927); Trotsky was exiled in 1928 and deported in 1929. Stalin then turned
on Bukharin over the NEP, and by 1929 he was unchallenged. Reasons for his success: control of
the party machine, skill at playing rivals off against each other, popular policies and his
opponents' mistakes.

## Why and how did Stalin transform the economy?

Stalin believed the USSR was "fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries" and had to
catch up within ten years or be crushed. The **NEP** had allowed private trade and left grain
supply in peasant hands; the grain procurement crisis of 1927–28 convinced him it had failed.

**Industrialisation** was planned by **Gosplan** through the **Five-year Plans** (1928–32, 1933–37 and
1938–41, the last interrupted by war). They concentrated on heavy industry — coal, steel, oil and
electricity — with huge projects such as Magnitogorsk and the Dnieper dam. In 1935 the miner Alexei
**Stakhanov** was credited with cutting over 100 tonnes of coal in a single shift, and
"Stakhanovites" were held up as models. **Successes:** output of heavy industry rose rapidly and
the USSR built the base that later won the war. **Failures:** poor-quality goods, waste, few
consumer goods, harsh labour discipline and the use of forced labour.

**Collectivisation** from 1929 merged peasant farms into **kolkhozes** (collective farms), which
received machinery from **Motor Tractor Stations**. The **kulaks** (better-off peasants) resisted,
were declared class enemies and were deported or killed. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than
hand it over. Grain requisitioning continued even as harvests fell, producing the **famine of
1932–33**, which killed millions, especially in Ukraine and Kazakhstan. By the late 1930s almost
all farms were collectivised and the state controlled the grain supply, but agriculture remained
inefficient.

## Why did Stalin purge the party, army and people?

The murder of the Leningrad party leader **Kirov** in December 1934 was used to justify mass
arrests. The **Moscow Show Trials** (1936–38) saw old Bolsheviks — Zinoviev, Kamenev and later
Bukharin — confess to invented crimes and be executed. Under **Yezhov**, head of the **NKVD** from
1936 to 1938, the Great Terror reached its height; hundreds of thousands were shot in 1937–38 and
many more sent to the **gulags**, labour camps where prisoners worked in brutal conditions. The
**armed forces** were purged too: Marshal Tukhachevsky was executed in 1937, along with a large share
of senior officers. The purges removed possible rivals and created fear, but they damaged the
economy and weakened the army before the war.

The **cult of personality** presented Stalin as the wise "father of the peoples" through portraits,
statues, songs and place names. **Censorship** and **socialist realism** required art and literature
to glorify Soviet achievement, and **history was rewritten** — for example, in the *Short Course*
history of the party (1938) — to exaggerate Stalin's role and erase his enemies.

## What was life like in the USSR, 1924–41?

In the towns, workers faced overcrowded housing, food shortages and strict discipline, though the
regime offered free education, healthcare and opportunities for promotion. In the countryside
collectivisation brought famine and hardship. Party officials and skilled workers enjoyed
privileges. **Women** gained new opportunities in education and work, but in 1936 abortion was
banned and divorce made harder as the regime promoted the family. **Education** expanded and
literacy rose, with lessons stressing loyalty to Stalin. **Ethnic minorities** were persecuted: whole
peoples, such as Soviet Koreans in 1937, were deported, and national cultures were suppressed.

## How did the USSR survive and win the Second World War?

Germany invaded on **22 June 1941** (Operation Barbarossa). Setbacks in 1941–42 were caused by
surprise, the effects of the military purges and Stalin's refusal to believe warnings. The USSR
survived because Moscow held in December 1941, factories were moved east of the Urals, **war
production** soared, Lend-Lease aid arrived from the Allies, and the population endured huge losses.
**Stalingrad** (August 1942–February 1943) ended in the surrender of the German Sixth Army and was the
turning point. Soviet forces reached Berlin in 1945. About 27 million Soviet citizens died.

After the war the **Fourth Five-year Plan** (1946–50) rebuilt heavy industry. Stalin's prestige as
the victor was at its height, yet repression returned: returning prisoners of war were sent to camps,
the Leningrad Affair purged officials, and the "Doctors' Plot" (1953) targeted mainly Jewish doctors.
When Stalin died in March 1953, the USSR was a nuclear superpower controlling Eastern Europe, but
its people were poor and many lived in fear.

## Frequently asked questions

**Was Stalin's success in 1924–29 down to Trotsky's weaknesses?**
Partly. A strong answer weighs
Trotsky's mistakes against Stalin's control of the party machine and his tactics.

**Were the Five-year Plans a success?**
For heavy industry, largely yes; for living standards and
consumer goods, no. Say which criterion you are using.

**What is the difference between the NKVD and the gulag?**
The NKVD was the secret police; the gulag
was the system of labour camps it ran.
