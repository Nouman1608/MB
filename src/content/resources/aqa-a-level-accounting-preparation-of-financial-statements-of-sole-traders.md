This guide covers section 3.6, Preparation of financial statements of sole traders, of the AQA A-level Accounting (7127) specification (version 1.1, October 2026, for A-level exams from June 2019 onwards). The qualification is not tiered. The specification places section 3.6 in the content assessed by Paper 1, a 3-hour written exam, and you must use a calculator in the exam.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-preparation-of-financial-statements-of-sole-traders-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-preparation-of-financial-statements-of-sole-traders-practice/). It builds on [the double entry model](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-double-entry-model/) and the concepts in [the role of the accountant](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-role-of-the-accountant/). See also the [AQA A-level Accounting hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/accounting/), the [7127 checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/accounting/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## What section 3.6 covers

| Specification content (3.6) | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The use of concepts in the preparation of financial statements | Record accruals and prepayments, depreciation (straight line and reducing balance), disposal of non-current assets, provision for doubtful debts, irrecoverable debts and their recovery, income due, income received in advance, goods taken for own use, goods on sale or return, and inventory |
| Financial statements of sole traders from ledger accounts, including adjustments | Prepare statements for service or trading businesses, and record the adjustments in ledger accounts |
| Income statements and statements of financial position from a trial balance, including adjustments | Turn a trial balance plus notes into both statements |

The specification notes that FIFO, AVCO and LIFO inventory valuation will not be examined.

## Why adjustments exist

A trial balance records what has been entered in the books. The financial statements must show the income earned and expenses incurred in the period, and assets at a prudent value. Four concepts drive almost every adjustment:

- **Accruals** -- match income and expenses to the period they relate to, whatever the cash timing.
- **Prudence** -- do not overstate assets or profit (doubtful debts, inventory at net realisable value).
- **Realisation** -- revenue is recognised when it is earned, not when goods leave the premises (sale or return).
- **Business entity** -- the owner's private use of business resources is drawings, not an expense.

Every adjustment has **two effects**: one in the income statement and one in the statement of financial position. Train yourself to write both.

An **accrued expense** has been incurred but not yet paid: add it to the expense and show it as a current liability. A **prepaid expense** has been paid for a later period: deduct it from the expense and show it as a current asset.

**Worked example: heating account.** At 1 January 2025 heating of £240 was owing. During 2025 Holme Studio paid £2,610. At 31 December 2025, £310 is owing.

```
Heating account
2025                    £      2025                     £
Bank               2,610      1 Jan Balance b/d         240
31 Dec Balance c/d   310      31 Dec Income statement 2,680
                   2,920                              2,920
                              2026 1 Jan Balance b/d    310
```

The charge is 2,610 - 240 + 310 = **£2,680**. The £240 paid this year belonged to last year, so it is not this year's expense. The credit balance of £310 carried down is the accrual in the statement of financial position.

## Income due and income received in advance

These mirror expense adjustments. **Income due** (earned, not yet received) is added to the income and shown as a current asset. **Income received in advance** is deducted from the income and shown as a current liability.

**Worked example.** A sole trader sublets a storeroom and received £6,500 rent during the year, of which £500 is for next year. Rent receivable in the income statement is 6,500 - 500 = **£6,000**; the £500 is a current liability.

## Depreciation

Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over the periods that use it (accruals).

- **Straight line:** (cost - residual value) / useful life, or a stated % of cost. The charge is the same every year.
- **Reducing balance:** a fixed % of the carrying amount (cost - accumulated depreciation). The charge falls every year.

Entries: debit depreciation (income statement), credit provision for depreciation. In the statement of financial position show cost, less accumulated depreciation, equals carrying amount. Always read the question's policy, for example a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal.

## Disposal of non-current assets

Close the asset's cost and accumulated depreciation to a disposal account:

1. Debit disposal, credit asset account (cost).
2. Debit provision for depreciation, credit disposal (accumulated depreciation).
3. Debit bank, credit disposal (proceeds).
4. The balance is the profit (balance on debit side) or loss (balance on credit side) on disposal, taken to the income statement.

**Worked example.** A machine cost £18,000 and has accumulated depreciation of £10,800. It is sold for £6,000.

```
Disposal account
                          £                                   £
Machinery (cost)     18,000    Provision for depreciation 10,800
                               Bank                        6,000
                               Income statement (loss)     1,200
                     18,000                               18,000
```

The carrying amount was 18,000 - 10,800 = £7,200 and the proceeds £6,000, so the **loss is £1,200**, shown as an expense.

## Irrecoverable debts, their recovery and the provision for doubtful debts

An **irrecoverable debt** is one the business is sure it will not collect. Debit irrecoverable debts (expense), credit the customer's account in the sales ledger.

If a written-off debt is later **recovered**, reinstate it so the customer's record is complete, then record the cash:

```
Dr Customer account (sales ledger)     Cr Irrecoverable debts recovered
Dr Bank                                Cr Customer account
```

The recovery is **income** in the year it is received. You do not reopen the earlier year.

A **provision for doubtful debts** is an estimate (prudence) of debts that may not be paid, often a % of trade receivables after writing off irrecoverable debts. Only the **change** in the provision goes to the income statement: an increase is an expense, a decrease is income. The full provision is deducted from trade receivables in the statement of financial position.

## Goods taken for own use

When the owner takes goods for private use, debit drawings and credit purchases **at cost**. This follows business entity: the goods were not used to earn revenue, so they must not sit in cost of sales.

## Goods on sale or return

Goods sent to a customer on sale or return remain the seller's property until the customer accepts them (realisation). If they have been recorded as a sale before acceptance:

- reduce revenue and trade receivables by the selling price;
- add the goods to closing inventory at cost (or net realisable value if lower).

## Inventory

Closing inventory is valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value** (NRV = expected selling price less costs to complete and sell). This applies prudence item by item. Closing inventory is deducted in cost of sales and is a current asset.

1. Inventory counted at cost £16,200 includes goods costing £800 with an NRV of £500.
2. Goods sent on sale or return on 28 March at selling price £1,500 (cost £1,000) were recorded as a credit sale. The customer has not accepted them. They are not in the inventory count.
3. Tamsin took goods costing £1,200 for her own use. Nothing was recorded.
4. Rent of £800 is prepaid; electricity of £350 is accrued; commission of £300 is due.
5. Write off a further irrecoverable debt of £400. The provision for doubtful debts is 5% of trade receivables.
6. Depreciate fixtures at 10% straight line on cost and the van at 25% reducing balance.

## Common errors

- Adding the opening accrual to this year's expense instead of deducting it.
- Charging the whole new provision for doubtful debts, rather than the change, to the income statement.
- Calculating the provision before writing off the extra irrecoverable debt.
- Valuing goods on sale or return at selling price instead of cost.
- Putting goods for own use through the income statement instead of drawings.
- Applying reducing balance to cost rather than to the carrying amount.
- Showing drawings as an expense.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are FIFO, AVCO and LIFO examined in AQA A-Level Accounting 7127 section 3.6?**
No. The specification notes that FIFO, AVCO and LIFO inventory valuation will not be examined. Closing inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

**How do you calculate an expense charge with accruals?**
Start from the cash paid, deduct any opening accrual (it belonged to last year) and add the closing accrual. For example, £2,610 paid − £240 opening accrual + £310 closing accrual gives a charge of £2,680.

**How do you record the recovery of an irrecoverable debt?**
Reinstate the debt by debiting the customer and crediting irrecoverable debts recovered, then debit bank and credit the customer. The recovery is income in the year it is received; the earlier year is not reopened.

**How are goods taken by the owner for own use recorded?**
Debit drawings and credit purchases at cost, following the business entity concept.

**What are the steps in a disposal account?**
Debit disposal with the asset's cost, credit disposal with the accumulated depreciation and the proceeds, and take the balancing figure to the income statement as a profit or loss on disposal.
