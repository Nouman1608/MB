This guide covers subtopic **6.2 Leaf structure** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to state that most leaves have a large surface area and are thin, and to explain how these are adaptations for photosynthesis. You must also identify and label the cuticle, and the cellular and tissue structures of a dicotyledonous leaf as seen in diagrams or photomicrographs, and explain how they are adapted for photosynthesis and gas exchange. The structures are: stomata and guard cells, spongy and palisade mesophyll cells, air spaces, vascular bundles (xylem and phloem), the distribution of chloroplasts, and the upper and lower epidermis.

The [Plant nutrition guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-plant-nutrition/) covers the whole topic. This page goes through the leaf layer by layer, as an examiner would, and shows how to turn each structure into a full-mark explanation. Use the [6.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-leaf-structure-revision-notes/) for recall and the [6.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-leaf-structure-practice/) to test yourself.

## Why leaves are large and thin

A typical leaf is a flat blade with a large surface area and very little thickness. Both features help photosynthesis.

- **Large surface area**: a wide blade catches more light, and the large area gives more room for stomata, so more carbon dioxide can enter by diffusion.
- **Thin**: light can pass through to reach all the cells, and gases have only a short distance to diffuse to reach the cells deep inside the leaf. A short diffusion distance means a faster rate of diffusion.

## A transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf

Reading from the top of the leaf to the bottom, the layers are:

1. waxy **cuticle**
2. **upper epidermis**
3. **palisade mesophyll**
4. **spongy mesophyll**, with **air spaces**
5. **lower epidermis**, with **stomata** and **guard cells**

The **vascular bundle** (a vein) runs through the middle, between the palisade and spongy layers. In a midrib it is large; in the rest of the leaf there are many small veins.

### Cuticle

The cuticle is a **waxy, waterproof layer** on the outside of the epidermis. It reduces water loss by evaporation from the leaf surface. It is transparent, so light passes through.

### Upper epidermis

The upper epidermis is a single layer of cells that fit closely together. The cells are **transparent** and contain **no chloroplasts**, so light passes through them to the palisade cells below. The layer also protects the leaf.

### Palisade mesophyll

Palisade cells are **tall, narrow cells packed closely together** in a layer just under the upper epidermis. They are the main site of photosynthesis.

- They contain **many chloroplasts**, so they absorb a lot of light.
- They are near the top of the leaf, where the light is brightest.
- Their long shape lets light pass through fewer cell walls on the way to the chloroplasts.
- Their narrow shape lets many cells fit in a given area.

### Spongy mesophyll and air spaces

Spongy mesophyll cells are **rounded and loosely arranged**, with large **air spaces** between them. They have fewer chloroplasts than palisade cells, but still photosynthesise.

- The air spaces allow **carbon dioxide** to diffuse from the stomata to the photosynthesising cells, and **oxygen** to diffuse out.
- The cells are covered with a thin film of water, in which gases dissolve before moving into the cells.
- Water evaporates from the surfaces of these cells into the air spaces, which links to transpiration.
- The large internal surface area helps gas exchange.

### Lower epidermis, stomata and guard cells

The lower epidermis has many small pores called **stomata** (one is a stoma). Each stoma is surrounded by two **guard cells**, which control whether it is open or closed.

- Stomata let **carbon dioxide** in and **oxygen** and water vapour out, by diffusion.
- Guard cells contain **chloroplasts**, unlike other epidermal cells.
- When guard cells take in water, they become turgid, their shape changes, and the stoma **opens**. When they lose water they become flaccid and the stoma **closes**, which cuts down water loss.
- Most stomata are on the **lower** surface. It is cooler and out of direct sunlight, so less water evaporates through them.

### Vascular bundle: xylem and phloem

The vascular bundle carries materials to and from the leaf cells.

- **Xylem** brings **water and mineral ions** to the mesophyll cells, and also supports the leaf.
- **Phloem** carries away **sucrose** (and amino acids) made in the leaf to other parts of the plant.

A dense network of veins means that no cell is far from a supply of water, and sucrose can be removed quickly.

## Distribution of chloroplasts

Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis. They are **not spread evenly** through the leaf.

| Cell type | Chloroplasts | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Palisade mesophyll | Many, mostly | Main site of photosynthesis, close to the light |
| Spongy mesophyll | Fewer | Receives less light |
| Guard cells | Some | The only epidermal cells that contain them |
| Upper and lower epidermis (other cells) | None | Transparent, to let light through |

## Worked example: explaining a structure

*Explain how the structure of a palisade mesophyll cell is adapted for photosynthesis.*

Use "feature, then function" pairs.

1. Contains **many chloroplasts**, so it absorbs a lot of light energy.
2. Tall and narrow with a **cylindrical** shape, so light passes through only a few cell walls and many cells fit near the surface.
3. Placed just **under the upper epidermis**, where light is most intense.
4. Thin cell wall and film of water, so carbon dioxide can dissolve and diffuse in quickly.

A weaker answer says "it has chloroplasts for photosynthesis". It names the structure but gives no reason why having more of it helps.

## Worked example: magnification on a leaf photomicrograph

*A photomicrograph of a leaf section has a magnification of 400. The leaf thickness on the image measures 60 mm. Calculate the actual thickness of the leaf in millimetres and in micrometres.*

Use the formula **actual size = image size divided by magnification**.

1. Actual thickness = 60 divided by 400 = 0.15 mm.
2. There are 1000 micrometres in one millimetre, so 0.15 x 1000 = **150 micrometres**.

Always show the formula, then the substitution, then the unit.

## Putting it together: gas exchange and photosynthesis

- Carbon dioxide diffuses in through the **stomata**, through the **air spaces**, dissolves in the water film and enters the **mesophyll cells**.
- Water arrives in the **xylem**, and moves into the mesophyll cells by osmosis.
- Light passes through the **cuticle** and **epidermis** to the **chloroplasts**.
- Glucose is made, then turned into sucrose, which leaves by the **phloem**.
- Oxygen diffuses out through the air spaces and stomata.

## Reading diagrams and photomicrographs

Questions usually give a labelled diagram or a photomicrograph of a transverse section (a slice cut across the leaf). Use these clues to name the layers.

- **Order from the top**: if you know which surface is the top, the layers follow the sequence cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade, spongy, lower epidermis.
- **Palisade**: look for the layer of tall, neat, column-like cells. They are the most densely packed and usually contain the most dots or green discs, which are chloroplasts.
- **Spongy mesophyll**: look for rounded cells with obvious white gaps between them. The gaps are the air spaces.
- **Stomata**: gaps in the lower epidermis flanked by two bean-shaped cells that are darker because they contain chloroplasts.
- **Vascular bundle**: a rounded group of cells in the middle of the leaf. The xylem is on the upper side of the bundle and the phloem is below it, towards the lower surface.
- **Cuticle**: a thin waxy line on the outer surface of the epidermis, which may not be visible on a low-power photomicrograph.

A common trap is the epidermis, which can look like a single line of cells with no content. The cells are transparent and have a large vacuole, so they look empty compared with the mesophyll. This is exactly the adaptation that lets light through.

## Feature and function pairs to learn

| Feature | Function in photosynthesis or gas exchange |
|---|---|
| Large surface area | Absorbs more light; more stomata for carbon dioxide |
| Thin blade | Short diffusion distance; light reaches all cells |
| Waxy cuticle | Reduces water loss |
| Transparent epidermis | Lets light through to the mesophyll |
| Many chloroplasts in palisade cells | Absorb much light energy |
| Air spaces in spongy mesophyll | Let gases diffuse quickly to and from cells |
| Stomata | Let carbon dioxide in and oxygen out |
| Guard cells | Open and close the stoma to control gas exchange and water loss |
| Xylem | Brings water to the cells; supports the leaf |
| Phloem | Takes away sucrose |

## Common mistakes

- Saying the epidermis "has chloroplasts". Only the guard cells do.
- Writing that the palisade cells are in the lower part of the leaf.
- Saying stomata "let air in and out" without naming carbon dioxide and oxygen, or water vapour.
- Describing the air spaces as holes in the cells. They lie between the cells.
- Saying xylem carries sucrose.
- Giving a feature without its function.
- Labelling the vascular bundle as a "vein cell" without naming xylem and phloem.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name a structure shown on a diagram.
- **State**: give the adaptation briefly.
- **Explain**: give the feature and the reason it helps.
- **Calculate**: work out the actual size, showing the formula.
