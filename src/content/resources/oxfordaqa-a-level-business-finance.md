## 3.2.3.1 Sources of finance

**Internal** finance comes from within the business; **external** finance comes from outside it.

| Internal source | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profits | No interest, no loss of control | Only exists if the business has made profit; shareholders get smaller dividends |
| Sale of assets | Turns unused assets into cash | Asset is gone; a forced sale may get a low price |
| Sale and leaseback | Releases a large sum while keeping use of the asset | Lease payments continue; the business no longer owns the asset |
| Working capital | Cutting inventory or chasing customers frees cash already tied up | Limited amounts; too little inventory can lose sales |

| External source | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Trade credit | Use supplies before paying for them | Late payment damages supplier relations; may lose discounts |
| Share capital | No repayment, no interest; large sums possible | Dilutes control; owners expect dividends |
| Overdraft | Flexible; interest only on what is used | High interest; the bank can withdraw it |
| Loans | Fixed repayments make planning easier | Interest; security often needed; repayment is due whatever happens to sales |
| Business angels | Money plus experience and contacts | Give up a share of ownership and some control |
| Private equity | Large sums for growth or restructuring | Investors may demand control and a fast return |
| Crowd funding | Reaches many small backers; tests demand | Campaign may miss its target; idea is public |

**Matching the source to the situation.** Ask four questions: how much is needed, for how long, can the business repay it, and will the owners give up control? A start-up has no retained profits and no trading record, so banks may lend only with security; business angels, crowd funding and the owner's own share capital are common answers. A short-term cash gap suits an overdraft or trade credit. A long-life asset such as machinery suits a loan, because repayments are spread over the asset's working life.

**Worked example.** Quillcote Print Studio, a sole trader with three years of profits and a workshop it owns outright, needs £40,000 for a large-format printer that will last eight years. *Judgement:* a bank loan fits -- the amount is fixed, the printer earns revenue for years, and the trading record and the workshop as security support borrowing; retained profit could cover part of it. Sale and leaseback of the workshop would raise more but adds rent for good, which is costly for a £40,000 need. An overdraft would be long-term debt repayable on demand.

## 3.2.3.2 Break-even analysis

| Term | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Do not change with output (rent, salaries) |
| Variable costs | Variable cost per unit × number of units |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Revenue (turnover) | Price per unit × quantity sold |
| Profit | Total revenue − total costs |
| Contribution per unit | Selling price per unit − variable cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Contribution per unit × number of units sold |
| Break-even output | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety | Actual output − break-even output |

**Worked example.** Emberfold Candles sells candles at £18 each. Variable cost is £7.50 per candle and fixed costs are £8,400 a month. It sells 1,150 candles a month.

```
Contribution per unit = 18 − 7.50 = £10.50
Break-even output     = 8,400 ÷ 10.50 = 800 candles
Margin of safety      = 1,150 − 800 = 350 candles
Revenue     = 18 × 1,150 = £20,700
Total costs = 8,400 + (7.50 × 1,150) = 8,400 + 8,625 = £17,025
Profit      = 20,700 − 17,025 = £3,675
Check: profit = margin of safety × contribution = 350 × 10.50 = £3,675
```

At 600 candles the business makes a loss: total contribution is 600 × 10.50 = £6,300, which is £2,100 short of fixed costs.

**Interpreting the chart.** A break-even chart plots revenue and total costs against output.

| Output (candles) | 0 | 400 | 800 | 1,200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (£) | 0 | 7,200 | 14,400 | 21,600 |
| Total costs (£) | 8,400 | 11,400 | 14,400 | 17,400 |

The fixed-cost line is flat at £8,400. Total costs start at £8,400 and rise by the variable cost per unit. The lines cross at 800 candles and £14,400: break-even. The vertical gap between the lines is the loss (left of 800) or the profit (right of 800).

**Manipulating the chart.** If the price rises to £19.50, the revenue line becomes steeper, contribution rises to £12 and break-even falls to 8,400 ÷ 12 = 700 candles. If instead fixed costs rise to £9,240, the fixed-cost and total-cost lines shift up in parallel and break-even rises to 9,240 ÷ 10.50 = 880 candles. A rise in variable cost per unit makes the total-cost line steeper.

**Significance.** Break-even shows the minimum sales a plan needs and the margin of safety shows how far sales can fall before losses start. It assumes every unit is sold at one price and that costs are linear, so treat it as a planning guide. Pricing links to the [Marketing study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-business-marketing/).

## 3.2.3.3 Profit and cash

### Profit and profit margins

**Profit** is revenue minus costs. It rewards owners, funds growth through retained profits and signals to lenders and investors that the business is viable.

```
Gross profit     = Revenue − Cost of sales
Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses
Net profit       = Operating profit − Interest − Taxes
Margin (%)       = Profit ÷ Revenue × 100   (for each level)
```

**Worked example.** Quarrymead Tiles Ltd: revenue £640,000; cost of sales £384,000; operating expenses £172,800; interest £9,600; taxes £16,000.

```
Gross profit     = 640,000 − 384,000 = £256,000   margin 256,000 ÷ 640,000 × 100 = 40%
Operating profit = 256,000 − 172,800 = £83,200    margin 13%
Net profit       = 83,200 − 9,600 − 16,000 = £57,600   margin 9%
```

The gap between a 40% gross margin and a 13% operating margin tells you overheads, not production costs, absorb most of the gross profit.

**Profit vs profitability.** Profit is an absolute figure; profitability measures profit relative to revenue (a margin). A firm earning £90,000 on £1.5 million revenue (6% margin) makes more profit but is less profitable than one earning £60,000 on £400,000 (15%).

**Improving profit and profitability.**

| Method | Advantage | Challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Raise price | Higher margin per unit | Volume may fall if demand is price elastic |
| Cut variable costs | Every unit earns more | Cheaper inputs may lower quality |
| Cut fixed costs | Lower break-even | Fewer staff or less marketing can cut sales |
| Increase sales volume | Spreads fixed costs | Promotion costs money and may need capacity |

### Budgeting

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period. **Variance = actual figure − budgeted figure.** A variance is **favourable** if it raises profit (revenue above budget, or costs below budget) and **adverse** if it lowers profit.

**Worked example.** Brackenmoor Bakery, one month:

| Item | Budget (£) | Actual (£) | Variance (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 48,000 | 51,600 | 3,600 F |
| Ingredients | 14,400 | 16,200 | 1,800 A |
| Wages | 12,000 | 11,400 | 600 F |
| Rent | 6,000 | 6,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 15,600 | 18,000 | 2,400 F |

The profit variance equals the net of the others: 3,600 − 1,800 + 600 = 2,400 favourable. Higher sales partly explain the ingredients overspend, so interpret a variance before blaming anyone.

**Value.** Budgets set targets, allocate spending and help coordinate departments. Variance analysis shows where to act early. But budgets based on poor forecasts mislead, and rigid budgets can stop managers responding to opportunities.

### Cash flow forecasts

**Cash** pays wages, suppliers and lenders when bills fall due; a profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash. A **cash flow forecast** predicts monthly inflows and outflows. It shows when the business may need an overdraft, supports a loan application and lets managers plan.

```
Net cash flow   = Total inflows − Total outflows
Closing balance = Opening balance + Net cash flow
Opening balance = Closing balance from the previous period
```

**Worked example.** Copperleaf Bike Repairs, opening balance £2,500.

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash sales | 5,000 | 4,600 | 7,400 |
| Receipts from credit customers | 4,000 | 3,800 | 5,200 |
| **Total inflows** | 9,000 | 8,400 | 12,600 |
| Supplies | 4,200 | 5,000 | 3,800 |
| Wages | 3,600 | 3,600 | 3,600 |
| Rent and other | 2,400 | 2,400 | 2,400 |
| **Total outflows** | 10,200 | 11,000 | 9,800 |
| Net cash flow | (1,200) | (2,600) | 2,800 |
| Opening balance | 2,500 | 1,300 | (1,300) |
| Closing balance | 1,300 | (1,300) | 1,500 |

Brackets show negatives. Month 2 closes £1,300 overdrawn, so Copperleaf should arrange an overdraft of at least that before month 2, or delay the large supplies order.

**Timing: payables and receivables.** **Receivables** are amounts customers owe the business; **payables** are amounts it owes suppliers.

```
Receivables days = Trade receivables ÷ Revenue × 365
Payables days    = Trade payables ÷ Cost of sales × 365
```

A firm with revenue £730,000, trade receivables £70,000, cost of sales £438,000 and trade payables £24,000 has receivables days of 70,000 ÷ 730,000 × 365 = 35 and payables days of 24,000 ÷ 438,000 × 365 = 20. It pays suppliers about 15 days before customers pay it, so it must finance that gap.

**Improving cash flow.**

| Method | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Debt factoring | Immediate cash for invoices | The factor keeps a fee, cutting profit |
| Shorter payment terms for customers | Cash arrives sooner | Customers may go to rivals |
| Early payment incentives | Encourages prompt payment | Discount reduces revenue |
| Credit checks | Fewer bad debts | Slower sales; some customers refused |
| Longer payment time to suppliers | Cash stays in the business longer | Suppliers may refuse or raise prices |
| Reducing outgoings | Lower outflows every month | Cuts can harm quality or capacity |
| Better invoice management | Invoices sent promptly and chased | Needs staff time or software |

## Common errors

- Dividing fixed costs by price instead of by contribution per unit.
- Rounding break-even down: 484.6 units means 485 must be sold.
- Using operating profit in the net profit margin.
- Calling every positive variance favourable; a cost above budget is adverse.
- Forgetting that each month's opening balance is the previous closing balance.
- Treating profit and cash as the same: credit sales create profit before cash arrives.
