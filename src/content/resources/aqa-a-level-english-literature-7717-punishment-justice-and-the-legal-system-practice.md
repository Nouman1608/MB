## Section A: the element in the set texts

different set texts. **[4]**

## Section B: an unseen extract and essay skills

**Unseen extract (original prose).** A young labourer, Tom Reece, is on trial for stealing a coat
from a shop in winter.

> The court was warm, which Tom had not expected. He stood where they told him and looked at the
> floor, which had been polished so well that he could see the judge in it, upside down. Counsel for
> the prosecution spoke for a long time about the sanctity of property. He used the word "sanctity"
> four times. Tom counted. The shopkeeper said the coat was worth nine shillings. The judge asked Tom
> whether he had anything to say, and Tom said that it had been very cold. There was a little
> laughter at the back. The judge did not laugh. He wrote something down, and then he looked at Tom
> for the first time, over his spectacles, as a man looks at a clock to see whether it is late.
> "Six months," he said, and the warmth went out of the room.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents justice in the extract. **[8]**

**7.** Explain how the writer controls the reader's sympathy for Tom. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the significance of the final sentence. **[3]**

**9.** Explain one connection between the extract and a set text in the way the legal system is
presented. **[3]**

**10.** A student writes: "Hamlet is about revenge." Explain two ways to develop this into an argument
about justice. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer for the question: "Crime writing shows that justice is rarely done by
the law." List four elements of your plan, naming two texts not used in your Section B answer.
**[4]**

**12.** Explain how context (AO3) could strengthen an argument about punishment in one set text.
**[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Legal justice is delivered by courts, police and prisons [1]; example [1]: Fagin's trial and
execution in *Oliver Twist*, or Robbie's conviction in *Atonement*. Retributive justice is revenge or
punishment taken into private hands [1]; example [1]: Hamlet's revenge on Claudius, or the poisoner
in "The Laboratory".

**2.** Mr Fang bullies witnesses and nearly convicts an innocent, fainting child [1]; the court, meant
to protect the innocent, is presented as crueller than the thieves [1]; Oliver is saved only when the
bookseller insists on testifying, so justice depends on chance rather than the system [1].

**3.** Robbie is accused on the word of a thirteen-year-old who misread what she saw [1]; his class
makes the accusation easier for others to believe [1]; the real assailant, Paul Marshall, goes
unpunished and marries Lola, so the legal system confirms an injustice instead of correcting it [1].

**4.** It fulfils the expectation because the murderer is exposed and dies [1], but bypasses it
because Poirot lets him take his own life rather than face a trial, so justice is private [1].

**5.** Its refrain that each man kills the thing he loves suggests that all people share the
condemned man's guilt in some form [1], so the reader is invited to question who has the right to
judge and punish [1].

**6.** Any eight, [1] each:

- The unexpected warmth of the court contrasts with the cold that drove Tom to steal.
- Tom stands "where they told him", showing his powerlessness.
- Seeing the judge "upside down" in the polished floor suggests a world in which justice is inverted.
- The polished floor suggests wealth and order, set against Tom's poverty.
- Counsel's repeated "sanctity of property" gives property an almost religious status.
- "Tom counted" shows him attending to words rather than to his fate, and mocks the rhetoric.
- The precise value, "nine shillings", contrasts a small sum with the severity of the sentence.
- Tom's defence, "it had been very cold", is simple and true, and the laughter makes it seem naive.
- "The judge did not laugh" builds tension and suggests indifference rather than mercy.
- The simile of looking at a clock presents Tom as an object to be processed.
- The short sentence "Six months" is abrupt, matching the brevity of the judgement.

**7.** The narrative follows Tom's perspective, so the reader sees the court through his eyes [1]; his
plain words and small observations make him seem innocent in spirit [1]; and the contrast between
the cold motive and the warm, wealthy court makes the punishment seem disproportionate [1].

**8.** It returns to the opening's warmth [1], so the cyclical image frames the trial [1], and the loss
of warmth suggests that the sentence returns Tom to cold and hardship, making the law's judgement
feel like a physical punishment [1].

**9.** A connection identified [1], supported by reference to a set text [1], with an explanation [1]:
for example, like Oliver before Mr Fang, Tom faces a court indifferent to a poor defendant's
suffering, so both texts satirise justice that protects property over people.

**10.** Two developments, [2] each for a developed point or [1] for a partial one: argue that the
law cannot act because the murderer is the king, so private retribution is the only route; weigh
whether revenge is justice or a second crime, using the deaths it causes; use Laertes as a foil to
show the dangers of swift revenge; consider divine justice, as in Hamlet's refusal to kill Claudius
at prayer.

**11.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis on "rarely"; two named texts different from the Section B text; a
paragraph comparing failures or absences of legal justice; a paragraph on cases where the law does
act; contexts for each text; an alternative interpretation; a comparative judgement.

**12.** A relevant context [1] tied to a specific scene or effect [1]: for example, public executions
outside Newgate help explain the crowd's appetite at Fagin's trial, supporting the argument that
Dickens presents punishment as spectacle.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about "justice" without naming the kind.
- Assuming the law is presented as fair.
- Unseen answers that list techniques without an argument about justice.
- In Section C, reusing the Section B text.

For full practice, write a timed Section A answer of about an hour on the unseen extract above.
