These revision notes condense Criterion C (Communicating) and Criterion D (Applying mathematics in real-life contexts) from the IB MYP *Mathematics guide* (for use from September 2020 and January 2021, first assessment 2022), using the Year 1, Year 3 and Year 5 criteria. ## The criteria at a glance

- Each criterion has a maximum of 8, in four bands: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-8.
- Teachers use best fit across the strands.
- Every strand of every criterion is assessed at least twice in each MYP year.
- The guide says Criterion C is often assessed on constructed responses and reports, alongside B or D.
- Criterion D is often assessed through modelling or curve-fitting tasks in authentic contexts.

## Criterion C strands

| Strand | Skill | What earns the top band |
|---|---|---|
| i | Language | consistently correct notation, symbols, terminology |
| ii | Representation | forms chosen to suit the information and used correctly |
| iii | Moving between forms | effective moves (Years 3 and 5 only) |
| iv | Reasoning | Year 1 coherent; Year 3 complete and coherent; Year 5 complete, coherent and concise |
| v | Structure | work consistently organised in a logical order |

Year 1 does not demonstrate strand iii at any band.

## Criterion D strands

| Strand | Skill | Year 1 | Year 3 | Year 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| i | Identify elements | identify | identify | identify |
| ii | Select strategies | select | select | select |
| iii | Apply strategies | apply | apply | apply |
| iv | Accuracy | explain | explain | justify |
| v | Makes sense | describe | explain | justify |

Strands i to iii do not change in wording across years. The mathematics you apply gets harder.

## Command terms (paraphrased from the guide)

| Term | Meaning | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| Describe | detailed account of a situation or process | say what happened or what the answer looks like |
| Explain | account including reasons or causes | say why |
| Justify | valid reasons or evidence supporting a conclusion | give reasons plus numbers or facts as evidence |
| Discuss | balanced review of several arguments or factors | weigh both sides, then conclude |
| Show | give the steps of a calculation or derivation | display every step |
| Verify | provide evidence that validates a result | substitute or check independently |

## Notation reminders

These come from the guide's conventions for on-screen examinations, and they are good habits in every task.

- Write mathematical notation, not calculator notation. For example 4.5 × 10³, not 4.5E3.
- Use superscripts and subscripts correctly: m², x₁.
- Unless told otherwise, give final answers to three significant figures.
- Estimate by rounding. Truncating does not count as estimating.
- Use the equals sign only between quantities that are equal. Do not chain unrelated results.

## Method: communicating a solution

1. State what you are finding and define any variable with units.
2. Choose a representation that fits the information.
3. Show one idea per line, in order.
4. Write the result with units and the right rounding.
5. Finish with a sentence that answers the original question.
6. Check that someone else could follow it without asking you anything.

## Method: applying mathematics

1. List the relevant facts. Cross out irrelevant ones (identify).
2. Choose a model and say why it fits (select).
3. Carry it out accurately (apply).
4. State the degree of accuracy and its cause (explain), then add evidence such as a range or an error size (justify).
5. Compare the answer with the real situation: size, units, whole numbers, limits (makes sense).

## Moving between forms: reminder

For a rule such as C = 18 + 22m: table rows give 40, 62, 84 for m = 1, 2, 3 after adding 18 to each 22m. The graph is a line with intercept 18 and gradient 22. In words, "18 to join and 22 each month". Reading back from the graph, C = 260 gives 22m = 242, so m = 11. Always check that each form agrees with the others.

## Model validity checklist

- State the domain where the model works.
- Test it against at least two data points. Record the gaps.
- Say whether the gaps are growing, shrinking or random.
- Name one thing the model leaves out.
- Do not predict outside the domain, or say clearly that the prediction is unreliable.

**Reminder.** A savings model B = 800 × 1.04ⁿ gives B = 1012.26 after 6 years, rounded to the nearest penny. It assumes the 4% rate never changes. If the bank changes the rate, the model is no longer valid.

## Band ladder, in short

| Band | Criterion C | Criterion D |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | limited language and forms; reasoning hard to follow | some elements found; limited success |
| 3-4 | some correct language; adequate forms; reasoning understandable | relevant elements; a solution reached; sense stated but not always correctly (Year 1), described (Year 3) or discussed (Year 5) |
| 5-6 | usually correct; some success moving between forms (Year 3) or usually moving (Year 5) | adequate model; valid solution; accuracy described (Years 1, 3) or explained (Year 5) |
| 7-8 | consistent; effective moves; reasoning complete and coherent | model chosen well; correct solution; accuracy explained (Years 1, 3) or justified (Year 5); sense described (Year 1), explained (Year 3) or justified (Year 5) |

In the lowest bands several strands read "not demonstrated at this level". That means those strands are not credited there.

## Worked reminders

**Rewrite a messy solution.** A response reads: "3(x + 4) = 2x + 19 → 3x + 12 = 2x + 19 → x = 7 = 33 = 33". The arrows and chained equals mix an equation with a check.

Better:

```
Solve 3(x + 4) = 2x + 19.
Expand: 3x + 12 = 2x + 19
Subtract 2x: x + 12 = 19
Subtract 12: x = 7
Check: left side 3(7 + 4) = 33, right side 2(7) + 19 = 33.
```

**One accuracy statement, three years.** For a bike-hire cost of £17.00 worked from times rounded to the nearest half hour:

- Year 1 (explain): the time was rounded, so the cost is approximate.
- Year 3 (explain): rounding to the nearest half hour could change the time by 15 minutes, so the cost could differ.
- Year 5 (justify): with a rate of £3 an hour, a 15 minute change moves the cost by £0.75, so the true cost lies within about £0.75 of £17.00, which is fine for budgeting.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| complete vs coherent | complete has no missing steps; coherent has steps in a logical order |
| coherent vs concise | concise means no unnecessary material |
| explain vs justify | explain gives reasons; justify gives reasons backed by evidence |
| describe vs explain | describe says what; explain says why |
| accuracy vs sense | accuracy concerns precision of the number; sense concerns fit to the situation |
| example vs proof | examples test; a general argument covers every case |

## Quick self-test

1. In which year is strand iii of Criterion C not demonstrated?
2. Which two strands of Criterion D use "justify" in Year 5?
3. Rewrite 3.6E-2 in mathematical notation.
4. Why is checking 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15 a weak argument that five consecutive integers always sum to a multiple of 5?
5. A candle model h = 24 − 1.5t has h in cm. For what values of t is it valid?
6. A floor needs 202.03 tiles. How many do you buy, and why?
7. Which command term asks for evidence in support of a conclusion?
8. A balance is modelled by B = 800 × 1.04ⁿ. Find B after 10 years, to the nearest penny.
9. Name the three adjectives that describe Year 5 reasoning at the top band.
10. Give one reason a linear model might fail outside the range of the data.

### Answers

1. Year 1 (it is not demonstrated at any band).
2. Strands iv (accuracy) and v (makes sense).
3. 3.6 × 10⁻².
4. It tests one case, so it does not show the result holds for all integers. A general argument using n − 2, n − 1, n, n + 1, n + 2 gives 5n.
5. 0 ≤ t ≤ 16, because 24 − 1.5t ≥ 0 gives t ≤ 16.
6. 203 tiles. You cannot buy part of a tile, so round up. 
7. Justify.
8. 800 × 1.04¹⁰ = 1184.20, so £1184.20.
9. Complete, coherent and concise.
10. The real situation may curve, level off or hit a physical limit, such as a negative height.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reasoning that shows only one or two examples and then claims a general rule.
- Chained equals signs such as 4 × 5 = 20 + 3 = 23.
- Graphs without labelled axes, units or a scale.
- Answers with no units or with rounding that does not suit the situation.
- Explaining accuracy with "it is approximate" and nothing more.
- Using "it makes sense" without comparing the answer with the context.
- Predicting beyond the domain of a model.
- Long, repeated working in Year 5, when a short complete argument is asked for.
- Starting with the calculation and leaving the question unstated, so the structure is hard to follow.
