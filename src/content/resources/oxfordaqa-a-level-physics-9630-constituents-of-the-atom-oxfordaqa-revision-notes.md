## Core facts

- Atom: nucleus of protons and neutrons (nucleons) with electrons around it.
- Atomic radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m; nuclear radius about 10⁻¹⁵ m; ratio about 10⁵ in radius and 10¹⁵ in volume.
- Almost all the mass is in the nucleus: the electrons of a carbon-12 atom are about 0.03% of its mass.
- No atomic mass units at International AS: masses in kg.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nucleon | A proton or a neutron |
| Proton number Z | Number of protons in the nucleus |
| Nucleon number A | Number of protons plus neutrons |
| Isotopes | Nuclei with the same Z and different A |
| Ion | Atom with more or fewer electrons than protons |
| Specific charge | Charge ÷ mass, in C kg⁻¹ |
| Nuclide notation | ᴬ_Z X: nucleon number top left, proton number bottom left |

## Counting particles

| Quantity | Rule |
|---|---|
| Protons | Z |
| Neutrons | A − Z |
| Electrons, neutral atom | Z |
| Electrons, ion of charge +n | Z − n |
| Electrons, ion of charge −n | Z + n |

## Specific charge: three shortcuts

1. A bare nucleus has specific charge (Z/A) × 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹.
2. An ion of charge ne and nucleon number A has specific charge (n/A) × 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹, ignoring electron mass, with the sign of the charge.
3. Working backwards: charge = specific charge × (A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷), then divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ to get the number of elementary charges.

Heavy nuclei have smaller Z/A, so smaller specific charge, than light ones.

## Rutherford scattering

| Set-up feature | Reason |
|---|---|
| Alpha particles | Positive, massive and fast: only a concentrated charge can turn one back |
| Thin gold foil | Each particle meets at most one nucleus; gold nuclei have a large charge |
| Vacuum | Air would absorb or scatter the alpha particles |
| Detector at many angles | Counts how many are scattered through each angle |

| Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most pass through, little deflection | Atom mostly empty space |
| A few large deflections | Positive charge concentrated in a tiny volume |
| Very few above 90° | That volume holds most of the mass |

What-if reasoning (all from the repulsive force):

- Lower-Z foil: weaker repulsion, fewer large-angle deflections.
- Slower alpha particles: more large-angle deflections.
- Thicker foil: multiple scattering and absorption blur the pattern.

## Changing models in four steps

1. Thomson identifies the electron (1897): atoms have internal structure.
2. Plum pudding: spread-out positive charge; predicts only small deflections.
3. Rutherford's nuclear model (1911): large deflections need a tiny, massive, positive nucleus.
4. Chadwick identifies the neutron (1932): accounts for nuclear mass beyond the proton count.

Answer pattern: prediction of old model → contradicting evidence → new model that fits.

## Exam traps

- "Attracted" instead of "repelled".
- Saying the plum pudding model predicted no deflection at all.
- Using Z instead of A − Z for neutrons.
- Using the neutral-atom electron count for an ion.
- Dropping the minus sign for an electron or negative ion.
- Including electron mass when the question says to ignore it, or ignoring it when told to include it.
- Writing relative mass of the electron as 0.

## Quick self-test

1. State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺.
2. Calculate the specific charge of a sodium ion, ²³Na⁺, ignoring electron mass.
3. By roughly what factor is an atom wider than its nucleus?
4. Why was a very thin foil used in the scattering experiment?
5. Which scattering result shows that the nucleus holds most of the mass?
6. Calculate the specific charge of a calcium ion, ⁴⁰Ca²⁺, ignoring electron mass.
7. An ion with 14 nucleons and proton number 7 has specific charge −2.05 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹. Find its charge and number of electrons.
8. State the relative mass of an electron.
9. What problem did the discovery of the neutron solve?
10. Two isotopes of an element are fully ionised. Explain why their specific charges differ.
11. What did the plum pudding model predict for alpha particles fired at a thin foil?
12. Calculate the specific charge of a beryllium-9 nucleus (Z = 4).

### Answers

1. 13 protons, 27 − 13 = 14 neutrons, 13 − 3 = 10 electrons.
2. 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (23 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.17 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹.
3. About 10⁵.
4. So each alpha particle is deflected by at most one nucleus and few are absorbed.
5. The very few deflections of more than 90°: only a massive target can turn the alpha particle back.
6. 2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (40 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.79 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹.
7. Charge = −2.05 × 10⁷ × 14 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = −4.79 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = −3e; electrons = 7 + 3 = 10.
8. About 1/1830 (5.5 × 10⁻⁴).
9. Nuclear masses were larger than the proton count alone could explain; an uncharged particle of similar mass to the proton accounts for the difference.
10. They have the same charge (same Z) but different numbers of neutrons, so different masses.
11. Only small deflections, because the positive charge was spread out, so the field inside the atom was never strong enough to turn the particles through large angles.
12. 4 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (9 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.26 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹.
