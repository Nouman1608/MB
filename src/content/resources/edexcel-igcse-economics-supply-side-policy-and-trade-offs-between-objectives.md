## Supply-side policy, productivity and output

**Supply-side policy** is government action that aims to raise the economy's capacity to produce, by making labour, capital and other resources more productive or more plentiful.

**Productivity** is output per worker (or per hour worked). **Total output** is everything the economy produces. If productivity rises and the number of workers stays the same, total output rises. If the same output needs fewer resources, costs per unit fall.

**Worked example (Country A, illustrative).** A small bakery has 8 workers and bakes 4,000 loaves a week.

```
Productivity before = 4,000 / 8 = 500 loaves per worker
After a training course: 4,600 loaves, still 8 workers
Productivity after  = 4,600 / 8 = 575 loaves per worker
% change in productivity = (575 - 500) / 500 x 100 = 15%
Total output rises by 600 loaves, also 15%
```

Spread across many firms, this raises the economy's **productive potential**: the most it can produce when resources are fully used. On a production possibility frontier, it moves the whole curve outwards. In words: the horizontal and vertical intercepts both move away from the origin.

Why this matters for objectives: more output with no extra pressure on prices supports growth and low inflation. More firms hiring supports lower unemployment. Cheaper domestic goods can improve the current account.

## The seven policies

For each policy learn the **chain**: what the government does, what firms or workers then do, what happens to productivity and output, and which objective improves. Then learn the **limits**.

### Privatisation

Privatisation moves ownership of a business from the public sector to the private sector.

- Chain: private owners want profit, so they cut waste and cost; competition may push them to improve quality; productivity rises; output per unit of resource rises.
- **Worked example.** A state-owned water firm produces 100,000 units at a total cost of 4,000,000. Average cost = 4,000,000 / 100,000 = 40. After sale, costs fall 10% to 3,600,000 for the same output, so average cost = 36.
- Limits: a private monopoly may raise prices; jobs may be cut, so unemployment can rise in the short run; essential services may be neglected if they are unprofitable.

### Deregulation

Deregulation removes or loosens rules that restrict who can enter a market or how firms operate.

- Chain: fewer barriers mean more firms enter; competition lowers prices and raises efficiency; new firms hire workers.
- **Worked example.** Licensing rules are simplified and new firm start-ups rise from 120 to 150 a year. Percentage rise = 30 / 120 x 100 = 25%. If each employs 10 people, that is 30 x 10 = 300 new jobs.
- Limits: weaker rules can reduce safety, quality or environmental protection; gains take time to appear.

### Education and training

- Chain: better skills raise productivity; skilled workers earn more and fill vacancies; firms face lower training costs; unemployment caused by lack of skills (structural) falls.
- The bakery example above shows the effect on productivity.
- Limits: it is costly and slow, because pupils take years to qualify. Workers may emigrate, and training may not match the skills employers want.

### Regional policies for high unemployment

These aim to boost regions where unemployment is above the national level, for example through grants for firms that locate there, cheaper land or tax breaks.

- Chain: firms move in; they create jobs; local incomes and spending rise; unemployment in the region falls.
- **Worked example.** Region X has a labour force of 200,000 and 24,000 unemployed, so its rate is 24,000 / 200,000 x 100 = 12%. The national rate is 5%. A policy creates 6,000 jobs filled by unemployed people. Unemployed = 18,000, so the rate = 18,000 / 200,000 x 100 = 9%.
- Limits: firms may take the grant and then leave; workers may lack the skills; the money could have been spent elsewhere (opportunity cost).

### Infrastructure spending

Roads, railways, ports, broadband and energy networks.

- Chain: transport and communication costs fall; firms operate faster and more cheaply; it becomes attractive to invest; output and productivity rise.
- **Worked example.** A new road cuts a delivery trip from 5 hours to 3.5 hours, a fall of (5 - 3.5) / 5 x 100 = 30%. Driver cost is 20 an hour: 100 per trip falls to 70, a saving of 30. With 40 trips a week, the saving is 40 x 30 = 1,200 a week.
- Limits: very high cost, long build time, and a lasting burden on the government budget.

### Lower business taxes to stimulate investment

- Chain: firms keep more profit; they can afford machines and new premises; investment raises capital and productivity; output grows.
- **Worked example.** A firm makes a profit of 80,000. At a 25% tax rate it keeps 80,000 x 0.75 = 60,000. At 20% it keeps 80,000 x 0.80 = 64,000, which is 4,000 more (a rise of 6.7%).
- Limits: firms may pay out the extra as dividends rather than invest; if demand is weak, they may see no reason to expand; government tax revenue falls.

### Lower income tax rates to encourage working

- Chain: take-home pay rises for each hour worked; more people take jobs or work more hours; labour supply and output rise.
- **Worked example.** A worker earns 2,000 a month. At 20% tax the net pay is 1,600. At 15% it is 1,700, a rise of 100 / 1,600 x 100 = 6.25%. Extra overtime of 100 is worth 80 after tax before the cut and 85 after.
- **Cost to the government.** Suppose 500,000 workers earn 2,000 each. At 20%, revenue = 500,000 x 2,000 x 0.20 = 200 million. At 15% with earnings unchanged, it falls to 150 million. If total earnings rise 4% because people work more, revenue = 500,000 x 2,000 x 1.04 x 0.15 = 156 million. It is still 44 million lower.
- Limits: some workers may choose to work less because they can reach the same income sooner. Many jobs offer fixed hours, so there is no change in behaviour.

## Evaluating supply-side policy

Points that apply to all seven:

- **Time lags**: results arrive in years, so unemployment or inflation targets may not improve quickly.
- **Cost and opportunity cost**: spending on training or roads cannot be spent on health.
- **Uncertainty**: the result depends on how firms and workers respond.
- **Strengths**: it can reduce unemployment and inflation together, because it increases output without more pressure on prices.

A good conclusion names the policy best suited to the problem. Skills shortages call for training. A remote region calls for regional policy. Weak competition calls for deregulation.

## The trade-off between objectives

A **trade-off** occurs when pursuing one objective makes another one worse. A policy that boosts spending usually creates jobs and growth, but it pushes prices up and sucks in imports. The trade-offs arise because most short-run policies push demand one way. Supply-side policy matters here because it can ease them.

### Unemployment and inflation

When the government stimulates demand, firms hire more workers to meet it. As spare workers run out, firms compete for staff, wages rise, and costs and prices rise.

**Worked example.** Country A has a labour force of 10,000,000.

| | Before | After stimulus |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployed | 800,000 | 500,000 |
| Unemployment rate | 8% | 5% |
| Inflation rate | 1.5% | 4.5% |

Unemployment rate = 800,000 / 10,000,000 x 100 = 8%. Unemployed workers fall by 300,000 / 800,000 = 37.5%. The unemployment rate fell by 3 **percentage points**, and inflation rose by 3 percentage points. Quote percentage points when you compare two rates.

Evaluation: the trade-off is clearest when the economy is near full employment. When there is much spare capacity, output can rise without price rises. Supply-side policies lower the cost of the trade-off.

### Economic growth and inflation

Fast growth in demand can outpace supply. Firms hit capacity limits, shortages appear, and prices rise.

**Worked example.** Gross domestic product (GDP) is measured at current prices as 800 billion, then 856.8 billion a year later. The price index rises from 100 to 102.

```
Nominal growth = (856.8 - 800) / 800 x 100 = 7.1%
Inflation      = (102 - 100) / 100 x 100 = 2%
Real GDP       = 856.8 / 1.02 = 840 billion
Real growth    = (840 - 800) / 800 x 100 = 5%
```

Only 5 of the 7.1 percentage points is extra output. The rest is higher prices. Evaluation: growth that comes from higher productive potential (supply-side) need not be inflationary.

### Economic growth and environmental protection

More output usually means more resources used and more pollution. Policies to protect the environment, such as taxes, regulation or limits, can raise firms' costs and slow growth.

**Worked example.** Real GDP rises from 500 to 520 billion (4%). Emissions were 40 million tonnes.

| Case | Emissions | Change | Emissions per billion of GDP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same methods | 41.6 | +4% | 41.6 / 520 = 0.080 |
| Cleaner technology | 40.8 | +2% | 40.8 / 520 = 0.078 |

Growth with cleaner technology still adds some pollution, but less per unit of output. Evaluation: the trade-off is weaker if firms switch to cleaner methods, but a country may pick growth over the environment if it is poor and needs higher living standards.

### Inflation and the current account

When domestic prices rise faster than prices abroad, a country's exports become dearer for foreign buyers and imports become relatively cheaper for domestic buyers. Exports fall, imports rise, and the current account moves towards deficit.

**Worked example.** Country B has inflation of 6% while its trading partners have 2%.

```
Exports: 120 -> 108   change = -12/120 x 100 = -10%
Imports: 100 -> 112   change = +12/100 x 100 = +12%
Balance before = 120 - 100 = +20 (surplus)
Balance after  = 108 - 112 = -4  (deficit)
```

Evaluation: a falling exchange rate can offset the loss of competitiveness, and the effect depends on how responsive buyers are to price. Higher imports are also a leakage from the circular flow of income.

## Common errors

- Defining supply-side policy as policy that changes demand or interest rates.
- Writing "unemployment goes up" for the trade-off. The trade-off is that cutting unemployment tends to raise inflation, and the reverse.
- Mixing up percentage change and percentage points.
- Treating lower income tax as certain to raise output, with no mention of the effect on tax revenue.
- Giving a list of seven policies with no chain and no conclusion.
