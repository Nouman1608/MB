This guide covers **Implementing policy**, the third content section of Component 02, Macroeconomics, in the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), for first assessment in 2021. It has four topics: 3.1 Fiscal policy, 3.2 Monetary policy, 3.3 Supply side policy and 3.4 Policy conflicts. Each topic ends with the same kind of outcome: evaluate the **effectiveness** of that policy in achieving the government's macroeconomic objectives. This guide is built around that shared judgement. It compares the three sets of instruments, shows how to match a tool to a problem, and works through the calculations the section requires. All countries and figures are invented.

For condensed recall use the [implementing policy revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-implementing-policy-ocr-alevel-economics-revision-notes/), and for exam-style work the [implementing policy practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-implementing-policy-ocr-alevel-economics-practice/). Each instrument is taught in depth in [fiscal policy: the budget and taxation](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-fiscal-policy-1-the-budget-and-taxation/), [deficits, debt and stabilisers](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-fiscal-policy-2-deficits-debt-stabilisers-and-fiscal/), [monetary policy and inflation targeting](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-monetary-policy-and-inflation-targeting/), [supply-side policy](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-supply-side-policy/) and [policy conflicts](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-policy-conflicts-and-the-evolution-of-macroeconomic/).

## 1. Matching the instrument to the problem

Start every policy question by diagnosing the problem with the AD/AS model.

| Problem | Likely cause | Instrument that targets the cause |
|---|---|---|
| High unemployment, low inflation, negative output gap | Weak aggregate demand | Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy |
| High inflation, positive output gap | Excess aggregate demand | Contractionary monetary or fiscal policy |
| High inflation with falling output | A leftward shift of SRAS, such as an energy price shock | Mainly time; supply-side measures over the longer term |
| Slow trend growth, weak productivity | Limited long-run aggregate supply | Supply-side policy |
| Persistent current account deficit from poor competitiveness | High unit costs, weak non-price factors | Supply-side policy, possibly a lower exchange rate |

Demand-side tools shift AD; supply-side tools shift LRAS (and some, such as cutting firms' costs, also SRAS). Choosing a demand tool for a supply problem is the most common error in this section.

## 2. Fiscal policy calculations and concepts

**Average and marginal tax rates.** The **average tax rate** is total tax ÷ total income. The **marginal tax rate** is the tax on an extra unit of income.

**Worked example 1: an invented income tax.** No tax on the first 12,000 currency units (cu); 20% on income from 12,001 to 50,000 cu; 40% above 50,000 cu.

| Income (cu) | Tax (cu) | Average rate | Marginal rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0 | 0% | 0% |
| 30,000 | 0.2 × 18,000 = 3,600 | 12.0% | 20% |
| 60,000 | 0.2 × 38,000 + 0.4 × 10,000 = 11,600 | 19.3% | 40% |

The average rate rises with income, so the tax is **progressive**. A **proportional** tax would take the same percentage at every income; a **regressive** tax takes a falling percentage, as an indirect tax on a basic good often does, because poorer households spend more of their income on it.

**Cyclical and structural positions.** Part of a deficit is **cyclical**: in a downturn, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically. The **structural** part would remain even with output at its trend level.

**Worked example 2.** Country Ardan's budget deficit is 5.0% of GDP. Its output gap is −3%, and each 1% of negative output gap adds about 0.5% of GDP to the deficit.

- Cyclical deficit = 3 × 0.5 = **1.5% of GDP**.
- Structural deficit = 5.0 − 1.5 = **3.5% of GDP**.

Growth would close only the 1.5%. Closing the other 3.5% needs discretionary tax rises or spending cuts. The figure for the response of the deficit to the output gap is itself an estimate, which makes the split uncertain.

**Discretionary policy and automatic stabilisers.** Discretionary policy is a deliberate change in tax rates or spending. Automatic stabilisers, such as progressive taxes and unemployment benefits, cushion the cycle without any decision. Stabilisers act immediately; discretionary changes face recognition, decision and implementation lags.

**Crowding out and the Laffer curve.** If government borrowing raises interest rates or uses resources that private firms would otherwise use, private investment may fall, reducing the net effect of fiscal expansion. This matters most when the economy is near full capacity. The **Laffer curve** shows that beyond some tax rate, higher rates reduce revenue, because work, investment or declared income fall. Where that point lies is disputed, so the curve is a reason for caution rather than a precise guide.

## 3. Monetary policy transmission

A cut in the policy interest rate works through several channels.

1. Borrowing becomes cheaper and saving less rewarding, so consumption and investment rise.
2. Mortgage payments fall for variable-rate borrowers, raising disposable income.
3. Asset prices tend to rise, increasing household wealth and confidence.
4. The currency tends to depreciate as savings move abroad, making exports cheaper and imports dearer.

AD shifts right, raising real output and the price level. A rise in rates reverses each step. An **inflation rate target** anchors expectations: if firms and workers believe inflation will return to target, they set prices and wages accordingly, which makes the target easier to hit.

**Quantitative easing.** When interest rates are already very low, the central bank can create money electronically and buy financial assets, usually government bonds. This raises bond prices and lowers long-term interest rates, and increases the money held by banks and investors, encouraging lending and spending. Its effect depends on whether banks lend and households and firms want to borrow.

**Exchange rates.** In an open economy the exchange rate is part of the transmission. A rise in interest rates that causes appreciation lowers import prices and therefore inflation, but also makes exporters less competitive, so it can deepen a slowdown in export industries.

## 4. Supply-side policy: market-based and interventionist

| Market-based | Interventionist |
|---|---|
| Privatisation and deregulation | Investment in infrastructure |
| Competition policy | Education and training |
| Reforms of tax and benefits to raise incentives to work and invest | Subsidies and funding for research and development |
| Greater labour market flexibility | |

Immigration control is also listed in the specification: changes in immigration rules affect the size and skills of the labour force, and so the position of LRAS.

**Worked example 3: a training scheme (invented).** A government spends 2 billion cu on retraining. If it raises output per worker by 1.5% for 4 million workers whose average output is 50,000 cu a year, annual output rises by 0.015 × 4 million × 50,000 = **3 billion cu**. On these assumptions the scheme pays for itself in output within a year, but the productivity gain is the uncertain part: it may be smaller, take years to appear, or depend on firms having jobs for the new skills.

## 5. Evaluating effectiveness: one framework for all three

| Criterion | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy | Supply-side policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed | Slow to change; stabilisers immediate | Quick to change; effect takes many months | Slow: often years |
| Strength | Depends on the multiplier and crowding out | Weak if confidence is low or rates are near zero | Depends on how much LRAS shifts |
| Targeting | Can target regions or groups | Affects the whole economy | Can target specific weaknesses |
| Side effects | Debt; possible higher interest rates | Asset bubbles; exchange rate effects | Inequality (some market-based measures); cost to public finances |
| Main objectives served | Growth, unemployment | Inflation | Growth, competitiveness, low inflation in the long run |

**Policy conflicts (3.4).** Expansionary demand policy reduces unemployment but can raise inflation and worsen the current account as imports rise. Contractionary policy to cut inflation can raise unemployment. Supply-side policy can ease these trade-offs by raising capacity, which is why it is attractive, but it is slow and its effects are uncertain.

**Worked example 4: choosing a mix.** Invented Country Brenn has unemployment of 8%, inflation of 1% and a current account deficit of 4% of GDP caused by poor competitiveness. Expansionary fiscal policy would cut unemployment, but extra spending on imports would widen the deficit. A better mix is a modest monetary loosening, which may also lower the exchange rate, combined with supply-side investment in training and infrastructure to address the deficit's cause. The judgement depends on the size of the multiplier, the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports, and how long the supply-side measures take.

## Common mistakes

- Using demand-side policy to fix a supply-side problem.
- Confusing the marginal tax rate with the average tax rate.
- Calling the whole deficit structural, or the whole deficit cyclical.
- Describing quantitative easing as "printing banknotes" without explaining the bond-buying mechanism.
- Ignoring time lags when evaluating.
- Evaluating each policy alone, without considering conflicts between objectives.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the tax bands separately, then divide by income for the average rate.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** say which curve shifts (AD, SRAS or LRAS) and the effect on real output and the price level.
- **Evaluate:** use the framework in section 5, apply it to the economy in the data, and decide which policy, or which mix, best fits that economy's problem.
