These questions cover section 1.2, The basic economic problem, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All people, places and figures are invented. Revise first with the [1.2 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem-ocr-gcse-economics/) or the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-basic-economic-problem-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by opportunity cost. **[2]**

**2.** State the **three** questions that make up the economic problem. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why scarcity exists even in a high-income economy. **[2]**

**4.** Amira has £30. In order of preference, she could buy a festival ticket (£30), a pair of jeans (£28) or three books (£20). She buys the festival ticket. State her opportunity cost. **[1]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a renewable and a non-renewable resource, using an example of each. **[2]**

**6.** A government spends £50 million on a new prison. Explain **one** possible opportunity cost of this decision. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A furniture factory can use its production line for one product next month.

| Product | Units per month | Profit per unit |
|---|---|---|
| Chairs | 2,000 | £15 |
| Tables | 1,200 | £22 |
| Stools | 3,000 | £8 |

**(a)** Calculate the total profit from each option. **[3]**
**(b)** The factory makes chairs. State the opportunity cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason the factory might choose tables instead, even though their profit is lower. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of allocating health care according to need rather than by price. **[4]**

**9.** A city council has £12 million to spend. It values the benefits of three schemes: flood defences £15 million, a sports stadium £13 million, and bus lanes £9 million.

**(a)** The council chooses the flood defences. State the opportunity cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the idea of environmental sustainability, explain why some residents might prefer the bus lanes despite their lower measured benefit. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how a market answers the "for whom" question of the economic problem. **[4]**

**11.** A family can drive to work for £2,400 a year or take the bus for £900 a year. The bus adds three hours of travel a week over 46 working weeks. One parent values time at £12 an hour.

**(a)** Calculate the money saved each year by taking the bus. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the value of the extra travel time, and compare it with the money saved. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** environmental and **one** social reason the family might still choose the bus. **[2]**

**12.** The government of an invented country, Norland, is deciding whether to allow a new coal mine. Evaluate the costs and benefits of this choice, including its impact on sustainability. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The value of the next best alternative [1] that is given up when a choice is made [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "What you give up" without "next best" usually earns only one mark.

**2.** What to produce [1]; how to produce it [1]; for whom to produce it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "How to allocate resources" sums up all three and is not a separate fourth question here.

**3.** Wants are unlimited: as incomes rise, people want new and better goods [1], while resources such as land and skilled labour remain limited [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer needs both halves: growing wants and limited resources.

**4.** The jeans [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Not the jeans and the books: only the next best alternative counts.

**5.** A renewable resource can be replaced or regrow if not overused, such as forests [1]; a non-renewable resource cannot be replaced once used, such as oil [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs a definition and an example together.

**6.** For example, the £50 million could have built a new hospital [1], so patients wait longer for treatment that could otherwise have been provided [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name a realistic alternative use of the same money and explain what is lost.

**7. (a)** Chairs 2,000 × 15 = **£30,000** [1]. Tables 1,200 × 22 = **£26,400** [1]. Stools 3,000 × 8 = **£24,000** [1].
**(b)** The tables, worth £26,400 profit [1].
**(c)** For example, a major customer has ordered tables, so making them keeps a long-term contract [1] that will bring more profit in future months than one month of chairs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), the stools are not part of the opportunity cost because they are the third choice.

**8.** Advantage: people on low incomes can still be treated [1], so access depends on medical need rather than ability to pay, which many see as fairer [1]. Disadvantage: without price rationing, demand can exceed what is provided [1], leading to waiting lists [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop each point; "it is fair" alone is one mark at most.

**9. (a)** The sports stadium, valued at £13 million [1].
**(b)** Bus lanes may reduce car use, cutting pollution and carbon emissions [1], which protects the environment for future residents, a benefit that may not be fully counted in the measured value [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), the second mark comes from explaining why the benefit lasts into the future.

**10.** In a market, goods go to people who are willing and able to pay the price [1]. Those with higher incomes can buy more [1], so the price rations goods between buyers [1]. As a result, people on low incomes may go without some goods, even essential ones [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Analyse" asks for a chain: price, income, rationing, outcome.

**11. (a)** 2,400 − 900 = **£1,500** [1].
**(b)** 3 × 46 × £12 = **£1,656** [1]. The value of the time lost is £156 more than the money saved, so in money terms the car is slightly better for this family [1].
**(c)** Environmental: the bus produces less pollution per passenger than a car [1]. Social: the bus service supports people without cars, and travel time can be used for reading or rest [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Time is a scarce resource with an opportunity cost. Valuing it changes the comparison.

**12.** Benefits: the mine creates jobs and incomes in the area [1], and coal sales earn revenue and tax for the government [1], supporting economic sustainability in the short run [1]. Costs: burning coal releases carbon dioxide and adds to climate change [1]; coal is non-renewable, so jobs last only while the coal lasts [1]; mining may damage land and local health, a social cost [1]. The opportunity cost includes using the land and investment for renewable energy instead [1]. Judgement: the mine may give short-run economic gains, but the long-run environmental and social costs are likely to outweigh them unless the jobs could not be created in any other way [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use all three types of sustainability and compare the short run with the long run before judging.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming more than one alternative as the opportunity cost.
- Explaining scarcity as a shop running out of stock.
- Forgetting that time and "free" services have opportunity costs.
- Writing "sustainable" without saying which type.
- One-sided evaluations with no final judgement.
