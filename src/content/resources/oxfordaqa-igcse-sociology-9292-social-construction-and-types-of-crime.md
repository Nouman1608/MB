## Crime and deviance

- **Deviance** is behaviour that breaks the **norms** of a group or society. It may attract informal
  sanctions such as disapproval.
- **Crime** is behaviour that breaks the **criminal law** and can be punished by the state.

The two overlap but are not the same. Queue-jumping is deviant but not criminal. Some crimes, such as
driving slightly over a speed limit, are so common that many people do not see them as deviant.
Deviance can also be positive in the sense of breaking norms in admired ways, as when campaigners
broke unjust laws that were later changed.

## What "socially constructed" means

To say crime and deviance are **socially constructed** is to say that they are not fixed qualities of
acts. They are created by the rules a society makes and by how people react. The same act can be
legal in one place and criminal in another, or acceptable in one period and condemned in a later one.

### Differences between societies

- **Alcohol** is legal to buy in most countries but prohibited in some, such as Saudi Arabia.
- **Cannabis** for recreational use was legalised nationally in Uruguay (2013) and Canada (2018), while
  remaining a criminal offence in many other countries.
- Norms of **dress**, public displays of affection and religious observance vary widely, so what is
  deviant in one society may be expected in another.

### Changes over time

Using England and Wales as an example:

- Sexual acts between men in private were partly **decriminalised** in 1967.
- The **breathalyser** was introduced in 1967, and drink-driving has since become strongly
  stigmatised.
- Rape within marriage was confirmed as a **crime** by the courts in 1991.
- **Smoking** in enclosed public places was banned in England in 2007.

Each change shows the law following, or leading, shifts in values. Apply the same reasoning to a
society you know.

### Labelling and moral panics

**Becker** argued that deviance is created when a label is successfully applied: rule-makers decide
what counts as deviant, and enforcement is selective.

**Stanley Cohen**, in *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972), studied the reaction to clashes between
"mods" and "rockers" at English seaside resorts in 1964. He found that the media **exaggerated** and
**distorted** what had happened, **predicted** further trouble, and turned the groups' clothes and
scooters into **symbols** of a threat. The public, police and courts responded with alarm. Cohen
called this a **moral panic**, and the groups targeted **folk devils**. The reaction itself made the
deviance seem larger and more organised than it was, and could increase it, a process known as
**deviancy amplification**.

Moral panics show social construction in action: a society's reaction shapes what is seen as a
serious problem.

## The eight types of crime

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Violent crime** | Crime involving force or the threat of force against a person | Assault, robbery, murder |
| **Property crime** | Taking or damaging property without the use of force against a person | Burglary, theft, vandalism |
| **Cybercrime** | Crime committed using computers or the internet | Hacking, online fraud, phishing, ransomware, online harassment |
| **White-collar crime** | Crime committed by people of high status in the course of their work (a term introduced by **Sutherland** in 1939) | Embezzlement, insider trading, fraudulent expense claims |
| **Corporate crime** | Crime committed by or for a company, to benefit the organisation | Price fixing, breaking safety laws, selling unsafe products, illegal pollution |
| **State crime** | Crime committed by governments or their agencies | Genocide, torture, war crimes, corruption |
| **Green or environmental crime** | Crime that harms the environment | Illegal logging, wildlife trafficking, illegal dumping of waste |
| **"Victimless" crime** | Crime with no obvious direct victim | Illegal drug use, illegal gambling |

### Points for analysis

- **White-collar and corporate crime** differ: white-collar crime benefits the individual; corporate
  crime benefits the organisation. Both are under-recorded because they are hard to detect and are
  often dealt with by regulators rather than the police.
- **State crime** is hard to punish, because the state controls the police and courts that would
  prosecute it. The genocide in Rwanda in 1994 is a widely studied example.
- **Green crime** raises the question of whether some harms to the environment that are legal should
  still be studied as crimes; the answer depends on whether crime is defined by law or by harm.
- **"Victimless"** is in quotation marks in the specification because the label is disputed: critics
  argue that drug use or illegal gambling can harm families and communities.
- **Cybercrime** crosses national borders, which makes investigation and prosecution difficult.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain what is meant by the social construction of crime, using an example. [4 marks]*

> The social construction of crime means that crime is not a fixed quality of an act (1) but is
> defined by the laws and reactions of a particular society (1). This is shown by differences between
> societies (1): for example, the sale of alcohol is legal in most countries but illegal in some, so
> the same act can be a crime in one place and not in another (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far the media create moral panics about crime. Use examples from your own society or a
society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **For:** Cohen's mods and rockers: exaggeration, prediction, symbolisation; deviancy amplification;
  Becker on labelling.
- **Against or qualifying:** media may reflect real public concern; social media spreads stories
  without traditional editors; some issues reported are genuinely serious.
- **Application:** a named society, for example a period of intense reporting about youth crime or
  knife crime.
- **Judgement:** the media play a major part in defining which crimes cause alarm, but panics also
  need politicians, police and public reaction.

## Common mistakes

- Using crime and deviance as if they meant the same.
- Saying an act "is" a crime without recognising that this depends on law and society.
- Confusing white-collar and corporate crime.
- Treating "victimless" crime as obviously harmless.
- Giving examples of change over time with no explanation of the changing values behind them.
- No example from a named society in an extended answer.
