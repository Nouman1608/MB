These questions practise the "Perspectives" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, where "a perspective is a coherent world view which is a response to an issue". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Research Report is coursework, so these questions practise the perspective-handling decisions behind it; they are not past-paper questions. All sources, organisations and data are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [perspectives study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-perspectives/).

## Section A

**1.** According to the syllabus, what three things is a perspective made up of? **[3]**

**2.** Explain what the syllabus means by an assumption. **[2]**

**3.** State whether each research question would allow different perspectives to be compared, and give a reason. **[4]**

- **(i)** "Genetic engineering"
- **(ii)** "Is genetically modified food a good solution to food shortages?"
- **(iii)** "Why is genetically modified food dangerous?"
- **(iv)** "Should governments restrict the sale of genetically modified food?"

## Section B

**4.** A candidate studying the question "Should wealthy countries accept more refugees?" writes: "Some people think yes and some people think no." Explain why this is not yet a perspective, and rewrite one side so that it is. **[4]**

**5.** Read this summary.

> Perspective X: "Zoos should be abolished." Evidence: studies showing stress behaviour in captive animals. Perspective Y: "Zoos are essential for conservation." Evidence: breeding programmes that returned species to the wild.

Identify one **assumption** that each perspective might make that is not stated. **[4]**

**6.** A candidate presents this "perspective": "A newspaper article from the Daily Standard said that cities should have more cycle lanes." Explain why this is not a perspective in the syllabus sense and what the candidate should do. **[3]**

**7.** A candidate writes about the question "Is online learning as effective as classroom learning?" and presents the "classroom" side only as: "Classroom learning is old-fashioned and boring, but some people like it." Explain what is wrong and rewrite the perspective in a strong form. **[4]**

**8.** Complete the comparison. Perspective A (for nuclear power) argues from the evidence of low carbon emissions. Perspective B (against) argues from the risks of waste and the cost of building. State **one** point of agreement, **one** difference in evidence and **one** possible difference in assumptions. **[3]**

**9.** Write a short judgement paragraph (about 60 words) on the question "Should cities ban private cars from their centres?" using the following summary: air-quality evidence strongly supports a ban; retail evidence is mixed; people with limited mobility need access; good public transport exists in some cities but not others. **[5]**

**10.** A candidate's report says: "I considered both perspectives and my view did not change." Explain why this may not meet the criterion about reflecting on alternative perspectives, and rewrite it so that it does. **[4]**

**11.** A candidate adds four different perspectives to her report on the question "Is tourism good for small island communities?" in 5000 words. Evaluate this decision. **[6]**

**12.** "A research question that suggests an answer in its wording cannot lead to a good report." Evaluate this claim, referring to the syllabus's treatment of research questions and perspectives. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Argument [1], evidence [1] and assumptions [1]. (The syllabus also says a perspective may be influenced by a particular context.) **[3]**

**2.** An assumption is an unstated reason [1] that leads people to value different kinds of evidence and to come to different conclusions [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (i) No: it is a topic, not a question [1]. (ii) Yes: people can reasonably argue for or against [1]. (iii) No: it is loaded and assumes the answer [1]. (iv) Yes: it is open and leads to debate about regulation and evidence [1]. **[4]**

**4.** It states a position only [1], without argument, evidence or assumptions [1]. Rewrite, for example: "Supporters of accepting more refugees argue that wealthy countries have the resources and a moral duty to help [1]; they point to evidence of refugees' economic contributions, and assume that countries can integrate newcomers successfully [1]." **[4]**

**5.** Perspective X, for example: it assumes that animals' welfare in captivity matters more than the conservation benefits, or that conservation can be achieved without zoos [2 for one assumption]. Perspective Y, for example: it assumes that captive breeding is an effective way of saving species, or that human intervention is acceptable to protect nature [2 for one assumption]. **[4]**

**6.** It is one source's claim, not a coherent world view with argument, evidence and assumptions [1]. A perspective may be supported by many sources [1]. The candidate should identify the position and its reasons and evidence, and use more than one source [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The classroom perspective is presented in a weak form, a straw man [1], which suggests it is easy to defeat, instead of being evaluated "carefully and sympathetically" [1]. Stronger, for example: "Supporters of classroom learning argue that face-to-face teaching allows immediate feedback and social development [1]; they cite studies showing higher engagement in classroom settings, and assume that learning is a social activity as well as an individual one [1]." **[4]**

**8.** One point of agreement, for example both want to reduce the harm of climate change [1]. One difference in evidence: A uses emissions data; B uses data on waste and construction costs [1]. One difference in assumptions: A assumes risks of nuclear waste can be managed; B assumes they cannot, or that the costs outweigh benefits [1]. **[3]**

**9.** For example: "The evidence on air quality strongly supports restricting cars in the busiest central streets [1]. The retail evidence is mixed, so economic harm is not clearly shown [1]. However, people with limited mobility need access, so a total ban would be hard to justify [1]. The answer also depends on public transport, which is good in some cities but not others [1]. A partial ban with exceptions is therefore more convincing than a total ban [1]." **[5]**

**10.** It says that both perspectives were considered but does not say how they influenced the argument [1]; the criterion asks the candidate to reflect on how alternative perspectives influenced the argument [1]. Rewrite, for example: "At the start I thought a full ban was best. The evidence from retailers and from people with limited mobility showed that access matters [1], so my conclusion now supports a partial ban with exceptions, while I kept my view that air quality is the strongest evidence [1]." **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content, up to 6 marks.

For: a wider range may show the complexity of the issue [1]; some questions do have several strong positions, such as a position depending on local conditions [1].

Against: with 5000 words, four perspectives leave little space for argument, evidence and assumptions in each [1]; the report may become a list, not a comparison [1]; the criterion asks for contrasting perspectives to be built and compared, which needs depth [1].

Judgement, for example: two or three well-developed perspectives are likely to be more effective than four thin ones [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Indicative content, up to 8 marks: up to 2 for the case for the claim, up to 4 for the case against it, and 2 for a justified judgement.

For: the syllabus says a question such as "Has industrialisation been environmentally damaging?" is not suitable because it suggests a one-sided, preconceived view and does not lead directly to a debate [1]; a loaded question tends to produce a report that proves one side [1].

Against: the objection is to questions that prevent debate, not to every question with a leaning [1]; a candidate can rephrase a loaded question into an open one, such as "Is industrialisation a good thing?" [1]; the candidate can still make a balanced investigation if the question is revised before the proposal is approved [1]; the teacher reviews the proposal to confirm the question gives scope to meet the criteria [1].

Judgement, for example: a loaded question limits the report, but it can be repaired at the proposal stage, so the claim is true of the question as worded but not of the project as a whole [2]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1**: giving "argument, opinion and fact" instead of argument, evidence and assumptions.
- **Question 4**: restating "yes" and "no" with no reasons, evidence or assumptions.
- **Question 7**: describing the opposing view in a weak form.
- **Question 10**: stating "I considered both sides" without saying how they changed the argument.
- **Question 12**: treating any question with a leaning as unusable, without noticing that it can be rewritten.
