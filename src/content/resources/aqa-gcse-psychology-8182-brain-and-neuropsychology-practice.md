## Section A

**1.** Which **one** of the following is part of the central nervous system? Shade one box. **[1]**

- A Autonomic nervous system
- B Somatic nervous system
- C Spinal cord
- D Sympathetic branch

**2.** Identify the area of the brain responsible for balance and co-ordination. **[1]**

**3.** State **two** changes in the body during the fight-or-flight response. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the difference between the **somatic** and **autonomic** nervous systems. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by **localisation of function**. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Read the scenario.

*Sam is walking home in the dark when a dog suddenly barks behind a fence. Sam's heart pounds and
he starts breathing fast. A second later, he realises he feels terrified.*

**(a)** Explain Sam's reaction using the James-Lange theory of emotion. **[3]**
**(b)** Give **one** criticism of the James-Lange theory. **[2]**

**7.** Describe Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth. **[3]**

**8.** A researcher wants to find which brain areas are most active when people solve puzzles, without
exposing participants to radiation. Identify the most suitable scanning technique and explain your
choice. **[3]**

**9.** Read the scenario.

*After a head injury, Dana can see and hear normally but has difficulty planning her day and often
acts on impulse in ways her friends say are "not like her".*

Which lobe of the brain is most likely to have been damaged? Explain your answer. **[3]**

**10.** Describe and evaluate Tulving's 'gold' memory study. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss what Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex tells us about the brain. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** Spinal cord [1].

**2.** The cerebellum [1].

**3.** Any two: increased heart rate [1]; faster breathing [1]; pupils dilate [1]; blood diverted to
the muscles [1]; digestion slows [1]; adrenaline released [1].

**4.** The somatic nervous system controls **voluntary** movement of skeletal muscles and carries
sensory information [1]. The autonomic nervous system controls **involuntary** functions such as
heart rate, breathing and digestion [1].

**5.** The idea that particular areas of the brain are responsible for particular functions [1], for
example the occipital lobe for vision or the motor area for voluntary movement [1].

**6. (a)** According to James-Lange, a bodily response comes **before** the emotion [1]. The bark
triggered physical arousal -- Sam's pounding heart and fast breathing [1] -- and Sam's brain then
interpreted these changes as fear, which is why he only felt terrified a second later [1].
**(b)** Any one, identified [1] and explained [1]: many different emotions involve similar bodily
changes (fear and excitement both raise heart rate), so arousal alone cannot explain which emotion
is felt; or people with spinal cord injuries, who receive reduced feedback from the body, still
report experiencing emotions.

**7.** When two neurons are repeatedly active at the same time, the connection between them becomes
stronger [1]. Groups of strongly connected neurons form **cell assemblies** [1], so learning
physically changes the brain and frequently used pathways become more efficient [1].

**8.** **fMRI** [1]. It shows brain **activity** by detecting changes in blood oxygen in active areas
[1], and unlike PET (radioactive tracer) or CT (X-rays) it does not use radiation [1].

**9.** The **frontal lobe** [1]. The frontal lobe is involved in planning, decision-making and
self-control [1]; Dana's vision and hearing are unaffected (so the occipital and temporal lobes are
less likely to be damaged), but her planning and impulse control have changed, which fits frontal
damage [1].

**10.** Level-based answer, up to 6 marks.
- *Description:* a radioactive form of gold was injected into a very small number of participants,
  including Tulving; their brain activity was measured while they thought about **episodic**
  memories (personal events) or **semantic** memories (facts); episodic memories were linked to more
  activity in **frontal** areas, semantic memories to more in the **back** of the cortex.
- *Evaluation:* provides biological evidence that episodic and semantic memories are different and
  localised; but the sample was tiny and included the researcher, who knew the aims (possible bias);
  results were not consistent across all participants; episodic and semantic memories overlap, so
  the tasks may not have separated them cleanly.
**5--6 marks:** accurate description and well-explained evaluation. **3--4:** reasonable
description, some evaluation. **1--2:** limited.

**11.** Level-based answer, up to 5 marks.
- Penfield electrically stimulated the cortex of awake patients undergoing surgery for epilepsy;
  stimulation of parts of the temporal lobes sometimes produced vivid experiences or apparent
  memories, which he linked to an **interpretive cortex**.
- This supports **localisation of function**: specific areas appear linked to specific experiences.
- It was studied in living, conscious people, giving direct evidence.
- However, only a small minority of patients reported such experiences; all had epilepsy, so their
  brains may not be typical; and the experiences may have been more like dreams than real memories,
  so it is limited as evidence of where memories are stored.
**4--5 marks:** accurate, with balanced discussion. **2--3:** some accurate points. **1:** limited.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing the order in the James-Lange theory.
- Choosing a scan without explaining why it fits the research aim.
- Naming a lobe in an application question without linking its function to the symptoms described.
- Evaluating Tulving or Penfield with generic comments instead of points about the sample and method.
