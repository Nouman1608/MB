These revision notes condense the [global contexts and topic vocabulary study guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-global-contexts-and-topics/) for IB MYP Language Acquisition. They are aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Language acquisition*, from 2020 (first eAssessment May 2023/November 2023), and cover the six global contexts named in the brief's curriculum model overview. They suit MYP years 4 and 5, including eAssessment candidates. There is no SL/HL split: courses are assessed at emergent, capable or proficient level.

MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units. These notes cover a topic the IB's brief lists (the global contexts) and the vocabulary skill that all four criteria rely on. Your teacher will share the task-specific clarifications for assessed work. Examples are in English; practise them in your target language.

## Definitions

- **Global context** -- one of six lenses, shared by every MYP subject, through which you explore the key and related concepts.
- **Topic** -- the actual subject matter of a lesson or task (food waste, festivals, online learning). One topic can sit under several contexts.
- **Thematic word bank** -- a layered, sorted collection of vocabulary for one topic.
- **Receptive vocabulary** -- words you recognise when listening or reading.
- **Productive vocabulary** -- words you can recall and use accurately when speaking or writing.
- **Chunk / collocation** -- a group of words that normally go together ("take part in", "make a difference").
- **Transfer** -- reusing the same bank across listening, reading, speaking and writing.

## What the brief prints (and what it does not)

| The brief states | The brief does not state |
|---|---|
| The six global contexts | A list of topics for each context |
| Key concepts: communication, connections, creativity, culture | Which context a school must use for a unit |
| Related concept examples: word choice, conventions, idiom | Vocabulary lists or word counts |
| Factual, conceptual and debatable questions are explored | How many topics you must know |
| Criterion C: topics of "personal, local and global interest and significance" | The content of the "language acquisition list" the exam draws on |
| On-screen exam: three tasks, may use any topic from the language acquisition list | Anything about which topic will appear |

Ask your teacher for the topic list your school works from.

## The six contexts at a glance

| Context | Think about | Example topics (Marlbridge suggestions) |
|---|---|---|
| Identities and relationships | Self, family, friends, health, feelings | Friendship, family roles, routines |
| Orientation in space and time | Places, journeys, home, change over time | Moving house, my town then and now, travel |
| Personal and cultural expression | Arts, festivals, food, leisure | Music, celebrations, hobbies |
| Scientific and technical innovation | Technology, inventions, medicine | Phones, online learning, transport |
| Globalization and sustainability | Environment, consumption, connections between places | Food waste, recycling, tourism |
| Fairness and development | Rights, equality, access, community | Volunteering, access to school, fair trade |

Memory hook, in brief order: **I**dentities, **O**rientation, **P**ersonal, **S**cientific, **G**lobalization, **F**airness -- "I Often Pick Six Good Frames".

## The five-layer bank

| Layer | Contains | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Core nouns | People, things, places in the topic | Recognition in A and B |
| 2. Verbs | Actions, learned with a subject and object | You cannot build sentences without them |
| 3. Describing words | Adjectives, adverbs, degree words | Precision and detail |
| 4. Opinion and linking phrases | Agree, disagree, contrast, give reasons | Structure in C and D |
| 5. Chunks and collocations | Word groups that go together | Natural, accurate language |
| + Scale row | One idea at personal, local, global level | Extends answers in C |

Target: eight to ten items per layer, so roughly 40--50 items per topic.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Context vs topic.** "Fairness and development" is a lens; "volunteering" is a topic. Never write the context name as if it were your subject.
- **Key concept vs related concept vs global context.** Key concepts (for example, culture) are broad ideas; related concepts (for example, idiom) belong to the subject; global contexts are the setting in which you explore them. The concepts have their own [key and related concepts study guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-key-and-related-concepts/).
- **Receptive vs productive.** Listening and reading need breadth and synonyms; speaking and writing need accuracy and chunks.
- **Personal vs local vs global.** Personal = your own life; local = your school, town or community; global = beyond your community.
- **Factual vs conceptual vs debatable question.** Factual has one answer ("What do we recycle at school?"); conceptual explores an idea ("How does advertising shape what we buy?"); debatable has reasonable answers on both sides ("Should plastic bags be banned?").

## Method in steps: build a bank

1. Brainstorm the topic in English.
2. Translate only items you will really use; add gender or article to nouns if your language needs it.
3. Sort into the five layers; fill the thin layers (usually verbs and chunks).
4. Check every chunk with a dictionary or your teacher.
5. Write one true sentence per verb.
6. Write the scale row: personal, local, global.
7. Mark each item R (recognise) or P (produce).

## Method in steps: transfer to a task

1. Identify the topic, then the context angle the task takes.
2. Rebuild the bank from memory in two minutes.
3. **Listening/reading:** predict words and at least two synonyms for each key idea.
4. **Speaking:** plan one chunk and one opinion phrase for each of personal, local, global.
5. **Writing:** pick linking phrases for the paragraph plan; upgrade basic verbs from layer 2.

## Chunks that belong in every bank

Some phrases work under any context. Learn them once in your target language and copy them into each new bank:

- Giving a view: in my opinion; I am convinced that; it seems to me that.
- Contrasting: however; on the other hand; whereas; although.
- Giving reasons and results: because; that is why; as a result; this means that.
- Adding examples: for example; such as; in my town, for instance.
- Moving scale: personally; in my community; around the world.

## Small worked reminders

- **Paraphrase in listening.** Question: "Why did she stop driving to school?" Recording: "The bus is quicker now, so I left the car at home." The idea "stop driving" = "left the car at home".
- **Word family in reading.** Know "pollute"? Then "pollution" (noun), "polluted" (adjective) and "polluter" (person) are in reach.
- **Scale in speaking.** Topic: festivals. Personal: my favourite festival. Local: how my town celebrates. Global: why festivals spread between countries.
- **Upgrade in writing.** "Tourism is bad" becomes "Although tourism brings money, it can damage fragile coastlines."
- **Spacing.** Learn on day 1; review on days 2, 4, 8 and 15.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the six global contexts in the order the brief lists them.
2. Give the four key concepts the brief lists for language acquisition.
3. Which criterion's wording mentions topics of personal, local and global interest?
4. A friend says their topic is "Orientation in space and time". What is wrong with this?
5. Name one topic that fits three different global contexts, and name the contexts.
6. Which two criteria rely mostly on receptive vocabulary?
7. Name the five layers of a thematic word bank.
8. Classify: "Should school uniforms be compulsory?" -- factual, conceptual or debatable?
9. You learn a set of words on day 1 and review 1, 2, 4 and 7 days after the previous study day. On which days do you review?
10. You add 20 new items a week for 6 weeks and keep 75% of them securely. How many items do you keep?
11. Does the brief print the topics in "the language acquisition list"?

### Answers

1. Identities and relationships; orientation in space and time; personal and cultural expression; scientific and technical innovation; globalization and sustainability; fairness and development.
2. Communication, connections, creativity and culture.
3. Criterion C (speaking).
4. That is a global context, a lens, not a topic. A topic would be something like "moving to a new town".
5. For example tourism: orientation in space and time; globalization and sustainability; fairness and development. Other justified answers are fine.
6. Criterion A (listening) and Criterion B (reading).
7. Core nouns; verbs; describing words; opinion and linking phrases; chunks and collocations (plus the scale row).
8. Debatable.
9. Days 2, 4, 8 and 15.
10. 20 × 6 = 120 items; 75% of 120 = **90 items**.
11. No. The brief says the exam may use any topic from that list but does not print it. Ask your teacher.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listening for the exact words of the question and missing a paraphrase in the recording.
- Using a noun with the wrong gender or article because it was learned without one.
- Knowing plenty of nouns but lacking the verbs to build a full sentence under time pressure.
- Word-for-word translation from English that produces collocations no native speaker uses.
- Staying at personal level when a speaking question invites a local or global answer.
- Writing the context name ("globalization and sustainability") as the subject of a paragraph instead of a real topic.
- Repeating the same basic adjectives ("good", "bad", "interesting") instead of drawing on layer 3.
- Opinion phrases with no reason or example after them.
- Revising by reading lists only, so words stay receptive and cannot be produced in the exam.
