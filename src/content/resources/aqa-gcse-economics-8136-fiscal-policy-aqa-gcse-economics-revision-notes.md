These notes cover **section 3.2.3.1 Fiscal policy** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "how fiscal policy can affect levels of income and expenditure within the economy", "how fiscal policy can be used to achieve government objectives" and "the meaning of a balanced budget" with "the consequences of operating a budget surplus and deficit". They add the types of government spending, automatic changes in the budget and more calculation types to the shorter [fiscal and monetary policy revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-fiscal-and-monetary-policy-revision-notes/). Full explanations are in the [fiscal policy study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-fiscal-policy-aqa-gcse-economics/); test yourself with the [fiscal policy practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-fiscal-policy-aqa-gcse-economics-practice/). Figures are invented unless a real UK fact is stated.

## Condensed recall

- **Fiscal policy** = government spending + taxation, used to influence the economy.
- In the UK the Chancellor of the Exchequer presents tax and spending plans in the Budget.
- **Expansionary**: spend more or tax less → demand rises → output and jobs rise; risk of inflation and more imports.
- **Contractionary**: spend less or tax more → demand falls → inflation eases; risk of slower growth and job losses.
- Extra spending has **knock-on effects**: one person's spending becomes another's income. Saving and imports weaken these effects.
- In a recession, benefit spending rises and tax receipts fall **automatically**, widening the deficit and supporting demand.
- **Deficit** = one year's gap (spending > revenue). **Debt** = total owed from all past borrowing.
- Any deficit, however small, adds to the debt.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Current spending | Day-to-day government spending, such as public sector wages |
| Capital spending | Government investment in long-lasting assets, such as roads and hospitals |
| Transfer payment | A payment such as a benefit or pension, made without any good or service in return |
| Direct tax | A tax on income or profit, such as income tax or corporation tax |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending, such as VAT or fuel duty |
| Expansionary fiscal policy | Higher spending or lower taxes to raise demand |
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Lower spending or higher taxes to reduce demand |
| Balanced budget | Government spending equals revenue |
| Budget surplus | Revenue exceeds spending |
| Budget deficit | Spending exceeds revenue |
| National debt | The total amount the government owes |

## Fiscal policy and objectives

| Objective | Policy direction | Main conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Growth and employment | Expansionary, especially capital spending | Inflation; larger deficit |
| Price stability | Contractionary | Higher unemployment; slower growth |
| Current account deficit | Contractionary (fewer imports bought) | Slower growth |
| Less inequality | Progressive taxes, benefits, public services | Weaker incentives; cost |

## Consequences of the budget position

| Position | Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| Deficit | Supports demand in a recession; can fund investment that raises future output | Adds to debt and interest; future tax rises or cuts; inflation if demand is already high |
| Surplus | Repays debt; lowers interest bills; room to borrow in a future downturn | Reduces demand; may come from cuts to services or investment |
| Balanced | No new borrowing | May require cutting spending in a recession, just when demand is weak |

## Worked calculations

**1. Surplus as a share of GDP.** Revenue 940, spending 905. Surplus = **35**. With GDP of 1,750: 35 ÷ 1,750 × 100 = **2.0%** of GDP.

**2. Debt falls with a surplus.** Debt of 1,500 and a surplus of 35 used to repay debt: debt falls to 1,500 − 35 = **1,465**.

**3. A recession's automatic effect.** A government had a surplus of 40. A recession raises benefit spending by 12 and cuts tax receipts by 30. New balance = 40 − 12 − 30 = **−2**, a small deficit, without any decision by the government.

**4. Deficit as a share of GDP.** A deficit of 48 with GDP of 1,600 is 48 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **3.0%**.

**5. VAT and a price.** A good costs £50 before VAT. At 20% VAT the price is 50 × 1.20 = **£60**. At 25% it would be 50 × 1.25 = **£62.50**.

## Limits to remember

- Time lags in planning, passing and building.
- Households may save tax cuts, especially when worried about jobs.
- Some extra spending goes on imports.
- Tax rises and spending cuts are politically difficult.
- A high existing debt limits further borrowing.

## Exam traps

- Interest rates are **monetary** policy, not fiscal.
- "The deficit fell" does not mean the debt fell.
- Benefits are government spending (transfer payments) even though they buy no output.
- A surplus is not automatically good; in a recession it can deepen the downturn.
- Always link the policy to a named objective and to a conflict.

## Self-test

1. State the two tools of fiscal policy.
2. Give one example each of current and capital government spending.
3. What is a transfer payment?
4. Distinguish between a budget deficit and the national debt.
5. Revenue 712, spending 760. State the budget position and its size.
6. Debt 900, then deficits of 30 and 25. What is the debt now?
7. Why does a recession widen a budget deficit even without a policy change?
8. Explain how a cut in VAT could increase spending.
9. Why might a tax cut have little effect on demand when consumers are worried about losing their jobs?
10. Give one benefit and one cost of running a budget surplus.
11. Which fiscal policy could reduce demand-pull inflation, and what is its main conflict?
12. Explain why capital spending can help growth in both the short and the long run.

### Answers

1. Government spending and taxation.
2. Current: teachers' salaries or medicines for hospitals. Capital: building a new road or school.
3. A payment such as a benefit or pension that moves income from taxpayers to recipients without buying output.
4. The deficit is the gap between spending and revenue in one year; the debt is the total owed from all past borrowing.
5. A deficit of 48.
6. 900 + 30 + 25 = 955.
7. Unemployment rises, so benefit spending increases, and incomes and spending fall, so income tax and VAT receipts fall.
8. Prices of many goods fall, so households can buy more with the same income and spending rises.
9. They may save the extra income as a precaution instead of spending it.
10. Benefit: it can repay debt and cut interest payments. Cost: it reduces demand, which may slow growth.
11. Higher taxes or lower government spending; the main conflict is higher unemployment and slower growth.
12. Short run: it raises demand and creates jobs in construction. Long run: new infrastructure raises productive capacity, so the economy can produce more.
