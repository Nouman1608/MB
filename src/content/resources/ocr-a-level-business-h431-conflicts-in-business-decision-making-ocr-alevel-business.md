## Why tools disagree

Each tool answers a different question, so it is no surprise that they can point in different directions.

| Tool | Question it answers | What it leaves out |
|---|---|---|
| Decision tree | Which option has the best average outcome, given the risks? | Timing of cash; qualitative factors |
| Payback | How quickly is the outlay recovered? | Cash after payback; time value of money |
| Average rate of return (ARR) | What is the average yearly return on the outlay? | Timing of cash |
| Net present value (NPV) | What are the cash flows worth in today's money, less the cost? | Depends on the discount rate chosen |
| Ansoff's matrix | How far does the option move from what the business knows? | Any figures at all |
| SWOT and qualitative views | Does it fit the business's strengths, culture and mission? | Measurement |

A conflict usually means the tools are measuring different things, not that one of them is wrong.

## Worked example 1: one proposal, five tools

**Case.** Kestle Leisure runs four gyms for adults. It is considering a £500,000 trampoline park for families. The finance director has prepared the following.

**Decision tree.** The park succeeds with probability 0.6, bringing total net cash inflows of £1.1 million over five years, or struggles with probability 0.4, bringing £300,000.

EV = (0.6 × 1,100,000) + (0.4 × 300,000) = 660,000 + 120,000 = £780,000. Net gain = 780,000 − 500,000 = **£280,000**.

**Investment appraisal**, using the expected annual inflows (£000), which total £780,000:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash inflow | 100 | 140 | 160 | 180 | 200 |

- **Payback:** cumulative inflows are 100, 240, 400 and 580. After 3 years, £100,000 remains, which is 100 ÷ 180 = 0.56 of year 4, so payback is **3.56 years**. Kestle's target is 3 years.
- **ARR:** total profit = 780 − 500 = £280,000; average a year = £56,000; ARR = 56,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **11.2%**. Kestle's target is 15%.
- **NPV at 10%:** using factors 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683 and 0.621, present values are 90.9 + 115.64 + 120.16 + 122.94 + 124.2 = £573,840, so NPV = **+£73,840**.
- **NPV at 15%:** using factors 0.870, 0.756, 0.658, 0.572 and 0.497, present values total £500,480, so NPV = **+£480**, almost zero.

**Ansoff's matrix.** Family entertainment is a new product for a new customer group, so this is diversification. It is related, since Kestle knows how to run leisure sites, but it has no experience of children's activities or safety.

**Qualitative evidence.** Managers say the gyms are quiet in the day and at weekends when families are free; there is no trampoline park within 20 miles; insurance for children's activities is expensive.

### Summary of the results

| Tool | Result | Points towards |
|---|---|---|
| Decision tree | Net gain £280,000 | Yes |
| Payback | 3.56 years, target 3 | No |
| ARR | 11.2%, target 15% | No |
| NPV at 10% | +£73,840 | Yes |
| NPV at 15% | +£480 | Marginal |
| Ansoff | Related diversification | Caution |
| Qualitative | Gap in local market; costly insurance | Mixed |

### Explaining the conflict

- The **decision tree** says yes by a large margin because it adds up cash without discounting it: £1 in year 5 counts the same as £1 today.
- **NPV** discounts later cash, so the gain shrinks to £73,840 at 10% and almost vanishes at 15%. The project's value is sensitive to the cost of finance.
- **Payback** says no because much of the cash comes late, in years 4 and 5.
- **ARR** says no because, averaged over five years, the return is below the target.
- **Ansoff** warns that the risk is higher than for Kestle's usual decisions, which may mean the 0.6 probability is optimistic.

## Resolving the conflict

A method for "evaluate which course of action":

1. **Decide which tools matter most for this business's objectives.** If Kestle is short of cash, payback matters most; if it wants long-term value, NPV matters most.
2. **Find the weakest assumption.** Here it is the 0.6 probability of success and the inflows in years 4 and 5, since the case depends on them.
3. **Test it.** If success were only 0.5 likely, the tree's EV would be £700,000 and net gain £200,000, still positive; but if every year's inflow fell in the same proportion, NPV at 10% would drop to about £15,000 (573,840 × 700 ÷ 780 = £514,985, less £500,000).
4. **Bring in qualitative factors and stakeholders.** Using quiet gym space suggests possible cost savings; children's safety adds a risk to reputation.
5. **Make a judgement with a condition.** For example: Kestle should go ahead only if it can finance the park at close to 10%, and should consider leasing equipment to cut the outlay, which would shorten payback. If finance costs 15%, the project adds almost no value and should be rejected.

## Judging likely success

To evaluate the likely success of a proposal, ask:

- **Are the inputs realistic?** Compare forecasts with the business's past record and with similar ventures.
- **How sensitive is the result?** Which single input, if wrong, would reverse the decision?
- **Does the business have the resources?** Cash, skills, management time.
- **Does it fit the objectives and mission?** A profitable project that contradicts the mission may damage the brand.
- **How will competitors and stakeholders react?**

## Usefulness of the tools

Strengths of using several tools: each covers another's blind spot; agreement between them gives confidence; disagreement shows exactly where the uncertainty lies. Limitations: all quantitative tools share the same forecasts, so if the forecasts are wrong, agreement is false comfort; tools take time and skill; managers may choose the tool that supports the answer they want. Tools are most useful when the forecasts are grounded in evidence and the decision is large enough to justify the analysis.

## Worked example 2: reasons for success or failure

**Case.** Thrushcross Cafés grew from two cafés to nine in three years, funded by loans. It went into administration last year. The evidence:

- Sales per café were forecast at £6,000 a week; the new cafés averaged £3,900.
- Interest rates rose during the period, raising loan repayments.
- Three of the new sites were in towns where a national coffee chain had just opened.
- Staff turnover at new sites was over 60%; customer reviews mentioned slow service.
- The owners did not review the plan until the seventh café had opened.

**Analysing the reasons.** Separate internal from external causes and link them:

- *External:* rising interest rates and strong competition reduced demand and raised costs.
- *Internal:* over-optimistic forecasts (actual sales 35% below forecast); rapid expansion funded by debt; poor staff retention leading to poor service; no review stage, so the problem was not caught.

**Judging the most important reason.** The rise in interest rates and the arrival of competitors made success harder, but the business could have survived them if it had grown more slowly. The most important reason was the decision to expand rapidly on borrowed money using forecasts that were never tested: when sales fell 35% short, there was no cushion. A Plan-Do-Review cycle after the third café would have shown the gap before the debt became unmanageable.

The same structure, identify causes, sort internal from external, link them, then choose the most important with a reason, works for explaining success too.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting each tool's result without explaining why they differ.
- Assuming the most sophisticated tool, usually NPV, must be right.
- Ignoring the business's objectives when deciding which tool matters most.
- Listing reasons for failure without saying which mattered most.

## Exam technique

"Explain why tools conflict": name what each measures and what it ignores. "Evaluate which course of action": state the results, explain the conflict, test the weakest input, bring in qualitative factors and judge with a condition. "Evaluate reasons for success or failure": prioritise.
