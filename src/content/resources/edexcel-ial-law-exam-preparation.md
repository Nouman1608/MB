Edexcel International A Level Law (YLA1) is the exception in Pearson's IAL suite: it is **not** split
into separate International Advanced Subsidiary and A2 stages. Both papers are compulsory, both are
taken in the same series, and that series is June only. Paper 1 (Underlying Principles of Law and the
English Legal System) covers the English legal system, the basics of the law of contract, and criminal
law. Paper 2 (The Law in Action) covers the law of tort and applied, synoptic content. Each paper
lasts **3 hours**, carries 100 marks and is worth 50%, and both are externally assessed. These notes
complement the site's guides to
[Underlying Principles of Law and the English Legal System](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-underlying-principles/)
and [The Law in Action](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-the-law-in-action/).

## Everything is examined at once -- so revision cannot be staged

Unlike the modular IAL sciences, this qualification offers no opportunity to bank a unit result and
move on. Both papers are sat together, and the June-only series means a resit is a full year away.
**Exam-preparation priority**: build a revision timetable that brings all content to readiness
simultaneously, rather than one that works through topics in teaching order and reaches tort last. The
material taught last is not the material examined last.

## Two three-hour papers in one series is an endurance demand

Six hours of extended legal writing is a substantial physical and cognitive load, and few candidates
will have rehearsed it. **Exam-preparation priority**: sit at least one full three-hour paper under
timed conditions before the exams -- not a series of shorter practices. Handwriting stamina, focus in
the third hour, and pacing across a long paper are all trainable, and none of them is trained by
40-minute practice essays.

## Paper 2's synoptic content draws on Paper 1's material

"Applied and synoptic" means Paper 2 expects the legal principles and legal-system knowledge from
Paper 1 to be available and used. **Exam-preparation priority**: do not treat Paper 1 revision as
complete once Paper 1 is sat -- and, before the exams, deliberately practise questions that require
connecting legal-system knowledge to a substantive area, since that connection is what synoptic
assessment rewards.

## Authority is the currency -- and a short reliable list beats a long shaky one

Legal answers are credited for supporting propositions with named cases or statutory provisions.
**Exam-preparation priority**: for each rule, learn one authority and one sentence on what it decided.
Under a three-hour paper's pressure, a small number of authorities you can state confidently is worth
far more than a long list you half-recall -- a misattributed case can do positive harm.

## Three hours for 100 marks makes planning affordable

At 1.8 minutes per mark, this paper is time-rich by A-level standards. **Exam-preparation priority**:
use that time on planning. Five minutes spent identifying the issues in a problem question before
writing produces a structured answer that addresses each issue in turn -- and structure is precisely
what distinguishes strong legal writing from a well-informed narrative.

## Worked routine: a problem question under a generous clock

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, mark scheme or examiner report.

```
Minutes 0-5: issue-spot the whole scenario first
List every legal issue raised, in order, before writing anything.
Note which party each issue concerns.

For each issue, in turn:
  a. State the issue narrowly and precisely.
  b. State the rule, with its authority, and one clause on what
     that authority established.
  c. Apply the rule to the specific facts, quoting the fact from
     the scenario that engages it.
  d. Address the strongest counter-argument or exception.
  e. Conclude on that issue specifically.

Final 10 minutes: overall conclusion and check
State the outcome for each party, and check that every issue on
your minute-5 list has actually been dealt with.
```

The minute-5 list is what the final check works against. The most common structural failure in a long
legal paper is an issue spotted early, mentally noted, and never written up.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: bring all content to readiness simultaneously -- both papers are sat in the
  same June series and a resit is a year away; sit at least one full three-hour timed paper; practise
  connecting legal-system knowledge to substantive areas for Paper 2's synoptic demand; learn one
  reliable authority plus its holding per rule.
- **During either paper**: spend the first five minutes issue-spotting the whole question.
- **On each issue**: state it narrowly, give the rule with authority, apply it to the quoted facts,
  address the counter-argument, and conclude on that issue.
- **In the last ten minutes**: check every issue on your opening list has been written up.

## Self-test

1. How does this qualification's structure differ from the modular IAL sciences, and what follows?
2. Why practise a full three-hour paper rather than several short essays?
3. What does "synoptic" mean in practice for Paper 2?
4. Why can a long list of half-remembered authorities be worse than a short reliable one?

**Answers:** 1. It is linear: both papers are compulsory and sat in the same June-only series, with no
banking of unit results -- so all content must be ready at once and a resit costs a full year.
2. Because handwriting stamina, third-hour focus and pacing across a long paper are trainable skills
that short practices do not develop, and six hours of extended writing is a substantial load.
3. That Paper 2 expects the legal principles and legal-system knowledge from Paper 1's content to be
available and connected to the substantive areas it examines. 4. Because a misattributed case can
damage an answer, whereas a small set of authorities stated confidently and accurately supports every
proposition it is offered for.
