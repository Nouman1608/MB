This guide teaches section 3.2.3.2 Break-even analysis of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1. The specification asks you to "interpret and manipulate break-even charts", to "calculate and interpret break-even output and profit/loss at different output levels", and to understand the significance of, and calculate, fixed costs, variable costs, total costs, revenue (or turnover), profit, break-even output, margin of safety, contribution per unit and total contribution. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. If your school is finishing the older 9625 specification, check its wording against your own copy.

The [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-finance/) introduces every formula with one example. This page goes further: it uses contribution as a shortcut for profit at any output, works through "what if" changes step by step, and shows how to turn the numbers into a judgement. Recall: [break-even revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725-revision-notes/) · Questions: [break-even practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725-practice/). Every business below is invented, and so are its figures.

## The idea in one sentence

Each unit sold brings in its price, but part of that price is used up straight away by the variable cost of making it. What is left over, the **contribution**, goes towards paying the fixed costs. Once enough units have been sold for total contribution to cover fixed costs, every further unit adds its full contribution to profit.

That sentence explains every formula in the section:

- Contribution per unit = selling price per unit − variable cost per unit
- Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold
- Profit = total contribution − fixed costs
- Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
- Margin of safety = actual (or planned) output − break-even output

The contribution route to profit gives the same answer as revenue − total costs, and it is quicker, which matters in the Unit 2 calculation questions.

## Worked example 1: Tallowmere Soap Co.

Tallowmere sells handmade soap bars at £6.00 each. Ingredients and packaging cost £2.40 a bar. Rent, salaries and insurance total £5,400 a month. It sells 2,100 bars a month.

```
Contribution per unit = 6.00 − 2.40 = £3.60
Break-even output     = 5,400 ÷ 3.60 = 1,500 bars
Margin of safety      = 2,100 − 1,500 = 600 bars
Profit                = (2,100 × 3.60) − 5,400 = 7,560 − 5,400 = £2,160
Check by the long way:
Revenue     = 2,100 × 6.00 = £12,600
Total costs = 5,400 + (2,100 × 2.40) = 5,400 + 5,040 = £10,440
Profit      = 12,600 − 10,440 = £2,160
```

**Profit or loss at other outputs.** The specification asks for profit or loss "at different output levels", so practise moving along the output axis.

| Bars sold | Total contribution (£) | Fixed costs (£) | Profit or loss (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,200 | 4,320 | 5,400 | (1,080) loss |
| 1,500 | 5,400 | 5,400 | 0 (break-even) |
| 2,100 | 7,560 | 5,400 | 2,160 profit |
| 2,500 | 9,000 | 5,400 | 3,600 profit |

Notice the pattern: every bar above 1,500 adds £3.60 of profit, and every bar below it adds £3.60 of loss. At 1,200 bars Tallowmere is 300 bars short of break-even, so the loss is 300 × £3.60 = £1,080. Profit can therefore also be found as margin of safety × contribution per unit.

**Output needed for a profit target.** If the owner wants £3,000 profit a month, total contribution must cover the fixed costs **and** the target: (5,400 + 3,000) ÷ 3.60 = 2,333.3, so 2,334 bars. Round up: 2,333 bars would fall just short of the target.

## Reading a break-even chart

Examiners give charts as well as figures. Read them in this order:

1. **Fixed costs**: the height where the total-cost line meets the vertical axis (output zero).
2. **Variable cost per unit**: the rise in total costs between two outputs, divided by the change in output.
3. **Price**: revenue at any output divided by that output.
4. **Break-even**: the output where revenue and total costs cross. Read down to the output axis for units, and across to the money axis for the revenue at break-even.
5. **Profit or loss at an output**: the vertical gap between the revenue and total-cost lines at that output. Below break-even the cost line is higher (loss); above it the revenue line is higher (profit).
6. **Margin of safety**: the horizontal distance between break-even and the actual output.

For Tallowmere, the total-cost line starts at £5,400 and reaches £10,440 at 2,100 bars, a rise of £5,040 over 2,100 bars, which gives £2.40 a bar. That is the method you use when a question gives only the chart.

## Manipulating the chart: what-if changes

"Manipulate" means showing what happens to the lines, the break-even output and the margin of safety when one figure changes. Change **one** figure at a time and recalculate from contribution.

| Change | Effect on chart | Contribution per unit | Break-even output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price rises | Revenue line steeper | Rises | Falls |
| Price falls | Revenue line flatter | Falls | Rises |
| Variable cost per unit rises | Total-cost line steeper (same starting point) | Falls | Rises |
| Fixed costs rise | Fixed-cost and total-cost lines shift up in parallel | No change | Rises |
| Sales volume rises | No line moves; actual output moves right | No change | No change |

The last row catches many candidates: selling more raises profit and the margin of safety but does **not** change break-even output.

### Worked example 2: a supplier price rise

Tallowmere's supplier raises the cost of ingredients so that the variable cost becomes £2.70 a bar.

```
New contribution = 6.00 − 2.70 = £3.30
New break-even   = 5,400 ÷ 3.30 = 1,636.4, so 1,637 bars
Margin of safety = 2,100 − 1,637 = 463 bars
Profit at 2,100  = (2,100 × 3.30) − 5,400 = 6,930 − 5,400 = £1,530
```

Profit falls by £630, which is 2,100 bars × the £0.30 rise in variable cost.

The owner considers raising the price to £6.50 to protect profit. Contribution becomes 6.50 − 2.70 = £3.80 and break-even falls to 5,400 ÷ 3.80 = 1,421.1, so 1,422 bars. But a higher price may lose customers. If sales drop to 1,900 bars, profit is (1,900 × 3.80) − 5,400 = £1,820, still above £1,530. Sales would have to fall below (1,530 + 5,400) ÷ 3.80 = 1,823.7 bars before the price rise left Tallowmere worse off. So the decision turns on one question: will sales fall by more than about 276 bars a month? That depends on how sensitive Tallowmere's customers are to price, which links to price elasticity in the marketing topic.

This is the shape of a strong answer: calculate the new position, compare it with the old one, find the point at which the decision changes, then judge using the context.

## The significance of each figure

- **Fixed costs** must be paid whatever is sold, so a business with high fixed costs needs high sales before it makes any profit, and suffers heavily when sales fall.
- **Variable costs** rise with output, so they are the costs a business can control by negotiating with suppliers or reducing waste.
- **Contribution per unit** shows how much each sale is worth to the business before fixed costs. A product with a high contribution reaches break-even quickly.
- **Break-even output** is the minimum a plan must achieve. Banks and investors look at it when judging a business plan.
- **Margin of safety** measures risk: a large margin means sales can fall a long way before losses begin.
- **Profit at different outputs** lets managers test a range of sales forecasts rather than relying on one figure.

## Strengths and limitations

**Strengths:** quick to calculate; shows the effect of a price or cost change before it is made; useful for start-ups that have no trading record; a clear visual for owners and lenders.

**Limitations:**

- It assumes every unit is sold at one price, but businesses offer discounts for bulk orders and run promotions.
- It assumes variable cost per unit is constant, but bulk-buying discounts can lower it as output rises.
- It assumes all output is sold, with no unsold stock.
- Fixed costs are fixed only within a range of output: a second production shift or extra premises makes them step up.
- It is hard to apply to a business selling many products that share fixed costs.
- The result is only as reliable as the cost and sales estimates behind it.

When you evaluate, pick the limitation that matters most for the business in the case. A bakery with seasonal discounts is hurt by the one-price assumption; a fast-growing firm near full capacity is hurt by the fixed-cost assumption.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of by contribution per unit.
- Rounding break-even down. If the answer is 1,636.4, the business must sell 1,637 units to avoid a loss.
- Giving break-even in pounds when the question asks for units, or the reverse. Revenue at break-even = break-even output × price.
- Writing the margin of safety as a negative number without saying the business is operating below break-even.
- Saying higher sales lower break-even output.
- Mixing time periods: monthly fixed costs with yearly sales.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out each step on its own line with units. If a later step goes wrong, correct earlier working can still earn credit.
- **Explain**: give the effect and the reason, in context. "Break-even rises because each soap bar now contributes 30p less towards Tallowmere's £5,400 of fixed costs."
- **Analyse**: build a chain from the change to the figures to the consequence for the business, such as a smaller margin of safety leaving it more exposed if a rival opens nearby.
- **Evaluate**: use your calculation, weigh it against at least one limitation that fits the case, and finish with a clear, supported judgement.
