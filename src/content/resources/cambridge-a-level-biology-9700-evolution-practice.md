## Section A

**1.** (17.3.1) Define the term *gene pool*. **[1]**

**2.** (17.3.1) Outline the theory of evolution. **[3]**

**3.** (17.3.2) Explain why comparing the base sequences of a gene can show differences between two closely related species that a comparison of the amino acid sequences of the protein does not show. **[2]**

**4.** (17.3.3) State what is meant by *genetic isolation* and explain why it is needed for speciation. **[2]**

**5.** (17.3.3) For each example, state whether it could lead to allopatric or sympatric speciation, with a reason.

**(a)** On one hillside, grass plants growing on metal-contaminated soil flower several weeks earlier than nearby plants on normal soil. **[1]**

**(b)** Rising sea level turns a peninsula into an island, separating a lizard population from the mainland population. **[1]**

**(c)** In one meadow, two groups of crickets differ in the pulse rate of the males' song, and females approach only one song type. **[1]**

**6.** (17.3.1) A student wrote: "Rabbits on the cold island evolved longer fur because they needed to keep warm." Rewrite this explanation correctly in terms of alleles and selection. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** (17.3.2) The same 80-base section of a gene was sequenced in four species, P, Q, R and S. The table shows the number of positions that differ.

| | P | Q | R | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | – | 2 | 12 | 20 |
| Q | | – | 13 | 19 |
| R | | | – | 22 |
| S | | | | – |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage difference between P and R. **[1]**

**(b)** State which two species share the most recent common ancestor. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe the order in which the four lineages branched, starting with the earliest split. **[2]**

**(d)** Assume one difference builds up in this section every 2.5 million years. Estimate how long ago the lineages leading to P and R diverged. **[2]**

**(e)** Give two reasons why this estimate is uncertain. **[2]**

**8.** (17.3.3) A single population of a freshwater fish lives in a large lake. During a long drought the lake shrinks into two separate lakes. One lake has clear water and many fish-eating birds; the other is muddy, with few birds.

**(a)** Explain how the two fish populations could become separate species. **[5]**

**(b)** Suggest how biologists could test whether the two populations have become separate species. **[2]**

**9.** (17.3.3) Caterpillars of a moth species feed only on plant species F. A new plant species, G, is introduced to the same area. Some female moths begin to lay eggs on G. Adult moths tend to mate on the plant species on which they fed as caterpillars.

**(a)** Explain how this could lead to the formation of a new moth species without a geographical barrier. **[4]**

**(b)** Name this type of speciation. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why speciation would be slower if some moths regularly mated on the "other" plant species. **[1]**

**10.** (17.3.3) Two populations of a songbird live in the same forest. Their songs differ. In a playback experiment, females from population A were played recordings. They approached the population A song in 46 of 50 trials and the population B song in 4 of 50 trials.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of trials in which females approached the population B song. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how this difference in behaviour could contribute to speciation. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest how DNA sequence data could be used to support the idea that the two populations are diverging. **[2]**

**11.** (17.3.2) Discuss how DNA sequence data can be used to show evolutionary relationships between species. **[6]**

**12.** (17.3.2) A protein of 104 amino acids was compared in species J and K. The amino acid sequences differed at 2 positions. The 312 coding bases of the gene differed at 9 positions.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage difference for the amino acid sequences and for the base sequences, each to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the base sequences show a greater percentage difference. **[2]**

**(c)** State why the same gene must be used when comparing species. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time [1].

**2.** New species form from pre-existing species [1]; as a result of changes to the gene pool / allele frequencies [1]; from generation to generation, over a long period of time [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Organisms change over time" without gene pools or allele frequencies scores at most one mark.

**3.** The genetic code is degenerate, so some base substitutions do not change the amino acid coded for [1]. These silent differences appear in DNA data but not in protein data, so DNA gives finer resolution [1].

**4.** A barrier that prevents gene flow (the exchange of alleles by interbreeding) between populations [1]. Without it, alleles would continue to mix, so the gene pools could not diverge independently [1].

**5. (a)** Sympatric: same area; separated ecologically by flowering time, so they cannot cross-pollinate [1].

**(b)** Allopatric: the sea is a geographical barrier [1].

**(c)** Sympatric: same area; separated behaviourally by song and mate choice [1].

**6.** Variation in fur length already existed, caused by different alleles (arising by random mutation) [1]. On the cold island, rabbits with alleles for longer fur were more likely to survive and reproduce [1]. They passed these alleles to offspring, so the frequency of the long-fur allele increased over generations [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Needed to" implies purpose; individuals do not change their alleles in response to need.

**7. (a)** 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = **15.0%** [1].

**(b)** P and Q [1]; they have the fewest differences (2), so mutations have had least time to accumulate since they shared a common ancestor [1].

**(c)** S branched off first, as it has the most differences from all others [1]; then R separated from the P–Q line, and P and Q separated most recently [1].

**(d)** 12 × 2.5 [1] = about **30 million years ago** [1].

**(e)** Any two: mutation rate may not be constant [1]; only one short section compared, which may not represent the genome [1]; one position may have mutated more than once; only one individual per species sampled.

**8. (a)** The drought creates a geographical barrier, so there is no gene flow between the populations [1]. Different mutations arise in each population [1]. The lakes have different conditions, for example predation by birds in the clear lake, so there are different selection pressures [1]; different alleles are selected for, such as those for camouflage colour in the clear lake [1]. Over many generations allele frequencies diverge, and genetic drift may add to this in small populations, until the fish can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1].

**(b)** Put fish from both lakes together under suitable conditions and see if they mate and produce offspring [1]; then test whether those offspring are themselves fertile [1].

**9. (a)** Moths feeding on G mate mainly with other moths on G, so gene flow between the F and G groups is reduced [1]. Different mutations arise in each group [1]. The two plants give different selection pressures, for example different leaf chemicals, so different alleles are selected for in each group [1]. Over many generations the gene pools diverge until the groups cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1].

**(b)** Sympatric speciation (ecological separation) [1].

**(c)** Mating between groups maintains gene flow, so alleles keep mixing and the gene pools diverge more slowly [1].

**10. (a)** 4 ÷ 50 × 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** Females choose mates with their own population's song, so there is little interbreeding / gene flow [1]. The gene pools then change independently through mutation, selection and drift [1]. Over many generations this can lead to reproductive isolation, where they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring [1].

**(c)** Sequence the same genes in birds from each population [1]; differences between populations greater than differences within each population would support divergence [1].

**11.** Indicative content, one mark each, to a maximum of 6:
- Compare the base sequence of the same gene or DNA region in different species.
- Count the number or percentage of positions that differ.
- Fewer differences suggest a more recent common ancestor / closer relationship.
- Mutations accumulate after species diverge, so differences increase with time.
- If the mutation rate is roughly constant, differences estimate the time since divergence (molecular clock).
- Data can be used to construct evolutionary trees, and can compare organisms with no obvious shared features.
- Limitation: mutation rates differ between genes and lineages.
- Limitation: short sections or single individuals may not be representative.
*Examiner insight:* For "discuss", at least one limitation is needed for full marks.

**12. (a)** Amino acids: 2 ÷ 104 × 100 = **1.9%** [1]; bases: 9 ÷ 312 × 100 = **2.9%** [1].

**(b)** Some base substitutions are silent because the code is degenerate [1], so they change the DNA but not the amino acid sequence [1].

**(c)** Only sequences that share an origin in a common ancestor can be meaningfully compared position by position [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining speciation with no mention of gene flow, alleles or allele frequencies.
- Saying that the environment causes mutations.
- Writing "cannot breed" instead of "cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring".
- Reading a difference table for the largest number when asked for the closest relatives.
- Giving a molecular-clock date with no sense that it is an estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

**How are these questions organised?**
Section A has short questions on single ideas; Section B has data and extended questions. The learning outcome is given in brackets at the start of each question.

**How do I decide between allopatric and sympatric in an unfamiliar example?**
Ask whether the populations live in the same place. If a physical barrier separates them, it is allopatric; if they share an area but differ in habitat use, timing or behaviour, it is sympatric.
