## Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

**Trend:** it decreases down Group 7 from fluorine to iodine.

**Explanation:**

- The atoms get bigger down the group, because each element has one more electron shell.
- The bonding pair of electrons is therefore further from the nucleus.
- There is more shielding by inner electrons, which offsets the larger nuclear charge.
- So the nucleus attracts the bonding pair less strongly.

Fluorine is the most electronegative element of all.

## Boiling point

The halogens exist as diatomic molecules (X₂) held together by weak London (induced dipole-induced dipole) forces. The boiling points rise down the group.

| Halogen | State at room temperature | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Fluorine, F₂ | Pale yellow gas | –188 |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | Pale green gas | –34 |
| Bromine, Br₂ | Red-brown liquid | 59 |
| Iodine, I₂ | Grey-black solid | 184 |

**Explanation:** down the group, molecules have more electrons, so the London forces between them are stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point is higher. The covalent bond within each molecule is not broken when the halogen boils.

## Oxidising ability and displacement reactions

Halogen atoms gain an electron to form halide ions: X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. A halogen that does this easily is a strong oxidising agent. **Oxidising ability decreases down the group:** fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent and iodine is the weakest.

This is shown by displacement reactions. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution.

| Halogen added | Halide solution | Observation | Ionic equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine water | Bromide | Colourless to orange | Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ |
| Chlorine water | Iodide | Colourless to brown (or a black solid) | Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂ |
| Bromine water | Iodide | Orange to brown | Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ |
| Bromine water | Chloride | No reaction | |
| Iodine | Chloride or bromide | No reaction | |

If an organic solvent such as hexane is shaken with the mixture, the colours are clearer: bromine appears orange-red and iodine appears purple in the upper (organic) layer.

### Worked example 1: writing the ionic equation

Chlorine is bubbled through potassium bromide solution.

- Chlorine is the stronger oxidising agent, so it takes electrons from bromide ions.
- Half-equations: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ (reduction); 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ (oxidation).
- Add them: **Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂**. Potassium ions are spectators.
- Chlorine is reduced (0 to –1) and bromide is oxidised (–1 to 0).

## Reducing ability of halide ions

Halide ions can lose electrons to form the halogen. **The reducing ability of the halide ions increases down the group.** The larger the ion, the further its outer electron is from the nucleus and the more shielded it is, so the more easily it is lost.

The trend is shown by the reactions of solid sodium halides with **concentrated sulfuric acid**.

| Halide | Reaction | Observations | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chloride | NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl | Steamy fumes of HCl | HCl only. Not a redox reaction. |
| Bromide | NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr, then 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | Steamy fumes, orange-brown fumes of Br₂, choking SO₂ | HBr, Br₂, SO₂ |
| Iodide | NaI + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HI, then 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; 6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O; 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O | Purple fumes or black solid I₂, yellow solid S, rotten-egg smell of H₂S | HI, I₂, SO₂, S, H₂S |

Sulfuric acid is reduced by bromide to sulfur dioxide (sulfur +6 to +4). Iodide is a stronger reducing agent: it reduces sulfur from +6 to +4 (SO₂), 0 (S) and –2 (H₂S).

### Worked example 2: the HI and H₂S reaction

Show that the formation of H₂S from HI is a redox reaction.

- In 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O, iodine changes from –1 in HI to 0 in I₂ (oxidised).
- Sulfur changes from +6 in H₂SO₄ to –2 in H₂S (reduced). That is a gain of 8 electrons per sulfur, matching the 8 iodide ions losing 1 electron each.

## Testing for halide ions: acidified silver nitrate

The test: add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution.

| Halide ion | Precipitate | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride | White | Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) |
| Bromide | Cream | Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s) |
| Iodide | Yellow | Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s) |

**Why silver nitrate:** it supplies Ag⁺ ions, which form insoluble silver halides with halide ions. Silver nitrate is soluble, and the nitrate ion does not interfere.

**Why acidified with nitric acid:** carbonate and hydroxide ions would also form precipitates with Ag⁺ (silver carbonate and silver oxide), giving a false positive. The dilute nitric acid reacts with these ions and removes them first. Nitric acid is used because its nitrate ion is already present and does not form a precipitate. Hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions, and sulfuric acid would form a precipitate of silver sulfate.

**Why ammonia is added:** the silver halide precipitates are similar in appearance, so ammonia solution separates them.

| Precipitate | Dilute ammonia | Concentrated ammonia |
|---|---|---|
| AgCl (white) | Dissolves | Dissolves |
| AgBr (cream) | Does not dissolve | Dissolves |
| AgI (yellow) | Does not dissolve | Does not dissolve |

The dissolving involves a soluble complex: AgCl + 2NH₃ → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + Cl⁻. The solubility of the silver halides in ammonia decreases from chloride to iodide.

### Worked example 3: identify an unknown

A white precipitate forms when acidified silver nitrate is added to solution X. It dissolves in dilute ammonia. X contains chloride ions. If the precipitate were cream and dissolved only in concentrated ammonia, it would be bromide.

### Worked example 4: a precipitation calculation

25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium chloride is added to excess acidified silver nitrate. Calculate the mass of silver chloride formed (Mᵣ = 143.4).

```
n(NaCl) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol
n(AgCl) = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol (1:1)
mass = 2.50 × 10⁻³ × 143.4 = 0.359 g
```

## Common mistakes

- Saying chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent because it is "more reactive" without referring to gaining electrons.
- Writing that the covalent bond breaks when a halogen boils.
- Using "bond strength" as the reason for the boiling point trend instead of London forces.
- Saying chloride ions reduce sulfuric acid. Only HCl forms.
- Forgetting the first step (formation of HX) before the redox step for bromide and iodide.
- Using hydrochloric acid or sulfuric acid to acidify the silver nitrate test.
- Describing the AgBr precipitate as white or yellow instead of cream.
- Mixing up which precipitates dissolve in dilute and in concentrated ammonia.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain the trend in electronegativity**: refer to atomic radius, shielding and attraction between the nucleus and the bonding pair.
- **Explain the trend in boiling point**: the number of electrons, the strength of London forces and the energy needed.
- **Explain why the silver nitrate is acidified**: name the ions that would interfere and say what the acid does.
- **Write the equation**: include state symbols if the question asks.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does iodine not displace bromine from bromide solution?**
Iodine is a weaker oxidising agent than bromine, so it cannot take electrons from bromide ions.

**Is fluorine considered in the displacement reactions?**
It is too reactive and dangerous for school use, and it reacts with water. The practical work uses chlorine, bromine and iodine.

**Why are concentrated sulfuric acid reactions done with solid halides?**
The reaction gives off gases, which can be seen and tested. In solution, the acid would be diluted and behave as an ordinary acid.
