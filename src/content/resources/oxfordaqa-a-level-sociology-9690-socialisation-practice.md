## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **primary socialisation**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **anticipatory socialisation**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what Oakley meant by **canalisation**. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what Mead meant by the **generalised other**. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** methods religions use to socialise their members. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways the media may socialise young people. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** examples of inadequate socialisation. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which the workplace acts as an agency of socialisation. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** differences between functionalist and Marxist views of socialisation in
schools. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which socialisation may be an active rather than a passive process.
**[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that the family is the most
important agency of socialisation. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the feminist view that socialisation reproduces gender inequality. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The early learning of basic norms, values, language and skills [1], mainly within the family
during infancy and early childhood [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Learning the norms and expectations of a role before entering it [1], for example a trainee
teacher copying experienced teachers' behaviour on placement [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Channelling children towards toys, games and activities seen as appropriate to their sex [1],
such as dolls for girls and construction toys for boys, so that gendered interests develop [1].
**[2]**

**4.** The general expectations and attitudes of society as a whole [1], which a child internalises
through games and interaction, allowing them to see themselves as others do [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Two methods, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Teachings and texts [1]: sacred texts and sermons pass on moral rules about behaviour [1].
- Rituals and festivals [1]: shared ceremonies reinforce belonging and collective values [1].
- Community expectations [1]: approval or disapproval from fellow believers encourages conformity [1].
- Religious schooling [1]: formal teaching of beliefs and practices to children [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- Role models [1]: celebrities and influencers present lifestyles and values young people may copy [1].
- Representations [1]: repeated images of gender, ethnicity or body shape shape ideas of what is normal [1].
- Advertising [1]: encourages consumer identities and the value placed on possessions [1].
- Online feedback [1]: likes and comments act as sanctions that reward some behaviour [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two examples, 2 marks each:
- Extreme isolation [1]: cases such as Genie, found in California in 1970, show children deprived of
  interaction fail to acquire language fully [1].
- Institutional neglect [1]: children in poorly staffed institutions may lack the attention needed
  to develop social skills [1].
- Contested "underclass" claims [1]: New Right writers such as Murray argued some families failed to
  pass on mainstream norms, though critics reject this as blaming the poor [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example [1]):
- **Induction and training** [1]: new employees learn formal rules and skills [1], which also teaches
  the organisation's values, such as customer service or punctuality [1]; for example, staff training
  in fast-food chains standardises how workers greet customers [1].
- **Organisational culture and sanctions** [1]: colleagues and managers reward or disapprove of
  behaviour [1], through promotion, praise or exclusion [1]; for example, informal norms about working
  late can spread long-hours expectations [1].
- Credit: anticipatory socialisation; codes of conduct; uniforms and dress codes.
**[8]**

**9.** Two differences, 4 marks each:
- **Whose values** [1]: functionalists say schools pass on shared values that integrate society
  (Durkheim's society in miniature) [1]; Marxists say schools pass on ruling-class ideology [1],
  making inequality appear fair (Althusser) [1].
- **Purpose of the hidden curriculum** [1]: Parsons sees school teaching universalistic standards
  and achievement for a meritocracy [1]; Bowles and Gintis see the hidden curriculum corresponding to
  the workplace [1], teaching obedience and acceptance of hierarchy so workers can be exploited [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Development of the self through interaction** [1]: Mead argued children learn by taking the role
  of others [1], interpreting expectations rather than simply absorbing them [1], producing an "I"
  that can respond creatively as well as a socialised "me" [1].
- **Resistance** [1]: people can reject what agencies teach [1]; Willis's lads rejected school values
  and created a counter-school culture [1], showing pupils interpret and negotiate norms [1].
- Credit: Cooley's looking-glass self; young people choosing media and online communities; Wrong's
  critique of the oversocialised view.
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge of several agencies and perspectives; sustained application to a named
  society; balanced evaluation; justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* primary socialisation lays foundations of language, norms and identity;
Parsons sees families as personality "factories"; Oakley shows gender is first learned at home;
religion, language and class culture are transmitted through families; in many societies extended
kin remain central to moral education. *Against:* schools spend many hours with children and teach
universalistic norms (Parsons) and ideology (Bowles and Gintis); peers dominate in adolescence;
media and online platforms increasingly shape values and identities; religion may be the main
moral agency in some societies; the workplace continues socialisation in adulthood; interactionists
argue individuals actively select influences. *Application:* reward examples from a named society,
such as the role of grandparents, religious schooling or social media. *Conclusion:* supported
judgement, for example that the family is most important in early life but its relative weight
falls as other agencies take over. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *For the view:* Oakley's canalisation and manipulation; gendered toys, clothing
and chores; schools' subject choices and teacher expectations; media stereotypes of women's bodies
and domestic roles; religions that define separate roles; radical feminists see socialisation
reproducing patriarchy; Marxist feminists link it to women's unpaid labour. *Against:* Sharpe's
comparison shows girls' priorities shifting towards careers; girls now outperform boys in education
in many societies; liberal feminists see progress through reform; interactionists stress agency
and resistance; gender inequality also results from structures such as labour markets and laws, not
socialisation alone; intersectionality shows experiences vary by class and ethnicity.
*Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement. **[20]**
