Condensed notes for section 1.1, *The process of learning and socialisation*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 1. The full explanations and a worked answer are in the [1.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-process-of-learning-and-socialisation/); practise with the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-process-of-learning-and-socialisation-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Culture, roles, norms, values, beliefs, customs, ideology, power and status as elements in the social construction of reality.
- The importance of socialisation in influencing behaviour, including the nature versus nurture debate.
- Agencies of socialisation and social control: family, education, peer group, media and religion.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Socialisation | The lifelong process of learning the culture of a society |
| Culture | A group's whole way of life, material and non-material, passed between generations |
| Values | General beliefs about what is good or worth achieving |
| Norms | Specific rules of behaviour that put values into practice |
| Customs | Norms that have become traditional over a long period |
| Beliefs | Ideas held to be true that justify values and customs |
| Status | A social position; ascribed (given at birth) or achieved (earned) |
| Role | The expected behaviour attached to a status |
| Role conflict | When the demands of two roles clash |
| Ideology | Ideas that present a group's interests as natural or universal |
| Power | The ability to get your way even against resistance (Weber) |
| Social construction of reality | The idea that social categories are created and maintained by society, not fixed by nature |
| Social control | Processes that make people conform to norms |
| Resocialisation | Replacing earlier learning with new norms and identity |

## Condensed recall

**How the elements link.** Values produce norms; norms that last become customs; beliefs justify them. Statuses carry roles. Power and ideology decide whose culture is treated as normal.

**Three proofs of social construction.** Behaviour and categories vary between cultures, change over time and are contested between groups.

**Types of socialisation.**
- Primary: early childhood, family, language and basic norms.
- Secondary: school, peers, media, religion, work; rules for dealing with strangers.
- Resocialisation: total institutions strip the old self (Goffman, *Asylums*, 1961).
- Anticipatory: learning a role before entering it.

**Nature versus nurture.**
- Nature: E.O. Wilson's *Sociobiology* (1975) links some social behaviour to evolution; hormone research on aggression.
- Nurture: cross-cultural variation; rapid historical change; deprived or isolated children lacking language; evidence of differential treatment of boys and girls.
- Best conclusion: interaction. Biology supplies capacities; socialisation shapes their use. Critics say "natural" explanations can act as ideology.

**Theories of socialisation.**
- Functionalist: internalisation of shared values. Critique: Dennis Wrong (1961), the "oversocialized" view of people.
- Marxist: socialisation spreads ruling-class ideology.
- Feminist: socialisation reproduces patriarchy through gender norms.
- Interactionist: Cooley's looking-glass self (1902); G.H. Mead's play and game stages and the generalised other (1934). The self is built through interaction.

## The five agencies

| Agency | Socialises through | Controls through | Key name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family | Imitation, praise, gender norms | Punishment, withdrawal of affection | Parsons (personality formed around shared values) |
| Education | Formal and hidden curriculum | Grades, detention, exclusion | Bowles and Gintis (1976): hidden curriculum prepares for work hierarchy |
| Peer group | Acceptance, shared styles | Teasing, exclusion | Interactionist negotiation of identity |
| Media | Role models, repeated representations | Shaming, moral panics | Bandura (1961) imitation versus Blumler and Katz (1974) active audience |
| Religion | Moral codes, ritual, sacred texts | Promise of reward, fear of sanction, community disapproval | Durkheim: collective conscience |

## Exam traps

- **Defining without linking.** Show how values become norms and how power shapes both.
- **Forgetting social control.** The syllabus names "agencies of socialisation *and social control*".
- **One-sided nature versus nurture.** Give the biological case before rejecting or qualifying it.
- **Passive learners.** Use interactionist ideas and the active audience to show people interpret and resist.
- **All agencies agree.** Peer groups and media can contradict family and school; say so.
- **Name-dropping.** A study only earns credit if you say what it shows.
- **Confusing customs and norms.** All customs are norms, but not all norms are customs.

## Self-test

1. Define *norms* and give an example.
2. What is the difference between ascribed and achieved status?
3. Give an example of role conflict.
4. State three kinds of evidence that reality is socially constructed.
5. What is resocialisation, and where does Goffman say it happens?
6. What did E.O. Wilson argue in *Sociobiology* (1975)?
7. Give two pieces of evidence for the nurture side of the debate.
8. What did Dennis Wrong criticise in 1961?
9. Explain Cooley's "looking-glass self".
10. What is the "generalised other" in G.H. Mead's theory?
11. According to Bowles and Gintis, what does the hidden curriculum teach?
12. How does the active audience view challenge the idea that the media socialise people directly?

### Answers

1. Specific rules of behaviour based on values, such as queuing or removing shoes before entering a home.
2. Ascribed status is given at birth, such as sex category or royal birth; achieved status is earned, such as a qualification or job.
3. A parent who is also an employee and must choose between caring for a sick child and attending work.
4. Variation between cultures, change over time, and disagreement between groups over definitions.
5. Replacing earlier norms and identity with new ones; in total institutions such as prisons, psychiatric hospitals and military training.
6. That some social behaviour has an evolutionary, genetic basis because it helped genes survive.
7. Any two: cross-cultural variation in gender roles; rapid historical change in behaviour; isolated children failing to acquire language; differential treatment of boys and girls.
8. The functionalist "oversocialized" view that people absorb society's values completely and simply act them out.
9. We form our self-image from how we imagine others see and judge us.
10. The general expectations of society as a whole, which a child learns to take into account in the game stage.
11. Acceptance of hierarchy, obedience and punctuality, preparing pupils for work in capitalism.
12. It says audiences choose and interpret media for their own needs, so effects are not automatic.
