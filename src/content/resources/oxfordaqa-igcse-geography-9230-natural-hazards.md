This guide covers section 3.1.1.1, Natural hazards, the first theme of Section A (The challenge of natural hazards) in Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The key idea is: "Natural hazards pose major risks to people and property." The specification content is short: a definition of a natural hazard, types of natural hazard, and factors affecting hazard risk. Short does not mean easy. These three ideas are the toolkit you use again in every later hazard question, so it pays to know them well. For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/).

## What the specification lists

- A definition of a natural hazard.
- Types of natural hazard.
- Factors affecting hazard risk.

Unit 1 is examined in Paper 1, which lasts 1 hour 30 minutes and carries 80 marks. Section A of that paper is worth 30 marks, and all of it must be answered. Hazard questions often mix short recall with longer explanations, so you need both precise definitions and the ability to build a chain of reasoning.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural hazard | A natural process or event that has the potential to cause death, injury, damage to property or disruption to people's lives |
| Natural disaster | A hazard that actually happens and causes serious harm to a community; a disaster needs people to be affected |
| Magnitude | The size or strength of a hazard event |
| Frequency | How often events of a given size occur |
| Vulnerability | How easily people and property can be harmed by a hazard |
| Capacity to cope | The ability of a community to prepare for, withstand and recover from a hazard |
| Risk | The probability that a hazard will cause harm to a particular population |
| Prediction | Saying when and where a hazard will happen; some hazards can be predicted far better than others |

## Defining a natural hazard

A natural hazard is a natural event or process that threatens people or property. The words that matter are "natural" and "threatens". A factory fire is a hazard but not a natural one. A volcano erupting on an uninhabited island is a natural event, but it is not much of a hazard because nothing is threatened.

This leads to the difference between a hazard and a disaster. A hazard is the threat. A disaster is what happens when the threat meets a vulnerable population and serious harm results. The same earthquake can be a minor event in one place and a disaster in another, depending on who and what is in its path. Exam answers that say "an earthquake is a disaster" lose precision. Say "an earthquake is a hazard, and it becomes a disaster when it causes major loss of life or damage".

A good one-mark definition: "A natural event that can cause harm to people or damage to property." A good two-mark definition adds a second idea, such as the difference between hazard and disaster, or an example.

## Types of natural hazard

The specification asks for types of natural hazard without fixing a list, so a sensible classification by origin works well.

**Geophysical (tectonic) hazards.** These come from processes inside the Earth. Examples are earthquakes, volcanic eruptions and tsunamis. Tectonic hazards are examined in more detail in the next theme.

**Atmospheric (weather) hazards.** These come from processes in the atmosphere. Examples are tropical storms (hurricanes, cyclones and typhoons), tornadoes, severe thunderstorms, heatwaves, cold spells, and drought, which is a long period of low rainfall.

**Hydrological hazards.** These involve water. River floods and coastal floods are the clearest examples. Floods often follow atmospheric hazards, since tropical storms and heavy rain supply the water.

**Geomorphological hazards.** These involve movement of land. Landslides, mudflows, rock falls and avalanches are examples. They can be triggered by earthquakes, heavy rain or human activity on slopes.

**Biological hazards.** These involve living organisms, such as disease epidemics and wildfires (which also depend on dry weather). Some courses include them, though the specification's hazard examples centre on tectonic and weather hazards.

Two points help in the exam. First, hazards are often linked. An earthquake can set off a tsunami and landslides. A tropical storm brings wind, storm surge and flooding together. The main hazard is called the primary hazard, and the ones it triggers are secondary hazards. Second, a named type should be matched to a process. "Floods" is a weak answer to "name a hazard caused by the atmosphere" because floods are usually classed as hydrological. "Tropical storm" or "drought" is safer.

## Factors affecting hazard risk

Geographers often express risk as an idea:

**Risk is higher when the hazard is more severe, when people are more vulnerable, and when capacity to cope is lower.**

Some textbooks write it as risk = (hazard × vulnerability) ÷ capacity to cope. You do not need to calculate it for this specification, but it is a useful way to organise answers. Group the factors into three sets.

### 1. The nature of the hazard

- **Magnitude.** Bigger events usually release more energy and cause more harm. A magnitude 7 earthquake releases far more energy than a magnitude 5.
- **Frequency.** Hazards that happen often give people less time to recover. Rare events can catch communities unprepared.
- **Duration.** Long events, such as a drought lasting years or a volcanic eruption lasting months, cause cumulative harm.
- **Speed of onset.** Earthquakes strike with no warning. Tropical storms can be tracked for days. Drought builds slowly. The less warning there is, the higher the risk.
- **Predictability.** Hazards that can be forecast allow evacuation. Earthquakes cannot be predicted with useful accuracy.
- **Spatial extent.** A hazard that affects a wide area, such as a drought across a whole region, harms more people than one that affects a single valley.

### 2. Vulnerability: who and what is exposed

- **Population density.** More people in the hazard zone means more potential casualties.
- **Urbanisation.** Dense cities with poor-quality buildings, such as informal settlements on steep slopes, are particularly vulnerable to earthquakes and landslides.
- **Location.** Low-lying coasts are exposed to tropical storm surges. Slopes beneath volcanoes are exposed to lava and ash. River floodplains attract settlement because the land is fertile and flat, yet they flood.
- **Poverty.** Poorer households live in weaker homes, have fewer savings, less insurance and less ability to move away.
- **Age and health.** Very young people, older people and people with disabilities may find it harder to escape.
- **Building quality and land use.** Buildings made of unreinforced masonry collapse easily in earthquakes; those with strong frames and flexible foundations survive.
- **Environmental change.** Deforestation increases flood and landslide risk because there are fewer tree roots to hold soil and fewer leaves to intercept rain.

### 3. Capacity to cope

- **Wealth.** Wealthier countries can pay for monitoring equipment, hazard-resistant buildings, emergency services and insurance.
- **Governance.** Stable governments enforce building regulations, run education campaigns and organise evacuations. Corruption can lead to poor-quality construction going unchecked.
- **Preparedness and education.** Communities that practise earthquake drills or know where cyclone shelters are can act quickly.
- **Warning systems and communication.** A warning is only useful if it reaches people in time and they understand it.
- **Infrastructure.** Good roads, hospitals and communications speed up rescue and recovery.
- **Aid and international links.** Outside help can reduce long-term harm, though it takes time to arrive.

## Worked example: comparing the risk in two places

The figures below are **illustrative**, made up to show the method. They do not describe real countries.

| | Place X | Place Y |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard | Earthquake, magnitude 6.5 | Earthquake, magnitude 6.5 |
| Population density in affected area | 900 people per km² | 150 people per km² |
| Building standard | Few controls, many unreinforced buildings | Strict codes, reinforced frames |
| Emergency services | Limited, slow to arrive | Well equipped, fast response |
| Warning or drills | None | Regular drills in schools |

**Question:** Explain why the risk from the same earthquake is higher in Place X than Place Y. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "The hazard is the same, so the difference lies in vulnerability and capacity to cope. Place X has a higher population density, so more people are exposed to the shaking. Its buildings are weakly constructed, so many are likely to collapse and trap people. Place X also has limited emergency services that arrive slowly, so fewer survivors are rescued and the injured wait longer for treatment. In Place Y, drills and strict building codes reduce casualties, so the same earthquake causes much less harm."

Why this scores well: the first sentence identifies what is constant, the middle sentences link each factor to an outcome, and the answer finishes by returning to the comparison. Every point is two-step: a factor, then its effect.

## Using the factors in longer answers

Extended answers (6 marks and above) often ask you to "assess" or to say "to what extent" something is true. A strong approach is to take three or four factors and order them. For example, for "Assess the importance of wealth in reducing hazard risk" you could argue:

1. Wealth allows investment in monitoring, building standards and emergency response, which directly cut casualties.
2. However, wealth does not protect against the largest events. A very powerful earthquake or storm can overwhelm even a rich country's defences.
3. Governance and education also matter. A moderately wealthy country with good planning can outperform a richer but badly governed one.
4. Conclusion: wealth is a major factor, but it works through planning and preparation, and the size of the hazard sets a limit.

The conclusion needs a judgement, not a summary.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating hazard and disaster as the same word.** Define them separately.
- **Listing factors without effects.** "Poverty" alone earns little; "poverty means weak buildings, so more collapse" earns the mark.
- **Saying that richer countries have fewer hazards.** They face the same hazards but are often less vulnerable. Death tolls may be lower, but economic losses can be higher because more valuable property is damaged.
- **Ignoring physical factors.** Magnitude, frequency and predictability are part of risk, not only human factors.
- **Forgetting secondary hazards.** Tsunamis, landslides and fires after earthquakes often cause more harm than the first shock.
- **Using made-up facts as if real.** If you cite a real event, you must be sure of the details. If unsure, describe the process instead of quoting figures.
