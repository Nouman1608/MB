This guide covers the OCR A Level Business (H431) content headed "Stakeholders", in the area of study "Business objectives and strategy" (specification Version 2.1, January 2026; final assessment summer 2027). The specification asks you to "explain what is meant by the term 'stakeholder'", to "identify the internal and external stakeholders of a business", to "analyse the objectives of the stakeholders", to explain the reasons for conflict between stakeholder groups and why a business must manage it, to "evaluate the impact on a business of different stakeholders having conflicting objectives", to "recommend and justify how a business should deal with" that conflict, to "evaluate the influence different stakeholders have on a business including its aims and objectives, decision making, behaviour and performance", and to "evaluate the impact of business decisions on different stakeholders groups".

The combined guide lists the stakeholder groups and their usual objectives. This page goes further: grey-area stakeholders, the deeper causes of conflict, how to measure power, and how to build evaluation that reaches a justified judgement. Every business named here is invented.

## Stakeholders in the grey areas

A **stakeholder** is any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business. Most groups sort easily into internal (owners, directors, managers, employees) and external (customers, suppliers, lenders, government, the local community, pressure groups, competitors). Examiners reward students who handle the awkward cases:

- **Shareholders of a large public limited company** are owners, so they are usually classed as internal, yet most never enter the business and act more like outside investors. Say which view you are taking.
- **Employees who own shares** through a share scheme have two sets of interests: pay and job security, and dividends and share price. Their reaction to a cost-cutting plan may be divided.
- **Agency staff and contractors** work inside the business but are employed by someone else, so their loyalty and objectives differ from permanent staff.
- **Competitors** are sometimes listed as stakeholders because they are affected by a firm's decisions, such as a price cut, even though the business has no duty to them.

## Analysing stakeholder objectives

"Analyse" needs a chain of reasoning, not a list. Use: **group → objective → why → what it means for this decision**.

*Ridgeway Bus Co. plans to cut its evening services in rural villages.*

> "Rural passengers want a reliable, affordable service **[objective]** because many have no car and depend on buses to reach work and shops **[why]**, so they will oppose the cut strongly and may complain to the local council, which subsidises some routes **[link to decision and consequence]**."

The same chain works for each group, and the "what it means" step is where the marks for analysis are earned.

## Why stakeholder objectives conflict

The combined guide gives the main reasons. At A Level, sort them into five causes, because each points to a different solution:

| Cause | Example | Points towards |
|---|---|---|
| Sharing a limited surplus | Higher wages mean lower dividends | Negotiation, profit-sharing |
| Different time horizons | Shareholders want profit this year; managers want to invest for five years | Explaining long-term benefits; phased plans |
| Different attitudes to risk | Lenders want low risk; owners may accept high risk for high reward | Security, covenants, mixed finance |
| Different values | A pressure group objects on environmental grounds regardless of profit | Changing the decision, or public engagement |
| Different information | Staff fear job losses because no plan has been shared | Better communication, consultation |

Many conflicts mix causes. Identifying the main one makes a recommendation much sharper.

## Why the conflict must be managed, and its impact

Unmanaged conflict can lead to industrial action, lost customers, loans refused or recalled, planning objections, adverse publicity and legal challenges. Even without open conflict, it wastes management time and slows decisions.

When evaluating the **impact** of conflicting objectives, consider:

- **Severity**: a strike stops output entirely; grumbling on social media may not.
- **Duration**: short-term disruption against permanent loss of trust.
- **Which group is unhappy**: conflict with a group that controls a vital resource (key staff, the main lender, the only supplier) matters far more.
- **Possible benefits**: conflict can expose risks managers had missed and lead to a better decision.

## Measuring power: the power and interest grid

Stakeholders differ in **power** (their ability to affect the business) and **interest** (how much they care about a particular decision). A widely used grid, often called Mendelow's matrix, gives four approaches:

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely (key players) |
| **Low power** | Monitor | Keep informed |

Sources of power to look for in a case: control of finance (lenders, shareholders), control of a scarce input (skilled staff, a sole supplier), legal authority (government, regulators, planning committees), numbers and organisation (trade unions, customers acting together), and public attention (media, pressure groups). Power and interest change with the decision: local residents have little interest in a firm's pricing, but high interest in a plan to extend its factory.

## Influence on aims, decision making, behaviour and performance

The specification lists four things stakeholders influence. Have an example for each:

- **Aims and objectives**: shareholders in a plc may press for profit or dividend targets; trustees of a charity set its purpose.
- **Decision making**: a bank's loan conditions may limit further borrowing; a planning authority can block a new site.
- **Behaviour**: customer and pressure-group expectations push firms towards ethical sourcing or lower packaging.
- **Performance**: employee motivation affects productivity and quality; supplier reliability affects delivery.

To **evaluate** influence, ask how much power the group has in this case, whether it is organised, and whether the business depends on it. A single customer has little influence over a supermarket chain, but a supplier's largest customer may effectively set its prices.

## Worked example: impact of a decision on different stakeholders

*Penhallow Quarries Ltd plans to install automated loading equipment costing £420,000. It will replace 14 of its 50 loading jobs, saving £30,000 per job per year in wage costs. Lorry movements will move to 24 hours a day to make full use of the equipment. (All figures are invented.)*

**Calculations**

- Annual saving: 14 × £30,000 = **£420,000**, so the equipment pays for itself in **one year**.
- Jobs lost: 14 ÷ 50 = **28%** of the loading workforce.

**Impact by group**

- **Shareholders**: gain from lower costs and higher profit once the equipment has paid for itself.
- **Employees**: 14 lose their jobs; those remaining may fear further automation, and morale may fall, but some may be retrained to operate the equipment.
- **Local community**: loses local jobs and faces night-time lorry noise, which may prompt complaints to the council.
- **Customers**: may gain from faster loading and possibly lower prices.
- **Local council**: may receive complaints and could restrict operating hours.

**Annotated judgement**

> "The financial case is strong, with payback in one year **[uses the figure]**. However, the impact falls unevenly: shareholders gain while employees and residents bear the costs **[weighs groups]**. The community's power is greater than it looks, because the council could limit night working and remove the benefit of 24-hour operation **[power in this case]**. Penhallow should go ahead, but offer retraining to some of the 14 and limit night-time lorry movements, accepting a smaller saving in return for keeping the council and community on side **[justified recommendation]**."

## Recommending how to deal with conflict

A strong recommendation names **one main approach**, ties it to the cause of the conflict and the power of the groups involved, and accepts a cost:

1. Identify the groups in conflict and the main cause.
2. Judge their power and interest in this case.
3. Choose the approach: prioritise, compromise, consult, compensate, phase the change, or change the decision.
4. Say who still loses and why the business can accept that.

## Common mistakes

- Treating every stakeholder group as equally important.
- Listing groups and objectives without linking them to the decision in the case.
- Describing conflict without its cause, so the recommended solution does not fit.
- Assuming power is fixed rather than depending on the decision.
- Evaluating impact on one group only.
- Recommending "satisfy all stakeholders", which avoids the judgement the question asks for.

## Exam technique

- **Identify**: name the group and say whether it is internal or external, noting grey areas.
- **Analyse**: group → objective → why → effect on this decision.
- **Evaluate**: weigh at least two groups, use their power in the case, and reach a judgement with a condition ("this depends on...").
- **Recommend and justify**: one approach, linked to the cause and the power of the groups, with the cost accepted.
