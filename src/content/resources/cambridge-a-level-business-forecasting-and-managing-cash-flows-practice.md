## Questions

**1.** Define the term "cash flow forecast". **[2]**

**2.** Explain two purposes of a cash flow forecast for a business. **[4]**

**3.** A business has an opening balance of £2,300 in a month. It expects cash inflows of £9,400 and cash outflows of £10,150. Calculate the net cash flow, the closing balance and the opening balance for the next month. **[3]**

**4.** Vexford Catering has prepared part of its cash flow forecast.

| £ | March | April | May |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 14,000 | 16,500 | 12,800 |
| Total outflows | 15,200 | (b) | 17,300 |
| Net cash flow | −1,200 | 900 | −4,500 |
| Opening balance | 3,000 | (a) | 2,700 |
| Closing balance | 1,800 | 2,700 | (c) |

**(a)** Calculate the opening balance for April. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total outflows for April. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the closing balance for May. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain what the May figure means and suggest one action. **[3]**

**5.** A wholesaler sells goods for £30,000 in March on 60 days' credit. In March it pays £22,000 for the materials and wages used to make them.

**(a)** Calculate the profit on this sale and the cash flow from this sale in March. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a profitable business may still have cash flow problems. **[3]**

**6.** Strelley Cycles hires out bicycles. Its original forecast is below.

| £ | June | July | August |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 10,000 | 12,000 | 9,000 |
| Total outflows | 9,000 | 11,500 | 10,500 |

The opening balance in June is £5,000. In July a hotel group delays a £4,000 payment until August. In August the business also has an unexpected repair bill of £2,000.

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for each month in the original forecast. **[3]**
**(b)** Amend the forecast for the changes and calculate the new closing balance for each month. **[3]**
**(c)** Analyse the effect of the changes on Strelley Cycles. **[2]**

**7.** A business sells £40,000 of goods on credit each month, and customers usually pay after 60 days. It offers a 2% discount to customers who pay within 10 days. Customers owing 25% of the value of its credit sales take up the offer.

**(a)** Calculate the cash the business receives early each month. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly cost of the discount. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse one limitation of using an early-payment discount to improve cash flow. **[3]**

**8.** Skelmanthorpe Tiles has unpaid invoices of £24,000. A debt factor offers to advance 85% of the invoice value now and to charge a fee of 2.5% of the invoice value. The factor passes on the rest when customers pay. Calculate the cash advanced, the fee, the balance paid later and the total cash the business receives. **[4]**

**9.** Mereworth Garden Centre expects to open January with £8,000. Its forecast net cash flows are shown below.

| £ | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash flow | −6,000 | −7,500 | −3,500 | 13,000 | 11,000 |

The owner is considering three options: a bank overdraft of up to £10,000 at 14% a year; a sale and leaseback of its delivery van, raising £12,000 with a lease cost of £4,000 a year; or asking suppliers for 30 more days of credit on the spring stock.

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for each month. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate the three options for Mereworth. **[7]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why the cash flow forecast of a new business may be unreliable. **[4]**

**11.** Hapsford Kitchen is a restaurant. Its forecast shows a closing balance of −£4,500 in two months' time. It buys £6,000 of ingredients a month from suppliers on 30 days' credit and holds £9,000 of ingredients in stock. Option A is to ask suppliers for 45 days' credit. Option B is to cut stock to £6,000 by ordering more often from the same suppliers. Recommend, with justification, how Hapsford Kitchen should deal with the forecast deficit. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** An estimate of the cash a business expects to receive and pay out over future periods [1], showing the expected cash balance at the end of each period [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say what is forecast (cash in and out) and what it shows (balances). "A plan of money" is too vague.

**2.** Purpose 1: it shows when cash shortfalls are likely [1], so the business can arrange an overdraft or loan before it runs out of cash [1]. Purpose 2: it supports a request for finance [1], because a bank or investor can see that the business expects to meet repayments [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each purpose needs a development as well as a name.

**3.** Net cash flow = 9,400 − 10,150 = **−£750** [1]. Closing balance = 2,300 + (−750) = **£1,550** [1]. The opening balance for the next month is the same figure, **£1,550** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the net cash flow first, with its sign.

**4. (a)** The April opening balance is the March closing balance: **£1,800** [1].
**(b)** Closing 2,700 = opening 1,800 + inflows 16,500 − outflows, so outflows = 1,800 + 16,500 − 2,700 = **£15,600** [1].
**(c)** 2,700 + (−4,500) = **−£1,800** [1].
**(d)** It expects to be £1,800 overdrawn at the end of May [1], so it cannot pay all bills from its own cash [1]. One action: arrange a bank overdraft, or ask suppliers for longer credit, before May [1].
*Examiner insight:* For missing figures, use the link between opening balance, net cash flow and closing balance. In (d), say what the number means for the business.

**5. (a)** Profit on the sale = 30,000 − 22,000 = **£8,000** [1]. Cash flow in March = **−£22,000**, since no customer has paid [1].
**(b)** Profit counts a sale when it is made, but cash arrives only when the customer pays [1]. Costs such as wages and materials are paid before customers pay [1]. If the business cannot pay suppliers or wages when they fall due, it can fail even though it is profitable [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the stem to illustrate the point.

**6. (a)** June: 5,000 + 10,000 − 9,000 = **£6,000** [1]. July: 6,000 + 12,000 − 11,500 = **£6,500** [1]. August: 6,500 + 9,000 − 10,500 = **£5,000** [1].
**(b)** July inflows become 12,000 − 4,000 = 8,000, so net cash flow is −3,500 and the closing balance is **£2,500** [1]. August inflows become 9,000 + 4,000 = 13,000 and outflows 10,500 + 2,000 = 12,500 [1]; net cash flow is +500, so the closing balance is **£3,000** [1]. June stays at £6,000.
**(c)** Late payment cuts the July balance from £6,500 to £2,500 [1]. By August the repair bill leaves it £2,000 lower than forecast, so it may need short-term finance [1].
*Examiner insight:* Change only the lines affected and carry the new balance forward. Note that late payment moves cash between months, while the repair really reduces it.

**7. (a)** Customers taking the offer owe 25% of £40,000 = £10,000 [1]. The cash received is 10,000 × 0.98 = **£9,800** early each month [1].
**(b)** The discount costs 10,000 × 0.02 = **£200** a month [1].
**(c)** Customers may not take the discount, so the cash gain is smaller than planned [1]. Those who do pay less, so revenue falls by £200 a month [1]. A cash-short business with small margins may struggle to afford this [1].
*Examiner insight:* A limitation needs applying. "It costs money" alone is thin.

**8.** Advance = 0.85 × 24,000 = **£20,400** [1]. Fee = 0.025 × 24,000 = **£600** [1]. Balance paid later = 24,000 − 20,400 − 600 = **£3,000** [1]. Total cash received = 20,400 + 3,000 = **£23,400** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check that the three amounts add to the invoice value (20,400 + 600 + 3,000 = 24,000).

**9. (a)** January: 8,000 − 6,000 = **£2,000** [1]. February: 2,000 − 7,500 = **−£5,500**; March: −5,500 − 3,500 = **−£9,000** [1]. April: −9,000 + 13,000 = **£4,000**; May: 4,000 + 11,000 = **£15,000** [1].
**(b)** The deficit peaks at £9,000 in March and clears by April, so the gap is temporary and seasonal [1]. Overdraft: flexible and cheap; £9,000 for two months costs about 9,000 × 0.14 × 2/12 = £210 [1], though the bank can ask for repayment [1]. Sale and leaseback: £12,000 covers the gap, but the lease costs £4,000 a year for ever and the van is no longer owned [1], which is poor value for a gap that lasts a few months [1]. Supplier credit: costs nothing in interest and matches the stock bought in spring [1], but suppliers may refuse, and it may not close the whole £9,000 gap. Judgement: use the overdraft, helped by supplier credit if agreed, because the gap is temporary and leaseback costs are permanent [1].
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs a reasoned choice. Spotting that the deficit is seasonal, then judging each option against that, beats describing each in turn.

**10.** Reason 1: a new business has no trading history, so its sales estimates are guesses [1]; if sales are lower than forecast, inflows will fall short and cash will run out sooner [1]. Reason 2: owners can be over-optimistic or leave out costs they have not yet met [1], so outflows may be higher than forecast and the closing balances overstated [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each reason to what goes wrong in the forecast. "It is only an estimate" fits every forecast.

**11.** Option A: 15 extra days on £6,000 a month defers about 6,000 × 15/30 = £3,000 of payments [1]. Option B: cutting stock from £9,000 to £6,000 releases £3,000 [1]. Each is a one-off effect, and neither alone covers a £4,500 deficit [1]. Risks of A: suppliers may refuse or charge more, harming the relationship [1]. Risks of B: running out of ingredients, so dishes cannot be served and sales are lost [1]. Together they release about £6,000, enough for the £4,500 gap [1]. Recommendation: do both if suppliers agree, with no borrowing needed [1]; start with B, which the restaurant controls, and use A for the remainder [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the numbers to show whether one option solves the problem, and say which risk matters more in a restaurant.


## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the month of sale, not the month of payment, for credit sales.
- Forgetting that this month's closing balance is next month's opening balance.
- Losing a minus sign on a negative balance or net cash flow.
- Writing "improves cash flow" without saying how the money moves.
- Not applying answers to the business in the stem.
- Recommending a long-term source for a short gap, or the reverse.
- Evaluating each option in turn without a final judgement.
