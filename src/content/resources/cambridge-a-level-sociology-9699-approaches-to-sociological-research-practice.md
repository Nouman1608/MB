## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *longitudinal study*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** aims of positivist research. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between *triangulation* and *methodological pluralism*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what Weber meant by *verstehen*. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a case study may be useful to sociologists. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what Karl Popper meant by *falsification*. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why interpretivists prefer qualitative methods. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** problems sociologists face when carrying out longitudinal studies. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of ethnography to study life in a religious community. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the use of social surveys to study attitudes to marriage. **[8]**

**11.** *"Sociological research can and should be value-free."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "Sociology should use the methods and procedures of the natural sciences." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Longitudinal study [2]

Research that studies the same group of people [1] repeatedly over a long period, often years, to track change, for example the UK Millennium Cohort Study [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Positivist aims [2]

Any two, one mark each: objectivity, reliability, value-freedom, generalisability, discovering causal relationships or social laws, testing hypotheses [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Triangulation and methodological pluralism [4]

- Triangulation uses two or more methods, sources or researchers [1] to cross-check findings and so test their validity, for example comparing interview answers with observed behaviour [1].
- Methodological pluralism uses several methods [1] to build a fuller picture, each method adding a different kind of insight, for example a survey for patterns and interviews for meanings [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Verstehen [3]

Weber's term for empathetic understanding [1] of social action from the point of view of the person acting [1]; sociologists must grasp the meanings and motives behind behaviour, not only observe it, which supports interpretivist methods such as participant observation [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Usefulness of case studies [3]

Reason identified [1], explained [1] and illustrated [1]. For example: a case study allows in-depth study of one example [1], revealing processes and meanings that a broad survey would miss [1]; Goffman's *Asylums* (1961), based on fieldwork in one psychiatric hospital, developed the concept of the total institution [1]. Also credit testing whether a general theory holds in a particular case, or studying rare cases. **[3]**

### 6. Falsification [3]

Popper argued that a theory is scientific only if it can be tested and could, in principle, be shown to be false [1]. Scientists should try to disprove theories rather than seek confirming evidence [1]. Theories that explain away all contrary evidence, which Popper said of Marxism, are not scientific [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Why interpretivists prefer qualitative methods [6]

Up to 3 marks per reason.

- **Meaning:** human action is based on meanings, so methods must uncover them; unstructured interviews let people explain in their own words.
- **Validity:** close contact, rapport and natural settings produce a truer picture of experience than imposed categories.
- **Verstehen:** participant observation lets the researcher share the experience of those studied.
- **Rejection of social facts:** interpretivists see statistics as social constructions (for example Douglas, 1967, on suicide), so they prefer to study how meanings are made. **[6]**

### 8. Problems with longitudinal studies [6]

Up to 3 marks per problem.

- **Attrition:** participants drop out through moving, death or loss of interest; if those who leave differ from those who stay, representativeness falls.
- **Cost and time:** funding must be secured for many years, and results take a long time.
- **Hawthorne effect:** being studied repeatedly may change participants' behaviour.
- **Changing context:** questions and concepts may become outdated, but changing them weakens comparison over time. **[6]**

### 9. Ethnography of a religious community [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations, applied.

**Strengths:**
- Long-term immersion reveals the meanings members attach to worship, ritual and belief [1].
- Members are observed in their natural setting, so behaviour is more natural over time, raising validity [1].
- Trust built over time may give access to beliefs and practices that outsiders rarely see, as in Barker's (1984) study of a new religious movement [1].
- The researcher can follow up observations with informal questions [1].

**Limitations:**
- Access may be refused, especially by closed or suspicious groups [1].
- The researcher may go native, losing objectivity, or be drawn into the group [1].
- Findings from one community cannot be generalised to others [1].
- Ethical issues if participation is covert, and practical demands of time and cost [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Social surveys on attitudes to marriage [8]

**Strengths:**
- Large, representative samples allow generalisation about a whole population [1].
- Standardised questions produce reliable, comparable data [1].
- Repeated surveys can show trends over time, such as changing approval of cohabitation [1].
- Data can be broken down by age, religion or class to show patterns [1].

**Limitations:**
- Closed questions impose the researcher's definitions of "marriage" and "commitment" [1].
- Social desirability may lead respondents to give acceptable rather than honest answers [1].
- Surveys show what people say they think, not how they behave in relationships [1].
- Low response rates or online-only surveys can make samples unrepresentative [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- Positivists argue that sociology can be value-free if it uses standardised, scientific methods [1].
- Researchers should keep personal, political or religious views out of how they collect and interpret data [1].
- Questionnaires and structured interviews reduce the influence of the researcher [1].
- Value-freedom gives findings credibility with policymakers and the public [1].
- Weber argued that sociologists' values may guide the choice of topic but should not enter the conduct of research [1].
- Without value-freedom, sociology risks becoming opinion or propaganda [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Gouldner:** claims of value-freedom are themselves a value position and can be used to avoid responsibility for how research is used.
- **Becker (1967):** research inevitably takes sides, often of the underdog or of authority, so sociologists should declare whose side they are on.
- **Feminist research:** Oakley (1981) argued for involved, non-hierarchical interviewing; committed research can be more valid, not less.
- **Funding and topic choice:** funders' interests shape what is researched and published, so values enter before any data is collected.
- **Interpretation:** even quantitative results must be interpreted, and categories reflect the researcher's assumptions.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: sociology and the natural sciences [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Comte and positivism: society can be studied scientifically.
- Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) found patterns in social facts.
- Benefits of objectivity, reliability, hypothesis testing and generalisation.
- Value of large-scale data for social policy.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Interpretivism: humans act on meanings; verstehen requires qualitative methods.
- Official statistics as social constructions (Douglas, 1967; Atkinson, 1978).
- Ethical and practical limits on experiments with people.
- Popper: much sociology is not falsifiable.
- Kuhn (1962): natural science is less objective than positivists assume.
- Realists such as Sayer: sociology can be scientific in its own way, studying underlying structures.

**Evaluation:** separate "can" from "should"; methodological pluralism and triangulation as a middle way; the answer depends on the research question.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers contrast positivism and interpretivism and use views of science (Popper, Kuhn, realism) to reach a supported judgement; middle-level answers give both sides with limited evaluation; low-level answers describe one approach.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing approaches (survey, ethnography) with methods (questionnaire, observation).
- Defining triangulation as simply "using several methods".
- Method evaluations not applied to the topic in the question.
- Treating Weber as rejecting value-freedom altogether.
- Essays that never address whether sociology *should* follow the natural sciences.
