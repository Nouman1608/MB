| Point | One-line summary |
|---|---|
| What managers do | Setting objectives, analysing, leading, making decisions, reviewing |
| Types of styles and influences | Autocratic to laissez-faire; shaped by task, people, culture, time, risk |
| Named theories | Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum; Blake Mouton grid |
| Effectiveness | Judge each style against the context and against evidence |

## What managers do

The role of managers includes five activities. Learn them as a cycle.

1. **Setting objectives**: decide what is to be achieved, with measurable targets.
2. **Analysing**: examine information about the market, the team and past results.
3. **Leading**: guide, motivate and direct people towards the objectives.
4. **Making decisions**: choose between options.
5. **Reviewing**: compare results with objectives, then feed the lessons into the next round of objectives.

Reminder for answers: link each activity to the case. "The manager reviews sales" scores less than "reviewing the second-quarter sales against the target showed the new menu was not working".

## Styles: definitions to recall

| Style | Who decides | Communication | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Manager alone | One way, downward | Staff disengage and leave |
| Paternalistic | Manager, in staff's perceived interest | Mostly downward, with explanation | Staff feel treated like children |
| Democratic | Manager with staff input, or the group | Two way | Slow, compromise |
| Laissez-faire | Staff, within limits | Little from the manager | Drift, lack of direction |

These four labels are standard terms. The specification itself asks for "types of management and leadership styles", and the theories below are what it names.

## Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum

- A **line**, not boxes. At one end the manager uses authority and decides alone. At the other the team has freedom to decide within limits.
- As freedom for the team rises, the manager's use of authority falls.
- The theory says the right position depends on the manager, the team and the situation.

Standard seven-position summary (not a quotation):

| Position | Manager behaviour |
|---|---|
| 1 | Decides and announces |
| 2 | Decides and persuades ("sells") |
| 3 | Presents ideas, invites questions |
| 4 | Presents a tentative decision open to change |
| 5 | Presents the problem, takes suggestions, then decides |
| 6 | Sets the limits, the group decides |
| 7 | Team acts freely within defined limits |

**Quick method for placing a manager**

1. Ask: who makes the final decision?
2. Ask: was the staff view sought before or after the decision?
3. Ask: how much freedom is left to the team?
4. Name the position (or range) and quote the evidence.

## Blake Mouton grid

- Two axes, each scored 1 to 9: **concern for production** and **concern for people**.
- Write the score as (production, people). The order matters: (9,1) is not (1,9).

| Position | Name (standard form) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| (1,1) | Impoverished | Minimal effort on both |
| (9,1) | Produce or perish | Task first, people as a means |
| (1,9) | Country club | People first, results weak |
| (5,5) | Middle of the road | Compromise, neither fully met |
| (9,9) | Team management | High on both |

**Quick method for scoring a manager**

1. Collect evidence on the task: targets, monitoring, deadlines.
2. Collect evidence on people: training, listening, support.
3. Give each a score from 1 to 9 and write them in the right order.
4. Check the evidence is enough. A single email is thin evidence.

## Continuum against grid

| | Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum | Blake Mouton grid |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Who decides, and how much freedom the team has | How much the manager cares about the task and the people |
| Shape | One line | Two scales |
| Question answered | How should decisions be made here? | What is the manager's orientation? |
| Link | A (9,9) manager can sit anywhere along the continuum, depending on the decision | |

A good answer can use both. For example: a manager at position 2 on the continuum who spends time on people's welfare may fit (7,6), not (9,1).

## Influences on style

Group them to remember them.

- **The task**: urgency, risk, complexity, safety.
- **The people**: skills, experience, motivation, size of the team.
- **The manager**: personality, beliefs about people, own skills.
- **The organisation**: culture, size, structure, history.
- **The situation**: crisis, competition, labour market, time available.

## Effectiveness: a ready-made argument frame

1. **Define the measure**: output, quality, turnover, speed of decision, staff motivation.
2. **Give the case for the style**, using case evidence.
3. **Give the case against**, using case evidence.
4. **Add the context**: skills of staff, time available, risk, culture.
5. **Judge**: which style, for which period, because of which factor.

Small reminders, with checked calculations in the standard forms:

- Labour turnover = leavers ÷ average staff × 100. Example: 6 leavers from an average of 30 staff = 20%.
- Output per employee = output ÷ average staff. Example: 5,400 units from 30 staff = 180 units each.

## Worked reminder: one case, three tools

Bexley Reach Print has a new production manager. In her first month she sets weekly output targets, checks them daily, holds a ten-minute briefing each morning, and asks the press operators which jobs jam the machines before she rewrites the schedule. She keeps the final say on the schedule.

1. **Manager's role.** Setting objectives (weekly targets), analysing (asking about jams), leading (the briefings), making decisions (the new schedule), reviewing (the daily check).
2. **Continuum.** She asks for suggestions and then decides, so position 5.
3. **Grid.** High concern for production (targets, daily checks), fairly high concern for people (briefings, listening), so roughly (8,6) on the evidence given. Say "roughly", because one month is little evidence.
4. **Effectiveness.** Likely strengths: the operators' knowledge shapes the schedule, and the targets stay clear. A risk is that the daily checks feel like pressure. A fair judgement is that the style suits a skilled press crew, and a month is too soon to call it effective until turnover and output have been tracked over time.

## Must-know distinctions

| Idea A | Idea B | The difference |
|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Paternalistic | Both decide alone, but the paternalistic leader explains and acts for staff welfare |
| Democratic | Laissez-faire | The democratic leader joins in the decision. The laissez-faire leader steps back |
| Concern for production | Concern for people | Task results against staff needs |
| Continuum | Grid | Decision sharing against manager orientation |
| Management | Leadership | The specification lists leading as one of the manager's activities |

## Quick self-test

1. List the five activities that make up the role of managers.
2. At which end of the Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum does the manager use most authority?
3. What do the two axes of the Blake Mouton grid measure?
4. Which grid position is "country club"?
5. A manager announces a new shift pattern and then explains the reasons to staff. Which continuum position is closest?
6. Give two influences on the style a manager uses.
7. A team has an average of 40 staff and 6 leavers in the year. Calculate labour turnover.
8. A depot sorts 31,200 parcels with an average of 26 staff. Calculate output per employee.
9. Give one reason an autocratic style might be effective.
10. Why is "democratic is best" a weak evaluative statement?

### Answers

1. Setting objectives, analysing, leading, making decisions, reviewing.
2. The end where the manager decides alone and announces (positions 1 and 2 in the standard seven-position form).
3. Concern for production (the task) and concern for people.
4. (1,9).
5. Position 2, where the manager decides and then persuades or explains.
6. Any two from: urgency of the task, skills of the workforce, the manager's personality, organisational culture, size of the business, time available, level of risk.
7. 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
8. 31,200 ÷ 26 = 1,200 parcels per employee.
9. It is quick and clear, which helps in a crisis or with new staff who need instruction.
10. It ignores the context. Effectiveness depends on staff skills, time, risk and what the business is trying to achieve.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing the five activities of a manager with no link to the business in the question.
- Describing a style but never judging whether it suits this case.
- Writing a grid score in the wrong order, or giving only one number.
- Treating the continuum as four styles instead of a line.
- Claiming (9,9) is always best, without considering time, skills and cost.
- Using the words "motivated" or "demotivated" with no evidence from the case, such as turnover or output.
- Mixing up the authority held by the manager with the freedom held by the team, and so reading the continuum backwards.
- Drawing a conclusion from one measure only, such as output per employee, and ignoring quality and turnover.
- Writing a conclusion that repeats the points made and does not reach a judgement.
