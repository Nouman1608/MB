This guide covers subtopic **11.1 Atoms, nuclei and radiation** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (AS Level). The broader [Particle physics study guide](/resources/as-physics-particle-physics/) summarises the nuclear atom, the three radiations and a decay equation; this page goes deeper: exactly which α-scattering observation supports which conclusion, how to read and balance every type of decay equation, how to count the decays in a decay chain, why α and β energy spectra differ, and how to work with the unified atomic mass unit. For quick recall use the [Atoms, nuclei and radiation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-atoms-nuclei-and-radiation-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Atoms, nuclei and radiation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-atoms-nuclei-and-radiation-practice/).

## What 11.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists twelve outcomes. In summary, candidates should be able to:

- infer from the results of the **α-particle scattering experiment** the existence and small size of the nucleus
- describe a simple model for the nuclear atom (protons, neutrons and orbital electrons)
- distinguish between **nucleon number** and **proton number**, and understand **isotopes**
- use the standard notation for nuclides: the symbol X with nucleon number A written as a superscript and proton number Z as a subscript before it
- understand that **nucleon number and charge are conserved** in nuclear processes
- describe the composition, mass and charge of **α-, β- and γ-radiations** (both β⁻ and β⁺)
- understand **antiparticles**, with the positron as the antiparticle of the electron
- state that (electron) **antineutrinos** are produced in β⁻ decay and (electron) **neutrinos** in β⁺ decay
- understand why α-particles have **discrete** energies but β-particles a **continuous range**
- represent α- and β-decay by a **radioactive decay equation**
- use the **unified atomic mass unit (u)** as a unit of mass

## The α-particle scattering experiment

In the experiment carried out by Geiger and Marsden and interpreted by Rutherford, a narrow beam of α-particles was directed at a very thin gold foil in a vacuum, and the numbers scattered through different angles were counted. Learn the observations and the inference from each as matched pairs:

| Observation | Inference |
|---|---|
| Almost all α-particles passed straight through or were deflected by very small angles | Most of the atom is empty space; the α-particles rarely come close to anything that can deflect them strongly |
| A very small fraction were deflected through large angles, some more than 90° (back towards the source) | There is a very small region of concentrated **positive charge** that repels the positive α-particles strongly when they approach it closely |
| Those large deflections show the deflecting region is not pushed aside | The small region contains **most of the mass** of the atom, much more than an α-particle |
| Large deflections are extremely rare | The nucleus is **tiny** compared with the atom, so very few α-particles pass close to one |

The experiment is done in a **vacuum** so that α-particles are not absorbed or deflected by air, and the foil is **very thin** so that each α-particle is deflected by at most one nucleus. A nucleus is of the order of 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across, while an atom is of the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m, so the nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom.

## The nuclear atom and nuclide notation

The simple nuclear model: a tiny nucleus containing **protons** (charge +e) and **neutrons** (no charge), surrounded by **orbital electrons** (charge −e). In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

```
nuclide written with A at top left and Z at bottom left of the symbol X
A = nucleon number (protons + neutrons)
Z = proton number
number of neutrons N = A − Z
```

For ⁵⁶₂₆Fe: 26 protons, 56 − 26 = 30 neutrons, and 26 electrons in the neutral atom. The charge of the nucleus is Ze = 26 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.16 × 10⁻¹⁸ C.

**Isotopes** are forms of the same element with the same proton number but different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different nucleon numbers. Carbon-12 (¹²₆C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴₆C) are isotopes: both have 6 protons, but they have 6 and 8 neutrons respectively. Because chemistry depends on the electrons, and the number of electrons equals Z, isotopes behave the same way chemically.

## The radiations

| Radiation | What it is | Mass | Charge | Symbol in equations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| α | Helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | about 4 u | +2e | ⁴₂α |
| β⁻ | Electron, emitted from the nucleus | about 1/1840 u (5.5 × 10⁻⁴ u) | −e | ⁰₋₁β |
| β⁺ | Positron, emitted from the nucleus | same as electron | +e | ⁰₊₁β |
| γ | Electromagnetic radiation (a photon) | 0 | 0 | γ |

An **antiparticle** has the **same mass but opposite charge** to its corresponding particle. The **positron** is the antiparticle of the electron.

Each β decay also produces a neutral, almost massless particle:

- **β⁻ decay** produces an (electron) **antineutrino**, ν̄.
- **β⁺ decay** produces an (electron) **neutrino**, ν.

## Writing and balancing decay equations

In every nuclear process, **nucleon number** and **charge** are conserved. Check the top numbers and the bottom numbers separately.

**α-decay** (A falls by 4, Z falls by 2):

```
²¹⁰₈₄Po → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁴₂α
top: 210 = 206 + 4      bottom: 84 = 82 + 2
```

**β⁻ decay** (A unchanged, Z rises by 1; a neutron has become a proton):

```
¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄
top: 14 = 14 + 0        bottom: 6 = 7 + (−1)
```

**β⁺ decay** (A unchanged, Z falls by 1; a proton has become a neutron):

```
²²₁₁Na → ²²₁₀Ne + ⁰₊₁β + ν
top: 22 = 22 + 0        bottom: 11 = 10 + 1
```

**γ emission** changes neither A nor Z. It usually follows α or β decay when the new nucleus is left with excess energy, which it emits as a γ-ray photon.

The new element is identified from its proton number, so in an exam you will be told, or can read from a given list, which element has the new value of Z.

## Worked example 1: counting decays in a chain

Uranium-238 (²³⁸₉₂U) decays through a series of α and β⁻ decays to the stable lead isotope ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb. Determine the number of each type of decay.

```
change in nucleon number = 238 − 206 = 32
only α-decay changes A, by 4 each time:  32 / 4 = 8 α-decays
8 α-decays reduce Z by 8 × 2 = 16:  92 − 16 = 76
the final Z is 82, so Z must rise by 82 − 76 = 6
each β⁻ decay raises Z by 1:  6 β⁻ decays
```

*Annotation.* Always use the nucleon number first, because β decay does not change it. Then use the proton number to find the β decays. Check: 92 − 16 + 6 = 82.

## Why α energies are discrete and β energies continuous

In α-decay, the energy released is shared between just **two** bodies, the α-particle and the recoiling daughter nucleus. Conservation of energy and momentum then fix how the energy is shared, so every α-particle from a given decay has the **same** kinetic energy: the energy spectrum is a sharp line (or a few lines).

In β-decay, the energy released is shared between **three** bodies: the β-particle, the (anti)neutrino and the daughter nucleus. The share taken by the (anti)neutrino can vary from decay to decay, so β-particles emerge with a **continuous range** of kinetic energies, from almost zero up to a maximum (when the (anti)neutrino takes almost nothing). The continuous β spectrum was the historical evidence that another, undetected particle must be emitted.

## The unified atomic mass unit

```
1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
```

The unified atomic mass unit is close to the mass of one proton or one neutron, so the mass of a nucleus is roughly A u. The mass of an electron is far smaller: 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg ÷ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 5.5 × 10⁻⁴ u.

## Worked example 2: mass and charge of a nucleus

Estimate the mass in kilograms and the charge of a ²³⁸₉₂U nucleus.

```
mass ≈ 238 u = 238 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.95 × 10⁻²⁵ kg
charge = 92e = 92 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.47 × 10⁻¹⁷ C
```

*Annotation.* "Estimate" signals that using A u is acceptable even though the true nuclear mass differs slightly. Quote the answer to three significant figures at most.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up observations and inferences** in α-scattering answers, or giving only one of them.
- **Saying most α-particles "bounced back".** Only a very small fraction were deflected through large angles.
- **Confusing nucleon number with the number of neutrons.**
- **Writing the β-particle as ¹₋₁β** or the α-particle with charge +4. Check both rows of numbers.
- **Swapping the neutrino and antineutrino**: antineutrino with β⁻, neutrino with β⁺.
- **Saying the β-particle is an orbital electron.** It is created and emitted by the nucleus.
- **Calling a γ-ray a particle with mass.** It is electromagnetic radiation with no mass and no charge.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** (α-scattering results): give the observations in terms of the proportion of α-particles and their angles of deflection.
- **Explain** or **state what can be inferred**: pair each observation with a conclusion about size, charge or mass of the nucleus.
- **Distinguish** (nucleon number and proton number): one sentence each, starting with what is counted.
- **Write** or **complete** a decay equation: include every product, including the (anti)neutrino for β decay, and check both conservation rows.
- **Explain** (continuous β spectrum): "shared between three bodies" and "the antineutrino or neutrino takes a variable share".
- **Calculate**: multiply by 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg to convert u to kg.
