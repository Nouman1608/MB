These questions cover section 3.13.2, Wind energy, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [wind energy study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wind-energy-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics/) and the [wind energy revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wind-energy-oxfordaqa-alevel-physics-revision-notes/). Take the density of air as 1.2 kg m⁻³ unless a question gives another value, and R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A wind of speed v passes through the circle swept by blades of length r. Show that the mass of air passing through each second is ρπr²v. **[2]**

**2.** The blades of a turbine are made twice as long and the wind speed halves. Calculate the factor by which the maximum available power changes. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the maximum power available from a turbine with 30 m blades in a wind of 9.0 m s⁻¹. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a wind turbine cannot extract all the kinetic energy of the wind passing through it. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why turbines in a wind farm are spaced well apart. **[2]**

**6.** State two environmental disadvantages of wind turbines. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A turbine has blades of length 45 m.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum power available in a wind of 12 m s⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** At this speed the turbine delivers 2.5 MW of electrical power. Calculate its efficiency. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why turbines are stopped in very strong winds. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the wind speed at which the maximum available power falls to 2.5 MW. **[2]**

**8.** Over one week (168 hours) a turbine with 35 m blades experiences these wind speeds.

| Wind speed / m s⁻¹ | 3.0 | 8.0 | 14 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours | 50 | 80 | 38 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean wind speed over the week. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum energy available over the week. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the energy that would be predicted using the mean wind speed for all 168 hours, and explain why it differs from (b). **[2]**

**9.** In a wind farm, a turbine directly downwind of another receives air at 70% of the speed reaching the first turbine.

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the first turbine's maximum available power that is available to the second. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the air behind a turbine is slower and more turbulent. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest how the turbines should be arranged to reduce this effect. **[1]**

**10.** The density of air is given by ρ = pM/RT, where M = 0.029 kg mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the density of air at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 300 K. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the density of air at 0.95 × 10⁵ Pa and 270 K. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in maximum available power from the conditions in (a) to those in (b), at the same wind speed. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest one reason, other than density, why turbines in many places produce more energy in winter. **[1]**

**11.** An engineer designs a turbine to deliver 3.0 MW at a wind speed of 10 m s⁻¹. The turbine converts 35% of the maximum available power into electrical power.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum available power required. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the blade length needed. **[2]**

**(c)** On average, each turbine delivers 1.0 MW. Calculate the number of turbines needed to deliver an average of 600 MW. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why such a wind farm could not, on its own, replace a 600 MW power station that runs continuously. **[1]**

**12.** A coastal town plans to build either an onshore wind farm on nearby hills or an offshore wind farm several kilometres out to sea. Discuss the factors that should influence this choice and reach a conclusion. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** In one second the air moving at v travels a distance v, so the air passing through forms a cylinder of length v and cross-section πr² [1]; volume per second = πr²v, so mass per second = ρπr²v [1] **[2]**

**2.** P ∝ r²v³, so the factor = 2² × (½)³ [1] = 4/8 = 0.5 [1] **[2]**

**3.** P = ½ × π × 30² × 1.2 × 9.0³ [1] = 1.2 × 10⁶ W [1] **[2]**

**4.** If all the kinetic energy were removed the air would stop at the rotor [1], blocking the flow of further air, so the air must leave with some kinetic energy [1] **[2]**

**5.** Air leaving a turbine is slower and turbulent (a wind shadow) [1]; since power depends on v³, a turbine in the shadow would produce much less, so spacing lets each receive faster wind [1] **[2]**

**6.** Any two: visual impact on the landscape; noise; harm to birds or bats; large area of land or sea needed; intermittent output requiring back-up [1] [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** P = ½ × π × 45² × 1.2 × 12³ [1] = 6.6 × 10⁶ W [1]

**(b)** 2.5/6.6 = 0.38, or 38% [1]

**(c)** Very high forces on the blades and tower could damage them [1]

**(d)** v³ = 2P/(πr²ρ) = 2 × 2.5 × 10⁶/(π × 2025 × 1.2) = 655 [1]; v = 8.7 m s⁻¹ [1] **[6]**

**8. (a)** (50 × 3.0 + 80 × 8.0 + 38 × 14)/168 = 7.9 m s⁻¹ [1]

**(b)** ½πr²ρ = ½ × π × 35² × 1.2 = 2309 W per (m s⁻¹)³ [1]; energy = 2309 × (50 × 27 + 80 × 512 + 38 × 2744) × 3600 [1] = 1.2 × 10¹² J [1]

**(c)** 2309 × 7.87³ × 168 × 3600 = 6.8 × 10¹¹ J [1]; power depends on v³, so the high-speed hours contribute far more than the low-speed hours take away, and the mean speed underestimates the energy [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Averaging speeds before cubing is a common shortcut that loses nearly half of the energy here.

**9. (a)** 0.70³ = 0.34 [1]

**(b)** The first turbine has taken kinetic energy from the air, so it moves more slowly [1]; the rotating blades disturb the flow, leaving it turbulent [1]

**(c)** Space the turbines several rotor diameters apart, with the greatest gaps along the prevailing wind direction, and stagger the rows [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** ρ = (1.01 × 10⁵ × 0.029)/(8.31 × 300) = 1.17 kg m⁻³ [1]

**(b)** ρ = (0.95 × 10⁵ × 0.029)/(8.31 × 270) = 1.23 kg m⁻³ [1]

**(c)** P ∝ ρ, so the change is 1.228/1.175 = 1.045: an increase of about 4.5% [1]

**(d)** Winds are often stronger in winter, and power depends on v³ [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** 3.0/0.35 = 8.6 × 10⁶ W [1]

**(b)** r = √(2P/(πρv³)) [1] = √(2 × 8.57 × 10⁶/(π × 1.2 × 1000)) = 67 m [1]

**(c)** 600/1.0 = 600 turbines [1]

**(d)** The wind varies, so output is sometimes far below 600 MW; back-up or storage is needed when the wind is light [1] **[5]**

**12.** Marking points, one mark each, up to 6 **[6]**:

- Offshore winds are usually stronger and steadier, and power depends on v³, so each offshore turbine gives more energy [1]
- Onshore hills are also windy and often have fast winds, but the air may be more turbulent over uneven land [1]
- Offshore sites cost more to build, connect by cable and maintain [1]
- Onshore turbines near the town cause more visual impact and noise for residents; offshore turbines are far from homes [1]
- Both must consider wildlife (birds on hills, seabirds and marine life offshore) and the large area needed for spacing [1]
- A justified conclusion, for example: offshore is preferred because the stronger, steadier wind and lower impact on residents outweigh the higher cost (another reasoned conclusion is accepted) [1]

*Examiner insight:* The last mark needs a conclusion that follows from the points made.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the diameter for r.
- Cubing the mean speed instead of averaging the cubes.
- Treating maximum available power as electrical output.
- Explaining spacing without linking slower wind to v³.
- Ending an evaluation without a conclusion.
