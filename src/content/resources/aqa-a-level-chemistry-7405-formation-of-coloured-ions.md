## Where colour comes from

White light contains all the visible wavelengths (about 400 nm for violet to 700 nm for red). When white light passes through a solution of a transition metal ion, some wavelengths are **absorbed** and the rest are **transmitted**. Your eye sees the transmitted light, which is the **complementary colour** of the light absorbed.

| Colour absorbed | Colour seen |
|---|---|
| Red | Blue-green |
| Orange | Blue |
| Yellow | Violet |
| Green | Purple |
| Blue | Orange |
| Violet | Yellow-green |

For example, hexaaquacopper(II) ions absorb in the red and yellow end of the spectrum, so a copper(II) solution looks blue.

## The d-electron explanation

In an isolated transition metal ion, the five 3d orbitals have the same energy. When ligands bond to the ion, their lone pairs repel the d electrons. The repulsion is not the same for every d orbital, so the d orbitals split into two groups with a small difference in energy, ΔE.

In the ground state, the d electrons are in the lower-energy orbitals. When light of the right energy shines on the ion, **a d electron absorbs the energy and moves to a higher d orbital (an excited state)**. Only photons whose energy matches ΔE are absorbed. The remaining wavelengths pass through.

The energy gap and the absorbed light are linked by:

```
ΔE = hν = hc/λ
```

where:

- ΔE is the energy difference between the ground state and the excited state, in joules for one ion;
- h is Planck's constant, 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s;
- ν is the frequency of the light absorbed, in s⁻¹ (Hz);
- c is the speed of light, 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹;
- λ is the wavelength in metres.

### Worked example 1: energy from wavelength

A complex absorbs light of wavelength 600 nm. Calculate ΔE for one ion and for one mole.

```
λ = 600 nm = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m = 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m
ΔE = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
per mole: 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.00 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹ = 200 kJ mol⁻¹
```

### Worked example 2: wavelength from energy

The energy gap in a complex ion is 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Calculate the wavelength of light absorbed and decide which colour is absorbed.

```
λ = hc/ΔE = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.85 × 10⁻⁷ m = 485 nm
```

485 nm is blue light, so the solution appears orange.

### Worked example 3: frequency

Light of frequency 5.40 × 10¹⁴ Hz is absorbed. Find ΔE and the wavelength.

```
ΔE = hν = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.40 × 10¹⁴ = 3.58 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
λ = c/ν = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.40 × 10¹⁴ = 5.56 × 10⁻⁷ m = 556 nm (green)
```

Check the units every time: wavelength must be in metres, and the energy comes out in joules.

## Which ions are coloured?

Colour needs d electrons that can move to a vacant place in a higher d orbital. That means an **incomplete d sub-level**.

| Ion | d configuration | Coloured? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cu²⁺ | 3d⁹ | Yes (blue) | Incomplete d sub-level |
| Fe³⁺ | 3d⁵ | Yes (yellow-brown) | Incomplete d sub-level |
| Sc³⁺ | 3d⁰ | No | No d electrons to excite |
| Cu⁺ | 3d¹⁰ | No | Full d sub-level, no space to move into |
| Zn²⁺ | 3d¹⁰ | No | Full d sub-level |

## What changes the colour

The size of ΔE depends on the environment of the ion. Changing it changes the wavelength absorbed and so the colour seen.

| Change | Example | Colour change |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidation state | Fe²⁺(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) | Pale green → yellow-brown |
| Co-ordination number | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [CuCl₄]²⁻ | Blue → yellow-green |
| Ligand | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | Pale blue → deep blue |
| Ligand | [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [CoCl₄]²⁻ | Pink → blue |

In an exam answer, state which of the three has changed, state that the energy gap ΔE changes, and then state that the wavelength of light absorbed therefore changes.

## Colorimetry

A colorimeter measures how much light a coloured solution absorbs. It is used to find the concentration of a coloured ion.

1. Choose a **filter** that is the complementary colour of the solution (so that it passes the light that is absorbed most strongly). For blue copper(II) solution, use a red or orange filter.
2. Make a series of **standard solutions** of known concentration (often by diluting a stock solution).
3. Zero the colorimeter with distilled water (or a blank) and measure the **absorbance** of each standard.
4. Plot a **calibration graph** of absorbance against concentration. It is a straight line through the origin.
5. Measure the absorbance of the unknown and read off its concentration.

For a colourless ion (for example Fe³⁺ with thiocyanate or Cu²⁺ with ammonia), you can add a ligand that forms a strongly coloured complex before measuring.

### Worked example 4: using a calibration graph

Absorbance readings for copper(II) standards are:

| Concentration / mol dm⁻³ | 0.0020 | 0.0040 | 0.0060 | 0.0080 | 0.0100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absorbance | 0.11 | 0.22 | 0.33 | 0.44 | 0.55 |

The graph is a straight line through the origin with a gradient of 0.55 ÷ 0.0100 = 55 dm³ mol⁻¹. A sample has absorbance 0.385.

```
c = absorbance ÷ gradient = 0.385 ÷ 55 = 0.0070 mol dm⁻³
```

If this solution was prepared by diluting 5.00 cm³ of the original to 50.0 cm³, the original concentration is 0.0070 × 10 = 0.070 mol dm⁻³.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the colour seen is the colour absorbed. It is the complementary colour.
- Saying the electrons "emit" light. In absorption, d electrons move to a higher energy level.
- Explaining colour as electrons moving between shells instead of between d orbitals of different energy.
- Using nanometres without converting to metres in ΔE = hc/λ.
- Forgetting to multiply by the Avogadro constant for energy per mole.
- Explaining a colour change with "the ligand changes" without saying that ΔE changes.
- Claiming that Zn²⁺ is colourless because it is not a metal. The reason is the full d sub-level.
- Using a filter of the same colour as the solution in colorimetry.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain why a complex is coloured**: absorbs some wavelengths; d electron promoted from ground state to excited state; the rest of the light is transmitted.
- **Explain why the colour changes**: name what changed (oxidation state, co-ordination number or ligand); ΔE changes; wavelength absorbed changes.
- **Calculate**: convert units, show the equation, give units.
- **Describe colorimetry**: filter, standards, calibration graph, read off the concentration.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are some transition metal compounds white?**
If no d electron can be excited by visible light, or if there are no d electrons or the d sub-level is full, the compound does not absorb visible light and appears white or colourless.

**What does the colorimeter filter do?**
It passes only the wavelengths that the solution absorbs most strongly, which gives the biggest and most accurate reading.

**Why must the graph pass through the origin?**
No coloured ions means no absorbance, so zero concentration gives zero absorbance after the blank is subtracted.
