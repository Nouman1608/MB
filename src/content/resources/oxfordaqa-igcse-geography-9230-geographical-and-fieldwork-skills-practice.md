These questions cover section 3.3, Geographical and Fieldwork skills, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3 (exams May/June 2020 onwards). They practise the skills in 3.3.1 and the enquiry process in 3.3.2, which are assessed in Paper 3 (1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks, 28% of the qualification); geographical skills can also appear on Papers 1 and 2. All places and data are fictional, except where a question asks about your own enquiry.

Revise first with the [study guide for this unit](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/) and the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** Explain the difference between primary data and secondary data. **[2]**

**2.** On a fictional map extract, a church lies in grid square 4718. It is three-tenths of the way east across the square and six-tenths of the way north.

**(a)** Give the six-figure grid reference of the church. **[1]**
**(b)** On a 1:50 000 map, the church is 3.8 cm from the railway station. Calculate the real distance in kilometres. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** On a 1:25 000 map, a 120 m contour and a spot height of 270 m are 3.0 cm apart. Calculate the average gradient between them as "1 in x". Show your working. **[3]**

**3.** Students counted litter items in 11 equal-sized quadrats in a fictional park, Hartsgrove Park.

| Quadrat | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | H | I | J | K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Litter items | 6 | 15 | 3 | 9 | 41 | 6 | 11 | 5 | 12 | 8 | 6 |

**(a)** Find the median. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the inter-quartile range. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the median is a better measure of typical litter levels than the mean for this data. **[2]**

**4.** Visitors to a fictional forest park, Glenarra, rose from 48,000 in 2015 to 66,000 in 2025. Over the same period, the number of bird species recorded there fell from 32 to 26.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in visitors. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage decrease in bird species, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** A survey found that the 75th percentile of visitor group size was 6 people. State what this means. **[1]**

**5.** Suggest the most appropriate method to present each data set, and justify your choice.

**(a)** The number of males and females in five-year age groups in a fictional town. **[2]**
**(b)** The home towns of 80 visitors to a fictional music festival. **[2]**
**(c)** The percentage of each land use along one street. **[2]**

**6.** Students measured percentage vegetation cover in quadrats along a transect inland from the sea at a fictional dune system, Pellarby Dunes.

| Distance from sea (m) | 10 | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 | 130 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vegetation cover (%) | 5 | 18 | 30 | 22 | 55 | 63 | 78 | 90 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship between distance from the sea and vegetation cover. Use figures. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the anomaly and suggest one reason for it. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the mean point that a line of best fit should pass close to. **[2]**
**(d)** Using a line of best fit, estimate the vegetation cover at 100 m. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why using the line to predict cover at 300 m would be unreliable. **[2]**

**7.**

**(a)** A choropleth map of a fictional district, Kelvaston, shows the percentage of households without a car. The classes are 0 to 14.9%, 15 to 29.9% and 30 to 44.9%. Ward values are: A 12%, B 27%, C 41%, D 18%, E 35%, F 9%. Which wards are shaded in the darkest class, and which in the lightest? **[2]**
**(b)** On an isoline map of noise, the 60 dB and 65 dB isolines are 2 cm apart beside a main road but 6 cm apart inside a park. Explain what this shows. **[3]**

**8.** Fifty visitors to a fictional market, Saltmere, gave their travel distance.

| Distance (km) | 0 to under 5 | 5 to under 10 | 10 to under 15 | 15 to under 20 | 20 to under 25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 6 | 15 | 12 | 9 | 8 |

**(a)** State the modal class. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the cumulative frequency for each class. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the class that contains the median. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage of visitors who travelled less than 15 km. **[1]**

**9.** A fictional council leaflet contains this graph and claim.

> A bar chart titled "Recycling soars!" shows two bars: 2023, 9,400 tonnes, and 2025, 9,900 tonnes. The y-axis starts at 9,000 tonnes. The 2025 bar looks more than twice as tall as the 2023 bar.

**(a)** Calculate the actual percentage increase in recycling, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify **two** weaknesses in how the data is presented, and explain how each could mislead. **[4]**

**10.** Students at a fictional school, Brookhill, test the hypothesis: "Air temperature is higher over tarmac than over grass on the school site."

**(a)** Describe one method of collecting primary data for this enquiry, including the equipment. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a sampling strategy and justify it. **[3]**
**(c)** Identify one risk in this fieldwork and explain how it could be reduced. **[2]**
**(d)** At 1 pm on one sunny day, the mean of five readings was 21.4°C over tarmac and 19.6°C over grass. The students concluded that the hypothesis is true. Evaluate this conclusion. **[4]**

**11.** Give the title of your individual fieldwork enquiry. To what extent did your data collection methods allow you to reach a reliable conclusion? **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of alternative methods of data collection, such as selecting internet data or interviewing people at home, compared with practical fieldwork, for an individual fieldwork enquiry. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary data is collected first-hand by the investigator, for example by measuring or interviewing [1]. Secondary data has already been collected by someone else, for example census figures or published maps [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* each mark needs a clear contrast; an example alone, with no definition, is not enough.

**2. (a)** **473186** [1].
**(b)** 3.8 x 500 m = 1,900 m [1] = **1.9 km** [1].
**(c)** Horizontal distance 3.0 x 250 = 750 m [1]. Rise 270 - 120 = 150 m [1]. 750 / 150 = 5, so **1 in 5** [1].
*Examiner insight:* gradient marks are lost when centimetres are divided by metres; convert the map distance first and show each step.

**3. (a)** Ordered: 3, 5, 6, 6, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15, 41. Median = 6th value = **8** [1].
**(b)** Total = 122 [1]; 122 / 11 = **11.1** [1].
**(c)** Lower quartile (3rd value) = 6 and upper quartile (9th value) = 12 [1]; IQR = **6** [1].
**(d)** The mean is pulled up by one extreme value, 41 in quadrat E [1]; the median of 8 is closer to most quadrats, so it better represents typical litter [1].
*Examiner insight:* "the mean is affected by outliers" needs the actual outlier from the table to gain the application mark.

**4. (a)** (66,000 - 48,000) / 48,000 x 100 [1] = **37.5%** [1].
**(b)** (32 - 26) / 32 x 100 [1] = **18.8%** decrease [1].
**(c)** Three-quarters (75%) of visitor groups had 6 people or fewer [1].
*Examiner insight:* the most common slip is dividing by the 2025 figure; always divide by the original value.

**5. (a)** Population pyramid [1], because it shows males and females side by side for each age group, so the age-sex structure is clear [1].
**(b)** Desire lines on a map [1], because straight lines from each home town to the festival show where visitors came from and how far they travelled [1].
**(c)** Pie chart or divided bar [1], because the land uses are parts of one whole that add to 100% [1].
*Examiner insight:* the justification must link to the type of data; "it is easy to read" earns nothing.

**6. (a)** Positive relationship: vegetation cover increases with distance from the sea [1]. It rises from 5% at 10 m to 90% at 150 m [1]. The increase is steepest between 70 m and 90 m, from 22% to 55% [1].
**(b)** The anomaly is 22% at 70 m, lower than at 50 m [1]. A footpath or trampling by visitors may have removed vegetation at that point [1].
**(c)** Mean distance = 640 / 8 = 80 m [1]. Mean cover = 361 / 8 = 45.1% [1].
**(d)** About **57%** (accept any value from 52% to 62% read from a sensible line) [1].
**(e)** 300 m is far beyond the data, so the trend may not continue [1]; the line predicts about 180%, which is impossible because cover cannot exceed 100% [1].
*Examiner insight:* in "describe the relationship" questions, quote paired values (distance and cover) and name the anomaly; explanation is not credited.

**7. (a)** Darkest class: wards C and E [1]. Lightest class: wards A and F [1].
**(b)** Near the main road, the isolines are close, so noise changes quickly over a short distance [1], showing a steep noise gradient away from the traffic source [1]. In the park, wide spacing shows noise changes slowly, because there is no strong source nearby [1].
*Examiner insight:* isoline answers need both spacing and meaning: say "close together, so a steep gradient", not just "close together".

**8. (a)** 5 to under 10 km [1].
**(b)** 6, 21, 33 [1]; 42, 50 [1].
**(c)** The median is the 25th value, in the 10 to under 15 km class [1].
**(d)** 33 / 50 x 100 = **66%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* write the cumulative totals in a column beside the table; this makes the median class and percentages quick to read off.

**9. (a)** (9,900 - 9,400) / 9,400 x 100 [1] = **5.3%** [1].
**(b)** The y-axis starts at 9,000 tonnes, not zero [1], so a 5.3% rise looks like more than doubling [1]. Only two years are shown [1], so we cannot tell whether 2025 is part of a trend or a one-year rise [1].
*Examiner insight:* weakness questions need the effect on the reader explained; naming "truncated axis" alone gains only half the marks.

**10. (a)** Use a digital thermometer held at the same height above the ground at each site [1] and record readings after the display settles, for the same length of time at each site [1].
**(b)** Stratified sampling [1]: choose an equal number of sites on tarmac and on grass [1], so both surfaces are fairly represented and can be compared [1].
**(c)** Risk: vehicles in the school car park on tarmac sites [1]. Reduce it by working in pairs with one person watching for traffic, or by measuring when the car park is closed [1].
**(d)** The data supports the hypothesis: tarmac was 1.8°C warmer [1]. However, readings were taken at one time on one sunny day [1], so results may differ on cloudy days or in the morning [1]. Five readings is a small sample, and shade from buildings may affect some sites, so the conclusion is only partly reliable [1].
*Examiner insight:* "evaluate" needs a judgement; finish with how reliable the conclusion is, not just a list of problems.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show what earns credit. Example title: "How does land use change with distance from the centre of a local town?"

- States the title and the idea being tested, that commercial land use falls away from the centre [1].
- Describes the methods precisely: ground floor land use recorded every 100 m along two roads, for 1 km each [1].
- Justifies a method: systematic sampling gave even coverage and was easy to repeat [1].
- Explains a strength: two roads reduced the effect of one unusual road [1].
- Uses own results: commercial use fell from 80% of sites within 200 m of the centre to 10% at 1 km [1].
- Identifies a problem: some buildings had mixed uses, so classifying them was a judgement [1].
- Identifies a limit: only two roads were sampled, and land between sites was missed [1].
- Suggests improvement: two more roads and a local planning map would test the result [1].
- Reaches a judgement: methods allowed a largely reliable conclusion; the trend was clear on both roads [1].
**[9]**
*Examiner insight:* top-level answers use details from their own enquiry throughout; general comments about "sample size" with no figures stay in the lower levels.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. Indicative points:

- The specification allows alternative methods if they involve planning and selecting, not ready-processed data [1].
- Internet data such as census tables can cover large areas and long time periods [1].
- It can be selected to fit the enquiry question, so it still develops selection skills [1].
- However, it may be out of date or collected for another purpose, so it may not match the enquiry [1].
- Interviews at home are safe and quick, and give qualitative views [1].
- But a small, convenient sample of family or friends may be biased [1].
- Practical fieldwork gives first-hand data and lets you see the place you study [1].
- It brings risks, cost and time limits, so samples may be small [1].
- Judgement: alternative methods are useful when fieldwork is not possible, but real fieldwork gives better understanding of the place and is recommended by the specification for full access to the marks [1].
**[9]**
*Examiner insight:* a balanced answer that ends with a supported judgement scores higher than a one-sided list of advantages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing northings before eastings in grid references.
- Mixing map centimetres with ground metres in gradient calculations.
- Dividing by the new value in percentage change.
- Finding the median before putting the data in order.
- Explaining a pattern when the question only asks you to describe it.
- Naming a risk with no way to reduce it.
- Writing about fieldwork in general instead of your own titled enquiry in Section C.
- Listing problems in an evaluation with no final judgement.
