These notes cover **hormones and pheromones**, which fit the **chemical messengers** term of the biological approach in IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). The 2027 guide asks for the role of one or more chemical messengers in human behaviour; it does not name pheromones, so treat the pheromone material as extension. The 2019 course listed hormones and pheromones as a topic; its last examination session was November 2026. For the approach as a whole, use the [biological approach revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-biological-approach-revision-notes/); for the full explanations and a worked Section A answer, see the [hormones and pheromones study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-hormones-and-pheromones/).

## Recall in one minute

- A hormone is released by an endocrine gland into the blood and acts on distant target cells; slower, longer-lasting and more widespread than a neurotransmitter.
- A pheromone is a chemical signal between members of the same species.
- Every explanation needs two parts: the mechanism (gland, receptor, brain area) and the specific behaviour.
- Hormone studies are often experiments with a dose and a placebo, so they support causal claims but use artificial doses.
- Human pheromone research is contested: no human pheromone has been chemically identified.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Endocrine system | glands that release hormones into the bloodstream |
| Target cell | a cell with receptors that a particular hormone binds to |
| HPA axis | hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal glands: the hormonal stress pathway ending in cortisol release |
| Cortisol | stress hormone from the adrenal cortex; acts on receptors in the hippocampus, among other areas |
| Oxytocin | made in the hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary; linked to bonding and trust |
| Testosterone | mainly from the testes and ovaries; linked to dominance and competition |
| Pheromone | chemical signal released by one individual that affects another of the same species |
| Releaser pheromone | triggers an immediate behaviour |
| Primer pheromone | causes a slower physiological change, such as altering a cycle |
| MHC | major histocompatibility complex: immune-system genes that influence body odour |
| Intranasal administration | giving a hormone by nasal spray, as in oxytocin studies |

## Hormone studies

| Study | What was done | Finding | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newcomer et al. (1999) | double-blind; healthy adults given a high dose of cortisol, a low dose or a placebo for four days; prose recall tested | high dose impaired verbal declarative memory; recovered after treatment stopped | cortisol and memory; a causal hormone study |
| Kosfeld et al. (2005) | men given oxytocin or placebo by nasal spray, then a trust game with real money, plus a control game against a computer | oxytocin group transferred more to a human trustee; no effect against the computer | oxytocin and trust |
| De Dreu et al. (2011) | oxytocin or placebo before choices about in-group and out-group members | oxytocin increased in-group favouritism | shows oxytocin's effect depends on context |
| Nave, Camerer and McCullough (2015) | critical review of oxytocin and trust research | replications have been inconsistent | evaluation of Kosfeld |

## Pheromone studies

| Study | Finding | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| McClintock (1971) | women living together showed increasing menstrual synchrony | later studies (for example Yang and Schank, 2006) did not find synchrony |
| Stern and McClintock (1998) | odourless armpit compounds from donors shortened or lengthened recipients' cycles, depending on the donor's phase | consistent with primer-pheromone action; the active chemical was not identified |
| Wedekind et al. (1995) | women preferred the T-shirt odour of men with dissimilar MHC genes; reversed in women on oral contraceptives | odour preference is not proof of a pheromone; could involve learning |
| Hare et al. (2017) | androstadienone and estratetraenol did not change judgements of gender or attractiveness of faces | challenges the two best-known candidate chemicals |
| Wyatt (2015) | review argues no human pheromone has been identified | candidate molecules were chosen without first isolating an active chemical |

## Evaluation toolkit

- **Strength of hormone experiments:** manipulation plus random allocation and a placebo allows cause and effect to be inferred.
- **Artificial dose:** a tablet or spray may not act like the body's own release, so ecological validity is limited.
- **Samples:** all-male samples (Kosfeld) and healthy young adults (Newcomer) limit generalisation.
- **Replication:** a single influential finding is weak support if later studies fail to repeat it.
- **Reductionism:** a hormone rarely explains a behaviour alone; appraisal, expectations and culture also matter.
- **Animal evidence:** pheromones are well established in many species, but generalising to humans is the point in dispute.

## How the topic is examined

| Paper 1 task | What to do |
|---|---|
| Section A (two short-answer questions, 8 marks in all), for example "explain the role of one chemical messenger in human behaviour and give an example" | mechanism, one example explained, a final sentence linking the example to the question; no evaluation needed; study details are not assessed |
| Section C (one concept-based extended response, 15 marks), for example "evaluate the role of chemical messengers in human behaviour, with reference to causality" | relevant research as evidence, strengths and limitations, links to the concept, a reasoned judgement |

## Exam traps

- **No mechanism.** "Oxytocin makes people trust each other" is a claim, not an explanation.
- **Wrong direction of evidence.** Newcomer shows high cortisol *impairs* memory; do not say it improves it.
- **Leaving out the control game.** It is what shows Kosfeld's effect was about trusting people, not taking risks.
- **"Humans have pheromones."** Say the evidence is suggestive but contested.
- **Evaluating in Section A.** It wastes time that Section C needs.
- **Invented details.** If you cannot remember a sample size, leave it out.

## Self-test

1. Give two differences between a hormone and a neurotransmitter.
2. Which gland releases cortisol, and at the end of which pathway?
3. What did Newcomer et al. (1999) find in the high-dose group?
4. Why can Newcomer et al. (1999) support a causal conclusion?
5. What was the purpose of the computer condition in Kosfeld et al. (2005)?
6. Give one limitation of Kosfeld et al. (2005).
7. What did De Dreu et al. (2011) add to our understanding of oxytocin?
8. Define a pheromone.
9. What is the difference between a releaser and a primer pheromone?
10. What did Wedekind et al. (1995) find, and what changed the pattern?
11. Give one reason the existence of human pheromones is disputed.
12. Which Paper 1 section asks for an explained example but not evaluation?

**Answers**

1. Any two: hormones travel in the bloodstream rather than across a synapse; they act more slowly; their effects last longer; they act on more widespread target cells.
2. The adrenal cortex, at the end of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.
3. Poorer recall of a prose passage (impaired verbal declarative memory), which returned to normal after treatment ended.
4. Cortisol was manipulated, participants were randomly allocated to dose or placebo, and the procedure was double-blind.
5. To check whether oxytocin increased risk-taking in general; it did not, so the effect was on trusting another person.
6. For example: an all-male sample; an artificial nasal dose; inconsistent replication (Nave et al., 2015).
7. Oxytocin increased favouritism towards the participant's in-group, so its social effects depend on context rather than making people trusting of everyone.
8. A chemical signal released by one member of a species that affects the behaviour or physiology of another member of the same species.
9. A releaser pheromone triggers an immediate behaviour; a primer pheromone causes a slower physiological change.
10. Women preferred the odour of men with MHC genes unlike their own; the preference was reversed in women taking oral contraceptives.
11. For example: no human pheromone has been chemically identified (Wyatt, 2015); candidate chemicals failed in tests such as Hare et al. (2017); synchrony findings were not replicated; expectations and learning are hard to rule out.
12. Section A, the short-answer questions.
